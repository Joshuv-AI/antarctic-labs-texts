Examining inclusive research practices to engage communities: a qualitative inquiry
Authors: Sze Wan Celine Chan, Kimberly Diaz, Nelson Lin, Trisa Chakraborty, Shahmir H. Ali, Lan N. Ðoàn, Simona C. Kwon, Erinn M. Hade, Stella S. Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEfforts to improve representation of diverse communities in clinical research have progressed, yet suboptimal reach due to lack of language support, mistrust, and cultural misalignment, persist. While research often prioritizes generalizability or recruiting a specific community, a tailored approach is essential to recruit and retain diverse communities. This study aims to illustrate the depth and breadth of existing community-engaged research methods that have been used to increase recruitment and retention of diverse communities.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted to identify common themes and best practices of inclusive research with 48 principal investigators and their study staff who had successfully engaged with different diverse communities from research institutions in the United States. Data were analyzed using a rapid qualitative analysis approach and organized thematically.FindingsSix key themes related to conducting community-engaged research emerged: research team as community health advocate, community-academic partnership characteristics, best practices for conducting research with diverse communities, incurred financial costs, sustainability of inclusive research efforts, and system-related challenges. Participants emphasized the role of research teams to uplift community voices, build authentic and collaborative partnerships, prioritize cultural practices of the community, and use data to inform policy. Best practices included providing incentives, offering bilingual materials and having bilingual staff, and using multi-pronged recruitment and retention strategies. Financial costs included both tangible costs such as transportation and honoraria, while intangible costs such as time and trust-building were also highlighted. Sustainability required early planning, capacity-building, and continuous fiscal support. Challenges included insufficient institutional support, burnout, and staff turnover, stressing the need for multi-level strategies and solutions integrated into the research environment to support inclusive research practices.ConclusionsA general research approach that tailors to specific communities’ preferences and fosters collaborative partnerships will better enhance representation in research. However, the research environment requires attention to multi-level strategies to minimize challenges to conducting community-engaged research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28230-2.

Background

Over the past decades, ensuring representation of diverse communities has become a key focus in clinical research, with increased efforts to include historically underrepresented and excluded communities. While research inclusivity is something of global significance, it can differ from country to country in terms of which groups are more or less represented. Different policies and funding may also encourage or prevent representation. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine define the term ‘underrepresented’ in reference to “studies, research foci, funding streams, and other components of the research ecosystem that draw from populations whose demographic characteristics are not representative of the people who ought to benefit from it [1].”

Increasingly, funders and the scientific community recognize the importance of broad representation and have made progress in identifying evidence-informed methods and implementation strategies that promote recruitment and engagement of diverse communities. However, the representation of certain groups — defined by age, sex, racial or ethnic identity, disability and/or place of residence – in research remains low in the United States (U.S.). For instance, Latine individuals comprise 18% of the U.S. population but represent only 9.3% of participants enrolled in National Institutes of Health (NIH)-funded research [2]. This underrepresentation persists even though racial and ethnic minority groups report similar rates of willingness to participate in clinical trials as White populations [3, 4]. Another review of > 14,000 ClinicalTrials.gov trials demonstrated that almost 20% of interventional clinical trials for adults in the U.S. required that participants be native English speakers or be able to speak, read, and/or understand English. This is in contract to only 2.7% of studies offering translation accommodation and 2.3% offering Spanish language accommodation despite an estimated 8.4% of households in the U.S. spoke English less than very well [5, 6]. Beyond race and ethnicity, 14% of U.S. residents live in rural areas but only 3% of National Cancer Institute’s clinical research from 2011 to 2016 focus on rural populations [7]. Without appropriate and thorough representation of the U.S. population in health research, the benefits of interventions being tested in clinical trials may have limited reach beyond the general population in improving health across all populations. Lack of representation in health research also has implications for reducing costs long-term, promoting innovation, improving data quality and data sharing, and enhancing precision medicine [1].

Community-engaged research (CEnR) is a process where researchers collaborate with communities and directly incorporate their voices and priorities into the research process [8]. CEnR has been demonstrated as an effective way to mitigate barriers and factors preventing participation in research, and has also been identified as an evidence-based practice [3, 9]. Strategies cited by community-engaged researchers emphasize reducing structural barriers to recruitment and retention (e.g., providing transportation and in-language information) as well as building cultural competency/cultural sensitivity within the research team [10–14]. Other strategies include hiring staff representative of the community, forming partnerships with trusted community-based organizations, and adapting or tailoring an intervention to align with social and cultural norms and values [15, 16]. Strategies may be unique to certain communities, such as developing formal agreements with tribal governments when conducting research with Indigenous communities or using plain language in consent process for people with low health literacy [15, 17]. These CEnR practices have not fully been adopted in clinical, administrative or epidemiologic settings.

Although there has been a growing emphasis on conducting research that targets underrepresented communities in the U.S., such efforts often take a narrow lens in terms of a singular population focus, and the added value to the scientific knowledge base may be scrutinized under traditional epidemiological standards as lacking generalizability [18, 19]. Research often aims for generalizability to increase benefit for as many people as possible. Evidence, however, indicates that a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach to recruitment and retention does not effectively reach underrepresented communities and that strategies need to be adapted to specific groups and communities to increase research participation [15, 20]. This presents a methodological challenge for researchers, who must balance the need for generalizable findings but also be inclusive in their engagement and recruitment of diverse populations. While ongoing focused efforts provide valuable insights to better reach different communities, there is still room to further refine practices and develop a framework that can be applied across multiple groups to advance the overall field of research. There is a need for a research approach that balances the necessity of group-specific tailoring with the identification of generalizable methods of community engagement that can be applied to address the needs of diverse underrepresented groups simultaneously.

This study focuses specifically on academic settings because policies and ethical review broad procedures within academic institutions play a critical role in how research can be conducted by individual research teams [21]. Although community voices are crucial in CEnR, we start by seeking to better understand the landscape of the academic environment in community-academic partnerships. The driving question behind this study asks whether it is possible to have a generalizable research approach to study recruitment that is effective at reaching underrepresented communities while honoring best practices in reaching specific groups. Specifically, our questions were: (1) are there commonalities in the strategies to reach and engage diverse communities? and (2) what methods and approaches are foundational to reaching a specific group? Thus, the aim of this paper is to identify existing practices and strategies that have been used to increase recruitment and retention of underrepresented communities in research within the U.S.

Methods

Semi-structured interviews with dyads of principal investigators (PI) and their study staff who had successfully used standard CEnR practices to engage with communities experiencing health disparities from U.S. research institutions were used to distill common themes and best practices of inclusive research. Populations with health disparities as defined by the NIH National Institute of Minority Health and Health Disparities (NIMHD) are “populations who experience greater challenges to optimal health and are closely linked with intergenerational social, economic, and/or environmental factors [22]. ” NIMHD designated populations with health disparities include racial and/or ethnic minority populations, populations with low socioeconomic status, underserved rural populations, people with disabilities, and sexual minority groups [22]. Ethical approval for the study was granted by NYU Langone Health’s Institutional Review Board.

Participants and recruitment

The research team created a list of CEnR PIs that work across 14 populations. The list of PI contacts was created based on a combination of searches in published peer-review manuscripts, the research team’s network of community-engaged researchers, and recommendations from participants that have been interviewed or potential interviewees. The selection of populations was guided by the NIMHD’s list of ‘NIH-designated populations with health disparities [22]. ’ The research team included additional populations by consulting CEnR experts and CEnR literature. The final selection of populations included: 1) American Indian and Alaska Natives (AI/AN); 2) Asian Americans; 3) Black or African Americans; 4) Hispanic or Latine; 5) Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Transgender, Queer, Intersex, and Asexual (LGBTQIA+); 6) low income communities; 7) Middle Eastern and North Africans (MENA) or Arab Americans; 8) Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islanders (NH/PI); 9) older adults; 10) people with disabilities; 11) rural communities; 12) women; 13) cross-racial research efforts (e.g., inclusive of two or more race groups); and 14) multi-ethnic (e.g., inclusive of two or more ethnic groups). The study aimed to recruit two dyads of PIs and study staff for each category for a total of n = 56 interviews.

The study coordinator (S.W.C) formally invited potential PI interviewees over email with information about the study. A total of three email attempts were sent until a PI was marked as ‘no response.’ PIs expressing interest were emailed an Informed Consent Form (ICF) for review. Prior to the interview, S.W.C went over the ICF again and asked if the PI had any questions. Verbal consent to participate and to be audio recorded took place right before the interview. After the PI completed the interview, S.W.C asked the PI to recommend one of their study staff to be interviewed. The study coordinator formally invited potential study staff interviewees over email with information about the study. The recruitment process for PI interviewees was replicated for study staff interviewees. Recruitment began April 2024 and was completed February 2025.

Data collection

An interview guide was developed and was informed by Research Triangle Institute International’s (RTI) Framework for Equity-Centered Transformative Research. Briefly, the framework incorporates various research approaches and paradigms intended to shift power to minoritized and marginalized communities to achieve social transformation through research. The framework consists of three elements of equity-transformative research (researcher, research, and process of researching) and examines five core equity principles (diversity, inclusion, equity, belonging, and transformation/liberation) in relation to each element [23]. Like the framework, the interview guide was divided into three sections representing the three elements and questions in each section covering the five principles (Table 1).

Interviews were conducted via Zoom between April 2024 and March 2025. Interviews were about 45 to 60 min. All interviews were conducted in English by S.W.C with another research team member (K.D, N.L, or T.C) who served as a notetaker. The notetaker acted as a scribe to document the interviewees’ answers in bulleted summaries and notable quotes during the interview in a data extraction template (Supplementary file 1) [24–26]. All interviews were audio-recorded and reviewed by the notetaker to refine the interview notes, as needed. Upon completion of the interview, participants received a $50 incentive for their time.

Data analysis

A rapid qualitive analysis approach as described by Hamilton was used to analyze the data. Briefly, this analysis approach uses matrices to summarize key points and organizes data from all participants across domains of interest [27, 28]. For our study, notetakers (K.D, N.L, and T.C) cleaned up their notes after interview completion and if certain sections in the data extraction template were lacking context, the audio recording would be used to supplement details. S.W.C reviewed all summaries to ensure completeness of data extraction template and consistency in the level of detail provided by notetakers.

After verification, S.W.C then summarized all interview notes into two matrices (one for PI and one for study staff) on Microsoft Excel [29]. Matrices were organized so that each column represented either a PI or a study staff and each row represented a core equity principle for the three elements in RTI’s Framework for Equity-Centered Transformative Research. Notetakers (K.D, N.L, and T.C) then reviewed the matrices to verify the summaries notes were accurately organized to the corresponding row. Any discrepancy was resolved through discussion in bi-weekly team meetings (S.W.C, K.D, N.L, and T.C) and aided with review of audio recordings as necessary. Consensus was reached when all team members agreed upon the organization of matrices.

After consensus was reached, S.W.C synthesized the matrices into general vs. population-specific findings. If multiple PIs or study staff mentioned a research practice that was broadly applicable across populations, it was organized as a general finding. General findings thus acted as recurring results and demonstrated saturation emergence. If a research practice was discussed specifically in relation to the population the PI or study staff worked with, it was organized as a population-specific finding. Lastly, S.W.C grouped general findings into emerging CEnR themes. The initial themes were then reviewed and refined by the senior investigators of this study, who have multiple years of experience in CEnR. The iterative review process at each stage ensured a deeper and shared understanding of the data.

Participants

In total, 59 PIs were invited to participate in the research, and 28 agreed to be interviewed and audio recorded. Of the remaining PIs who were not interviewed, 17 did not respond to the outreach email attempts and 14 declined to participate. When given specific reasons for declining the invitation to participate, PIs stated they either felt they were not the best fit for the research scope, had busy schedules, or were on sabbatical leave. We also invited study staff of the 28 PI who were interviewed to participate in the research. 20 study staff agreed to be interviewed and audio recorded while eight study staff were unable to be interviewed. Of the eight study staff who were not interviewed, four were because the PI had a recent change in staffing and do not have a current staff person who could speak about the research work and four were because the study staff did not respond to the outreach email attempts (Table 3). Interview outreach outcomes for study staff

Six key themes were identified in relation to conducting CEnR: (1) research team as community health advocate; (2) partnership characteristics; (3) research best practices; (4) financial costs; (5) sustainability; and (6) challenges. Below we report findings from each of the themes and highlight community-specific findings, where applicable. The letters ‘P’ and ‘S’ followed with participants’ ID numbers will be used to distinguish quotes from PIs vs. study staff respectively.

Research team as community health advocate

Two sub-themes emerged in relation to how the research team acted as community health advocates: for the community and to inform policy. First, both PIs and study staff agreed that for the community, research teams had to uplift community voices, create visibility, build community partners’ capacity, and bring tools and resources into the community. One study staff shared that “data is one of the most powerful forms of storytelling and research is helping others have their story told for social good” (S010). Similarly, a PI discussed their role in “empowering community members and allowing them an elevated voice to highlight what their communities really need” (P027). PIs go one step further by specifically stating their role to provide equal representation to all subgroups. For example, P004 discussed creating opportunities to highlight subgroups in the LGBTQIA+ community while P025 tried to be mindful and “be a proxy voice to the voices that are missing at the table.”

Second, both PIs and study staff recognized the role of research teams to advocate to policymakers about data justice or translatable practices. For example, a PI suggested that their role is “to produce data documenting needs of communities, which allows for funding and policy changes” (P005). Or S007 recognized that “the way we interpret data tells the story of the community so researchers have to make sure data that is collected tells a story that helps inform decisionmakers.” Community-specific examples mentioned by both PI and study staff included advocating for data disaggregation for Asian American populations, advocating for a separate racial category for MENA populations, and honoring indigenous methodologies for the NH/PI and AI/AN communities.

CEnR partnership characteristics

Interviewees discussed two sub-themes: best practices to develop partnerships and the value of having partnerships. Both PIs and study staff agreed to develop authentic partnerships, the academic-community partnership need to be “collaborative,” “bi-directional,” “respectful,” “flexible,” “honest,” and “transparent.” For NH/PI communities, PIs specifically mentioned that partnership development had to be rooted in pillars of fa’a (respect, relationships, love, community, reciprocity, spirituality) and Talanoa (shared participatory dialogue). Specific partnership building examples listed by both PIs and study staff included asking and prioritizing community needs and interests, forming community advisory boards (CAB) with diverse representation of collaborators across sectors (e.g., providers, community-based organizations, health departments, advocacy organizations), and volunteering the research team’s time to be present outside the scope of research (e.g., attending events within the community on weekends). Beyond the shared examples, PIs also pointed out logistical examples such as setting aside dedicated community-partnership budgets, building administrative support for community partners, and offering technical assistance (e.g. grant writing).

When discussing the value of having community partnerships, both PIs and study staff described that it takes time and continuous engagement to build trust within partnerships. Despite the time and engagement needed, both PIs and study staff emphasized the idea that research would “not be possible without partnerships” (S002) and that the authentic partnerships led to their team being introduced to the community or future expansion of their research. PIs further articulated the value of community partnerships in terms of methodological benefits. For example, P006 felt that their “work feels more valid and generalizable when using participatory approach” because it allowed them to “develop meaningful research questions to the community” while P026 discussed how a community-based approach makes their research more robust by “bringing in multiple expertise rather than relying on one scientist’s expertise, which is a limitation of traditional research approaches.”

Briefly, to facilitate community partner involvement in research, both PIs and study staff listed best practices such as providing honoraria or being flexible about timeline shifts when working with community partners – especially in the context that community partners have “different bandwidth and workloads” (S017). To engage participants, both PIs and study staff mentioned providing the correct type of incentive for community members (e.g. “gift certificates to local ethnic grocery store” P002), providing food at research encounters, or “offering transportation to and from study center” (S012). For recruitment of participants, both PIs and study staff cited best practices such as using a multi-pronged outreach strategy (e.g., social media, snowball, word-of-mouth, in-person) and working with important members of the community (e.g. priests, imams, Mayor). P009 emphasized that research teams should not “expect the community to come to you, and go where the community is.” For retention of participants, both PIs and study staff discussed examples such as providing swag bags or maintaining contact with participants through newsletters and holiday cards. Finally, in terms of lived experience and training of research teams, both PIs and study staff agreed that research teams should understand the cultural practices of the community and hire bilingual staff.

Financial costs of CEnR

Interviewees brought up four types of costs associated with conducting CEnR: community partner and participants, research, research team, and intangible. For community partner and participant costs, both PIs and study staff mentioned tangible costs the research team should budget to be spent on community partners and participants, with specific examples such as transportation (e.g. taxi, subway rides, Uber/Lyft, gas reimbursement), honoraria to community partner, incentives to compensate for participants’ time (ranging from $50–100), swag (e.g. branded socks or backpacks), food and snacks, and childcare.

For research costs, both PIs and study staff listed tangible costs related to conducting research, which included translations that are valid and reliable, transportation for research team to attend community events, social media ads and other advertisements for recruitment, space rentals to conduct research activities, research equipment (e.g. devices to measure environmental exposures), subcontract with labs (e.g. for urine samples), and open-access publications. Community-specific research costs may include budgeting to work with trusted advocates and leaders (e.g., drag queens) for recruitment with the LGBTQIA+ community, or hiring sign language interpreters when working with people with disabilities.

Costs related to the research team included salaries and staff time to conduct CEnR. PIs specifically discussed a need to hire bilingual/bicultural staff or people from the community as well as a need to hire for specific community engagement personnel. For example, P010 described their research team comprising a participant experience director, community engagement lead, and two community engagement specialists. However, PIs were also cognizant of the limited budget to spend on personnel. Even though “community research is personnel driven, salary levels have gone up within universities but ceilings for budgets haven’t” (P022). Although study staff did not specifically discuss staff roles and general salary costs, some mentioned the need for additional salaries to be paid for staff time to ensure language and cultural concordance of research materials. Translations were viewed as “double the work” (S024) and to ensure cultural appropriateness, study staff would check “all developed materials three times” (S027).

Intangible costs were mentioned by both PIs and study staff. CEnR research teams, in addition to the research activities, were managing time-intensive community relationships. Research teams recognized that “trust isn’t something you can put into a budget” (S021), which meant an investment of time. Both PIs and study staff felt they had to be readily available to community partners such as “having meetings at night, doing personal asks, or having a 24/7 open-door policy and make it as easy as possible for community partners to engage in research” (P005). Beyond time and trust-building, PIs specifically discussed having to work with their academic institutions to uphold CEnR research best practices. For example, food, which was a mentioned engagement strategy for participants, had to be paid for by “stored up funds and endowment funds” by P011 since “different universities have different ways of handling financials allowances” and because “food is not covered on federal grants.” In another example, P005 had “to put in special requests with HR to get new staff paid more” for their key language skills and lived experiences necessary for conducting CEnR.

Sustainability for CEnR

Interviewees discussed three sub-themes relating to sustainability: fiscal, researcher, and community partner. The first two sub-themes were brought up exclusively by PIs. To fiscally sustain their research, PIs discussed the need to continue applying for grants, embedding programs into existing mechanisms (e.g. social services, non-research funding), and using their research academic discretionary funds to maintain community partnerships. PIs also brought up general advice for researchers to sustain CEnR work, which included thinking about sustainability early on and investing in research projects that will continue without funding, and training the next generation of researchers to continue their research work. The last sub-theme was discussed by both PIs and study staff. Both PIs and study staff interviewees agreed that to sustain community partnerships, research teams had to help build community partners’ organizational capacity, use a train-the-trainer model to continue an intervention without the research teams, and assist community partners to write their own grants.

CEnR challenges

Three interrelated sub-themes related to the challenges of conducting CEnR emerged: insufficient institutional support, burnout, and staff turnover. Both PIs and study staff admitted to feeling insufficient institutional support for CEnR. PIs discussed insufficient institutional support from a fiscal lens with one PI wishing for “funding for admin support” (P018). Similarly, another PI “felt strapped for resources even though working within a larger university” and a “sense of guilt asking partners” to assist with resource costs (P016). Study staff on the other hand, felt insufficient institutional support for conducting research. For example, S028 reflected that their research team have to “teach IRB about community-engaged work.”

The lack of infrastructure for research teams to conduct CEnR contributes to burnout. In addition to the time-intensive nature of conducting CEnR, research teams may be experiencing emotionally-charged interactions within their work, which was most salient in study staffs’ answers. In some instances, study staff may face “microaggression or discrimination” when doing CEnR work, requiring “resources for bicultural research team to process” what they experienced (P024). In other instances, community members may be directly sharing their needs (e.g. food) to the research team that may have to be ignored because study staff are unable to provide “referrals and access to resources” or because the research study did not have budget for a social worker (S026). For PIs on the other hand, burnout manifested more so in having to balance competing demands of upholding CEnR best practices with more traditional research expectations. PIs discussed experiencing burnout from the “pressures inherent in universities to publish” (P022) and “doing extra work to navigate with institutional policies” (P005).

Lastly, for staff turnover, PIs viewed rapid staff turnover as bad for research recruitment and retention. Due to the time it takes to form relationships with partners and community members and to build capacity of the research team to be trained in CEnR best practices, many PIs felt it was crucial to “maintain core group of staff members by investing in their professional development and career path” (P023). Other PIs expressed the importance to offer “a competitive salary to retain a bilingual and bicultural team” (P024) or having to “bolster team morale with internal team events and celebrate their work” (P004).

Discussion

This study examined existing research methods that have been used to increase recruitment and retention of understudied health disparities communities in the U.S. with dyads of PI and study staff. PIs and study staff enumerated best practices in terms of the role researchers should assume, partnership development, conducting research, research budgets, and sustainability. In addition to enumerating an inclusive research approach, PIs and study staff also brought up challenges of conducting CEnR such as having insufficient institutional support and navigating burnout and staff turnover specific to the context of CEnR. Within the literature, these challenges have been described as ‘hidden curriculum,’ which are the implicit norms and values within institutions that affect research team’s ability to conduct inclusive research. These ‘hidden curriculum’ affects the trust and relationships built over time with partners as well as straining the research team [30–32].

Together, findings from this study form an understanding of the components necessary for supporting an inclusive research approach that prioritizes the recruitment and retention of diverse communities in the U.S. (Fig. 1). Institutional support, funding, and authentic partnerships form the core components of the inclusive research approach. At the institutional level, support of inclusive research encompasses the ease of research teams to be able to engage in research best practices or implement staff retention strategies without having to navigate additional administrative policies [11, 13]. The theme ‘CEnR challenges’ best contextualizes this component. Within the interviews, PIs and study staff brought up the potential burnout and even staff turnover from operating in climates that do not adequately support CEnR teams. Funding for inclusive research accounts for various research costs at all stages of a research project [33]. As demonstrated throughout the ‘financial costs of CEnR’ and ‘sustainability for CEnR’ themes, inclusive research spans from partnership building, to implementation, and maintenance. Each stage requires accounting for different costs such as honoraria, staff salaries, translations, research equipment, or time. Authentic partnerships comprise of the formation of a diverse research team that can ensure interactions with community stakeholders are flexible, bi-directional and collaborative [13]. This component is reflected in the ‘CEnR partnership characteristic’ and ‘CEnR research best practices’ themes. Across the board, PIs and study staff highlight the importance of having team members from the community or who are bilingual, bicultural, and/or understand specific community context and preferences.

Our results further emphasize the multi-level nature of supporting CEnR in the U.S., which has also been reflected in other recent work. For example, Engage for Equity PLUS (E2PLUS) has highlighted structural and systemic barriers within academic health centers to start and sustain CEnR. Barriers identified by E2PLUS include limited resources and funding for forming and sustaining partnerships, fiscal and administrative challenges to provide community subawards, and lack of recognition of community partners/CEnR in traditional institutional review boards. Addressing all the barriers would require support and solutions at the institutional-level [31, 34]. Beyond barriers within academic health centers, language-related challenges such as lack of training for research teams in how to include participants who use languages other than English, lack of validated measures in non-English languages, and lack of free or low-cost interpretation/translation services also all require investments from research institutions and funders [33]. Barriers to engaging in inclusive research are more effectively addressed at the systems-level and should not solely be burdened by individual researchers, which may contribute to burnout and strain on the research team.

There are some limitations to this study. First, 28 research teams were interviewed, which only capture a snapshot of CEnR conducted within the U.S. The perspectives of other teams, especially additional community-specific findings, may be missed. However, we included a diverse sample of research teams and were able to reach saturation in the data analysis phase – especially of findings describing best practices of inclusive research. Further, the approach and methodology used in this study may be transferred to similar research questions.

Second, findings represent the point of view of academic research personnel only and did not include community partners who may have been involved in CEnR. Certain findings such as financial costs (e.g., intangible costs of trust-building) or challenges (e.g., burnout from emotionally-charged interactions or insufficient institutional support) of CEnR may be overrepresented and not salient for community partners in the community-academic partnership. However, our study aim was to illustrate research methods to increase recruitment and retention of understudied health disparities communities from a research institutional lens. A research-only lens provides an initial first step in identifying inclusive research best practices within the realities of current structures that research teams operate in. Future work to incorporate community perspectives and even academic leadership would further contextualize our research fundings. A follow-up series of interviews can be conducted with leadership and staff at community-based organizations, faith-based organizations, and universities.

Lastly, this study was limited to U.S.-based research teams and findings may only be transferable to the unique historical and social contexts of American communities. However, similarities in the factors contributing to global health inequalities (e.g., healthcare access, education, food insecurity, digital divide), including inequalities within the U.S., may increase the relevancy of our findings to other countries [35].

Conclusions

Amongst health equity researchers, there are mounting arguments that ‘hard-to-reach’ populations are misnomers. Too often, research teams, research institutions, and funding organizations are ‘hardly-reaching’ underrepresented communities and opting to stick to conventional research procedures instead of addressing barriers to participation [20, 36]. Although there are researchers who have been doing CEnR, they are working within the limitations of existing research climate. Our findings are the start of a potential approach to conduct inclusive research that is transferable across diverse groups in the U.S. This general approach still requires specific strategies for different groups – where the researcher has the responsibility to design the research to fit the needs of the community and their localized contexts and not the other way around. Moreover, the researcher needs to be supported in a multi-level environment that minimizes challenges to conducting CEnR. Findings underscore the importance of centering community preferences, fostering collaborative partnerships, and addressing systemic barriers to ensure research is both inclusive and sustainable. Future research efforts may focus on the intangible costs of conducting research and the prevention of burnout. By adopting inclusive research best practices, tailoring efforts to specific communities, and advocating for institutional support and change, researchers can take concrete steps in their research methods and processes to better enhance representation in research.
