Unique Transcriptional Responses by the Sea Anemone Nematostella vectensis to Individual Bacteria: Roles for Bacterial Species, Secreted Products and the Microbiome
Authors: Quinton Krueger, Britney Phippen, Adam M Reitzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnimal responses to bacteria, viruses and other microorganisms are important for understanding how host–microbe interactions lead to divergent molecular responses by the host and the assembly of the holobiont. The immune system of the animal and the resident microbial community are critical factors in the response, or lack thereof, by a host to a newly encountered microbe. Here, we describe the transcriptional response of the model cnidarian Nematostella vectensis when exposed to three Live Cell (LC) isolates ( Bacillus velezensis , Pseudoalteromonas spiralis and Vibrio diabolicus ) or their Cell Free Supernatants (CFS) with and without the native microbiome. Gene expression revealed large variation in the overall response ranging from 829 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for antibiotically treated anemones exposed to B. velezensis to 49 DEGs in xenic individuals inoculated with V. diabolicus . The anemone's response similarly varied widely if the exposure was to live bacteria or the secreted products, especially for B. velezensis . Genes associated with the RIG‐I‐like immune pathway showed large differences in expression whereas the transcriptional variation for NF‐kB and toll‐like receptor were not significantly different. The response to each gram‐negative bacteria differed from a purified bacterial ligand (lipopolysaccharide). Together, these results support the hypothesis that N. vectensis mounts unique transcriptional responses to individual bacterial species and their products, which are dependent on the presence of the native microbiome. The complexity of this molecular response further highlights the dynamic interplay between cnidarians and microbes in the assembly and maintenance of the holobiont.Keywords: bacteria, cnidarians, immunity, transcriptomics

Introduction

Bacteria associate with multicellular organisms for a variety of functions through inter‐kingdom interactions. These microbial consorts differ from the environment (Carrier and Reitzel 2019; Baldassarre et al. 2021), indicating relationships that can be independent from the environmental microbiome. Because microbial roles as mutualists, pathogens, or commensals can depend on host and environmental context, how individual bacteria interact with hosts across conditions and whether marine invertebrate hosts exhibit distinct responses to single non‐pathogenic bacterial species remains poorly characterized (Delgadillo‐Ordoñez et al. 2024; Domin et al. 2018; Muñoz‐Cerro et al. 2024; Peixoto et al. 2017). Determining the strength of these effects on a resultant microbiome is important as more research aims to engineer host associated bacterial communities with probiotics to improve physiological resilience of organisms, including those in marine environments (Ahmed et al. 2019; Costa et al. 2021; Damjanovic et al. 2019; Doering et al. 2021; Peixoto et al. 2021; Dungan et al. 2022). Many interactions have been hypothesized via genomic inquiry and rely on correlations of shifts in the community with changes in molecular responses (Bourne et al. 2016; Wegley Kelly et al. 2018). This approach is limited in breadth because most bacteria remain uncultured and thus interpretations typically depend on predictions from genome content for the microbe (Huggett and Apprill 2019). Utilizing non‐pathogenic environmental isolates that naturally associate with the host organism will help to elucidate the complexity of the interactions that result in a holobiont. Characterization of these ecologically relevant bacteria and their variation in impacts on hosts can further increase the applicability and understanding of how hosts interact with individual bacteria species that compose a complex microbiome.

Previous research has also shown that cnidarians associate with a diverse microbial community that is shaped by the host, the environment and their interactions (McCauley et al. 2023; McFall‐Ngai et al. 2013; Thompson et al. 2015). The effects of antibiotics on corals have shown to be largely effective at eliminating the microbiome of invertebrate hosts (Connelly et al. 2022; Neely et al. 2020). Similarly, antibiotics have been shown to be largely effective at eliminating culturable bacteria in N. vectensis , while having limited impacts on the transcriptome of the host (Krueger et al. 2024), there is still a negative effect on regeneration (Da‐Anoy et al. 2025). The reduction and potential elimination of the resident microbiome creates the opportunity to study individual effects specific bacteria have with the host. Many studies have investigated microbiome level shifts in cnidarian hosts, but few have investigated the direct effects of individual mutualistic or commensal bacteria on the host (Billaud et al. 2023; Poole et al. 2016; Roesel and Vollmer 2019). Additionally, the native microbiome can protect against introduced colonizing bacteria (Muñoz‐Cerro et al. 2024; Ritchie 2006). This function to prohibit or allow bacteria colonization via bacteria‐bacteria interactions may impact the host due to bacterial lysis of the competing cells (Ortiz et al. 2021; Rubio‐Portillo et al. 2020). Particular pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Serratia marcescens associated with Exaptasia diaphana, Vibrio coralliilyticus with corals and sea anemones) can have severe negative effects on the cnidarian host, including tissue loss and death (Brennan et al. 2017; Poole et al. 2016), which can be dependent on the environment (e.g., temperature). However, in many cases naturally occurring pathogenic bacteria are unknown, or studies have used bacteria unlikely to associate with the cnidarian species investigated. Thus, various studies have investigated the effects of components of bacteria that may be pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), like lipopolysaccharides (LPS), on cnidarian hosts. LPS has been shown to elicit transcriptional effects in Exaiptasia (Schmittmann et al. 2021; Seneca et al. 2020) and a variety of other cnidarians (Bisanti et al. 2024; Connelly et al. 2020; Fuess et al. 2016; Pita et al. 2018). How LPS or any other components derived from single bacterial species mimic the response of a cnidarian host has to a live mutualistic bacterium remains largely unexplored (Connelly et al. 2020).

Specific bacteria are known to perform specific functions within host organisms. Additionally, the effects of the bacterial cells can differ from the effects of proteins and other compounds that are secreted from the bacteria. For example, some bacteria provide beneficial functions for the host including some species of Bacillus in a variety of organisms. These functions include probiotic effects in a variety of marine invertebrates (e.g., growth rate, pathogen defence, positive shifts in the microbiome) (Delgadillo‐Ordoñez et al. 2024; Elbahnaswy et al. 2024; Emam and Dunlap 2020; Jamali et al. 2015). Secondly, Pseudoalteromonas species have been shown to provide a benefit to marine invertebrate hosts, including the induction of settlement (Peng et al. 2020); and antagonism of other pathogenic species including pathogenic Vibrio (Delgadillo‐Ordoñez et al. 2024; Tang et al. 2020; Tebben et al. 2011). Alternatively, members from the Vibrio genus have mechanisms to induce pathogenicity in marine invertebrate hosts including secretion systems and other virulence factors (Cervino et al. 2004; Ericson et al. 2022), but some species may be symbiotic (Nyholm et al. 2000). Overall, the application of bacteria into marine invertebrate hosts, whether natural or artificially induced, presents a new stimulus for the host to contend with. Interestingly, the innate immune system has been recently described in these organisms, which may play a role in modification in the bacterial microbiome.

Cnidarians have an increasingly well described innate immune system composed of multiple receptors, transcription factors and effector genes that are hypothesized to be central to the immune response (Bisanti et al. 2024; Levy et al. 2021; Levy and Mass 2022; Miller et al. 2007; Saco et al. 2023; Young et al. 2023). Genomic data has shown that cnidarians have several pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that may be important for detecting and mounting immune responses to microbes, including Toll‐like receptors (TLRs), cyclic GMP‐AMP synthase (cGAS) and retinoic acid inducible gene I like receptors (RLRs) which may sense foreign bacterial and viral ligands. Diverse TLRs have been identified in cnidarians and at least one TLR from Nematostella vectensis functions in response to the bacterial ligand flagellin (Brennan et al. 2017). Similarly, there are diverse cGAS proteins in cnidarians that bind nucleic acids but remain largely uncharacterized (Li et al. 2023). One of two RLRs from the sea anemone Nematostella vectensis binds to double stranded RNA but the other does not (Lewandowska et al. 2021). These foreign ligand sensors then may signal to downstream effectors (Williams and Gilmore 2022). NF‐kB has been known to function as a major signalling pathway to initiate transcription for many genes, including innate immune responses (Brennan and Gilmore 2018; Brennan et al. 2017; Williams and Gilmore 2022). Stimulator of interferon response cGAMP interactor 1 (STING) is present in cnidarians, but it is unknown if it is involved in immune response to foreign bacterial products. The mitochondrial anti‐viral signalling protein (MAVS) may receive signal from RLRs during viral infections in some animals, thus activating interferon responses, but its potential functions in cnidarians are unknown. Interferons are responsible for promoting innate immune responses in a wide variety of animals. These functions are yet to be characterized in depth within the context of bacterial infections in cnidarian hosts, and there is no evidence cnidarians have interferon signalling (Williams and Gilmore 2022). Cytosolic nucleic acid sensors detect bacterial pathogens in murine models (Mills Erin et al. 2025) as well as human microglia (Johnson et al. 2020). Additionally, marine invertebrate TLRs have recently been characterized to sense bacterial products including lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and flagellin (Brennan et al. 2017; Miller et al. 2007; Saco et al. 2023). These specific receptors (both in ligands and location in or on the cell) may be activated by specific bacteria species and their products during colonization or infection. Together, these studies suggest cnidarians may have many components of the innate immune pathway of bilaterians, but how these components are expressed in relation to microbial exposures of naturally occurring bacteria have been largely unexplored.

Nematostella vectensis has developed into a model cnidarian system to determine the composition, dynamics and potential functions of bacterial communities (Artamonova and Mushegian 2013; Domin et al. 2023; Fraune et al. 2016; Har et al. 2015). While the microbiome varies based on host genotype, developmental stage, tissue and environmental conditions, connecting the molecular responses of the anemone host to these variations in the microbiome or to individual bacterial species remains little explored. Bacteria that associate with N. vectensis have been cultured in the lab; thus, they are amenable to comparative effects of individual species on the host's molecular response (Domin et al. 2018, Har et al. 2015, Krueger et al. 2022, Krueger and Reitzel 2024). Bacterial colonization of N. vectensis is dependent on the host, as the microbiome with anemones composed of lab‐associated bacteria differs from the control medium (Domin et al. 2023). Interestingly, the lab and field‐associated microbiomes of N. vectensis are vastly different, with field microbiomes typically harbouring a higher diversity of bacteria (Baldassarre et al. 2023). Other studies with cnidarians have focused on naturally associated bacteria and their impacts on host phenotypes or transcriptional responses (Billaud et al. 2023; Krueger et al. 2022; Seneca et al. 2020).

Previously we have demonstrated that the effects of antibiotics on the transcription of the host N. vectensis have limited direct effects on the gene expression of this anemone species (Krueger et al. 2024). However, the impact of the antibiotic application on the resident microbial community can have a substantial effect on the host's response to the introduction of new colonizing bacteria or to the secreted products from these new bacteria. We hypothesized these responses could be due to the reduction in bacteria‐bacteria interactions in the bacteria‐depleted anemones, the absence of resident bacteria to interact and potentially metabolize the secreted products, or the changes in the biology of the colonizing bacteria when associating with a host with a depopulated microbiome. Here, we characterize the effects of individual bacteria and their products on gene expression of the starlet sea anemone, Nematostella vectensis . The bacteria and their secreted products were introduced to xenic and antibiotic treated sea anemones. We compare the impacts of these naturally associated bacteria with purified lipopolysaccharide to test if this commonly used ligand of bacteria simulates similar effects as naturally associated bacteria.

Methods

Animal and Bacteria Collection

The animals used in this experiment were from a lab acclimated population of Nematostella vectensis in the Reitzel Lab at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte. These anemones were originally isolated from Rhode River, Maryland in the 1980s and cultured in the laboratories of Dr. Mark Martindale (University of Florida) and Dr. John Finnerty (Boston University) for many years thereafter. The Reitzel lab started their lab colony at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte in 2012 from animals from this lab population. Thus, individuals have been in laboratory culture for decades prior to these experiments. Adult anemones were maintained in glass dishware in 15 parts per thousand (ppt) Artificial Seawater (ASW) (Instant Ocean) and fed freshly hatched Artemia salina three times per week. Anemones were cultured at room temperature prior to experimentation. Culturing bowls were cleaned weekly and fresh ASW was replaced at the same time. Environmental bacteria were collected and isolated as previously described (Krueger and Reitzel 2024; Krueger et al. 2022). Bacteria species were isolated from an estuary and N. vectensis in Baruch, South Carolina, USA. Bacillus velezensis and V. diabolicus were isolated from N. vectensis , while P. spiralis was isolated from the surrounding water. Individual anemones were homogenized via mechanical dissociation with 1.5 mL tubes and pestles. Homongenates or estuarine water were then struck onto marine broth (MB) agar. Individual colonies were subsequently struck three additional times on new MB plates.

Generation and Maintenance of Xenic and Antibiotic Treated Nematostella vectensis

Adult anemones (n = 4) were incubated in two conditions to determine how acute and chronic exposure to antibiotics impacts gene expression as previously described (Krueger et al. 2024). Here, we used anemones that were treated with the antibiotic cocktail (i.e., 3 days antibiotic exposure followed by 3 days sterile in ASW) to reduce the potential effects of antibiotic stress (Krueger et al. 2024). Briefly, antibiotic treated anemones were exposed for 3 days in sterile filtered ASW. Then anemones were then exposed to bacteria or their products the following day. The antibiotic solution consisted of 50 μg mL−1 of Ampicillin, Streptomycin, Neomycin and Rifampicin, each. All antibiotics were mixed into 15 ppt ASW. After antibiotic treatment, anemones were cultured in bacteria media (Marine and Estuarine broth) and not bacterial growth was observed. However, it is possible that there may be other bacteria present that would grow with specific media containing other nutrients. Thus, we will refer to these anemones as ‘antibiotically treated’, although under the conditions tested, they were axenic. Anemones in all treatments were fed freshly hatched Artemia salina three times per week. To eliminate the transfer of bacteria from the A. salina to the anemones, cysts were cultured in a 1% w/v solution of thiomersal prepared in 30 ppt ASW prior to their isolation for feeding (Verschuere et al. 1999). Artemia salina were washed in filter sterilized 30 ppt ASW and confirmed to not harbour culturable bacteria (turbidity test in Estuarine Broth) prior to their use as food.

Bacterial Exposures to Nematostella vectensis

Following the antibiotic treatments for native community and antibiotic treated conditions, anemones were exposed to live bacteria (live cell, or LC) and cell free supernatant (CFS) for three species: Bacillus velezensis, Pseudoalteromonas spiralis and Vibrio diabolicus . Bacteria were grown to 108 CFUs mL−1 in Marine Broth, centrifuged and washed with sterile ASW, and exposed to N. vectensis . CFS of B. velezensis , P. spiralis and V. diabolicus was generated by growing these bacteria through log phase to a concentration of 108 CFUs mL−1, washing via centrifugation, and subsequently returned to sterile 15 ppt ASW for 1 h. The bacteria were then pelleted by centrifugation, and the supernatant was collected for exposures to the anemones. Individual anemones were exposed to 4 mL of this solution (n = 4) for 1 day until preservation for RNA extraction. Lipopolysaccharides ( Escherichia coli 0127:B8, Sigma‐Aldrich L3129‐100MG) were introduced to N. vectensis at two concentrations, 5 μg mL−1 and 5 ng mL−1 until preservation (50 μL of 1 mg mL−1 stock and 5 μL of 10 μg mL−1 stock, respectively). All bacterial and LPS exposures to N. vectensis were 24 h prior to preservation in RNAlater. None of the exposures resulted in phenotypic changes indicating poor health for the duration of the experiment.

RNA Extraction and Sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from 72 individual animals (2 controls, 2 antibiotic (i.e., antibiotically treated/xenic) treatments per each of the following conditions: 3 Live Cell (LC) treatments, 3 Cell Free Supernatant (CFS) treatments, and 2 LPS treatments, 4 replicates for each condition) using the RNAqueous kit (Ambion) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, after pipetting off and discarding RNAlater from each sample, whole animals were lysed by pipetting in lysis buffer, washed 2–3 times and eluted on a column. Genomic DNA was removed using DNA‐free kit (Invitrogen). RNA was assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA was shipped for tag‐based library preparation at the University of Texas at Austin's Genomic Sequencing and Analysis Facility (GSAF) as in Meyer et al. (2011) and adapted for Illumina HiSeq 2500. Briefly, total RNA was heat‐fragmented and then reverse‐transcribed into first‐strand cDNA. The cDNA was purified using AMPure beads and PCR‐amplified for 18 cycles. Unique Illumina barcodes were added in an additional PCR step for indexing of each sample. After an additional purification step, libraries were pooled, spot‐checked for quality on a Bioanalyzer (Agilent and Pico) and size‐selected using BluePippin (350–550 bp fragments). A full version of the library preparation protocol can be found at https://github.com/z0on/tag‐based_RNAseq.

Sequence Analysis

Read processing was performed following the tagseq processing pipeline (https://github.com/z0on/tag‐based_RNAseq). Briefly, raw sequences were adapter clipped and trimmed using cutadapt with the options ‐a AAAAAAAA ‐a AGATCGG ‐q 15 ‐m 25 (Martin, 2011). Next, the trimmed sequences were aligned to the indexed N. vectensis Vienna transcriptome with Bowtie2, using standard options (Fredman et al. 2022) Bowtie2 was implemented to align the trimmed and quality‐controlled reads with the options –local –no‐hd –no‐sq –no‐unal ‐k 5 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012). The alignment sam file was converted to the bam file type with samtools with standard options (Li et al. 2009). The trimmed and indexed reads that were successfully aligned to the reference transcriptome were quantified using the taqseq analysis Perl script samcount.pl. to enumerate alignments to isogroups with the standard options (Meyer et al. 2011). The resulting count table can be found in Table S1 and is publicly available at NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus database GSE167114.

Differential Gene Expression

DESeq2 generated the differential gene expression using a negative binomial generalized linear model, ~condition (condition column, Table S2), which separates each individual bacteria or product exposure and antibiotic condition. Using this model, pairwise comparisons were performed using results(dds,contrast = c(‘B’,’A’)), where a Log2Fold Change > 2, and an adjusted p‐value < 0.05 was considered significant using the Wald test and Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure (Love et al. 2014). Normalized count data from DESeq2 were transformed using the rlog function with standard options. These normalized, rlog‐transformed counts were used for principle component analysis, which was performed with the prcomp function with standard options. The significance, based on Manhattan distances, was tested with the adonis function in the vegan package, using the model distance ~ antibiotic * source * bacteria (Table S2) with standard options (Oksanen et al. 2013). The total transcriptome was normalized and rlog‐transformed, which was used to generate the PCA plot in Figure 1. Genes identified as differentially expressed (Log2Fold Change > 2, and an adjusted p‐value < 0.05), were grouped using ComplexUpset package, which was used to generate the intersection table and the UpSet plots (Lex et al. 2014). The resulting UpSet plots were further stylized using Adobe Illustrator. We conducted an additional Principal Component Analysis with a truncated list of genes related to immune orthologs in Figure 5. These genes were identified in (Lewandowska et al. 2021), and significant differential gene expression for this subset of candidate immune genes for bacterial defence was Log2Fold Change > 2 and an adjusted p‐value < 0.05.

Gene Ontology

Pairwise gene expression tables from DESeq2 analysis were used as input for Gene Ontology analysis. GO enrichment was calculated using the GO_MWU (Mann–Whitney U rank sum) package with the continuous measurement of Log2Fold changes of all expressed genes, with the default two‐tailed test and signed genes, which groups genes based on the directionality of their expression, up‐ and down‐regulated genes (https://github.com/z0on/GO_MWU). Enriched and depleted modules are signified as red and blue, respectively. GO categories that were 10% or greater of the total comparison or had less than five genes were not considered, using gomwuStats(). Hierarchical clustering of enriched GO terms was performed with gomwuPlot(), with standard level configuration (0.05, 0.01, 0.001), unless otherwise specified.

Results

Principal Component Analysis

Tag based sequencing resulted in a total of 92.8 million reads after quality control (Table S3), which were subsequently mapped to the reference transcriptome with an average mapping efficiency of 77.7%. This resulted in an average of 1.2 million reads per sample, which is slightly lower than other studies (Leach and Reitzel 2019; Meyer et al. 2011). While this may reduce the ability to detect differential gene expression of lowly expressed genes, it does not prohibit a robust analysis of comparative gene expression (Love et al. 2014). A principal component analysis showed clustering of unexposed controls, LPS, and Vibrio diabolicus , while the Bacillus velezensis Cell‐free Supernatant (CFS) and the Live Cell (LC) of Pseudoalteromonas spiralis clustered separately along PC1 (Figure 1). Antibiotic pre‐treatment had a marginal effect on gene expression (p = 0.06, Table S4). Moreover, the addition of LC, CFS and LPS had a significant impact on gene expression, and the species of bacteria was also a significant factor (p = 0.001, p = 0.001, respectively). The interaction effect of antibiotic exposure and the bacterial source products (LC, CFS, LPS) was also significant (p = 0.029). Alternatively, the interaction effects of antibiotics and bacterial source products, LC, CFS and LPS with respect to bacterial species were both significant (p = 0.001, p = 0.001, respectively).

DEGs Across Treatments

The number of DEGs in each treatment revealed strong differences in the transcriptional responses of Nematostella vectensis to these individual bacteria and their secreted products. For anemones that were exposed while retaining the native microbial community, the majority of DEGs were downregulated relative to controls without addition of bacteria (Figure 2A). The largest transcriptional response resulted from anemones exposed to the CFS of B. velezensis (826 DEGs) followed by anemones exposed to LC of P. spiralis (725 DEGs, Figure 2B, see ‘Set Size’), most of which were downregulated. These two exposures, despite using different types of bacterial exposure (CFS vs. LC), shared 339 DEGs that were uniquely differentially expressed, and they all had the same direction in expression (up‐ or downregulated) compared with unexposed controls. Anemones exposed to LC of B. velezensis showed only 59 DEGs compared to controls, which was 93% lower than for the CFS treatment to the same species. Inversely, anemones exposed to CFS of P. spiralis had about half as many DEGs when compared with anemones exposed to LC (346 vs. 725 DEGs, respectively). Vibrio diabolicus resulted in the least number of DEGs of the three bacteria (95 for CFS, 49 for LC). Most of the genes were downregulated in both treatments with V. diabolicus with the majority being unique to V. diabolicus . LPS at both concentrations showed the smallest impact on anemones with their native microbiome intact, with only 73 DEGs at the high concentration and 47 at the low concentration. Thirty‐one genes were differentially expressed in all treatments compared with the control, all of which were downregulated.

Anemones that were pretreated with antibiotics to reduce the native microbial community showed similar gene expression profiles with some notable differences. Similar to anemones with the native microbial community, gene expression profiles were generally downregulated compared with anemones with only the antibiotic pretreatment (Figure 2C). An exception to this overall trend were anemones exposed to CFS from P. spiralis , where most genes (72%) were upregulated. Consistent with exposures to anemones with the native community present, the largest transcriptional response was observed for anemones exposed to the CFS of B. velezensis (Figure 2D). Antibiotic pretreated N. vectensis exposed to LC of B. velezensis had 918 DEGs when compared with antibiotic control, which was a substantially different response relative to anemones with the native microbial community condition that had only 59 DEGs when treated with LC of B. velezensis . For the antibiotically treated anemones exposed to LC B. velezensis , 200 out of 503 DEGs were upregulated compared to unexposed anemones. Interestingly, 299 DEGs were shared between the LC and CFS of B. velezensis compared to antibiotic controls, with only 87 of those being upregulated. The only other treatment with a large unique effect on gene expression was the CFS from P. spiralis introduced to antibiotic treated anemones, where there were 309 DEGs (483 total) compared to antibiotic pretreated anemones not exposed to the CFS. This response to CFS from P. spiralis was much larger than the exposure to LC from the same bacteria; an inversion of the response observed for anemones with the native microbiome. Similar to the anemones that retained the native microbial community, antibiotically pretreated anemones had a modest response to V. diabolicus and an even smaller response to both concentrations of LPS compared with unexposed control anemones. Thirty‐six genes were differentially expressed in all treatments compared with control, all of which were downregulated with all 31 shared with the anemones with the native microbial community plus five unique DEGs.

Overall, we found that Vibrio diabolicus , regardless of treatment (i.e., presence of microbial community, exposure to either LC or CFS) had minimal impacts on gene expression. Alternatively, the removal or presence of the microbial community showed stark differences in the transcriptional response of N. vectensis for other bacteria, particularly LC of B. velezensis and CFS of P. spiralis . In these conditions, the removal of the microbial community resulted in a large reduction in the number of DEGs for the LC of B. velezensis , while the opposite was observed for the CFS of P. spiralis .

Removal of the Native Microbiome Changes the Functional Categories of Anemone Genes When Exposed to Bacterial Products

Statistical comparisons of Gene Ontology (GO) terms represented in each treatment showed strong differences in the inferred functions for the DEGs dependent on the bacterial exposure. We observed new differentially expressed genes for anemones (xenic and antibiotically treated). However, exposures of anemones to B. velezeneis and P. spiralis revealed substantial differences in the functional roles for the DEGS.

For B. velezensis, we found that when N. vectensis maintains its microbiome, the LC showed minimal responses to this stimuli, while the opposite was apparent with the depletion of the microbiome. Functionally, GO categories of these DEGs with respect to the presence or absence of the microbiome resulted in a variety of different processes to be enriched or depleted. Importantly, helicase and microtubule motor were enriched, while oxidoreductase mechanisms were depleted for Molecular Function with removal of the microbiome (Figure 3A). Biological Processes included the enrichment of protein autophosphorylation, chromosome organization and detection of stimulus, while ‘antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigen via MHC class I’ and respiration related terms were depleted. The Cellular Component category resulted in the most significant terms, including the enrichment of cilium, chromosome, junctions and spindles. Alternatively, extracellular space, vacuole and mitochondria related terms were depleted with the application of antibiotics and the exposure to LC B. velezensis .

When anemones with their native microbial community were exposed to LC of P. spiralis there was a large response in gene expression when compared to the same exposure with a depleted microbiome. With respect to GO terms under Molecular Function, oxidoreductase was enriched, while protein kinases/phosphatases, tyrosine phosphatase and calmodulin binding were depleted (Figure 3B). Additionally, the removal of the native microbiome resulted in a depletion of several GO categories related to immune responses, for example, ‘regulation of immune system process’, ‘positive regulation of response to stimulus’ and ‘activation of immune response’, when the native microbiome is removed. Alternatively, several metabolic and biosynthetic processes were enriched, including lipopolysaccharide. Oxidation–reduction, and organic acid metabolic process. The Cellular Component category resulted in the depletion of neuron related terms, including synapse, cell junction and cell cortex, as well as IPAF inflammasome complex. Enriched terms due to the removal of the microbiome and the addition of LC P. spiralis were extracellular space, and mitochondria related terms.

Genes With Divergent Expression Between Bacteria Treatments

While the vast majority of differentially expressed genes observed between treatments had consistent direction of expression, a subset showed opposite expression patterns depending on the treatment (Figures 2A,C and 4A,B). Exposure to the CFS and LC of the bacteria P. spiralis resulted in seven genes having opposite responses between the treatments. These genes from this comparison were MAVS, betaine—homocysteine S‐methyltransferase, mortality factor 4‐like protein isoform X3, two uncharacterized ncRNAs, and two unknown transcripts (Figure 4A). All these genes were upregulated with the P. spiralis CFS and downregulated in the P. spiralis LC. The CFS of both B. velezensis and P. spiralis had a single gene with unknown function (NVE7473) that was upregulated in P. spiralis and down in B. velezensis . The CFS of B. velezensis and P. spiralis in the antibiotically treated anemone resulted in the mixed expression of three genes, which all were unannotated genes (Figure 4B). Interestingly four out of the 10 uniquely expressed genes in the application of CFS of B. velezensis and V. diabolicus in the antibiotically treated host were a mixed response. Three of these genes are not annotated, while the fourth gene, complement factor B, is a likely immune gene (Kimura et al. 2009). All four genes that were mixed in their responses were significantly downregulated with the addition of B. velezensis CFS and significantly upregulated in the V. diabolicus CFS (Figure 4B).

Shared Downregulated Genes for All Bacteria Treatments

The application of bacteria or their products resulted in a shared downregulation in a subset of genes, regardless of the state of the native microbiome. A total of 31 genes were differentially expressed across all bacterial treatments in the organisms that retained their native community of bacteria, all of which were downregulated (Figures 2B and 4C). Similarly, a total of 36 genes were downregulated across all treatments, when the host is pretreated with antibiotics (Figure 2D). Of these shared genes in the antibiotic pretreatment condition, all 31 genes from anemones with the native community were shared with the antibiotic treated anemones (Figure 4D). The annotated genes included genes related to redox responses (cytochrome p450), immunity (TNF receptor 3) and several genes predicated to be involved in neural function and development (e.g., nude‐like 1, synaptotogimin).

Live Bacteria and CFS Modulate Gene Expression for Immune Pathways

Comparisons between conditions of both antibiotic treated and anemones that possess their native microbiome revealed significant differences in the expression of putative innate immune genes (Figure 5, all genes listed in Table S4). Pretreatment with antibiotics was a significant factor of the expression of these genes (p = 0.001), while the bacterial source products (LC, CFS, LPS) was significant (p = 0.001) as was the specific bacteria these products originated from (p = 0.001). Additionally, the interaction effect of the bacteria and their products was statistically significant (p = 0.001) as was the bacterial product and antibiotic interaction (p = 0.021). When N. vectensis retains its native microbiome before bacterial exposure, both MAVS and STING are significantly downregulated with the addition of LC P. spiralis when compared to the no treatment controls, while RLRb is significantly upregulated (Figure 5B). When only the CFS of P. spiralis was added to the anemones, IRF1, MAVS, RLRa, RLRb and STING had higher expression, but no change in expression with NF‐κB and TLR. Meanwhile, the LC additions of the other two bacteria, B. velezensis and V. diabolicus , did not result in significant gene expression differences. B. velezensis was the only bacterial CFS that resulted in significant downregulation of any gene (STING) when compared to the no treatment controls, while the remaining genes had minimal differential gene expression. With removal of the native microbial community, we observed a similar modulation of the RIG‐I‐like sensors (Figure 5B). RLRa and RLRb, STING, MAVs and IRF1 are all significantly upregulated when anemones were exposed to CFS from P. spiralis . RLRb was also upregulated when exposed to LC and CFS of B. velezensis . In contrast, STING was significantly downregulated when exposed to LC of B. velezensis . Expression of NF‐κB and TLR were not significantly different for any exposure and none of these immune genes were differentially expressed for LC for P. spiralis or V. diabolicus as well as CFS for V. diabolicus .

Discussion

In the environment, both bacteria and their secreted products (cell free supernatant) are present and can potentially interact with the host and other members of the microbial community. To parse the effects of these factors, we separated the CFS from the live bacterial cells to differentiate how a cnidarian species responds to each of these constituents. Bacterial products that were introduced to the host generated significantly different transcriptional responses than the live bacteria, both in number of genes and their predicted functions. This difference in the transcriptional response of the host illustrates the importance of assessing the overall transcriptional variation the host has against the different products and components of bacteria in species' specific ways. For example, Bacillus species have been shown to produce antimicrobial compounds that get secreted into the surrounding environment (Emam and Dunlap 2020). When Bacillus is supplemented in the diet of marine invertebrates, their growth rates increase (Jamali et al. 2015; Thurlow et al. 2019), and have stabilization effects on the existing microbiome, which may be due to antimicrobial compound synthesis (Gao et al. 2017), antioxidative properties (Giarma et al. 2017; Yi et al. 2018), or enrichment of other probiotic bacteria (Delgadillo‐Ordoñez et al. 2024). Additionally, the CFS of Pseudoalteromonas species has been shown to have antimicrobial effects against pathogenic bacteria (Hamid and Rosmadi 2020; Holmström and Kjelleberg 1999; Tang et al. 2020). CFS produced by Pseudoalteromonas may also have benefits for the host, including iron scavenging (Nichols et al. 2005) and inducing settlement in marine host organisms (Peng et al. 2020; Tebben et al. 2011). Vibrio and Pseudoalteromonas, while phylogenetically closely related, elicit different transcriptional responses in N. vectensis . Determining the effects of individual bacteria and their products is crucial to the health of the holobiont. The differences were particularly striking when comparing exposure of anemones to live cells or the secreted products. Our results suggest that differentiating these two sources of bacterial signalling on hosts may be important to compare when working to determine how hosts do or do not respond to microbes.

The microbiome of N. vectensis significantly impacts the physiology of this species and may be a buffer for the host against other microbial species (Baldassarre et al. 2021; Domin et al. 2023; Domin et al. 2018; Krueger et al. 2022; Mortzfeld et al. 2016). Research with other cnidarians has similarly shown that the native microbiome can also buffer colonizing bacteria and other pathogens. For example, established bacteria typically maintain higher concentrations in Hydra as compared to inoculated bacteria, even if they are the same bacterial species (Wein et al. 2018). When the native microbiome was removed in Aurelia aurita , survival, growth and reproduction are impacted, which is further diminished with the subsequent introduction of a pathogen (Weiland‐Bräuer et al. 2020). Furthermore, modifying the microbiome with probiotic organisms (e.g., Pseudoalteromonas) prior to exposure of the pathogen Vibrio coralliilyticus decreased temperature related bleaching in Pocillopora damicornis (Rosado et al. 2019). The presence of the microbiome that is already colonized may prevent new bacteria from adhering (Dang and Lovell 2015). Alternatively, the effects of the microbiome acting in response to the CFS may elicit a host immune response, which may disrupt the microbiome. The antagonism and potential disruption of the native microbiome may induce transcriptomic responses in the host (Stabili et al. 2018). In this study, we measured substantial differences in gene expression when N. vectensis was treated with antibiotics prior to introduction of bacteria or their secreted products. Thus, the microbial community appears to play a significant role in how this species responds to new colonists in a surprisingly species' specific manner, which adds an important component to consider when interpreting the impacts of bacteria on holobionts.

Lipopolysaccharides are commonly used for innate immune stimulation to simulate the effects of bacterial exposure, including for various marine invertebrates (Bisanti et al. 2024; Cheng et al. 2016; Chiaramonte et al. 2019; Fuess et al. 2016; Schmittmann et al. 2021; Seneca et al. 2020). LPS is a ligand for some Toll Like receptors in a variety of animals but have limited characterization in marine invertebrates (Parisi et al. 2020; Trapani et al. 2016). Indeed, former research has suggested that LPS may not stimulate an immune response in N. vectensis (Brennan et al. 2017). Here, we have demonstrated that LPS weakly impacts gene expression of N. vectensis , without significant differences in expression for TLR, IRF1, NF‐κB and the remaining genes involved, or hypothesized to be involved, in the innate immune system. We tested two concentrations, 5 ng and 5 μg mL−1, with similar modest effects on gene expression compared with the bacterial isolates. Removal of the native microbiome also did not strongly impact the response of N. vectensis to LPS. Together, these data suggest that LPS at these concentrations and for this duration of exposure is not an informative surrogate for inducing an immune response for N. vectensis . LPS from E. coli may not be recognized as a foreign molecule by N. vectensis receptors because E. coli is not a natural part of this species microbiome. Exposures with isolated LPS from naturally occurring bacteria or for different durations may elicit a response in N. vectensis . The addition of bacteria‐based chemical cues (e.g., LPS and CFS) to N. vectensis may result in the slow degradation of the signal over our selected 24‐h transcriptomic measurement of the host. In contrast, the addition of live bacteria may have the opposite effect, where the bacteria can multiply over the duration of the experiment. Interestingly, the method of (LC) exposure, for example, through solution or via uptake through the prey, has a significant effect on the concentration of these bacteria over time in N. vectensis (Krueger et al. 2022). Further research comparing how cnidarians respond to LPS and naturally associated bacteria will be informative for better characterization of the specificity and sensitivity of the cnidarian immune response.

Many genes have been identified to potentially function in the innate immune pathways of cnidarians (Parisi et al. 2020). Notably, some of the sensors that have been identified to potentially recognize bacterial ligands include Toll‐like receptors and RIG‐I like receptors (Lewandowska et al. 2021). In N. vectensis , we identified several genes to be differentially expressed when exposed to bacterial products, including STING, MAVS, NF‐κB, RLRa and b and IRF1. Interestingly, we did not observe differential expression of NF‐κB or TLR, even with the addition of live cells and LPS to the host. These differences may indicate that either bacterial detection does not significantly drive transcription of these two innate immune genes or the roles for these genes in immunity may occur post‐transcriptionally. Previous research has shown that flagellin and another Vibrio species, V. coralliilyticus , induce the transcription and translation of NF‐κB, which under longer incubation periods results in mortality of the host (Brennan et al. 2017). Although not directly comparable, the lack of induction of the NF‐κB‐TLR pathway in our experiments may reflect host specificity to specific bacterial ligands. Interestingly, the gram‐positive bacteria, B. velezensis , did not activate transcription of either gene, which would indicate that TLR from N. vectensis may not bind to peptidoglycan. Neither of the gram‐negative bacteria induced the expression of the TLR gene. Together these results may indicate that the RIG‐I like receptors together with STING and MAVS may be an important pathway for detection of bacteria in this cnidarian species.

Probiotic applications have recently highlighted the use of gram‐positive species, such as Bacillus (Emam and Dunlap 2020; Gao et al. 2017; Jamali et al. 2015; Soltani et al. 2019; Thurlow et al. 2019; Yi et al. 2018). Determining conditions that may impact how these introduced bacteria interact with the host organism are important to determine to better predict efficacy. Our results support a hypothesis that these responses could vary dramatically for the species introduced to the microbial community and the resident microbial community. For example, both B. velezensis LC and their CFS induce a large transcriptional response for N. vectensis , which may represent an immune response. This effect is strongly influenced by the presence or absence of the native microbiome. Thus, any positive effects of B. velezensis may be mitigated by the native microbiome, potentially due to direct competition for physical space (Domin et al. 2023, 2018; Franzenburg et al. 2013; Verschuere et al. 1999; Wein et al. 2018). Another approach to combating bacterial pathogens is to use bacteria with antagonistic mechanisms to directly combat other bacterial cells. Pseudoalteromonas spp. have been shown to be antagonistic against a variety of bacteria (Delgadillo‐Ordoñez et al. 2024; Hamid and Rosmadi 2020; Holmström and Kjelleberg 1999; Tang et al. 2020). This principle is supported in our findings where we identified large transcriptomic responses in the host with the addition of P. spiralis only while the native microbiome was present. This effect was largely reduced when the native microbiome was reduced prior to the inoculation of P. spiralis . We found V. diabolicus to have the least change in the expression of the host organism for both the LC and the CFS regardless of the presence of the native microbiome of the host. While other members of the Vibrio genus have been characterized to induce mortality in cnidarians, for example, Vibrio coralliilyticus (Brennan et al. 2017), though Vibrio diabolicus NVE‐VD1 appears to be largely commensal, potentially due to the lack of a Type VI Secretion System (Krueger and Reitzel 2024).

Together this research supports a hypothesis that the transcriptional response of Nematostella vectensis to microbes and their secreted products is complex with unique gene expression to each bacterial species. These results suggest that the immune system of cnidarians can differentiate between each bacterium, which may explain the variation in microbial communities between life stages and individuals. Furthermore, the response of the anemone to each inoculation was strongly influenced by the presence of the native microbial community. We suggest future research aimed at introducing particular bacterial strains to cnidarian hosts, for probiotics or otherwise, may benefit from also assessing the native microbial community of the host individual when interpreting the impacts of these approaches.
