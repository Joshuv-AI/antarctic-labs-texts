To What Extent Do Different Criteria Influence 3‐Month Fusion Evaluation in Anterior Cervical Arthrodesis Trials?
Authors: Minghe Yao, Siyu Zheng, Zhijun Li, Tingkui Wu, Kangkang Huang, Shihao Chen, Xiaoqiang Zhao, Yi Deng, Hao Liu, Beiyu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesMultiple imaging criteria are available for assessing fusion following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF). In clinical trials, the 3‐month postoperative follow‐up serves as a critical timepoint for evaluating the efficacy of interventions on accelerating the fusion process. This study aims to determine how applying different fusion criteria influences the conclusions of a comparative analysis.MethodsPatients aged 18 or older who underwent ACDF with allograft or beta‐tricalcium phosphate artificial bone between C3 and C7 were reviewed from 1 April 2023 to 30 September 2023. Fusion rates between the two grafts at three‐month follow‐up were compared under different criteria. Fusion status was judged by CT or dynamic radiographs, or their combinations. Cut‐offs of dynamic indicators included angle changes of 4°, 3°, and 2°, and interspinous motion of 3, 2, and 1 mm. Criteria were applied singly, combined in pairs, or combined in groups of three, leading to a total of 31 criteria. Student's t‐test and Chi‐squared test were employed, and Cohen's kappa coefficient and phi coefficient were calculated.ResultsNinety‐eight segments were included. Twenty‐five criteria yielded higher fusion rates for artificial bone, with 7 out of 25 reaching statistical significance (p < 0.05). The remaining six criteria led to a reversed result, but none reached significance (p > 0.05). The agreement and correlation between CT and dynamic criteria were poor (kappa and phi < 0.200). In contrast, the agreement and correlation between two dynamic indicators were better, and even being close to moderate (kappa = 0.398, phi = 0.398) between 3° and 2 mm.ConclusionChanges in fusion criteria affected result significance but did not produce conflicting conclusions. There was a significant disagreement between the results under CT and dynamic radiographs criteria. Thresholds of 3° or 2 mm can be optimal choices for dynamic criteria.Keywords: anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, computed tomography, dynamic radiographs, early follow‐up, fusion criteria

Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a classic procedure for the surgical treatment of refractory cervical spondylosis [1]. Fusion, as the ultimate goal of ACDF, can currently be achieved in approximately 90% of patients by 2 years postoperatively under existing techniques, while this rate remains only around 50% at the 3‐month postoperative mark [2]. Consequently, enhancing earlier fusion attainment has become a key focus in current advancements of ACDF implant design, bone graft materials, and surgical techniques, as this could reduce the need for frequent follow‐ups and radiation exposure while accelerating patients' complete recovery and return to normal life [3]. In clinical studies, the 3‐month follow‐up typically represents either the first or second earliest assessment timepoint, making the fusion rate at this stage a critical indicator for evaluating the efficacy of the investigated interventions to promote fusion [4, 5]. However, the reliability and comparability of these study conclusions are limited by the varying fusion criteria employed across studies. For example, the reported 3‐month fusion rates show significant discrepancies between studies assessing by CT versus dynamic flexion‐extension radiographs [2].

The fusion criteria in ACDF have been a long‐discussed issue [6]. Numerous criteria by imaging have been proposed as alternative indicators, which commonly lean upon computed tomography (CT) and dynamic X‐ray radiographs. Each of these indicators has several common thresholds [7], spanning a wide stringency range from strict to undemanding. Clinical studies usually report fusion rates based on only one criterion, which has an unknown effect on their conclusions. For instance, in controlled trials, alterations in criteria may disproportionately affect treatment and control groups, potentially leading to a change in study conclusions.

The two‐group comparative study is a typical design for fusion rate comparison between two interventions [8]. Our purposes were: (i) to compare fusion rates under different criteria and their combinations at the 3‐month follow‐up; (ii) to investigate the influence of applying different criteria on the outcomes and conclusions of two‐group comparative studies; and (iii) to analyze the relationships among fusion outcomes classified by various criteria in order to provide insight into the consistency of fusion assessment methods. This study provides a systematic comparison of multiple fusion criteria, highlights the impact of metric selection on study conclusions, and offers guidance for future research in fusion judgment evaluation.

Methods

Study Population and Grouping

The study was approved by the institution's ethics committee (2023 No. 1602). Patients with fusion segments between C3 and C7 were included. Patients were excluded if their 3‐month follow‐up imaging lacked CT or plain radiographs, or if the imaging, though available, did not clearly demonstrate the operated segments. Dynamic radiographs with interspinous motion less than 4 mm in a non‐arthrodesed superjacent segment were also excluded due to inadequate motion [9, 10].

All the surgeries used the same zero‐profile device. Bone graft materials (allograft bone or beta‐tricalcium phosphate (β‐TCP) artificial bone) naturally divided the patients into two groups.

Radiographic Assessment

Dynamic lateral radiographs and CT were used for fusion assessment. The indicators were as follows (Figure 1). (1) Bone bridge formation (CT): Fusion was defined as a continuous bone bridge formation outside the cage either posteriorly or laterally, assessed on all three coronal, sagittal and axial CT planes with fixed axes. Due to the presence of the metal plate barrier and metal artifact, assessment of the bone bridge was not feasible inside or anterior to the cage. (2) Angle change (dynamic radiographs): The surgical segment Cobb angle change between flexion and extension was measured [11]. Cobb angle was determined by the upper endplate of the superior vertebra and the lower endplate of the inferior vertebra. Thresholds for fusion were set as 4°, 3°, or 2°. (3) Interspinous motion (ISM) (dynamic radiographs): The distance difference was measured between the spinous process tips of two vertebrae [9]. Cut‐offs were defined as 3, 2, or 1 mm. The cut‐offs were selected within the range of commonly reported thresholds in literature [2, 12].

Demonstration of dynamic indicators measurement. For each operated segment, Cobb angle (a) was determined by the superior endplate of the superior vertebrae and the inferior endplate of the inferior vertebrae, and interspinous distance (b) was measured by the straight‐line distance between spinous tips.

Combined criteria included combinations of two or three indicators (Table 1). When using combinations, fusion required all criteria included to be satisfied. For example, when using 2 mm combined with 2° as the criterion, only an ISM of less than 2 mm and, at the same time, a Cobb angle change less than 2° would be judged as fusion.

Measurements were performed using Image J (1.52version, NIH). Plain films were measured at a magnification of at least 150% [9]. Measurements were performed independently by two co‐trained evaluators who were not involved in the diagnosis, treatment, or follow‐up of the included patients. Anonymized images were assessed in a randomized sequence. The measurements were conducted twice by a 3‐week interval. Results from two measurements were averaged for dynamic indicators. A consensus of the CT result was reached after discussion with another spine surgeon.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was determined by the kappa value between CT and dynamic radiographs, using PASS (21.0.3 version, NCSS LLC). Preliminary results showed a value of no higher than 0.15 and a fusion rate of 40% (by CT criteria). Alpha was set at 0.05 with beta at 0.20. The kappa under the one‐sided null hypothesis was set at 0.4. According to the calculation, a sample size of at least 91segments was required.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were summarized as mean average and standard deviation (SD) or median with quantiles. Nominal data were presented as quantities and proportions. Comparison of continuous data was analyzed by Student's t‐test, with categorical data by Chi‐squared test and Fisher exact test. To evaluate the agreement and correlation of the results, we used Cohen's kappa coefficient and phi coefficient. Kappa value ranges from ≤ 0 (no agreement) to 1 (complete agreement), while phi ranges from −1 (strong negative correlation) to 1 (strong positive correlation). Inter‐ and intra‐observer reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Kappa coefficient (95% confidence interval CI). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (22.0 version, IBM SPSS Inc). Two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Demographic and Surgical Data

We reviewed patients admitted from 1 April 2023 to 30 September 2023. After excluding seven patients due to incomplete imaging data, a total of 98 segments from 59 patients were included with a mean (SD) age of 53.2 ± 8.2 years and mean (SD) follow‐up of 97.6 ± 3.2 days. Twenty‐six patients with 46 segments were in the allograft group, leaving 33 patients with 52 segments in the β‐TCP group. The demographic and surgical characteristics were comparable between the two groups (Table 2).

Inter‐ and Intra‐Observer Reliability

The intra‐observer reliabilities by ICC were 0.808 and 0.869 for Cobb angle, and 0.824 and 0.925 for interspinous distance (p < 0.001 for all). The intra‐observer kappa values for CT were 0.911 (95% CI 0.827–0.995) and 0.938 (95% CI 0.869–1.000). The inter‐reliabilities were 0.838 and 0.903 for angle and distance (95% CI 0.751–0.894 and 0.856–0.935) with a CT kappa value of 0.739 (95% CI 0.617–0.866).

Overall Fusion Rates

The overall segment fusion rate varied according to the criteria applied (Figure 2). For single indicator criteria, the fusion rate ranged from 19.4% (by interspinous distance threshold 1 mm) to 71.4% (by 3 mm). The CT bone bridge criteria found a rate of 37.8%. A combination of two indicators led to a lower fusion rate. Of the nine criteria pairs, CT and 1 mm yielded the minimum value of 6.1%, and the combination of 3 mm and Cobb angle 4° predicted the maximum value of 53.1%. The overall fusion rate declined further when using the combination criterion for all three indicators, with no combination yielding more than 20% but the combination of CT, 3 mm, and 4° which deemed a fusion rate of 24.5%. The most stringent criterion, i.e., CT, 1 mm and 2°, led to a rate of only 2.0% (Table S1).

Allograft Versus β‐TCP

As the criteria changed, the fusion rates of the two groups changed accordingly. Of the results by seven single indicator criteria, with the exception of 1 mm, which resulted in a higher fusion rate for allograft than β‐TCP, all the other criteria led to a superior result for β‐TCP. Two of the seven criteria reached statistical significance for the difference, namely 3 mm (p = 0.002), and 2° (p = 0.048) (Figure 2A). Relatedly, three of the four pairs of combined criteria involving 1 mm all concluded that allograft was superior, although none of the four reached significance. The other 11 paired combinations all showed β‐TCP to be superior to allograft, of which three reached significance: 3 mm and 4° (p = 0.009), CT and 4° (p = 0.010), and CT and 2° (p = 0.014) (Figure 2B). Under the nine combinations of all the three indicators, all yielded superior results for β‐TCP but two combinations, which were CT, 1 mm and 4° or 3°. Two reached significance: CT, 3 mm and 4° (p = 0.045), and CT, 3 mm and 2° (p = 0.043) (Figure 3C).

CT radiolucency with dynamic stability. At the caudal level, CT reconstruction was checked and radiolucency between endplates was confirmed on reconstructed slices with 0.4 mm thickness. However, dynamic radiographs showed a subtle motion of 0.9° and 0.1 mm.

Agreement and Correlation Between Indicators

The seven single criteria could be categorized into two groups, namely CT and dynamic radiographs. Tests of agreement between CT and six dynamic criteria were performed, and no kappa coefficients were greater than 0.20, implying that the agreement was not greater than slight. Within the dynamic criteria, a test of agreement between the angle and distance criteria was performed, and five of the nine tests achieved a kappa coefficient greater than 0.20 and a statistically significant difference from 0, implying a better agreement between them than that with CT (Table 3). Among all the angle and distance criteria, the best agreement is observed between 3° and 2 mm (kappa = 0.398), which approaches 0.4, indicating moderate agreement.

Discussion

The study found that substantial variation in fusion rates was observed depending on the specific criteria applied, underscoring the lack of standardization in assessment methods. The outcomes and statistical significance of comparative analyses between the two intervention groups were markedly influenced by the choice of fusion criteria, in some cases leading to conflicting conclusions regarding superiority. Among all thresholds evaluated, the pair of 2 mm and 3° for ISM demonstrated consistency.

Fusion Criteria and Conclusion Consistency

The influence of fusion criteria on reported fusion rates is definitely not “a novel concept”, which is widely recognized among spine surgeons [11, 13]. However, we contend that the key issue lies in the extent to which it undermines the credibility of study findings—a question that, to our knowledge, has not been thoroughly elucidated before. For instance, Hipp et al. [13] have demonstrated how varying angular thresholds affect pseudarthrosis rates: on 1‐year postoperative dynamic radiographs, a 4°intervertebral motion threshold yielded a 6% rate, whereas a 1° threshold produced a 44%. While such studies highlight these discrepancies, a deeper concern is that this bias undoubtedly affects both experimental and control groups. The critical question is: Is this impact equivalent across groups? Concerns may arise that the choice of criteria and thresholds could alter the conclusion of comparison. Assume that in one study, the fusion rate is significantly higher for drug A than for drug B when using 2 mm as the cut‐off point, but when using 3 mm as the cut‐off, significantly higher for drug B. If this occurs, the conclusion would largely depend on the selection of criteria, which is often subjective based on the researcher's perspective.

Therefore, our study systematically incorporated all commonly used thresholds and their potential combinations to comprehensively simulate outcomes under diverse evaluation criteria, thereby investigating the aforementioned hypothetical scenario. The results demonstrated that while statistical significance varied across different criteria, the collective evidence consistently supported the conclusion thatβ‐TCP was non‐inferior to allograft in fusion rate. Therefore, the current fusion judgment methodology can at least lead to a conclusion without potential self‐contradiction. However, given the divergence, we recommend using at least two criteria or thresholds separately, and parallely reporting both fusion rates to enhance credibility.

The Discrepancy of “Fusion” Under Different Criteria at 3‐Month Follow‐Up

Criteria frequently in use include small ISM distance [14] or Cobb angle [15] changes on dynamic radiographs, and the formation of a bony bridge or the absence of a translucent zone between the implant‐bone interface on plain radiographs [16] or CT [17]. These criteria seemed to be based on the notion that there should be as little relative motion as possible between the two vertebrae in the fused segment, and the formation of an intact and continuous bony connection should be recognized to account for this stability. However, in addition to recurring questioning on this notion itself (e.g., a certain range of motion could be reserved even with a bone bridge [18, 19, 20]), its applicability may further be challenged in the early postoperative period. In our study, although fusion rates were similar between CT and dynamic plain radiographs under certain thresholds, agreement and correlation analyses showed that the meanings of “fusion” by the two were largely not the same. A systematic review by Rhee et al. also reported the poor dynamic radiographs‐CT agreement at 3‐month follow‐up with kappa values of 0.12 and 0.35 [21], calculated from the raw data of two included studies [3, 22].

The divergence could be due to a short postoperative time. For patients in the late postoperative stages, when sufficient time has been given for bone growth and reconstruction, the fusion status may fall into two distinct categories, which are those who have completed union and achieved stability (fusion), and those who have failed in fusion and have not been correspondingly stabilized (nonunion or pseudoarthrosis). In contrast, vertebra bone in the early postoperative period has experienced a relatively short time. Thus, in some patients, stability may continue to be provided primarily by implant devices, and bone bridge formation is not necessary for stability during this period (“false positive” fusion on dynamic plain) (Figure 3). On the other hand, early postoperative CT may overestimate the fusion rate [3]. Subsidence is a common phenomenon for intervertebral fusion device [23]. It can result in close proximity of the upper and lower endplates, which, together with partial bone growth and volume averaging effect on CT, creates an illusion of bone bridge connection (“false positive” fusion on CT) (Figure 4). These hypotheses explain the inconsistency between CT and flexion‐extension films early after surgery. Therefore, a composite criterion combining them both may be more specific than a single criterion in identifying true bony fusion.

Cobb angle and interspinous distance should ideally have achieved better agreement since they are both dynamic indicators and measure the same thing—intervertebral motion. However, in our results, an ideal agreement was not reached among all pairs. It should be noted that the current method of motion measurement is not accurate enough, and the error could exceed the cut‐off point, rendering the results unreliable. The reasons for this include the plain film parallax effect, changes in patient position, or the limited ability of reviewers to accurately measure [24, 25]. We would recommend using 3° and 2 mm as better thresholds, as they showed good agreement as dynamic criteria should. A similar recommendation of 3° and 2 mm, but in combination, was also made by a recent post hoc analysis from a prospective multicenter randomized trial with a 5‐year follow‐up [26].

Evidence regarding the accuracy of the criteria is being gradually gained from pseudarthrosis revision surgery [9, 10, 20], where the gold standard is available. Although this evidence has provided reliable recommendations, it may not fully resolve the debate in early follow‐up. As stated above, the relationship between the implant and vertebrae is less clear within 3 months compared to later periods. This lack of clarity could manifest in imaging and produce a different distribution of observed values from that observed just before revision, thereby reducing diagnostic effectiveness. Studies have shown an increase over time in the percent agreement between different criteria [2, 3, 22], but further reports were still needed for this inference.

Limitations and Strengths

Our study results should be interpreted considering the following limitations. Aiming at evaluating the most common criteria for fusion, some newly proposed criteria on CT were not included [12]. The zero‐profile device we used had more metal artifacts (due to screws passing through the endplate) than the plate‐cage construct, which limited our ability to decipher fusion on the graft interface. We included only one type of zero‐profile fusion device in this study. Future research should incorporate a broader variety of implants, including plate and screw constructs. Moreover, the parallax effect in dynamic radiographs could also confound the result. Pinter et al. [24] reported that angular and distance variations that may occur during image capture may erroneously cause ISM to exceed the 1 mm threshold. A primary recommendation of 3° and 2 mm was made, while further cross‐institutional studies are needed to validate it and the calculating methodology.

This study offered several key strengths. It provided a systematic comparison of multiple fusion criteria and their combinations, quantitatively demonstrating the substantial impact of metric selection on both fusion rates and statistical outcomes. Furthermore, it identified the specific criterion pair (2 mm and 3°) with the strongest consistency, delivering support for standardizing assessment methods in future research.

Conclusion

Under the current methodology for detecting fusion at 3 months after ACDF, changes in indicators and thresholds altered the statistical significance of the results, but did not lead to opposed conclusions. There is a divergence in the meaning of “fusion” between CT and dynamic radiographs criteria. Among dynamic thresholds, a difference of less than 3° for Cobb angle or 2 mm for interspinous distance was recommended for better agreement between them.
