CSF microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3A and 3B levels are associated with tau pathology and Alzheimer’s disease risk through amyloid deposition and microglial signaling
Authors: Hai-Hua Guo, Dan Su, Bo Song, Zhi Geng, Lu Wang, Wen Wang, Wenjie Hu, Xiaoxue Li, Weiqiang Li, Gu Zhang, Miao Fang, Yulong Dai, Panpan Hu, Xingqi Wu, Kai Wang, Ling Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutophagy has recently attracted increasing attention for its critical involvement in Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) pathogenesis. However, the role of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 A (LC3A) and B (LC3B), key autophagic-associated proteins in cognitive decline, AD risk, and underlying pathology remain unclear.MethodsThis study included 568 participants from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) measurements of LC3A and LC3B. We systematically assessed the associations of LC3A and LC3B with cognition, dementia risk, AD biomarkers, microglial activity, brain volume, and metabolism through both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. Additionally, subgroup analyses based on ATN classification were conducted to evaluate stage-specific effects, and Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were employed to explore the clinical predictive value of LC3A and LC3B. Finally, mediation models were used to investigate the potential mechanisms by which LC3A and LC3B affect AD pathology and cognition.ResultsElevated CSF LC3A and LC3B levels were negatively associated with cognition, while positively associated with amyloid deposition, total tau (t-tau), phosphorylated tau (p-tau), progranulin (PGRN), and soluble Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid Cells-2 (sTREM2) at baseline. Higher CSF LC3A and LC3B concentrations were linked to an increased risk of dementia, accelerated longitudinal cognitive decline, greater brain atrophy, faster accumulation of t-tau, and longitudinal associations with sTREM2. Individuals with TN+ exhibited significantly higher CSF LC3A and LC3B levels compared to those with TN−. Moreover, CSF LC3A alone could predict TN−/TN+ status with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.903, which increased to 0.939 when combined with amyloid PET. Similarly, CSF LC3B alone yielded an AUC of 0.876, rising to 0.913 when combined with amyloid PET. Finally, amyloid deposition and microglial activation individually or jointly, mediated the effects of LC3A and LC3B on tau pathology and cognitive decline.ConclusionsCSF LC3A and LC3B, reflecting autophagic activation, are linked to elevated tau pathology and an increased risk of AD. They may jointly contribute to AD pathological progression alongside amyloid deposition and microglial activation. LC3A and LC3B as robust candidate biomarkers for monitoring pathological burden, stratifying disease risk, and informing clinical evaluation in AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03758-7.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Autophagy, Amyloid beta-Peptides, Microglia, Tau Proteins

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) represents a major challenge to aging populations and is characterized by progressive cognitive decline [1]. In the United States, an estimated 6.9 million adults aged ≥ 65 years currently live with AD, a number projected to reach 13.8 million by 2060, with care costs expected to exceed $360 billion by 2024 [2, 3]. Delaying disease progression remains a principal clinical objective.

Autophagy has recently attracted increasing attention for its critical involvement in AD pathogenesis [4, 5]. It is an evolutionarily conserved, dynamic process that degrades cytoplasmic proteins and selectively clears damaged organelles, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis, particularly in neurodegenerative conditions [6, 7]. Autophagy involves the sequestration of cytoplasmic components into autophagosomes, which fuse with lysosomes for degradation and recycling. Microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) is a core regulator of this process. Upon autophagy activation, cytosolic LC3-I is lipidated to form membrane-bound LC3-II, a widely used marker of autophagic activity [8, 9]. However, LC3-II is not detectable in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) due to its membrane-bound nature, whereas soluble LC3-I can be reliably quantified and has emerged as a practical surrogate of autophagic stress. The LC3 family mainly includes LC3A and LC3B. LC3B is ubiquitously expressed and regulates neuronal autophagy, axonal transport, and protein aggregate clearance [10, 11], while CSF LC3A likely reflects contributions from multiple central nervous system cell types, including neurons, astrocytes, and microglia [12]. Both LC3A and LC3B in CSF represent soluble forms of LC3-I and may indicate autophagic stress or autophagic response. Although experimental and postmortem studies have implicated LC3A and LC3B in AD-related autophagic dysfunction and cognitive impairment [13–15], their associations with cognition and AD risk have not been systematically examined in large human cohorts.

The hallmark neuropathology of AD, extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau, drive synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss [16]. Advances in positron emission tomography (PET) tracers for amyloid and tau, along with CSF biomarkers, have markedly improved the early diagnosis and monitoring of AD [17–19]. The canonical cascade posits that Aβ deposition triggers tau pathology, leading to synaptic failure, neurodegeneration, and cognitive decline. However, the presence of Aβ pathology does not uniformly result in AD, implying the involvement of modulatory processes. Emerging evidence suggests that macroautophagy is activated early, preceding Aβ plaque formation, with autophagic vesicles accumulating amyloid precursor protein and γ-secretase, thereby facilitating intracellular Aβ generation [20]. Initially, autophagy is upregulated as a compensatory response, but progressive impairment of autophagic flux leads to vesicular accumulation and toxic protein aggregates that exacerbate neuronal injury [21, 22]. Autophagy also contributes to tau degradation, highlighting the autophagy–lysosomal pathway as a potential therapeutic target [23]. Translating these mechanistic insights to humans requires elucidating how autophagy modulates Aβ and tau dynamics in vivo and whether autophagy markers can predict AD progression. However, integrative human studies combining autophagy markers with molecular, neuroimaging, and longitudinal cognitive data remain scarce, underscoring a critical translational gap.

This study aimed to examine, in a non-demented population: 1) associations of CSF autophagy markers LC3A and LC3B with cognitive decline and incident dementia; 2) their associations and predictive performance for AD pathology, microglial function, and neuroimaging measures; and 3) potential mechanisms linking CSF autophagy markers to AD pathology, microglial activation, and cognitive deterioration.

Methods

ADNI study design

Data were obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database (adni.loni.usc.edu). Initiated in 2003, ADNI aims to determine whether neuroimaging and fluid biomarkers can track AD progression [24]. All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, with written informed consent obtained from all participants and approval from institutional review boards at each site.

Participants

Of the 708 participants with baseline CSF LC3A or LC3B measurements, 137 with baseline dementia were excluded to minimize reverse causation [25], along with two participants with missing data and one outlier for each marker, yielding a final sample of 568 non-demented individuals. All excluded participants with AD met the diagnostic criteria established by the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) [26]. Non-demented participants were categorized based on cognitive status as cognitively normal (CN) or having mild cognitive impairment (MCI). CN participants were defined by a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≥ 24 and a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0. MCI participants met the following criteria: MMSE ≥ 24, CDR = 0.5, preserved activities of daily living, absence of dementia, and performance at least one standard deviation below the CN mean on the Logical Memory II subtest of the Wechsler Memory Scale [27]. Demographic characteristics, neuropsychological assessments, CSF AD biomarkers, microglial markers, structural MRI measures, and PET data were collected. The earliest assessment for each participant was defined as baseline, with all subsequent evaluations considered follow-up visits. Follow-up required at least one post-baseline assessment. In the ADNI cohort, evaluations are generally conducted every 6–12 months, with some participants followed for up to 10 years. Accordingly, all longitudinal analyses were performed with data from every available visit, not restricted to two time-points. CSF LC3A and LC3B proteomics measurements

CSF concentrations of LC3A (aptamer ID: X17781.191) and LC3B (aptamer ID: X11608.5) were quantified using the SomaScan 7 K platform (v4.1), which targets approximately 7,000 proteins. Detailed procedures for SomaScan measurements in the ADNI cohort have been described previously [29, 30]. All samples were processed at SomaLogic in a single batch to minimize technical variability and were randomly distributed across assay plates to reduce potential plate effects [31]. Quality control and normalization were conducted following SomaLogic’s standard protocols. Aptamers were excluded if their maximum deviation within a plate exceeded 0.5 of the plate median scale factor or if the cross-plate median coefficient of variation (CV) exceeded 0.15. After log₁₀ transformation, values exceeding 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR) were set as missing. Samples or aptamers with identification rates below 85% were removed from the analysis. The remaining relative fluorescence unit values for LC3A and LC3B were used in all subsequent statistical analyses.

Cognition assessments

All participants underwent comprehensive cognitive assessments at baseline and follow-up. Global cognition was evaluated using the MMSE, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDRSB), and Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale 13 (ADAS13). Domain-specific performance was evaluated with harmonized phenotypes from the Alzheimer’s Disease Sequencing Project, covering language (PHC_LAN), memory (PHC_MEM), executive function (PHC_EXF), and visuospatial (PHC_VSP) domains. Higher scores on MMSE, MoCA, PHC_EXF, PHC_MEM, PHC_LAN, and PHC_VSP indicate better cognition, whereas elevated scores on ADAS13 and CDRSB denote worse cognitive performance [32].

CSF PGRN, sTREM2 and AD biomarkers measurements

CSF collection procedures in the ADNI cohort have been described previously [33]. progranulin (PGRN) and soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (sTREM2) concentrations were quantified using Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) electrochemiluminescence immunoassays (ECLIA) [34, 35]. Briefly, streptavidin-coated 96-well plates were blocked overnight and sequentially incubated with biotinylated capture antibodies, samples or standards, primary antibodies, and SULFO-TAG-labeled secondary antibodies. Signals were measured at 620 nm using an MSD Sector Imager 2400. Samples were randomly distributed across plates and assayed in duplicate. To reduce inter-batch variability, all reagents and plates were from the same lot. Mean intraplate CVs were 2.2% for PGRN and 3.1% for sTREM2, with all duplicate CVs below 15%. Inter-batch–corrected values were used for all subsequent analyses.

CSF Aβ42, phosphorylated tau (p-tau), and total tau (t-tau) were measured using ECLIAs on the fully automated Elecsys cobas e601 system [36]. A single reagent lot was used for each biomarker. Sample normalization and quality control followed ADNI protocols, ensuring inter-plate precision with CVs < 15% [37].

FDG, amyloid, and tau-PET imaging measurements

All PET scans were acquired in accordance with the standardized ADNI protocols and underwent rigorous preprocessing and quality control procedures. For the [¹⁸F]fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG-PET) scans, subjects received an intravenous injection of approximately 4.5–5.5 mCi (~ 167–204 MBq) of tracer following a fasting period (typically ≥ 6 h) and serum glucose < 120 mg/dL. Scanning commenced 30 min post-injection and lasted 30 min (commonly 6 × 5-min frames which are motion-corrected, averaged and spatially normalized) [38]. Images were co-registered to the contemporaneous structural MRI, resampled to a common voxel grid (e.g., 160 × 160 × 96 with 1.5 mm³ voxels), smoothed to a uniform resolution (e.g., 8 mm FWHM or as harmonized to 6 mm FWHM). Regional standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was computed using a designated reference region (e.g., whole cerebellum) and a published metaROI in cortex [39].

For amyloid-PET scans, referred to as AV45, either [¹⁸F]florbetapir (FBP) or [¹⁸F]florbetaben (FBB) tracers were used. For FBP, images were acquired 50 min post-injection; for FBB, 90 min post-injection. Cortical SUVRs were calculated using the whole cerebellum as reference and subsequently converted to Centiloid units to allow harmonization across tracer types. For tau-PET imaging using [¹⁸F]flortaucipir (FTP/AV-1451), approximately 10 mCi (~ 370 MBq) of tracer was administered and data were acquired 75 min post-injection for 30 min (typically six 5-min frames). SUVRs were computed using the inferior cerebellar grey matter as the reference region, in line with the UC Berkeley pipeline [40, 41]. All scans included a low-dose CT for attenuation correction, and PET SUVRs were harmonized across sites and modalities to reduce scanner- and protocol-related variability [42].

AT(N) classification

According to the NIA-AA criteria [26], abnormal CSF and PET biomarkers of AD were defined as follows. A + was defined as CSF Aβ₄₂ < 976.6 pg/mL or amyloid-PET positivity (SUVR > 1.32 for FBP, > 1.08 for FBB) [43, 44]. T + was defined as CSF p-tau > 21.8 pg/mL. N + was defined as CSF t-tau > 245 pg/mL. Because T and N biomarkers were highly correlated, they were combined into a composite TN measure. TN negativity (TN–) required both T– and N– markers to be within normal limits. Participants were classified into four stages: Stage 0 (A–TN–), Stage 1 (A + TN–), Stage 2 (A + TN+), and suspected non-Alzheimer’s pathophysiology (SNAP, A–TN+) [26]. Detailed ATN classifications are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Regional brain volumetry

Structural MRI scans were acquired using 1.5T scanners following standardized ADNI protocols. T1-weighted images underwent automated processing with FreeSurfer (surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) including motion correction, intensity non-uniformity correction, and cortical/subcortical segmentation, followed by manual quality control consistent with ADNI procedures [45]. Volumetric analyses targeted AD relevant regions such as the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, fusiform gyrus, middle temporal lobe, whole brain, and lateral ventricles. Regional volumes were normalized to intracranial volume (ICV) to adjust for head‑size variability [46, 47]. Additionally, scans failing quality control (e.g., motion artefacts, incomplete coverage) were excluded from further analyses (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/data-samples/adni-data/neuroimaging/mri/mri-quality-control/).

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables that deviated from normality were transformed using the Box-Cox method (R package car) and subsequently standardized to z-scores. Values exceeding 3 standard deviations (SD) from the mean were excluded as outliers (1 data point each in LC3A and LC3B [48]. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group differences between CN and MCI participants were evaluated using two-sample t-tests or chi-square tests, as appropriate.

All analyses were adjusted for age, sex, education, and APOE ε4 carrier status. Analyses involving structural MRI additionally adjusted for intracranial volume using a residual approach, whereby regional volumes were regressed on intracranial volume and the resulting residuals were used in subsequent analyses. Tau-PET analyses were further adjusted for the interval between PET acquisition and baseline assessment. Cross-sectional associations of CSF LC3A and LC3B with cognitive performance, CSF PGRN, sTREM2, AD biomarkers, PET measures, and regional brain volumes were examined using multiple linear regression models. Longitudinal changes were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and slopes to account for within-subject variability over time. Dementia-free survival was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests (two-tailed P < .05). Associations between baseline LC3A and LC3B levels and incident dementia were further examined using Cox proportional hazards models in two analyses conducted exclusively among participants free of dementia at baseline: one including all participants and another excluding those who developed dementia within the first 2 years, to minimize potential reverse causation. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals (global test P > .05). We used leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) to evaluate the discriminative performance of LC3A and LC3B, alone or combined with other variables, for distinguishing TN − vs. TN + and A − vs. A+ within the ATN framework. Given the sample size (n = 314), LOOCV provides a favorable bias–variance trade-off and dense coverage of the prediction space, yielding stable estimates of ROC area [49]. In each iteration, models were trained on all but one participant and used to predict the held-out individual. Cross-validated predicted probabilities were used to construct ROC curves and compute AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy, with AUCs compared using the DeLong test [50]. Precision–recall (PR) curves were additionally generated as a secondary validation to account for class imbalance, with average precision (AP) reported as the summary measure.

To investigate potential mediating effects of LC3A and LC3B on the relationship between AD pathology and cognition, mediation analysis was applied. Mediation pathways included CSF LC3A and LC3B → amyloid deposition → p-tau and t-tau → cognition, and LC3A and LC3B → sTREM2 → p-tau and t-tau → cognition. Subject-specific slopes representing annual rates of cognitive change were derived from linear models, and similar mediation analysis analyses were conducted for longitudinal outcomes. A two-tailed P < .05 was considered statistically significant, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied and results reported as q-values (q < 0.05). All analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.0) using the car, lme4, survival, survminer, pROC, PRROC, and lavaan packages.

Results

Demographical and clinical characteristics

Table 1 presents the demographic, clinical, biomarker, and neuroimaging characteristics of the study cohort, which included 568 participants: 166 with CN and 402 with MCI. The mean age was 72.91 ± 7.18 years, 43.7% were female, and the average years of education was 16.05 ± 2.83. Overall, 44.1% of participants were APOE ε4 carriers. Compared with CN participants, individuals with MCI were younger, exhibited a higher prevalence of APOE ε4 carriage, and showed elevated CSF LC3A, p-tau, and t-tau levels, but lower CSF Aβ42. PET imaging revealed lower FDG uptake and greater cortical deposition of amyloid and tau in the MCI group. Cognitively, participants with MCI demonstrated poorer performance across both global measures (ADAS13, CDRSB, MMSE, MoCA) and domain-specific assessments (PHC_EXF, PHC_LAN, PHC_MEM, PHC_VSP). Structurally, MCI participants showed marked atrophy in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, accompanied by enlarged ventricular volumes. No significant group differences were observed in sex, education, CSF LC3B, PGRN, sTREM2, or the volumes of the fusiform gyrus, middle temporal lobe, and whole brain. The median follow-up duration for the cohort was 3.09 years (IQR: 1.96–6.38). The MCI group had a shorter follow-up period (median: 2.99 years; IQR: 1.08–5.01) compared with CN participants (median: 5.76 years; IQR: 3.01–9.51).

CSF LC3A and LC3B associations with cognitive function in cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses

Cross-sectional analyses revealed higher CSF LC3A levels associated significantly with worse cognitive performance across multiple domains, including ADAS13 (β = 0.162, q < 0.001), CDRSB (β = 0.104, q = 0.034), MMSE (β = -0.132, q = 0.005), PHC_LAN (β = -0.085, q = 0.034), and PHC_MEM (β = -0.158, q < 0.001). Similarly, elevated CSF LC3B levels were negatively associated with PHC_MEM (β = -0.104, q = 0.018) (Fig. 2A-F and Supplementary Table 2).

Longitudinal analyses demonstrated that higher CSF LC3A predicted accelerated cognitive decline across both global and domain-specific measures, including ADAS13 (β = 0.050, q < 0.001), CDRSB (β = 0.067, q < 0.001), MMSE (β = -0.072, q < 0.001), MoCA (β = -0.054, q < 0.001), PHC_EXF (β = -0.035, q < 0.001), PHC_LAN (β = -0.034, q < 0.001), and PHC_MEM (β = -0.038, q < 0.001). Consistently, higher CSF LC3B levels were associated with faster longitudinal cognitive decline in ADAS13 (β = 0.033, q = 0.005), CDRSB (β = 0.040, q = 0.006), MMSE (β = -0.049, q = 0.001), MoCA (β = -0.039, q = 0.001), PHC_EXF (β = -0.025, q = 0.01), PHC_LAN (β = -0.021, q = 0.026), and PHC_MEM (β = -0.023, q = 0.006) (Fig. 2G-T and Supplementary Table 2).

CSF LC3A and LC3B levels are associated with increased dementia risk

In the overall cohort, Kaplan-Meier survival analyses indicated that participants with higher CSF LC3A (log-rank P < .001) or LC3B (log-rank P = .021) levels exhibited significantly increased probabilities of developing dementia. Consistently, Cox proportional hazards models demonstrated that elevated CSF LC3A (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.312, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.123–1.516, P < .001) and LC3B (HR = 1.145, 95% CI = 0.995–1.317, P = .058) levels were associated with an higher risk of incident dementia. Excluding participants who developed dementia within two years of baseline helped mitigate the impact of reverse causality and did not materially alter these associations. In post-exclusion analyses, elevated CSF LC3A (log-rank P < .001; HR = 1.338, 95% CI: 1.155–1.668, P < .001) and LC3B (log-rank P = .011; HR = 1.199, 95% CI: 1.003–1.434, P = .046) levels remained independently predictive of AD risk (Fig. 2U-X and Supplementary Table 3).

CSF LC3A and LC3B levels were positively associated with AD biomarkers in CSF and on PET imaging across both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses

Cross-sectionally, higher CSF LC3A levels were significantly associated with elevated CSF p-tau (β = 0.756, q < 0.001) and t-tau (β = 0.803, q < 0.001), as well as PGRN (β = 0.139, q = 0.002) and sTREM2 (β = 0.513, q < 0.001). Additionally, higher CSF LC3A was associated with greater cortical amyloid deposition (β = 0.208, q < 0.001) and tau burden (β = 0.225, q = 0.005), whereas no significant associations were observed with CSF Aβ42 or FDG-PET uptake (Fig. 3A-F and Supplementary Table 4). Similarly, elevated CSF LC3B levels were cross-sectionally associated with higher CSF p-tau (β = 0.715, q < 0.001), t-tau (β = 0.767, q < 0.001), PGRN (β = 0.166, q < 0.001), and sTREM2 (β = 0.54, q < 0.001), as well as greater cortical amyloid deposition (β = 0.154, q = 0.001) and tau burden (β = 0.16, q = 0.044) measured by PET, with CSF Aβ42 and FDG-PET uptake exhibiting no significant association (Fig. 3G-L and Supplementary Table 4).

Predictive performance of CSF LC3A and LC3B and their distribution across ATN-classified groups

We constructed ten multivariable logistic regression models to predict TN− versus TN+ status. The predictive performance of CSF Aβ42 alone yielded an AUC of 0.692 (95% CI, 0.645–0.74), while amyloid-PET alone achieved an AUC of 0.76 (95% CI, 0.708–0.807). CSF LC3A alone showed an AUC of 0.903 (95% CI, 0.879–0.927), which increased to 0.939 (95% CI, 0.919–0.957) when combined with CSF Aβ42, and 0.939 (95% CI, 0.915–0.96) when combined with amyloid-PET. Further adjustment for demographic factors, including age, sex, education, and APOE ε4 carrier status, yielded an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI, 0.917–0.961). Similarly, CSF LC3B alone achieved an AUC of 0.876 (95% CI, 0.847–0.903), which increased to 0.928 (95% CI, 0.905–0.949) with CSF Aβ42, 0.936 (95% CI, 0.913–0.956) with amyloid-PET, and 0.938 (95% CI, 0.914–0.96) when further adjusted for demographic variables (Fig. 3S). For A− versus A+ prediction, four multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. CSF LC3A alone showed an AUC of 0.656 (95% CI, 0.596–0.713), increasing to 0.782 (95% CI, 0.732–0.83) after adjustment for demographic factors. CSF LC3B alone had an AUC of 0.619 (95% CI, 0.559–0.68), which increased to 0.776 (95% CI, 0.728–0.824) when combined with demographic covariates (Fig. 3T). The PR analysis further corroborated the ROC findings. Single biomarkers achieved moderate to high predictive performance, with CSF Aβ42 yielding an AP of 0.6 and amyloid-PET an AP of 0.824. CSF LC3A alone achieved an AP of 0.939, which increased to 0.951 when combined with Aβ42 and to 0.965 when combined with PET; inclusion of PET plus demographic covariates maintained an AP of 0.965. CSF LC3B alone yielded an AP of 0.914, which further improved when combined with Aβ42 (AP = 0.94) or PET (AP = 0.954), and increased to 0.959 after adding demographic covariates (Supplementary Fig. 1).

When ATN staging was defined using amyloid-PET in combination with CSF t-tau and p-tau, CSF levels of LC3A and LC3B were significantly elevated in both SNAP and Stage 2 groups relative to Stages 0 and 1 (all q < 0.001). A consistent profile was observed when ATN classification was based on CSF Aβ42, t-tau, and p-tau, with LC3A and LC3B again being higher in SNAP and Stage 2 compared to Stages 0 and 1 (all q < 0.001). Additionally, Stage 1 participants exhibited significantly lower LC3A and LC3B levels than those in Stage 0 (q < 0.05), and LC3B levels were lower in Stage 2 compared with the SNAP group (q < 0.05) (Fig. 3U-X and Supplementary Table 5).

CSF LC3A and LC3B levels are associated with brain atrophy in cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses

Cross-sectionally, higher CSF LC3A (β = -0.211, q < 0.001) and LC3B (β = -0.215, q < 0.001) levels were associated with smaller ventricular volumes, but no significant associations were observed for the entorhinal cortex, fusiform gyrus, hippocampus, middle temporal lobe, or whole-brain volumes (Fig. 4A-B and Supplementary Table 6). However, longitudinally, elevated CSF LC3A levels were significantly associated with faster atrophy in multiple brain regions, including the entorhinal cortex (β = -0.031 q < 0.001), fusiform gyrus (β = -0.026, q < 0.001), hippocampus (β = -0.025, q < 0.001), middle temporal lobe (β = -0.019, q < 0.001), and whole brain (β = -0.008, q = 0.004), but were not significantly associated with ventricular volumes (Fig. 4C-G and Supplementary Table 6). Likewise, higher CSF LC3B levels were longitudinally associated with accelerated atrophy in the entorhinal cortex (β = -0.024, q = 0.001), fusiform gyrus (β = -0.017, q = 0.001), hippocampus (β = -0.018, q < 0.001), middle temporal lobe (β = -0.011, q = 0.041), whereas no significant associations were observed for whole-brain or ventricular volumes (Fig. 4H-L and Supplementary Table 6).

CSF LC3A and LC3B independently or sequentially associated with tau pathology and cognitive performance via amyloid deposition and CSF sTREM2

We examined whether CSF levels of LC3A and LC3B influence tau pathology and cognition through two distinct pathways: (1) CSF LC3A and LC3B → amyloid deposition → CSF p-tau and t-tau → cognition; (2) CSF LC3A and LC3B → sTREM2 → CSF p-tau and t-tau → cognition.

In cross-sectional analyses, amyloid deposition and CSF p-tau and t-tau, either independently or sequentially, mediated the associations between CSF LC3A and multiple cognitive outcomes, including PHC_EXF, PHC_LAN, and PHC_MEM. For global cognitive measures (ADAS13, CDRSB, MMSE, and MoCA), mediation primarily occurred through amyloid deposition or CSF tau alone. Similarly, associations between CSF LC3B and cognitive performance, including ADAS13, CDRSB, MoCA, PHC_EXF, PHC_LAN, and PHC_MEM, were mediated by amyloid deposition and CSF p-tau and t-tau, either independently or sequentially. For MMSE, mediation was predominantly observed through amyloid deposition, p-tau, and t-tau individually (Fig. 5A-H and Supplementary Table 7). Regarding the sTREM2 pathway, CSF sTREM2 together with CSF p-tau and t-tau mediated the relationships between CSF LC3A and LC3B and cognitive performance, including ADAS13, CDRSB, MMSE, MoCA, PHC_MEM, PHC_EXF, and PHC_LAN, either through sequential mediation or via p-tau and t-tau alone (Fig. 5I-N and Supplementary Table 8).

Longitudinally, baseline CSF LC3A and LC3B were associated with subsequent cognitive decline through two baseline-mediated pathways involving amyloid deposition and CSF p-tau and t-tau, either independently or sequentially. Specifically, higher baseline levels of LC3A and LC3B were associated with greater baseline amyloid burden and more severe CSF tau pathology, which in turn predicted accelerated longitudinal decline in ADAS13, CDRSB, MMSE, MoCA, PHC_MEM, PHC_EXF, and PHC_LAN scores (Fig. 5O-R and Supplementary Table 9). Discussion

Our findings show elevated CSF LC3A and LC3B levels are linked to accelerated cognitive decline and higher AD risk. Moreover, higher baseline LC3A and LC3B correlates with longitudinal rises in CSF sTREM2, tau accumulation, and regional brain atrophy. Amyloid deposition and CSF sTREM2 partially mediate the LC3-tau pathology association. Importantly, CSF LC3A and LC3B alone or with amyloid effectively predicts TN- and TN+ status, outperforming amyloid alone.

Autophagy plasticity contributes to the maintenance of cognitive function in mouse models of AD, and modulation of the autophagy pathway can ameliorate AD-related pathology [13, 15, 51, 52]. However, evidence from human cohorts regarding this association remains limited. Impaired autophagic-lysosomal degradation function is related to poorer cognitive function, particularly in the domains of memory, executive function, and language, which is consistent with our findings [14]. However, LC3A showed a stronger association with cognitive decline and higher AD risk compared to LC3B. This discrepancy may be attributed to their differential expression patterns: LC3B is a broadly expressed autophagy marker with relatively low specificity, whereas LC3A is predominantly expressed in multiple central nervous system cell types. This brain-enriched expression profile may render LC3A more sensitive in reflecting AD-related cognitive impairment. Higher levels of LC3B-II were positively correlated with better cognitive performance in male mice, suggesting a protective role of autophagy in that model [53]. It is entirely consistent with our proposed hypothesis: during the early stages of autophagy, when the downstream pathway remains fully functional, autophagy effectively clears Aβ and thereby serves a protective, cognition-preserving function. In contrast, at later disease stages, when downstream autophagic flux is impaired, the accumulation of Aβ-laden autophagosomes due to their failed clearance means that the activation of the autophagy pathway becomes a marker of autophagic obstruction, which is subsequently associated with cognitive decline and increased dementia risk. These supporting their potential utility as biomarkers in AD progression.

Numerous studies demonstrated that lysosomal acidification declines significantly before the extracellular deposition of Aβ, suggesting that autophagy participates in Aβ clearance during the early stages of AD pathology [21, 54]. Another study reported that LC3 overexpression enhances neuronal autophagic activity and mitigates Aβ-induced neurotoxicity [55], supporting our findings and hypotheses. Autophagy also plays a pivotal role in tau metabolism. In hippocampal neurons, tau accumulation has been shown to induce autophagic defects, upregulate mTORC1 activity, and suppress autophagosome formation, marked by LC3-II, thereby establishing a vicious cycle that exacerbates tau aggregation [56]. Autophagy receptors mediating selective autophagy facilitate the clearance of phosphorylated tau in neurons, and their overexpression reduces tau pathology in vivo [57, 58]. These results are in close agreement with our findings, suggesting that pronounced tau accumulation disrupts the autophagic pathway, leading to further impairment of autophagic flux. In support of this notion, repeated ultrasound stimulation was recently reported to induce neuronal autophagy, promote tau clearance, and improve motor function in tau transgenic mice [59]. Similarly, overexpression of BAG3, a co-chaperone that promotes autophagy, reduces pathological tau accumulation in primary neurons, whereas BAG3 depletion aggravates it [60, 61]. Collectively, these findings emphasize the therapeutic promise of modulating neuronal autophagy in mitigating AD pathology and disease progression.

While acute microglial activation can be beneficial by reducing environmental stress, chronic exposure to antibodies and pro-inflammatory cytokines leads to sustained activation, neuroinflammation, and neurotoxicity [54]. In AD mouse models, microglial inflammasome activation and pyroptosis are regulated by NLRP3, whose ubiquitinated form is recognized by p62 and degraded through the autophagy–lysosome pathway, thereby suppressing microglial inflammation and cell death [15, 62]. These findings suggest that autophagy preserves microglial phagocytic function, ultimately exerting neuroprotective effects in AD. Moreover, fibrillar Aβ accumulation within endolysosomal compartments has been shown to increase lysosomal membrane permeability, trigger cathepsin release, and induce lysosomal cell death accompanied by microglial infiltration [21]. Additional evidence has identified the LC3-associated endocytosis (LANDO) pathway in microglia. Mice lacking microglial LANDO exhibit elevated hippocampal pro-inflammatory cytokine production and accumulation of neurotoxic antibodies and display disrupted neuronal signaling, accelerated neurodegeneration, and cognitive impairment [63]. Together, these findings highlight the essential role of microglial LAP/LANDO pathways in regulating neuroinflammation and maintaining neuronal integrity in AD. Previous studies have demonstrated that microglial ATG5 in the hippocampus maintains neural stem cell health, prevents neurodegeneration, and that impaired autophagy may reduce dendritic complexity, leading to brain atrophy and cognitive decline [64, 65]. These findings align closely with our conclusions, reinforcing the notion that stage-dependent alterations in autophagy critically influence AD progression. Moreover, although the cross-sectional and longitudinal associations of LC3A and LC3B with brain volumes appear contradictory in this study, they can be reconciled by considering whether early or late stage autophagy pathways are blocked, as discussed above.

This study offers several significant strengths. To our knowledge, the first population-based study to concurrently examine, both cross-sectionally and longitudinally, CSF LC3A and LC3B, microglial sTREM2, AD biomarkers, and cognition in a well-characterized cohort. Multimodal CSF and PET data enhance robustness, and the results delineate a plausible mechanistic cascade from autophagy to Aβ deposition, microglial activation, tau accumulation, and cognitive decline. However, there are several limitations. First, LC3 levels in cerebrospinal fluid can only reflect autophagic stress or autophagic responses and cannot fully capture intracellular autophagic flux within brain tissue. Second, tau PET measurements in ADNI were derived from follow-up scans rather than true baseline acquisitions, introducing potential bias. Third, although mediation analyses yield valuable insights, they cannot establish causality. Fourth, the limited sample size with longitudinal tau PET data may reduce statistical power to detect long-term associations. Fifth, future studies should also combine LC3A and LC3B with additional biomarkers to evaluate autophagic flux. Finally, external validation in independent and ethnically diverse cohorts is essential to confirm the generalizability of our findings.

In summary, our findings indicate that CSF LC3A and LC3B, reflecting autophagic activation, are linked to elevated tau pathology and an increased risk of AD. They may jointly contribute to AD pathological progression alongside amyloid deposition and microglial activation. These findings highlight intervening neuronal autophagy as a potential strategy to mitigate AD pathology and slow cognitive decline.
