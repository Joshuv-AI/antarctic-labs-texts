Neuropsychiatric association of tirzepatide and semaglutide in obesity with and without type 2 diabetes
Authors: Yu-Nan Huang, Kai-Wen Liu, Pin-Hung Li, Jo-Ching Chen, Gideon Meyerowitz-Katz, Pen-Hua Su, Chieh-Chen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Neuropsychiatric outcomes after starting newer anti-obesity medicines remain uncertain in routine care. We evaluate associations between initiation of tirzepatide or semaglutide and incident neuropsychiatric diagnoses in people with obesity, stratified by type 2 diabetes (T2D).Methods:Using a United States electronic health record network (2020–2025), we identified adults with obesity who newly started tirzepatide or semaglutide and matched them one-to-one to new users of naltrexone–bupropion, phentermine, or phentermine–topiramate. We modeled the time to first recorded diagnosis over 24 months; bariatric surgery recipients were examined as a contextual comparator. Confidence intervals are 95%.Results:Here we show that, versus naltrexone–bupropion, semaglutide is associated with lower hazards of anxiety disorder in adults with T2D (hazard ratio 0.67, 0.51–0.87) and without T2D (0.73, 0.64–0.83), and lower hazards of depression in both strata (with T2D: 0.69, 0.50–0.95; without T2D: 0.53, 0.45–0.61). Tirzepatide versus naltrexone–bupropion is associated with lower hazard ratios for anxiety disorder (with T2D: 0.55, 0.40–0.76; without T2D: 0.78, 0.68–0.89) and depression (with T2D: 0.49, 0.33–0.72; without T2D: 0.57, 0.49–0.66). In adults without T2D, tirzepatide versus semaglutide is associated with higher hazards of anxiety disorder (1.12, 1.06–1.18) and insomnia (1.13, 1.05–1.21).Conclusions:Initiation of tirzepatide and semaglutide is associated with differing hazard patterns across prescription comparators, with bariatric surgery providing additional context. These findings inform monitoring and shared decisions, while remaining subject to residual confounding and diagnosis misclassification.Subject terms: Epidemiology, Obesity, Epidemiology, Type 2 diabetes, Neurological disorders

Introduction

Obesity represents a complex metabolic disorder affecting over 650 million adults globally, with profound implications for both physical and mental health1,2. Beyond its established role in cardiometabolic complications, mounting evidence reveals intricate bidirectional relationships between obesity and psychiatric disorders3. Individuals with obesity face elevated risks of depression, anxiety, and cognitive impairments, while psychiatric conditions often precipitate weight gain through dysregulation of appetite, reduced physical activity, and medication side effects4,5.

The neurobiological links between obesity and psychiatric disorders involve inflammation, HPA axis dysregulation, and altered neurotransmitter systems6–9. While current treatments focus on lifestyle changes with pharmaceutical support10, traditional weight-loss medications have inconsistent psychiatric effects11, with some noradrenergic agents even worsening anxiety or mood symptoms in vulnerable patients12.

GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA) have transformed obesity management13–16, offering weight reduction while potentially delivering neuropsychiatric benefits through central nervous system actions17–20. Tirzepatide, as the first dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 RA14, shows superior metabolic outcomes, though its psychiatric effects remain understudied20,21. GIP is an incretin hormone secreted by intestinal K cells that augments glucose-dependent insulin secretion. In addition to its peripheral metabolic actions, GIP receptor signaling has been identified in hypothalamic and other brain regions involved in energy homeostasis and feeding, supporting biologic interest in dual GIP/GLP-1 agonism22,23. This dual-receptor mechanism may enhance benefits, as both GLP-1 and GIP receptors are found in key brain regions governing cognition, mood, and neuroprotection22,23.

In this U.S. electronic health record cohort of adults with obesity, initiation of tirzepatide and semaglutide shows varying associations with incident psychiatric diagnoses across active comparators and by type 2 diabetes (T2D) status. Compared with naltrexone–bupropion, both agents are associated with lower hazards of anxiety in several comparisons, whereas among individuals without type 2 diabetes, tirzepatide versus semaglutide is associated with higher hazards of anxiety and insomnia. These findings indicate comparator- and diabetes-specific heterogeneity and merit confirmation with standardized outcome assessment.

Methods

Ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chung Shan Medical University Hospital (approval number CS2-24100) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles. TriNetX provides de-identified data; the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board.

Data source

We used the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, which aggregates de-identified EHR data from participating US health care organizations. The study period was restricted to January 1, 2020, through November 30, 2025. The platform integrates comprehensive longitudinal clinical data, including demographics, diagnoses (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD-10-CM] codes), procedures, medications, and laboratory results.

Target trial emulation and design

We emulated a pragmatic target trial framework using propensity score matching to compare incident neuropsychiatric outcomes across weight-lowering treatment strategies (Supplementary Data 1). The framework prespecified eligibility criteria, treatment assignment, follow-up, and outcome ascertainment to approximate randomized comparisons while leveraging real-world EHR data. To limit potential bias from temporal changes in medication capture and prescribing, cohort entry was restricted to the period from January 1, 2020, through November 30, 2025. Follow-up was administratively censored on November 30, 2025. The index date was the first recorded prescription/administration of the index medication; for non-pharmacologic comparators, the index date was the first qualifying clinical encounter meeting cohort criteria. Primary analyses used an intention-to-treat (ITT) approach: patients were analyzed according to the index treatment and were followed from the index date until the occurrence of the outcome, death, loss to follow-up, or administrative censoring at study end, regardless of subsequent discontinuation or switching. We used ITT as the primary framework because, in EHR data, discontinuation and switching may be related to symptoms, tolerability, or clinical change, which can bias analyses that censor follow-up at treatment change. As a complementary sensitivity specification, we also conducted an as-treated analysis in which follow-up was censored at inferred treatment discontinuation or switching based on medication records. By anchoring time zero at treatment initiation and aligning cohort entry to a contemporary calendar period, this design aimed to reduce immortal time and time-related selection biases.

Cohort construction and study design

We used directed acyclic graph modeling to identify confounding factors and causal pathways based on clinical knowledge and literature (Supplementary Fig. 1).

We studied people with obesity (E66 or BMI ≥ 27 kg/m²) who initiated either GLP-1 RAs (tirzepatide, semaglutide) or conventional anti-obesity medications (active comparators: phentermine-topiramate, naltrexone-bupropion, and phentermine) between 2020 and 2025 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). In a prespecified sensitivity analysis, we repeated the primary ITT comparisons in a restricted cohort requiring both an ICD-10-CM obesity diagnosis code (E66) and BMI ≥ 27 kg/m² recorded at baseline. Active comparators were selected a priori based on clinical use for obesity and feasibility of constructing adequately sized propensity score–matched cohorts using structured medication documentation in TriNetX. Orlistat was screened as a candidate comparator, but structured RxNorm-coded capture was limited (RxNorm 37925; n = 23,584), and its frequent over-the-counter use raises concerns about exposure under-ascertainment; it was therefore not included. Metformin was not included as a primary active comparator because, in routine care, initiation is commonly aligned with glycemic management or other metabolic indications (e.g., prediabetes, insulin resistance, polycystic ovary syndrome) rather than a primary weight-loss intent. In de-identified EHR data, prescribing intent cannot be reliably distinguished using structured fields alone, and a metformin-initiator cohort constructed without indication adjudication would be clinically heterogeneous and less comparable to GLP-1/GIP initiators. Therefore, metformin exposure was modeled as a baseline covariate in propensity score estimation rather than as a primary comparator arm. Individuals were required to have a 6-month washout period before the index and at least one documented clinical encounter in the preceding year. Medication exposure was defined using EHR-documented use of the study medications. Key exclusion criteria included Type 1 diabetes, bariatric surgery history, organ transplantation, HIV infection and end-stage renal disease. We created separate cohorts for patients with and without T2D. A non-pharmacological weight-loss comparator group was carefully constructed to include individuals who achieved a comparable BMI reduction from ≥30 to <27 kg/m² within one year without medications, surgery, or malignancy. This comparator cohort was designed to contextualize neuropsychiatric outcomes among individuals achieving substantial weight loss without anti-obesity medications. However, it cannot fully separate medication-related associations from weight-loss trajectories or unmeasured behavioral and psychosocial factors. We estimated propensity scores using baseline demographic characteristics, comorbidities, healthcare utilization measures, concomitant medications, and available laboratory profiles, including baseline BMI and baseline HbA1c (when available), recorded in the 12 months prior to the index date. We additionally included an exploratory propensity score–matched comparison with bariatric surgery recipients (sleeve gastrectomy or gastric bypass) as a contextual comparator; this analysis is described as supplementary and interpreted cautiously (Supplementary Data 2).

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome was an incident diagnosis of anxiety disorders within 24 months following cohort entry. Secondary outcomes included cognitive deficit, insomnia, mood disorder, nerve disorders, depression, substance-related disorders, and peripheral neuropathy. Incident outcomes were defined as time to the first recorded ICD-10-CM diagnosis code for each endpoint during follow-up. The primary (broad) cognitive deficit definition used an ICD-10-CM code set that included dementia/MCI codes and codes that may reflect acute or transient altered mental status (e.g., delirium, somnolence, encephalopathy). In sensitivity analyses, we applied a strict cognitive deficit definition that excluded delirium/somnolence- and encephalopathy-related codes and retained dementia and mild cognitive impairment codes only (Supplementary Data 3). Nerve disorders were defined using G50–G59 codes. As a sensitivity analysis, we applied a strict definition excluding compressive/entrapment mononeuropathies to reduce heterogeneity within the broad category (Supplementary Data 3). Peripheral neuropathy was defined using G60–G65 codes, with a prespecified strict variant excluding selected codes (Supplementary Data 3). To assess potential unmeasured confounding and validate our analytical approach, we evaluated negative control outcomes (NCOs). For each endpoint, outcomes were analyzed using a time-to-first-event framework, defined as time to the first recorded ICD-10-CM diagnosis code during follow-up among individuals without a baseline history of that endpoint. Outcomes were identified using ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes recorded during routine clinical care (Supplementary Data 3); no standardized psychiatric interviews or formal cognitive assessments were available.

Covariates

We included a comprehensive set of covariates to ensure balanced comparisons between treatment groups. Variables encompassed demographic characteristics (age, sex, race, and socioeconomic status), lifestyle factors (nicotine dependence, alcohol-related disorders, lifestyle-related problems), and relevant comorbidities (cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders, renal diseases, and psychiatric conditions). Cardiovascular disease was operationalized as any baseline diagnosis of ischemic heart disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation/flutter, cerebrovascular disease, or peripheral arterial disease, defined using ICD-10-CM codes. We also accounted for concurrent medications (antidiabetic agents, cardiovascular medications, psychotropic drugs) and key laboratory parameters (BMI, HbA1c, lipid profiles, inflammatory markers). The presence of comorbidities was determined using corresponding ICD-10 codes (Supplementary Data 4).

Statistics and reproducibility

We employed propensity score matching using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor algorithm to generate balanced pairs for comparisons. The quality of matching was assessed through standardized mean differences, with values below 0.1 indicating adequate balance and ensuring comparability between groups24.

To evaluate the risk of psychiatric outcomes, we applied both crude and multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed to depict the cumulative incidence of outcomes over time, and the log-rank test was used to evaluate differences between groups.

Primary analyses followed an ITT framework, classifying patients by the index medication at cohort entry. To evaluate the potential influence of discontinuation, switching, and longitudinal medication documentation, we performed prespecified PP sensitivity analyses based on treatment persistence, operationalized as repeated administrations within predefined time windows (PP-1 to PP-9, ranging from a requirement for a second administration within 1–75 days to a requirement for up to ten administrations through 601–675 days). We additionally performed an as-treated sensitivity analysis to approximate on-treatment associations. In this specification, follow-up was censored at inferred discontinuation of the index medication or switching to an alternative weight-lowering therapy, operationalized from EHR medication records using a prespecified allowable gap after the last recorded administration. Because discontinuation and switching may be related to emerging symptoms, tolerability, or care utilization, as-treated estimates were interpreted as sensitivity results.

In our statistical framework, we used two-sided tests and a nominal alpha level of 0.05. To address multiplicity across the eight prespecified outcomes within each treatment comparison (incident anxiety disorder and seven secondary/exploratory outcomes), we controlled the false discovery rate using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure and report FDR-adjusted p-values (p adj); p adj < 0.05 was used as the reporting threshold for this prespecified outcome set. For the NCOs, we report unadjusted p-values because these endpoints were used as falsification tests and are interpreted descriptively. Subgroup analyses, alternative outcome definitions, and dose-stratified contrasts were prespecified as exploratory and are presented descriptively. To evaluate robustness to potential unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values for associations meeting the FDR criterion25.

To strengthen the validity of our primary findings, we performed several pre-specified secondary analyses. First, we conducted subgroup analyses stratifying results by sex (female/male), age ( > 65/ ≤ 65 years), race (White, Black, Hispanic or Latino or Black or African American), and specific clinical parameters including eGFR ( ≥ 45 or <45 mL/min/1.73m2), HbA1c (≥7% or <7%), BMI ( < 30, 30-34.9, 35-39.9 or ≥40 kg/m2), LDL ( < 100, 100-160, ≥160 mg/dL), presence of hypertension, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, proteinuria, and concurrent use of insulin, metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, or ACE inhibitors/ARBs.

BMI-stratified analyses were performed to examine outcome variations across obesity classes (Class I: 30-34.9 kg/m2, Class II: 35-39.9 kg/m2, Class III: ≥ 40 kg/m2). We assessed psychiatric medication utilization patterns, particularly focusing on prescriptions of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), and other psychotropic medications. For temporal robustness, we analyzed outcomes across different follow-up periods: extending to 5 years for GLP-1 RAs, reflecting their respective market availability periods. To address potential unmeasured confounding, we conducted negative control analyses using lisinopril and rosuvastatin as comparators. For all analyses, we maintained consistent statistical approaches, including propensity score matching and Cox proportional hazards modeling with appropriate covariate adjustment. All analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach without imputation.

The data analysis framework integrated multiple programming environments on the TriNetX analytics platform: Java 11.0.16 served as the foundational infrastructure, while R 4.0.2 facilitated statistical computations, complemented by Python 3.7 and Prism 10.0 for data visualization tasks. To safeguard patient confidentiality and maintain robust privacy standards, all numerical values below 10 were systematically rounded to the nearest decuple. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

This study uses de-identified electronic health records and includes no laboratory experiments; replicates are not applicable. Reproducibility is supported by applying the cohort definitions, covariates, outcomes, and analytic specifications detailed in the Supplementary Methods within TriNetX on the same data cut.

Results

Baseline characteristics and propensity score matching

Across obesity cohorts with and without T2D (Supplementary Data 5), baseline characteristics differed substantially between GLP-1 RA initiators and comparator groups before matching, most notably for psychiatric comorbidities and psychotropic medication use (maximum SMD ≈ 1.18). After 1:1 propensity score matching, covariates were closely aligned across comparisons; most SMDs were <0.05, with residual imbalance largely confined to a small number of low-frequency variables. Demographics, cardiometabolic comorbidities, healthcare utilization measures, and available laboratory profiles were similar between groups after matching.

Comparative neuropsychiatric outcomes of GLP-1 receptor agonists versus usual care and bariatric surgery

In propensity score–matched cohorts, initiation of tirzepatide and semaglutide was associated with lower hazards of several neuropsychiatric diagnoses compared with bariatric surgery (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3).

In separate propensity score-matched analyses comparing GLP-1 RA initiators (ITT cohorts) with bariatric surgery recipients, semaglutide and tirzepatide were associated with lower hazards of several psychiatric outcomes across diabetes strata, while neurologic outcomes showed less consistent patterns. Among individuals with obesity and T2D, semaglutide (vs. bariatric surgery) was associated with lower hazards of anxiety disorder (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.78–0.89, p adj = 6.12 × 10-8), cognitive deficit (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.66–0.77, p adj = 6.35 × 10-19), mood disorder (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.63–0.73, p adj = 2.09 × 10-22), and depression (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.61–0.72, p adj = 8.98 × 10-25). Tirzepatide (vs. bariatric surgery) showed similar reductions for anxiety disorder (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.71–0.83, p adj = 1.31 × 10-10), cognitive deficit (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.57–0.69, p adj = 1.88 × 10-22), mood disorder (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.55–0.66, p adj = 2.60 × 10-25), and depression (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.56–0.67, p adj = 2.68 × 10-24). In contrast, nerve disorders were not lower in these bariatric comparisons (semaglutide: HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.97–1.16, p adj = 0.21; tirzepatide: HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.01–1.26, p adj = 0.027).

Among individuals with obesity without T2D, semaglutide (vs. bariatric surgery) remained associated with lower hazards of anxiety disorder (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.79–0.89, p adj = 3.23 × 10-9), cognitive deficit (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.69–0.81, p adj = 1.01 × 10-11), mood disorder (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.55–0.64, p adj = 9.80 × 10-45), and depression (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.55–0.64, p adj = 4.03 × 10-45), but was associated with higher hazards of nerve disorders (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.07–1.29, p adj = 5.68 × 10-4). Tirzepatide (vs bariatric surgery) showed lower hazards of anxiety disorder (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.86–0.96, p adj = 8.70 × 10-4), cognitive deficit (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.70–0.82, p adj = 8.57 × 10-11), mood disorder (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.55–0.63, p adj = 6.2 × 10-50), depression (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.56–0.64, p adj = 2.93 × 10-46), substance-related disorders (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.57–0.68, p adj = 1.19 × 10-25), and peripheral neuropathy (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.58–0.74, p adj = 4.37 × 10-12), with no clear difference for nerve disorders (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.94–1.13, p = 0.50). NCOs were not materially different in any bariatric comparison.

Intention-to-treat and per-protocol associations of neuropsychiatric outcomes across matched weight-lowering treatment comparisons

In the ITT analysis (Tables 1–4 and Fig. 3), adjusted p-values (p adj) were calculated using Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the eight prespecified outcomes within each comparison. Overall, anxiety and depression were the most consistent psychiatric outcomes across the main comparisons, whereas the other outcomes showed more variable patterns. In obesity with T2D, semaglutide (vs. naltrexone–bupropion) was associated with lower hazards of anxiety (HR 0.67 [0.51–0.87], p adj = 0.013), cognitive deficit (0.64 [0.48–0.83], p adj = 0.007), insomnia (0.70 [0.52–0.94], p adj = 0.040), and depression (0.69 [0.50–0.95], p adj = 0.040). Tirzepatide (vs. naltrexone–bupropion) showed lower hazards of anxiety (0.55 [0.40–0.76], p adj = 0.001), depression (0.49 [0.33–0.72], p adj = 0.001), cognitive deficit (0.67 [0.49–0.91], p adj = 0.027), and mood disorder (0.64 [0.43–0.94], p adj = 0.044). In obesity without T2D, semaglutide (vs. naltrexone–bupropion) was associated with lower hazards of anxiety (0.73 [0.64–0.83], p adj = 3.00 × 10-6), cognitive deficit (0.74 [0.64–0.86], p adj = 1.45 × 10-4), insomnia (0.78 [0.68–0.90], p adj = 0.001), mood disorder (0.55 [0.48–0.64], p adj = 1.09 × 10-14), and depression (0.53 [0.45–0.61], p adj = 3.040 × 10-17). Tirzepatide (vs. naltrexone–bupropion) was associated with lower hazards of anxiety (0.78 [0.68–0.89], p adj = 0.001), cognitive deficit (0.79 [0.67–0.92], p adj = 0.006), mood disorder (0.61 [0.52–0.71], p adj = 5.96 × 10-10), depression (0.57 [0.49–0.66], p adj = 2.903 × 10-12), and substance-related disorders (0.75 [0.61–0.93], p adj = 0.013). Head-to-head, tirzepatide (vs semaglutide) in obesity without T2D was associated with higher hazards of anxiety (1.12 [1.06–1.18], p adj = 7.31 × 10-4) and insomnia (1.13 [1.05–1.21], p adj = 0.003); nerve outcomes were generally non-significant after adjustment.

To improve clinical interpretation of the primary outcome, we summarized the 24-month absolute risk difference (ARD) and estimated number needed to treat (NNT) for incident anxiety disorders. In individuals with obesity and T2D, the ARD for anxiety disorders was −6.8% (NNT = 15) for tirzepatide versus naltrexone-bupropion (Table 3) and −3.4% (NNT = 30) for semaglutide versus naltrexone-bupropion (Table 1). In individuals with obesity without type 2 diabetes, the corresponding ARDs were −2.7% (NNT = 37) for tirzepatide versus naltrexone-bupropion (Table 4) and −1.9% (NNT = 52) for semaglutide versus naltrexone-bupropion (Table 2). A smaller ARD of −0.7% (NNT = 151) was observed for semaglutide versus phentermine in the obesity-without-T2D subgroup. The larger absolute differences in the T2D subgroup were consistent with the higher baseline incidence of anxiety in that population. For tirzepatide versus semaglutide in obesity without type 2 diabetes, the 24-month absolute risk difference numerically favored tirzepatide for anxiety disorder and insomnia, whereas the corresponding hazard ratios favored semaglutide (anxiety HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.06–1.18; insomnia HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.05–1.21); this direction reversal reflects how absolute and relative measures integrate event timing differently under non-proportional hazards, and given the small absolute differences these two outcomes should be interpreted with caution.

In the PP analyses (Supplementary Data 6–9), estimates for comparisons versus naltrexone–bupropion were directionally consistent with ITT, while statistical strength varied with increasingly stringent persistence requirements. For semaglutide (vs naltrexone–bupropion) in obesity with T2D, depression remained lower (HR range 0.53–0.71; p adj range 0.001–0.036), whereas insomnia attenuated toward the null (HR 0.88–1.06; p adj 0.63–0.85). In obesity without T2D, associations for depression and mood disorder remained below 1 across PP definitions for both semaglutide and tirzepatide versus naltrexone–bupropion (e.g., semaglutide: depression p adj 3.12 × 10-20–5.59 × 10−8; mood disorder p adj 1.42×10-22–2.20 × 10-7). For tirzepatide versus semaglutide in obesity without T2D, the ITT signals for anxiety/insomnia were less stable under PP specifications (anxiety p adj 0.004–0.881; insomnia p adj 0.001–0.881).

In as-treated sensitivity analyses with censoring at inferred discontinuation or switching (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5), the direction of associations for key psychiatric outcomes was generally similar to the ITT estimates, although effect sizes varied across comparisons.

Medical treatment patterns and psychiatric medication utilization

Psychiatric medication use was lower among GLP-1 RA recipients than among matched comparators (Fig. 4). SSRI use was lower in the semaglutide group than in the naltrexone–bupropion group (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.40–0.74), whereas tricyclic antidepressant use showed a similar direction with confidence intervals including 1 (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.40–1.35).

Subgroup analysis

Across subgroup strata, hazard ratios for tirzepatide and semaglutide versus active comparators were generally below 1 for several outcomes, although effect sizes varied (Supplementary Data 10). In adults aged ≥65 years, tirzepatide versus comparators was associated with lower hazards of anxiety (HR range 0.28–0.30). Among individuals with indications for both diabetes and obesity management, hazard ratios for mood disorders were below 1 for tirzepatide (HR 0.31, 95% CI 0.12–0.82) and semaglutide (HR 0.18, 95% CI 0.04–0.77). In severe obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m²), tirzepatide versus comparators was associated with lower hazards of anxiety (HR range 0.34–0.47).

Stratified analysis of prior psychiatric history

Relative to the primary ITT analysis, the ITT sensitivity analysis restricted to psychiatrically naïve individuals (no baseline psychiatric diagnoses or psychotropic medication use) showed attenuation, especially in the T2D stratum (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 6). In obesity with T2D, no outcome met the FDR criterion in any comparison. In obesity without T2D, semaglutide versus naltrexone–bupropion remained associated with lower hazards of anxiety (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.52–0.75; p adj = 6.99 × 10-7), mood disorder (0.55, 0.44–0.69; p adj = 6.99 × 10-7), depression (0.52, 0.41–0.66; p adj = 2.53 × 10-7), and cognitive deficit (0.64, 0.46–0.88; p adj = 0.011), whereas insomnia did not. Tirzepatide versus naltrexone–bupropion retained associations for anxiety (0.68, 0.56–0.83; p adj = 2.69 × 10-4), mood disorder (0.60, 0.47–0.76; p adj = 1.25 × 10-4), and depression (0.58, 0.45–0.75; p adj = 1.25 × 10-4). Tirzepatide versus semaglutide was associated with higher hazards of anxiety and insomnia and lower hazards of cognitive deficit.

BMI-stratified analysis

BMI-stratified analyses showed heterogeneity in effect estimates across obesity classes (Fig. 5). In class I obesity (BMI 30–34.9 kg/m²), tirzepatide versus naltrexone–bupropion was associated with lower hazards of anxiety disorders (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.29–0.79) and mood disorders (HR 0.25, 95% CI 0.11–0.56).

Across BMI strata, the direction and magnitude of associations varied, with some contrasts appearing larger in higher BMI categories (Fig. 5). These subgroup patterns should be interpreted cautiously because subgroup estimates are less precise and may reflect residual differences in clinical characteristics, healthcare utilization, and treatment selection within strata. Subgroup results are therefore presented as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Exploratory dose-stratified comparisons of psychiatric outcomes

In exploratory dose-stratified analyses (Fig. 6) comparing tirzepatide dose strata (5 mg/ml and ≥10 mg/ml) with semaglutide dose strata, estimates for tirzepatide ≥10 mg/ml were generally close to null for the primary outcome and for insomnia. For anxiety disorder, tirzepatide ≥10 mg/ml was associated with an HR of 0.98 (95% CI, 0.73–1.31) versus semaglutide 1.34 mg/ml (Ozempic) and 1.16 (0.78–1.75) versus semaglutide 2 mg/ml (Wegovy). For insomnia, the corresponding estimates were 1.05 (0.70–1.58) versus semaglutide 1.34 mg/ml (Ozempic) and 1.23 (0.70–2.18) versus semaglutide 2 mg/ml (Wegovy). Across other secondary outcomes, confidence intervals were frequently wide and often spanned unity. These dose-stratified contrasts are presented descriptively and should not be interpreted as evidence of a dose–response relationship.

Longitudinal analysis of BMI and HbA1c

Over 24 months, body weight and HbA1c decreased more in the GLP-1 RA cohorts than in the naltrexone-bupropion and phentermine cohorts (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). In the T2D stratum, tirzepatide showed greater HbA1c reductions than naltrexone-bupropion (p = 0.028 at 6 months, p = 4.3 × 10−4 at 12 months, p = 8.2 × 10−6 at 18 months and p = 7.4 × 10−5 at 24 months), whereas body weight trajectories between the two groups were not significantly different (p = 0.17 to 0.93). Tirzepatide also showed larger HbA1c and body weight reductions than semaglutide (p = 1.1 × 10−6 at 6 months, p = 3.2 × 10−4 at 18 months, and p = 0.005 at 24 months for body weight). The bariatric surgery cohort achieved greater weight loss than both GLP-1 RA cohorts across all time points (all p < 1 × 10−50), while tirzepatide and bariatric surgery showed comparable HbA1c reductions in the T2D stratum (p = 8.3 × 10−5 at 12 months, p = 2.8 × 10−6 at 18 months).

Sensitivity analysis

When restricting the cohort to individuals meeting both E66 and BMI ≥ 27 kg/m² at baseline (Supplementary Fig. 9), estimates were directionally similar (without T2D: HR 0.78, 0.66–0.93, p adj = 0.017; with T2D: HR 0.75, 0.51–1.12, p adj = 0.260), with wider CIs consistent with smaller matched cohorts. Positive control outcomes showed expected directions consistent with prior evidence (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Sensitivity analysis for cognitive deficit and nerve outcomes

Outcome definitions materially influenced the cognitive deficit estimates (Supplementary Fig. 11). In the obesity with T2D cohort comparing semaglutide versus naltrexone–bupropion, the broad cognitive deficit definition showed a lower hazard in the semaglutide group (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.51–0.91; p = 0.008), whereas the association was attenuated under the strict definition (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.55–1.74; p = 0.943), with fewer events. Because the cognitive deficit definition sensitivity analysis was performed in a separately matched subset restricted to this endpoint, the estimate under the broad definition here differs slightly from the corresponding primary analysis reported in Table 1.

For nerve disorders and Peripheral neuropathy (Supplementary Fig. 11), estimates were imprecise and varied across definitions (broad: HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.74–1.54; p = 0.738; strict: HR 1.53, 95% CI 0.77–3.06; p = 0.224; HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.55–1.17; p = 0.256).

Discussion

In this US EHR-based cohort study, initiation of GLP-1–based therapy was associated with differential hazards of several neuropsychiatric diagnoses compared with commonly used weight-lowering pharmacotherapies, with patterns that varied by comparator and T2D status. Associations for anxiety and depression were generally directionally consistent across ITT and persistence-based sensitivity analyses, whereas some outcomes, particularly cognitive deficit, were sensitive to outcome definition, with attenuation under stricter definitions that exclude diagnoses plausibly reflecting acute altered mental status. These findings support cautious interpretation and underscore that diagnosis-coded neuropsychiatric outcomes may not represent uniform clinical phenotypes. Interpretation of these contrasts requires consideration of the pharmacologic profiles of the active comparators and the clinical contexts in which they are prescribed.

One alternative interpretation is that part of the observed contrast may reflect differences in neuropsychiatric symptom profiles across comparator therapies, rather than a uniquely favorable effect of incretin-based treatment. Phentermine–topiramate and naltrexone–bupropion have central nervous system activity, and insomnia, anxiety, and mood-related symptoms have been reported in some patients receiving centrally acting weight-loss medications26–28. Under real-world prescribing, the observed between-group differences could therefore capture both a potentially more favorable neuropsychiatric profile among incretin-based therapies and a potentially less favorable profile among certain active comparators. The present design does not allow these components to be disentangled.

GLP-1 and GIP receptors are expressed in central nervous system tissues22,29–31, and preclinical as well as early clinical investigations have suggested potential links to neuroinflammatory signaling and reward-related pathways20,32–35. These hypotheses are directionally compatible with some observed associations but cannot be tested within our EHR framework. Mechanistic inference, therefore, remains speculative and requires dedicated experimental and prospective clinical studies36–38.

The cognitive deficit finding requires cautious interpretation. In the obesity with T2D cohort, semaglutide versus naltrexone-bupropion was associated with a lower hazard of cognitive deficit under the broad definition. However, when acute and transient conditions, including delirium, somnolence, and encephalopathy, were excluded in the prespecified strict-definition sensitivity analysis, the association was attenuated to the null. This pattern suggests that the broad-definition signal was driven mainly by codes related to acute altered mental status rather than chronic cognitive impairment. These results should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that GLP-1-based therapy is associated with a lower risk of dementia or progressive cognitive decline. More broadly, diagnosis-code groupings under a single endpoint may combine clinically distinct conditions, and sensitivity analyses using stricter definitions are important for identifying which components drive the observed association. Our results extend prior GLP-1 RA research conducted largely in diabetes populations39,40 to individuals with obesity. Because mechanistic measures were not available, proposed pathways from preclinical studies (e.g., neuroinflammation, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal survival)41–44, should be interpreted as contextual hypotheses rather than inferences supported by this EHR-based analysis.

Notably, associations for tirzepatide were larger in comparison with natural weight-loss achievers than with untreated controls. This contrast may reflect differences not captured by measured weight trajectories, but it may also arise from unmeasured health behaviors, treatment motivation, or differences in clinical ascertainment across comparator groups. These patterns are compatible with, but do not establish, medication-related neurobiological effects mediated through GLP-1 and GIP signaling in brain regions involved in cognition and mood regulation. The observed reduction in substance-related disorders among tirzepatide initiators also warrants attention, given the potential clinical relevance9. Mechanistically, this association could plausibly involve modulation of dopamine-dependent reward pathways, consistent with receptor expression in regions related to addiction and reward behaviors17,42. The consistency of this association across comparator groups supports further evaluation in prospective studies. These observations align with experimental and clinical evidence that GLP-1-based therapies can influence reward processing and reward-seeking behaviors45, and dual receptor activation may have additional implications for reward circuitry21. Findings from the bariatric surgery comparator analysis further support that these patterns persist even when contrasted with a high-magnitude weight-loss intervention, but differences in surgical selection and perioperative assessment limit interpretability.

Beyond diagnosis-coded neuropsychiatric outcomes, we observed lower subsequent utilization of psychiatric medications among GLP-1 RA initiators. This pattern may be clinically relevant because commonly used psychotropics, including SSRIs, SNRIs, and atypical antipsychotics, have been associated with weight gain and adverse metabolic profiles in some patients46,47. Reduced exposure to these agents could therefore coincide with more favorable metabolic trajectories; however, psychiatric medication use in EHR data is influenced by care access, prescribing preference, symptom burden, and follow-up intensity, and residual confounding is likely. Although metformin is frequently used in contemporary weight management, particularly in individuals without T2D, we did not include it as a primary comparator because EHR-structured data do not allow reliable separation of weight-focused prescribing from other metabolic indications. In addition, patients initiating metformin under these diverse contexts may differ materially from GLP-1/GIP initiators in baseline disease burden and care pathways, which would increase concerns about non-comparability and residual confounding even after matching. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as comparisons among the prespecified active anti-obesity medication cohorts rather than as contrasts against metformin. Experimental studies have also reported effects of GLP-1 signaling on blood-brain barrier function and vascular inflammation17,48, which may provide biologic plausibility, but these pathways cannot be evaluated in the present dataset.

Several aspects of the association patterns merit comment. The PP sensitivity analyses generally showed stronger associations than the ITT estimates. This pattern may have several explanations. Patients who remained on treatment over time may represent a subgroup with better adherence, better tolerability, and more consistent follow-up, which could make treatment-outcome associations appear stronger in the PP analyses. In addition, censoring follow-up at discontinuation or switching may introduce informative censoring if treatment changes are related to emerging psychiatric symptoms, adverse effects, or other clinical changes. We therefore retained the ITT framework from treatment initiation as the primary analysis, because it is less sensitive to these selection mechanisms and is more representative of the broader population of treatment initiators. The PP findings are best interpreted as supportive sensitivity analyses showing that several of the main associations remained directionally similar among patients with more sustained exposure. Differential metabolic trajectories between treatment groups may partly account for the observed associations. GLP-1 RA initiators showed greater reductions in body weight and HbA1c than naltrexone-bupropion and phentermine initiators over 24 months, and these differences were statistically significant at multiple time points. Because weight loss and glycemic improvement may themselves influence neuropsychiatric risk, the observed associations could reflect both direct central nervous system effects and indirect metabolic pathways. We did not adjust for post-baseline weight or HbA1c changes because these variables are potential mediators on the treatment-outcome pathway; conditioning on a mediator can introduce collider bias and obscure the total association of interest. Baseline BMI and HbA1c were balanced through propensity score matching, ensuring that starting metabolic status did not confound the between-group comparisons. Estimates were directionally similar across sensitivity analyses, including alternative follow-up windows and negative controls, although such consistency does not eliminate bias related to comparator choice or differential monitoring. Separation of cumulative incidence curves became more apparent after approximately 12 months, which may reflect time-varying confounding, treatment persistence differences, or longer-term adaptations that are not measurable in structured EHR data. Associations were larger in individuals with severe obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m²) and, for cognitive outcomes, in older adults ( ≥ 65 years). These subgroup patterns should be interpreted cautiously and warrant replication in studies with standardized neuropsychiatric phenotyping and longitudinal symptom assessment.

Our observations are directionally consistent with meta-analyses that have reported lower risks of cognitive outcomes among GLP-1 RA users, although included studies vary in design, populations, and outcome definitions49. In our study, contrasts observed for tirzepatide relative to traditional anti-obesity medications should be interpreted as comparative associations that may reflect pharmacology, prescribing context, tolerability, and differential monitoring, rather than evidence of clinical superiority.

Our findings can be compared with a small number of prospective and observational studies that have looked at GLP-1 RAs and mental health outcomes. In a prespecified analysis of the REWIND trial, in which adults with T2D were randomized to dulaglutide or placebo and followed for a median of 5.4 years, dulaglutide was associated with a lower rate of substantive cognitive impairment50. A pooled analysis of double-blind randomized trials combined with Danish registry data also reported a lower incidence of dementia in patients treated with GLP-1 RAs51. Using a United States electronic health record network, a target trial emulation reported lower first-time diagnoses of Alzheimer’s disease in adults with T2D who received semaglutide compared with other antidiabetic drugs52. A separate real-world analysis found the same general pattern for GLP-1 RAs and SGLT-2 inhibitors on Alzheimer’s disease risk53. The first phase 3 trials with cognition as the primary endpoint, EVOKE and EVOKE + , are testing oral semaglutide in early symptomatic Alzheimer’s disease and have not yet reported results54. Evidence for mood outcomes is thinner. A recent systematic review of GLP-1 RAs in depression found signals across preclinical, observational and small clinical studies, but no randomized trial with a psychiatric primary endpoint55. A narrative psychiatric review reached the same conclusion56. The lower anxiety and depression hazards that we observed in the obesity with T2D stratum are in the same general direction as these prior reports, although study designs, exposures and outcome measures differ across settings. Several gaps remain. Prospective trials in adults with obesity have not used standardized psychiatric instruments such as the PHQ-9 or GAD-7 as primary endpoints. Prospective mental health data on tirzepatide are also lacking, so the higher hazards for anxiety and insomnia that we observed for tirzepatide versus semaglutide in adults without T2D cannot yet be checked against trial data. Patients with active psychiatric illness have been excluded from most large cardiometabolic trials, so current estimates may not apply well to the population in which the clinical question matters most. Pragmatic randomized or prospective cohort studies in adults with obesity with and without active psychiatric illness, using standardized instruments at prespecified time points and including tirzepatide alongside semaglutide as a separate arm, would help close these gaps.

Several limitations warrant emphasis. Despite propensity score matching with an expanded covariate set, including baseline psychiatric diagnoses and psychotropic medication use, residual confounding remains plausible, particularly for outcomes influenced by factors not well captured in structured EHR data (e.g., symptom severity, social context, socioeconomic status, and care-seeking patterns). Comparator choice may introduce bias. Medications with substantial non-prescription use, such as orlistat, may be under-ascertained in EHR medication fields and were therefore not included as active comparators. Active comparators differ in indications, tolerability, and prescribing pathways, and non-pharmacologic weight-loss cohorts (where used as supplementary context) may differ systematically in motivation, health behaviors, and access to care. Comparisons with centrally acting anti-obesity medications warrant additional caution. These agents have distinct neuropsychiatric adverse-event profiles and may also differ in tolerability and discontinuation patterns. As a result, observed contrasts may reflect comparator-related symptoms, differential treatment persistence, and differences in clinical monitoring, in addition to any association related to incretin-based therapy. Such differences may not be fully measurable and limit the interpretability of between-group contrasts. Detection bias is another important consideration. Patients starting newer, higher-cost therapies such as GLP-1 RA may undergo more frequent follow-up for dose titration, adverse effect monitoring, or insurance-related documentation than patients receiving older medications. This could increase the likelihood that neuropsychiatric conditions are recorded in the GLP-1 RA group. In our study, baseline healthcare utilization variables were included in the propensity score model, and these measures were well balanced after matching, with standardized mean differences below 0.05. However, post-baseline differences in visit frequency and coding intensity could not be fully measured or controlled. If GLP-1 RA users were monitored more closely after treatment initiation, this would tend to bias the results toward higher, not lower, hazards in that group. Even so, more complex differences in coding practice across treatment groups cannot be excluded. Exposure ascertainment in EHR data is imperfect. Although the revised primary analysis uses an ITT framework anchored at treatment initiation and PP analyses based on persistence definitions to reduce duration-based selection, exposure misclassification and treatment switching remain possible and may be informative with respect to emerging symptoms or adverse effects. As-treated analyses may introduce informative censoring because discontinuation or switching can be related to tolerability, emerging neuropsychiatric symptoms, or healthcare utilization; therefore, we treat as-treated results as sensitivity analyses and avoid causal interpretation. Outcome misclassification is an important concern because outcomes are defined by ICD-10-CM codes rather than standardized psychiatric interviews or cognitive testing. This is particularly relevant for cognitive deficit, where broader code sets may capture acute medical states (e.g., delirium or somnolence) rather than chronic cognitive impairment; we therefore present strict-definition sensitivity analyses and interpret cognitive findings conservatively. Finally, generalizability is limited to healthcare settings similar to the TriNetX US network, where obesity treatment access, mental health service availability, and coding practices may differ from those in other countries and health systems. Evidence from multinational pharmacovigilance and other non-US data sources may reflect different reporting environments and patient contexts57. TriNetX is not population-based; participating organizations may differ from the broader US healthcare landscape with respect to patient mix, documentation intensity, and coding practices. Dose-stratified findings are exploratory. Tirzepatide and semaglutide have different pharmacologic properties and dosing scales; therefore, cross-medication dose-level contrasts should not be interpreted as evidence of a graded relationship across dose levels. In addition, dose-strata sample sizes and follow-up may limit precision for some outcomes.

These findings may inform future comparative effectiveness and safety research in patients with obesity who have coexisting cognitive impairment or substance use disorders. Given the observational design and reliance on diagnosis codes, the estimates should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in prospective studies with standardized assessments. The directionally similar associations observed in older adults support dedicated evaluation in older populations, where baseline risks, comorbidity burden, and clinical monitoring differ from those in younger patients.

By comparing tirzepatide initiators with both active anti-obesity medication comparators and a non-pharmacological weight-loss cohort, we observed consistent associations between tirzepatide initiation and a lower incidence of several neuropsychiatric diagnoses. These findings generate hypotheses regarding potential central effects of incretin-based therapies, but they do not establish a mechanism or directionality. Prospective studies with standardized neuropsychiatric phenotyping, careful characterization of treatment switching and adherence, and mechanistic measures will be needed to distinguish medication-related effects from comparator tolerability and residual confounding.
