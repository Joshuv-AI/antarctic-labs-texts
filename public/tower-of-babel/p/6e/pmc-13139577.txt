Green colloidal composites from Putranjiva roxburghii wall extract (PRWE) for the prevention of mild steel corrosion in an acidic medium of 1.0 M HCl
Authors: Ashish Sihmar, Suresh Kumar, Humira Assad, Ravi Kumar, Hemant Tanwar, Hariom Dahiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the fact that various studies have proved the efficacy of plant-based extracts as green corrosion inhibitors, the possibility of using unwanted and invasive species as sustainable corrosion inhibitors is still underexplored. In the current study, Putranjiva roxburgii extract (PRWE), which is an abundantly available weed, is, explored as a value-added, eco-friendly corrosion inhibitor to mild steel in a 1.0 M HCl acidic environment. The electrochemical method, the gravimetric analysis, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and the computational evaluation were used to analyze the PRWE corrosion inhibition performance. These findings indicated the presence of a concentration-related inhibition behaviour, with PRWE being a mixed-type inhibitor, as shown by anodic and cathodic polarization slopes. The corrosion inhibition potential of PRWE was systematically assessed via potentiodynamic polarisation (PDP), Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and weight loss (WL). PRWE exhibited efficiency of 95.57% as determined by EIS and 97.51% as obtained via WL at 298 K that decreased as the temperature was elevated. Increases in charge transfer resistance (Rct) and polarization resistance (Rp) and a reduction in the constant phase element value, confirming the presence of a protective adsorbed layer over the mild steel surface. Theoretical computations also indicated the vigorous adsorption of PRWE components on the steel surface, characterized by a low energy gap (ΔE), which showed good agreement with experimental results. Based on all the above findings, the present work presents weeds as a low-cost, low-utilized, sustainable source of corrosion inhibitors, which have the dual advantage of resisting corrosion and environmental management.Keywords: Mild steel, Corrosion, Purtanjiva roxburghii, Mixed adsorption, Inhibition efficiency, Density functional theorySubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

The superior electrical and mechanical attributes of metals make them a common material employed in human endeavors1,2. Their safeguarding is a top concern to keep these metals in the optimum environment. The undesirable condition that brings about the weakening of metals most frequently is corrosion3. This organic process results from the electrochemical reaction between metals and their corrosive surroundings. Interactions between the metal outer layer and the corrosive media result in the generation of stable forms like sulfides, oxides, and other substances4. Numerous businesses across the economy are impacted by corrosion, which is typically assessed from multiple angles, including the economic aspect (cost, downtime of machinery, loss, and profits), as well as the safeguarding of workers and environmental safety if it occurs5.

Due to its superior mechanical attributes, mild steel (MS) is the most widely used metal in diverse industries such as petroleum, energy, chemical, food, and construction sectors. This metal exhibits high mechanical resistance, resilience, and robustness, among other qualities, making it a widely used substance at a reasonable price. Therefore, finding remedies to issues related to the deterioration through corrosion of steel (mostly mild steel) is a high-priority subject. The corrosion rate of metals used in industrial applications is being reduced in a variety of ways to ensure long and trouble-free operation. Numerous practical and economic methods are employed to lower the rate of metal corrosion, but preventing corrosion is still a considerable challenge that industries must overcome6.This is because it is expensive to design machines that are resistant to corrosive media and to create environments that are safe for human life by reducing breakage and explosion brought on by corrosion7. The use of corrosion inhibitors (CIs), among other techniques, is the most successful method for corrosion abatement8,9. Polyphosphates, chromates, and other CIs have been used in mild steel corrosion mitigation up until this point10. Unfortunately, many commercialized CIs are expensive, and a few of them might be dangerous and harmful to ecological systems and human well-being. Hence, the production of efficient, inexpensive, and environmentally benign CIs has moved to the forefront of the priority list, taking detrimental effects on the environment and issues related to human health into account11.

One of the widely adopted and environmentally sustainable viable substitutes to costly, traditional, and dangerous CIs is the application of extracts from natural resources, including weeds, herbs, plants, shrubs, crop residue and other natural sources12–14. These extracts offer numerous benefits over conventional hazardous and environmentally detrimental CIs, notably good effectiveness, widespread conventional accessibility, benign qualities, affordability and reduced risks to ground, aquatic and terrestrial organisms. The natural extract’s notable corrosion resistance to a variety of metals is primarily due to the presence of a variety of simple to complex phytochemicals, such as flavonoids, tannins, terpenes, vitamins, and phenols, which comprise multiple bonds and electron-rich polar functional units, which encourage their adherence to the metal’s exterior15. For instance, Thakur et al. studied the effects of an aerial extract of Cnicus Benedictus (CBE) on the deterioration of mild steel in a caustic atmosphere containing 0.5 M HCl. This study showed that CBE is an environmentally friendly and sustainable MSCI with an inhibition efficacy of around 92.45% at a concentration of 1000 ppm12. Nadi and co-workers reported that Sargassum muticum extract effectively inhibited steel corrosion in acidic media, with the inhibition efficiency remaining ~ 90% at 1.5 g L⁻¹. Saxena et al. investigated Cuscuta reflexa extract in H2SO4 and achieved an inhibition efficiency of ~ 95% at 500 mg L⁻¹, alongside a detailed discussion of its inhibition mechanism. Similarly, Aourabi et al. tested the MS corrosion prevention capability of Ammi visnaga’s extract in corrosive solutions containing 1 M HCl16. More recently, Zhang et al. evaluated Punica granatum L. extract for Q235 steel in H₂SO₄ using combined experimental and theoretical approaches, reporting an inhibition efficiency of ~ 94% at 500 mg L⁻¹. Additionally, Chen et al. assessed Pisum sativum L. leaf extract as a green corrosion inhibitor for copper in sulfuric acid medium. Quercetin and catechin, the primary phytoconstituents detected in the extracts, demonstrated opposing effects on MS acidic degradation using electrochemical techniques. At 0.15 g/L of inhibitor concentration, they showed a maximum efficiency of 93%. Additionally, Tan et al. employed betel leaf extracts (BLE) in 1 M HCl media as a non-toxic MSCI. The results demonstrate that BLE prevented Q235 steel’s cathode and anode reactions according to the electrochemical test, exhibiting anti-corrosion effectiveness of up to 90% at 400 mg/L of BLE17. Additionally, several other studies such asa maximum IE% of 94% was displayed by Juglans regia extract at a concentration of 1000 ppm18, 98.6% inhibition efficiency was exhibited by aqueous extract of purple hedge leaves at 40ppm19, Pennisetum purpureum extract at a dosage of 5.0 g/L demonstrated the highest IE of about 81.7%20, at optimum dosage of 1000 ppm, Rollinia occidentalis attained 85.7% IE21. Nevertheless, very few researchers have made an effort to look at the ability of weeds to limit corrosion on metals in an acidic setting. Because weeds are typically unwanted plants that are unpleasant in certain circumstances, we intend to investigate the effectiveness of weeds’ ability to impede corrosion in this study. Weeds have a variety of detrimental effects on the environment as well as on agricultural practices. Weeds contend with crops, diminishing particular crop production by 5–50%. They also affect the profitability of the farming industry, impede labor advancement, lessen product value, and raise cleaning costs. Weeds lower the intrinsic worth of the landscape and compromise the quality of types by accidental cross-pollination22. Therefore, all these circumstances pushed us towards working on the weeds, harnessing undesirable resources to build a breakthrough, ecologically sound, and economically viable CI. The control of weeds and the abatement of the perennial problem of corrosion will both be resolved simultaneously as a result of this research.

This study systematically evaluates the corrosion-inhibition performance of Putranjiva roxburghii, a medicinal plant from the Euphorbiaceae family. Notably, the species can attain heights of up to ~ 12 m and is investigated here as a promising, sustainable source of bioactive constituents for mitigating corrosion in aggressive media. Analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory properties of Putranjiva roxburghii(PR) have been used for centuries to treat muscle sprainhemorrhoids, rheumatism, arthralgia, gynecological conditions, and fever23. Several distinct phytochemicals, including glycosides, saponins, triterpenes, and flavonoids, have been detected in PR leaf, root, and stem24. This study tested PR, which is widely available in many countries, as a sustainable and environmentally friendly MI corrosion inhibitor in an acidic environment. As far as we can recall, no papers have looked into the application of PRWE to prevent mild steel corrosion in the solution of 1.0 M HCl environment to attain the excellent corrosion inhibition efficiency (IE%). These dispersed particles slow down corrosion in a stagnant 1 M HCl solution because of their special qualities and capacity to deposit on the MS surface, leading to the development of a protective layer and halting corrosion25. In addition to reusing resources and lowering CI costs, the use of PR components as a green CI for MS in low pH solutions also greatly promotes PR cultivation. The fundamental facet of this research is that there are few, though negligible, negative effects on people and the environment. In this study, weight loss and electrochemical methods were used to test the PRWE’s ability to inhibit MS corrosion (Fig. 1).

Experimental of PRWE

Preparation of samples

The study involves investigating the susceptibility of mild steel to corrosion when exposed to acidic environments. The mild steel specimen employed in the experiment possess the following elemental composition by wt%: Phosphorus (P) = 0.019%, Titanium (Ti) = 0.002%, Nickel (Ni) = 0.009%, Silicon (Si) = 0.015%, Sulphur (S) = 0.017%, Chromium (Cr) = 0.056%, Molybdenum (Mo) = 0.018%, Manganese (Mn) = 0.260%, Copper (Cu) = 0.010% and Carbon (C) = 0.540% and remaining Iron. The mild steel was cut into 3 cm × 1 cm coupons to ensure consistent experimental parameters across all MS specimens during the weight loss, EIS and PDP analysis. The specimens of mild steel were polished mechanically utilizing a series of emery papers with abrasive grades ranging from 200 to 2000 before experimentation. These polished mild steel surfaces were then degreased with dimethyl ketone, thoroughly rinsed with deionized water, and air dried to eliminate any residual contaminants that might affect the reliability of the results26. To provide a corrosive medium analogous to realistic acid exposure, 37% HCl was diluted by double-distilled water to produce a 1.0 M HCl medium. Deionized water was employed to ensure the absence of impurities that could interfere with the corrosion studies27. The working electrodes were prepared to facilitate diverse electrochemical studies, including potentiodynamic polarization and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy. The working electrode with an exposed interfacial area of 1 cm² was employed. The cell configuration ensured that only a portion of the metallic specimen was in contact with the acidic environment throughout the analysis28.

Test solution

The present investigation emphasizes on the inhibitory efficiency of PRWE for mild steel immersed in 1.0 M HCl. A series of PRWE concentrations (250, 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 ppm) were aggregated with 1.0 M HCl. PRWE was properly dissolved in 1.0 M HCl medium to obtain a standardized inhibitor formulation of 2000 ppm. The desired inhibitor dosages were produced by mixing the standard solution with the required HCl medium. To guarantee the accuracy and correctness of the outcomes new solutions were prepared prior to each test29.

Preparation of Putranjiva roxburghii wall extract

The Putranjiva roxburghii wall Extract (PRWE) seeds were purchased from Khari Baoli (a street and market in Delhi). The seeds were rinsed with Double-deionized water, and then it was air-dried in the sun for 4–5 days to eliminate moisture. Then the PRWE seeds were ground using mortar and pestle. After desiccation, the coarse-grained seeds were extracted using methanol via the Soxhlet extraction method.The concentration of the extract was achieved using a rotary evaporator until it was completely dried. This process facilitated complete removal of the solvent without thermal degradation of bioactive compounds. The concentrated residue yielded a crude methanolic extract. The PRWE seed extract acted as a bio-based corrosion inhibitor, exhibiting a synergistic effect attributable to its composition of multiple phytochemicals. Weight loss assessment

Corrosion performance was assessed using the weight-loss method, wherein pre-weighed coupons were immersed, cleaned, and re-weighed to obtain mass loss. A weight loss study is used to study the efficacy of corrosion inhibitors in the material. The technique measures a sample’s mass loss due to corrosion, which examines the rate of metal loss and the inhibitor’s protection efficiency. Rectangular Mild steel coupons were employed as substrates and immersed in 25 mL of 1.0 mol L⁻¹ HCl under inhibitor levels of 250, 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 ppm, including an inhibitor-free condition30. The MS samples were immersed for 24 h at 298 K. Post-immersion, the mild-steel coupons were withdrawn; corrosion products were removed, the sample was ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol and acetone, and then it was rinsed with deionized water. The mild steel strips were dried under vacuum at 100 °C, then re-weighed on an analytical balance (0.1 mg resolution) to determine weight loss31. The complete method was carried out in triplicate to confirm the consistency of the outcomes. Electrochemical study

The electrochemical investigated was conducted on “Parastat 4000 instrument” equipped with a three-electrode system for PDP and EIS analysis. Corrosion and its inhibition in materials are commonly explored through advanced electrochemical investigations like potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). By studying the change in the sample impedance during the corrosion inhibitor studies, the efficacy of corrosion inhibitors can be calculated34. The cathodic (βc) and anodic (βa) slopes of MS were compared via potentiodynamic polarization in both inhibitor-free and inhibitor-containing environments. The polarization slopes (βc and βa) define the correlation between electrode potential (E) and corrosion current density (icorr), facilitating the determination of corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion rate (CR). The analysis of the polarization curves revealed that the inhibitor acts as both a cathodic and anodic inhibitor, and further, it also determines that the surface adherence on mild steel is both chemisorptive as well as physisorptive35. The electrochemical characteristics of the specimens were investigated using a mono-compartment electrochemical cell. Three electrode systems were used in the electrochemical cell: a working electrode formed from a mild steel coupon with a 1 cm² immersed area a counter electrode formed from a platinum rod (purity 99.9%) and a reference electrode formed from a saturated calomel electrode (SCE)36. The working electrode was engaged in a 1.0 M HCl solution with and without the PRWE conc. For 600 s at the open-circuit potential (OCP), this test ensures that the surface of MS maintains a steady, stable status. The frequency span of EIS was 10 kHz to 0.01 Hz with a 10 V amplitude. The evaluation of icorr was attained through extrapolation of the anodic (βa) and cathodic (βc) Tafel slopes to the corrosion potential (Ecorr). The polarization measurements were carried out using a potentiodynamic technique with a scan rate of 0.33 mVs− 1. Surface morphology: field emission scanning electron microscopy

The protective effect of the material against corrosion was investigated through FESEM. The Mild steel specimens were submerged in a 1.0 M HCl corrosive environment, both in the presence and absence of PRWE, for 24 h to evaluate the surface topography of the MS specimens. The FESEM analysis indicated the extent of protective film developed on mild steel by PRWE particles. The mild steel strips were taken out and dried after being immersed for 24 h. Surface morphology offers that a informative and time efficient method for evaluating the early stages of corrosion and the protective film formed by the inhibitor on mild steel while preserving its interesting intrinsic surface attributes38.

Contact angle (CA) investigation

The contact angle (CA) measurements are an extensively utilized strategy in the province of corrosion inhibition to assess surface attributes of mild steel after exposure to a corrosion inhibitor. A spatter of water is placed on the mild steel platform, and the angle constructed between the two is evaluated. The wetting behavior of the mild steel substrate indicates the engagement between the corrosion inhibitor and the mild steel surface, as evident from the contact angle in the corrosion inhibition investigation. In the contact angle technique, the “KRUSS ADVANCE 1.6.2.0 Drop shape Analyzer DSA25” software analyzed contact angles for both inhibitor-free and inhibitor-containing samples39.

Computational evaluation: density functional theory

Density functional theory (DFT) is a technique taken into use to explain molecular properties and characteristics, as well as assumptions about how accurately corrosion inhibitors can be studied. The most widely used and best theoretical technique in materials science for corrosion inhibition studies is the Density Functional Theory. DFT can be applied to investigate the interaction among a corrosion inhibitor and a mild steel in a corrosion inhibition environment13. Contact angle (CA)

Contact angle measurements following immersion in the testing medium with and without 2000 ppm of inhibitor molecules were used to examine the degree of hydrophilicity of the film that the plant extract produced on the surface of MS. After 6 h of immersion in 1.0 M HCl media with and without an inhibitor, the wetting characteristics of the metal surface were examined. The acquired contact angles between an MS coupon and a water droplet are portrayed in Fig. 5. The polished mild steel coupon (Fig. 5a) demonstrated a clear hydrophobic characteristic with an average contact angle of 93.8°. The hydrophobic surface of the metal was distorted after 6 h of immersion in a 1.0 M HCl, as shown by the drastic acid attack that caused the contact angle with the water droplet to drop to 61.3° (Fig. 5b). This relatively low angle indicates that the metal surface has been heavily wetted by the acidic solution, demonstrating that the corrosion prevention effect is minimal. The metal was exposed to an acidic solution without much resistance, based on the significant degree of wetness26. However, as illustrated in Fig. 5(c), the incorporation of plant extract lessened the impact of the acid attack because it developed a protective film on the MS surface with an enhanced contact angle value of 82.8°. This notable rise in contact angle suggests a modification of the surface characteristics attributable to the protective film created by the metal. The inhibitor effectively altered the interaction of the metal surface with the corrosive environment, as evidenced by an elevated contact angle, which also suggests enhanced hydrophobicity or reduced surface wettability. The increase in CA demonstrates that the inhibitor create a hydrophobic coating on the surface of MS shielding it from the corrosive acid. The protective coating significantly improves corrosion resistance by preventing the acid from damaging the metal substrate42. The estimated CA values are in line with earlier analytical results from this study and demonstrate how well the corrosion inhibitor works to prevent damage and protect the metal in acidic surroundings.

Electrochemical tests

Open circuit potential

To achieve a stable state, the open-circuit potential (OCP) of the metal was evaluated as a function of time in a 1.0 M HCl environment, both with and without different PRWE concentrations. Figure 6 presents the variation of the OCP in the presence of varying PRWE doses. The OCP indicates that a 600-second immersion time interval was suitable for both the uninhibited and inhibited systems to achieve a stable OCP condition. However, the OCP graph was shifted towards the more negative value after adding different concentrations of PRWE while the shape of OCP-time curve followed a similar trend across all concentrations. This behaviour illustrates the initial solubilization of the oxide film developed in air prior to exposure, essentially due to the cathodically controlled process. In solutions having different inhibitor proportions, however, the OCP levels shifted in a less inverse direction for some concentrations owing to the inhibitor’s selective adsorption on the anodic regions of the mild steel surface.

Electrochemical analysis

Figure 7 presents the Bode plots showing the corrosion behavior of mild steel in without PRWE medium and in the presence of PRWE (plant extract) at concentrations ranging from 250 to 2000 ppm. The plots include both the phase angle (°) and impedance response (|Z|) as a behavior of the logarithm of frequency (log f). The phase angle plots show a single blank solution, relatively low phase maximum, indicating protective coating formation on mild steel. The addition of PRWE, the phase angle values increase significantly, and the curves broaden across a wider frequency range. At high frequencies, the comparable outcomes portrayed by |Z| curves revealed that the response is mainly dependent on solution resistance (Rs), whereas at intermediate to low frequencies, the effect of the inhibitor is most evident, where interfacial processes prevail. The increment in the phase maxima from blank to inhibited demonstrates a transition towards a more capacitive response that arises from enhanced surface coverage as well as reduced heterogeneity owing to the adsorption of the inhibitor. This response suggests the development of a more stable and enhanced corrosion-resistant, capacitive protective layer on the steel surface. The enhancement in phase angle with PRWE doses implies improved barrier characteristics and stronger adsorption of inhibitor molecules onto the mild steel surface. In the log |Z| versus log f plots, the impedance magnitude for the blank solution is considerably lower compared to that of the inhibitor-containing solutions. The impedance values increase progressively with increasing PRWE concentration up to 2000 ppm, indicating higher charge transfer resistance and lower corrosion rates. This confirms that the extract effectively reduces the active cathodic as well as anodic processes on the steel surface43.

The semicircle denotes the impedance behavior of mild steel and its corroded surface, whereas the straight line corresponds to the entirety of the resistance of the metal. Measurement of potentiodynamic polarization

Using potentiodynamic polarization, the Corrosive interaction of mild steel with 1.0 M HCl acid medium was characterized for uninhibited media and for solutions containing 250–2000 ppm plant extract (PRWE). Prior to polarization measurements, the open-circuit potential (OCP) was assessed for 600 s to achieve system stabilization. A consistent OCP was observed after this duration. The addition of the plant extract resulted in a slight negative shift in OCP, indicating disruption of the initial oxide layer and cathodic control characteristics, which collectively suggest a mixed-type inhibition mechanism48. Figure 7a illustrates the polarization curves for MS immersed in uninhibited and inhibited acidic solutions. The corresponding electrochemical parameters, including corrosion current density (Icorr), cathodic Tafel slope (βc), anodic Tafel slope (βa), and inhibition efficiency (IE%), are presented in Table 4. These parameters were extracted from the extrapolation of the anodic and cathodic Tafel regions49. The data reveal a marked decrease in corrosion current densities with increasing concentrations of the plant extract, indicating its effective role in suppressing electrochemical reactions. This inhibitory action is attributed to the adsorption of phytoconstituents present in the extract, which form a protective layer on the steel surface, therefore inhibiting both the anodic and cathodic corrosion processes. A significant reduction in corrosion rate from 149.08 mm/year at 250 ppm to 11.069 mm/year at 2000 ppm was recorded, highlighting the remarkable protective capability of the plant extract. The optimum inhibition efficiency of 92.57% was achieved at the maximum tested concentration of 2000 ppm, underscoring the extract’s potential as a highly efficient green corrosion inhibitor. Importantly, the overall trend in polarization behavior and the minimal shift in electrochemical parameters confirm the mixed-type nature of the inhibition. These findings align with previously reported criteria for classifying inhibitors based on their effect on anodic and cathodic reactions, thus reinforcing the dual-action protective mechanism of the plant-based inhibitor50.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was employed to analyse the surface topography of metallic samples after 24-h exposure to 1.0 M HCl solution at 298 K, both in the absence and presence of PRWE. Density functional theory

In order to evaluate molecular attributes, elucidate the mechanistic mode of action, and predict the protective performance of the inhibitor, quantum chemistry techniques, more especially, density functional theory (DFT), are being utilized on a growing basis52,53. A popular theoretical method in materials research, including corrosion inhibition, is DFT. The performance of materials as corrosion inhibitors (PRWE) can be predicted by using DFT simulations, which can offer important insights into the electronic framework and reactive nature of materials54,55. DFT is employed to investigate how a possible inhibitor interacts with an exposed metal surface in inhibition adsorption. As further shown by Sanaei et al.56, Thakur et al.26, and Goyal et al.57, the molecular geometries were determined through global optimizations that were characterized by vibration frequencies calculation utilizing the DFT method (via Dmol3, which comprised the B3LYP (water) model). This allowed DFT to predict both the efficiency of the inhibitor’s adsorption onto the metallic substrate and the IE% by assessing the electronic attributes of PRWE and the surface. Figure 10 shows the HOMO and LUMO density distributions of the inhibitors under study, together with their optimized molecular structures. It is evident that practically every molecule in plant extract, such as PRWE 4, PRWE 3, PRWE 2, and PRWE 1, has a distribution of HOMO and LUMO densities. It was discovered that the LUMO and HOMO of the PRWE molecule are mostly found on the benzene ring and –OH groups, whereas the HOMO is mostly found on the benzene rings and just partially on the N atom and -OH groups. As a result, electrons from the inhibitor molecules can enter the iron atom’s empty orbital (d) to create a coordination leap. This suggests that the inhibitor molecules and the iron atom can coordinate to form a stable combination58,59. A higher EHOMO is associated with increased donor capacity, while a lower ELUMO is associated with increased acceptor capacity. All four of the plant extract molecules (PRWE 1- PRWE 4) had high EHOMO and low ELUMO values, as shown in Table 5, which indicates their capacity to transfer and receive electrons when communicating with the metallic substrate. The adsorption of the plant extract on the metal substrate and, consequently, the IE% are facilitated by higher levels of EHOMO, which also affect the electronic transmission across the adsorbed layer. Furthermore, as LUMO represents a molecule’s propensity to acquire electrons (electrophilic), lower values of ELUMO are expected to have a stronger adsorption capability (the likelihood of the molecule collecting electrons is substantial) and greater corrosion inhibition60,61. It is challenging to articulate the variations in the ΔE of the PRWE 1, PRWE 2, PRWE 3, and PRWE 4 in terms of their geometry; however, the frontier orbitals (LUMO, HOMO) energies, as well as other quantum descriptors of the inhibitors that were calculated and shown in Table 5, will be of greater use. When comparing compound 2 (PRWE 2) to the three remaining inhibitors, it is shown that each inhibitor molecule has a different EHOMO value and that compound 2 has the best electron transfer capacity. EHOMO and ELUMO energy fluctuations also indicate the energy gap (ΔE), another important parameter to examine and validate a corrosion inhibitor’s stability index. Effective corrosion protection is exhibited by the lower (ΔE) values, which in all cases show improved intermolecular interaction between the PRWE and the Fe surface (4.008, 6.715, 7.835, and 7.975). As a result, the inhibitors’ electrons will be able to cross the metal surface more readily, and the IE% will be higher the lower the energy difference (ΔE) between the donor and acceptor’s frontier orbitals. For instance, a higher value will result in low chemical reactivity. In comparison to the other compounds (PRWE 1, PRWE 2, and PRWE 3), compound 1 (PRWE 1) is the most chemically reactive and least stable due to its smallest energy difference (4.008 eV).

Conclusion

For the first time, the inhibitory behaviour of plant extract, this paper illustrates the effective use of the widely available weed Viz. Putranjiva roxburgii extract (PRWE) as an effective and sustainable corrosion inhibitor in mild steel in a 1 M HCl acidic solution. The PRWE corrosion inhibitor was evaluated in a systematic manner through gravimetric analysis, electrochemical analysis, surface morphology analysis, and theoretical analysis, providing a comprehensive understanding of its inhibitory behaviour. The WL results indicated that PRWE has a high concentration-dependent inhibition, reaching a maximum inhibition efficiency of 97.51% at an optimal concentration of 2000 ppm at 298 K. Electrochemical measurements showed PRWE is a mixed type of inhibitor, which significantly increases the charge transfer and polarization resistance but reduces the constant phase element value, indicating the formation of a stable and protective adsorbed layer on the mild steel surface. In the adsorption studies, it was found that the R2 value was close to 1 (~ 0.999) indicating that the adsorption of the inhibitor onto a mild steel surface in 1.0 M HCl solution was in accordance with the Langmuir adsorption isotherm.Furthermore, important features were discovered by the quantum analysis of PRWE compounds such as PRWE 1, PRWE 2, PRWE 3, and PRWE 4. With the lowest ELUMO (− 2.231 eV) and the greatest EHOMO (− 10.205 eV), PRWE 4 stands out as having a robust electron transport capability. The thorough findings of this investigation support PRWE’s efficacy as an MS corrosion inhibitor, especially in challenging, low-pH conditions. Putranjiva roxburghii extract (PRWE) exhibits strong potential for future deployment as a green corrosion inhibitor, providing an environmentally sustainable alternative to conventional toxic inhibitors. The corrosion rate decreased significantly (74.901–1.86 mg cm−2 h−1) while the inhibition efficiency rose (86.6–97.35%) as the PRWE concentration increased at 298 K, according to the gravimetric study. Moreover, the activation parameters revealed that the adsorption of PRWE is largely enthalpy-controlled, which highlights its effectiveness as an effective corrosion inhibitor. Surface analysis also supported the effectiveness of PRWE in reducing corrosion damage, and theoretical calculations indicated high adsorption interactions between PRWE constituents and the metal surface, as indicated by a small energy gap. The main importance of this work extends beyond its technical performance to the fact that it is a low-cost, environmentally-friendly, and sustainable resource of corrosion inhibition that will offer a solution to both corrosion protection and environmental management issues. The research will provide a new and environmentally friendly alternative to the traditional dangerous corrosion inhibitors by transforming a plant that is unwanted and harmful to the environment into a useful inhibitor. These findings demonstrate the potential of weeds as an under-explored natural resource and create new avenues of the development of green, cost-effective, and sustainable corrosion inhibition strategies to be used in industry.
