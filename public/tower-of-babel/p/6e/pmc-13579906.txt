An ICOSL-armed oncolytic adenovirus activates CD4+ T cell to potentiate antitumor immunity and synergizes with anti-PD-1 or CAR-T cell therapy in colorectal cancer
Authors: Wencui Liu, Xuanqi Ren, Yuxin Zhang, Qian Yue, Xiangmei Chen, Dan Zhou, Xiao Li, Qilei Xin, Lingkai Kong, Hao Wu, Ciliang Guo, Xiaosong Gu, Junhua Wu, Chunping Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe efficacy of oncolytic adenoviruses (ADVs) in colorectal cancer (CRC) is limited by their inability to effectively expand CD4+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME). This study aimed to elucidate the underlying mechanism and overcome this bottleneck through viral engineering.MethodsWe constructed an oncolytic adenovirus expressing inducible T cell co-stimulator ligand (ICOSL), designated ADV-ICOSL, based on the backbone virus ADV-NC. Its antitumor efficacy, safety, and ability to induce immunological memory were systematically evaluated in MC38 and CT26 murine colorectal cancer (CRC) models. The mechanism of action was investigated using flow cytometry, co-culture assays, RNA sequencing, and specific pathway inhibitors. The synergistic potential with anti-PD-1 or chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy was assessed. Furthermore, a humanized ICOSL-expressing virus (ADV-hICOSL) was developed and validated in cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models.ResultsWe initially discovered that ADV-NC treatment led to decreased ICOSL levels in the TME, resulting in insufficient co-stimulation for CD4+ T cells. In contrast, ADV-ICOSL significantly elevated ICOSL expression and demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy and the capacity to induce long-term immune memory. Mechanistic studies revealed that ADV-ICOSL, via elevating ICOSL, activated the PI3K/Akt-NF-κB signaling axis in CD4+ T cells, promoting their secretion of IL-2. IL-2, in a non-contact-dependent manner, potently enhanced the proliferation and cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells. This process was strictly dependent on CD4+ T cells. Furthermore, ADV-ICOSL exhibited significant synergistic effects when combined with either anti-PD-1 or CAR-T therapy. Importantly, ADV-hICOSL effectively increased the infiltration of CD4+ T and GZMB+ CD8+ T cells in CDX/PDX models, exerting potent antitumor effects.ConclusionsOur study not only reveals that the suboptimal efficacy of conventional oncolytic adenovirus is associated with ICOSL downregulation and impaired CD4+ T cell help, but also successfully develops ADV-ICOSL to reverse this limitation. This study provides a novel combinatorial strategy and a solid experimental foundation for oncolytic virotherapy in CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-04994-7.

Background

A report on global cancer incidence and mortality in 2023 revealed that CRC ranked fifth in incidence and fourth in mortality, representing a significant health burden and a global public health challenge [1]. Although conventional treatments such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy have been continuously optimized, their efficacy remains limited for patients with advanced or metastatic CRC, with over 50% experiencing recurrence after multimodal therapy [2]. In recent years, cancer immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockade and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy, has demonstrated groundbreaking efficacy against various malignancies [3, 4]. However, the vast majority of patients with CRC do not benefit from immunotherapy, highlighting the urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes [5–7].

Among the currently available immunotherapeutic approaches, oncolytic viruses (OVs) are living microorganisms capable of selectively replicating in tumor cells. OVs not only directly lyse tumor cells but also elicit a systemic antitumor immune response [8–10]. Currently, a growing body of research has focused on engineering oncolytic viruses to enhance their anti-tumor immune potency [11–16]. Among various OVs, ADVs have emerged as widely used representative vectors because of their favorable safety profile and clinical applicability [17–20]. However, limitations remain in the application of ADVs for CRC, particularly their inability to effectively promote the proliferation of CD4⁺ T cell within the TME [21]. As central regulators of antitumor immunity, CD4⁺ T cells play indispensable roles in the full activation, functional maintenance, and memory formation of CD8⁺ T cells [22]. Therefore, addressing the inadequate proliferation of CD4⁺ T cell represents a crucial strategy for enhancing the efficacy of ADVs.

On the basis of the aforementioned background, this study revealed that conventional ADVs downregulated the expression of ICOSL in the TME during treatment, leading to insufficient costimulatory signaling for CD4+ T cell activation. The ICOS-ICOSL pathway, a key costimulatory signal within the CD28 family, plays a central role in regulating the proliferation and function of CD4+ T cell [23]. This pathway primarily activates downstream signals such as PI3K/Akt to promote T-cell proliferation and enhance cellular immune responses [24]. Although the expression level of ICOSL is closely associated with T-cell infiltration in the TME and patient prognosis, research on the relevant mechanisms through which ICOSL exerts significant antitumor efficacy when expressed in ADVs is insufficient [25]. Therefore, in-depth exploration of the mechanisms by which ICOSL potentiates oncolytic virotherapy is highly important for the development of novel combined immunotherapy strategies.

In this study, on the basis that ADV-NC treatment downregulates ICOSL expression in the TME, leading to insufficient costimulation for CD4⁺ T cell activation, a secreted form of ICOSL (rather than the transmembrane form) was engineered into the oncolytic adenovirus. Although ICOSL is physiologically expressed as a transmembrane costimulatory ligand, its interaction with ICOS is mediated primarily through the extracellular immunoglobulin-like domains [26]. Previous studies have shown that soluble ICOSL/B7-H2 fusion proteins can bind ICOS-expressing activated T cells and provide functional costimulatory activity under appropriate T-cell receptor stimulation conditions [27]. Therefore, in the present study, we used a secreted ICOSL construct to focus on the extracellular ICOSL-mediated interaction with ICOS and to evaluate its potential paracrine immunomodulatory effect, rather than to fully recapitulate the cell-contact-dependent activity of membrane-bound ICOSL.The resulting construct, ADV-ICOSL, was successfully generated, and its antitumor efficacy, safety, and ability to induce immune memory in murine CRC models were systematically evaluated. Mechanistic investigations revealed that ADV-ICOSL upregulates ICOSL to activate the PI3K/Akt-NF-κB axis in CD4⁺ T cell, triggering IL-2 secretion, which ultimately augments CD8⁺ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity via a contact-independent mechanism. Furthermore, ADV-ICOSL demonstrated significant synergistic effects when combined with either anti-PD-1 therapy or CAR-T cell therapy. To further advance clinical translation, we also constructed ADV-hICOSL and validated its efficacy and safety in dual-humanized CDX and PDX models with both reconstituted immune systems and human-derived tumor tissues. This research elucidates the molecular mechanism by which ADV-ICOSL enhances antitumor immunity via the ICOS-ICOSL pathway in CRC, providing not only a solid theoretical and experimental foundation for ICOSL-based oncolytic virotherapy but also novel strategies for expanding the application of OVs in cancer immunotherapy.

Methods

Mice

Six-week-old mice were used for the experiments. WT BALB/c mice, WT C57BL/6J mice (purchased from the Comparative Medicine Centre of Yangzhou University), nude mice and NKG mice (purchased from Cyagen Biosciences, Inc., China) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at a temperature of 18 °C to 24 °C with water and food and maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital, Medical School of Nanjing University (IACUC-D2402006).

Cell lines

The mouse CRC cell lines CT26-car and MC38-car (car: coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor), CT26-luc and MC38-luc, MC38-CD19, the human CRC cell line HCT116, H22-car and human embryonic kidney 293T cells (purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China)) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Method details

Construction of recombinant adenovirus

The adenovirus shuttle plasmid encoding the ICOSL was synthesized by GenScript. For the secretion and detection of ICOSL, the IL-2 signal peptide (MYRMQLLSCIALSLALVTNS) was designed upstream of the ICOSL sequence to direct the secretion of ICOSL into the extracellular space, and the Flag-tag (DYKDDDDK) was designed downstream of the ICOSL sequence. Furthermore, the ICOSL variant used in this study is inherently of a secretory form, not a membrane-bound type. The ENTR plasmid was further recombined with the pAd-DEST human adenovirus type 5 backbone to generate a recombinant adenovirus expression vector. After digestion with the restriction enzyme PacI, the recombinant adenovirus was generated and amplified in 293T cells.

Furthermore, the ICOSL variant used in this study is inherently of a secretory form, not a membrane-bound type.

Western blotting

The cells were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail, phosphatase inhibitor, and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Protein concentrations were measured via a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit. Proteins were separated by 8 ~ 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. ECL working solutions A and B were mixed for exposure, and photos were taken. Related antibody information can be found in Supplementary Table S2.

Isolation of primary T cells

Spleen and/or tumor tissues were harvested from six-week-old BALB/c and/or C57BL/6J mice. CD4⁺ T cells and CD8⁺ T cells in the spleens of WT mice were purified via a CD4⁺ T cell isolation kit and a CD8⁺ T cell isolation kit, respectively.

qRT‒PCR

Total cellular RNA was collected and then reverse transcribed to cDNA. mRNA expression levels were analyzed via SYBR Green PCR master mix and an ABI QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and normalized to β-actin expression. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

CCK-8

CT26-car, MC38-car and HCT116 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates and infected with various adenoviruses at specified MOIs. After 48 h of incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for an additional hour. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm via a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated via the following formula: Cell viability (%) = [ (Atreatment − Ablank) / (Acontrol − Ablank)] × 100%.

ELISA

CD4+ T cells stimulated with different interventions were collected, and the concentration of mouse IL-2 was subsequently detected via related ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The supernatants of tumor cells and tumor tissues stimulated with different interventions were collected, and then the concentrations of mouse ICOSL were detected via related ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell co-incubation assay

According to different experimental purposes, T cells were isolated from the spleens of wild-type mice or tumor tissues of tumor-bearing mice. The sorted T cells were activated in 24-well plates pre-coated with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies. Subsequently, the activated T cells were subjected to different treatments for 48 h according to specific experimental requirements (e.g., treatment with LY294002). Subsequently, different cells (including CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, and tumor cells) were co-cultured according to different experimental objectives, and cell samples were collected for analysis after 48 h. The ratio of CD4⁺ T cells to CD8⁺ T cells in the co-culture system was 2:1 [28].

Electroporation

siRNA was introduced into CD4+ T cells via electroporation to knock down IL-2 expression. The electroporation conditions were 300 V and 350 µF.

Lipofectamine transfection

293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. Transfection was initiated when the cells reached 70 ~ 90% confluency, as observed under a microscope. The transfection mixture was prepared by combining viral DNA with P3000™ reagent, Lipofectamine™ 3000, and Opti-MEM® medium. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for five minutes, after which the DNA‒lipid complexes were added to 6-well plates containing preseeded cells.

CAR-T cell generation

The vectors encoding the CD19-specific CAR (CD19. CAR), incorporating the CD28 and 4-1BB costimulatory endodomains, and the methodology for the production of retroviral supernatant and CAR-T cells has been described previously [29–31]. The CD19 CAR comprised the mouse CD8α signal peptide, the FMC63‑derived scFv, the mouse CD8α transmembrane domain, and the mouse 4‑1BB and CD3ζ cytoplasmic domains, cloned into a lentiviral backbone.

Subcutaneous tumor model

Exponentially growing MC38-car, CT26-car or H22-car cells were harvested and then injected subcutaneously into the flanks of the mice. The tumor volume was calculated via the formula length×width2/2. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 50 ~ 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into different groups. Then, 0.1 mL of.

ADV-NC, ADV-ICOSL or ADV-hICOSL was injected intratumorally every other day for a total of three times. The viral titer injected into the CT26 mouse model was 1.2 × 10⁹ PFU, while that injected into the MC38 mouse model was 1.5 × 10⁹ PFU. The tumor volume was measured three times per week during the treatment. For survival experiments, the mice were sacrificed when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3. For flow cytometry analysis of tumors and/or spleens, the mice were euthanized at related time points.

Rechallenge model

For the rechallenge model, cured mice administered immunotherapies were injected subcutaneously with 1 × 106 CT26-car cells on day 0, and naive mice were used as the control group. The tumor volume was measured three times per week during the treatment. The mice were sacrificed when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3. For flow cytometry analysis of the spleen, the mice were euthanized at related time points.

PBMC-humanized CDX model

Exponentially growing HCT116 cells were harvested and then injected subcutaneously into the flanks of NKG mice on day 0. PBMCs (obtained from human peripheral blood) were inoculated into the mice on day 1. When the tumor volume reached approximately 50 ~ 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into different groups. Then, 0.1 mL of ADV-NC or ADV-hICOSL (2 × 108 PFU) was injected intratumorally every other day for a total of three times. The tumor volume was measured every other day. For survival experiments, the mice were sacrificed when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3. For flow cytometry analysis of tumors, the mice were euthanized at related time points.

PBMC-humanized PDX model

Tumor fragments (10 ~ 30 mm3) were cut via a scalpel and implanted subcutaneously in the flank regions of the mice under anesthesia on day 0. PBMCs (obtained from human peripheral blood) were inoculated into the mice on day 1. When the tumor volume reached approximately 50 ~ 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into different groups. Then, 0.1 mL of ADV-NC or ADV-hICOSL (2 × 108 PFU) was injected intratumorally every other day for a total of three times. The tumor volume was measured every other day. For survival experiments, the mice were sacrificed when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3. For flow cytometry analysis of tumors, the mice were euthanized at related time points.

In vivo immune cell depletion

For CD4+ T cell depletion, BALB/c and C57BL/6J mice were intraperitoneally injected with 200 µg of anti-CD4a antibodies every other day for a total of three times at related time points (n = 6 mice/group). The viral titer injected into the CT26 mouse model was 1.2 × 10⁹ PFU, while that injected into the MC38 mouse model was 1.5 × 10⁹ PFU. To confirm cell depletion effects, tumors from the mice injected with anti-CD4a were harvested, and flow cytometry confirmed the anti-CD4a depletion effects at related time points.

Anti-PD-1 immunotherapy

Exponentially growing MC38-car cells were harvested and then injected subcutaneously into the flanks of the mice. The tumor volume was calculated via the formula length×width2/2. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 50 ~ 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into different groups. Then, 0.1 mL of anti-PD-1 antibody (200 µg per mouse) was injected via the tail vein every three days for a total of three injections. After the first anti-PD-1 antibody treatment, 0.1 mL of ADV-ICOSL (1.5 × 108 PFU) was injected intratumorally every other day for a total of three times. The tumor volume was measured three times per week during the treatment. For survival experiments, the mice were sacrificed when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3.

CAR-T cell immunotherapy

Exponentially growing MC38-CD19 cells were harvested and then injected subcutaneously into the flanks of the mice. The tumor volume was calculated via the formula length×width2/2. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 50 ~ 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into different groups. Then, 0.1 mL of ADV-ICOSL (1.5 × 108 PFU) was injected intratumorally every other day for a total of three times. CAR-T cells were injected via the tail vein between the second and third treatment doses. The tumor volume was measured three times per week during the treatment. For survival experiments, the mice were sacrificed when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3.

Nude mouse model

Exponentially growing CT26-car cells were harvested and then injected subcutaneously into the flanks of the mice. The tumor volume was calculated via the formula length×width2/2. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 50 ~ 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into different groups. The mice were intravenously injected with 5 × 106 splenic CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells on day 1. Then, 0.1 mL of ADV-ICOSL (2 × 108 PFU) was injected intratumorally every other day for a total of three times. The tumor volume was measured every other day. For survival experiments, the mice were sacrificed when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3. For flow cytometry analysis of tumors, the mice were euthanized at related time points.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The statistical significance of differences among multiple groups was analyzed by one-way ANOVA via GraphPad Prism. The survival of the tumor-bearing mice was analyzed via the Kaplan‒Meier method with the log rank test. Student’s t test or paired t test was used to compare two independent or matched groups. The data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. The data are shown as the means ± SEMs (NS, not significantly different; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

Results

ADV-NC induces a reduction in ICOSL and the inadequacy of CD4⁺ T cell proliferation in the CRC microenvironment

Previous studies have reported limitations in the therapeutic efficacy of ADV-NC in murine CRC models, particularly its failure to significantly promote the proliferation of CD4⁺ T cell [21]. However, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. To investigate the reason for the lack of a substantial increase in the number of CD4⁺ T cell following ADV-NC treatment, this study first evaluated the efficacy of ADV-NC in two murine CRC models, MC38 and CT26 (Fig. 1a, b). Compared with the control, intratumoral injection of ADV-NC effectively inhibited tumor growth and prolonged mouse survival. No significant toxicity was observed during the treatment period, as indicated by the stable body weights of the treated mice (Fig. 1c, d; Additional file 1: Fig. S1a, b). To further analyze changes in T-cell subsets within the TME, tumor tissues were harvested on day 7 after the initial treatment and subjected to flow cytometry analysis. The results revealed a significant increase in the number of CD8⁺ T cells in the ADV-NC treatment group, whereas no marked change was observed in the number of CD4⁺ T cell (Fig. 1e, f). To evaluate systemic T cell distribution, we assessed CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell numbers in peripheral blood, spleen, and proximal lymph nodes following ADV-NC treatment. No significant changes in CD4⁺ T cell numbers were observed in any of these compartments in either CT26 or MC38 models (Additional file 1: Fig. S1c, d). For CD8⁺ T cells, no significant changes were detected in peripheral blood or spleen; however, a significant increase was observed in the lymph nodes (Additional file 1: Fig. S1e, f).

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying this phenomenon, we collected tumor tissues from CT26 model mice 48 h after the third treatment and analyzed gene expression changes associated with T-cell proliferation via RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq). RNA-seq analysis revealed that following ADV-NC treatment, Icos gene expression was significantly upregulated in the TME, while its ligand Icosl was markedly downregulated, and Cd40lg (encoding CD40L) expression was also significantly increased (Fig. 1g). To determine whether the decrease in ICOSL levels following ADV-NC treatment was attributed to lymphocytes or tumor cells in TME, we assessed ICOSL expression in mouse tumor tissues by flow cytometry. The results showed that in the CT26 mouse model, ICOSL expression levels were significantly decreased in both tumor cells and lymphocytes after ADV-NC treatment. In the MC38 mouse model, ICOSL expression levels were significantly decreased in tumor cells following ADV-NC treatment, whereas no significant change was observed in lymphocytes (Fig. 1h, i). Subsequently, we assessed intratumoral ICOSL expression levels in the CT26 and MC38 models by ELISA. The results showed that ICOSL protein levels in TME were significantly decreased following ADV-NC treatment (Fig. 1j). Therefore, we conclude that the reduction in ICOSL levels in the TME induced by ADV-NC treatment is primarily attributed to the decreased ICOSL expression in tumor cells.

Furthermore, analysis of CD4⁺ T cell function-related genes revealed that the expression of multiple T-cell inhibitory genes was significantly upregulated in the ADV-NC treatment group (Fig. 1k).

Given that T cell-mediated immune responses are highly dependent on dendritic cells (DCs), we assessed the changes in DC number and activation status in tumors and proximal lymph nodes after ADV-NC treatment. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that in tumor tissues, both the total dendritic cell (DC) count and activation status (CD40 expression) were significantly reduced in both mouse models following ADV-NC treatment (Additional file 1: Fig. S1g, i). In lymph nodes, distinct alterations were observed between the two models. In the CT26 model, the total DC number decreased without a significant change in activation status (Additional file 1: Fig. S1h). In the MC38 model, the total DC number remained unchanged, while DC activation status was significantly increased (Additional file 1: Fig. S1j). The differential lymph node DC changes between models likely reflect treatment-induced DC loss or impaired migration in CT26 model, whereas in MC38, efficient DC migration from tumors together with replenishment by immature DCs sustains DC numbers, along with possible activation of resident DCs.

In summary, this study confirmed that although ADV-NC has certain antitumor efficacy in murine CRC models, it fails to effectively increase the number of CD4⁺ T cells. This phenomenon may be attributed to ADV-NC-induced downregulation of ICOSL in the TME, which consequently deprives CD4⁺ T cell of essential costimulatory signaling through the ICOS receptor, ultimately limiting their proliferation.

ADV-ICOSL demonstrates superior antitumor efficacy, induces immune memory, and exhibits a safety profile similar to ADV-NC

An increase in the number of CD4⁺ T cell has been confirmed to significantly enhance antitumor immunity [32]. To overcome the limitations of ADV-NC in promoting the proliferation of CD4⁺ T cell, this study aimed to augment the efficacy of ADV-NC by modulating the TME. The inability of ADV-NC to significantly increase CD4⁺ T cells number is attributed to its downregulation of ICOSL in the TME, which leads to insufficient costimulatory signaling through the ICOS receptor on CD4⁺ T cells, ultimately restricting their proliferation. Previous studies have demonstrated that the ICOS–ICOSL signaling pathway plays a crucial antitumor role by promoting the proliferation and activation of intratumoral T cells [23]. Therefore, we engineered an ADV-ICOSL on the basis of the ADV-NC backbone (Fig. 2a), with the goal of compensating for the inadequacy of ADV-NC in promoting the proliferation of CD4⁺ T cell [21].

Firstly, we confirmed ICOSL expression in the supernatant of tumor cells infected with ADV-ICOSL via Western blotting (Fig. 2b). To evaluate its oncolytic activity and replication capacity, MC38-car and CT26-car cells were infected with either ADV-ICOSL or ADV-NC. qPCR analysis revealed comparable levels of viral replication in tumor cells between the two groups (Fig. 2c). Further analysis of the oncolytic effect via a CCK-8 assay revealed that ADV-ICOSL is not primarily dependent on a sustained increase in direct oncolytic potency (Fig. 2d; Additional file 1: Fig. S2a). To evaluate whether ICOSL expression affects antiviral immune responses, we measured viral replication levels in tumor tissues by qPCR. No significant difference was observed between the ADV-ICOSL and ADV-NC groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S2b). Additionally, serum from treated mice was tested for neutralizing activity against ADV-NC. ICOSL expression did not induce significant production of neutralizing antibodies compared with ADV-NC (Additional file 1: Fig. S2c, d). To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of ADV-ICOSL in vivo, we performed intratumoral injections in mouse models bearing CT26 and MC38 tumors (Fig. 2e, g). Compared with ADV-NC treatment, ADV-ICOSL treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth and prolonged survival (Fig. 2f, h) without inducing observable toxicity, as evidenced by the stable body weights of the treated mice (Additional file 1: Fig. S2e, f). Further ELISA analysis of tumor tissue samples confirmed significantly elevated ICOSL protein levels in the ADV-ICOSL treatment group (Additional file 1: Fig. S2g).

In CT26 model, complete tumor regression was observed in some mice treated with ADV-COSL. To investigate whether ADV-ICOSL induces long-term immune memory, a tumor rechallenge experiment was performed on mice with tumor regression, and the results revealed that no new tumors formed after reinoculation of tumor cells (Fig. 2i), indicating that ADV-ICOSL elicits potent immune memory protection. Flow cytometric analysis of memory T cell subsets in the spleen was performed using CD44 and CD62L staining to identify naïve (CD44⁻CD62L⁺), central memory (CM, CD44⁺CD62L⁺), and effector memory (EM, CD44⁺CD62L⁻) populations. Flow cytometric analysis further revealed a significant increase in the proportion of effector memory T cells in the spleens of the ADV-ICOSL treatment (Fig. 2j). Additionally, viral safety was assessed in immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice. H&E staining of major organs from ADV-NC-treated control mice and mice treated with ADV-ICOSL showed no significant organ damage (Fig. 2k).

To enhance the generalizability of our findings, the antitumor efficacy of ADV-ICOSL was further validated in the H22 hepatocellular carcinoma model, another immunologically ‘hot’ tumor (Additional file 1: Fig. S2h–j).

In summary, ADV-ICOSL exhibits superior antitumor efficacy to ADV-NC in subcutaneous CRC models, induces long-term antitumor immune memory, and demonstrates acceptable tolerance in the current murine model.

ADV-ICOSL promotes the proliferation and cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells in a CD4+ T Cell-dependent manner

To elucidate the mechanism of action of ADV-ICOSL, we first analyzed immune cell populations in the TME of subcutaneous CT26 and MC38 tumor models via flow cytometry. Compared with ADV-NC, ADV-ICOSL treatment significantly increased the number of CD4⁺ T cells and CD8⁺ T cells in TME (Fig. 3a, b). Flow cytometry analysis of CD4⁺ T cell activation in the TME revealed that ADV-NC treatment significantly increased the number of ICOS⁺ CD4⁺ T cells but did not significantly change CD137⁺ CD4⁺ T cells. Compared with ADV-NC, ADV-ICOSL treatment significantly increased the number of CD137⁺ CD4⁺ T cells, while ICOS⁺ CD4⁺ T cells showed no significant change (Additional file 1: Fig. S3a, b). In addition, we examined the changes in the number and activation status of DCs in the TME following ADV-ICOSL treatment. Flow cytometry results showed that compared with ADV-NC treatment, ADV-ICOSL treatment significantly increased the number of tumor-infiltrating DCs, including cDC1 (XCR1⁺) and cDC2 subsets. In the CT26 model, CD40 expression on DCs was upregulated, whereas in the MC38 model, CD80 expression was decreased (Additional file 1: Fig. S3c, d). The inconsistent changes in CD40⁺ DCs between CT26 and MC38 models may be attributed to the intrinsic differences in tumor immunogenicity and baseline immune microenvironment between the two cell lines. Finally, we also examined changes in other immune cells within the TME, including macrophages and NK cells, using flow cytometry (Additional file 1: Fig. S3e, f).

To validate the functional necessity of CD4+ T cell in ADV-ICOSL-mediated antitumor immunity, we depleted CD4+ T cell in CT26 and MC38 tumor-bearing mice with anti-CD4 antibodies (Fig. 3c, e). Effective depletion was confirmed by flow cytometry (Additional file 1: Fig. S4a, b). CD4+ T cell ablation substantially diminished the antitumor efficacy of ADV-ICOSL, as evidenced by increased tumor volume and reduced survival (Fig. 3d, f), indicating that CD4+ T cell is essential for ADV-ICOSL-induced antitumor effects.

To investigate how CD4+ T cell contribute to this process, we first assessed whether they directly mediate tumor killing (Fig. 3g). CD4+ T cells isolated from different treatment groups showed no significant differences in cytotoxic activity when co-cultured with tumor cells (Fig. 3h, Additional file 1: Fig. S4c), indicating that CD4⁺ T cells do not exert direct cytotoxic effects during the anti-tumor activity of ADV-ICOSL. Further analysis revealed no significant change in the proportion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the TME following ADV-ICOSL treatment (Additional file 1: Fig. S4d), ruling out immune suppression via Tregs induction.

Given the central role of CD4⁺ T cell in supporting CD8+ T cell function, we evaluated whether ADV-ICOSL enhances CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity indirectly through CD4+ T cell modulation. In co-culture experiments, CD4+ T cells from the ADV-ICOSL treatment group significantly enhanced the tumor-killing capacity of splenic CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3i, Additional file 1: Fig. S4e). To exclude direct effects of ADV-ICOSL on CD8+ T cell, we cultured CD8+ T cells with supernatant from virus-infected tumor cells and observed no significant changes in their proliferation or cytotoxicity (Additional file 1: Fig. S4f), confirming that ADV-ICOSL-mediated enhancement of CD8+ T cell function is CD4+ T cell dependent.

To further validate these findings, we evaluated the efficacy of ADV-ICOSL in a nude mouse model (Fig. 3j, Additional file 1: Fig. S4g). In the absence of functional T cells, ADV-ICOSL failed to exhibit significant antitumor effects, supporting its dependence on the CD4+ T–CD8+ T cell axis. Next, we used flow cytometry to assess changes in the killing ability of CD8⁺ T cells following ADV-ICOSL treatment in immunocompetent mice using the CT26 and MC38 models, as well as in nude mouse models. The results demonstrated that, compared with the ADV-NC treatment, ADV-ICOSL treatment significantly enhanced the killing ability of CD8⁺ T cells, and this process was strictly dependent on the presence of CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. 3k, Additional file 1: Fig. S4h). In vitro co-culture assays further confirmed that CD4+ T cells from the ADV-ICOSL treatment group robustly promoted CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity (Fig. 3l).

In summary, the recombinant oncolytic adenovirus ADV-ICOSL activates CD4⁺ T cell in the tumor immune microenvironment, promotes the proliferation and cytotoxic capacity of CD8⁺ T cells through intercellular help, and ultimately achieves potent antitumor efficacy.

ADV-ICOSL enhances CD8⁺ T cells antitumor function by activating the PI3K/Akt-NF-κB signaling axis in CD4⁺ T cells to promote IL-2 secretion

To elucidate the mechanism by which ADV-ICOSL enhances antitumor immunity through CD4+ T cell, we investigated the functional effects of ADV-ICOSL on CD4+ T cell and their helper function toward CD8+ T cell. Previous reports have shown that the ICOS–ICOSL interaction activates the PI3K/Akt pathway and promotes T-cell proliferation [24]. CD4⁺ T cells were isolated from the spleens of wild-type mice, and we first examined these cells via Western blotting and observed that culture supernatants of tumor cells treated with ADV-ICOSL significantly increased the expression of PI3K/Akt pathway components in CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. 4a, Additional file 1: Fig. S5a). To confirm the role of this pathway in CD4+ T cell proliferation, we used a PI3K inhibitor. Inhibition of PI3K/Akt signaling significantly attenuated ADV-ICOSL-induced CD4+ T cell proliferation (Additional file 1: Fig. S5b), indicating that ADV-ICOSL treatment promotes CD4+ T cell proliferation through PI3K/Akt activation.

We next explored how CD4+ T cell regulate CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity. Transwell co-culture experiment revealed that the percentage of GZMB⁺ CD8⁺ T cells was significantly higher in the presence of CD4⁺ T cells (both in contact and Transwell cultures) than in CD8⁺ T cells alone, with no significant difference between the contact and Transwell conditions. This indicates that CD4+ T cells enhance CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity in a contact-independent manner (Fig. 4b, c). To investigate the underlying mechanism, we sorted CD4⁺ T cells from tumor tissues of mice in different treatment groups and used qPCR to measure the transcript levels of key cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-12, and IL-2) that regulate CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity, qPCR analysis of CD4⁺ T cells revealed that IL‑2 transcription was markedly upregulated following stimulation, whereas IFN‑γ, TNF‑α, and IL‑12 levels showed no significant change (Additional file 1: Fig. S5c). Accordingly, IL-2 was identified as the key cytokine in the present study. ELISA confirmed that ADV-ICOSL treatment significantly enhanced IL-2 secretion by CD4+ T cells both in vitro and in vivo in CT26 and MC38 models (Fig. 4e, f). In this experiment, we further demonstrated that supernatant from ADV-ICOSL‑infected tumor cells contained infectious viral particles capable of infecting fresh tumor cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S5d) but did not infect T cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S5e) or reduce T cell viability (Additional file 1: Fig. S5f). The inability of oncolytic adenoviruses to infect or kill T cells is consistent with the low expression of the car receptor on T cells [33] and the absence of tumor‑permissive signaling pathways in normal T cells [34]. To determine the functional importance of IL2, CD4⁺ T cells were sorted from tumor tissues of ADV-ICOSL-treated mice and divided into two groups: one group was subjected to IL2 knockdown (KD) using siRNA, while the other group was left untreated as a control. CD8⁺ T cells were simultaneously isolated from the spleens of wild-type mice. The two groups of CD4⁺ T cells were then co-cultured with CD8⁺ T cells at a ratio of 2:1. After 48 h, the proliferation and activation of CD8⁺ T cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. The results demonstrated that IL-2 knockdown significantly impaired the ability of CD4⁺ T cells to promote the proliferation and cytotoxicity of CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 4g), indicating that IL-2 is a key mediator of ADV-ICOSL-induced CD4+ T cell helper function. Western blot analysis confirmed that IL-2 protein expression was significantly reduced in CD4⁺ T cells following siRNA transfection (Additional file 1: Fig. S5g).

To further elucidate the signaling pathway by which ADV-ICOSL regulates IL-2 expression, we hypothesized that PI3K/Akt signaling can activate NF-κB [35], a key transcriptional regulator of IL-2 [36]. Western blot analysis revealed that ADV-ICOSL stimulation upregulated both NF-κB pathway components and IL-2 expression in CD4+ T cell (Fig. 4h, Additional file 1: Fig. S5h). To determine whether PI3K/Akt and NF-κB are involved in IL-2 production, we treated CD4+ T cells with the PI3K inhibitor LY294002. This inhibited both PI3K/Akt and NF-κB pathway activation and significantly reduced IL-2 expression (Fig. 4i, j, Additional file 1: Fig. S5i, j). To clarify the causal relationship between the NF-κB and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, we employed an Akt inhibitor (artemisinin) and an NF-κB pathway inhibitor (BAY 11-7085) for Western blotting analysis. The experimental results further supported our conclusion that the PI3K/Akt pathway, acting as an upstream signaling cascade, regulates the downstream NF-κB signaling pathway, ultimately affecting the protein expression level of IL-2 (Additional file 1: Fig. S5k, l). We also performed a co-culture experiment in which CD4⁺ T cells treated with a PI3K inhibitor and untreated CD8⁺ T cells were used. The results showed that after inhibition of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, the proliferative-promoting and cytotoxic effects of CD4⁺ T cells on CD8⁺ T cells were also suppressed (Fig. 4k). Together, these data indicate that ADV-ICOSL promotes IL-2 production via the PI3K/Akt–NF-κB axis in CD4+ T cell, ultimately enhancing CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity.

Finally, to validate this mechanism in vivo, we assessed the role of IL-2 in the antitumor efficacy of ADV-ICOSL in a nude mouse model. The results showed that after the knockdown of IL-2 in CD4⁺ T cell, both the tumor growth inhibition and survival time prolongation mediated by ADV-ICOSL treatment were significantly attenuated (Fig. 4l). Flow cytometry further revealed that IL-2 knockdown decreased both the number and cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells in the TME (Fig. 4m), confirming that ADV-ICOSL depends on IL-2 to increase CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity. To determine whether siRNA-mediated IL-2 knockdown is relevant to long-term mice survival, we also quantified the IL-2 transcriptional level in CD4⁺ T cells subjected to in vitro IL-2 knockdown using qPCR. qPCR analysis confirmed that siRNA‑mediated IL‑2 knockdown in CD4⁺ T cells remained effective for at least 7 days in vitro (Additional file 1: Fig. S5m), supporting its biological relevance in survival studies lasting up to 12 days. In summary, the ADV-ICOSL activates the PI3K/Akt pathway in CD4+ T cell to promote their proliferation and, via downstream NF-κB signaling, induces IL-2 secretion. This enhances CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity, leading to potent antitumor activity.

ADV-ICOSL synergistically increased the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade and CAR-T cell therapy

We further evaluated the synergistic antitumor effects of combining ADV-ICOSL with immune checkpoint blockade in immunocompetent MC38 tumor-bearing mice. Given that ICOSL expression in the TME can increase the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [37]. Considering the central role of anti-PD-1 therapy in cancer immunotherapy, we combined ADV-ICOSL with anti-PD-1 therapy to assess potential combinatorial benefits (Fig. 5a). Monitoring of tumor volume, survival, and body weight revealed that the combination of ADV-ICOSL and anti-PD-1 therapy significantly inhibited tumor growth and prolonged mice survival compared with monotherapy or ADV-NC combined with anti-PD-1 therapy (Fig. 5b, c). The combination was well tolerated, with no observable toxicity and stable body weight throughout the treatment period (Fig. 5d).

Compared with CD8+ T cell, CD4+ T cell has been reported to better sustain the antitumor efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy upon tumor stimulation, mediate potent long-term antitumor function [38], and potentially enhance CAR-T cell immune responses through interactions with other innate and adaptive immune cells [39]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the increased number of CD4⁺ T cell in the tumor immune microenvironment induced by ADV-ICOSL treatment could also enhance the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy. We next assessed the core biological functions of oncolytic activity and replication capacity. In HCT116 cells, CCK-8 assays and qPCR analysis demonstrated that ADV-hICOSL and ADV-NC had comparable tumor cell killing and replication rates (Fig. 6c, d), indicating that the insertion of the hICOSL sequence did not compromise viral fitness.

To simulate the human immune microenvironment, this study established a dual-humanized CDX model with both reconstituted immune systems and human-derived tumor tissues from NKG mice (Fig. 6e) and evaluated the antitumor efficacy of ADV-hICOSL in vivo. Compared with ADV-NC treatment, ADV-hICOSL treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth and prolonged mice survival (Fig. 6f), with no significant body weight changes, indicating minimal toxicity (Additional file 1: Fig. S7a). Flow cytometry results showed that compared with the ADV-NC group, the number of CD4+ T cells in the TME was significantly increased after ADV-hICOSL treatment, and both the number and cytotoxic capacity of CD8+ T cells were significantly increased (Fig. 6h ~ j), demonstrating its ability to modulate the humanized tumor immune microenvironment. In addition, we examined the activation status of CD4⁺ T cells in mouse tumor tissues following ADV-hICOSL treatment using flow cytometry. The results showed that the numbers of CD137⁺ and ICOS⁺ CD4⁺ T cells in the TME were significantly increased after ADV-hICOSL treatment compared with ADV-NC, indicating that ADV-hICOSL administration can activate CD4⁺ T cells in the tumor microenvironment (Additional file 1: Fig. S7b).

To further assess the clinical relevance of ADV-hICOSL, we tested it in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model (Fig. 6e). Compared with ADV-NC treatment, ADV-hICOSL treatment again showed superior tumor growth inhibition and survival benefits (Fig. 6g), with no significant toxicity (Additional file 1: Fig. S7a). Consistent with the CDX results, flow cytometry confirmed the increased infiltration of CD4+ T cells and GZMB+ CD8+ T cells in the TME of PDX tumors (Fig. 6h ~ j), supporting the robustness of the immunomodulatory mechanism.

Since all adenoviruses used in this study carry the EGFP gene, representative bright-field and fluorescence images confirmed EGFP expression in MC38-car and CT26-car cells infected with ADV-ICOSL, and in HCT116 cells infected with ADV-hICOSL (Additional file 1: Fig. S7c).

In summary, by constructing an ADV-hICOSL virus and combining in vitro functional verification with PDX and CDX models, this study demonstrated that ADV-hICOSL, while maintaining the core functions and safety of the virus, can effectively increase the numbers of CD4⁺ T cells and effector CD8⁺ T cells in the TME, thereby exerting a significant antitumor effect and supporting further development toward potential clinical translation.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the suboptimal efficacy of conventional oncolytic adenoviruses is associated with ICOSL downregulation in the tumor microenvironment. To overcome this, we engineered ADV-ICOSL, which activates the PI3K/Akt-NF-κB-IL-2 signaling axis in CD4⁺ T cells, thereby enhancing CD8⁺ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity. Given that oncolytic viruses (OVs) can elicit systemic antitumor immune responses and that adenoviruses (ADVs) are widely used vectors with favorable safety profiles [17–19, 40], our findings provide a novel synergistic strategy and solid experimental evidence for oncolytic virotherapy in colorectal cancer (CRC).

The ICOS-ICOSL pathway is a key costimulatory signal within the CD28 family. ICOSL binding to ICOS initiates and sustains T-cell-mediated adaptive antitumor immunity, playing a central role in T-cell proliferation [23]. The ICOS-ICOSL immune checkpoint is crucial for enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapies and is applicable not only to B-cell lymphoma but also to solid tumors, including breast, colon, pancreatic, and lung cancers [41]. Reduced ICOSL levels impair ICOS binding to its receptor on T-cell, leading to insufficient T-cell activation, particularly of CD4+ T cell [42]. This study demonstrated that increasing ICOSL in the TME increases CD4+ T cell numbers, promotes IL-2 secretion, and enhances CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity, ultimately improving the antitumor efficacy of ADVs significantly. Consistent with our findings, other studies have shown that ICOSL enhances OV efficacy; for example, the engineered herpesvirus expressing ICOSL more effectively controls lung cancer progression [43]. Newcastle disease virus expressing ICOSL has significant antitumor effects on melanoma (B16-F10) and CRC (CT26) mouse models [44]. Overall, ICOSL represents an effective target for enhancing CD4+ T cell-mediated antitumor efficacy.

CD4+ T cell, which modulates the TME, is essential for effective antitumor immunity. They can target tumor cells through various mechanisms and initiate gene expression programs in CD8+ T cell, enhancing cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) function through multiple molecular pathways. Most cancer immunotherapy strategies may benefit from approaches that activate CD4+ T cell helper functions or their downstream effectors [22]. Impaired CD4+ T cell infiltration or activation compromises tumor immunotherapy outcomes [45]. Previous reports indicate that ADVs treatment does not significantly alter CD4+ T cell infiltration or activation in the TME [21]. Our study further revealed that this lack of significant change in CD4+ T cell numbers following ADV-NC treatment was associated with decreased ICOSL levels in the TME. By utilizing ICOSL to activate CD4+ T cells, we promoted their proliferation and activation, thereby enhancing the antitumor effect of ADVs.

Although previous studies have reported the critical role of ICOSL in regulating CD4+ T cells and mediating antitumor effects, the specific mechanisms by which ICOSL modulates CD4+ T cells in oncolytic virotherapy remain unclear [25, 44, 46]. Existing evidence suggests that ICOS-ICOSL binding activates the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway to promote T-cell proliferation [24], that PI3K/Akt regulates downstream NF-κB signaling [35], and that NF-κB is a key transcriptional regulator of IL-2 [36]. Our study validated these pathways and revealed a complete signaling axis: ADV-ICOSL exerts its antitumor effect by activating the PI3K/Akt-NF-κB signaling axis in CD4+ T cells, increasing IL-2 secretion, which subsequently promotes CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity, ultimately resulting in potent antitumor efficacy. Our work thus provides new targets for enhancing cancer immunotherapy through ICOSL (Fig. 7).

In summary, our results not only identify a key mechanism limiting the efficacy of ADVs but also clarify the role and mechanism of ICOSL in ADV immunotherapy. Insights into the related molecular pathways provide targets for developing novel interventions targeting the TME. Accordingly, we developed the engineered virus ADV-ICOSL to increase ICOSL protein level in the TME. Given that existing research indicates that ICOSL expression in the TME can increase the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [37], we validated its combination therapy potential and clinical translation prospects. These findings provide new perspectives and solid experimental evidence for optimizing oncolytic virus design on the basis of costimulatory signals, potentially opening new avenues for treating CRC and other malignancies.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations in the current safety evaluation. First, the observation period was relatively short, which limits the assessment of long-term or immune-mediated toxicities. Second, comprehensive systemic cytokine profiling was not performed. Third, the humanized CDX/PDX models cannot fully recapitulate human immune regulation and potential toxic responses. Therefore, caution should be taken when extrapolating the safety profile and translational potential to clinical settings.

In addition, regarding mechanistic investigation, this study primarily focused on the effects of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, without an in-depth discussion of the role of DCs in the antitumor effect of ADV-ICOSL. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the contribution of other immune cell subsets, including dendritic cells, to the antitumor efficacy of ADV-ICOSL.

Conclusions

Our study identifies that the suboptimal therapeutic efficacy of conventional oncolytic adenoviruses is closely linked to ICOSL downregulation and impaired CD4+ T cell helper function. To address this limitation, we successfully engineered a novel oncolytic adenovirus, ADV-ICOSL, which remodels the TME by activating the PI3K/Akt-NF-κB–IL-2 signaling axis in CD4+ T cells. Notably, IL-2 acts in a non-contact-dependent manner to robustly boost the proliferation and cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells, a process strictly dependent on CD4 + T cells. While IL‑2 plays a key role in CD4⁺ T cell helper function, we acknowledge that ICOS signaling may regulate additional CD4⁺ T cell effector programs beyond IL‑2, which could also contribute to the observed antitumor efficacy. Furthermore, ADV-ICOSL exerts remarkable synergistic antitumor effects when combined with either anti-PD-1 immunotherapy or CAR-T therapy. In PDX and CDX models, ADV-hICOSL effectively enhances the infiltration of CD4+ T cells and GZMB+ CD8+ T cells, achieving potent tumor suppression with a favorable safety profile. Collectively, our findings establish ADV-ICOSL as a promising candidate for oncolytic virotherapy and provide a novel combinatorial strategy to improve the treatment outcomes of CRC.
