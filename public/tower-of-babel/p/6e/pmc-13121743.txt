Cascading geomorphic hazards triggered by fluvial floods in an anthropogenically-impacted beach
Authors: C. Arróspide, G. Aguilar, P. Lazo, P. Ojeda, V. Flores-Aqueveque, A. Garcés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Beach erosion represents a major hazard to coastal regions worldwide, yet the role of fluvial floods remains an understudied component of beach dynamics. Accounting for fluvial flooding is essential, as its neglect introduces biases and uncertainties in coastal zone management, particularly in complex, anthropized environments. We propose an integrated approach to investigate the relationship between fluvial floods and beach erosion along an anthropogenically impacted beach of the Atacama Desert in northern Chile. Bathymetric changes induced by flood events (2015 and 2017) were delimited on the beach formed by mining-related tailings accumulation (1938–1975). Interannual variations in beach erosion rates were quantified using satellite imagery by comparing post-flood conditions with the pre-flood period. In addition, a numerical model was implemented to assess the spatial variability of wave-driven erosion and to identify the main factors controlling the post-flood increase in erosion. Simulated erosion rates based on post-flood bathymetry are consistent with shoreline change rates derived from satellite data. We conclude that anthropogenic beach modification, combined with flood-triggered processes, generated a positive feedback cascade that led to a prolonged erosive phase lasting several years after the flood events.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39269-x.

Introduction

Natural and human-induced coastal changes represent a growing threat to coastal populations worldwide, particularly in regions exposed to extreme events. In recent decades, changes in shoreline position and beach erosion have intensified as a consequence of global climate change, whether due to global sea level rise or the increase in the frequency and magnitude of extreme hydrometeorological events such as fluvial floods1. At the river-coast interfaces, these events can trigger cascading geomorphological processes that drive beach erosion or amplify the impacts of fluvial floods. It has been well-documented that both the catastrophic impacts of floods and increases in beach erosion rates are strongly influenced by human-induced morphological and sedimentary changes at river-coast interfaces1,2. Therefore, quantifying and evaluating coastal changes and their driving mechanisms, alongside improved understanding of cascading geomorphic hazards related to extreme events and human activity, can provide valuable information for developing effective coastal risk management strategies.

A large proportion of the population in northern Chile resides along the coast, largely due to the arid climate of the Atacama Desert interior. This rocky coast is located on the Andean forearc, where active geodynamic processes generate multiple geomorphic hazards3, including earthquake-related subsidence, tsunamis, wave-driven erosion, landslides, cliff collapse, and fluvial floods. Among these, fluvial floods and wave erosion constitute the primary climate-related risks to which coastal infrastructure and real estate in northern Chile are most exposed. Climate change projections indicate an increase in the frequency and magnitude of fluvial floods and wave erosion, driven by enhanced storm intensity4–7. To develop more effective and resilient coastal management strategies, it is essential to consider both natural and human-induced processes that control geomorphic hazards and their associated impacts8,9.

Anthropogenic interventions have substantially altered environmental conditions along the rocky coast of northern Chile10,11. Coastal landscapes in this region have been affected by port, mining, aquaculture, seawater desalination, and wind energy developments. In this context, the coast of Chañaral serves as an emblematic example of long-term human alteration along this shoreline (Fig. 1). Artificial beaches have formed along this coast as a result of decades of mining waste accumulation12,13. From 1938 to 1975, mine tailings were discharged into Chañaral Bay through the El Salado River channel, leading to a severe mining-induced growth of the Playa Grande Pocket Beach (PGPB). Following the embankment of the bay, waste disposal was redirected northward. Between 1975 and 1990, continued tailings discharge resulted in the formation of another pocket beach along the rocky coast of Agua Hedionda Bay, located north of Chañaral Bay.

In 2015 and 2017, extreme fluvial flood events severely impacted the town of Chañaral and the PGPB14,15. Although these floods were short-lived events, they produced substantial morphological changes that may have influenced the subsequent evolution of the beach. This raises the following questions: Are the effects of fluvial floods amplified on this anthropogenically modified beach? Do flood-induced morphological changes lead to persistent, interannual changes in shoreline position and beach erosion rates beyond the duration of the flood events? To address these questions, we examined previous studies on the impact of fluvial floods and we conducted the following analyses: (1) analysis of bathymetric conditions before and after the fluvial flood events in Chañaral Bay; (2) assessment of interannual shoreline change rates to capture the medium-term post-flood adjustment of the beach using satellite imagery; and (3) numerical modeling of the spatial variability of beach erosion before and after the flood events.

Background information

Anthropogenic intervention

Chañaral Bay is located on the southern edge of the Atacama Desert in northern Chile (Fig. 1). The PGPB in Chañaral Bay is a notable case of anthropic intervention in the Pacific coastal area. The mining-induced accumulation of waste at the PGPB originated from tailings produced by the El Salvador and Potrerillos copper mines12. Between 1938 and 1975, continuous tailings were conducted through the El Salado River and their accumulation in the river mouth led to the advance of the beach out to sea at an average rate of 22 m/year13. After tailings disposal ceased in 1975, the beach began to retreat at a mean rate of -1.5 m/year. In contrast, high advance rates of around 2.3 m/year persist to the present on the artificial beaches of Agua Hedionda Bay, where tailings were discharged between 1975 and 1990.

Recent catastrophic fluvial floods

The El Salado River is an ephemeral system with negligible long-term sediment flux under natural conditions, particularly when compared to the volumes of mining tailings historically discharged into Chañaral Bay13. As a result, the present PGPB represents a sedimentary system overwhelmingly dominated by mining-derived material rather than by fluvial supply.

The March 2015 fluvial flood was triggered by exceptionally intense rainfall in the Andes, associated with a cutoff low of atmospheric systems, with total precipitation of 1 to 3 days exceeding 40–90 mm in the El Salado River basin. These values are several orders of magnitude higher than the regional annual mean16. Flow measurements in the town of Chañaral recorded peak discharges of up to 2,700 m3/s, with a total flood volume of 0.113 km317. Based on their high suspended sediment load and turbulent flow behavior, surges of this fluvial flood were classified as mudflows18. The mudflows that affected the PGPB and the Chañaral town were primarily caused by runoff-driven entrainment of channel infill within the El Salado River watershed19,20 (area of 7,530 km2). In May 2017, a second fluvial flood event, also linked to a cutoff low, affected the area. Unlike the 2015 event, rainfall in 2017 was concentrated at lower elevations within the Salado River watershed15. The 2015 flood event resulted in more than 30 fatalities, 16 missing persons, and affected about 164,000 people, while also causing extensive damage to critical infrastructure in northern Chile21,22.

The geomorphological impact of fluvial floods along the PGPB is illustrated on the map created in part with information compiled from previous works23,24 (Fig. 2). The map resumes the spatial extent of erosional and depositional features associated with the 2015 and 2017 fluvial flood events. Following the 2015 fluvial flood, extensive alluvial deposits were emplaced in the southern and central parts of the PGPB, whereas the geomorphological effects of the 2017 fluvial flood were restricted to the southern part of the beach, and no significant morphological changes related to the 2017 flood were detected in the rest of the beach. The 2015 alluvial deposits form a fan-shaped accumulation extending approximately 4 km along the beach and up to 1 km from the fan apex to the shoreline. The spatial distribution of these deposits indicates a flood-generated depositional feature prograding onto the beach surface. As a result, the southern and central sectors of the beach became largely covered by 2015 alluvial deposits. In the southern part, the alluvial deposits mainly consist of embanked mudflow material, reaching a thickness of up to 3.2 m at the interface between the El Salado River outlet and the pre-existing tailings deposits17,18.

Hydraulic and geological studies reported the formation and subsequent collapse of the mud embankment during the March 2015 flood event14,17,18. This collapse resulted in the erosion of two channels across the fan (Fig. 3A). The embankment redirected part of the flow northward, promoting overflow of mudflow material over tailing deposits in the central part of the beach. As a result, the central part was partially covered by alluvial sediments that reached the shoreline, with thickness reaching up to 1 m in the southern part of the beach17,18 and 0.5 m in the central part (Fig. 3B, C). In contrast, no substantial flood-related deposition was observed over tailing deposits in the northern part of the PGPB (Fig. 3D, E).

Ocean conditions and substrate composition

The northern Chilean coast corresponds to a wave-dominated, microtidal environment, characterized by a mean significant wave height of 1.93 m, semidiurnal tides, and a relative tidal range of 0.78 m, as derived from the CoastSat WebGIS (http://coastsat.wrl.unsw.edu.au/, accessed on November 3, 2024). Wave parameters from the Bahía Inglesa deepwater node (27°S, 72°W) of the Chile Wave Atlas25 were used to characterize the offshore mean wave climate near Chañaral bay (Fig. 1). The most frequent wave conditions are characterized by deep water wave heights (H0, m) between 1.8 and 2.4 m, a mean wave period (T, s) of ~ 8 to 9 s, and predominant wave propagation from the southwest. This wave climate drives a net northward longshore sediment transport.

The PGPB lies on top and at the back of a shore platform carved on the Paleozoic Chañaral Epimetamorphic Complex26. The thickness of PGPB is estimated to be 10–15 m covering an area of 4.5 km227,28. Eolian fine sand predominates on the top layer, while silty to clayey grain-size materials comprise deeper layers, including tailings. Quartz and feldspar are the most abundant minerals, while the carbonate content is low (less than 1%)12. The sediments lack biogenic components as the nearshore marine fauna disappeared with the disposal of tailings29. Layers of mud-flow deposits generated by the fluvial floods of 2015 cover the surface of the southern and central parts of the beach (Figs. 2 and 3). Overall, poorly sorted muddy sands comprise mudflows deposits18.

Methods

Bathymetry data

Bathymetry data for Chañaral Bay were collected and digitized from Nautical Charts N°2213, “Puerto Chañaral de las Animas”, and N°2215, “Bahía y Puerto Chañaral”, produced in 2002 and 2019, respectively, by the Hydrographic and Oceanographic Service of the Chilean Navy (SHOA in its Spanish acronym). The nautical charts were digitized into depth points using the QGIS software. Depth points from each chart were interpolated into digital elevation models (DEMs) with a uniform grid resolution of 10 × 10 m using the scattered data interpolation with multilevel B-spline techniques implemented in QGIS software. All depths used in this study are expressed as negative values below the still water level. Although both DEMs were georeferenced with metric precision, a co-registration procedure was applied to ensure accurate alignment before differencing.

Bathymetric changes were assessed through profile analysis and by differencing the 2019 DEM and the 2002 DEM. The resulting DEM of Difference (DoD 2019–2002) was used to quantify elevation changes attributable to deposition and erosion within Chañaral Bay. Bathymetric data from 2002 were considered representative of pre-flood conditions, while data from 2019 were used to represent post-flood conditions, as no bathymetric surveys were available immediately before and after each fluvial flood event. Finally, the bathymetry data were also used as input for the numerical model of beach erosion under pre-flood (2002-DEM) and post-flood (2019-DEM) conditions. The scales of the 2002 and 2019 nautical charts are 1:25.000 and 1:15,000, respectively, corresponding to a horizontal accuracy of approximately 2 m. Echo sounders used for bathymetric measurements have a vertical accuracy of ± 0.1 m. Overall, the data accuracy is sufficient for quantifying bathymetric changes over decadal timescales.

Shoreline monitoring

To detect shoreline position variations and calculate interannual shoreline change rates following fluvial flood events (2015 to 2023), time series of satellite imagery were processed using CoastSat (https://github.com/kvos/CoastSat). CoastSat is an open-source software toolkit written in Python that uses publicly available imagery from Google Earth Engine (GEE) to extract shorelines on beaches30. The instantaneous water line31 is used as shoreline indicators in the images. We used a total of 706 images acquired between 2015 and 2023 from the Landsat 7 and 8 satellite missions (30 m spatial resolution) and the Sentinel-2 mission (10 m spatial resolution). Shoreline changes were quantified along 102 shore-normal transects spaced at 50 m intervals and numbered from 1 to 102 from south to north (Fig. 1). Extracted shoreline positions were corrected for tidal effects using the global tidal model FES201432 and an average beach slope of 0.055 rad for PGPB7. Interannual rates of shoreline changes were calculated as the slope of the linear trend fitted to the shoreline position time series and are expressed in meters per year. The post-flood shoreline change rates derived in this study were compared with interannual shoreline change before the 2015 fluvial flood, covering the period from 1997 to 2014, based on the time series reported by [7] and obtained from CoastSat WebGIS (http://coastsat.wrl.unsw.edu.au/). This series uses the same procedures to evaluate shoreline changes as in our analysis, but considers 51 transects spaced at 100 m intervals, corresponding to the paired transects used in our analysis (Fig. 1). Shoreline change data are provided in Supplementary Table A for the post-fluvial floods calculated in this work and Supplementary Table B for the pre-fluvial flood period extracted from CoastSat WebGIS.

Numerical model

To evaluate alongshore variations in erosion of the PGPB before and after the fluvial floods, a simplified numerical model was applied33. The model is based on a wave-ray-tracking approach and parametrizations of wave breaking that incorporate bathymetric planform and cross-shore geometry. It calculates: (1) the location of the breaking point, the wave force at breaking (Fb; kg/m2), and the width of the surf zone (Ws; m); and (2) the erosive force reaching the shoreline (Ew; kg/m2), which correspond to the wave force available for erosion at the surf-rock interface, and the average total erosion of the beach along each ray path (ET: backwearing plus downwearing). The complete method explanation and result data are provided in Supplementary Appendix A and Supplementary Table C for pre-flood conditions, and Supplementary Table D for post-flood conditions.

Table 1 summarizes the pre- and post-fluvial flood scenarios used in the numerical model, incorporating different bathymetric datasets (DEM-2002 and DEM-2019) and Emin values (kg/m2). This parameter represents a threshold erosive force term that accounts for both the substrate resistance to erosion34 and the fact that not all incoming waves possess sufficient energy to overcome this resistance. Given that the shoreline of Chañaral Bay comprises sandy beach deposits and bedrock exposed in headlands, a range of resistance values was considered, following [33] based on [35] and [36]: 0 kg/m2, 20 kg/m2, 100 kg/m2, and 200 kg/m2.

Results

Bathymetric change generated by fluvial floods

Overall, the difference map reveals a nearshore zone parallel to the shoreline in which water depth increased following the fluvial floods. The width of this zone varies alongshore, ranging from approximately 300 m in the southern part to up to 600 m in the central and northern parts.

The magnitude of deepening also increases northward. In the southern part, maximum deepening reaches approximately -3 m, whereas values of up to -4 m are observed in the central part and up to -6 m in the northern part. This near-shore deepening indicates the formation of a shore-parallel trough associated with post-flood erosion, a feature that was not present prior to the fluvial flood events. Seaward to the trough, a decrease in offshore water depth is observed. In the southern part, this shoaling reaches up to 5 m, while in the central and northern parts, depth reductions do not exceed 2 m. This pattern suggests offshore sedimentation and the formation of a subtidal bar following the fluvial floods. Although located outside the beach environment, a decrease in water depth is also observed near the northern headland of Chañaral Bay. This feature may indicate northward longshore sediment transport and sediment accumulation against the headland following the fluvial flood events.

The spatial distribution of interannual shoreline change rates reveals a clear alongshore segmentation of the PGPB into southern, central, and northern parts (Fig. 5). The southern part of the beach exhibits a remarkable growth rate of up to 28 m/year. The areas with the most significant growth are concentrated near the mouth of the El Salado River and coincide with transects that intersect channels excavated during the mud embankment collapse in 2015. In contrast, the central and northern parts show persistent shoreline retreat during the post-flood period, with rates of up to − 3.4 and − 5.2 m/year, respectively. Throughout the analyzed period, there is a pattern of alternating high and moderate retreat rates along the central and northern parts of the PGPB. Overall, the retreat trend persisted in the central-northern parts following the fluvial flood in May 2017, while the growth trend in the southern part was interrupted by a retreat episode (Fig. 5).

Comparison of our results with pre-flood interannual shoreline change rates for the period 1997—2014, extracted from the CoastSat WebGIS (Fig. 5), highlights a strong contrast in alongshore behavior. Before 2015, shoreline change rates were relatively homogeneous along the beach, whereas the post-flood period is characterized by increased spatial variability, with a clear divergence between growth in the southern part and erosion in the central and northern parts (Fig. 5). That is, a marked contrast is evident in the southern part of the beach, where the retreat trend before the 2015 fluvial flood changes to a phase of strong beach growth after the flood event, which coincides with the part of the beach affected by fluvial erosion during the 2015 flood event. On the contrary, the rate of beach retreat increases in the central and northern parts following the 2015 fluvial flood. Wave energy and beach resistance variability

This section evaluates the sensitivity of modeled beach erosion to changes in bathymetry and substrate resistance under both pre-flood and post-flood conditions, to identify the primary factors controlling alongshore variability in erosion rates at the PGPB. Results are presented for the sandy pocket beach, while model outputs for adjacent rocky shore platforms are provided in the Supplementary Appendix B.

Changes in surf zone geometry and depth distributions are reflected in the distribution of wave breaking points along the beach (Figs. 6 and 7A, C). Under post-flood bathymetric conditions, wave breaking occurs systematically closer to the shoreline compared to pre-flood conditions. This shoreward shift of wave breaking indicates an increase in the proportion of wave energy reaching the shoreline after the flood-induced modification of nearshore bathymetry.

Modeled surf zone widths (Ws) display marked alongshore variability and systematic differences between pre-flood (2002) and post-flood (2019) bathymetric conditions (Fig. 6). For all wave regimes, Ws values calculated using the post-flood bathymetry are consistently narrower than those obtained with the pre-flood bathymetry. This reduction in surf zone width is observed along the entire beach but is most pronounced in the northern sector of the central part, where the difference in depths between the two bathymetric datasets is higher (Fig. 4).

Modeled average erosion rates derived from wave energy inputs show a general increase under post-flood bathymetric conditions (Fig. 7B, D). For scenarios with low substrate resistance, erosion rates increase markedly after flooding across most of the beach. This increase is most evident in the central part, where post-flood erosion rates significantly exceed pre-flood values by a ratio of three to four on average (Table 3). When higher substrate resistance values are considered, the magnitude of modeled erosion decreases for both pre-flood and post-flood conditions. However, even in more resistant conditions, wave energy reaching the shoreline may encompass erosion in post-flood conditions (Fig. 7D), which is not possible to observe in pre-flood conditions, where erosion is only possible under low resistance values (Fig. 7B). In addition, the relative difference between post-flood and pre-flood erosion rates remains significant under higher resistance scenarios. Increasing substrate resistance partially offsets the enhanced wave energy reaching the shoreline but does not fully counteract the effect of bathymetric change. This interaction is reflected in the ratios of post-flood to pre-flood average erosion rates reported in Table 3, which decrease with increasing substrate resistance but remain greater than one in most scenarios.

Discussion

The amplified catastrophic effect of the fluvial flood

From 1938 to 1975, mine tailings were discharged into Chañaral Bay through the Salado River channel, resulting in a severe mining-induced growth of the PGPB. This long-term accumulation of tailings substantially modified the coastal morphology at the river mouth, producing an embankment that significantly altered flood dynamics during extreme hydrological events. During the 2015 fluvial flood, this tailings-induced embankment acted as a levee, constraining mudflows and forcing both northward and southward overflows across the PGPB. This behavior is consistent with hydraulic modeling of the 2015 flood, which identified a levee effect at the boundary between the natural beach and the tailings deposits17. The southward overflow affected the Chañaral town, whereas the northward overflow covered large portions of the beach with mudflow deposits. This embankment subsequently collapsed, triggering an erosional phase during the 2015 fluvial flood that produced large undercuts within the tailing deposits. This erosional phase resulted in a shoreline retreat of up to 150 m to a position close to that existing before 1938, when mine tailings disposal began. However, littoral sand bars developed within the excavated channels following the 2015 flood. In contrast, the central and northern parts of the beach experienced only minor shoreline retreat during the 2015 fluvial flood event.

Overall, the results are consistent with a cascading geomorphic amplification of fluvial flood impacts associated with the mining-induced accumulation of tailings on the PGPB. The long-term deposition of tailings at the mouth of the El Salado River led to the formation of an artificial embankment, which modified the local topography and flow pathways. During the 2015 fluvial flood, this embankment was associated with lateral overflow of mudflows across the beach surface and subsequent erosion of the tailing deposits, as evidenced by the mapped distribution of alluvial deposits and channelized features (Fig. 8). Rather than demonstrating a dam structure in hydraulic terms, our observations indicate that tailings accumulation served as a flow-confining obstacle promoting embankment, lateral overflow, and subsequent undercutting during extreme discharge conditions. These sequential geomorphic responses are interpreted as a cascade of flood-related processes conditioned by prior anthropogenic modification of the coastal system.

In contrast, during the 2017 fluvial flood, neither embankment nor significant mudflow overflows were observed. Instead, the channels excavated during the 2015 flooding event served as preferential pathways allowing mudflows to reach the ocean more efficiently. Therefore, these channels functioned as effective sediment conduits, increasing flow confinement and limiting the re-establishment of embankment conditions. Although direct hydrodynamic measurements during flood events are not available, the spatial correspondence between tailing deposits, overflow features, post-flood erosional landforms, and channelized pathways supports this interpretation.

From a coastal management perspective, it is critical to preserve these channels leading to the ocean, as they mitigate the potential for renewed embankment and lateral overflow during future fluvial floods. However, at present, these channels are being severely impacted by human activities and sedimentary dynamics, including sediment accretion and the formation of coastal sand bars, which partially or completely obstruct them and form wetlands (Fig. 8). Such channel infilling may restore embankment conditions, but it may also increase flood-related coastal hazards in future events.

The effects of fluvial floods on long-term beach erosion

In some coastal settings, fluvial floods are commonly assumed to promote beach accretion by delivering large volumes of sediment to the coastal zone37,38. However, the results obtained at the PGPB indicate that this intuitive response does not necessarily apply to beaches of anthropogenic origin that are sedimentary unbalanced or partially disconnected from their natural sediment supply at the catchment scale. In such systems, the effect of extreme fluvial events is not sustained sediment nourishment, but rather a perturbation of nearshore bathymetry by erosion during the event. This bathymetric perturbation modifies wave transformation processes and enhances wave-driven erosion for several years after the fluvial flood, constituting a cascading geomorphological process that drives long-term beach erosion.

The results indicate that the cascading geomorphological processes triggered by the 2015 fluvial flood drove significant variations in interannual shoreline change rates (Fig. 8). In the southern part of the beach, the pre-flood erosion trend is obscured by the sediment accretion that followed the extreme erosion caused by the fluvial flood. As a result, this part grows at an average rate of 7.2 m/year after the flooding. The numerical model used in this work is not designed to simulate sediment accretion or beach growth processes such as those observed in the southern segment of the beach after the fluvial floods. Therefore, it was not possible to evaluate the factors controlling this growth response of this beach, although it is most likely related to sediment accumulation within the undercuts generated by the river flood events.

In contrast, the central and northern segments exhibit a marked increase in shoreline retreat following the fluvial flood compared to pre-flooding conditions (Fig. 8). In the central part, the beach retreated at an average rate of -2 m/year after flooding, whereas in the northern part, the average retreat reached -3.5 m/year, with local values as high as -5.2 m/year in some specific areas. These sectors correspond to areas where flood-induced bathymetric deepening was greatest, suggesting a direct link between nearshore morphological change and enhanced erosion, which drove the increased beach retreat rates following the fluvial flood.

Model results indicate that post-flood bathymetric changes reduced surf zone width and promoted shoreward migration of wave breaking. This nearshore bathymetric reconfiguration increased the proportion of wave energy reaching the shoreline, resulting in a higher erosion potential under post-flood conditions. The magnitude and alongshore distribution of modeled erosion are consistent with the observed increase in interannual shoreline retreat rates (Tables 2 and 3), supporting the interpretation that bathymetric configuration represents a first-order control on post-flood beach erosion.

Pocket beaches may also experience rotational shoreline adjustments in response to variations in wave approach direction, which can contribute to alongshore variability in erosion and growth patterns39. However, in the PGPB, the close correspondence between zones of maximum erosion and areas of the largest bathymetric changes suggests that rotational effects alone cannot account for the observed post-flood erosion pattern. In this context, rotation could be interpreted as a secondary process superimposed on the first-order control exerted by flood-induced bathymetric changes.

The ratio between modeled average erosion rates before and after the flood is 4.4 (Table 3), which is higher than the observed ratios of 2.5 and 3.2 in the central and northern parts, respectively (Table 2). This discrepancy indicates that additional processes partially modulated the erosive response. The numerical model does not explicitly resolve these processes and is therefore inferred from the comparison between modeled and observed shoreline changes. One possible mechanism is a local increase in substrate resistance following flood-related deposition40–42. In arid coastal environments, fine-grained mudflow deposits can rapidly desiccate and develop a hardened surface crust, temporarily increasing their resistance to wave-driven erosion. In addition, part of the sediment eroded during the flood and deposited on the bay floor may subsequently accrete onto the beach43, a process that is also not represented in the model, contributing to a partial reduction in net erosion.

When increased substrate resistance is incorporated into the model, the agreement between measured and modeled erosion rates improves (a ratio of 3.4; Table 3), suggesting partial compensation for bathymetry-driven erosion. However, it is important to note that the numerical model does not explicitly simulate post-flood changes in sediment properties, such as consolidation, surface hardening of fine-grained deposits, or sediment accretion from the bay floor to the beach. Therefore, the inferred increase in substrate resistance should be interpreted as a plausible mechanism supported by model sensibility and spatial correspondence with observed flood deposits, rather than as a direct model output. Accordingly, the inferred increase in substrate resistance of the beach is consistent with the spatial distribution of alluvial deposits from the 2015 fluvial flood. Nevertheless, these compensating effects are secondary, whereas the increase in erosion potential associated with flood-induced bathymetric change represents a first-order control on the medium-term erosive response of the beach.

Conclusion

The direction of flows and sediment deposition during the 2015 and 2017 fluvial floods was strongly controlled by the spatial distribution of mine tailings that form the PGPB. During the 2015 fluvial flood, the tailings deposit acted as an embankment that constrained mudflows and promoted lateral overflow across the beach and toward the town of Chañaral. The subsequent collapse of this embankment triggered channel erosion in the southern part of the beach. In contrast, during the 2017 fluvial floods, these previously eroded channels acted as effective conduits, allowing sediments to be transported directly to the ocean and substantially reducing flood impacts within the town. Therefore, these channels play a key role in maintaining sediment connectivity between the river and the coastal system, representing a critical factor for reducing fluvial flood risk.

Overall, our results indicate that the artificial origin of the PGPB amplified fluvial flood hazards and promoted a cascade of geomorphological processes that generated positive feedback on their impacts on the coastal zone and the town of Chañaral. If the beach had remained in its natural state, without human intervention, the fluvial flood would have discharged into the bay rather than into the town of Chañaral, thereby preventing dozens of deaths and the destruction of the town.

In the southern part of the beach, coastal sedimentary dynamics promoted sediment accretion in the form of coastal bars within channels eroded during the 2015 fluvial flood. As a result, this part exhibits very high post-flood growth rates, reaching an average of 7.5 m/year, whereas prior to the flood, it experienced erosion comparable to the rest of the beach. In contrast, the central and northern parts of the beach show a significant increase in shoreline retreat rates following the 2015 fluvial flood, from average pre-flood values of approximately -0.8 to -2 m/year in the central part and from -1.1 to -3.5 m/year in the northern part.

The numerical model demonstrates that this increase in erosion rates is primarily controlled by post-flood bathymetric changes within the bay, which enhance wave energy reaching the shoreline. These bathymetric changes represent a first-order control on medium-term beach erosion following the fluvial flood. Nevertheless, an increase in substrate resistance and sediment availability after the flood, likely associated with flood-related sediment deposition, may partially counterbalance wave-driven erosion in the central and northern parts of the beach, acting as a secondary modulating factor.

Overall, the fluvial floods altered both erosion and sediment accretion patterns along the entire anthropogenically-impacted beach, with geomorphological impacts persisting for several years after the events. The most significant outcome is that extreme fluvial flooding triggered an erosive phase, affecting an artificial beach that had formed over decades due to the accumulation of mining waste. Beach erosion was driven both directly by fluvial processes during events, particularly in the southern part, and indirectly by flood-induced geomorphological changes, especially modifications to bay bathymetry that increased wave-driven erosion over subsequent years.
