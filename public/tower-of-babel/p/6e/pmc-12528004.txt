Impacts of Caregiver Stress on the Receptive Language Skills of Children With Language Impairment During the COVID‐19 Pandemic
Authors: Carrie Hutchins, Sonali Poudel, Sherine Tambyraja, Mary Beth Schmitt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe purpose of this study was to examine the extent to which ecological factors (e.g., COVID‐related caregiver stress and parental warmth) were related to the receptive language skills of children with language impairment (LI) during the COVID‐19 pandemic.MethodThirty‐five children with LI receiving speech therapy within US public schools and their families participated in this study. Standardised tests of receptive language were administered to measure children's language. Caregiver questionnaires were used to collect data on familial household experiences, parental stress, and parental warmth during the COVID‐19 pandemic.ResultsChildren with LI and their families experienced a range of adversities during the global pandemic. The frequency of experienced adversities positively correlated with COVID‐related caregiver stress levels. Regression analysis revealed that the average level of COVID‐related caregiver stress was the strongest predictor of child receptive language. Finally, parental warmth was found to marginally moderate the association between COVID‐related caregiver stress and receptive language.ConclusionsThese findings illustrate the importance of ecological factors, namely COVID‐related caregiver stress, on the receptive language skills of children with LI, particularly during times of crisis. We provide three specific considerations for future inclusive emergency response plans and current policy, particularly relevant to families of children with developmental disabilities.WHAT THIS PAPER ADDS What is already known on the subject Ecological factors are well‐documented to have an impact on child development and learning. Few studies have examined the role of environmental mechanisms, particularly caregiver stress, as they relate to children's language outcomes for children with developmental disabilities. COVID‐19 offered an opportunity to examine the influence of caregiver stress on child development, including receptive language outcomes, of which impacts are ongoing. What this paper adds to the existing knowledge This study demonstrated that caregivers of children with language impairment experienced variability in parental stress related to COVID‐19. Furthermore, COVID‐19‐related parental stress was significantly positively correlated with children's receptive language. However, parental warmth may be a protective mechanism for children with language impairment during times of high parental stress. What are the potential or actual clinical implications of this work? Implications and recommendations for future emergency response and current policy based on these findings are provided. Specifically, we explore familial protective factors, such as social support and access to resources. Introduction

A long‐standing body of research has consistently found positive and significant associations between aspects of the home environment and children's language development (e.g., Attig and Weinart 2020; Garrett‐Peters et al. 2016; Lecheile et al. 2020; Miser and Hupp 2012). Most of these studies have focused on children with typical language development and largely assert that it is the quantity and quality of language input in the home environment that relates to children's language skills. A smaller literature base has sought to understand the extent to which these associations are similar for children with language difficulties, specifically children with language impairment (LI). In this study, LI refers to children experiencing persistent challenges in language comprehension and production, which may be in the absence of a biomedical aetiology (i.e., children with developmental language disorder; Bishop et al. 2017) or in the presence of a biomedical aetiology (i.e., children with an associated language disorder; Bishop et al. 2017). Although the home environment is not causally related to LI, the scarce but available literature indeed suggests notable differences between the home environments of children with and without LI (e.g., Justice et al. 2016; Tambyraja et al. 2017) and that some children with LI experience fewer and less complex language input opportunities in their homes compared to their peers without LI (e.g., Blom et al. 2023).

It is possible, therefore, that significant changes within a child's home environment may in turn have an impact on these observed relations. The COVID‐19 pandemic was officially declared a global health emergency by the World Health Organization (World Health Organization, n.d.) and exerted unprecedented effects on families’ home environments as well as children's developmental, psychological, and academic outcomes (Adams et al. 2021; Baten et al. 2023; Maldonado and De Witte 2022). Although the long‐term impacts of the pandemic on children's outcomes are yet unknown, emerging evidence suggests that the stress that many families experienced during this time may have lasting impacts on children's development (e.g., Aviles et al. 2024; Dillmann et al. 2022). Indeed, data on the academic outcomes of children suggest that learning was stalled during the pandemic (National Center for Education Statistics 2022). Moreover, the academic, social‐emotional, and mental health gaps triggered during COVID‐19 persist, particularly for children with disabilities (Fuchs et al. 2023; Lewis and Kuhfeld 2023; Panchal et al. 2023). Relevant to the current study, the available research suggests that children's language learning environments and language skills were impacted by the pandemic (e.g., Mcgillion et al. 2023; Nevo 2023; Zuniga‐Montanez et al. 2024). Specific to children with LI, however, studies have primarily investigated the provision and efficacy of online therapy services for children with LI (Schmitt et al. 2022; Shahouzaie and Gholamiyan Arefi 2024; Yi et al. 2022). Because children with LI are susceptible to poorer or slower language learning compared to pre‐pandemic periods, pre‐existing barriers and COVID‐related household adversities may result in cumulative negative repercussions for the language outcomes of children with LI (Catts and Petscher 2022; Evans et al. 2013; Turney 2020).

As a first step to understanding the ways in which crisis events such as the COVID‐19 pandemic may impact the language skills of children with LI, the present study investigates the home environment of children with LI during the pandemic, and the extent to which specific aspects of the home environment were concurrently associated with their receptive language skills.

Bioecological Model of Human Development

The bioecological model of human development provides a theoretical framework that may help conceptualise how a global phenomenon such as the COVID‐19 pandemic could impact children's language development. The model maintains that human development is a function of individual biological capabilities, the environments they inhabit, and the dynamic and reciprocal interactions between these entities (Bronfenbrenner 1979; Merçon‐Vargas et al. 2020; Rosa and Tudge 2013). The nested environments that surround the developing human include the microsystem, the mesosystem, the exosystem, the macrosystem, and the chronosystem. The innermost layer is the microsystem, which includes children's immediate physical environments (e.g., home, school, classroom), the characteristics of significant individuals within the immediate environment (e.g., caregivers, teachers, peers), and the child's interactions with those significant individuals or proximal processes. The mesosystem consists of interactions between entities in the microsystem (e.g., between caregivers in the home and teachers in the school). The exosystem (e.g., caregivers’ workplace) can impact the child as a result of affecting those in their microsystem. The macrosystem pertains to cultural norms, societal influences, and the political environment, which during the COVID pandemic included lockdown, school closures, and mitigation measures. The chronosystem is the outermost layer and reflects the broader context of a child's environment, particularly the time period and historical events (e.g., a global pandemic). In this framework, the child is at the centre of these nested contexts and is variably but inevitably impacted by occurrences at any level of these environmental contexts. Research on child language development supports the bioecological model and highlights the importance of studying children's language within this multilevel contextualised system (Rowe and Weisleder 2020). The present study further tests the biological model by specifically examining the extent to which aspects of the home environment during COVID were associated with language skills in children with language impairment.

Importance of the Micro‐Context

Although each of the nested contexts may exert some level of influence on children's development, some data indicate that it is the micro context (e.g., physical home environment, quality and quantity of interactions within the home) that may have the most impact on developmental outcomes (Lundqvist and Sandström 2019; Merçon‐Vargas et al. 2020; Rosa and Tudge 2013). For example, there is a well‐established body of literature that has identified linkages between children's home environment (e.g., parents’ knowledge of child development, book reading practices, socioeconomic status, parent‐child interactions) and children's receptive language (e.g., Ece Demir‐Lira et al. 2019; Justice et al. 2019; Maguire et al. 2018; Martin et al. 2012; Rowe 2008; Vernon‐Feagans et al. 2012). The present study examines the relations between specific aspects of the micro‐context, specifically the home environment, that were impacted by the COVID‐19 pandemic and the language skills of children with LI, as well as protective factors that may have mitigated potential negative impacts.

Home Environmental Factors Impacted by the Pandemic

Household Adversities

Although the long‐term effects of the pandemic on children's developmental outcomes are yet unknown, some research suggests that stressful experiences, such as natural disasters, can have a significant impact on the home environment and children's development (Fonseca et al. 2016; Masten and Narayan 2012). Emerging evidence regarding changes in the home environment in the acute period following the global pandemic suggests that some households experienced household adversities and associated elevated levels of stress and trauma (Johnson et al. 2022; Kerr et al. 2021). Moreover, recent research also suggests that during COVID school closures, caregivers of children with LI experienced more challenges and observed greater negative impacts on their child's educational outcomes compared to caregivers of children without LI (Radville et al. 2024).

Broadly, household adversities are defined as negative experiences within children's homes that lead to toxic or maladaptive stress responses and have impacts on children's developmental outcomes (Claypool and Moore de Peralta 2021; Garner et al. 2021; Hoyniak et al. 2021). An experimental survey designed by the US Census Bureau revealed that during the height of the COVID‐19 pandemic, 52.1% of families reported loss of income, 99.4% indicated education changes, 12.1% reported food scarcity, and 40.1% experienced delays in healthcare (US Census Bureau 2024). A survey of 433 parents conducted by Adams et al. (2021) reported that the most impactful household adversities were changes in their children's daily structure and routines coupled with demands related to children's online schooling.

Covid‐Related Caregiver Stress

In addition to facing increased household adversities, there is also evidence that caregivers worldwide experienced increased psychological distress as they navigated the threatening and unpredictable circumstances of the COVID‐19 pandemic (Johnson et al. 2022; Kerr et al. 2021; Kracht et al. 2021; Yamamura and Tsustsui 2021). Parents across the board reported higher levels of depression and anxiety compared to pre‐pandemic (Johnson et al. 2022; Kracht et al. 2021). Parents’ perception of stress during the pandemic was related to both direct (e.g., diagnosed COVID‐19) and indirect (e.g., social media) exposure to COVID‐19‐related events (Bridgland et al. 2021).

Furthermore, some studies suggest that experienced adversities and stress levels were higher for families of children with disabilities (Constantino et al. 2020; Houtrow et al. 2020) compared to families of non‐disabled children. Although there is some evidence that caregiver stress during the pandemic was related to child outcomes, specifically externalising behaviour in typically developing children (Giannotti et al. 2020; Romero et al. 2020; Spinelli et al. 2020), associations between COVID‐related caregiver stress and children's language skills during the pandemic have not been well‐studied. Such investigations are warranted because evidence from research on child language development shows that caregiver stress plays a germane role in children's language outcomes (Justice et al. 2019; Markfeld et al. 2023). In a study, caregiver stress indirectly impacted children's receptive and expressive language scores via the role of caregiver language input. In another study (Justice et al. 2019), parental distress (stress related to parenting) was indirectly related to children's expressive and receptive language skills via the mediating role of parent‐child interaction quality.

Protective Factors

Notably, not every child who experiences household adversities is at a disadvantage or encounters the same degree of harm (Masten et al., 2014; Brown et al. 2013; Tso et al. 2022). Rather, children who have certain assets or resources within themselves (e.g., child characteristics) and within their microsystems (parent‐child relationships) often exhibit resilience, or the ability to adapt to and recover from a disturbance (e.g., Masten, 2014). For example, factors such as children's cognitive ability, prosocial skills, and caregiver health have been shown to promote children's resilience in the face of adversities (Brown et al. 2013; Yoon, 2018).

Emerging evidence concurs that certain aspects of children's home environments may have promoted resiliency and buffered children from the harmful effects of adversities incurred during COVID‐19. Specifically, these studies reveal the importance of strong family relationships (Prime et al. 2020; Tso et al. 2022) and socioemotional resilience (Marks et al. 2023) as protective factors for children's outcomes. While research is beginning to identify characteristics and the role of microcontextual factors, there is a paucity of evidence examining these effects for children with developmental disabilities. Thus, the current study aims to explore one aspect of the micro context—parental warmth—and its protective role on the language outcomes of children with LI during the COVID pandemic.

Parental Warmth

Parental warmth consists of behaviours that demonstrate parental support, affection, and attunement to their child's emotional and behavioural cues. A wealth of evidence suggests that parental warmth has direct and indirect effects on children's early language outcomes (Barnett et al. 2012; Mistry et al. 2004; Pungello et al. 2009). For example, Mistry et al. (2004) reported a strong correlation between maternal positive regard and affection and children's verbal comprehension at 3 years of age. Similarly, Pungello et al. (2009) found that children whose mothers responded warmly to their communicative bids and showed affection and enjoyment during a play episode at 12 months of age displayed a faster rate of receptive language development from 18 months to 2 years of age. Furthermore, parental warmth is shown to be a longitudinal predictor of receptive vocabulary in children with language delays (Hudson et al. 2015; Levickis et al. 2014) and a buffer for children's language outcomes in the presence of financially adverse conditions (Raviv et al. 2004).

Specific to the chrono context of the COVID‐19 pandemic, evidence suggests that a history of parental warmth prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic predicted less severe externalised behaviours for adolescents during the COVID‐19 pandemic (Skinner et al. 2021). In their study, Skinner et al. (2021), parental warmth, as measured by adolescent report, played an attenuating role on behavioural symptoms. While no prior research has explored the role of parental warmth as a mediator for child language outcomes of children with LI during the pandemic, pre‐pandemic research suggests that such a protective role may be plausible for this population (Girolametto et al. 1999; Hudson et al. 2015; Levickis et al. 2014; Zauche et al. 2016). Considered together, substantial evidence demonstrates the potential of parental warmth as a protective mechanism against the cascading effects of the global pandemic.

Study Rationale and Research Aims

Taken together, and in accordance with the bioecological model of human development, ample evidence suggests that the COVID‐19 pandemic impacted aspects of the home environment important for children's language skills, specifically household adversities and COVID‐related caregiver stress. However, the extant research does not include children with developmental and language disabilities, whose language skills may be particularly vulnerable to changes in the home environment. For example, Law et al. (2013) found that children with disabilities tend to engage in ‘less complex’ home activities (e.g., watching TV) than their typically developing peers. Considering that children with disabilities may experience fewer interactions in the home and that home interactions are directly related to language development (e.g., Nix 2013), changes in the home environment may be particularly important.

As such, the first aim of this study is to describe the home environment of children with LI throughout the prolonged period of the COVID‐19 pandemic. A growing body of evidence also indicates that COVID‐related household adversities had negative impacts on children's emotional and behavioural regulation, mental health and family relationships within the home (Cassinat et al. 2021; Fosco et al. 2022; Gordon‐Hacker et al. 2022; Spinelli et al. 2020), but these studies have not examined potential associations with children's language skills. Thus, the second aim of this study is to examine the extent to which household adversity and COVID‐related caregiver stress during the COVID‐19 pandemic related to the receptive language skills of children with LI. Finally, adding to the growing body of research investigating protective factors in children's home environment, the final aim of this study is to determine the extent to which parental warmth may have been a protective factor within the home environment for the language skills of children with LI. Findings from this study will provide crucial insights into the role of ecological factors on children with LI during the pandemic and will inform policies and clinical interventions to best serve families of children with developmental disabilities, including LI.

Methods

Project Overview

This research is one component of a larger intervention study investigating optimal treatment intensity for young children with language impairment. To ensure safety and maintain feasibility amidst the COVID‐19 pandemic, the parent project, funded by the National Institutes on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders, was redesigned to operate fully remotely. All study procedures were approved by the University of Texas at Austin Internal Review Board. Due to the ongoing nature of this research, data are not publicly available. This study was not preregistered.

Eligibility Criteria

To be eligible for participation, children had to meet four criteria: (1) speak English as their primary language, (2) receive speech‐language therapy in their public school, (3) be between the ages of 5:0 and 8:11 and (4) have treatment goals addressing receptive and/or expressive language. Eligibility was established based on information provided by caregivers (e.g., school‐based evaluations and Individual Education Plans).

Recruitment

Multiple methods were used to recruit families, including referrals from school‐based speech language pathologists (SLPs), online flyers distributed to schools, physical flyers displayed in the community, social media posts on the Children's Language, Literacy, and Learning Lab's page (@UTCL3lab), and advertisements on websites of organizations that assist families of children with developmental language impairment (e.g., DLD and Me: https://DLDandme.org; RADLD: Raising Awareness of Developmental Language Disorder: https://radld.org). Families communicated their interest and confirmed eligibility through e‐mail, telephone, or completing a Qualtrics survey. Prior to consenting for participation, an online orientation session with a research staff member was provided. This session informed potential participants of study requirements, benefits, and potential risks. Access to technology (e.g., internet connection and IPAD) required for participation was provided to families, if needed. Families who successfully completed the orientation and three baseline assessment sessions were compensated for their time and effort.

Participants

Participants included 43 children (M age: 6.03, Range: 4.9–8.9, 42% female), whose caregivers completed three questionnaires between December 2020 and 11 May 2023, coinciding with the World Health Organization declaration of the global emergency for COVID‐19. Questionnaires were completed by caregivers, of which 89.5% were mothers. According to parent report, 10 children were exposed to languages other than English in their home, including Akan, ASL, Cambodian, French, Hindi, Spanish, and Tamil. Per parent report, all children spoke English as their primary home and educational language. Total family income was reported categorically, with a median between $65 001 and $75 0001 annually. Maternal education level ranged from high school diploma to doctoral degree, with the median earning a bachelor's degree (n = 14, 36.8%). Race and ethnicity were reported by study participants and included White (n = 27, 71.1%), Black (n = 6, 15.8%), Asian (n = 2, 5.3%), and Other/unreported (n = 3, 7.8%). All children in the study were identified with language impairment by their attending school, as reported by caregiver and verified through receipt of Individual Educational Plan. Researcher‐administered measures were used solely for analyses, not diagnostic purposes. Sociodemographic statistics are detailed in Table 1.

General Procedures

Due to the COVID‐19 health emergency, all study procedures, including recruitment, obtaining consent, administering questionnaires, conducting assessments and implementing the intervention, were conducted remotely. After obtaining supplemental funding, we were able to provide cellular‐capable tablets and internet connections to families as needed, ensuring uninterrupted access to the study.

After consenting to participation, families participated in an online orientation session with a research staff member to review the study purpose, answer questions, and learn required online tools (e.g., annotations and volume control). Following orientation, children were scheduled for three testing sessions (i.e., baseline testing). Each testing session lasted approximately 45–75 min. Testing was divided into three sessions to reduce fatigue and improve engagement.

All measures were administered by research assistants (RAs) who had completed a multistage training process consisting of ethical practices for working with human subjects and standardised testing procedures. In response to COVID‐related study modifications, RAs were also trained in videoconferencing and virtual test administration. RAs were required to achieve and sustain a minimum of 80% accuracy in test administration fidelity.

Caregiver questionnaires were divided into three online 10‐ to 15‐min surveys. Surveys were distributed sequentially, in the same order for all participants. Each survey collected information about the child and their environment, encompassing demographic information, COVID‐related experiences, and aspects of the caregiver‐child relationship.

Technology

In adherence to COVID safety protocols, the caregiver orientation and three assessment sessions were conducted online using a university‐approved and secure Zoom video conference. Authorisation was obtained from PRO‐ED Publishing, Inc., and standardised measures were administered using digital examiner manuals and picture books in accordance with virtual protocols outlined by the publishers via the RedShelf platform (https://redshelf.com/). Assessment methodologies involved screen sharing and annotation features (see Schmitt et al. 2022). The primary adaptation to standardised testing procedures involved annotation during receptive language and nonverbal cognition tasks. For these, participants were instructed to ‘Place your stamp on ______’ rather than ‘Point to ______’ (see Schmitt et al. 2022 for a detailed description of testing procedures).

Measures

Direct child language measures involved remote administration of standardised receptive language and nonverbal cognition assessments. Caregiver questionnaires were completed to measure aspects of home environment, COVID‐related experiences, and parental warmth. All study measures are presented in Table 1 and detailed below.

Language and Nonverbal Cognitive Measures

Language. For the current study, receptive language was used as the language outcome measure as it has been found to be more vulnerable to caregiver stress (e.g., Roberts and Kaiser 2015). To measure children's receptive language skills, two subtests of the Test of Language Development—Primary, Fifth Edition (TOLD‐P:5; Newcomer and Hammill 2019) were administered (i.e., Picture Vocabulary and Syntactic Understanding). During receptive language tasks, modifications were implemented for virtual administration. Specifically, during the Picture Vocabulary subtest, children were instructed to ‘place a stamp on’ one of four picture options that best represented a word orally presented by the examiner. Similarly, during the Syntactic Understanding subtest, children were instructed to ‘place a stamp on’ (i.e., annotate by inserting an icon on their picture choice) one of three picture options that best represented a sentence spoken by the examiner. Participant responses for all subtests were scored as correct (1) or incorrect (0). Testing continued until children reached a ceiling (i.e., three consecutive incorrect responses). Based on precedence from prior studies that examined the relation between language and household experiences (e.g., Justice et al. 2019; Martin et al. 2012; Vernon‐Feagans et al. 2012), we combined the Picture Vocabulary and Syntactic Understanding subtests to comprise the Listening Composite Score. This composite score was used for all subsequent analyses and is referred to as Child Language. The TOLD‐P:5 Listening Composite demonstrates high levels of internal consistency (0.89), test‐retest reliability (0.89), and inter‐scorer reliability (0.99).

Nonverbal Intelligence. The Primary Test of Nonverbal Intelligence (PTONI; Ehrler and McGhee 2008) was administered to measure children's nonverbal reasoning abilities. The PTONI consists of minimal verbal instructions and requires a nonverbal (i.e., point) response. This measure was selected due to its strong utility for young children with documented language weaknesses. This test was adapted for virtual administration by instructing children to ‘put a stamp on’ the picture that does not belong when presented with a series of images. Responses are recorded as either correct (1) or incorrect (0), and testing concludes when the child reaches a ceiling (i.e., five incorrect responses out of seven consecutive items). Standard scores on the PTONI ranged from 51 to 142 (M = 100.33, SD = 24.22). The reported internal consistency of the PTONI is 0.93, and interscorer reliability is 0.99.

Measures of Household Adversity

To measure the construct of household adversity experienced during the COVID‐19 pandemic, a caregiver questionnaire was created from the COVID Families Study Survey (CFS; Schmeer et al. 2023). The COVID questionnaire was distributed to caregivers via Qualtrics and consisted of questions that contributed to two composite scores: Experienced Adversities and COVID‐related Caregiver Stress.

Experienced Adversity. Experienced Adversity was derived by summing caregivers’ responses to 21 yes/no queries pertaining to employment, income and available resources during the pandemic (e.g., ‘Have you or another family member contracted COVID?’; see Table 2). Reliability of the Experienced Adversity scale for families who answered all 21 questions (n = 13) was satisfactory (Cronbach's α = 0.845). Three items were not experienced by participants and were removed from analysis: ‘Have you experienced loss of housing?’ ‘Have additional family or friends moved into your home as a result of losing housing?’ and ‘Do you have difficulty accessing technology or the internet?’

COVID‐related Caregiver Stress. COVID‐related Caregiver Stress was evaluated using items from the COVID questionnaire that inquired about perceived level of stress in response to each experienced adversity. Following their response to whether they had experienced an adverse event, caregivers rated their corresponding degree of stress by answering, ‘How much stress has this caused?’ Responses were categorised as follows: 0 (no stress), 1 (a little stress), 2 (some stress), and 3 (a lot of stress). Responses from the 18 stress items were summed and averaged, with higher scores indicating greater levels of stress. Because parents only answered stress‐related questions if they acknowledged experiencing the adverse event, there was insufficient data to test the reliability of this scale.

Measure of Parental Warmth

Parental Warmth was assessed using the Parent Feelings Questionnaire (PFQ; Deater‐Deckard 1996). The PFQ Parent Positivity subscale is an 11‐item questionnaire that consists of statements regarding parental feelings toward their child. For example, it includes items such as, ‘When I think about this child, it usually gives me warm feelings’. Each statement is rated on a 5‐point Likert‐type scale ranging from 1 (definitely untrue for me) to 5 (definitely true for me). Item scores were averaged to yield the Parent Positivity Subscale, which demonstrated satisfactory internal reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.780, n = 43).

Data Analysis

As an initial step to the study analyses, descriptive statistics were conducted for all study variables in R Studio (R Core Team 2019; Table 2). Apart from the two demographic variables, Total Income and Caregiver Education Level, all study variables were normally distributed. Independent variables in the sample had minimal missing data. Family Income, Experienced Adversities, and COVID‐related Caregiver Stress each had three missing values (6.98% of total data). The variable of interest for the interaction analyses, Parental Warmth, had one missing value (2.32% of total data). The outcome variable, Receptive Language, had no missing values. All subsequent analyses excluded cases with missing data.

To address the first study aim, we assessed descriptive statistics of the two indicators of household adversity: Experienced Adversities and COVID‐related Caregiver Stress. These variables were used to describe the home environment of children with language impairment during a crisis event, namely the COVID‐19 pandemic.

The second aim of this study investigate the extent to which household adversities during the pandemic were associated with Receptive Language. For this aim, we initially conducted bivariate correlations among all study variables using the Spearman Rank Correlation test. This test was selected to accommodate non‐normal distributions of Family Income, Parental Warmth and Caregiver Education. Caregiver Education Level was not significantly correlated to any study variables and was excluded from subsequent analyses. Two candidate multivariate regression models were fitted to test whether caregiver stress alone or caregiver stress combined with frequency of adverse events provided a more accurate prediction of Receptive Language. Both models included family income as a control variable, based on significant correlations with caregiver stress and child receptive language level. Considering theoretical precedence and empirical evidence suggesting a strong correlation between caregiver stress and child outcomes, particularly during the COVID‐19 pandemic (e.g., Fong and Iarocci 2020; Russell et al. 2020; Thibodeau‐Nielsen et al. 2021), testing a model excluding COVID‐related Caregiver Stress was deemed inappropriate in this analysis. The coefficient of determination (R 2) and Akaike information criterion (AIC), which are statistical measures of model fit, were used to determine the most parsimonious model for predicting child language.

The final research aim was to assess the extent to which parental warmth, an aspect of caregiver‐child relationships, buffered negative effects of adverse household experiences and caregiver stress on receptive language abilities of children with language impairment during the COVID‐19 pandemic. For this aim, we conducted a regression model using an interaction of COVID‐related Caregiver Stress and Parental Warmth.

Results

Research Aim 1: Describe the Home Environment of Children With Language Impairment During the COVID‐19 Pandemic

To describe the home environments of children with LI during the COVID‐19 pandemic, we conducted descriptive analyses on caregiver‐reported measures of household experiences, namely Experienced Adversities and COVID‐related Caregiver Stress. Families encountered an average of 5.68 adversities (n = 40; SD = 3.55, range: 0–14), such as loss or change in income source, contracting COVID, and challenges managing work, home, and caregiving demands. Research Aim 2: To what Extent Does Household Adversity Relate to the Receptive Language Skills of Children With Language Impairment During the COVID‐19 Pandemic?

To investigate associations between household adversity experienced during the COVID‐19 pandemic and language skills of children with LI, we first ran zero‐order correlations among study variables using Spearman Rank Correlation Tests (Table 3). Notably, we discovered a significant negative correlation between COVID‐related Caregiver Stress and Receptive Language (r = 0.41, p < 0.01). Experienced Adversities was not significantly correlated with child language.

The two measures of home environment, namely COVID‐related Caregiver Stress and Experienced Adversities, had a significant positive correlation (r = 0.45, p < 0.001). Among demographic variables, there was a significant positive correlation between Family Income and Receptive Language (r = 0.39, p < 0.05) and a significant negative correlation between Family Income and COVID‐related Caregiver Stress (r = −0.45, p < 0.01). Therefore, all subsequent analyses retained Family Income as a control variable. Caregiver Education Level was not significantly associated with any other study variables and was removed from further analyses.

Given the significant correlations, we were interested in examining whether aspects of the home environment during the COVID pandemic, specifically quantity of adverse events and level of caregiver stress, may be predictive of receptive language. To accomplish this, we ran regression analyses on two candidate models as outlined in the analysis plan. The best‐fitting statistical model included COVID‐related Caregiver Stress as the predictor variable with Total Income as the covariate (F (2, 34) = 4.19, p < 0.05, AIC = 233.69). This model explained 15% of the variance in Receptive Language (R2 = 15.1) and revealed that for every point increase in COVID‐related Caregiver Stress, Receptive language decreased by 3.1 standard score points (β = −3.14, p < 0.048). Regression coefficients and parameter estimates of the multivariate regression analysis are presented in Table 4. Assumptions of linear regression residuals were satisfied based on tests for independence of errors (Durbin‐Watson test p = 0.012), normality (Shapiro‐Wilk test p = 0.27), and constancy, as confirmed by visual inspection of the quantile‐quantile probability plot.

Research Aim 3: To what Extent Does Parental Warmth Moderate the Relation Between Household Adversity and Receptive Language?

As a preliminary step to our final research aim, we conducted descriptive statistics on the moderator variable, Parental Warmth. Most caregivers reported high levels of parental warmth on the Parent Feelings Questionnaire (M = 4.63, Range = 3.64–5.00) and endorsed statements such as, ‘When I think about my child, it usually gives me warm feelings.’

To examine whether parental warmth buffered the negative relation between caregiver stress levels and language skills in children with LI during the COVID pandemic, we performed moderation analyses. The fitted model included the interaction of COVID‐related Caregiver Stress and Parental Warmth as the predictor variable, Family Income as a covariate, and Receptive Language as the outcome variable (F (4, 31) = 4.17, p < 0.005). Including the moderation term in the model explained an additional 20% of variance in Receptive Language (R2 = 0.35). The interaction between COVID‐related Caregiver Stress and Parental Warmth was marginally positively significant (β = 7.60, p = 0.06; Table 5, Figure 1). To further explore moderation effects, Parental Warmth was examined at both high (+1 SD, >4.63) and low (−1 SD, < 4.63) levels. Notably, children whose parents reported higher levels of parental warmth experienced nearly complete moderation of caregiver stress on their receptive language skills (β = 5.57, p = 0.48). Conversely, children whose parents reported lower levels of parental warmth did not experience a comparable moderation effect on their receptive language skills (β = 31.01, p = 0.02). Said in another way, higher levels of parental warmth emerged as a significant protective factor for children's receptive language, particularly in the presence of caregiver stress associated with the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to characterise household environments of children with language impairment (LI) during the global COVID‐19 pandemic and assess associations with children's receptive language. While families of children with disabilities have received limited attention in pandemic‐related literature, developmental disabilities, such as LI, are highly prevalent. Moreover, this population was more susceptible to COVID‐19 challenges due to predisposed vulnerabilities, including concomitant diagnoses (e.g., ADHD and dyslexia), delayed literacy development (Catts et al. 2002), reduced academic success (Ziegenfusz et al. 2022), social difficulties (St Clair et al. 2011), and diminished caregiver well‐being (Bonifacci et al. 2016). Therefore, this study fills an immediate and critical gap in the role of ecological factors on receptive language and emergency response literature.

To address the current study's aims, we initially described the home environments of children with LI pertaining to COVID‐related adverse experiences and caregiver stress responses. Next, we investigated the extent to which adverse household experiences were associated with children's receptive language. Finally, we evaluated the role of the caregiver‐child relationship in protecting children's language development against negative associations with caregiver stress. These research aims were positioned within the bioecological framework of human development, which posits that distal phenomena, such as a global health crisis, bidirectionally impact proximal processes such as home environment and collectively influence developmental outcomes. Specifically, we examined associations between children's receptive language and caregiver stress (a component of the microsystem) during the COVID‐19 pandemic (a component of the macrosystem). Each of the study's findings and implications is discussed in turn.

Household Experiences During the COVID‐19 Pandemic in Children With LI

Regarding the home environments of families of children with LI, our results recount a challenging period for families, with only 5% of our study population escaping COVID‐related adversities. Families experienced an average of 5.67 adverse events (SD = 3.55, Range: 0–14), with the most common being COVID contraction, loss or change of work, and increased caretaking responsibilities. This was not surprising given that 77% of schools transitioned some or all classes to remote (U.S. Education in the Time of COVID, n.d.), impacting more than 55.1 million children and their caregivers. Indeed, 60.5% of families identified increased caretaking responsibilities as their most prevalent adversity. Interestingly, family income level was not found to buffer families’ experience of adversities during the pandemic. Adding to the growing body of literature, our findings suggest that balancing work, home and caretaking responsibilities was a predominant obstacle amongst families with and without children with disabilities (e.g., Michelson et al. 2021; Patrick et al. 2020; Radville et al. 2024).

While it was not within the scope of the current study, existing research suggests that although types of adversity experienced may be universal, the frequency and severity of experiences during crisis events may have an additive and disproportionate effect on caregivers of children with disabilities. The available literature recounts a crisis response with students with disabilities receiving little or insufficient support for virtual learning (Averett 2021; Root et al. 2023; Shapiro and Bassok 2022; Steed et al. 2022), including speech‐language therapy. These data suggest that experiences of caregivers at the intersectionality of crisis events and disability are unique and require a tailored response (Houtrow et al. 2020). These unique experiences are demonstrated by a recent study that investigated both types and frequencies of challenges reported by caregivers of children with language impairment (i.e., Developmental Language Disorder; a neurobiological condition impacting language understanding and use without a known aetiology) and typically developing language caregivers. Despite similar types of challenges (e.g., time constraints), caregivers of children with language impairment reported a higher frequency of challenges than caregivers of children without language disorders (78.26% and 36.36%, respectively; Radville et al. 2024).

Variability in Caregiver Stress. Considering caregiver stress, participants reported mild to moderate levels of stress on average (M = 6.83); however, stress levels varied widely among families (Range: 0–21). For example, two caregivers reported that their household experienced 10 total adverse COVID‐related events. One rated their level of subsequent stress considerably below the mean, whereas the other faced a relatively high level of stress. These findings corroborate with extant literature on self‐reported stress of caregivers of disabled children during the pandemic, which varied from high (Prime et al. 2020) to mild‐moderate (Dhiman et al. 2020).

Variability in caregiver stress may be related to the phase during which data was collected with families potentially having higher stress during acute stages of the pandemic and decreased stress levels later. We collected data over the duration of the pandemic, from initial lockdown until the end of the COVID emergency (i.e., 5 May 2023). Thus, our findings represent an average of caregiver stress over a period of approximately 30 months, indicating that, despite potentially fluctuating stress levels during the pandemic, caregiver stress was not limited to the initial period of lockdown, but persisted well into the post‐pandemic transition.

According to the bioecological framework, micro‐contextual factors, such as family income, social support and access to resources, are another consideration for variability in reported stress. The average family income for this study was comparable to the national average (Creamer et al. 2022). Our findings, consistent with existing data, suggest that crisis events, such as the pandemic, disproportionately affect low‐income families who experienced higher levels of stress (Hall et al. 2022; Santana et al. 2021; Wolfson et al. 2021). Indeed, these findings further support the critical role of the environment on families’ experiences from the macro‐ to the micro‐context. Together, these findings foreground the need for policymakers to prioritise this population at the intersection of disability and limited income, particularly during emergency responses.

The Extent to Which Household Adversities Impacted Receptive Language Skills in Children With LI

Our second finding demonstrates a significant negative relation between caregiver stress and child language. Said differently, higher levels of COVID‐related caregiver stress were associated with lower child receptive language. While we cannot imply that COVID‐related caregiver stress directly caused the variability in receptive language scores, this relation supports the bioecological model and exemplifies the importance of both the macrosystem (i.e., the global pandemic) and the microsystem (i.e., home environment), particularly for children with LI. Links between COVID‐related caregiver stress and child language were not unexpected, as they have been well established in extant literature (Justice et al. 2019; Markfeld et al. 2023; Nelson et al. 2017). Caregiver stress, regardless of source (e.g., work, relationships, financial hardships and crisis events), may reduce caregivers’ abilities to have sustained, language‐based interactions understood to bolster children's receptive language (Markfeld et al. 2023; Nelson et al. 2017).

While it is beyond the scope of this study to understand underlying mechanisms influencing child language and caregiver stress, we consider existing evidence regarding the effects of caregiver stress on children during the pandemic. For example, one study found that children's stress levels mirrored increased caregiver stress and depression during the COVID pandemic (Lee et al. 2021). This study of 405 families examined the transition to home‐based education and found significant positive associations between children's anxiety, parental anxiety and parenting stress. Moreover, the study demonstrated a negative correlation between remote education preparedness and increased parent depression and parenting stress. As such, parents with high levels of depression and parenting stress demonstrated reduced self‐efficacy and more frequently endorsed statements such as, ‘I do not have the resources I need to educate my child at home’ and ‘I have felt overwhelmed by responsibilities to educate my child at home.’ Lee and colleagues’ findings, coupled with the current study's negative association between parent stress and child language, could be due to compounding effects of increased stress and reduced school access. Future studies are necessary to explore the mechanisms by which caregiver stress affects child language outcomes. Regardless of mechanism, public policies to mitigate parent stressors, particularly for families of children with developmental disorders, are of critical importance.

The Extent to Which Parental Warmth Moderates the Relation Between Household Adversity and Receptive Language

Considering protective factors, we found that parental warmth marginally moderated effects of COVID‐related caregiver stress on child language in the study population. Remarkably, children whose parents had high levels of parental warmth (i.e., <1 SD) experienced nearly complete preservation of their language skills compared to children whose parents had low levels of parental warmth, despite high COVID‐related caregiver stress. Theoretically, this trend further supports the bioecological model, showing that the environment not only affects children and caregivers, but also that individual characteristics and relationships within the micro context may influence the extent and direction to which children respond to the phenomenon in the broader chrono context.

While pre‐pandemic studies suggest that parental warmth is strongly correlated with receptive language (Barnett et al. 2012; Mistry et al. 2004; Pungello et al. 2009), this is the first study to examine the protective role of parental warmth on receptive language during the COVID‐19 crisis. We hypothesised that high levels of parental warmth, which have been shown to support receptive language through language‐rich parent‐child interactions, may buffer the effects of parental stress during anxiety‐provoking events, such as a global health pandemic. Our findings approached significance (p = 0.06) in our population sample, which was statistically underpowered. Specifically, a prior power calculation suggests that with 43 participants, our study is able to detect small effects with 95% confidence at 58% power. Despite being marginally significant (Pritschet et al. 2016), adding the interaction variable accounted for 20% of variance in receptive language, suggesting that the role of caregiver warmth may be a meaningful factor pertaining to children's pandemic outcomes (Carnahan and Brown 2024). Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to establish sufficient statistical power to draw scientific conclusions. Meanwhile, considering that caregivers of children with language impairment report fewer environmental‐level protective factors compared to families of children without language impairment (Radville et al. 2024), external support for parental psychological and emotional health may be of critical importance for this unique and vulnerable population.

Implications

The present findings suggest multilevel, bidirectional ramifications for families of children with developmental disabilities during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Critically, the effects on children's receptive language are disparate and demand policy attention. Specifically, caregivers who have the resources to maintain strong and positive relationships with their children may protect their children from the deleterious language effects of stress. However, considering long‐standing health inequities, particularly for under‐resourced families, racially minoritised families and families with disabled children, the onus must not be disproportionately placed on vulnerable caregivers. Rather, supportive federal, state, and local policies are critical for caregivers of children with disabilities, including LI, who were statistically more likely to experience parenting‐related exhaustion during the COVID pandemic (Marchetti et al. 2020). Specifically, ongoing and future disability inclusive emergency responses for these families should consider direct financial aid, flexible work arrangements, and access to affordable childcare.

The federally funded Child Tax Credit (CTC) during the COVID‐19 pandemic provided families with direct and unconditional funds of up to $3600 per child. A review of short‐ and long‐term outcomes of the CTC found that these funds likely improved health and well‐being for children, particularly for under‐resourced children (Aizer et al. 2024). Indeed, these funds have been shown to reduce families’ food insecurity (Pilkauskas et al. 2022), reduce depression and anxiety in low‐income and racial minority caregivers (Batra et al. 2023), and allow low‐income caregivers to either spend more time at home caring for their children or to spend more on childcare (Hamilton et al. 2021). Emerging evidence suggests that families with disabilities, who often incur greater routine and healthcare expenses, relied on the CTC to cope with pandemic‐related expenses (Brugger et al. 2023). Further research examining the compounding effects of caregivers experiencing multilayer systemic health inequities (e.g., disability and racial minority or disability and poor) is critically needed to ensure that these families have appropriate financial support to maintain healthy caregiver‐child relationships and thus protect their children from negative effects of adversity and household stress.

Another implication of these data is the need for accessible and affordable childcare. The COVID emergency necessitated significant schooling modifications. During the spring of 2020, 77% of public schools shifted to virtual learning with children learning from home (National Center for Education Statistics, n.d.). The current data support extant research and suggest that this shift—and the consequential increase in childcare duties, including facilitation of online schooling—was a predominant COVID‐related adversity. Furthermore, our data, which were collected throughout the pandemic, suggest that while many schools opened with health precautions in the fall of 2020, the need for childcare extended beyond school reopening. Prior to the pandemic, approximately one‐third of urban families had unmet childcare needs (Garg et al. 2015). Undoubtedly, the cost of childcare compounded by a lack of available childcare (i.e., ‘childcare deserts’) was exacerbated during the COVID pandemic and may have prohibited many children from accessing alternative nurturing and enriching environments while their parents continued to work. Given this study's findings, access to secure and warm caregiver‐child relationships has integral value as a protective mechanism for language outcomes of children with LI.

In addition to financial assistance, employer policies that support flexible and individualised work arrangements may empower caregivers (who in this study were primarily mothers) to more effectively manage adversities and related stress, both those incurred by the pandemic and those that are common to caregiving (e.g., child illness). Indeed, flexible work arrangements, such as flexi‐time, working from home, and job sharing, have been shown to effectively support working caregivers (Dizaho et al. 2017) and reduce stress (Shiri et al. 2022). Given an employee‐oriented work environment may enable caregivers to have greater ability to balance work, home and childcare demands. Further, caregivers may have more opportunities to engage in quality time with their children, thus promoting an environment conducive to language learning. However, due to unequal access to flexible work arrangements, particularly for women and racial minorities, future research should explore the nuances of work arrangements specific to caregivers of children with developmental disabilities, to inform policy initiatives unique to this population.

Conclusion

The purpose of this study was to investigate home environments and associated language outcomes for children with LI during the COVID‐19 pandemic as a guide for ongoing and future crisis response. Data were collected through caregiver surveys and direct child language measures. Descriptive analysis and linear regression methods were utilised to explore study aims. Taken together, our findings confirm and extend the existing body of literature on impacts of the COVID‐19 pandemic on children's outcomes to include those with developmental disabilities. This is the first study of which we are aware to characterise home environments of children with LI during the initial and extended phases of the pandemic. Families of children with developmental disabilities are a vulnerable group that entered the pandemic with pre‐existing risk factors. Therefore, examining their unique experiences and outcomes provides guidance on the development of future inclusive emergency responses and post‐pandemic policies.

Theoretically, our findings support the bioecological model of human development. This model highlights the bidirectional role of a child's environment, both distally (e.g., a global pandemic) and proximally (e.g., the home environment), on developmental outcomes. Consistent with this framework, we found that the global COVID‐19 pandemic prompted a cascading effect on household adversities, caregiver stress and language outcomes of children LI.

Overall, our research suggests that families of children with LI experienced adversities during the pandemic, and that these adversities had prolonged and significant impacts on children's language skills, likely influenced by COVID‐related caregiver stress and parent‐child relationships. These findings highlight the need for caregiver wellbeing and personal agency, which consequently benefit caregiving practices and, in turn, children's receptive language outcomes for children. We have presented three distinct policy considerations that we believe support optimal conditions for healthy caregivers and thriving families, particularly for children with developmental disabilities.

Limitations

While the current study examined novel research questions and uncovered several important findings, results should be interpreted with the following limitations: First, there was no pre‐pandemic data pertaining to home experiences of the sample population from the pre‐pandemic period. As a result, we were not able to directly assess how COVID impacted these households. Moreover, this study suggests that families' experiences of adversity and related stress vary, suggesting a need for future research aimed at understanding more about the factors that support resiliency in the face of adversity. Next, the results are specific to our study population: families of young children with LI. While these findings may extend to other populations, broadly generalising these results without additional evidence is cautioned. Finally, this study was correlational by design, meaning that while we can confirm a significant association between COVID‐related caregiver stress and language skills of children with LI, we cannot make conclusions regarding directionality of this association, nor causality. Future studies are needed to explore whether and how parenting practices and caregiver wellbeing directly affect language skills for children with developmental disabilities.
