An intelligent approach to predict the drilling penetration rate using acoustic emission technique (AET)
Authors: Mehrbod Khoshouei, Raheb Bagherpour, Mohammad Hossein Jalalian, Ehsan Purhamadani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Optimization has become a central concern in geotechnical engineering with increasing constraints on energy resources and the rising demand for cost-effective operations. Drilling, as a critical and energy-intensive component of mining and tunneling (particularly in transportation infrastructure), requires efficient and intelligent performance strategies. Monitoring While Drilling (MWD) provides a promising approach for real-time acquisition of drilling conditions. Recent advancements, including the integration of Acoustic Emission Technique (AET) with artificial intelligence (AI), enhance data-driven modeling and predictive analysis of drilling performance. In this study, vibroacoustic signals and drilling parameters were analyzed to predict penetration rate (PR) using three machine learning models: Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Regression (SVR). Comparative evaluation showed that all three models achieved reliable predictive accuracy, with ANN reaching R2 = 0.744, MAPE = 36.98%, RMSE = 0.161; RF yielding R2 = 0.816, MAPE = 31.54%, RMSE = 0.142; and SVR attaining R2 = 0.808, MAPE = 29.52%, RMSE = 0.141. The results demonstrate the feasibility of integrating vibroacoustic monitoring with AI-driven models for accurate PR prediction. This approach supports real-time decision-making, enhances drilling efficiency, and promotes sustainable practices in both underground and surface excavation projects.

Introduction

Drilling is recognized as one of the most complex and cost-intensive operations in geotechnical engineering, particularly in the fields of mining, tunneling, and construction1. This operation significantly impacts both capital and operational expenses. In mining, drilling influences production costs and overall operating expenses2. More than 25–35% of mining operation costs are related to drilling3,4. Drilling plays a crucial role in tunneling and mining, particularly in hard rocks, where it is used for creating blast holes, bolting for rock reinforcement, coring, water drainage, and monitoring deformations or fractures in the rock mass5. The effectiveness of drilling directly impacts achieving the desired rock fragmentation in mining6.

Rock drilling is conducted using various techniques depending on the hardness of the rock. In extremely hard rocks, rotary-percussive drilling is employed, while in medium-hard rocks, rotary-crushing drilling is used, and in soft rocks, cutting methods are applied2. In rotary drilling, drill bits penetrate rocks ranging from soft to extremely hard. The borehole diameters in this method can range from 150 to 455 millimeters7. Today, this drilling equipment is widely used in large open-pit mines for creating blast holes with large diameters, and different types of this equipment are designed to accommodate a wide range of drilling parameters suited to specific drilling conditions8,9.

Given the significant costs associated with drilling, optimizing this process is essential. The primary goals of drilling optimization, especially in the early stages, include reducing drilling time, lowering drilling costs, and enhancing efficiency10,11. The drilling process is inherently complex, with factors influencing its performance categorized into three main groups: parameters related to the drilling machine and cutting tool, rock properties, and operational parameters12,13. A thorough understanding and optimization of these parameters are crucial for improving drilling efficiency and effectiveness. Figure 1 shows some of the parameters affecting drilling performance.

Parameters influencing drilling can generally be classified into controllable and uncontrollable. Controllable parameters, also known as dependent variables, can be identified and managed. On the other hand, uncontrollable parameters, or independent variables, are factors dictated by natural conditions beyond the influence of humans or machines. The interaction of these parameters introduces complexities in drilling performance that are only sometimes easy to evaluate or predict12,15. Therefore, developing a comprehensive method or indicator to accurately gauge the impact of these factors without disrupting ongoing operations could be highly beneficial for optimizing drilling processes.

Optimizing drilling operations is closely linked to reducing energy consumption and enhancing the penetration rate (PR). Drawing from field experience, several strategies exist to lower drilling costs. A critical approach is fine-tuning drilling parameters to achieve the highest possible PR. The initial step in this optimization process involves the precise and real-time prediction and evaluation of the PR. Recognizing the critical importance of accurately assessing and forecasting the PR, numerous studies have been conducted to model and compute it, considering various drilling parameters, including operational conditions and rock properties. Considering the significant complexities and costs involved in drilling operations, particularly in the mining industry, optimizing the PR is crucial for improving efficiency and reducing operational expenses. The accurate prediction of PR is vital, as it directly influences drilling time, energy consumption, and overall project costs. This study uses intelligent methods and Acoustic Emission Technique (AET) to predict one of the most critical parameters in mining drilling operations—the PR. The proposed intelligent model can be utilized to predict PR, which is essential for optimizing the drilling process in mining operations.

Penetration rate (PR)

Forecasting drilling performance is crucial for selecting suitable equipment, optimizing operational efficiency, managing project costs, and executing drilling operations effectively. Rock penetrability is an essential factor in this process, often quantified by metrics such as the PR and the specific energy required for drilling16. The ease of drilling rock depends on various factors, including hardness, abrasiveness, brittleness, discontinuities, and overall rock mass characteristics16. Mechanical parameters affecting drilling efficiency include rotational speed, torque, water or drilling mud application, air pressure for borehole cleaning, applied force, drilling speed, drill bit type, and borehole dimensions. Among these, the geological and mechanical properties of the rock formation are particularly significant17. “Penetrability” and PR, though sometimes used interchangeably, refer to different aspects of drilling performance. Penetrability describes the ease with which a drill bit advances through rock, reflecting the rock’s resistance5. On the other hand, PR, measured in meters per minute, quantifies the speed at which the drill bit penetrates the rock over a given period. Thus, while penetrability indicates how readily a drill bit can move through the rock, PR measures the rate of this advancement10,17,18. Accurate measurement and optimization of PR are vital for project design and cost estimation in various fields, including mining, dam construction, tunneling, road construction, and drainage systems. For instance, PR significantly impacts operating costs and production efficiency6,19. Enhancing PR is critical to improving drilling performance, as increasing PR can notably reduce drilling time and associated costs. In the oil and gas sector, achieving the highest possible PR can significantly decrease drilling costs10. For instance, Mantegazini et al. demonstrated in a case study that, when achieving the optimum PR (i.e., obtaining the highest possible values), savings in cost and time of USD 1,056,180 to USD 1,151,898 and 19.50 to 21.27 h, respectively, were obtained. In contrast, an inefficient drilling operation, with a very low PR, resulted in drilling taking 25.42 h at a cost close to USD 1,387,33520.

Overall, time is a critical parameter affecting drilling costs21. Therefore, optimizing parameters such as PR is essential for cost efficiency. Real-time analysis during drilling should focus on optimizing weight on bit (WOB), rotational speed (RPM), pumping rate (Q), and other relevant parameters (such as torque on bit (TOB), mud properties)22,23. These factors substantially impact drilling performance, as indicated by PR. Maximizing PR can reduce drilling time and cost per foot24. Accurate PR prediction models are crucial for drilling optimization, as these models need to account for how various parameters influence PR 25–28. Understanding the behavior of drilling data and the effects of different parameters on PR is essential for developing reliable prediction models29.

Acoustic emission technique (AET)

Acoustic emission involves pressure waves generated by energy release due to material deformation, fracture, or failure, which sensors, such as ultrasonic sensors, can detect to analyze material properties12,30. This non-destructive technique is widely used in industrial and scientific fields for monitoring and predicting physical processes31,32. The applications of the AET33.

In mining, accurate predictions of factors such as drill bit wear, PR, and blasting performance are essential for informed decision-making regarding equipment34. Predicting these factors precisely and cost-effectively is crucial for project success. Various non-destructive methods, including acoustic emission, have gained traction for this purpose. Over the years, research has underscored the effectiveness of acoustic emission in assessing rock properties35. Vardhan et al.36 investigated the relationship between sound levels and rock compressive strength using laboratory-scale tests with a jackhammer. Similarly, Kumar et al.37 examined the use of sound levels to predict rock properties at the field level. Kumar et al.38 employed multivariate regression to relate rock properties such as uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), tensile strength, and porosity to operating parameters like equivalent sound level, drill bit diameter, and rotation speed. Bastari et al.39 developed a method for determining powder particle sizes using acoustic emission signal processing techniques. Gradl et al.40 analyzed sounds generated during drilling to explore the relationship between drill bit design and vibro-acoustic properties. Kahraman et al.41 utilized sound levels to predict the abrasion resistance of rocks. Karakus et al.42 examined the spectrum of acoustic signals produced during rock coring, while Flegner et al.43 focused on vibro-acoustic signals in rotary drilling processes. Xiao et al.44 evaluated the PR using acoustic emission under laboratory conditions. They utilized the average acoustic emission energy during drilling with a polycrystalline diamond compact (PDC) bit. It was observed that as the drilling PR increased, the average acoustic emission energy also increased, a trend that was associated with larger fracture sizes generated throughout the drilling process.

Recent studies have highlighted the practical applications of acoustic emission in rock analysis. Yari et al.45–47 conducted laboratory drilling tests to estimate rock properties such as porosity, P-wave velocity, UCS, and Brazilian tensile strength (BTS) using acoustic emission. Sheng et al.48 employed variational mode decomposition (VMD) to decompose acoustic signals into three components corresponding to low-, mid-, and high-frequency bands during the waterjet drilling process. Their findings indicated that the high-frequency band exhibited a stronger correlation with the PR compared to the other bands. Moreover, the study demonstrated that fracture density, a phenomenon observed during the rock failure process, can be effectively identified in relation to the PR through the analysis of acoustic signals. Khoshouei and Bagherpour49 analyzed the relationship between UCS, BTS, and Schmidt Rebound Number (SRN) during drilling and how these properties correlate with acoustic parameters like sound pressure level (SPL) and dominant frequency. In a subsequent study, Khoshouei et al.33 investigated whether rock class and strength properties could be estimated from acoustic signals recorded during drilling, revealing a significant correlation between the RMS values of these signals and rock strength. Kumar et al.50 developed Artificial Neural Network (ANN) models to predict the geomechanical properties of sedimentary rocks using dominant frequencies of acoustic signals recorded during drilling. Furthermore, Khoshouei et al.51 explored rock type identification through acoustic signal analysis, demonstrating the effectiveness of frequency behavior analysis in geological assessments. Khoshouei et al.52 studied monitoring while drilling (MWD) and optimizing operations using AET. They analyzed vibroacoustic signals generated during drilling to assess specific energy (SE). Their research established correlations between these signals and SE, suggesting a practical approach for an industrial monitoring system to detect excessive energy consumption and signal when a drill bit nears the end of its useful life.

In addition, acoustic emission can be used to estimate the optimal operating modes, speeds, and tools for specific non-destructive tasks and the energy consumption involved. Wiliams and Hagan53 investigated the relationship between acoustic signals and changes in rock cutting conditions. Karakurt et al.54,55 explored noise levels in rock cutting operations, while Xing Zhu et al. studied low-frequency acoustic signals generated by natural phenomena such as rockfall and erosion. Jaini et al.56 developed a tool monitoring system using a gap sensor and an MLP neural network to classify drill conditions based on signal fluctuations related to tool wear.

The growing application of acoustic emission in various engineering disciplines, including geotechnics, drilling, and mining, underscores its advantages. This method enables rapid, non-destructive assessment, significantly contributing to advancements in geoscience, exploration, drilling engineering, mineral processing, and rock mechanics.

Artificial intelligence (AI)

AI is a critical branch of computer science focused on developing machines that can perform specific tasks autonomously without explicit instructions. The overarching objective of AI is to enable machines to attain a level of intelligence comparable to that of humans57. Deep learning and machine learning are vital subfields within this domain, where algorithms are designed to train machines by recognizing patterns and extracting data from prior examples. These machine learning algorithms are increasingly favored due to their applicability in large-scale projects, cost-effectiveness, rapid processing capabilities, and user-friendly nature58.

In contemporary society, AI has garnered substantial attention and has become deeply integrated into various aspects of human life. Its applications range from accelerated cancer detection, surpassing human experts’ capabilities, to identifying criminal patterns across vast financial transactions, converting extensive video content into structured data through speech and sound recognition, and developing chatbots that can better understand and interact with human language. These applications continue to expand rapidly58–60. AI-driven systems are particularly valued for their ability to predict, diagnose, learn, respond, and resolve complex problems57.

AI and machine learning are also making significant inroads across diverse industries, including filmmaking, gaming, healthcare, construction, and mining61,62. Although the mining and metals sector has been slower in adopting digital technologies than other industries, and the process of automating operations and machinery with intelligent technology has progressed at a measured pace, there is a growing recognition of these technologies’ critical role. Specifically, they are increasingly seen as essential for enhancing safety and productivity in mining operations. AI and machine learning can be applied throughout the mining lifecycle, from exploration and production to mine closure and reclamation58. Machine Learning is a field focused on designing computer programs that learn from historical data to model, control, or predict outcomes using statistical methods. It encompasses several critical approaches: Supervised Learning involves training machines on labeled datasets to make decisions, categorized into classification and regression tasks63. In contrast, Unsupervised Learning deals with finding patterns in unlabeled data, utilizing techniques such as clustering and dimensionality reduction64. Reinforcement Learning (RL) centers on learning optimal actions through interactions with an environment to maximize rewards65. Finally, Deep Learning, the most advanced technique, leverages neural networks to achieve more precise predictions than traditional methods66–68.

In recent years, deep learning has gained wide applications across various fields, providing solutions to numerous real-life challenges69,70. Among the intelligent techniques employed in mining engineering, particularly in drilling operations, ANN have emerged as one of the most effective methods71–75. ANN mimics the human brain’s biological neurons, facilitating data learning and feature extraction76,77. In this study, an intelligent ANN model, combined with the AET, is used to predict the PR in drilling operations, offering a robust approach to optimizing the drilling process.

Accurate prediction of the PR remains a central challenge in drilling due to its significant impact on operational efficiency, cost, and safety. Over the years, various approaches have been employed to estimate PR, ranging from empirical and regression-based models to more advanced machine learning techniques. Although widely used in practice, empirical and regression models are limited by simplifying assumptions and insufficient capacity to capture the complex nonlinear interactions between drilling parameters and rock properties. Conventional machine learning algorithms such as Support Vector Regression (SVR) and Random Forests (RF) have improved prediction accuracy by handling nonlinearities more effectively. Yet, their performance can still be constrained when processing high-dimensional and nonstationary signals generated during drilling.

AET has recently emerged as a non-invasive method for real-time monitoring of different processes. AET provides access to dynamic information on rock–drill bit interactions by recording transient acoustic signals generated from crack propagation, friction, and other deformation mechanisms. Despite its potential, direct use of acoustic signals is challenging due to noise, variability in rock properties, and the complexity of extracting meaningful patterns. The integration of AET with advanced predictive models has been proposed to address these challenges. In particular, ANNs demonstrate strong capability in learning complex nonlinear relationships, while RF and SVR provide complementary strengths in interpretability and robustness. By combining AET-derived features with these machine learning models, PR prediction can be performed with greater accuracy and reliability than traditional approaches. Accurate prediction of the PR is essential for optimizing drilling efficiency, reducing costs, and ensuring wellbore stability. Traditional approaches, which rely on empirical coefficients and region-specific correlations, often lack generalizability and robustness under heterogeneous formations. Machine learning methods offer a superior alternative by uncovering complex nonlinear relationships in drilling data, providing higher predictive accuracy and stability, and enabling real-time decision-making. Moreover, ML techniques integrate multi-source data and employ advanced optimization schemes to improve adaptability under challenging conditions78–80. This study used three models (ANN, RF, and SVR) to evaluate the potential of acoustic and vibration signals and drilling parameters in predicting PR under controlled laboratory conditions. The comparative analysis of these models provides insight into their respective strengths and limitations, thereby advancing current methodologies for drilling performance prediction and optimization.

Methodology

As previously noted, accurate prediction of the PR is of critical importance in drilling operations, and the use of AE monitoring techniques has increasingly gained attention as a real-time diagnostic tool. In this study, a systematic experimental framework was designed to investigate the correlation between drilling AE signals and PR under controlled laboratory conditions. For this purpose, drilling tests were performed on rock samples with different lithological and mechanical properties to ensure a representative range of drilling responses. Throughout the drilling experiments, acoustic signals were continuously acquired using high-sensitivity sensors placed near the drilling zone. The raw AE signals were then pre-processed to remove noise and irrelevant components, followed by detailed signal processing and feature extraction in both time and frequency domains. These extracted features were subsequently analyzed to determine their relationships with PR, and machine learning models (ANN, RF, and SVR) were further employed to evaluate quantitatively. Signal processing procedure

The acoustic signals captured during drilling consist of signals from various sources. The sound sources in a drilling test can be broadly categorized into three classes: the drilling machine, interaction between the drill bit and the sample, and ambient acoustic sources from the surrounding environment. To accurately predict PR, it’s essential to identify the frequency range associated with the characteristics of the rock being drilled. This involves recording and processing acoustic signals under various laboratory conditions, including laboratory environment noise, drilling machine sound, and drilling machine sound in the presence of drilling fluid. The collected acoustic data is analyzed using a Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) to obtain the intensity at each frequency and the duration of its presence in the signal spectrum81,82. For the STFT analysis, a Hamming window of 512 samples with 50% overlap was used to balance time–frequency resolution. Table 2 provides the frequency range and maximum amplitude of these sources. A cut-off value of 50% of the maximum signal amplitude was used to identify the signal frequency range. This table shows that the maximum frequency resulting from components other than the interaction between the drill bit and rock samples is 1500 Hz.

The analysis of acoustic signals recorded during rock drilling is approached using three methodologies. Initially, the signals are examined in the time domain to extract peak amplitude values. Following this, a two-dimensional time-frequency spectral analysis utilizing a Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) is performed to identify the frequency ranges associated with the rock characteristics. A Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) spectral analysis is conducted to isolate the dominant frequencies, defined as those with the highest energy intensity.

A comparison of the acoustic spectra obtained during drilling with those from other sources reveals that the frequency range of 6500–10,000 Hz is particularly significant, as it correlates with changes in operational parameters and is characterized by overtones in the higher frequency ranges. This frequency band is subsequently utilized to extract the dominant frequencies.

The vibrations generated during the drilling process are captured by a three-axis accelerometer sensor strategically positioned on the drilling machine, aligned with the drill bit to minimize torque effects and potential sensor inaccuracies. The sensor measures vibration acceleration in m/s2.

Once the vibration signals are recorded, they undergo pre-processing to prepare them for primary analysis and frequency evaluation. This involves implementing a bandpass filter within the 2–20 Hz frequency range to eliminate noise, as frequencies below this range are primarily associated with the drilling machine’s vibrations, while those above are deemed noisy. This filtering aims to enhance the clarity of the vibration signals, specifically targeting frequencies pertinent to changes in operational parameters.

Machine learning models for PR prediction

This section introduces the machine learning models in this study, ANN, RF, and SVR, for predicting drilling PRs based on acoustic and vibration data.

ANN

ANNs were first conceptualized by McCulloch and Pitts, who designed these networks based on simple binary threshold units83. Inspired by the structure and function of the human brain, these networks consist of interconnected nodes or neurons organized in layers. The brain comprises thousands of interconnected neurons and is responsible for essential functions such as responding to input signals and enabling complex processes like speech and image recognition. Similarly, ANNs are modeled after biological neural networks, structured with interconnected nodes that function comparably.

A neural network has three fundamental components: node characteristics, network topology, and learning rules. Node characteristics encompass the number of inputs and outputs associated with each neuron, the weights assigned to each input, and the activation function applied to the weighted sum of inputs. Network topology defines how neurons are organized and interconnected, generally involving input, hidden, and output layers. Learning rules govern the adjustment of weights during the training process to enhance the network’s performance84,85.

During training, ANNs adjust their weights based on input data and desired outputs through supervised learning, where the network is trained with labeled data, or unsupervised learning, where patterns are identified in unlabeled data86. Each node receives multiple inputs through connections with associated weights. When the weighted sum of these inputs surpasses the node’s threshold, the node activates, processes the signal through an activation function, and transmits it to adjacent nodes. Nodes are arranged in linear arrays known as layers, including input, output, and hidden layers. The network topology specifies the number of nodes per layer, the number of layers, and the connectivity among nodes, which were initially set based on intuition and refined through experimentation. Fundamentally, ANNs can be viewed as functions that map one or more inputs to outputs by processing these inputs through layers of interconnected neurons, applying weights, biases, and activation functions to generate decisions or predictions87,88.

Random forest

Random forests are an ensemble of decision-tree predictors, where each tree depends on the values of a random vector. This vector is sampled independently and identically for all the trees in the forest. The random forest algorithm was first introduced by Breiman 2001. In this algorithm, each tree node corresponds to a hyperrectangular cell. The root of the tree and each node (or its corresponding cell) are split into two parts during the tree construction process. The terminal nodes (leaves) collectively cover a portion of the initial space of the tree’s root. It is noted that this algorithm operates by growing a large number of trees, combining multiple decision trees, and aggregating their predictions through averaging (Fig. 8)89,90. Random forest increases diversity among trees and prevents excessive correlation between them by growing trees on different subsets of the training data generated through the bagging method. Bagging involves random sampling with replacement from the original dataset to create new training sets. As a result, some training data may never be used, while others may be used more than once. It performs exceptionally well when the number of variables is much larger than the number of observations. Moreover, it is powerful enough to be applied to large-scale problems, easily used for various machine learning tasks, and ultimately returns variable importance measures89.

Results

Machine learning framework (ANN, RF, and SVR)

Three machine learning models were employed to predict the drilling PR: ANN, RF, and SVR. These models were selected due to their complementary strengths in capturing the nonlinear and complex relationships between AE signals, vibration data, and PR. The ANN is capable of approximating nonlinear patterns, the RF provides robust ensemble learning and insights into feature importance, while the SVR offers strong generalization with limited samples through kernel-based regression. The input parameters, include count, maximum of power, peak factor, skewness, root mean square (RMS), mean, maximum, standard deviation, kurtosis, vibration RMS, and maximum vibration, extracted from acoustic and vibration signals obtained during drilling experiments, are shown in Fig. 9. Machine learning models details and results

This section explores the potential for predicting drilling PRs using characteristics of acoustic signals, vibration data, and drilling conditions. Three machine learning models (ANN, RF, and SVR) were employed and systematically evaluated to achieve this objective. The ANN model consisted of 17 neurons in the hidden layer, with a learning rate of 0.01 and a momentum term of 0.001. These parameters were carefully selected through preliminary trials to ensure stable training convergence and to avoid overfitting while capturing non-linear relationships between input features and PR. The model was trained on a dataset comprising acoustic and vibration signals recorded during drilling operations, enabling the prediction of the drilling PR, a key performance metric in drilling efficiency. The RF model was configured with 30 trees, maximum depth control, and bootstrap sampling. It was used to provide robust prediction and to assess feature importance within the vibroacoustic dataset. The SVR model employed a radial basis function (RBF) kernel optimized via grid search to achieve optimal generalization performance. All three models were trained and validated using the same dataset, and their performance was evaluated by comparing predicted PRs with actual measurements. Performance evaluation of the models

In general, several criteria and methods are available for evaluating the performance and effectiveness of machine learning models and algorithms. Key metrics used to assess predictive models include:

Mean Absolute Error (MAE) This metric indicates the average deviation of predictions from actual values and is easily interpretable. It is less sensitive to outliers and retains the same unit of measurement as the original data. Mean Squared Error (MSE) This criterion measures the precision of a predictive model by calculating the average of the squared differences between predicted and actual values. It is more sensitive to outliers, making it suitable for models where outliers are a concern. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) RMSE provides a measure of how well a model performs by evaluating the square root of the average squared errors. It is particularly useful for comparing models as it is more sensitive to larger errors. Coefficient of Determination (R2) This statistic shows the proportion of variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variables. Its value ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating better model performance. Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) MAPE assesses the accuracy of predictions in percentage terms, offering a relative measure of error which can be helpful for comparative analysis. K-Fold Cross-Validation This technique involves partitioning the dataset into k subsets. In each iteration, k-1 subsets are used for training and one subset for validation. This method provides a more comprehensive evaluation of model performance and helps prevent overfitting. Conclusions

Accurate prediction of PR is essential in drilling operations, as it directly affects efficiency, costs, and project timelines. Optimizing PR in mining and drilling processes can significantly reduce energy consumption and operational expenses while improving overall productivity. Incorporating innovative approaches, such as acoustic emission techniques combined with machine learning, is crucial for enhancing decision-making and operational control. Acoustic emission methods enable real-time, non-destructive monitoring of drilling processes, providing insight into drill bit conditions and rock interactions. When integrated with machine learning models, including ANN, RF, and SVR, these approaches facilitate modeling complex relationships between drilling parameters, rock properties, and PRs. This study used vibroacoustic signals and drilling parameters as inputs to the three models to predict PR. Comparative evaluation of the models demonstrated their capability to capture intricate patterns in the data. Performance metrics for the ANN were R2 = 0.844 (training) and 0.744 (testing), MAPE = 19.92% (training) and 36.98% (testing), RMSE = 0.123 (training) and 0.161 (testing); for RF, R2 = 0.914 (training) and 0.816 (testing), MAPE = 17.54% (training) and 31.54% (testing), RMSE = 0.1 (training) and 0.142 (testing); and for SVR, R2 = 0.976 (training) and 0.808 (testing), MAPE = 04.36% (training) and 29.52% (testing), RMSE = 0.0486 (training) and 0.141 (testing). These results confirm the robustness and generalization ability of the models and highlight the added value of incorporating vibration data alongside acoustic signals. The models enable timely prediction of drill bit performance and optimization of operational parameters, enhancing drilling efficiency and reducing costs. Their effectiveness across various rock types, including granite, marble, and travertine, demonstrates versatility and applicability under diverse geological conditions. Overall, integrating ANN, RF, and SVR models with acoustic and vibration data provides a powerful framework for optimizing drilling operations. Such intelligent systems can significantly contribute to more efficient, sustainable, cost-effective mining and drilling practices.
