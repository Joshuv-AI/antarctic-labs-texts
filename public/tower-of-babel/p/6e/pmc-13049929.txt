A Decade of cGAS Discovery: Point of Convergence in Oral Diseases
Authors: Lu Sun, Pengfei Gao, Peng Xiao, Sirui Li, Dayong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsTo provide the research progress on the cGAS-STING signalling pathway and its related roles in various oral diseases.MethodsWe reviewed the recent research on cGAS-STING in PubMed. This article summarises recent research achievements in the discovery and confirmation of the cGAS-STING signalling pathway, as well as its biological and immunological roles in various diseases.ResultsThe cGAS-STING pathway and its associated cytokines play a crucial role in host immunity. It plays various roles in antiviral infections and autoimmune diseases, while also playing an important role in tumour surveillance and cellular aging processes. Research has shown that the cGAS-STING pathway affects various oral diseases such as periodontitis, pulp infections, oral mucosal diseases, maxillofacial tumours and many others.ConclusionThe cGAS-STING pathway plays a crucial role in various diseases, and its excessive activation often leads to immune dysregualtion. The role of cGAS-STING in oral diseases needs to be further elucidatedClinical RelevanceThe cGAS-STING pathway represents a promising target for the development of new therapeutic strategies for a range of oral diseases.

Introduction

The Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research of 2024 honoured the discovery of the cGAS enzyme-sensing self and foreign DNA - by Zhijian “James” Chen.1 This groundbreaking work opened a new field and launched a race to unearth themysteries of the immune response.

In short, the host relies on innate immune receptors that are named pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to recognise distinct pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Upon PAMPs or DAMPs recognition, PRRs initiate multiple signal cascades for a variety of genes transcription elements associated with the immune response, as well as non-transcriptional activities including phagocytosis, autophagy or cell death. PRRs can be divided into different subgroups based on protein domain homology: Toll-like receptors (TLRs), C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), retinoic acid-inducible gene-I (RIG-I)-like receptors (RLRs), nucleotide oligomerisation domain (NOD)-like receptors (NLRs) and several cytosol DNA sensors (CDS).

The cGAS-STING sensing pathway has emerged as a crucial element of innate immunity since the first identification of cGAS (cyclic GMP-AMP synthase) and cGMP (cyclic GMP-AMP).1,2 Stimulator of IFN genes (STING)3 is a protein that mainly resides in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) that senses cyclic dinucleotides,4 which are second-messenger signalling molecules normally produced by bacteria. cGAS undergoes a conformational change to an active state when activated upon binding DNA and produces cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) that is subsequently detected by STING to activate TANK-binding kinases (TBK1).3 Interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) undergoes TBK1-mediated phosphorylation and nuclear translocation to activate the type I interferons (IFN-I), which is crucial for bridging innate and adaptive immunity5,6 while also enhancing the expression of ISGs that orchestrate antiviral defence mechanisms (Figure 1). However, not all DNAs activate cGAS, especially for short DNA elements. Biological importance of the cGAS-STING pathway

Role in viral response

There is a growing number of studies suggesting that the role of cGAS-STING is closely associated with the clearance of viruses. An interferon-induced signalling pathway is one of the most pivotal antiviral mechanisms for hosts, and much has been learned about the complexity of IFN signalling during viral infection. Generally, the JAK-STAT pathway is initiated when IFN binds to its receptors, leading to the transcription of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs), which play key roles in antiviral defences. Depletion of cGAS- and STING-enhanced virus replication and spread further confirms their antiviral roles.7 As the number and quality of multi-omics studies have grown, a transcriptome analysis has revealed the crucial role of cGAS-STING in the course of viral infection.8

A proteomic study confirmed that cGAS was upregulated in Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV)-infected mouse embryonic fibroblasts.9 The depletion of cGAS results in reduced transcriptional levels of specific antiviral genes, whereas mice lacking STING are more susceptible to viral infection. Even though IFN mediated by cGAS-STING is critical for antiviral responses, viruses have developed different mechanisms to evade host immune responses to antagonise this signalling pathway, such as disrupting STING oligomerisation and interacting with the STING-TBK1 complex to inhibit phosphorylation of IRF3.10 Therefore, there is certainly a need to identify and explore further the multiple mechanisms of this pathway during viral infections.

Role in tumour surveillance

Anti-tumour therapies, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy and immunotherapies, activate the cGAS-STING pathway to provoke anti-tumour immunity. Although anti-tumour treatments induce a slight increase in tumour-derived IFN-I and elicit the anti-tumour effect in a cGAS-STING dependent manner,11 it is still debatable whether tumour-intrinsic cGAS-STING signalling provokes anti-tumour immunity by the contribution of IFN-I production or other related pathways. The phagocytosed dsDNA is released into the cytosol of immune cells and activates the cGAS-STING signalling, which further enhances the recruitment of tumour-specific CD8+ T cells to exert anti-tumour effects.12 Meanwhile, recent research supports that cGAS-STING can inhibit RT-mediated DNA damage repair, thereby reducing RT-induced anti-tumour immunity and promoting tumour development and metastasis.13 In addition, sustained activation of cGAS-STING reshapes the immunosuppressive tumour immune microenvironment (TME), thereby helping tumours to evade immune surveillance, which undoubtedly promotes disease progression.14 Therefore, the cGAS-STING pathway exhibits dual functionality in the TME: either facilitate tumour progression or provoke anti-tumour immunity. Thus, to explore more treatment strategies, additional research is needed to further elucidate the role of the cGAS-STING pathway in tumour immunity.

Role in autoimmune disease

Autoimmune disease causes tissue damage or dysfunction due to the immune responses to its antigens. The cGAS-STING pathway can recognise the host’s DNA and induce type I IFN production. Hundreds of ISGs can be upregulated by activated IFN, which in turn can result in the pro-inflammatory cytokines secretion. Therefore, there is great potential for the role of the cGAS-STING signalling pathway in autoimmune diseases. Recently, key molecules of the pathway have been explored to further unravel how cGAS-STING triggered autoimmune diseases. Even though the role of cGAS-STING in SLE is still unclear, cGAS and STING deficiency led to upregulated autoantibodies in a SLE mouse model.15 Meanwhile, cGAS-STING is associated with the occurrence of rheumatoid arthritis.16 Murine and human STING activation also activates NF-kB and MAPK pathways, albeit employing an unconventional route for these transcription factors, which is unlike other PRR cascades that recruit the TAK and IKK complexes.17 In summary, more studies are needed to further investigate the mechanisms of cGAS-STING in autoimmune diseases and clinical treatment options. Hopefully, clinical trials will demonstrate the role of cGAS inhibitors in autoimmune diseases.

Role in cellular senescence

Cellular senescence is one of the important ways for tissue homeostasis maintenance. Many diseases are linked to dysregulated senescence. Either cellular or external stress or oncogenes can induce senescence. Yang et al. found that cGAS is essential for cellular senescence induced by DNA damage based on the fact that reduced signs of senescence were observed in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) from cGas−/− mice compared with MEFs of WT mice.18 Although a role of the cGAS-STING axis in the cellular senescence regulation has been previously described,19 how this axis controls the cellular senescence in age-related inflammation in vivo had remained unclear until Ablasser’s group revealed cGAS-STING converged with chronic neurodegenerative conditions.20 Furthermore, as many molecular mechanisms are yet unclear, more studies are essential to shed light on the role of cGAS in cellular senescence.

cGAS-STING and oral diseases

Various oral diseases caused by plaque microorganisms are usually a continuous process. As a result, DNA released by invaded microorganisms or host cells gradually accumulates in the disease area. Previous studies have confirmed that DNA plays a crucial role in oral diseases, and its impact on oral diseases is likely to be achieved through the cGAS-STING pathway. Although cGAS is traditionally known for its role in the cytosol, it has been suggested that cGAS can interact with mitochondria. Mitochondrial damage is increasingly recognised as a key endogenous source of DAMPs that activate the cGAS-STING pathway, contributing to inflammation and disease progression in oral pathologies.21 Mitochondrial dysfunction and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) are the major drivers for releasing mtDNA (mitochondrial DNA) into cytoplasm. The released mtDNA from damaged mitochondria into the cytosol, via several mechanisms such as mitochondrial membrane rupture, mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening or defective mitophagy, is recognised by cGAS to further activate IRF3 and NF-kB. Thus, the disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis participates the pathogenesis of various oral diseases by regulating abnormal inflammatory responses. In recent years, more studies have revealed how cGAS-STING influences oral diseases such as periodontitis, endodontic infection, mucosal diseases, and maxillofacial tumours, among many others. cGAS-STING and periodontitis

Periodontitis is a common, complex disease leading to alveolar bone loss and is associated with different systemic diseases. Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria such as P. gingivalis (P.g), A. actinomycetemcomitans (A.a) and Tannerella forsythia (T. forsythia) are the main microbial communities that promote the occurrence and progression of periodontitis. An in vitro study has found that P.g can significantly upregulate the expression of cGAS in human periodontium, indicating that cGAS plays an important role in the recognition of bacterially derived dsDNA in periodontal tissue and participates in recognising cytosolic nucleic acid PAMP.22 Immunohistochemical analysis showed that the expression of STING in periodontal connective tissue was significantly higher than that in healthy gingival tissue,23 which shows that the cGAS-STING pathway plays an indispensable role in the progression of periodontitis. Interestingly, the absence of STING inhibits inflammation and bone loss, which may be explained by its reduced expression of cytokines that promote osteoclast.24

P.g produces outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) that are their common toxic product. There is evidence that P.g-OMVs could activate the cGAS-STING pathway in periodontal tissues after DNA released by P.g-OMVs is transferred to the cytoplasm.25 A recent study showed that P.g-OMVs activate the cGAS-STING signalling pathways in gingival epithelial cells and increase the expression of IL-6 and IL-8, whereas the expression of both cytokines was significantly dampened when the STING expression was blocked.26 Moreover, P.g-OMVs facilitated alveolar bone resorption via elevating the levels of STING in the blood vessel and decreasing the number of Runx2+ cells around the bone.27 Wang et al. confirmed that insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2) can inhibit M1 macrophage polarisation to promote the expression of osteogenic-related genes by downregulating the expression of cGAS and STING, which highlights the potential therapeutic treatment of periodontitis in future.28 Periodontal pathogens such as P.g could induce mitochondrial stress in gingival epithelial cells, fibroblasts and some innate immune cells, which causes abnormal mitochondrial metabolism and damages mitochondria.29 The release of mtDNA, especially oxidised mtDNA, in the cytosol or extracellular space is particularly potent in immune activation including cGAS-STING pathway. Mitochondrial-derived mtDNA in periodontitis also activates NLRP3 inflammasome and TLR9,30 and these pathways synergise with cGAS-STING to exacerbate tissue destruction. Periodontitis is initiated by microbial dysbiosis and TLR signalling, and cGAS-STING contributes to sustaining chronic inflammation via type I IFNs and NF-kB to promote osteoclastogenesis and bone loss. Therefore, cGAS-STING is not the initiating signalling but might serve as a secondary driver that amplifies signalling pathways and further perpetuates tissue destruction. In summary, the cGAS-STING pathway plays a crucial role in periodontitis, and more research is needed to explore the mechanism of this pathway in the progression of periodontal disease for the development of precision treatments.

cGAS-STING and endodontic infections

Apical periodontitis is a local inflammation affecting periapical tissues. Cleaning a root canal infection and promoting periapical bone healing are the primary therapeutic methods for apical periodontitis. However, bacteria and bacterial metabolites may affect the prognosis of chronic apical periodontitis via long-term stimulation. Nucleic acid sensors influence root canal inflammation through complex pathways, and the cGAS-STING pathway may play a significant role. Previous studies have found that cGAS is significantly upregulated in human periapical lesions, and immunofluorescence labelling shows that cGAS expression is co-localised with IL-1 and IL-18, which implied that cGAS may be involved in the pathogenesis of periapical periodontitis.31 However, the concrete role and underlying mechanisms of cGAS-STING pathway in mediating apical periodontitis remain unclear.

Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) is one of the main organisms causing pulpitis. STING was expressed when odontoblast-like cells were stimulated by F. nucleatum at the early stage, and animal experiments have shown that after 28 days of establishing a pulpitis model with F. nucleatum, STING knockout mice exhibited significantly reduced periapical bone destruction and serum IFN-β levels compared with the control group.32 These and other studies cited above strongly support that F. nucleatum exacerbates the pulpitis progress through the cGAS-STING pathway.

Tian et al. explored the presence of the cGAS-STING pathway in human dental pulp cells (HDPCs), and results showed that microbial DNA can initiate inflammation in HDPCs via the cGAS-STING signal pathway, which confirmed that either a knock down or an inhibitor of cGAS or STING could abolish the inflammatory mediators production.33 They further confirmed that STING was a direct target of miR-4691-3p. The miR-4691-3p mimic suppressed STING expression, and by contrast, the miR-4691-3p inhibitor enhanced STING expression, resulting in the phosphorylation of p65 and IRF3 and the production of IFN-β and IL-6 production. Such data provide new insights for managing endodontic disease by using cGAS-STING pathway-dependent regulatory effect.34 Another study identified that both cGAS and STING were highly expressed in pulpitis tissues from patients and that mtDNA was a crucial inflammation driver in odontoblasts.35 Yet another study demonstrated that the STING inhibitor C-176 relieved inflammation progression and bone loss in an apical periodontitis mice model by reducing the expression of RANKL and the number of osteoclasts in periapical tissues. This type of intervention may serve as a valuable auxiliary tool for management of apical periodontitis.36

cGAS-STING and oral mucosal diseases

Oral lichen planus (OLP), a common chronic autoimmune disease of oral mucosa, is considered a potentially malignant disease. The concentration of cfDNA in saliva and plasma of OLP patients has been confirmed to be significantly higher than that in the healthy control group. Meanwhile, immunohistochemistry results confirmed that STING expression was high and phosphorylated STING protein levels were also increased in OLP tissues. Not surprisingly, STING knockdown inhibited cfDNA-induced THP-1 macrophage inflammation in OLP patients, and coincidentally PAMAM-G3, a nucleic acid binding polymer that can inhibit STING activation, reversed the inflammatory state caused by cfDNA OLP, suggesting that STING activation is a necessary step in inflammation induced by cfDNA-OLP. Deng et al. believe that this may stem from the activation of STING by cfDNA in OLP, which produces cytokines that can activate T lymphocytes. CD8+T lymphocytes can induce keratinocyte apoptosis by releasing cytotoxins, thereby releasing more cfDNA and forming a positive feedback loop.37 Huang et al. conducted in-depth mechanistic studies to explore the roles of gamma delta T cells and STING-TBK1 pathway in the pathogenesis of OLP. They found that the expression of gamma delta T cells in peripheral blood of OLP patients was downregulated, whereas the expression of gamma delta T cells in diseased tissues was upregulated. The proportion of CD69+, NKG 2D+ and peripheral blood HLA DR+, TLR4+ and gamma delta T cells is increased in OLP patients. The elevation of IFN-γ and IL-17A levels is positively correlated with the activation of the STING-TBK1 pathway and gamma delta T cells, suggesting that the upregulation of the STING-TBK1 pathway in gamma delta T cells may be involved in the immune regulation of OLP.38

Oral candidiasis and oral leucoplakia are common oral precancerous lesions (OPLs), but research on their pathogenesis and carcinogenic mechanisms is still limited. To determine the role of the cGAS-STING pathway in C. albicans, Harding et al. revealed that mice lacking either cGAS or STING had an improved survival rate after being infected with C. albicans, which demonstrated that mice lacking the cGAS-STING pathway exhibit significantly increased resistance to C. albicans. Meanwhile, the cGAS-STING pathway can also be activated by C. albicans–related DNA in extracellular vesicles, significantly affecting the production of inflammatory cytokines in human monocytes stimulated by C. albicans.39 Shi et al. found that the content of Mn2+ in peripheral blood of OLK patients was negatively correlated with the progression of OLK. Further in vitro experiments showed that Mn2+promotes the maturation and antigen presentation of DCs and macrophages by activating the cGAS-STING pathway, leading to increased activation of CD8+ T cells and effective killing of OLK. In addition, in a mouse OPLs model induced by carcinogen 4NQO, a single local dose of programmed death receptor-1 (PD-1) inhibitor can restore the expression of STING and CD11c and increase the infiltration of CD8+T cells into the lesion area, showing significant inhibitory effects on lesion progression. This may provide a new direction for OPLs treatment.40,41 Overall, these studies provide novel insights into oral mucosal diseases.

cGAS-STING and oral tumour

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the most common type of oral cancer, which is difficult to treat and prone to recurrence and distant metastasis. A recent study revealed the role of the cGAS-STING pathway in tumour-associated immunity in OSCC patients. A key gene signature was applied to represent the activation status of the cGAS-STING pathway, and the results showed that OSCC patients with high expression of cGAS-STING have a better prognosis, and the infiltration ratio of immune cells and the expression profiles of immune-related genes were elevated when the cGAS-STING pathway was activated.42 Mutant p53, commonly found in oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) and OSCC related to tobacco, alters the immune microenvironment by inhibiting the STING pathway. In mouse models of oral cancer with a p53 gene mutation similar to human OSCC, researchers observed that p53 mutations increased the resistance of M2 macrophages and Tregs to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), whereas STING agonists successfully improved the sensitivity of tumours to anti- PD-1 mAb, developing effective immunotherapeutic approaches for OSCC and other ICI-resistant solid tumours.43 Chen et al. found through establishing a mouse OSCC model that P.g-OMVs significantly inhibit cGAS-STING innate immune signalling, weaken IFN-β production and promote immune evasion by limiting the recruitment of natural killer cells and dendritic cells to the tumour microenvironment, once again demonstrating the important role of the cGAS-STING axis in anti-tumour immunity.44 Moreover, casein kinase 2 interacting protein 1 (CKIP-1) is a scaffold protein that was found to have a higher expression in OSCC tissues. It has been demonstrated that CKIP-1 silencing could significantly antagonise OSCC via the TFAM (mitochondrial transcription factor A)/cGAS-STING axis, which may be a new approach for OSCC treatment.45 Human papilloma viruses (HPV) are a major cause of oropharyngeal cancer, and recent studies have shown that in HPV16-induced oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) cells, the oncogene E7 can block the cGAS-STING response, whereas knocking out this gene results in normal expression of the cGAS-STING pathway. Future work may involve treating related tumours by using STING agonists to promote anti-tumour responses and ameliorate tumour progression.46

Similar to tumours in other parts of the body, not all activation of the cGAS-STING pathway is beneficial for the body to combat oral tumours, and in some cases may have the opposite effect. Researchers have found that the expression of cGAS increases the recurrence rate of OSSC and reduces the survival rate of OSSC patients, which is related to cGAS-STING mediating tumour cell dormancy and helping them to adapt to the immune microenvironment. Therefore, targeting the cGAS-STING–mediated dormancy may be a potential strategy for preventing OSCC recurrence and metastasis.47 Necroptosis plays a key role in driving tumour cell invasion and lymph node metastasis through the release of tumour type–dependent DAMPs. Widespread necroptosis and high expression of tumour cells are associated with tumour progression and low survival rates in HNSCC patients. Previous studies have shown that the intrinsic coordination between necroptosis and cGAS-STING signalling leads to the production of a set of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) as HNSCC-dependent necroptotic DAMPs. Blocking the release of ISG 15 or the interaction between ISG 15 and the necroptotic microenvironment can significantly inhibit epithelial to mesenchymal transition and lymph node metastasis driven by necroptosis. This provides us with some insights into how blocking cGAS-STING may be an effective way to control tumour progression and metastasis.

Overall, the cGAS-STING pathway plays an important role in the setiopathology of oral diseases. In inflammatory diseases, this pathway is typically the main innate immune response used to detect the antibacterial response of microbial DNA. However, as the disease progresses, it can also exacerbate tissue damage. In autoimmune diseases, pathway activation is usually dysregulated, acting as a mediator driven by tissue damage, cell death and abnormal immune responses. For tumours, this is a complex and environment-dependent process. Initially, cGAS-STING may exert tumour-suppressive effects by promoting immune surveillance, but chronic activation may lead to a pro-tumour environment that supports tumour progression.

Targeting cGAS-STING for therapeutic directions

The cGAS-STING pathway serves as a potent dual-natured driver in disease pathogenesis, capable of promoting both chronic inflammation and anti-tumour immune responses. This dual nature presents both unique therapeutic opportunities and significant challenges in oral and maxillofacial diseases. Within the oral cavity, the diseased microenvironment is frequently saturated with endogenous DNA ligands, which perpetuate pathological cGAS-STING activation. Consequently, a viable strategy would require ultra-precise, sustained-release biomaterials (e.g. hydrogels) that deliver a low, immunoresolutive dose to potentially restore mucosal barrier function in early lesions. Crucially, the efficacy of a cGAMP-scavenging hydrogel in promoting periodontal restoration demonstrates that inhibition, not activation, is currently the more rational local approach for established diseases.48 Conversely, using cGAS-STING agonists with rational combination therapies in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) presents a potential therapeutic strategy.

To develop targeted control strategies for oral diseases, a better mechanistic understanding of immunometabolism49 and the tissue-specific roles of the cGAS-STING axis in various diseases is essential, as this knowledge is likely to directly inform therapy. The regulation of IFN-I-related pathways is crucial for maintaining homeostasis in the body. IFNs activate both innate and adaptive immunity to promote anti-tumour effects by stimulating and activating DC. The cGAS-STING pathway can be harnessed to enhance the immune system’s ability to detect and attack tumour cells. Although manganese (Mn) is a trace element required for a variety of physiological processes, its immune function is largely unknown. Interestingly, Zn2+ or Mn2+-pre-immunised mice had significantly suppressed tumour growth, and both elements synergistically improved the immunotherapy efficacy in mouse models via cGAS-STING.50 Given these, metallo-immunotherapy could be used as an anti-tumour strategy with clinical potential. On the other hand, some viruses have evolved mechanisms to suppress the cGAS-STING pathway. Therefore, developing drugs that counteract viral evasion mechanisms could improve antiviral therapies.51 Meanwhile, overactivation of the cGAS-STING pathway is implicated in several autoimmune diseases. cGAS inhibitors are being explored as potential treatments for these conditions by dampening the overactive immune responses.52 However, this approach requires thorough consideration, as a complete suppression of the cGAS-STING pathway could lead to increased susceptibility to tumours or infections.

There are certainly challenges in targeting the cGAS-STING pathways such as balancing activation, immunosuppressive factors contained in the tumour microenvironment and risk of off-target side effects. In conclusion, the development of chemical inhibitors targeting the cGAS-STING axis will unlock potential therapeutic benefits for treating viral, autoimmune or inflammatory diseases as well as cancer. While the potential benefits are significant, ongoing research is necessary to fully understand the complexities of the cGAS-STUNG pathways and to develop safe and effective therapies that can be applied in clinical settings.
