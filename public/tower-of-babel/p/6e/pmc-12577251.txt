Evaluating the effectiveness of psychological first aid training for disaster nursing: a mixed-method study
Authors: Emel Bahadır Yılmaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychological first aid (PFA) training significantly improves appropriate psychosocial response and PFA skills in supporting people in acute distress, thus increasing self-efficacy and promoting resilience. Nevertheless, nurses’ perceptions of disaster preparedness are not at the desired level. More studies are needed to increase the knowledge, skills and preparedness of nurses for better disaster preparedness and management in disaster situations.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of psychological first aid training on nursing students’ perception of self-efficacy in disaster intervention and disaster preparedness.MethodsThis mixed-method study was conducted in a nursing school in Türkiye. The sample consisted of 62 fourth-year nursing students during the fall semester of 2023. Thirty-two nursing students in the intervention group participated in psychological first aid training. The control group consisted of 30 nursing students who did not participate in the training. Data were collected with the Descriptive Information Form, Metaphor Determination Form on Psychological First Aid, Preparedness Perception Scale of Disaster in Nurses, and Disaster Response Self-Efficacy Scale. Independent samples t test and dependent samples t test were used to compare scale scores. Metaphor analysis was used in qualitative data analysis.ResultsThe mean age differed significantly between groups (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed regarding gender, disaster exposure, or willingness to work in disaster areas (p > 0.05). Total self-efficacy level in disaster response, on-site rescue competency subscale, disaster psychological nursing competency subscale, and disaster role quality and adaptation competency subscale scores showed a statistically significant difference between the groups (p < 0.05). Total perception of disaster preparedness and after the disaster stage subscale scores showed a statistically significant difference between the groups (p < 0.05). Four categories were obtained in the metaphor analysis: need-related, help-related, growth and development-related, and struggle-related.ConclusionsThe study findings indicate that disaster nursing education significantly enhances nursing students’ self-efficacy in disaster response and their perception of disaster preparedness. Integrating disaster preparedness and psychological first aid (PFA) into nursing curricula can better equip students for disaster response.Keywords: First aid, Psychological, Nursing students, Efficacy, Self, Disaster nursing, Research, Qualitative

Introduction

On 6 February 2023, a very large earthquake occurred in Türkiye, which has been named the Kahramanmaraş Earthquake. According to the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Interior, as of 8 February 2024, a total of 53,537 citizens lost their lives and 107,213 citizens were injured in the provinces of Kahramanmaraş, Gaziantep, Şanlıurfa, Diyarbakır, Adana, Adıyaman, Osmaniye, Hatay, Kilis, Malatya, and Elazığ [1]. Türkiye is located on the Alpine-Himalayan orogenic belt, one of the most active earthquake zones in the world. This makes the country extremely vulnerable to both large and destructive earthquakes [2]. Earthquakes are classified as natural disasters. Disasters pose significant physical and psychological challenges for affected populations worldwide. Nurses are often required to respond not only to immediate injuries but also to acute psychological distress among survivors [3]. Although most healthcare personnel are experienced in first aid and basic life support, they often lack training in identifying psychological needs and providing appropriate interventions for disaster victims [4].

In disasters such as earthquakes, people lose their loved ones and homes, may be forced to leave their families or communities and witness intensity, destruction and death. All these traumatic experiences they endure and witness can lead to serious mental health problems. In a study examining the prevalence of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety among elderly earthquake survivors in China, Japan, Nepal, and Mexico, the prevalence rates were 19.3% for PTSD, 23.5% for depression, and 10.9% for anxiety [5]. In a post-earthquake study conducted in Nepal, 43.2% of university students reported depressive symptoms and 19.2% reported PTSD symptoms, with the likelihood of these symptoms increasing as earthquake exposure increased [6]. A cross-sectional study involving 461 earthquake survivors after the 2023 Kahramanmaraş reported prevalence rates of 17.4% for PTSD, 61.2% for depression, 79.4% for anxiety, and 58.4% for stress [7].

When the studies are examined, psychological first aid (PFA) training has been found to significantly improve appropriate psychosocial responses and PFA skills in supporting individuals in acute distress, thereby increasing self-efficacy and promoting resilience [8]. PFA is an evidence-based, humane support approach implemented after a crisis, disaster, traumatic event, or emergency situation to help affected individuals feel safe, identify their basic needs, reduce stress reactions, and guide them toward further support [5]. The dimensions of Psychological First Aid (PFA) are based on providing holistic support to individuals following a traumatic event. These dimensions are categorized into five main areas: cognitive, emotional, behavioral, social, and cultural. The cognitive dimension involves understanding the individual’s thoughts and perceptions related to the event, while the emotional dimension addresses feelings such as anxiety, fear, and sadness. The behavioral dimension includes concrete assistance such as ensuring safety and meeting basic needs. The social dimension encourages connection with support systems, and the cultural dimension emphasizes sensitivity to the individual’s values and belief systems [9].

For disaster victims, PFA helps reestablish a sense of safety, provides emotional comfort, and connects them with essential services and social support systems, thereby promoting adaptive functioning and recovery [10]. For rescuers and frontline workers, receiving PFA training and support enhances their coping mechanisms, empathy, and emotional preparedness, reducing burnout and compassion fatigue [11, 12]. Evidence suggests that structured PFA interventions not only improve perceived competence and empathy in responders but also serve as protective factors against psychological strain during crises [13]. Thus, incorporating PFA into emergency preparedness protocols benefits both recipients and providers of care in disaster contexts. It has been determined that nursing and midwifery students who participated in psychological first aid training improved their mental health knowledge, increased their confidence in providing support, showed greater willingness to offer assistance, and developed less stigmatizing attitudes toward individuals with mental health problems [14]. However, studies conducted with nursing students on this subject appear to be quite limited.

It is believed that conducting more studies on how to implement and culturally adapt PFA training would make a significant contribution to the field [8]. Healthcare workers who care for disaster victims and help meet their needs should possess knowledge and preparedness related to disasters [15]. However, it has been reported that nurses’ perceptions of disaster preparedness are not at the desired level [16, 17]. A study conducted with psychiatric nurses indicates that their perception of disaster preparedness is at a moderate level, and 67.2% of the nurses state that they only partially know what to do during a disaster [18]. A study conducted in Indonesia found that only half of the nurses rated their disaster preparedness competencies as high [19].

More studies are needed to enhance the knowledge, skills, and preparedness of nurses for improved disaster readiness and management in disaster situations [20]. It is important to integrate these preparations, practices, and trainings into vocational nursing education. Nurses are among the first healthcare professionals to respond on the ground during disasters. Training received during the student period prepares them for rapid and effective intervention in times of crisis. Such training enhances nursing students’ crisis management skills, boosting their self-confidence and psychological resilience. Embedding this awareness during vocational education supports not only individual preparedness but also teamwork, coordination, and leadership skills. Therefore, this mixed-method study aims to assess the effect of psychological first aid training on disaster preparedness perception and disaster response self-efficacy. This study design will both evaluate the effectiveness of the training and reveal nursing students’ perceptions of the training. Revealing nursing students’ perceptions and identifying the benefits and shortcomings of the training will also provide guidance for future studies.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is a mixed-methods research design consisting of two components. In the first phase, a pretest-posttest quasi-experimental study with a non-randomized control group was conducted to evaluate the effect of psychological first aid training on nursing students’ self-efficacy in disaster intervention and their perception of disaster preparedness. The second phase employed a qualitative approach to explore nursing students’ metaphors related to psychological first aid training through metaphor analysis. This method was chosen as it offers a deeper understanding of behaviors and provides greater insight into the meaning behind participants’ experiences [21]. The Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) checklist was used to guide data reporting [22].

Population and sample

The population of the study consisted of 90 senior nursing enrolled in the Department of Nursing at XXX University, Faculty of Health Sciences. Purposive sampling method was employed. The sample size was calculated using the Raosoft Sample Size Calculator (http://www.raosoft.com/samplesize.html) and determined to be 67, based on a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error and an expected response rate of 80%. Initially 71 nursing students participated in the study, but the quasi-experimental study has been completed with 62 nursing students. In the post hoc analysis conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 with the post-test DRSES mean scores, the effect size was calculated as d = 0.855. According to Cohen’s classification of effect sizes, a value of 0.855 indicates a large effect size in the comparison of independent means [23]. In the second phase, where the metaphor analysis was conducted, the sample consisted of 32 nursing students from the intervention group.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: being a final-year nursing student, being over 18 years of age, not having received psychological first aid training previously, volunteering to participate in the research, and being able to generate well-articulated and readily comprehensible metaphors. The exclusion criteria included having incomplete or missing data collection forms and being absent from the sessions more than once.

Data collection forms

Descriptive information form

The questionnaire was prepared by the researchers and consisted of seven items: gender, age, department, first aid training status, psychological first aid training status, exposure to any disaster, and willingness to work in a disaster area.

Metaphor determination form on psychological first aid

This form was prepared by the researcher to explore nursing students’ perceptions of psychological first aid following the training through the use of metaphors. Nursing students were asked to complete the sentence “Psychological first aid training is similar to … because …” by providing a metaphor and explaining the reason for the metaphor.

Preparedness perception scale of disaster in nurses (PPSDN)

The scale has been developed by Ozcan [24]. It consists of three dimensions as preparation stage subscale (six items), intervention stage subscale (nine items), and after the disaster stage subscale (five items). The 5-point Likert-type scale consists of 20 items. Scores range from 6 to 30 for the Preparation Stage subscale, from 9 to 45 for the Intervention Stage subscale, and from 5 to 25 for the After the Disaster Stage subscale. High scores obtained from the scale indicate that the perception of disaster preparedness is also high. A high score on the first subscale indicates good disaster preparedness; a high score on the second subscale reflects the ability to carry out necessary interventions and procedures during a disaster; and a high score on the third subscale demonstrates the ability to provide psychological support and meet the needs of disaster victims in the post-disaster period. Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale has been calculated as 0.90; 0.89 for preparation stage subscale, 0.89 for intervention stage subscale, and 0.90 for after the disaster stage subscale.

Disaster response self-efficacy scale (DRSES)

The scale has been developed by Li et al. [25]. The Turkish validity and reliability of the scale has been conducted by Koca et al. [26]. It consists of three dimensions: On-site rescue competency subscale (11 items), disaster psychological nursing competency subscale (four items), and disaster role quality and adaptation competency subscale (four items). The 5-point Likert-type scale consists of 19 items. High scores indicate that self-efficacy in disaster response is high. A high score on the first subscale indicates competence in rescue efforts; a high score on the second subscale reflects psychological intervention competence in disaster situations; and a high score on the third subscale demonstrates a strong ability to adapt to the situation. Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale has been calculated as 0.96; 0.92 for on-site rescue competency, 0.94 for disaster psychological nursing competency, and 0.96 for disaster role quality and adaptation competency.

Data collection

This study was conducted between 15 October 2023 and 26 November 2023. Data were collected using the Sociodemographic Information Form, the Self-Efficacy in Disaster Response Scale, and the Disaster Preparedness Perception Scale. Completing the data collection forms took approximately 15–20 min, and the forms were administered in classrooms through face-to-face explanations. A randomization method was not used. Instead, 41 nursing students who voluntarily chose to participate in the training were included in the intervention group. However, nine students who did not complete the posttest forms were excluded from the study, resulting in an intervention group of 32 students. The control group consisted of 30 nursing students who chose not to participate in the training but voluntarily completed both the pretest and posttest forms (Fig. 1).

Preparation and application of training

This programme is based on the resource “Psychological first aid: Guide for field workers” created by the World Health Organization (WHO) [27]. The programme has been adapted with some modifications by the researcher. On 6 February 2023, the programme was expanded to include content on grief reactions and post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms, in order to better address the psychosocial needs of individuals requiring psychological first aid following the significant losses and trauma caused by the Kahramanmaraş Earthquake. In this disaster, healthcare workers also experienced profound pain and trauma. Therefore, an additional topic was incorporated to address the identification and management of their own needs while providing psychological first aid. In-group discussions were supported using therapeutic communication techniques.

Based on the feedback received from nursing students prior to the implementation of the program, it was assessed that they lacked sufficient helping communication skills and were unsure about how to manage the grieving process. Therefore, the topics of sessions 1, 2, 4, and 6 from the WHO’s Psychological First Aid guide were adopted. In addition, sessions 3 and 5, which address helping communication skills and grief response, were developed and added by the researcher (Table 1). Expert opinions were obtained from two academicians who completed their doctorates in the field of psychiatric nursing for the training to be implemented, and the final version was prepared accordingly.

Application of post-tests

The programme was applied to the intervention group face-to-face in the classroom. The programme is an additional course consisting of six sessions and was conducted on Mondays between 15.30 and 17.00 h for 90 min (Table 1). The programme was implemented by the researcher, an associate professor in the field of psychiatric nursing who has received training in psychological first aid. Teaching methods included lecturing, interactive question-and-answer sessions, and case presentations. On 6 February 2023, experiences related to the Kahramanmaraş Earthquake were shared as part of the training.

Data analysis

The data were analysed in SPSS 25.0 package programme. Descriptive statistics such as number, percentage, mean and standard deviation values were used to analyse the sociodemographic characteristics of nursing students. Sociodemographic characteristics other than age were compared with chi-square test. Scale scores were compared between groups using independent samples t-test, and paired samples t-test was used for pre-post comparisons within groups. Normality was assessed by Shapiro-Wilk test, skewness, and kurtosis values. p value was accepted as p < 0.05.

Metaphor analysis

The analysis of the collected data was conducted in five stages, drawing upon various sources for methodological guidance [28–30].

Reading phase

Each nursing student was assigned a code (e.g., S1, S2, S3). Metaphors provided by nursing students regarding psychological first aid were meticulously read multiple and the underlying explanations conveyed through their metaphors. Statements that lacked comprehensibility or provided insufficient explanation were excluded from the evaluation process.

Metaphors creation phase

During this phase, marginal notes were taken alongside nursing students’ explanations to capture their expressed emotions or the events they associated with the metaphors (e.g., tree, water, need). These notes were derived from both the metaphors and the explanations accompanying them.

Categories creation phase

Subsequently, the metaphors were thoroughly reviewed and categorized based on their shared characteristics, resulting in the formation of four distinct thematic groups. Existing literature on the subject was consulted, and brainstorming sessions were conducted to finalize the categories within these groups.

Validation phase

The Miles and Huberman model was used in the validation stage to ensure internal consistency and determine the reliability of the dataset. Achieving consensus among different coders was essential, and the reliability coefficient was calculated using the following formula: Δ = ∁ ÷ (∁ + 𝜕) × 100, where Δ represents the reliability coefficient, ∁ is the number of items with coder agreement, and 𝜕 is the number of items without agreement. According to coding audit standards, a minimum consensus level of 80% is generally expected to ensure internal consistency [28]. In addition to the researchers, the analysis incorporated the evaluations of two independent experts—both doctoral-level psychiatric nurses. The consensus between these two experts exceeded 80%: “(27) ÷ (27 + 5) × 100 = 84.4.”

Presentation of data

At this stage, the metaphors created by the nursing students were transferred to IBM SPSS Statistics 25 and presented using numerical values. To support the identified main themes and metaphors, direct quotations from the students’ statements were included. Each quotation was accompanied by a code in brackets to indicate the source of the statement.

Trusthworthiness of qualitative data

The reliability of the qualitative data was established using four main criteria: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. For credibility, two doctoral lecturers were consulted during data analysis, and participants were asked to verify the data. Findings were described in rich and detailed detail to demonstrate the applicability of the results to other settings or groups. For dependability and confirmability, the methods and process used in the research were recorded in detail and transparently [31, 32].

Ethical dimension

Ethics approval was obtained from Ethics Committee of the University (Date: 01.03.2023, No: 03/20). All participants gave written informed consent. The research has been conducted and finalised in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the rights of the participants have been observed in their best interests.

Category 1: Growth and development-related

In this category, nursing students used metaphors that conveyed the idea that psychological support contributes to a person’s growth and development, helping them re-engage with life and adapt to it. In this category, nursing students used metaphors such as the sprouting of a sapling, the reading of a book, the healing of wounds, the blooming of flowers in spring, and the growth of a tree.

Saving each individual, whether psychologically or physically, offers them an opportunity to start life anew. (S3)

An individual who has experienced a disaster is like a tree with dried branches or a flower with withered petals; receiving psychological first aid allows them to be revived and renewed. (S10)

I believe that psychological first aid is essential for helping individuals in crisis—those who feel lost, helpless, and without resources—return to life, adapt to their circumstances, and receive the vital support they need, like life-giving water. (S25)

Category 2: Struggle-related

In this category, nursing students used metaphors to express that psychological first aid supports an individual’s ability to cope with and navigate life’s struggles. In this category, nursing students produced metaphors such as taking control, martial arts, moonlight, safe harbour, and saviour intervention.

No matter how dark the night or how difficult the experiences we endure, the moonlight breaking through the clouds soothes our fears and helps us see the path ahead. (S14)

It reminds us that even in the face of life’s greatest challenges, we should not be afraid—and that we are capable of overcoming them. (S20)

We never know when or where something might happen to us, so we must always be prepared. (S30)

Category 3: Help-related

The metaphors in this category focused on the theme of people helping and supporting one another. Nursing students used metaphors such as a mirror, help, lending a hand to someone on the edge, giving water to someone in the desert, sharing, and providing support.

We help the other person overcome their trauma more easily. (S4)

Even when physical assistance is provided, psychological support is often overlooked—yet it is a crucial factor in helping those who feel helpless. (S9)

Even if the society resists psychological help, we can still recognize the need and reach out to offer support. (S19)

Category 4: Need-related

In this category, nursing students emphasized psychological first aid as a fundamental human need. In this category, nursing students used metaphors such as pouring water on a burning forest, trusting and loving, basic need, basic requirement, and basic first aid to describe psychological first aid.

I believe that psychological first aid is not given the attention it deserves during disasters. In my opinion, it should be valued just as much as physical first aid. (S23)

Talking and communicating are ordinary parts of our daily lives, just like eating, sheltering, and excreting. (S24)

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of psychological first aid training on senior nursing students’ perceptions of self-efficacy in disaster intervention and their level of disaster preparedness. The training led to significant improvements in both the students’ self-efficacy in responding to disasters and their perception of disaster preparedness.

Perception of disaster preparedness

Nurses’ perceptions of disaster preparedness are generally at a moderate level [16, 17]. Many nurses report that they receive insufficient training on disasters and express a clear need for further education in this area [33]. Interestingly, those who acknowledge the need for disaster training tend to have higher perceptions of disaster preparedness [34]. Addressing this gap, the present study aims to enhance nursing students’ perceptions of disaster preparedness through psychological first aid (PFA) training. The findings show that students’ perceptions of disaster preparedness improve significantly following the training. Previous research also demonstrates that PFA training for nursing students leads to increased disaster preparedness perceptions [35]. These results underscore the importance of integrating PFA training into undergraduate nursing curricula or offering regular courses and webinars on this topic.

Self-efficacy in disaster response

While nurses are expected to possess basic competencies in disaster intervention and management, studies have shown that their competencies—particularly in areas such as critical thinking—are often inadequate [36]. In the present study, psychological first aid (PFA) training was designed to enhance nursing students’ self-efficacy, and the findings indicated a significant increase in their self-efficacy in disaster intervention following the training. This improvement also encompassed psychological competence. Similarly, previous research demonstrated that PFA training for healthcare workers enhances their ability to assess and intervene in the psychosocial needs of individuals affected by disasters [37]. The e-learning-based PFA training provided to nurses increased their competence in disaster situations and led to a decrease in the personal distress dimension of empathy while enhancing their perspective-taking ability [38].

Psychological first aid is a method used to identify an individual’s physical and psychological needs in an acute situation, understand their reactions to the event, and support them in coping with the experience [7]. From this perspective, effective implementation of PFA in disaster response requires a high level of self-efficacy among those providing the support. In this study, psychological first aid training led to increased levels of on-site rescue competency, disaster psychological nursing competency, and disaster role quality and adaptation competency among nursing students. Education and training, technological advancement, psychological support, and interprofessional collaboration play critical roles in strengthening disaster nursing preparedness [39]. Nursing students typically participate in disaster-related training and drills at low rates, which limits their competence in disaster response [40]. Moreover, their disaster management literacy remains low, highlighting a need for additional workshops, training sessions, and drills [41]. Together, these findings indicate that nursing education programs should incorporate more comprehensive and practical training in disaster management and psychological preparedness. Updating curricula in this direction is essential to ensure that nurses can respond effectively to disasters.

Perceptions on psychological first aid

The metaphors created by nursing students regarding psychological support revealed that PFA is perceived as a fundamental need, promotes growth and development, fosters cooperation, and contributes to individuals’ ability to cope with life’s challenges. Psychological first aid is a psychological crisis intervention designed to enhance the psychological resilience of individuals experiencing trauma or acute distress [42]. PFA training is shown to improve healthcare professionals’ understanding and acquisition of appropriate psychosocial responses and skills in supporting individuals facing acute adversities [37]. A recent study demonstrates that PFA training enhances healthcare workers’ emotional regulation, listening and communication skills, and self-confidence [43]. Research with social work students indicates that the training improves confidence, disaster preparedness, and self-care strategies needed for providing psychosocial support to individuals and families [44]. Similarly, Zhang et al. [45] report that PFA training increases nursing students’ resilience and self-efficacy. Although these studies do not directly measure perception, they effectively evaluate the overall impact of the training. The qualitative findings of the present study align with the results of these previous studies.

Qualitative findings

The metaphors that reflect themes of need, growth, development, cooperation, and struggle—shared by nursing students participating in PFA training—demonstrate that the training achieves its intended purpose and empowers students to recognize the emotions and needs of disaster victims. This training teaches nursing students how to identify and address the needs of disaster victims while increasing their awareness of their own needs. It also shows them how to approach and communicate with individuals affected by disasters. Said et al. [11] report that PFA training enhances nurses’ ability to cope with psychological challenges during emergencies and disasters. The training increases nurses’ competencies in recognizing post-disaster psychological responses, developing stress management strategies, and regulating both their own emotional reactions and those of their patients. Furthermore, Mtiraoui et al. [12] find that PFA training significantly improves nursing students’ perceived competencies, disaster preparedness, and capacity to provide assistance. The training equips nurses with skills to approach and communicate with disaster victims more effectively, enabling them to provide psychological support in a more competent manner.

Limitations

This study is the first to use a mixed-methods approach to evaluate the effectiveness of psychological first aid (PFA) training. While participant reflections indicate the training was beneficial, some limitations should be noted. The findings are based on a specific sample and cannot be generalized. Metaphor analysis was used to better understand students’ perceptions, but future phenomenological research could explore their experiences in more depth. Results rely on self-reported data, which may differ among individuals, so further metaphor-based studies are recommended. In addition, randomization was not possible due to the curriculum-based design, and students joined the study voluntarily. Future research should consider randomized controlled trials for stronger evidence.

Conclusion

According to the results of this study, psychological first aid training enhanced nursing students’ perceptions of disaster preparedness and increased their self-efficacy in disaster response. The training proved to be especially impactful in improving competencies related to actions and decisions during the After the Disaster Stage. These findings indicate that the integration of psychological first aid training into nursing education can significantly strengthen students’ readiness and effectiveness in disaster management and post-disaster intervention contexts. The qualitative findings revealed that nursing students internalized the purpose of psychological first aid training, describing it with metaphors that reflected need, cooperation, and personal growth. These perceptions not only underscore the meaningful impact of the training, but also demonstrate how students embraced PFA as both a supportive process and a catalyst for professional development.

Nurse educators must recognize the importance of PFA training in enhancing nursing students’ self-efficacy in disaster response. Traditional first aid courses included in nursing curricula often do not address the psychological aspects of care. However, PFA training is relatively simple, easy to understand, and highly practical. Therefore, it is essential to integrate PFA training into undergraduate nursing curricula. In this context, it is recommended that PFA training programs be developed, implemented, and continuously evaluated based on feedback from nursing students to ensure relevance and effectiveness.
