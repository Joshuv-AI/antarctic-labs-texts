Emotion processing in schizophrenia and its relation to μ-opioid receptor availability
Authors: Ekaterina Shatalina, Abhishekh H Ashok, Matthew B Wall, Jim Myers, Tiago Reis Marques, Eugenii A Rabiner, Oliver D Howes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Schizophrenia shows intact hedonic-network activation during emotion tasks.•Baseline μ-opioid receptor availability did not predict emotion-evoked BOLD in either group.•μ-Opioid receptor density in the anterior cingulate was inversely related to emotional faces-evoked BOLD in controls.•Whole-brain differences in emotion-evoked BOLD were in visual regions, with greater activity in schizophrenia.Keywords: Mu opioid receptor, Emotion processing, Functional MRI, PET

Introduction

Schizophrenia is associated with negative symptoms such as emotional blunting and anhedonia (Galderisi et al., 2018, Howes et al., 2023). Emotion processing deficits, particularly in the identification and recognition of facial expressions, are well documented in people with schizophrenia and correlate with symptom severity (Amini et al., 2023). Meta-analyses of brain activation during facial emotion processing in schizophrenia have reported reduced activation in regions such as the bilateral amygdala (Anticevic et al., 2012, Taylor et al., 2012), parahippocampal gyrus, frontal cortex, and temporal lobe (Delvecchio et al., 2013, Li et al., 2010). Similarly, studies using emotionally salient pictures from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) have found reduced activation in schizophrenia patients in the amygdala and connected limbic regions when viewing pleasant or unpleasant images (Takahashi et al., 2004, Morris et al., 2012, Pankow et al., 2013). However, not all studies reported significant group differences (Hägele et al., 2016, Ursu et al., 2011), suggesting heterogeneity in findings and the potential influence of confounding factors.

Brain opioid receptors are key neuromodulators of hedonic tone and emotion (Amini et al., 2023). μ-Opioid receptors (MORs), densely distributed in limbic and cortical regions, regulate affective responses by modulating GABAergic inhibition and dopamine release (Amini et al., 2023). In animal models, MOR dysfunction leads to anhedonic and asocial behaviour: for example, MOR knockout mice exhibit social withdrawal and reduced pleasure-seeking, which can be rescued by MOR agonists (Amini et al., 2023). In humans, neuroimaging studies have shown lower MOR availability in brain regions including the striatum, amygdala, insula, orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) in schizophrenia compared to controls, supporting a role for the endogenous opioid system in the pathophysiology of the disorder’s negative symptoms (Ashok et al., 2019). MORs have been linked to the regulation of mood and response to rewarding stimuli; for instance, higher baseline MOR binding is associated with blunted hemodynamic responses to arousing emotional scenes in healthy individuals (Liberzon et al., 2002, Karjalainen et al., 2019). Additionally, activation of the endogenous opioid system accompanies positive emotional experiences (Koepp et al., 2009) and social distress (Hsu et al., 2013, Hsu et al., 2015) in healthy populations, whereas these opioid responses are blunted in mood disorders (Hsu et al., 2015). Pharmacological studies in humans further highlight the opioid system’s role: blocking MORs with naltrexone slows the recognition of sad and fearful emotional stimuli (Wardle et al., 2016) and reduces the perceived attractiveness of faces (Chelnokova et al., 2014), whereas MOR agonists like morphine can enhance perceived attractiveness of neutral facial expressions (Chelnokova et al., 2014).

Despite this converging evidence that the μ-opioid system influences emotion and reward processing, it remains unknown whether MOR abnormalities in schizophrenia contribute to the functional brain differences seen during emotional tasks. We hypothesised that schizophrenia patients, particularly those with prominent negative symptoms (including anhedonia), would show altered brain activation to emotional stimuli and that this might be associated with lower MOR availability. Specifically, we aimed to (1) compare fMRI activation in key emotion-processing regions (amygdala, ACC, OFC, insula, striatum) between schizophrenia patients and healthy controls during two emotional tasks (viewing emotional IAPS pictures and viewing emotional facial expressions), and (2) test whether regional MOR availability is associated with the magnitude of fMRI activation in these regions.

Methods

Participants

The study was approved by the London Camberwell St. Giles Research Ethics Committee, UK, and the Administration of Radioactive Substances Advisory Committee (ARSAC, UK). All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a complete description of the study.

We recruited 20 patients meeting DSM-5 criteria for schizophrenia from outpatient psychiatric services. Inclusion criteria for patients included moderate severity of negative symptoms (defined as either a score ≥ 4 on at least one item of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) negative subscale, or ≥ 3 on at least two negative subscale items; Kay et al., 1987) to enrich the sample for negative symptoms. All patients were clinically stable and on a stable dose of antipsychotic medication for at least 4 weeks prior to scanning. One patient was excluded due to an incidental structural brain abnormality on MRI, leaving 19 patients in the final sample. Nineteen healthy control volunteers (no current or past psychiatric disorder) were recruited from the same community through advertisements, screened with the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID) to confirm absence of psychopathology. Controls had no family history of psychosis in first-degree relatives. Exclusion criteria for all subjects included: current or recent (within 1 month) substance use disorder (aside from tobacco), history of significant head injury or neurological illness, use of opioids or other psychoactive medications (e.g. antidepressants, mood stabilizers, anticonvulsants) in the month prior to study, and any contraindications to MRI or PET (such as metal implants or pregnancy).

All participants underwent clinical assessments including detailed medical/psychiatric history, substance use history, and physical examination. Symptom severity in patients was assessed with the PANSS (Kay et al., 1987). Negative symptom severity was further quantified using the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS; Andreasen, 1982). Trait anhedonia was measured in all participants using the Revised Social Anhedonia Scale (Eckblad et al., 1982) and Revised Physical Anhedonia Scale (Chapman et al., 1976). Current pleasurable experience was assessed with the Temporal Experience of Pleasure Scale (TEPS), which provides anticipatory and consummatory pleasure subscales. Depressive symptoms were evaluated in patients using the Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia (Addington et al., 1990).

Neuroimaging Acquisition

All subjects underwent both an fMRI and a PET scanning session (Ashok et al., 2019, Shatalina et al., 2023). For each participant, MRI and PET were conducted on the same day or within a few days of each other.

MRI Data Acquisition: MRI scanning was performed at the Invicro Centre for Imaging Sciences (now Perceptive Inc. London, UK) on a 3 Tesla Siemens Verio scanner with a 32-channel head coil. First, a high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical image was acquired for registration (Magnetization Prepared Rapid Gradient Echo, MPRAGE, following the ADNI-GO protocol: 160 sagittal slices, matrix 256 × 240, voxel size 1 × 1 × 1 mm, TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, flip angle = 9°, bandwidth = 240 Hz/pixel). Functional images were then acquired using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with multiband acceleration (University of Minnesota multiband EPI sequence, version WIP v012b). Forty-two slices were acquired in interleaved order (no gap) with the following parameters: TR = 1200 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 62°, voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3 mm, matrix 64 × 64, bandwidth = 1906 Hz/pixel. A multiband factor of 2 and in-plane parallel acceleration (GRAPPA factor 2) were used to achieve high temporal resolution. The first 6 volumes of each run were discarded to allow for T1 equilibration. These acquisition parameters were chosen based on recent advances in multiband fMRI techniques (Auerbach et al., 2013, Setsompop et al., 2012, Cauley et al., 2014, Xu et al., 2013) to optimise signal-to-noise and spatial coverage and based on sequences previously tested and validated by Demetriou et al., (Demetriou et al., 2018).

Emotion Processing Tasks: We employed two functional tasks during fMRI to probe emotional processing, all participants performed the tasks in the same order, with the IAPS task first, followed by the Faces task. The neural basis underlying the deficits in processing of emotional faces vs other emotional stimuli could differ in schizophrenia, thus we included two tasks which engaged different aspects of emotion processing, to have coverage of both social (face-specific) and non-social (scene-based) affective processing, and to distinguish domain-specific from general emotion-processing alterations.

•IAPS Picture Viewing Task: This task probes neural responses to emotional scenes (Fahim et al., 2004, Lang et al., 1999, Pankow et al., 2013). Participants viewed photographs from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS; Lang, Bradley & Cuthbert, 1999) of three valence categories: pleasant (positive) images, unpleasant (negative) images, and neutral images. We excluded any extremely graphic content (e.g., mutilation, sexual content) to focus on normal emotional experiences. A total of 25 images per category (pleasant, unpleasant, neutral) were selected. Stimuli were presented in a block design. Each block consisted of 5 images of the same valence shown for 3 s each (15 s per block). Participants rated each image’s emotional impact on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“very unpleasant”) to 5 (“very pleasant”) via a button box. Ten blocks of each category were presented, interspersed with an equal number of 15-second rest blocks (fixation cross). Block order was pseudorandomized with the constraint that no two blocks of the same category occurred consecutively. The entire task lasted approximately 10 min (40 blocks total, plus an initial fixation period), yielding 508 fMRI volumes.

IAPS Picture Viewing Task: This task probes neural responses to emotional scenes (Fahim et al., 2004, Lang et al., 1999, Pankow et al., 2013). Participants viewed photographs from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS; Lang, Bradley & Cuthbert, 1999) of three valence categories: pleasant (positive) images, unpleasant (negative) images, and neutral images. We excluded any extremely graphic content (e.g., mutilation, sexual content) to focus on normal emotional experiences. A total of 25 images per category (pleasant, unpleasant, neutral) were selected. Stimuli were presented in a block design. Each block consisted of 5 images of the same valence shown for 3 s each (15 s per block). Participants rated each image’s emotional impact on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“very unpleasant”) to 5 (“very pleasant”) via a button box. Ten blocks of each category were presented, interspersed with an equal number of 15-second rest blocks (fixation cross). Block order was pseudorandomized with the constraint that no two blocks of the same category occurred consecutively. The entire task lasted approximately 10 min (40 blocks total, plus an initial fixation period), yielding 508 fMRI volumes.

•Facial Emotion Recognition Task: This task examines neural responses to facial affect. We used a similar block design, presenting photographs of faces expressing happy, sad, or neutral emotions (Ekman-style facial expression stimuli) (Tottenham et al., 2009). Each block (15 s) contained 5 faces of the same emotion, each displayed for 3 s. We included rest blocks with a fixation cross as baseline. There were 8 blocks of each facial emotion and 8 rest blocks (32 blocks total), in pseudorandom order. The task duration was ∼ 8 min (plus 10 s buffer), yielding 408 volumes. To ensure attention to the faces without explicitly demanding emotion recognition, participants performed an orthogonal gender discrimination task: pressing one button if the face was male and another if female.

Facial Emotion Recognition Task: This task examines neural responses to facial affect. We used a similar block design, presenting photographs of faces expressing happy, sad, or neutral emotions (Ekman-style facial expression stimuli) (Tottenham et al., 2009). Each block (15 s) contained 5 faces of the same emotion, each displayed for 3 s. We included rest blocks with a fixation cross as baseline. There were 8 blocks of each facial emotion and 8 rest blocks (32 blocks total), in pseudorandom order. The task duration was ∼ 8 min (plus 10 s buffer), yielding 408 volumes. To ensure attention to the faces without explicitly demanding emotion recognition, participants performed an orthogonal gender discrimination task: pressing one button if the face was male and another if female.

fMRI Preprocessing: Functional MRI data were analyzed using FSL (FMRIB Software Library) version 5.0.4. Preprocessing included motion correction (MCFLIRT), high-pass temporal filtering (100 s cutoff), and spatial smoothing with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Each participant’s functional images were co-registered to their own T1-weighted structural image and then normalized to the MNI152 standard brain (2 mm isotropic resolution) using affine and nonlinear transformations. High motion for a subject was defined as a mean relative root-mean square displacement that exceeds 0.5 mm. For subjects with high motion, plots of their mean and relative displacement were visually inspected and if high motion affected over 30 consecutive volumes, they were excluded from the analyses. Task responses were evaluated to confirm participants were engaged and focused on the task.

For each task, first-level statistical analysis was carried out using the general linear model (GLM) in FSL’s FEAT module. Regressors for each condition block (IAPS: pleasant, unpleasant, neutral; Faces: happy, sad, neutral) were convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function. Extended motion regressors were included in the model as nuisance regressors. First-level contrasts of interest were defined to identify brain activation for emotional stimuli (of both positive and negative valence) relative to neutral stimuli or basline activity. In the IAPS task, the main contrast was pleasant + unpleasant > neutral; effect of task was confirmed for each group. In the faces task, the main contrast of interest was happy + sad > baseline, as there was no effect of task in the happy + sad > neutral contrast, which is common for tasks with similar designs (Nord et al., 2017, Wall et al., 2025). Pooling of pleasant/unpleasant and happy/sad into the same contrasts was done to increase power. Contrast images from each subject were carried to higher-level analysis.

Primary analyses of interest were region-of-interest (ROI) analyses focusing on key regions of the brain’s “hedonic network” involved in emotion and reward processing. Based on prior literature on pleasure and emotion circuits in humans (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2015), we selected bilateral anatomical ROIs for the amygdala, striatum (including nucleus accumbens), insular cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, and ACC. These ROIs were defined in standard space using the CIC Neuroanatomical atlas (Tziortzi et al., 2011) to spatially match ROIs extracted from PET analyses. For each subject mean parameter estimates for each ROI were extracted for the main task contrasts. These values were used for further statistical analyses. Additionally, we tested group differences at the whole-brain level for each contrast using mixed-effects modelling (FSL FLAME). Results were thresholded at Z > 2.3 with cluster-wise correction at p < 0.05 for multiple comparisons across the whole brain.

PET Data Acquisition: PET scans were conducted to measure MOR availability using the selective MOR agonist radioligand [11C]-carfentanil. Participants were scanned on a Siemens Biograph HiRez XVI PET/CT scanner. A bolus of [11C]-carfentanil (mean injected activity ∼ 200 MBq, injected mass < 30 μg, specific activity > 100 GBq/μmol at time of injection) was administered intravenously. A dynamic emission scan was acquired for 90 min in 3D mode. During PET scanning, subjects lay at rest in the scanner. Attenuation correction was performed with a low-dose CT. PET images were reconstructed with corrections for attenuation, scatter, and deadtime, yielding a series of frames (increasing duration from 15 s to 5 min) for kinetic analysis.

PET Data Processing: Dynamic PET data were reconstructed into 26 frames (6 × 30 s, 3 × 1 min, 2 × 2.5 min, 15 × 5 min frames). Motion correction was applied using SPM12 (frames realigned to a summed image). Each participant’s PET image was co-registered to their MRI using a rigid-body transformation. Parametric images of MOR availability were generated using the simplified reference tissue model (SRTM) with a reference region devoid of MORs. We selected the occipital cortex (a region with minimal MOR expression) as the reference region (Liberzon et al., 2002), consistent with prior [11C]carfentanil studies (Ashok et al., 2019). The outcome measure was non-displaceable binding potential (BPND), which reflects the density of available MORs. We computed BPND for ROI regions including the amygdala, striatum, insula, OFC, ACC, by averaging the BPND within each ROI, with the striatum.

Statistical Analysis: Statistical analyses were performed using the scipy stats module. Group differences in demographic and clinical variables were assessed with independent-samples t tests or chi-square tests as appropriate. To test for overall group differences in task-related activation across the five preselected ROIs, we performed a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) with group (schizophrenia vs. control) as the between‐subjects factor, and the five ROI estimates as the dependent variables. Omnibus significance was assessed with Pillai’s trace (α = 0.05, two‐tailed). Post hoc exploratory analyses included independent t-tests comparing the groups for each ROI separately (α = 0.05, two‐tailed, FDR-correction performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995)).

To assess the relationship between MOR and task related activation, a Mixed Linear Effects Model was implemented separately for each task. Parameter Estimates (pleasant + unpleasant > neutral for IAPS, sad + happy > baseline for faces tasks) in the five predefined ROIs were coded as the dependent variable, PET ([11C]Carfentanil BPnd in the same ROIs as the fMRI data), group, ROI, and PET x Group interaction were included as fixed effects. Exploratory post hoc analyses included Pearson’s correlations to assess the relationship between MOR and task activation in each individual ROI, separately for each group. This was followed by a Fisher’s r-to-Z transform to compare correlation coefficients between the groups in each ROI (α = 0.05).

Results

Sample characteristics

Key demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. The patient and control groups were well-matched in age and sex distribution. Patients had a significantly higher body-mass index (BMI) than controls ( p = 0.005). As expected, patients reported significantly higher anhedonia compared to controls. In particular, patients had higher scores on the Revised Social Anhedonia Scale (p = 0.006) and Revised Physical Anhedonia Scale (p = 0.027). The consummatory pleasure subscale of the TEPS was lower in patients than controls (p = 0.034), consistent with reduced experienced pleasure, while the anticipatory pleasure subscale did not differ between groups (p = 0.28). Patients had mild depressive symptoms on the Calgary scale (mean ∼ 7.7, indicating minimal to mild depression). All patients were receiving antipsychotic medication at the time of scanning, with a mean illness duration of about 11 years.

fMRI task activation and group comparisons

Two participants were excluded from the Faces task analysis due to high motion (Faces control n = 19, schizophrenia n = 17), all participants had usable data for the IAPS task. All participants were engaged with the tasks (responded to > 95 % trials). Both tasks produced robust activation in brain regions expected for emotional processing (Fig. 1 and Fig. 2). In the IAPS task, there were no significant differences in how control and schizophrenia participants rated the images (Positive t(36) = 0.47, p = 0.640, Negative t(36) = 0.11, p = 0.909, Neutral t(36) = 0.24, p = 0.815, independent t-tests). Viewing pleasant images (versus neutral) activated occipital visual regions, ventral temporal cortex (including fusiform gyrus), and limbic regions, including the amygdala and ventromedial prefrontal cortex in both groups (supplementary figures 1). Viewing unpleasant images (versus neutral) yielded a broadly similar activation pattern, with additional engagement of the insula and ACC, consistent with processing of aversive stimuli (supplementary figure 2). These activation patterns replicate prior studies of emotional picture viewing. In the facial emotion task, viewing emotional faces (happy + sad > baseline) significantly activated the face-processing network, including the occipital face area, fusiform gyrus, and superior temporal sulcus, as well as the amygdala and medial prefrontal cortex in both groups (Fig. 2).

At the whole-brain level, there was greater activation in the control group (happy + sad > baseline, Z > 2.3, cluster p < 0.05) in the occipital cortex (supplementary figure 4). Relationship between MOR availability and fMRI activation

We tested whether regional MOR availability (PET BPND) predicted fMRI activation during the IAPS task (pleasant + unpleasant > neutral) using a linear mixed‐effects model (LMEM) which included data for all five hedonic ROIs. The PET-fMRI slope was non-significant for both the control (β = − 4.56, SE = 3.04, z = − 1.50, p = 0.133) and the schizophrenia groups (β = − 1.01, SE = 3.35, z = − 0.30, p = 0.762). The MOR-fMRI relationship did not differ between groups (β = +3.55 (SE = 2.74, z = 1.29, p = 0.196). Post hoc exploratory Pearson’s correlations confirmed there were no significant relationships in any ROI in either group (supplementary table 3). A Fisher’s r-to-Z transform showed correlation coefficients were comparable between groups for each individual ROI in the hedonic network (supplementary table 4).

For the Faces task, we used a similar LMEM including data for all five ROIs. The PET-fMRI slope was non-significant for both the control (β = 5.07, SE = 4.34, z = 1.17, p = 0.242) and the schizophrenia groups (β = 0.69, SE = 4.79, z = 0.15, p = 0.885). The MOR-fMRI relationship did not differ between groups (β = –4.38, SE = 4.06, z = –1.08, p = 0.281). Post hoc exploratory Pearson’s correlations showed there was a significant negative association between MOR density and happy + sad > baseline parameter estimates in the ACC in controls only and not the schizophrenia group (Fig. 5, Controls Pearson’s r = –0.598, p = 0.006, pFDR = 0.03, schizophrenia Pearson’s r = 0.104, p = 0.691, pFDR = 0.691). The r-coefficient was significantly different between groups in the ACC (Z = 2.17, p = 0.03) and Insula (Z = 2.13, p = 0.03). There were no other significant relationships in either group (supplementary table 5).

Discussion

Our main finding is that schizophrenia patients with moderate negative symptoms did not show significant deficits in neural activation during the processing of emotional faces or pictures, relative to healthy controls, in regions comprising the hedonic network (amygdala, striatum, ACC, orbitofrontal cortex, insula). Moreover, when jointly assessing these five ROIs, there were no associations between MOR availability and the magnitude of emotion-related brain activation in either group.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between μ-opioid receptor availability and emotion-processing brain activity in patients with schizophrenia and provides evidence that MOR are not major determinants of the emotional processing alterations associated with schizophrenia. However, we did see significant differences in emotion-related brain activity in visual processing regions in both tasks, with hypoactivation during the IAPS and hyperactivation during the faces task. This is in line with the results of previously published meta-analyses, which report both hyper- and hypoactivation in visual processing regions in people with schizophrenia compared to controls (Taylor et al., 2012). Given the low levels of MOR expression in these regions, the differences are unlikely to be driven by MOR (Ashok et al., 2019). We additionally saw hypoactivation of the left amygdala and hippocampus in the schizophrenia group in the sad faces contrast, in the whole-brain analyses. This is in line with earlier meta-analyses of emotional processing (Taylor et al., 2012), including one focusing on the processing of emotional faces in schizophrenia (Li et al., 2010). The lack of an association between MOR density and amygdala activation during the faces task, however, suggests MOR density and signalling do not directly underlie this deficit. The most recent meta-analysis of emotional processing, which included faces tasks amongst other emotion processing tasks, found activation of the hedonic network was comparable between chronic schizophrenia and controls (Pilon et al., 2025). This is largely in line with our findings across the two tasks; however, different experimental paradigms probing emotion processing may implicate different circuitry, and therefore, would benefit from separate sub-analyses. At the whole-brain level, we saw a difference in the activation of visual regions between the schizophrenia and control groups; however, as this region has low MOR density, differences in MOR are unlikely to underlie this difference in BOLD response. Both groups engaged with the tasks, responding to > 95 % trials, suggesting that attentional dysfunction is unlikely to drive this difference. Some studies suggest people with schizophrenia may have impaired visual processing of faces (Belge et al., 2017, Marosi et al., 2019). While this could underlie the differences in activation of visual regions that we see in our study, further studies would be required to test this directly.

The lack of association between MOR in the hedonic network and brain activation related to emotion processing is somewhat unexpected in light of the known role of MORs in emotion and reward processing (Berridge et al., 2009). As discussed in the introduction, MORs are abundantly expressed in key affect-regulatory brain regions (Nummenmaa & Tuominen, 2018), and their pharmacological manipulation directly impacts emotional perception. Neuroimaging studies in healthy subjects have reported that baseline MOR binding is inversely related to neural responses to emotional stimuli, suggesting that endogenous opioids can buffer or dampen emotional reactivity (Liberzon et al., 2002, Karjalainen et al., 2019). Importantly, this aligns with our finding of an inverse relationship between ACC MOR and brain activity during the processing of emotional faces in the control group. Given this background, we hypothesised that if schizophrenia patients have a deficiency in MOR signalling (as suggested by lower MOR binding; reported previously) (Ashok et al., 2019), they might show exaggerated or dysregulated neural responses to emotional stimuli. However, our findings did not support this hypothesis: we observed neither a hyperactivation nor a hypoactivation in patients’ limbic responses in the task contrasts of interest, and MOR levels did not predict brain activation across these regions. Given that we did not see the same inverse relationship between brain function and MOR density in the ACC in the schizophrenia group, and the correlation coefficients were significantly different, our results point to a possible dysregulation of MOR involvement in emotional faces processing in schizophrenia, but further studies are required to confirm this.

One important consideration is that all patients in our study were receiving antipsychotic treatment. Antipsychotic medications (particularly some second-generation antipsychotics) can affect the opioid system. For instance, chronic antipsychotic administration has been reported to upregulate MOR expression in animal models, and there is evidence that these drugs may influence endogenous opioid release. It is possible that antipsychotic treatment mitigated or obscured any underlying differences in MOR-related function between patients and controls. If antipsychotics elevate MOR availability or downstream signalling, this could normalise patients’ emotion circuit activation to a level comparable with controls. In our data, we did not find a correlation between antipsychotic dose (chlorpromazine equivalents) and MOR binding or fMRI measures. This suggests that antipsychotic treatment is not confounding the results, but the sample may not be large enough to detect subtle medication effects. Future studies in unmedicated or minimally treated patients (e.g., early in illness or in medication washout) would help address whether antipsychotic exposure confounded our results. Notwithstanding this consideration, our findings indicate that MOR is unlikely to be a major mechanism underlying emotional processing alterations that persist in patients with schizophrenia when taking antipsychotic treatment. There are some other limitations to acknowledge. Our sample size, while comparable to similar PET-fMRI studies, was modest, which limits power to detect small effect sizes or subtle correlations (see supplement). The study was powered to detect differences between groups in MOR binding (Ashok et al., 2019) and compares favourably with many PET studies; however, in the context of an fMRI study, the study is underpowered and risks a type II error as a result. However, while it is not powered to detect a result with a small effect size, the clinical significance of a small effect size is questionable. Overall, our results should be interpreted with caution until replicated in a larger cohort. We also studied a chronic patient sample; the findings may not generalise to early-stage schizophrenia or to those with more severe active symptoms. It is worth noting that emotion processing is a broad construct, and our study focused on a limited set of stimuli (happy/sad facial expressions, and pleasant/unpleasant scenes). It is possible that MOR effects might be more apparent for other emotional domains or more complex social-emotional processing. For example, MORs might play a larger role in modulating responses to pain, stress, or social attachment cues, which were not directly tested here. Our tasks did not probe emotions such as fear or anger, which might engage the opioid system differently. Follow-up studies using other emotional stimuli or tasks (e.g., social rejection paradigms, reward anticipation tasks) could provide a more comprehensive picture of MOR involvement in schizophrenia during emotional processing.

Despite these considerations, our findings have some clear implications. Firstly, the schizophrenia patients in our study did not exhibit the expected deficits in task-based emotion activation that might have been predicted from the literature. This suggests that when using simple perceptual tasks of emotion (passively viewing emotional faces or scenes), medicated patients with moderate negative symptoms can show essentially normal activation in core limbic regions like the amygdala. This aligns with those prior studies that also failed to find significant group differences (Hägele et al., 2016, Ursu et al., 2011). It may be that more demanding tasks or those requiring explicit emotional judgment reveal deficits more clearly than passive viewing tasks. Our patients’ intact activation could indicate that their emotion perception at the neural level is relatively spared, even though clinically they report high anhedonia (diminished pleasure). This raises the interesting question of whether anhedonia in schizophrenia stems more from downstream interpretation or utilisation of emotional signals rather than the initial neural response to emotional stimuli. A key consideration, however, is the inherent difficulty of assessing anhedonia under laboratory conditions. While negative affect can be reasonably induced using aversive stimuli such as unpleasant images or pain, eliciting robust and sustained positive emotional responses is considerably more challenging. This methodological limitation may constrain the sensitivity of experimental paradigms in detecting subtle impairments in positive affect processing, particularly those relevant to anticipatory or consummatory anhedonia. Future work may benefit from incorporating more ecologically valid or immersive approaches (e.g., virtual reality, social reward paradigms) to better capture real-world hedonic experience.

Secondly, while we saw an inverse relationship between MOR density and emotion-processing-related brain activation in the ACC in controls in the faces task only, the lack of association between MOR and brain activation in other regions suggests that baseline MOR availability is likely not a major determinant of the BOLD response amplitude for these kinds of stimuli in either group. One prior study in healthy people found an inverse relationship between MOR binding and cerebral blood flow responses during an emotional picture task in the temporal lobe, and no association in the amygdala (Liberzon et al., 2002). Notably, our study employed BOLD contrast and not cerebral blood flow measures; however our findings in controls during the faces task do have the same direction as the results of the previous study, where those with higher baseline MOR have lower activation when viewing emotional faces. One explanation for this is that when emotional stimuli arrive, endogenous opioids act at the receptors in the ACC to dampen neuronal firing. Finally, in line with (Liberzon et al., 2002) we found no association in the amygdala in either task in either group.

In conclusion, this multi-modal imaging study found that schizophrenia patients did not differ from controls in brain activation to emotional stimuli, and that MOR availability was not linked to the neural processing of these stimuli. These findings suggest that, at least under the conditions tested, baseline μ-opioid receptor levels do not explain the presence or absence of emotion-processing deficits in schizophrenia. Emotion-processing impairments in schizophrenia might arise from other neural or molecular mechanisms such as dopamine dysregulation, glutamate dysfunction, or network dysconnectivity rather than a static deficit in the opioid system. Our results also hint that antipsychotic-treated patients can show relatively normal activation to simple emotional cues, raising questions about how this neural reactivity translates into the subjective experience of pleasure or affect (which patients still report as blunted). Ongoing and future investigations should explore the dynamic interactions between neurotransmitter systems – including the endogenous opioid, dopamine, and cannabinoid systems – and how they collectively influence emotional experience in schizophrenia. A better understanding of these mechanisms could open the door to novel treatments targeting negative symptoms and anhedonia, such as medications modulating the opioid system.
