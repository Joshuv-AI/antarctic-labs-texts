Autosomal dominant focal segmental glomerulosclerosis by INF2 p.Arg218Trp and p.Ser186Pro mutations: three case reports and literature review
Authors: Shunian Guo, Minghua Shang, Chao Su, Yifeng Zhuge, Wenqian Wei, Shu Rong, Lijie Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFocal segmental glomerulosclerosis(FSGS) is a histologic lesion of podocyte injury with diverse etiologies including mutations in the inverted formin-2 gene(INF2), which belongs to the formin family and regulates actin cytoskeleton-dependent cellular processes.Case presentationWe report two cases of INF2 p.Arg218Trp and one case of p.Ser186Pro, all exhibiting an autosomal dominant(AD) inheritance pattern. The clinical presentations of the two patients with INF2 p.Arg218Trp varied widely. One patient, diagnosed with proteinuria in adolescence, maintained normal renal function, while the other experienced a youthful onset followed by rapid progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and required hemodialysis treatment. The patient with p.Ser186Pro also had an early onset, with renal biopsy revealing progression from minimal change disease (MCD) to FSGS, leading to ESRD and necessitating hemodialysis treatment.ConclusionsThese cases add to the clinical spectrum associated with INF2-related FSGS and underscore the importance of genetic evaluation in patients with early-onset or familial kidney disease. They show considerable intrafamilial and interfamilial variability in penetrance and expressivity, highlighting the complexity of genotype-phenotype correlations. Genetic screening and family counseling remain essential in suspected hereditary FSGS. Further studies are warranted to clarify the molecular mechanisms and guide management strategies.Keywords: Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, INF2 mutation, End-stage renal disease, Case report

Background

Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) is one of the leading glomerular causes of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) worldwide, and its prevalence appears to be increasing globally [1, 2]. Contemporary understanding of proteinuria and nephrotic syndrome pathogenesis has redefined FSGS as a histopathological pattern rather than a distinct disease entity. This podocytopathy manifests as segmental sclerosis in scattered glomeruli, progressing through three pathogenic phases: initial podocyte injury, subsequent podocyte detachment and depletion, and ultimately irreversible glomerular scarring. The clinical spectrum ranges from isolated proteinuria to nephrotic syndrome, with disease progression frequently culminating in ESRD at advanced stages [3].

The etiologies of secondary FSGS are varied and include viral infections, metabolic disorders, pharmacologic exposures, genetic factors, and maladaptive forms of FSGS. Among these, genetic factors are particularly noteworthy, with pathogenic variants inducing both renal and extra-renal phenotypic manifestations [3, 4]. Idiopathic forms of FSGS, characterized by unclear etiology and resistance to immunosuppressive therapies, remain a clinical and therapeutic challenge [5]. Historical and current evidence suggests that FSGS may be triggered by circulating permeability factors (such as cardiotrophin-like cytokine factor 1 or anti-CD40 antibodies) that cause podocyte injury. In 1974, Shalhoub hypothesized that a T-cell-derived “basement membrane toxin” could increase glomerular permeability [6]. Later, in 1996, Savin et al. showed that serum from patients with recurrent FSGS increased glomerular albumin permeability in vitro, and this effect could be reversed by plasmapheresis, supporting the role of a pathogenic circulating factor. However, the specific causative molecule remains unknown [3, 7]. Notably, Hengel et al. identified circulating autoantibodies against nephrin—a key slit diaphragm protein—in approximately 9% of adult patients with primary FSGS and up to 90% of children with idiopathic nephrotic syndrome prior to immunosuppressive treatment, indicating a potential antibody-dependent mechanism in selected patients [8].

Recent large-scale exome sequencing analyses have demonstrated that rare pathogenic variants in known kidney disease genes account for approximately 33.6% of individuals with a histological diagnosis of FSGS, after stringent filtering to exclude genes with comparable variant burdens in control populations. Notably, the frequency was higher in familial cases (37.2%) than in sporadic ones (29.7%), underscoring the heterogeneous and partially heritable nature of FSGS. In addition, two high-risk coding variants (G1 and G2) in the APOL1 gene have been strongly associated with increased susceptibility to FSGS among individuals of African ancestry, explaining a substantial proportion of cases in this population and contributing to more rapid disease progression. However, a non-negligible background of similar variants (8.3%) was also detected in controls, highlighting the interpretive limitations of current genetic testing approaches in clinical practice [9]. Moreover, the interpretation of rare variants remains challenging due to the limited representation of non-European ancestries in public reference databases, underscoring the need for ancestry-informed variant classification in FSGS research and diagnostics. Mutations in the INF2 gene were first identified as monogenic drivers of FSGS 14 years ago. This gene encodes a formin-family actin regulatory protein, with disease-associated mutations inducing podocyte-specific phenotypic changes that disrupt actin cytoskeletal organization in podocytes [10]. Here, we describe the clinical information and molecular genetic testing results of three patients with INF2 gene mutations in exon 4 and their families in detail.

Case presentation

Patient and clinical information

Patient 1, a 22-year-old Chinese female, was diagnosed with FSGS. At age 11, the patient had a 24-hour urine protein level of 357 mg, and since then, both urine protein and kidney function remained stable. By age 21, however, the urine protein level reached 1515 mg/24 h. Due to hypotension, RAS inhibitors were not prescribed, and the patient was treated with dapagliflozin(10 mg/d). Following this treatment, there was no further deterioration in kidney function or increase in urine protein. At age 21 years and 3 months, her urine protein had decreased to 868 mg/24 h, and by age 21 years and 5 months, it had further declined to 780 mg/24 h, with 2+ proteinuria on urinalysis and no hematuria. She was hospitalized for a kidney biopsy, which showed that 2 out of 14 glomeruli exhibited segmental capillary tuft sclerosis with adhesion to the Bowman capsule. Segmental mesangial matrix expansion was observed without evidence of glomerular hypercellularity. Tubulointerstitial changes included focal interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy affecting approximately 10% of the cortical area, along with infiltration of mononuclear inflammatory cells in the interstitium. No significant vascular abnormalities were observed. Immunofluorescence microscopy was performed on four glomeruli and demonstrated negative staining for IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q, C4d, κ, λ, and fibrinogen, indicating absence of immune complex deposition. Additionally, electron microscopy showed segmental thickening of the glomerular basement membrane (260–620 nm) and 40% segmental foot process effacement, with no electron-dense deposits observed in subepithelial, subendothelial, or mesangial areas (Fig. 1). These findings are consistent with a non–immune complex–mediated form as usual-type FSGS. Dapagliflozin treatment continued, and by age 22, the urine protein level had reduced to 571 mg/24 h, with a serum creatinine of 49 μmol/L and the urine routine test showed negative for urine protein. The tests were negative for common viruses, and the patient had a BMI of 20.96. There is no history of antiviral medications, mTOR inhibitors, or CNI usage. His father was diagnosed with ESRD of unknown cause at age 26, with a serum creatinine of 500 μmol/L and severe atrophy of one kidney. He has been on hemodialysis without a kidney transplant.

Patient 2, a 26-year-old Chinese male, was clinically diagnosed with suspected FSGS. At age 23, he was admitted with a 10-day history of headache. He was found to have elevated blood pressure (210/110 mmHg), serum creatinine > 1000 μmol/L, hemoglobin of 82 g/L, urine protein 3+, and urine RBC 1 + . He started maintenance hemodialysis and has remained on it since. No prior kidney function or urine routine tests were conducted. Patient 2 has a twin brother, who was also evaluated at age 23. His serum creatinine was 193 μmol/L, with a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) of 2830 mg/g, no microscopic hematuria, and serum albumin of 45.4 g/L. He had hypertension, and ultrasound revealed increased cortical echogenicity in both kidneys. After excluding secondary causes with the family, genetic testing was performed. He was prescribed sacubitril-valsartan 200 mg qd, amlodipine, and a low-salt, high-quality protein diet. At age 24, follow-up of the twin brother showed a serum creatinine of 220 μmol/L, serum albumin 46 g/L, and UACR of 2131 mg/g. A kidney biopsy was not performed in him or his twin brother, and the diagnosis was made based on clinical findings. Neither of the patient’s parents has hypertension, and both have normal kidney function and urine tests.

Patient 3, a 43-year-old Chinese male, was diagnosed with proteinuria (+) and hypertension at the age of 27 during a routine check-up, and started treatment with losartan. At age 29, his 24-hour urine protein was 1970 mg and kidney biopsy performed at that time revealed 17 glomeruli with preserved architecture, no mesangial or endocapillary hypercellularity, and no thickening of the glomerular basement membrane. Mild vacuolar degeneration of tubular epithelial cells and focal interstitial fibrosis were noted, without vascular abnormalities. Immunofluorescence microscopy showed weakly positive staining (±) for IgG and IgA in the mesangial area, and trace lambda light chain (±), while IgM, C3, C1q, κ were all negative. Electron microscopy showed that the overall morphology of the glomerulus was generally normal, with no electron-dense deposits observed. A small amount of foot process effacement was noted, accompanied by a small amount of microvillous formation. These findings supported a diagnosis of minimal change disease (MCD) (Fig. 2). He continued treatment with losartan. At age 32, due to persistent proteinuria and an increase in serum creatinine to 100 μmol/L, a second kidney biopsy was performed (Fig. 3). Of the 25 glomeruli sampled, 2 were globally hyaline sclerotic and approximately 4 exhibited clear segmental sclerosis，with some showing features of collapse. Some mesangial matrix expansion and hypercellularity were observed. Tubulointerstitial findings included diffuse tubular epithelial injury, focal atrophy, mononuclear inflammatory infiltration, and interstitial fibrosis. Tubular epithelial cell degeneration or necrosis was not prominent. A few tubules contained proteinaceous casts, and occasional tubular dilatation was noted. Scattered inflammatory cell infiltration was observed in the interstitium, along with mild interstitial fibrosis. Immunofluorescence microscopy demonstrated negative staining for IgA, IgG, IgM, C3, C1q, κ, and λ, indicating no evidence of immune complex deposition. The findings support a non–immune complex–mediated pathogenesis of collapsing type of FSGS in this patient (Fig. 3). Electron microscopy revealed a globally hyaline sclerotic glomeruli without electron-dense deposits or tubular basement membrane changes. At age 39, his serum creatinine was 146 μmol/L, eGFR was 48 mL/min/1.73 m2, and serum albumin was 37.4 g/L. At age 41, his 24-hour urine protein was 9303 mg, serum creatinine was 285 μmol/L, and losartan was discontinued. He was switched to sacubitril-valsartan 200 mg qd. At age 41 years and 5 months, his serum creatinine had increased to 511 μmol/L, with an albumin level of 26.5 g/L and a UACR of 740.29 mg/g, and he was diagnosed with CKD stage 5. At age 42, the patient developed an upper respiratory tract infection, which led to an acute increase in serum creatinine to 1256 μmol/L, with fluctuating hemoglobin and uric acid levels. His eGFR declined to 3.76 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Table 1). Since then, he has been undergoing maintenance hemodialysis three times per week. Notably, his mother underwent kidney transplantation at the age of 46 due to ESRD of unknown etiology. At her last evaluation at age 67, her renal function was stable, with a serum creatinine of 98.2 μmol/L, eGFR of 52 mL/min/1.73 m2, and no proteinuria.

Genetic analysis

Genetic analysis was performed as part of the etiological investigation in all three patients, given the early-onset proteinuria in Patients 1 and 2, the family history of end-stage renal disease in Patient 1, and the long-standing proteinuria and biopsy-proven FSGS progression in Patient 3. No identifiable secondary causes such as infections, toxins, or systemic diseases were found in any of the cases. Therefore, whole-exome sequencing(WES) was conducted to explore potential hereditary factors underlying the podocytopathy. WES was performed for all three probands using the Illumina next-generation sequencing platform. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood, and targeted enrichment was applied to capture genes associated with FSGS and hereditary nephropathies. Sequencing reads were aligned to the UCSC hg19 human reference genome using BWA, and variant calling was conducted with the GATK toolkit. Variants were annotated using the Variant Effect Predictor (VEP), referencing public databases including ClinVar, OMIM, HGMD, and gnomAD. Pathogenic missense variants in the INF2 gene were identified in all three patients, suggesting a shared genetic etiology.

In Patient 1, the c.652C > T (p.Arg218Trp) variant was detected as a heterozygous mutation (Fig. 4). Segregation analysis in affected family members, such as her father who had a history of kidney disease, has not been performed due to the unavailability of DNA samples. For Patient 2, parental analysis confirmed a de novo origin of the same c.652C > T variant, as both parents were wild-type, while the proband and his brother shared the heterozygous mutation (Fig. 5). This indicates a de novo mutation, likely arising very early in embryonic development, possibly in the zygote or during the first few cell divisions. The presence of the mutation in both twins strongly suggests monozygosity, although this was not genetically confirmed. A less likely possibility is germline mosaicism in one parent, but this is unsupported by current data. For Patient 3, segregation analysis was performed via Sanger sequencing in the proband, his mother, and two children, all of whom were found to be heterozygous carriers of the INF2 c.556T > C (p.Ser186Pro) variant (Fig. 6). Variant classification was performed according to the 2015 ACMG/AMP guidelines, incorporating multiple lines of evidence. Both INF2 variants were located within the Diaphanous Inhibitory Domain (DID), a functionally important region (PM1), and were absent from large population databases such as gnomAD (PM2). Deleterious effects were predicted by multiple in silico tools (PP3), and both variants have been previously reported in association with FSGS (PP5). Additionally, familial segregation data were available for Patient 3, supporting co-segregation with disease (PP1). Based on these criteria, both variants were classified as likely pathogenic.

For Patient 2, parental analysis confirmed a de novo origin of the same c.652C > T variant, as both parents were wild-type, while the proband and his brother shared the heterozygous mutation (Fig. 4). This variant was absent in the gnomAD database and was classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic based on current evidence. Neither variant has been previously reported in population databases, supporting their potential disease-associated roles.

Discussion

Genetic factors have played an important role in the occurrence and development of FSGS over the past two decades, and more than 50 single gene forms have been found to be associated with FSGS [9]. INF2 locates on chromosome 14 (14q32.33), containing 23 exons, which is one of the 15 members of mammalian formin family of proteins. These members not only share a Formin Homology Domain(FH2) which controls actin polymerization, but most of them, including INF2, also contain other special regions of homology outside of FH2 domain that are important for actin assembly and for regulating the interactions of binding partners. These regions include the diaphanous autoregulatory domain (DAD) near the INF2 C terminus and the diaphanous inhibitory domain(DID) close to the N terminus [11]. The interaction between DID and DAD may contribute to formin autoinhibition, and the mutation results in the disruption of this state, strongly explaining the outgrowth of the INF2 dominant trait. All identified pathogenic FSGS mutations in INF2 so far are localized to the DID. In addition, the C-terminus of INF2 can accelerate depolymerization by sequestering actin monomers and rapid filament serving, whereas DID binding to the DAD compete for actin monomers to inhibit depolymerization. INF2 mutations can lead to abnormal actin in podocytes, but it is not clear how actin influences podocyte function [10, 12]. Although much of the research on INF2 has focused on its actin-regulating function, recent studies have highlighted an additional role for INF2 in dynein-mediated trafficking in podocytes. Mutations such as R218Q disrupt INF2’s interaction with dynein light chain 1, thereby promoting dynein-dependent trafficking of nephrin toward proteolytic degradation pathways and reducing its recycling essential for maintaining slit diaphragm integrity. This mistrafficking contributes to podocytopathy, which underlies the proteinuric phenotype seen in INF2-related FSGS. The recognition that mutations in INF2 can disrupt both actin dynamics and vesicular trafficking pathways has broadened our understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving the disease [13, 14]. Recent evidence suggests that mutations in INF2 may not only impair podocyte cytoskeletal dynamics but could also intersect with pathways regulating endothelial integrity and complement activation. In particular, some individuals with INF2 mutations have been reported to carry aHUS risk haplotypes and to develop thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), including de novo TMA following kidney transplantation. This raises the possibility that INF2 dysfunction may predispose to endothelial injury or dysregulated complement activity, thereby linking podocytopathy with microvascular damage. Such interactions could contribute to the phenotypic heterogeneity observed in INF2-related renal disease and may have implications for transplant outcomes and therapeutic strategies [15]. As such, further research into the genetic and molecular factors influencing this variability is crucial for developing targeted treatments, particularly those that can modulate the actin dynamics and dynein-mediated trafficking pathways disrupted by INF2 mutations.

The mutation c.652C > T (p.Arg218Trp) in patients 1 and 2, located in exon 4, affects glomerular function by interfering with the polymerization and depolymerization of encoding actin filaments, thereby triggering AD FSGS [16]. In a 2021 study by Schrezenmeier et al., a 42-year-old male with no reported family history was identified to carry the heterozygous c.652C > T (p.Arg218Trp) INF2 variant during a next-generation sequencing screen of 635 patients with undetermined CKD awaiting kidney transplantation. Although detailed clinical information was not provided, the case supports the pathogenicity of this variant in apparently sporadic FSGS cases [17]. Brown et al. first identified p.Arg218Trp mutation in the FGEA and FGME family. The prediction of both p.Arg218Trp mutations were deleterious [18, 19]. Caridi et al. demonstrated a high prevalence of INF2 gene mutations in 28 families, with the p.Arg218Trp mutation identified in the GE07 family. In that research, mild proteinuria (range 1–2.5 g/L) was the prevalent clinical phenotype in most patients, but evolution to ESRD was frequent and usually begins during the third decade [16]. Hiroko Ueda et al. found the p.Arg218Trp mutation in 5 TO families in a cohort. The index patient was a male who developed proteinuria at age 18 with a UACR > 10. He developed ESRD at age 23 and underwent kidney transplantation, which has not recurred. His mother and brother developed proteinuria at age 29 & 11 and ESRD at age 32 & 17, respectively, confirming Caridi’s view that FSGS due to INF2 progresses rapidly and frequently to ESRD [20]. The above families are presented in Table 3. The clinical presentation of these patients, as well as that of patients 1 and 2 in our report, suggests that INF2 p.Arg218Trp have a relatively young onset and are heterogeneous within and between families, with most progressing rapidly to ESRD. Notably, it is hypothesized that the variant in patient 2 is most likely de novo, while the possibility of his parents being germ cell chimeric carriers cannot be completely ruled out.

The sequence change c.556T > C(p.Ser186Pro) of patient 3 has been proved to likely weaken the autoinhibitory DID-DAD interaction and increase INF2 interaction with profilin 2 and the F-actin capping protein, giving rise to aberrant regulation of actin dynamics [21]. In 2010, Brown et al. found the variant for the first time in both the FGER and FGBR families, showing that it was co-segregating with FSGS (Table 3). It was shown that p.Ser186 is located on the opposite side of the DAD-binding region on the DID and is prominently exposed on the surface of the DID, allowing it to increase its interaction with other molecules. In contrast, p.Arg218 is located near the DAD-binding region, buried beneath the surface of the DID, with a small area of exposure, and may be important in maintaining the overall folding of the DID. Although individual case reports of the p.S186P mutation remain scarce in the literature, its pathogenicity has been previously supported by familial segregation studies, as shown in the FGER and FGBR families [18].

Drug-based therapies such as cyclosporine A or steroids are not effective in hereditary FSGS caused by INF2 mutations. In this report, Patient 1, who did not have diabetes mellitus, was treated with dapagliflozin to control proteinuria, while Patients 2 and 3 were receiving maintenance hemodialysis as renal replacement therapy. In this report, Patient 1 (without diabetes) did not receive RAS blockers due to hypotension and was treated with Dapagliflozin to control proteinuria. Patient 2 presented with renal insufficiency and started maintenance hemodialysis treatment. Patient 3 had been on losartan for symptomatic treatment and, at the age of 41, switched to sacubitril-valsartan due to disease progression. After 5 months, RAS blockers were discontinued due to further deterioration of renal function, and maintenance hemodialysis was initiated as renal replacement therapy. However, it is important to note that while laboratory parameters may remain stable in patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis, this does not imply stabilization of the underlying disease process. However, kidney transplantation is the only option that can completely correct the renal defects caused by INF2 [4]. In patients with primary or immune-related FSGS, the risk of recurrence posttransplant is high. In contrast, the recurrence rate in patients with genetic FSGS is generally low [21]. Genetic molecular diagnosis can provide a favorable prognosis in terms of kidney transplant recurrence.

Notably, most previously reported cases of INF2-related FSGS have been identified in individuals of European ancestry, with a few cases also reported among Asian and African American populations, as summarized in Table 3. Our current cases involve Chinese individuals from unrelated families, thereby expanding the ethnic spectrum of INF2 mutations and supporting the notion that these pathogenic variants are not confined to a specific ancestry. This highlights the importance of considering genetic screening in diverse populations, particularly when familial patterns of proteinuria or early-onset FSGS are observed.

In conclusion, we report three patients with FSGS due to missense variant in INF2 and show their mutation loci. Mutations in INF2 are one of the leading causes of AD FSGS, with considerable intra- and/or inter-familial variability, and genetic screening as well as familial genetic counseling are recommended for suspected patients with a family history of FSGS. Further studies of genotype-phenotype correlation are necessary for FSGS triggered by more mutant loci in INF2, and the mechanisms and therapeutic approaches still have much space for exploration.
