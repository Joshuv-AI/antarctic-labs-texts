Horizontal partial laryngectomy for laryngeal neuroendocrine tumor: A case report

Introduction

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs), also referred to as carcinoids, are rare tumors that most commonly occur in the gastrointestinal tract and lung [1]. The histological grading of the severity of neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) is crucial in determining the course of treatment. Moreover, laryngeal NETs are the most common NENs in the head and neck region, accounting for less than 1 % of systemic NENs; however, they are the most common non-squamous cell carcinomas of the larynx [2,3]. Moderately differentiated NETs are the most common type of laryngeal NEN, commonly arising in supraglottic lesions. Furthermore, they have a high metastatic rate and limited disease-specific survival [4]. We report a case of partial laryngectomy for a NET of the larynx in which the patient achieved long-term palliation but had an unfavorable outcome of death owing to metastasis to the gastrointestinal tract.

Presentation of case

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE Criteria [5]. A laryngeal tumor was detected in a 74-year-old man with 48 years of smoking during an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy as part of a health checkup at his workplace. The patient was referred to our department in May 2009. Laryngeal endoscopic findings showed a prominent lesion slightly to the right of the laryngeal surface of the epiglottis when observed under white light (Fig. 1A). No abnormal findings were observed in the mucosa of the tumor on narrow-band imaging (Fig. 1B). Vocal cord movement was not impaired, and the cervical lymph nodes were not palpable. During this period, the patient experienced diarrhea (three times a week) and lost a few kilograms of weight.

Endoscopic views of the larynx.

A: White light is used to view the larynx. B: Narrow-band imaging of the larynx. The location of the lesion is indicated with circles.

Axial non-contrast computed tomography (CT) of the neck showed a neoplastic lesion on the epiglottis, and axial contrast-enhanced CT (Fig. 2A) showed a uniformly contrasted mass lesion. No invasive changes in the perineural neck were observed on the coronal contrast-enhanced CT (Fig. 2B). There was no significant enlargement of the cervical lymph nodes. Magnetic resonance imaging shows a T2-weighted section (Fig. 2C) and a Gd contrast-enhanced axial section (Fig. 2D). A tumor lesion with an intermediate signal on T1-weighted images and a heterogeneous signal on T2-weighted images was found on the laryngeal side of the epiglottis, indicating an early contrast effect. There was no invasion of the surrounding area, consistent with the CT findings. A biopsy under local anesthesia revealed a moderately differentiated NET (T1N0M0), and the patient was admitted to the hospital for surgery.

CT, computed tomography; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

During surgery, a tracheostomy was performed first. A horizontal partial laryngectomy was performed at the level of the hyoid bone through a neck skin incision (Fig. 3A). The suprahyoid muscles were dissected from the hyoid bone. The hyoid bone was dissected medially from the epiglottic vallecula to open the pharyngeal cavity. The epiglottis was removed and placed in a clear visual field (Fig. 3B), and the tumor was resected with a safety margin above 5 mm. Finally, the right epiglottis was resected, except at the tip (Fig. 3C). The excised specimens are shown in Fig. 3D. The remaining epiglottis and pharyngeal mucosa were sutured, the pharyngeal cavity closed, and the hyoid bone and suprahyoid muscles were sutured.

Intraoperative findings.

A: Photograph showing the skin incision design. B: The epiglottis was imaged before tumor resection. C: Epiglottis after tumor resection. D: Excised specimen is shown.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed subepithelial cells with irregularly distributed round nuclei and vitreous-like organelles under the mucosal epithelium, covered with multilinear columnar epithelium and mild nuclear atypia (Fig. 4A). Immunostaining was positive for chromogranin A (Fig. 4B), synaptophysin (Fig. 4C), CD56, AE1/3, and KL1 and negative for SMA and p63. The positive rate of Ki-67 was 9.5 % (Fig. 4D). Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with a moderately differentiated NET. Resection margins were negative. Oral intake was started on the 12th postoperative day. The patient initially experienced aspiration without pneumonia, and the intake gradually increased. No regional or cervical lymph node recurrence was observed during the follow-up. In October 2012, diarrhea and weight loss were observed, and an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed a mass lesion in the stomach. A biopsy of the mass revealed a pathological diagnosis of NET in the stomach. After hospitalization, the patient had watery stools approximately ten times a day, as well as carcinoid syndrome, and was treated with octreotide (sandostatin 150 mg/day), but unfortunately died.

Discussion

Neuroendocrine neoplasms are tumors derived from neuroendocrine cells that secrete hormones and peptides [6]. Because these cells are distributed throughout the body, the tumors can also occur in any organs throughout the body but are most commonly observed in the digestive organs, lungs, and gastrointestinal tract [1]. Diagnosis of NENs is typically made based on the presence of neuroendocrine morphology on conventional light microscopy, including organoid nesting, peripheral palisading of tumor nests, trabeculae, and demonstration of neuroendocrine differentiation by immunohistochemistry [7]. The potential of NENs for metastasis or local invasion depends on location and subtype [8]; therefore, they demonstrate different natural histories and outcomes [9]. The treatment options and prognoses of different pathological types of laryngeal neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) also vary; thus, a variety of names have been used to refer to NENs, resulting in confusing and controversial terminology in need of standardizing [7].

Laryngeal NETs are the most common NETs in the head and neck region but account for less than 1 % of all systemic NETs. However, they are the most common non-squamous cell carcinomas of the larynx [2,3,10,11]. They mainly occur in middle-aged and older men, especially those with a history of smoking, and the most frequent site of origin is the supraglottic larynx, including the epiglottis and aryepiglottic folds [7,9]. There is a male preponderance of 3:1 for all subtypes, except for typical carcinoid tumors [12].

As there are no unified treatment guidelines [13], surgical resections are preferred. The treatment options and prognoses of various pathological types of laryngeal NEC are different [13]. In this case, horizontal partial laryngeal surgery was performed through an external cervical incision, as in previous reports [1,11,13]. Recently, transoral techniques such as transoral robotic surgery and transoral video laryngoscopic surgery have increased. For patients with limited early lesions and good exposure, it is appropriate to remove the tumor using transoral CO2 LASER or low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation, which can achieve the same therapeutic effect as open surgery to remove the mass altogether and improve the patient's quality of life [13]. Zhang et al. analyzed the treatment of 25 patients with laryngeal NECs with no significant differences in the 5-year survival rate between those with early-stage (T1, T2) laryngeal NECs who received CO2 laser therapy under a supportive laryngoscope and those who received open surgery. This case may have been a suitable indication for such a technique [14].

Few reports prove the usefulness of radiotherapy for NEC. Van der Laan demonstrated that patients with NET G2 treated with surgery had better disease-specific survival than those treated with radiotherapy [12]. Although postoperative irradiation can be expected to have some effects in preventing local recurrence [4,5,11,12], in this case, local control was possible without irradiation because the resection margins were negative on pathological examination.

As mentioned above, the prognosis of laryngeal NET remains poor. A meta-analysis comparing 11,842 cases of neuroendocrine tumors using the Niigata Registry identified the pancreas (43.2 %), esophagus (43.5 %), and larynx (47.6 %) as having the worst 5-year survival rates [15]. As in this case, the cause of death is usually metastasis rather than local recurrence. As mentioned earlier, the proportion of Ki-67-positive cells in this case was approximately 9.5 %. Their mitotic potential is not higher than that of squamous cell carcinoma of the larynx. However, the patient in this case died of distant metastasis despite local surgical control. Although chemotherapy for laryngeal NET has not yet been established, practice guidelines recommend everolimus or sunitinib for pancreatic NET (G1/G2) and octreotide for gastrointestinal NET (G1/G2) to control carcinoid syndrome [4]. Chemotherapy with streptozocin is also an option for gastrointestinal and pancreatic NET (G3). For gastrointestinal and pancreatic NET (G3), platinum-based combination therapy is recommended, similar to the treatment of small cell carcinoma [4]. In this case, after metastasis to the stomach, the patient presented with carcinoid syndrome, and somatostatin analog therapy (octreotide) was administered before the patient's death. The outcome of laryngeal NEC is unsatisfactory, and new therapeutic strategies, such as the rapidly developing area of molecular targeted therapy, are needed to improve survival outcomes.

We previously investigated the expression of receptor-type tyrosine kinases in laryngeal NEC [10]. In the future, we aim to gather more cases to clarify the clinical course of laryngeal NEC and to advance our understanding of molecular targeted therapy.

Conclusion

Here, we report a case of moderately differentiated NEC arising from the epiglottis. This type of tumor is a rare malignancy that forms in the submucosa of the larynx, and surgery is the first-line treatment. The patient underwent horizontal partial laryngectomy, which resulted in local control, but distant metastasis to the stomach occurred, leading to death. Long-term follow-up is necessary because distant metastasis is likely to affect the prognosis of this disease, and effective adjuvant therapies, including molecular targeted therapies, must be established in addition to surgery.
