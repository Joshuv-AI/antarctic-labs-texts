Longitudinal Changes in Frailty and Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events in Patients With Coronary Heart Disease
Authors: Yihong Ding, Di He, Jiabei He, Jiaying Wu, Zhida Shen, Songzan Chen, Dan Zhou, Changzheng Yuan, Yimin Zhu, He Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is common among patients with coronary heart disease (CHD). This study aimed to examine both baseline frailty and its longitudinal changes in relation to the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in patients with CHD.MethodsData were sourced from the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) study. Frailty status was assessed using a modified Fried frailty phenotype at 2 consecutive surveys. The outcome was MACEs, defined as a composite of recurrent CHD, incident heart failure, stroke, or cardiovascular death. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs.ResultsIn baseline frailty analyses, 1793 patients with CHD were included. Compared with robust patients, prefrail and frail patients had a higher risk of MACEs (HR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.08–1.42] for prefrail; and HR, 1.75 [95% CI, 1.43–2.15] for frail). In frailty transition analyses, 862 patients with CHD were included. Patients who progressed from robust to prefrail/frail status had a higher MACE risk (HR, 1.43 [95% CI, 1.07–1.91]) compared with those who remained robust. Similarly, progression from prefrail to frail status was associated with an increased risk of MACEs (HR, 2.30 [95% CI, 1.62–3.27]) compared with stable prefrailty. Conversely, recovery from prefrail to robust status was related to a decreased MACE risk (HR, 0.62 [95% CI, 0.40–0.96]).ConclusionsBoth baseline frailty and its temporal changes were associated with the risk of MACEs among patients with CHD, highlighting the clinical value of monitoring and intervening for frailty in CHD management.

Coronary heart disease (CHD) stands as a leading cause of premature death, placing a substantial burden on individuals and health care systems worldwide. 1 , 2 , 3 With advances in the treatment and management of CHD, most patients now experience improved prognoses and survive longer after their initial CHD episodes. 3 However, despite improved survival, patients with CHD remain at high risk for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), 4 , 5 underscoring the need to identify prognostic factors and refine risk stratification to guide personalized secondary prevention.

As global populations age, frailty, a common geriatric syndrome, has emerged as a critical public health challenge. 6 , 7 , 8 Characterized by reduced physiological reserves across multiple organ systems, 7 , 8 frailty increases vulnerability to stressors and is associated with higher risks of disability, diminished quality of life, cognitive decline, and premature death. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Although the prevalence of frailty varies among community‐dwelling older adults, the highest rates are observed in patients with CHD, with pooled estimates reaching up to 36%. 14 , 15 , 16

Previous studies have examined the associations between frailty and several adverse health outcomes in patients with CHD, 17 , 18 , 19 but the specific relationship between frailty and the risk of MACEs remains uncertain. Moreover, most of these studies assessed frailty at a single time point, without considering longitudinal changes in frailty during follow‐up. Compared with static assessments, evaluating temporal changes in frailty over time may provide a more comprehensive understanding of how frailty trajectories influence disease progression and health outcomes. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that frailty is not necessarily irreversible, and targeted interventions may help reverse its progression and improve clinical outcomes. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 However, the impact of these changes on CHD prognosis remains unclear. Importantly, ascertaining whether interventions aimed at reversing frailty can mitigate the risk of MACEs has substantial implications for the management of patients with CHD.

To address these knowledge gaps, we leveraged data from the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) study, a large‐scale cohort study in the United States, to examine the associations of baseline frailty and its longitudinal changes with the risk of MACEs in patients with CHD. We hypothesized that frailty progression would be associated with a higher MACE risk, whereas frailty improvement would be associated with a lower MACE risk.

Methods

Data Availability

Data supporting the findings of this study are available from a third party and are not publicly available. ARIC data are available from the Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center (https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/home/) upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Patients

Detailed study design and procedures of ARIC have been described previously. 24 , 25 In brief, ARIC originally recruited 15 792 participants aged 45 to 64 years from 4 communities (Forsyth County, NC; Jackson, MS; Minneapolis, MN; and Washington County, MD) between 1987 and 1989 (visit 1). In this study, visit 5 (2011–2013) was designated as the baseline because frailty was first assessed at this visit. Frailty assessment was repeated at visit 6 (2016–2017), which was regarded as the second survey. The ARIC study received ethical approval from the institutional review boards at each participating center (Wake Forest University, University of Mississippi Medical Center, University of Minnesota, and Johns Hopkins University), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Figure 1 illustrates the selection process of the study patients. First, 2100 patients with CHD at baseline were included. Prevalent CHD was defined on the basis of self‐reported history of CHD at visit 5 or hospitalization discharge codes for CHD before visit 5. 26 We then excluded 307 patients with missing data on frailty at baseline. The remaining 1793 patients with CHD were included for baseline frailty analyses. For transition analyses, 832 patients did not attend the second survey, and 99 patients with missing data on frailty at the second survey were further excluded, leaving 862 patients with CHD for inclusion in the final analyses.

Assessment of Frailty

Frailty was assessed using the Fried frailty phenotype, 27 which comprises 5 criteria: unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, low physical activity, slowness, and weakness. 28 , 29 , 30 Detailed definitions of the 5 frailty phenotype criteria are provided in the Supplemental Methods and Table S1. Frailty severity was quantified by summing the number of criteria met, resulting in a score ranging from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater frailty. Frailty status was classified into 3 categories: robust (score 0), prefrail (score 1–2), and frail (score ≥3). 27 , 28 , 29 , 30

Changes in frailty status were assessed on the basis of frailty status at baseline (visit 5, 2011–2013) and the second survey (visit 6, 2016–2017). Participants initially classified as robust at baseline were categorized into 2 groups on the basis of their frailty status at the second survey: stable robust (reference) and robust to prefrail/frail. For participants classified as prefrail at baseline, 3 patterns were identified: stable prefrail (reference), prefrail to robust, and prefrail to frail. Among participants classified as frail at baseline, 2 patterns were identified: stable frail (reference) and frail to robust/prefrail (Figure S1).

We used the cumulative frailty score and changes in frailty scores to further assess temporal changes in frailty. Specifically, the cumulative frailty score was calculated as the sum of the frailty scores from 2 surveys, while changes in frailty scores were calculated by subtracting the first survey score from the second survey score. To ensure statistical power, we categorized the cumulative frailty score into 0 (reference), 1, 2, 3, and >3 scores, and changes in frailty scores into stable (reference), an increase of 1 score, an increase of >1 score, a decrease of 1 score, and a decrease of >1 score on the basis of the population distribution (Figure S2).

Outcome Ascertainment

The outcome of interest was the occurrence of MACEs, defined as a composite of recurrent CHD, incident heart failure (HF) and stroke, or cardiovascular death. 31 , 32 , 33 These conditions were ascertained by expert panels through annual telephone follow‐up, informant interviews, medical records, and death certificate data. 26 , 34 , 35 Specifically, recurrent CHD was defined as the first fatal CHD, definite or probable nonfatal myocardial infarction, silent myocardial infarction, or coronary revascularization during follow‐up. 26 Incident HF was defined as the first hospitalization or death related to HF during follow‐up. 34 Incident stroke was defined as the first definite or probable ischemic stroke or hemorrhagic stroke during follow‐up. 35 All deaths were ascertained from death certificates, with causes of death coded according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD). Cardiovascular deaths were defined as those with ICD, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) codes 390 to 459 or ICD, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes I00 to I99. 26

Covariates

The covariates included sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, and clinical risk factors, selected on the basis of previous literature. 36 , 37 , 38 We obtained covariate information from questionnaires, anthropometric measurements, and laboratory examinations, including age, sex (male or female), race (White or Black), education level (below college or college and above), household income (<$25 000, $25 000–$49 999, or ≥$50 000), smoking status (never, former, or current smokers), drinking status (never, former, or current drinkers), body mass index, high‐sensitivity troponin T, NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide), and medication use (antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid‐lowering drugs). To quantify the overall health condition, we calculated a comorbidity score for each patient based on the presence of 7 comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, HF, stroke, chronic kidney disease, and cancer. The comorbidity score was a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 7, with higher scores indicating worse health condition. Detailed assessments of the cardiac biomarkers and 7 comorbidities are shown in the Supplemental Methods.

Statistical Analysis

For descriptive statistics, continuous variables were presented as mean±SD or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables were presented as number (percentage). Baseline characteristics, according to frailty status at baseline or temporal changes in frailty status, were compared using 1‐way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables, and χ2 tests for categorical variables. The missing rates of covariates are summarized in Table S2. Missing data were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations. 39 , 40

Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association between baseline frailty and the risk of MACEs in patients with CHD. Survival time for each observation was calculated from baseline to the first occurrence of a MACE, death, or censoring date (December 31, 2018), whichever came first. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were calculated with sequential adjustment for the aforementioned covariates. Using the same modeling approach, we analyzed the associations of temporal changes in frailty with the risk of MACEs in patients with CHD. In analyses of changes in frailty scores, we further controlled for the baseline frailty score in the multivariable‐adjusted model. The proportional hazards assumption was tested and verified using Schoenfeld's residuals methods, and the results showed no violation in the analyses (all P>0.05).

Additionally, we assessed whether adding baseline frailty improved discrimination for MACE risk in patients with CHD using time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for the base model (including all covariates) and the base model plus frailty status at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively. An increase in AUC indicated improved discrimination. Risk reclassification was further evaluated using the net reclassification index and integrated discrimination improvement. In addition, decision curve analysis was performed to compare the clinical utility of the models by quantifying net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to examine the robustness of our main results. First, we repeated the main analyses using the Lin Wei Yang Ying model. The Lin Wei Yang Ying model is an extension of the Cox model that accounts for multiple recurrent events per patient. 41 Second, we further adjusted for age at the first CHD episode and the number of CHD episodes before baseline. Third, we further incorporated laboratory measurements (systolic blood pressure, blood glucose, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, and estimated glomerular filtration rate) into the multivariable‐adjusted model. Fourth, the main analyses were repeated after excluding patients who experienced a CHD episode within 90 days before baseline. Fifth, we restricted our analyses to patients without baseline HF and stroke. Sixth, considering the potential competing risk of death before incident MACEs, we applied a competing risk model to the main analyses. 42 Seventh, we repeated the main analyses by excluding patients with missing data on covariates to explore the impact of the multiple imputation. Finally, we defined follow‐up as starting from the second survey and reassessed the associations between changes in frailty and MACEs. In addition, we performed stratified analyses by age (<75 or ≥75 years) and sex (male or female).

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We presented 2‐sided P values throughout, and P<0.05 indicated statistical significance. Given the large number of statistical tests performed in this study, we additionally conducted post hoc multiple testing correction using the false discovery rate method, with Q<0.05 considered statistically significant after correction.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 1793 eligible patients with CHD were included for baseline frailty analyses. The mean age was 76.6 years, and 842 (47.0%) were female patients (Table 1). Among these patients, 203 (11.3%) were classified as frail, 941 (52.5%) as prefrail, and 649 (36.2%) as robust. Among the study population, 312 (17.4%) patients had a history of acute myocardial infarction, 379 (21.1%) had unstable angina, and 985 (54.9%) had chronic coronary syndrome. Compared with robust patients, frail and prefrail patients were older, more often women, and had lower levels of education and household income. Moreover, frail and prefrail patients also had higher body mass index and comorbidity scores.

Association of Frailty at Baseline With the Risk of MACEs

During a median follow‐up of 3.6 (interquartile range, 1.4–5.8) years, 1153 MACE cases were documented. Figure S3 shows the Kaplan–Meier curves for the cumulative incidence of MACEs by baseline frailty status. Among this population, frail patients had the highest incidence of MACEs, followed by prefrail patients, whereas robust patients had the lowest incidence. Table 3 presents the association of baseline frailty with the risk of MACEs after adjusting for covariates. Frail patients presented a significantly higher risk of MACEs than robust patients (HR, 1.75 [95% CI, 1.43–2.15]; P<0.001). The significantly increased risk of MACEs was also observed in prefrail patients (HR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.08–1.42]; P=0.002). Similar associations were found for MACE subtypes, including recurrent CHD, incident HF, and incident stroke (Table S3).

Discriminative Performance of Baseline Frailty for the Risk of MACEs

We assessed whether adding baseline frailty improved discrimination for MACE risk beyond a traditional clinical risk model including sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical risk factors (Figure 2). For the 1‐year follow‐up, the addition of frailty status to the clinical risk model (AUC, 0.648 [95% CI, 0.615–0.681]) did not significantly improve model discrimination (AUC, 0.658 [95% CI, 0.625–0.691]; ΔAUC, 0.010; P=0.179). The net reclassification index was 0.079 (95% CI, −0.042 to 0.245), and the integrated discrimination improvement was 0.009 (95% CI, 0.003–0.017). At 3 years, adding frailty status was associated with a statistically significant improvement in discrimination, with the AUC increasing from 0.648 (95% CI, 0.622–0.674) to 0.662 (95% CI, 0.636–0.688) (ΔAUC, 0.014, P=0.012), along with improvements in net reclassification index (0.166 [95% CI, 0.066–0.287]) and integrated discrimination improvement (0.015 [95% CI, 0.006–0.024]). Similarly, at the 5‐year follow‐up, the AUC increased from 0.686 (95% CI, 0.661–0.712) to 0.702 (95% CI, 0.677–0.727) after adding frailty status (ΔAUC, 0.016; P=0.004), with corresponding improvements in net reclassification index (0.168 [95% CI, 0.083–0.320]) and integrated discrimination improvement (0.016 [95% CI, 0.007–0.026]). In addition, decision curve analysis indicated that incorporating frailty status provided greater net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, indicating potential improvement in clinical utility.

Associations of Longitudinal Changes in Frailty With the Risk of MACEs

During a median follow‐up of 5.1 (interquartile range, 2.0–6.3) years, 487 MACE cases were documented. Figure 3A shows the association of changes in frailty status with the risk of MACEs. In comparison with stable robust patients, patients who progressed from robust to prefrail/frail status presented a significantly increased risk of MACEs (HR, 1.43 [95% CI, 1.07–1.91]; P=0.016). The elevated risk of MACEs was also observed in prefrail patients who progressed to frail status (HR, 2.30 [95% CI, 1.62–3.27]; P<0.001) when compared with stable prefrail patients. Notably, patients who recovered from prefrail to robust status had a significantly decreased risk of MACEs compared with those who remained prefrail (HR, 0.62 [95% CI, 0.40–0.96]; P=0.032). However, no significant difference was observed in MACE risk for frail patients who reverted to robust/prefrail status (HR, 1.03 [95% CI, 0.52–2.03]; P=0.930) compared with stable frail patients.

As shown in Figure 3B, a higher cumulative frailty score was associated with an increased risk of MACEs. Each 1‐score increase in cumulative frailty score corresponded to a 19% increased risk (HR, 1.19 [95% CI, 1.12–1.26]; P<0.001). The HRs were 1.23 (95% CI, 0.92–1.64; P=0.168) for 1 score, 1.33 (95% CI, 0.99–1.79; P=0.062) for 2 scores, 1.46 (95% CI, 1.06–2.00; P=0.021) for 3 scores, and 2.49 (95% CI, 1.82–3.40; P<0.001) for >3 scores, demonstrating a dose–response relationship. In addition, patients with increasing frailty scores over time had a higher risk of MACEs, whereas those with decreasing scores had a lower risk (Figure 3C). The respective HRs were 1.40 (95% CI, 1.12–1.75; P=0.003) for 1 score increase, 1.80 (95% CI, 1.31–2.47; P<0.001) for >1 score increase, 0.69 (95% CI, 0.51–0.94; P=0.018) for 1 score decrease, and 0.68 (95% CI, 0.41–1.12; P=0.126) for >1 score decrease.

Sensitivity Analysis

Our findings were consistently shown in multiple models with different sets of covariates and remained statistically significant after correction for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (Tables S3 and S4). When using Lin Wei Yang Ying models to repeat the main analyses, consistent results were found compared with those from Cox models (Table S5). Additional adjustments for age at the first CHD episode and the number of CHD episodes before baseline (Table S6) or laboratory measures (Table S7) did not alter the observed associations, although some estimates were attenuated. The findings remained generally unchanged after excluding patients who experienced a CHD episode within 90 days before baseline (Table S8) or excluding patients with HF and stroke at baseline (Table S9). Results were consistent with the main analyses when using the competing risk models (Table S10) or excluding patients with missing data on covariates (Table S11). When follow‐up was defined as starting from the second survey, the association between recovery from prefrail to robust status and MACEs was slightly attenuated (Table S12). In the stratified analyses by age and sex, similar results were observed (Tables S13 and S14).

Discussion

In this prospective study, we investigated the associations of baseline frailty and its longitudinal changes with the prognosis of patients with CHD. Our findings indicated that baseline frailty was linked to an increased risk of MACEs in patients with CHD. Incorporating baseline frailty into a traditional risk model significantly improved discrimination for MACE risk. Additionally, we identified significant associations between longitudinal changes in frailty and MACE risk among patients with CHD. Patients who progressed from robust to prefrail/frail or from prefrail to frail status exhibited an increased risk of MACEs. Conversely, recovery from prefrail to robust status was associated with a decreased MACE risk. These findings suggest that frailty assessment may be useful for risk stratification among patients with CHD and highlight the potential value of longitudinal frailty monitoring in clinical practice.

Previous studies have demonstrated close associations between baseline frailty and adverse health outcomes in adults with CHD. 17 , 18 , 19 In the National Inpatient Sample, frailty was associated with higher rates of in‐hospital morbidity and death among patients hospitalized for myocardial infarction. 17 Similarly, Campo and colleagues observed a significant relationship between frailty and the risk of MACEs in 402 patients with acute coronary syndrome. 18 In general, our results were in line with these findings and added valuable evidence to the current literature that prefrailty or frailty was related to an increased risk of MACEs in patients with CHD, particularly given the relatively larger sample size and extended follow‐up period of our study. The elevated incidence of MACEs among frail patients with CHD may be explained by shared physiological pathways such as inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and coagulopathy. 43 , 44 , 45 Additionally, factors like a higher prevalence of sarcopenia, reduced physiological reserves, and impaired rehabilitation capacity might further elucidate these associations. 46

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to explore the associations between longitudinal changes in frailty and MACE risk among patients with CHD. Given emerging evidence that frailty may be reversible through appropriate interventions, 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 frailty may represent a potentially modifiable prognostic factor. Using data from 2 surveys of frailty assessment to capture longitudinal frailty changes, we identified 7 distinct transition patterns of frailty phenotypes. We found that patients who progressed from robust to prefrail/frail status or from prefrail to frail status faced an increased risk of MACEs. These findings highlight the adverse influence of frailty progression on the prognosis of patients with CHD. Conversely, patients with CHD who recovered from prefrail to robust status exhibited a decreased risk of MACEs. However, recovery from frail to robust/prefrail status was not associated with a reduced risk of MACEs. This finding is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that once individuals reach an advanced stage of frailty, a potential “point of no return” may be reached, beyond which the underlying physiological decline is less reversible and the risk of adverse outcomes remains elevated. 47 In addition, individuals who were frail at baseline likely had more severe comorbid conditions, which may further attenuate the potential benefits of modest frailty improvement. However, because only a small number of patients with CHD achieved full recovery from frail to robust status during follow‐up (Figure S1), we could not evaluate the prognostic impact of this specific transition pattern. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence against the potential effectiveness of interventions targeting frailty in patients with CHD. Of note, we used the cumulative frailty score and changes in frailty scores to further capture longitudinal frailty changes, offering unique insights into this field. The results demonstrated a clear dose–response association between cumulative frailty score and the risk of MACEs. Patients who exhibited rising frailty scores between 2 assessment points faced a greater risk of MACEs, whereas those with declining scores experienced a reduced risk. These findings suggest that reliance on single‐point frailty measurements may underestimate its impact on CHD prognosis, highlighting the importance of ongoing frailty monitoring in clinical practice.

Our findings have several critical clinical and public health implications. First, frailty was significantly associated with MACE risk among patients with CHD, and adding frailty to a traditional risk model significantly improved its discrimination for MACE risk. Therefore, integrating frailty assessment into routine clinical practice for prognostic evaluation and risk stratification of patients with CHD is essential. Patients identified as prefrail or frail may represent a high‐risk group warranting closer clinical management and longitudinal monitoring to optimize cardiovascular care. Assessments of frailty risk factors should also be conducted on robust patients to identify at‐risk individuals early, allowing for the implementation of preventive measures to delay frailty progression. Furthermore, our findings highlight that frailty is a dynamic and potentially reversible condition. Interventions targeting prefrailty may be associated with improved prognosis among patients with CHD. However, the prognostic impact of reversing established frailty remains inconclusive. Future prospective studies and clinical trials are warranted to determine whether interventions aimed at improving frailty status could reduce the risk of adverse cardiovascular events.

The strengths of this study include the use of a prospective cohort with validated approaches to assess frailty status and active ascertainment to reduce misclassification of CHD. By examining both baseline frailty and its longitudinal changes, our study provides a more comprehensive understanding of the prognostic relevance of frailty in patients with CHD. However, several limitations should be accounted for when interpreting these findings. First, because we focused on older patients with CHD, survival bias should be considered, as those who survived to baseline may have been healthier than the broader population of patients with CHD. In addition, selection bias may have been introduced in the frailty transition analyses because 931 patients without frailty data at visit 6 were further excluded. We compared the baseline characteristics of included and excluded patients (Table S15), and the results suggested the presence of healthy participant bias. Consistent with this concern, the proportion of patients included in the transition analyses decreased with worsening baseline frailty status, from 59.5% among robust patients to 44.3% among prefrail patients and 29.1% among frail patients (Figure 1). This selection process may have led to underrepresentation of patients with more severe frailty, greater comorbidity burden, or poorer cardiovascular health. Consequently, the distribution of frailty transitions and their associations with MACEs may have been affected by differential participation in the second survey, and the generalizability of findings from the transition analyses should be interpreted with caution. Second, frailty status at baseline may be influenced by underlying disease severity, which could lead to potential misclassification of frailty and its transitions. However, individuals experiencing acute or severe conditions that could have affected the measurement of frailty components at the time of the baseline assessment may have been less likely to participate in the baseline survey. In addition, the prevalence of frailty in this study was comparable with that reported in previous cohorts (11.3% versus 9.9%). 48 These considerations suggest that, although misclassification cannot be ruled out, its overall impact may be limited. Third, the generalizability of our findings from the US cohort to other populations requires further investigation. Fourth, the lack of detailed information on CHD severity is a notable limitation. However, we have included cardiac biomarkers (high‐sensitivity troponin T and NT‐proBNP), comorbidities, and medication use in our analyses to partly account for baseline disease severity and further considered total prior events and laboratory measurements in sensitivity analyses. Finally, we were unable to explore the impact of recovery from frail to robust status on CHD prognosis due to an insufficient number of patients experiencing this transition pattern across the 2 data collection waves. Larger longitudinal studies are warranted to construct more complex transition models and better clarify the potential causal relationships between long‐term changes in frailty and CHD prognosis.

Conclusions

Baseline frailty was associated with an elevated risk of MACEs among patients with CHD. Progression from robust to prefrail/frail, or from prefrail to frail status, was associated with a higher risk of MACEs, whereas recovery from prefrail to robust status was associated with a lower risk. Future research should evaluate whether tailored interventions targeting frailty can delay frailty progression, promote frailty recovery, and improve cardiovascular prognosis in patients with CHD, thereby informing the development of precise prevention strategies for this high‐risk population.
