Fahr syndrome in an 18-year-old male with pseudohypoparathyroidism: Clinical-radiological dissociation
Authors: Syed Musa Mufarrih, Abida Akbar, Bushra Rehman, Saad Zaman, Fozia Memon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFahr syndrome, characterized by bilateral intracranial calcifications, is a rare but clinically significant complication of chronic calcium–phosphate dysregulation in pseudohypoparathyroidism (PHP). Clinical manifestations frequently do not correlate with the extent of radiological findings, posing diagnostic challenges. An 18-year-old male with longstanding PHP presented with occipital headaches, carpopedal spasms, and depressed mood following more than two weeks of medication non-compliance. Non-contrast computed tomography (CT) of the brain confirmed extensive bilateral calcifications involving the basal ganglia, thalami, dentate nuclei, and subcortical white matter, with supportive findings on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including T1 hypointensity, variable T2 signal, and marked blooming artifact on susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). Neurological examination was largely unremarkable. Laboratory evaluation revealed hypocalcemia (serum calcium 7.2 mg/dL) with elevated intact parathyroid hormone (137 pg/mL), consistent with end-organ PTH resistance; serum phosphate (4.2 mg/dL) and 25-hydroxyvitamin D (42.4 ng/mL) were within the normal range. Electroencephalography showed no epileptiform activity. Optimization of calcium and calcitriol supplementation, together with symptomatic treatment, resulted in resolution of carpopedal spasms and significant improvement in headaches and mood at 15-day follow-up. This case illustrates the marked clinical–radiological dissociation characteristic of Fahr syndrome. Neurological evaluation or neuroimaging should be considered in PHP patients who develop new neurological or neuropsychiatric symptoms, particularly in the setting of poor biochemical control.Keywords: Fahr syndrome, Pseudohypoparathyroidism, Intracranial calcifications, Basal ganglia calcification, Hypocalcemia, Clinical–radiological dissociation

Introduction

Fahr syndrome is a rare neurological condition characterized by bilateral, symmetrical intracranial calcifications most commonly affecting the basal ganglia, thalami, dentate nuclei, and cerebral cortex [1]. While idiopathic cases exist (Fahr disease), secondary forms result from metabolic, infectious, or autoimmune disorders. Among metabolic causes, disorders of parathyroid hormone (PTH) regulation—including hypoparathyroidism and pseudohypoparathyroidism—represent the most frequently identified etiologies [2,3].

Pseudohypoparathyroidism (PHP) comprises a heterogeneous group of genetic disorders characterized by end-organ resistance to PTH despite normal or elevated circulating PTH levels. This resistance results in chronic hypocalcemia and, classically, hyperphosphatemia, creating conditions that may promote ectopic calcification in metabolically active brain regions [4,5]. The clinical spectrum is heterogeneous, ranging from incidental radiological findings to severe neurological and psychiatric manifestations, including movement disorders, seizures, cognitive impairment, and mood disturbances [6].

A particularly challenging aspect of Fahr syndrome is the frequent discordance between radiological burden and clinical severity. Patients may harbor extensive intracranial calcifications with minimal or no symptoms, while others with modest radiological findings may present with significant neurological dysfunction [7]. This clinical–radiological dissociation often contributes to delayed diagnosis and underscores the importance of maintaining diagnostic vigilance in at-risk populations.

We present the case of an 18-year-old male with PHP who developed extensive bilateral basal ganglia calcifications yet maintained a largely normal neurological examination. The case highlights key diagnostic challenges, particularly the distinction between hypocalcemic tetany and epileptic seizures, and illustrates the importance of comprehensive evaluation in PHP patients presenting with new neurological symptoms.

Case presentation

History and clinical features

An 18-year-old male with PHP, diagnosed at two years of age, presented to a tertiary care hospital in Karachi, Pakistan, with a two-month history of occipital headaches, recurrent painful carpopedal spasms, and low mood. The initial diagnosis had been established following evaluation of recurrent hypocalcemic seizures in infancy, with biochemical findings consistent with PHP. He had been maintained on oral calcium and calcitriol supplementation with documented optimal calcium levels on prior follow-up. However, his father reported that the patient had discontinued his medications for more than two weeks due to low mood and frustration with his treatment regimen, and that the current symptoms developed after this period of non-compliance.

The carpopedal spasms involved both hands and feet, occurred spontaneously several times daily, lasted 30-60 seconds, and were accompanied by paresthesias described as “pins and needles.” These episodes significantly disrupted daily activities but were not associated with altered consciousness, automatisms, urinary or fecal incontinence, tongue biting, or postictal confusion. Sleep and appetite were preserved. The patient denied recent hair loss, weight change, heat or cold intolerance, polyuria, or gastrointestinal symptoms.

Low mood was identified during history-taking and confirmed by formal psychiatric assessment; a direct causal relationship between mood symptoms and intracranial calcifications cannot be established on the basis of a single case.

Birth and developmental history were unremarkable, with age-appropriate milestone achievement. The patient was born to consanguineous parents. Siblings were healthy, and family history was otherwise non-contributory. Genetic testing was not performed due to financial constraints; this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Physical examination

The patient was alert, oriented, and cooperative. Vital signs were stable. Weight and height were within normal ranges for age. Thyroid examination was unremarkable, and puberty had progressed normally (Tanner stage 5). Mental status assessment revealed low mood with constricted affect.

Cranial nerves II-XII were intact with normal pupillary responses and full extraocular movements. Motor examination demonstrated normal muscle bulk, tone, and strength (5/5) in all extremities. Mild bradykinesia was noted on rapid alternating movements, and subtle rigidity was observed on passive limb movement; these findings may correlate with basal ganglia involvement. Finger–nose testing remained accurate. No dystonia, tremor, or involuntary movements were observed. Deep tendon reflexes were 2+ and symmetrical with downgoing plantar responses. Sensory examination was intact to light touch, pinprick, and vibration throughout. Gait was steady with normal base.

Notably, Chvostek and Trousseau signs were negative despite active carpopedal spasms and documented hypocalcemia; ionized calcium was not obtained, and the recognized low sensitivity of these clinical signs—reported negative in up to 30% of patients with biochemically confirmed hypocalcemia—may also account for this finding [8]. General examination revealed a soft, non-tender abdomen without organomegaly. Ophthalmologic examination showed no cataracts or papilledema.

Ionized calcium and albumin-corrected calcium were not obtained at this presentation; reliance on total calcium alone is a recognized limitation, as total calcium is susceptible to variations in serum proteins. The hypocalcemia at presentation was attributed to recent medication non-compliance. Serum phosphate was within the normal range, which should be interpreted cautiously given that hyperphosphatemia is classically expected in untreated PHP; the normal value in this patient likely reflects the effect of prior long-term supplementation, although phosphate levels at the time of initial diagnosis were not available for comparison. Overall, these findings were consistent with the established diagnosis of PHP with end-organ PTH resistance.

Electroencephalography

Standard EEG demonstrated normal background activity with no epileptiform discharges or focal abnormalities. During periods of carpopedal spasms captured on EEG monitoring, only movement artifact was recorded without any corresponding cerebral epileptiform activity, supporting a peripheral, non-epileptic mechanism. While a normal EEG does not independently confirm tetany, the combination of normal EEG findings, the clinical characteristics of the spasms, documented hypocalcemia, and complete resolution with calcium correction strongly supports hypocalcemic tetany as the underlying mechanism.

Neuroimaging

Neuroimaging was pursued in the setting of recurrent carpopedal spasms, occipital headaches, hypocalcemia, and neuropsychiatric symptoms in a patient with longstanding PHP, to evaluate for intracranial complications of chronic calcium–phosphate dysregulation.

Non-contrast computed tomography (CT) of the brain was performed at the time of presentation and provided the primary radiological confirmation of Fahr syndrome. CT demonstrated extensive, symmetric, hyperdense calcifications involving bilateral basal ganglia (caudate nuclei, putamen, and globi pallidi), thalami, dentate nuclei, subcortical white matter, and cerebellar hemispheres; the distribution and symmetry were characteristic of Fahr syndrome. Representative non-contrast axial CT images are shown in Fig. 1A and Fig. 1B. Non-contrast CT remains the reference modality for confirming intracranial calcifications and constituted the basis of the radiological diagnosis in this patient.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without contrast was subsequently performed and demonstrated signal characteristics in the regions identified on CT that were supportive of calcium deposition. On T1-weighted imaging, the affected areas demonstrated low signal intensity (Fig. 2), a pattern compatible with calcium deposition and distinct from copper or manganese deposits, which typically appear hyperintense on T1. T2-weighted sequences showed variable signal intensity within the same regions (Fig. 3). Susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) demonstrated marked signal dropout (blooming artifact) in bilateral basal ganglia, thalami, and dentate nuclei, with additional susceptibility noted in bilateral frontal subcortical white matter and cerebellar hemispheres (Fig. 4). SWI blooming in this anatomical distribution is highly characteristic of paramagnetic deposits and, in this clinical and biochemical context, is strongly supportive of calcium deposition. MRI findings should nevertheless be interpreted as supportive of, rather than confirmatory for, intracranial calcifications, with non-contrast CT remaining the diagnostic standard.

Management and clinical course

The primary therapeutic approach focused on correction of the underlying metabolic abnormality. Oral calcium supplementation was optimized, calcitriol dosing was adjusted, and medication compliance was reinforced.

Given the extensive basal ganglia calcifications and theoretical seizure risk, empiric antiepileptic therapy was initiated despite the absence of epileptiform activity on EEG. It should be acknowledged that prophylactic antiepileptic therapy in patients with Fahr syndrome and no documented seizures or epileptiform activity lacks robust supporting evidence; this decision reflected clinical caution in the setting of extensive calcification burden rather than guideline-based practice. Levetiracetam 500 mg twice daily was selected as first-line therapy given its favorable side-effect profile. Lamotrigine was subsequently added using standard titration (25 mg, increased to 50 mg, then 100 mg over successive weeks), in part for its established mood-stabilizing properties. A clear plan for reassessment of antiepileptic therapy was established (see Outcome and Follow-up).

Symptomatic treatment included amitriptyline 25 mg at bedtime, selected for its dual benefit in managing both depressed mood and chronic headache, and naproxen 500 mg twice daily for headache management.

The patient demonstrated a favorable clinical response. Carpopedal spasms resolved completely within one week of treatment initiation, temporally consistent with correction of hypocalcemia. Headaches improved significantly, and mood symptoms showed gradual amelioration. No further episodes of altered consciousness or concerning neurological symptoms occurred during hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up

At 15-day follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of carpopedal spasms and significant improvement in headaches and mood. Follow-up laboratory studies confirmed normalization of serum calcium (9.1 mg/dL). The complete resolution of symptoms with calcium correction, together with the absence of epileptiform activity on EEG and the clinical characteristics of the episodes, strongly supports hypocalcemic tetany rather than seizures as the underlying mechanism. Antiepileptic medications were continued pending further observation, given the extensive calcification burden. The patient’s motor and behavioral symptoms will be reassessed at the 1-month follow-up clinic; if he remains clinically stable, a gradual taper of antiepileptic therapy to the lowest effective dose will be initiated.

The patient and family received detailed counseling regarding PHP, its complications, and the importance of medication compliance and regular monitoring. Genetic testing was recommended to establish the specific PHP subtype and to allow more precise prognostic counseling; however, the family declined due to financial constraints. Based on the clinical profile—PTH resistance with hypocalcemia, absence of Albright hereditary osteodystrophy features (normal stature, no brachydactyly, no subcutaneous ossifications), and normal physical appearance—the presentation is most consistent with PHP type 1b, which is characterized by renal PTH resistance without the somatic features of PHP type 1a [4,9]. This represents a clinical impression rather than a definitive diagnosis, given the absence of genetic confirmation. Follow-up was scheduled at one month, with planned ongoing monitoring of calcium homeostasis.

Discussion

This case illustrates several clinically important aspects of Fahr syndrome in the setting of pseudohypoparathyroidism, including clinical–radiological dissociation, the critical distinction between hypocalcemic tetany and epileptic seizures, and the central role of metabolic correction in management.

Clinical–radiological dissociation is a well-recognized feature of Fahr syndrome. Our patient harbored extensive bilateral calcifications involving multiple brain regions yet maintained a remarkably preserved neurological examination, a phenomenon documented in prior reports and systematic reviews [[7], [10], [14]]. Several mechanisms have been hypothesized to account for this dissociation, including compensatory neuroplasticity in the setting of slowly progressive calcification, preferential deposition in regions that do not disrupt critical neural circuits, and individual variation in neural reserve; these proposed mechanisms remain speculative. The discordance nevertheless carries important diagnostic implications, as clinicians may underestimate disease severity on the basis of examination findings alone.

Pseudohypoparathyroidism produces end-organ resistance to PTH at the renal tubule and bone, resulting in impaired calcium reabsorption and vitamin D activation despite elevated circulating PTH levels [11]. It is hypothesized that chronic hypocalcemia and hyperphosphatemia create conditions favoring ectopic calcification when the calcium–phosphate product exceeds the solubility threshold, with calcium deposition occurring preferentially in metabolically active regions—particularly the basal ganglia, thalami, and cerebellar dentate nuclei [12]. In our patient, serum phosphate at presentation was within the normal range, likely reflecting the effect of long-term supplementation; nevertheless, the extensive calcifications suggest a period of sustained metabolic dysregulation, possibly prior to or during early treatment.

A critical diagnostic challenge in this case involved distinguishing hypocalcemic tetany from epileptic seizures. Hypocalcemia increases neuronal excitability, which can manifest as either tetany or seizures [8]. Several features strongly favored tetany in our patient: the painful quality of the spasms, accompanying paresthesias, brief self-limited episodes, preserved consciousness, absence of epileptiform discharges on EEG, and complete resolution with calcium correction alone. EEG monitoring during captured spasms showed only movement artifact without cerebral correlate, further supporting a peripheral, non-epileptic mechanism.

Despite this clinical evidence for tetany, empiric antiepileptic therapy was initiated given the extensive calcification burden and theoretical seizure risk. Prophylactic antiepileptic therapy in Fahr syndrome patients without documented seizures or epileptiform activity lacks robust supporting evidence, and this decision represents an area of clinical uncertainty. The ongoing need for antiepileptic therapy in this patient requires careful reassessment, weighing the potential risks of unnecessary medication against the uncertain seizure risk conferred by extensive calcifications.

From a radiological standpoint, this case illustrates the complementary roles of CT and MRI in characterizing Fahr syndrome. Non-contrast CT remains the reference standard for detection and confirmation of intracranial calcifications because of its high sensitivity for hyperdense calcium deposits. MRI, while not a substitute for CT, provides supportive characterization through T1 hypointensity and—particularly—blooming artifact on susceptibility-weighted imaging, which is highly specific for paramagnetic deposits including calcium. The multi-sequence MRI characterization in this case, including SWI, offers a complementary radiological perspective that is not consistently emphasized in published Fahr syndrome reports.

The cornerstone of Fahr syndrome management in PHP is correction of the underlying metabolic abnormality. Calcitriol is the preferred vitamin D analog, as it bypasses the impaired renal 1-alpha-hydroxylation characteristic of PHP [15]. In our patient, optimization of calcium and calcitriol supplementation with reinforcement of medication compliance led to rapid symptom resolution. Metabolic correction does not reverse existing calcifications; the primary goal is prevention of progression and amelioration of hypocalcemic symptoms [13]. Symptomatic treatment should be tailored to the individual presentation, including appropriate psychotropic interventions when warranted.

Regarding screening for intracranial calcifications in PHP, no established guidelines currently recommend routine neuroimaging surveillance in asymptomatic patients. A pragmatic approach would favor reserving neuroimaging for patients who develop new-onset or changing neurological or psychiatric symptoms, or for those with long-standing suboptimal metabolic control, on a case-by-case basis.

Several limitations merit acknowledgment. First, genetic testing was not performed; the diagnosis of PHP type 1b therefore represents a clinical impression rather than a confirmed classification. Second, ionized and albumin-corrected calcium were not obtained, which would have provided a more precise assessment of calcium status. Third, formal neuropsychological testing was not performed, potentially missing subtle cognitive deficits. Fourth, long-term follow-up data beyond 15 days are not yet available. Fifth, although representative non-contrast CT images are included as Fig. 1A and Fig. 1B, the original PACS/DICOM export was not available; the included images were obtained by photography of the printed film, with consequent limitations in image quality compared with a direct radiology-system export. Nevertheless, the characteristic bilateral, symmetric, hyperdense calcifications in the basal ganglia and thalami remain clearly appreciable. Finally, the continuation of antiepileptic medications despite a normal EEG and confirmed tetany represents an area of clinical uncertainty that warrants extended observation.

Conclusion

This case illustrates key diagnostic and management considerations in Fahr syndrome secondary to pseudohypoparathyroidism. The marked discordance between extensive radiological calcification and preserved neurological function exemplifies the clinical–radiological dissociation characteristic of this condition. The distinction between hypocalcemic tetany and epileptic seizures has important therapeutic implications. Optimal care requires correction of underlying metabolic abnormalities through calcium and calcitriol supplementation, supplemented by individualized symptomatic therapy [16]. Multidisciplinary collaboration among endocrinology, neurology, psychiatry, and genetics services may be beneficial for comprehensive management.
