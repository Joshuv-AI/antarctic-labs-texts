Integration of newborn screening for sickle cell disease into primary health care in Bukavu, Democratic Republic of the Congo: a pilot study
Authors: Nash Mwanza Nangunia, Olivier Mukuku, Viviane Bianga Feza, Yves Mulindilwa Kyembwa, Théophile Barhwamire Kabesha, Zacharie Kibendelwa Tsongo, André Kabamba Mutombo, Stanislas Okitotsho Wembonyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited genetic disorder that represents a major public health challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). This disease, characterized by an amino acid substitution in the beta-globin chain, requires early management to reduce serious complications and improve patients’ quality of life. This study aimed to test the feasibility of integrating newborn screening for SCD into primary health care in Bukavu, in the DRC, to improve health outcomes for affected infants and reduce serious complications associated with the disease.MethodsA prospective cohort pilot study was conducted from November 2022 to December 2023 in 15 primary healthcare facilities in Bukavu. Mothers were screened for SCD at antenatal clinics and maternities. Blood samples from mothers and newborns were collected and tested using the HemoTypeSC™ point-of-care testing. Results were confirmed by Gazelle® electrophoresis in case of a positive result.ResultsOut of the 6,353 sensitized mothers, 4,496 consented to undergo screening, resulting in an acceptance rate of 70.77%. Among them, 91.61% were HbAA, 8.16% were HbAS, and 0.22% were HbSS. Among the mothers with HbS, only 77 received follow-up until delivery, demonstrating the feasibility of the follow-up process. The findings revealed that out of the 77 newborns, 66.23% were HbAA, 29.87% were HbAS, and 3.90% were HbSS.ConclusionsThe integration of newborn screening for SCD into primary health care in Bukavu, in the DRC, has demonstrated feasibility and effectiveness in the early identification of affected infants. This initiative has the potential to mitigate the serious complications linked to the disease and enhance health outcomes for patients. These findings set the stage for broader adoption of this approach to address the implications of SCD on maternal and child health in the DRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-02976-x.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited hemoglobinopathy caused by a point mutation (20 A→T; Glu6Val) at the 6th codon of the HBB gene, resulting in the substitution of glutamic acid with valine in the beta-globin chain. Normal adult hemoglobin (HbA) is composed of two alpha chains and two beta chains (α₂β₂), encoded by genes located on chromosomes 16 and 11, respectively [1]. SCD is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. The most severe form, sickle cell anemia, occurs in homozygous individuals (HbSS) who inherit two copies of the mutated gene. This mutation leads to the formation of sickle-shaped red blood cells, which obstruct blood vessels and cause severe complications, including vaso-occlusive crises, hemolytic anemia, and organ damage [2, 3]. Heterozygous carriers (HbAS), who inherit one copy of the mutated gene, typically exhibit few or no symptoms but can pass the abnormal gene to their offspring. This status is known as sickle-cell trait (SCT). When two parents are carriers of the sickle-cell trait (HbAS), each pregnancy has a 25% chance of producing a healthy baby (HbAA), a 50% chance of producing a baby with the HbAS genotype, and a 25% chance of producing a child with sickle cell disease (HbSS) [2]. There are other abnormal variants of the beta-globin chain that can be co-inherited with HbS, leading to composite forms of SCD. These composite heterozygotes include HbSC, resulting from the simultaneous inheritance of the HbS and HbC genes, HbSβ-thalassemia, resulting from the association of HbS with a beta-thalassemia gene, and HbSO, caused by co-inheritance of the HbS gene with a structural variant of hemoglobin HbO. Collectively, all forms, including sickle cell anemia and these composite heterozygotes, are referred to as SCD [1]. SCD poses a major public health challenge, especially in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where its prevalence is high. Recent estimates indicate that the global incidence of SCD has increased over the past two decades, primarily due to population growth in western and central SSA and India. In 2021, approximately 515,000 (425,000–614,000) newborns were affected by SCD worldwide, representing a 13.7% increase since 2000 [4]. SCD incidence and prevalence notably increased in SSA, where 405,000 births were affected in 2021 (343,000–478,000), marking a rise of 27.2% (23.4–30.2) since 2000. The region’s share of the global SCD burden grew significantly, from 70% (318,000/453,000) in 2000 to 79% (405,000/515,000) in 2021 [4]. SSA remains the most affected continent, with these births representing about 79% of all children born with haemoglobinopathies worldwide. The highest disability burden is concentrated in western and central SSA. Countries with an incidence at birth between 1,000 and 2,000 per 100,000 live births in 2021 included Bahrain, Angola, the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Kenya, Ghana, Guinea, Niger, and São Tomé and Príncipe [4]. In the DRC, up to 40% of the population may carry the sickle cell trait, with approximately 40,000 children born with SCD annually [5]. In a recent study in Lubumbashi, the prevalence of HbAS in newborns was 26.21%, while 5.01% of newborns were homozygous (HbSS). This gives a total prevalence of HbS (HbAS and HbSS) of 31.22% [6].

In Africa, SCD remains a major contributor to childhood mortality, with 50–80% of affected children dying before the age of five, particularly in SSA, where it accounts for 6.4% of under-five deaths [7, 8]. SCD was responsible for 38,403 deaths in 2019, reflecting a 26% rise since the year 2000 [8]. In the DRC, SCD mortality rates are particularly alarming, with 15.3% (95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 13.3–17.3) of children under one year, 36.4% (95% CI: 33.4–39.4) of those under five years, and 43.3% (95% CI: 39.3–47.3) of children under ten years dying from the disease [9]. These high mortality rates are influenced by several factors, including poor access to healthcare, limited medical resources, and socio-economic challenges [10]. Nevertheless, emerging evidence suggests that early diagnosis and effective care can lead to significant reductions in mortality. A cohort study in Kilifi, Kenya, for instance, found that children with SCD who were enrolled in a specialized outpatient clinic had much lower mortality rates compared to those who did not receive early care (adjusted incidence rate ratio: 0.26, 95% CI: 0.11–0.62) [11]. This highlights the importance of early detection and prompt medical intervention in improving survival rates among children with SCD.

SCD is a chronic, heterogeneous condition whose management aims to prevent complications, improve quality of life, and extend survival. It is based on early screening, regular medical follow-up, patient and family education, and the integration of appropriate therapeutic interventions [12]. Universal newborn screening is recommended to initiate prompt care, including vaccination, antibiotic prophylaxis, and hydroxyurea, to reduce morbidity and mortality [13]. Medical follow-up allows for monitoring disease progression and optimizing treatment adherence. Preventing complications relies on vaccination against encapsulated infections, antibiotic prophylaxis, and management of vaso-occlusive crises [12, 13]. Acute complications, such as severe infections, acute anemia, and acute chest syndrome, require rapid intervention. Chronic complications, including leg ulcers, gallstones, and nephropathy, require specific monitoring and tailored care [12, 13]. Beyond medical aspects, emotional support, therapeutic education, and the integration of patient- and family-centered care are crucial to improving treatment adherence and the quality of life for patients [14]. An integrated approach, enhancing care coordination, optimizes management and offers patients a more promising future [12, 15, 16].

Integrating newborn screening for SCD into primary healthcare through antenatal care (ANC) programmes in the DRC is crucial given the significant burden this genetic disease places on the Congolese population. It presents a valuable opportunity to detect affected infants from the earliest days of life, facilitating timely medical intervention and a substantial decrease in the severe complications and fatalities linked to this condition (including strategies to prevent severe infections and vaso-occlusive crises). Moreover, with the utilization of rapid screening tests, this process is becoming more accessible and efficient, ensuring broader availability of this essential service. Furthermore, this initiative forms part of a comprehensive approach to maternal and child health, emphasizing prevention, early identification, and effective management of medical conditions in newborns and their families.

The aim of the present study was to test the feasibility of implementing a screening programme for SCD using rapid diagnostic test devices within existing antenatal care in primary healthcare facilities (HCFs) in Bukavu, in the DRC. This study is crucial as it sets the foundation for early detection and management of SCD from antenatal care, which could enhance health outcomes for affected infants and bolster efforts to mitigate the impact of the disease on maternal and child health.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We conducted a prospective cohort pilot study from November 2022 to December 2023 in the municipality of Ibanda, situated in Bukavu, DRC, with an area of 11.57 km² (Fig. 1). The study took place in 15 HCFs providing primary health care in the commune, including Panzi General Referral Hospital, Chai Hospital Center, Cidasa Health Center, Philadelphia Hospital Center, SkyBorn Hospital, Biopharm Hospital Center, Ruhigita Clinic, Malikiya wa Amani Health Center, Bioglodie Medical Center, CELPA Hospital Center, Saint Luc Hospital Center, Olive Lembe Hospital Center, Mulungulungu Hospital Center, Kabuye Health Center, and Maman Mwilu Health Center. This diversity allowed for a more accurate representation of the local population and enabled the collection of meaningful data for this pilot study.

Prior to commencing the screening activity, we obtained authorization and collaborated with the Provincial Health Division of South Kivu, in the DRC, to inform the designated HCFs about the SCD screening programme and to gather information on existing services, such as ANC visits, deliveries, and pediatric care. Simultaneously, we consulted with healthcare workers (HCWs) in these HCFs to integrate screening for pregnant women using point-of-care testing in ANC wards. The HCFs involved were carefully selected, and their current resources and services were evaluated to ascertain the feasibility of incorporating maternal and newborn screening for SCD through point-of-care testing. These HCFs were chosen from a list of all HCFs in the Ibanda Health Zone provided by the Provincial Health Division of South Kivu, based on specific criteria: a minimum of ten maternity beds, at least ten nurses and four midwives, and a minimum of three permanent general practitioners. Furthermore, the facilities needed to offer ANC visits, delivery services, and pediatric care. The selected HCFs, which include referral hospitals and referral health centers, deliver comprehensive primary healthcare services, with teams of midwives and physicians ensuring continuous care for both mothers and newborns. These HCFs also maintain a functional connection between ANC and pediatric services, enabling seamless care from ANC follow-up to postnatal care, encompassing vaccination and infant development monitoring.

A minimum sample size of 6,060 was determined using the formula: n = z²pq/d², with a 95% CI and a standard deviation of 1.96. Since the prevalence of SCD among pregnant women in the DRC is unknown, a 50% prevalence rate was used as recommended. The calculation also accounted for a 5% margin of error and a 5% non-response rate. After calculating the sample size, 404 was obtained, which was then multiplied by 15 HCFs. The final sample size was 6,353 pregnant women who were sensitized for SCD screening.

Pregnant women, regardless of their gestational age, were included in the study if they voluntarily agreed to participate and attended ANC visits at one of the 15 selected HCFs. They had to be residents of Bukavu city and provide informed consent for SCD screening. Women not meeting these criteria were excluded. Only newborns of mothers with HbSC, HbAS, or HbSS genotypes were included in the study, while those born to HbAA mothers were excluded due to a lower SCD risk. Couples of mothers with HbAS or HbSS and their newborns who couldn’t be reached through phone contact or other contact methods (using community relays and peer educators) were classified as lost to follow-up.

Procedures

In our study, all mothers who provided written consent during ANC visits in the selected HCFs were screened for SCD using the HemoTypeSC™ point-of-care testing (Silver Lake Research, Azusa, CA, USA). The questionnaire utilized in this research was specifically designed for the study (Supplementary file). A small blood sample was obtained from each mother via a finger prick and applied to the HemoTypeSC™ absorbent pad following the manufacturer’s instructions [17] (Supplementary file). These mothers were monitored until delivery to collect blood samples from their newborns’ heels for newborn SCD screening using the HemoTypeSC™ point-of-care testing. If the HemoTypeSC™ point-of-care testing indicated HbAS or HbSS, additional blood spots were collected for confirmation using the Gazelle® device per the manufacturer’s instructions (Supplementary file). The design of the Gazelle® Hb Variant (by Hemex Health, Portland, Oregon, USA) has been previously documented [18, 19]. Essentially, Gazelle® is a single-use cartridge test that provides rapid, cost-effective, quantitative, and precise point-of-care diagnosis of SCD and carrier status. It operates as a cellulose acetate-based microchip electrophoresis system in a portable device, utilizing standard electrophoresis methods with built-in analysis software, electronic data storage, and wireless data transmission capabilities. The device is battery-powered with rechargeable lithium batteries, allowing tests to be performed throughout the day on a single charge [19].

Recruitment and screening were conducted by trained HCFs. Twenty midwives and nine laboratory technicians received training on the HemoTypeSC™ point-of-care testing, while laboratory technicians received additional instruction on using the Gazelle® device for result confirmation. These HCWs were assigned to 15 selected HCFs, with midwives subsequently training their colleagues to ensure seamless protocol implementation. The Gazelle® electrophoresis was performed by the nine trained laboratory technicians, divided into three teams across the three referral hospitals equipped with the device. In HCFs without Gazelle® device, blood samples from newborns of HbS-carrying mothers (HbSC, HbAS, or HbSS) were transported to the nearest referral hospital via a designated courier.

The HemoTypeSC™ point-of-care testing, Gazelle® devices, and cartridges were provided through the DREPACCI project, funded by the Pierre Fabre Foundation and the Agence Française de Développement, in collaboration with the National Sickle Cell Disease Control Programme in the DRC. This project handled stock management and replenishment. Financial incentives, based on the number of tests conducted, were given to HCFs and HCWs to support logistical expenses and promote involvement.

Screening was integrated into ANC visits following a health education session on SCD and the importance of early detection, using visual aids (picture boxes and leaflets) provided by the project (Supplementary file). Each pregnant woman was offered the HemoTypeSC™ point-of-care testing during ANC visits, performed on a blood sample taken alongside other routine hematological tests (e.g., HIV, hemoglobin levels, etc.). Results were recorded in her medical file. Screening could take place at any ANC visit, including at delivery if the mother had not been tested earlier. In cases of HbSC, HbAS, or HbSS results, a confirmatory test was conducted using the Gazelle® device. Newborns of HbS-carrying mothers were systematically tested with Gazelle® electrophoresis, either before discharge from the maternity ward or during preschool visits if screening was not performed at birth. The interpretation and explanation of results were ensured by midwives or the attending physician responsible for the mother and child. The ANC visit form included a dedicated section for SCD screening, facilitating follow-up and identification of high-risk cases. A single consent form, covering both mother and infant, was attached to the medical record (Supplementary file). Only contact details of at-risk mothers (AS, SS, or SC status) were recorded to ensure follow-up while maintaining confidentiality. Mothers diagnosed with HbSS were referred to Panzi General Referral Hospital for specialized obstetric care. HbSS newborns were managed at the Clinique Ami des Enfants, a center specializing in SCD management. These facilities were selected for their expertise in providing optimal care for affected mothers and infants.

Newborns identified as having SCD received pneumococcal and other vaccines according to the national vaccination schedule at the designated health facilities (Supplementary file). Follow-up appointments were set every 3 months post-screening, with the provision of folic acid (5 mg/day for 15 days per month, lifelong) and oral penicillin V, administered in different doses per day based on age according to national guidelines (125 mg twice daily for children aged 0–3 years, 250 mg twice daily for children aged 3–6 years, and 250 mg three times daily for children over 6 years), along with zinc supplementation at a dose of 10 mg per day, aimed at preventing infections, especially pneumococcal sepsis and meningitis. This treatment plan continued until the child reached five years, at which point penicillin V could be discontinued if no major complications arose and the child had received all necessary vaccinations. Emphasis was placed on the importance of adhering to the prescribed medication regimen to avert potential complications during the initial follow-up visit. Parents or guardians were instructed to dissolve penicillin V or folic acid tablets in a 10 mL container before administration; for breastfed neonates, using breast milk as a diluent was advised. At each visit, they were reminded to strictly follow the treatment plan and cautioned against sharing the medication with other family members or friends. In cases where participants missed their scheduled appointments, phone calls were made to encourage them to reschedule and provide additional information on the importance of ongoing follow-up care.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 16 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Proportions of the various types of hemoglobin (HbAA, HbAS, HbAC, and HbSS) in both mothers and newborns were calculated, and the outcomes were displayed with a two-sided 95% confidence interval utilizing the Wilson score method with continuity correction.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Official University of Bukavu (Approval No. UOB/CEM/010/2022). The study adhered to ethical guidelines for human subject research as specified by both the DRC and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki, including its subsequent revisions. Prior to participation, written informed consent was secured from each individual, ensuring the confidentiality of their information and the freedom to withdraw from the study at any point. Participants were informed that their involvement in the study was voluntary. Blood samples were obtained for genotype confirmation at no expense to the participants, and measures were taken to reduce any discomfort during the blood sampling process.

Discussion

One of the major achievements of the pilot study in Bukavu, in the DRC, is the robust integration of newborn screening for SCD into the existing primary healthcare infrastructure. This study has significant implications for public health in the region. The results of this study show that newborn screening is not only feasible but also essential for improving the care and health outcomes of infants with SCD. The study reported a 70.77% acceptance rate for SCD screening among mothers who had been screened, reflecting the effectiveness of the communication and education strategies employed by the healthcare team. Of the 6,353 mothers reached, 4,496 agreed to be screened for SCD, indicating a strong willingness on the part of the community to engage in genetic screening initiatives within the antenatal care system. The acceptance rate indicates good receptiveness on the part of the target population. This figure is encouraging and demonstrates that, despite the socio-cultural and logistical challenges, mothers are willing to take part in screening programmes that have the potential to improve the health of their children. Acceptance and participation rates clearly indicate that there is fertile ground for public health initiatives targeting genetic diseases and that, with the right strategies, these can be successfully implemented in regions such as the DRC. To successfully implement a newborn screening programme for SCD, several elements are essential. These include ensuring access to HCFs, simple and affordable screening devices, educational materials and counseling services, and adequate follow-up and management to prevent serious chronic complications [16, 17]. In our study, approximately 30% of potential participants did not accept the SCD screening. This reluctance may have been due to a lack of awareness about SCD, concerns about stigma, cultural beliefs, or misconceptions about the screening process. Additionally, some participants may have been deterred by perceived costs or inconvenience. To address these barriers, future studies should focus on raising awareness through education, normalizing screening in routine ANC, and offering counseling to address concerns. Ensuring that the screening process is accessible, convenient, and culturally sensitive could improve participation rates.

The positive response to screening mothers for SCD indicates a potential shift in addressing genetic diseases in high-prevalence regions. The data underscore the urgent need for broad screening programmes in areas like the DRC, where SCD prevalence is significant. This is supported by Ezenwosu et al.’s study [20], emphasizing the success of community Hb genotype testing for pregnant women in Nigeria. The effectiveness and reliability of HemoTypeSC™ as a rapid, cost-efficient point-of-care testing tool for early SCD detection and sickle cell trait identification [17, 21, 22] were also affirmed, further endorsing its use in our study.

Despite the successes, the study faced significant challenges, particularly the loss of follow-up for a substantial number of mothers before delivery (299 out of 376, or 79.52%), which could potentially undermine the effectiveness of the SCD management programme. Integrated programmes like the Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission of HIV (PMTCT) and the National Malaria Control Programme have streamlined the process of screening pregnant women for SCD by utilizing a single sample for all these tests. It is crucial that the integration of SCD screening leverages existing integrated programmes. However, certain interventions such as free childbirth in pilot areas led to the displacement of some women with the HbS who were screened in HCFs where childbirth was not free. These women sought childbirth services in HCFs where it was free but not covered by our study, resulting in their newborns missing screening. To address these issues, it is vital to implement strategic methodologies, as suggested by Isa et al. [23], to enhance healthcare services for SCD patients. This would involve establishing robust follow-up systems to ensure mothers and infants remain in the healthcare continuum, guaranteeing that those diagnosed with SCD or carrying the sickle cell trait receive the necessary care and management.

The integration of SCD screening into ANC services provides a unique opportunity to detect and manage SCD at the earliest possible stages. The importance of early detection and comprehensive management of children with SCD cannot be overstated, as it has been shown to significantly improve health outcomes. For example, emerging evidence suggests that early diagnosis and effective care can lead to significant reductions in mortality. Uyoge et al. [11] showed lower mortality in children with SCD attending a specialized clinic compared to those without early care. In addition, studies by Katamea et al. [6, 24] highlight the critical need to reverse the worrying trend of premature deaths in SSA by instituting and intensifying newborn screening programmes for SCD. These findings echo the data collected in our pilot study and underline the urgent need for such initiatives. However, while the accuracy of screening tools such as HemoTypeSC™ and Gazelle® is well established and family acceptance is generally high, the sustainability and large-scale implementation of SCD newborn screening for face significant challenges. The primary barriers lie in the availability of human resources and the financial investment required to ensure widespread coverage and long-term viability. Effective implementation necessitates a well-structured healthcare workforce, adequate training, and logistical coordination to facilitate sample collection, diagnosis, and follow-up care. Additionally, sustainable funding mechanisms must be in place to support the procurement of test kits, laboratory infrastructure, and workforce remuneration. Addressing these challenges requires strong governmental leadership and strategic partnerships with international organizations and non-governmental entities. Similar to the PMTCT programme [25], integrating SCD newborn screening into national health strategies and securing dedicated financial resources could enhance its feasibility and sustainability. Without such commitments, scaling up these programmes remains uncertain, potentially limiting their impact in reducing SCD-related childhood mortality in SSA. Furthermore, ensuring continuous follow-up of mother–infant pairs within SCD newborn screening remains a critical yet challenging aspect. Innovative and efficient strategies are needed to enhance retention and long-term engagement of families, ensuring that infants diagnosed with SCD receive timely and appropriate care. Strengthening community-based follow-up systems and leveraging digital health solutions could be key in improving adherence and reducing loss to follow-up.SCD screening results revealed that 4,119 (91.61%) of mothers were HbAA, 367 (8.16%) were HbAS, and 9 (0.20%) were HbSS. A Nigerian study of a series of 35,976 pregnant women reported that 28,815 (80.09%) were HbAA, 7 109 (19.77%) were HbAS, and 52 (0.14%) were HbSS [26]. In Italy, a study that screened 993 pregnant women found that 179 (18.02%) were HbAS, and one (0.1%) was HbSS [27]. Early detection is crucial as it enables immediate and appropriate medical intervention.

The prevalence of the different Hb genotypes among the 77 newborns at risk (66.23% HbAA, 29.87% HbAS, 3.90% HbSS) highlights the distribution of SCD in our study. In Lubumbashi (DRC), in a series of 538 newborns, Katamea et al. [6] found that 5.01% of newborns were HbSS, 26.21% were HbAS and 0.19% HbAC. In Italy, out of 1,011 at-risk newborns screened, Lodi et al. [27] found that 4 (0.4%) were HbSS and 90 (8.9%) were HbAS. Our results underscore the importance of a robust monitoring system that can identify at-risk newborns early in life, enabling healthcare providers to initiate early interventions. The implications of these interventions are profound, as they can prevent or mitigate serious complications commonly associated with SCD, leading to better health outcomes for the infants in question [16]. It is important to consider the social and health implications for babies born to mothers with SCD, especially in terms of awareness. If the mothers were not informed about their status before pregnancy, it may have limited their access to proper ANC, impacting both maternal and infant health. Additionally, this lack of awareness about disease transmission could lead to emotional, financial, and social challenges for the family. Educating women and providing genetic counseling during pregnancy is crucial to prevent these risks and reduce the social stigma associated with SCD. The study not only lays a solid foundation for future research but also has important implications for policy development. It underscores the critical need to integrate newborn screening for SCD into routine ANC services or immunization programmes as a key strategy to combat genetic diseases. However, given the significant loss to follow-up observed in the study, it is essential to take a more cautious approach when assessing the feasibility of screening. Additionally, the general lack of knowledge among HCWs about SCD and the limited availability of diagnostic and treatment resources, as observed in Bukavu [28], highlight the necessity of improving HCW training and equipping healthcare facilities to optimize the management of SCD patients. Future research should adopt an implementation science approach, specifically qualitative research, to explore the reasons behind this high attrition rate and identify factors that hinder sustained participation in follow-up care. This would provide valuable insights into improving screening processes and follow-up practices. Additionally, it would be beneficial to study the long-term outcomes for children diagnosed with SCD through newborn screening programmes and develop strategies to support their health and well-being throughout their lives [16].

The infrastructure and resources required for confirmatory testing using electrophoresis represent a major challenge for the large-scale implementation of newborn screening for SCD. In many low-resource settings, access to electrophoresis remains limited due to the high cost of equipment, reagents, and specialized personnel required for its operation [28]. The need for a centralized laboratory capable of performing confirmatory tests adds logistical constraints, including sample transportation, storage conditions, and turnaround time for results. These barriers can delay definitive diagnosis and initiation of early interventions, thereby reducing the effectiveness of screening programmes. Integrating SCD newborn screening into existing routine healthcare services, such as ANC services and immunization programmes, may help optimize available resources and improve feasibility. However, addressing the infrastructural and financial limitations of confirmatory testing will require strategic investments, governmental commitment, and support from international partners to ensure sustainable and widespread implementation.

The study has several important limitations and strengths. Among the limitations, the sample size and duration of the study were relatively small, which limits the generalizability of the results to the entire population of Bukavu city or the whole of the DRC. In addition, the loss of follow-up of mothers carrying the sickle cell trait was notable, with only 77 mothers followed through to delivery, indicating logistical challenges and socio-cultural barriers. The infrastructure and resources required to confirm results by electrophoresis were also a significant barrier, limiting large-scale implementation. Finally, although the acceptance rate was high, the study did not thoroughly evaluate the effectiveness of awareness campaigns and the impact of education on mothers’ perceptions of SCD.

Despite these limitations, the study has several notable strengths. The high acceptance rate of 70.77% shows a strong willingness to participate on the part of mothers, which is encouraging for public health interventions. The early identification of newborns with SCD and carriers of the trait demonstrates the feasibility of SCD newborn screening, which is crucial for early and appropriate interventions. By demonstrating this feasibility, the study paves the way for wider adoption of SCD newborn screening in the DRC, which could significantly improve health outcomes and reduce morbidity and mortality associated with SCD. The rigorous methodology employed, with the use of HemoTypeSC™ point-of-care testing and confirmation by Gazelle® electrophoresis, reinforces the reliability of the results. Finally, this study provides valuable data and practical lessons for future research and public health policy development, highlighting the challenges and success factors for successful implementation of SCD newborn screening.

Conclusion

This pilot study demonstrates that the integration of SCD newborn screening into primary health care in Bukavu, DRC, is not only feasible but also effective in the early identification of affected infants. The high acceptance rate among mothers and the results obtained show great potential for reducing the serious complications associated with SCD and improving the quality of life of affected children. The challenges encountered, such as loss to follow-up and infrastructural limitations, underline the need to strengthen the capacity of local health systems and raise community awareness. The lessons learned from this study provide a solid basis for scaling up this initiative, thereby helping to mitigate the impact of SCD on maternal and child health in the DRC. The success of this programme could serve as a model for other regions in SSA, where SCD represents a major public health challenge.
