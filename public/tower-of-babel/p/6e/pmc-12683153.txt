Microbiome and metabolome changes in laying hens induced by dietary epsilon-polylysine
Authors: Xiaolong Zhou, Han Wang, Mengqi Liu, Feifei Yan, Xue Du, Songbai Yang, Ayong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpsilon-polylysine (ε-PL) is a natural antimicrobial peptide that has been broadly applied in the food industry as a preservative. The effects of ε-PL on the intestinal microbiota, animal metabolism, and its potential as a "new feed additive" require further exploration. In this study, ninety Hy-Line Brown laying hens were randomly divided into three groups. Egg-laying performance, egg quality, and liver parameters were measured; expression of gut barrier, immunity, and ferroptosis related genes in each intestinal segment were detected; microbiome and metabolomic analyses were performed. The results demonstrated that dietary ε-PL supplementation significantly increased average egg weight and egg quality (P < 0.05). It also markedly, ε-PL supplementation significantly increased the villus height to crypt depth ratio in the duodenum and ileum (P < 0.01), significantly reduced the mRNA levels of interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), and elevated the mRNA levels of interleukin-10 (IL-10) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in the intestine (P < 0.05). Moreover, ε-PL inhibited intestinal mucosal ferroptosis (P < 0.05) and mitigated severe fatty liver development, as evidenced by a significantly reduction in hepatic malondialdehyde (MDA) content (P < 0.01). Microbiota analysis revealed that ε-PL supplementation significantly increased the abundance of Lactobacillus and Saccharimonadales; while decreasing the abundance of Anaerotruncus and Slackia in the cecal microbiota. Additionally, ε-PL supplementation enhanced the amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, the digestive system, arginine biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, bile secretion, and steroid hormone biosynthesis in the intestine, which might contribute to improved egg quality and intestinal absorptive capacity, as well as the inhibition of fatty liver formation. In summary, dietary ε-PL supplementation exerts multiple beneficial effects in laying hens, including enhancing egg quality, alleviate inflammation, boosting antioxidant capacity, improving intestinal morphology, modulating gut microbiota, and protecting liver health.Keywords: ε-polylysine, Gut microflora, Laying hens, Metabolomics, Intestinal health

Introduction

The application of antibiotics in animal production has played a pivotal role in improving in animal health, growth rates, and feed efficiency, particularly in intensive livestock systems, the prophylactic and sub-therapeutic use of antibiotics has long been practiced to maintain productivity and economic viability. However, uncontrolled antibiotics usage has led to increasingly prominent drawbacks. The problems caused by the extensive use of antibiotics in animal agriculture have garnered more attention globally. Growing evidence suggests that this practice has unintended consequences, including the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a major global public health concern. Pathogens resistant to multiple antibiotics have been detected not only in animals but also in the human population and the environment, forming a dangerous cycle of resistance transfer across ecosystems. In response, regulatory frameworks have been tightened in many regions. The European Union banned the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in 2006, and similar restrictions have been implemented in China and the United States in recent years.

These conditions have spurred an urgent search for alternative feed additives that can maintain animal performance and health without contributing to AMR. Previous studies had shown that increased intestinal absorption correlates with the eggshell thickness, being metabolically safe, non-residual, environmentally friendly, and economically viable. In this context, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have garnered significant attention. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are small, alkaline polypeptide (2 to 7 kDa, 20 to 60 amino acid) with potent antibacterial activity. Most AMPs exhibit characteristics of strong alkalinity, heat stability, and a broad spectrum of antibacterial effects. Over 300 different antimicrobial peptides have been identified and can be roughly divided into four main classes: defensins rich in Cys residues, magainins rich in Pro residues, melittins rich in Gly residues, and cecropins (bactericidal peptides). AMPs are evolutionarily conserved molecules found in virtually all living organisms, serving as a critical component of the innate immune defense. Their mechanisms of action typically include disruption of microbial membranes, inhibition of intracellular synthesis pathways, and modulation of host immunity responses. These modes action differ significantly from those of traditional antibiotics and thus reduce the likelihood of resistance development. Compared with antibiotics, antimicrobial peptides have many advantages, such as wide antibacterial spectrum, high antibacterial activity, low tendency to produce drug resistance, and good stability (Joerger, 2003; Parisien et al., 2008).

In addition to antibacterial effects, AMPs exhibit antiviral activity against influenza virus, herpes virus, hepatitis B virus through mechanisms including direct virion binding and disruption (Chia, et al., 2011), which showed that antimicrobial peptides could adhere to nervous necrosis virus and dissolve it; inhibition of viral gene expression, for example, day-old sericin antimicrobial peptides could inhibit the gene expression of HIV-1 (Wachinger, et al., 1998); and interference with viral entry processes. Among the various AMPs identified, ε-PL is an AMP consisting of 25-35 L-lysine residues, forming an amide bond between the ε-amino and α-carboxyl groups of the amino acid. Since l-lysine is one of the important raw materials for the synthesis of animal and plant proteins, ε-PL is non-toxic and widely used as a food-grade preservative (Bi, et al., 2016; Chang, et al., 2010; Mandal, et al., 2018). First identified in alkaloid-producing Actinomycetes by Shima and Sakai in in soil, ε-PL demonstrates potent antimicrobial activity against bacteria, yeast, fungi, and viruses, alongside stability across a wide pH range and resistance to high temperatures (120°C for > 20 min) (Chen, et al., 2018; Li, et al., 2014; Shih, et al., 2006). The ε-PL is metabolized to lysine in the body; enhancing its safety profile, and has been recognized as a safe food preservative by the U.S. FDA (FDA-2004). Therefore, ε-PL is a nutritive bacteriostatic agent, safer than other chemical preservatives, and its acute oral toxicity is 5 g / kg. China has gradually replaced the traditional preservative with ε-PL in baked goods and cooked meat products after 2010 (GB2760-2014).

The gastrointestinal tract (GIT) has emerged as a central focus in animal nutrition. The gut is not only responsible for digesting and absorbing nutrients but also acts as a complex and dynamic ecosystem involving trillions of microorganisms. The gut microbiota contributes significantly to the development of the immune system, metabolism of indigestible compounds, and production of key metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acids, and vitamins. Disruption of this microbial balance (dysbiosis) can impair nutrient utilization, weaken immunity, and predispose animals to disease, ultimately reduce performance. In poultry production, laying hens represent a critical group where gut health directly influences egg production, quality, and the longevity of laying cycles. Maintaining intestinal integrity and microbial balance is particularly vital during the laying period when hens face metabolic stress and high nutrient demands.

Although ε-PL has been studied in the context of food preservation and pathogen inhibition, its application in poultry feed, especially in relation to gut health, egg performance, and microbial ecology, remains poorly characterized. Additionally, the effects of ε-PL on host metabolism and markers such as liver function and oxidative stress are not well understood. As liver health is central to nutrient metabolism and detoxification in poultry, enhancing its function could improve overall physiological resilience. Furthermore, advances in metabolomics and 16S rRNA gene sequencing now permit comprehensive profile of microbial and metabolic response to dietary interventions, offering novel insights into host-microbe interactions. Therefore, this study aims to investigate how ε-PL influences the composition and diversity of gut microbes and metabolites, which are crucial for nutrient absorption and overall health. ε-PL was added to a wheat-soybean meal diet to examine its effects on intestinal morphology, egg quality, laying performance, liver damage, gut microbes, and metabolites of laying hens during the laying period. By assessing the overall efficiency of nutrient utilization with ε-PL supplementation, this research seeks to provide practical recommendations for optimizing feed formulations in commercial poultry production while reducing reliance on antibiotics.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

In this research, all experimental procedures involving animal experiments were carried out in accordance with the Measures for the Administration of Experimental Animals of Zhejiang Province (approved by the People's Government of Zhejiang Province in 2009, promulgated by Order No. 263). All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Animal Protection and Use Committee of Zhejiang A&F University to ensure compliance with international animal welfare guidelines (Ethical license number: ZAFUAC2023034).

Materials and experimental design

The ε-PL (99 % purity) used in this study was provided by Zhejiang Yinxiang Bioengineering Co., LTD (Taizhou, Zhejiang, China). The physical form of ε-PL is white powder, easily soluble in water and prone to absorbing moisture. The feeding experiment was conducted at Hangzhou Linan Daqing Poultry Co., LTD (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). Healthy 21-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens were selected and divided into three treatment groups with 30 hens in each group. Each hen in an enriched cage had 750 square centimeters of space and three inches of feeder space; the lip of the feeder was level with the bird's back height (van Staaveren, et al., 2018). During the experiment, all laying hens were allowed to eat and drink freely, daily fresh and clean water was essential-never let hens go without water for more than 12 hours. The hen house was equipped with an automatic lighting system that provided 16 h of light daily, and the chicken coop was equipped with a ventilation system. There was a chicken manure collection and transportation belt beneath the chicken cages. The manure is regularly removed to maintain the air quality inside the coop. Hens in the control group were fed a basal diet, while the experimental groups were fed the basal diet supplemented with 0.01 % and 0.05 % ε-PL, respectively. The hen house also utilized an automatic control system to regulate temperature, humidity, and ventilation.

During the study, hens for each treatment were divided into 6 replicates, with each replicate consisting of 5 hens fed in one cage. Hens in the pre-laying period were fed a diet of corn-soybean meal and then switched to wheat-soybean meal for 3 weeks. Starting from 24 weeks of age, the hens were caged in groups under the same feeding and management conditions. Egg quality was measured regularly during feeding. At 36 weeks of age, the hens were fasted for 12 h before being slaughtered. The fasting and slaughter were carried out in accordance with animal welfare standards approved by the Animal Protection and Use Committee of Zhejiang A&F University. One hen from each cage was slaughtered for the samples; we selected two extra birds for fatty liver, microbiome, and metabolome experiments. Tissues and organs were collected, and then stored at −80°C.

According to China's Specification for Healthy Poultry Production (GB/T 32148-2015), the basic diet was prepared, and the dry diet composition and nutritional composition are shown in Table 1. The contents of crude protein, crude fat, calcium, phosphorus, and amino acids in the feed were determined in accordance with Chinese standards GB/T 6432-2018, GB/T 6433-2006, GB/T 6436-2018, GB/T 6437-2018, and GB/T 18246-2019, respectively. The metabolizable energy was then calculated according to the calculation method of feed metabolizable energy in the China Feed Database (https://chinafeeddata.org.cn/).

Analysis of hen production

The numbers of eggs laid per day from each cage were recorded; the feed intake per group per week was calculated. Additionally, the daily laying rate, average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed-to-egg ratio were calculated. Daily laying rate (%) = total number of eggs produced / (number of test chickens × laying days) × 100 %. Average daily feed intake (g) = {(feed quantity - feed surplus) / number of test chickens} / laying days. Feed-to-egg ratio = total feed intake (g) / total egg weight (g).

Egg quality determination

Laying hens were fed wheat-soybean diet for 8 weeks and 12 weeks; twenty eggs were randomly selected from each treatment group for egg quality testing. The automatic egg quality tester (ORKA EA-01, Israel) was used to determine egg weight, albumen height, and Haugh unit. The eggshell thickness tester (ORKA ESTG-01, Israel) was used to measure the thickness of the blunt, tip, and middle end of the eggshell, and their average values were calculated. The eggshell strength tester (ORKA EFR-01, Israel) was used to determine the eggshell strength (kg/cm2). An electronic balance was used to determine yolk weight and calculate the yolk ratio (Average egg weight (g) = total egg weight / total egg production; yolk ratio = yolk weight / egg weight × 100 %).

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis

Primers for TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, GCLC, and other genes were designed using the Primer 6 software. Primers for glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) were used as an internal control. Total RNA was extracted from the samples using the TRIzol® Reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription of total RNA (1 μg) was performed using a RevertAid™ RT Reagent Kit (RR036A, Takara) in a 10 μL reaction volume, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression levels were quantified using SYBR Green-based quantitative PCR (qPCR), normalized to GAPDH, and calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Primer information for the real-time quantitative PCR is available in the supplemental information (Table S1).

Microbial diversity analysis

Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from cecal contents using the PF Mag-Bind Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Georgia, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The hypervariable region V3-V4 of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primer pairs 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) by an ABI GeneAmp® 9700 PCR thermocycler (ABI, CA, USA). The PCR amplification was performed, and all samples were amplified in triplicate. The PCR product was extracted from 2 % agarose gel and purified, then quantified using a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, USA). Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and paired-end sequenced on an Illumina PE300 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) according to the standard protocols by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sequences were processed using QIIME2, and taxonomic classification was assigned based on the SILVA database. The raw sequencing reads were deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (Accession Number: PRJNA1095718).

Bioinformatic analysis of the gut microbiota was carried out using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Based on the ASVs information, rarefaction curves and alpha diversity indices including observed ASVs, Chao1 richness, Shannon index, and Good’s coverage were calculated with Mothur v1.30.2. The similarity among the microbial communities in different samples was based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity using the Vegan v2.4.3 package. The PERMANOVA test was used to assess the percentage of variation explained by the treatment along with its statistical significance. The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/LEfSe) was performed to identify the significantly abundant taxa (phylum to genera) of bacteria among the different groups (LDA score > 2, P < 0.05). Since there is a multicollinearity problem among the clinical parameters, the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each variable was estimated using the VIF function in the car package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/car/car.pdf). The distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) was performed using the Vegan v2.4.3 package to investigate the effect of clinical parameters on gut bacterial community structure. Forward selection was based on Monte Carlo permutation tests (permutations = 9999). Values of the x- and y-axes and the length of the corresponding arrows represented the importance of each clinical parameter in explaining the distribution of taxon across communities. Linear regression analysis was applied to determine the association between major clinical parameters identified by db-RDA analysis and microbial alpha diversity indices. The co-occurrence networks were constructed to explore the internal community relationships across the samples. A correlation between two nodes was considered to be statistically robust if the Spearman’s correlation coefficient was over 0.6 or less than −0.6, and the P-value less than 0.05.

Metabolome profiling analysis

Cecal content samples were collected once the chickens were slaughtered; at least two faecal particles were placed in a sterile conical tube and immediately frozen at −80 °C for further analysis. Cecal contents (50 mg) were added to a 2 mL centrifuge tube and a 6 mm diameter grinding bead was added. 400 μL of extraction solution was added (methanol: water = 4:1 (v: v) containing 0.02 mg/mL of internal standard l-2-chlorophenylalanine for metabolite extraction. Samples were ground by the Wonbio-96c (Shanghai Wanbo Biotechnology co., Ltd) frozen tissue grinder for 6 min (−10°C, 50 Hz), followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5°C, 40 kHz). The samples were left at −20°C for 30 min, centrifuged for 15 min (4°C, 13,000 g), and the supernatant was transferred to the injection vial for LC-MS/MS analysis. The pretreatment of LC/MS raw data was performed by Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) software; at the same time, the metabolites were identified by searching databases, and the main databases were the HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/), Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/) and Majorbio Database. The data matrix obtained by searching databases was uploaded to the Majorbio cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com) for data analysis. Firstly, the data matrix was pre-processed, as follows: at least 80 % of the metabolic features detected in any set of samples were retained. After filtering, for specific samples with metabolite levels below the lower limit of quantification, the minimum metabolite value was estimated and each metabolic signature was normalized to the sum. To reduce the errors caused by sample preparation and instrument instability, the response intensities of the sample mass spectrometry peaks were normalized using the sum normalization method to obtain the normalized data matrix. Meanwhile, the variables of QC samples with relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30 % were excluded and log10-transformed to obtain the final data matrix for subsequent analysis. Then, the R package “ropls” (Version 1.6.2) was used to perform principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal least partial squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and 7-cycle interactive validation was used to evaluate the stability of the model. The metabolites with VIP>1, p < 0.05 were determined as significantly different metabolites based on the variable importance in projection (VIP) obtained by the OPLS-DA model and the p-value generated by student’s t-test. Differential metabolites between two groups were mapped into their biochemical pathways through metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis based on KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). Python package “scipy.stats” (https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/) was used to perform enrichment analysis to obtain the most relevant biological pathways for experimental treatments.

Intestinal morphology

The intestinal tissues were fixed by 4 % paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and paraffin-embedded according to standard procedures; they were cut into 5 μm thick sections, and then the samples were stained with hematoxylin-eosin. Samples from each group were observed under a Nikon microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and the villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured using NIS-Elements microscope imaging software (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Villus height and crypt depth were measured using ImageJ software from five well-oriented villi and crypts per section. The ratio of villus height to crypt depth was calculated using the formula: VCR = villus height / crypt depth. The average value obtained was taken as the final measurement.

Oil Red O (ORO) Staining of liver

Liver samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for at least 2 h at room temperature or overnight at 4°C. The samples were washed 3 times with PBS. Next, the samples were permeabilized in 0.1 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 5-10 min at room temperature and washed again three times with PBS. To block non-specific binding sites, the samples were immersed in 1 % BSA for 1 h at room temperature. ORO powder was dissolved in isopropanol to make a working solution of desired concentration (0.5 % in isopropanol). The samples were stained by ORO for 10-30 min at room temperature. They were washed three times with distilled water. For nuclear staining, the samples were stained with hematoxylin for 1-2 min at room temperature and then washed three times with distilled water. The stained samples were observed using NIS-Elements microscope imaging software (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistics

SPSS 20.0 (ver. 20.0, SPSS Inc, USA) software was used for the statistical analysis of experimental data. The significant difference between the groups was analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The mathematical model of ANOVA: Yij=μ+τi+ϵij [Yij is the j-th observation in the i th group (for i = 1,2,…, k groups and j = 1,2,…, nij=1,2,…, ni observations per group), μ is the overall mean effect (common to all groups), τi is the treatment effect specific to group i (with the constraint ∑ki=1 τi=0 so the effects sum to zero), ϵij is the random error term, assumed to be independently and identically distributed normal variables with mean zero and variance σ2, i.e., ϵij-N(0,σ2)]. Data were expressed as mean ± SEM and P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference. The correlation between the differential microbes and differential metabolites was determined by Spearman’s correlation coefficient.

Results

Effects of ε-PL on egg quality and laying performance of laying hens

In this study, diets supplemented with ε-PL had no effect on the egg production, daily feed intake, or feed conversion ratio of laying hens (Table S2). However, diets supplemented with both 0.01 % ε-PL and 0.05 % ε-PL significantly increased the yolk percentage at week 12 (P < 0.001). The 0.05 % ε-PL supplementation group also exhibited significantly higher eggshell thickness (P = 0.039), albumen height (P = 0.02), average egg weight (P = 0.017), and Haugh unit (P = 0.017) (a measure of egg freshness) at week 12 compared to the control group. In contrast, the addition of ε-PL had no influence on eggshell strength (Table S3).

Effects of ε-PL on morphology of intestines and immune-related genes

Morphological analysis of the ileum, jejunum, and duodenum in laying hens is presented in Figure 1A and Table S4. Dietary supplementation with 0.01 % ε-PL significantly increased the VH (P = 0.005) and decreased the CD in the duodenum (P < 0.001), but had no significant impact on VH in the jejunum or ileum. On the other hand, the addition of 0.05 % ε-PL significantly decreased the CD in the duodenum compared to the other groups (P < 0.001). The addition of 0.05 % ε-PL also significantly increased the VH in the ileum compared to the other groups (P = 0.013). Supplementation with 0.01 % and 0.05 % ε-PL significantly increased the VCR in the duodenum (P < 0.001) and ileum (P = 0.003), but had no effect on the VCR in the jejunum.

We further detected the mRNA levels of immune-related genes in different intestinal segments. In the duodenum, the results indicated that the mRNA levels of IL-6 were significantly decreased in the ε-PL 0.01 % and 0.05 % groups compared to the control (P = 0.011), while the mRNA levels of IL-10 (P = 0.063) and TNF-α (P = 0.005) were significantly increased in the ε-PL 0.01 % group compared to the control. However, the gene IL-1β showed no changes among the three groups. In the jejunum and ileum, the mRNA levels of IL-6 (jejunum P < 0.001, ileum P = 0.003) were also significantly decreased in the ε-PL 0.01 % and 0.05 % groups compared to the control, and the mRNA levels of IL-1β (jejunum P < 0.001, ileum P = 0.001) were significantly decreased in the ε-PL 0.05 % group compared to the control. In the jejunum, the mRNA level of TNF-α was significantly increased in ε-PL 0.01 % group compared to the other groups (P = 0.04). In the ileum, the mRNA levels of IL-10 and TNF-α showed no significant changes (Table S5). Ferroptosis is one type of regulated cell death (RCD) characterized by iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxidation. Inhibiting ferroptosis has an anti-inflammatory effect and can attenuate intestinal injury in IBD and I/R injury (Xu, et al., 2021). In this study, we found that ε-PL addition significantly increased the mRNA level of GCLC (P = 0.002) and NQO1 (P = 0.008) in the ileum, but significantly decreased the mRNA level of NCOA4 in the duodenum (P = 0.048) and ileum (P = 0.032) (Table S6). Intestinal barrier-related genes were also evaluated by RT-PCR, and ε-PL addition significantly elevated the mRNA levels of the intestinal barrier-related genes such as OCLN (P = 0.038), ZO-1 (P < 0.001), ZO-2 (P < 0.001), and CLDN1 (P = 0.071) in the duodenum, OCLN (P = 0.029), ZO-2 (P = 0.007), and CLDN2 (P = 0.004) in the jejunum, and ZO-2 (P = 0.026), CLDN1 (P = 0.09), and CLDN2 (P = 0.074) in the ileum (Table S7).

The effect of ε-PL on liver morphology and antioxidant index

Histological analysis showed that the incidence of severe fatty livers was markedly higher in the control group compared with ε-PL addition groups. Oil red O and hematoxylin staining were performed, and the results indicated that the ratio of fatty liver (100 %) in the control group was higher than that in the other groups (0.01 % group: 50 %, 0.05 % group: 75 %); the ratio of severe fatty liver was 75 % in the control group and 25 % in the other groups. The effect of inhibiting fatty liver was better in the ε-PL 0.01 % group compared to the ε-PL 0.05 % group (Figure 1B and Table S8). We also detected the antioxidant index of the liver; the results indicated that ε-PL addition significantly increased the GSH-Px levels compared to the control group (P < 0.001) and significantly decreased the MDA levels compared to the control group (P < 0.001). However, ε-PL addition had no effect on the SOD levels in the liver (Table S9).

Gut microbial composition and function

To investigate the influence of ε-PL on the composition of the gut flora of Hy-Line Brown laying hens, 16S rRNA sequencing was performed. A total of 1,867,900 high-quality sequences were obtained and classified into 6,497 Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) based on 97 % similarity (Table S10). The Venn diagram presents the number of common and unique species across multiple samples. The number of unique ASVs in the ε-PL 0 %, ε-PL 0.01 %, and ε-PL 0.05 % groups was 2,662, 591, and 1,935, respectively (Figure 2A). The ACE index, Chao index, and Sobs index were used to evaluate the alpha diversity of cecum content. The results indicated that adding 0.01 % ε-PL to the diet significantly decreased the ACE index, Chao index, and Sobs index. Adding 0.05 % ε-PL to the diet had no significant influence on the alpha diversity of cecum content (Figure 2B, C, and D).

Venn diagram illustrating the number of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) shared and unique across the groups (N = 8). (B) Ace index. (C) Chao index. (D) Sobs index.

At the phylum level, the dominant differential phyla in the cecum content were Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Actinobacteriota, and Patescibacteria, accounting for about 95 % of the total cecum bacteria. Supplementation with 0.01 % and 0.05 % ε-PL significantly increased the proportion of Patescibacteria in the cecum content compared to the control group. Patescibacteria can regulate plant and animal immune systems, build antiviral and antimicrobial resistance, suppress odors, and remove toxic substances. It can also improve the survival ability of plants and animals (Figure 3A). At the genus level, the dominant differential genera in the cecum content were Bacteroides and Bacteroidales, followed by Rikenellaceae-RC9-gut-group, Muribaculaceae, Bifidobacterium, etc. Supplementation with 0.01 % ε-PL significantly increased the proportion of Lactobacillus, Saccharimonadales, Clostridia_UCG-014, RF39, Bacilli, etc. (Figure 3B and Figure S1A). Lactobacillus is often considered a probiotic and regulates fermentation of carbohydrates, gut barrier integrity, immune modulation, and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production. Saccharimonadales is often involved in carbohydrate metabolism and modulation of gut pH. Clostridia_UCG-014 can regulate SCFA production, gut homeostasis, and interaction with pathogens. However, the abundance of Ruminococcaceae, Clostridia, and Anaerotruncus significantly decreased in response to 0.01 % ε-PL. Supplementation with 0.05 % ε-PL significantly increased the proportion of Prevotellaceae and Holdemania, while the proportion of Slackia and Eubacterium_hallii_group was significantly decreased in response to 0.05 % ε-PL (Figure 3B and Figure S1B). Slackia is a genus of gram-positive, anaerobic bacteria that belong to the phylum Actinobacteria. While not as widely studied as some other gut bacteria, Slackia species are still known to play important roles in gut health, particularly in terms of their metabolic functions and interactions within the microbiome. Discussion

AMPs have been widely applied in the livestock breeding industry. Previous studies have shown that AMPs can enhance intestinal health and immune function (Zhang, et al., 2021; Zhu, et al., 2022). The expression of avian β-defensins (AvBDs) in the isthmus and uterus increased in aged layers, suggesting AMPs may sustain mucosal defenses during extended production cycles (Elhamouly, et al., 2019). Supplementing late-laying hens’ diets with AMPs significantly increases laying rate and improves feed efficiency: in a 45-day trial with 72-week-old Hy-Line Brown hens, inclusion of 50 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg AMP elevated daily egg production and reduced feed/egg ratio compared with controls. They also found that cecal microbiota diversity and richness declined in AMP groups, with lower abundance of Verrucomicrobia and Cyanobacteria, indicating selective modulation of gut communities (Chen, et al., 2020). A previous study found that eggshell strength and yolk color were modestly improved in AMP-fed groups relative to antibiotic controls; Haugh unit, and albumen height were also increased by AMP treatment (Shastri, et al., 2025). Unhealthy hens often exhibit distinct phenotypes compared to normal, healthy birds, including differences in their intestinal microbiome, liver tissue, inflammatory factors, metabolites, and host gene expression. By evaluating a range of physiological and biochemical indices in the experimental hens, this study aimed to explore the beneficial effects of dietary supplementation with ε-PL. We utilized microbiome and metabolome analyses to gain deeper insights into how ε-PL influences the gut, liver, and overall health of the laying hens.

The villus length (VH) and crypt depth (CD) ratio in each intestinal segment of animals is a well-recognized indicator of intestinal health and functionality. A higher VH:CD ratio typically reflects enhanced nutrient absorption and mucosal maturation, as taller villi increase surface area for absorption, while shallower crypts indicate reduced epithelial turnover and better stability of the intestinal lining. Therefore, this ratio is widely used as a reliable histological parameter for assessing small intestinal integrity and efficiency. In this study, we found that the addition of ε-PL significantly improves the VH in the ileum and duodenum, as well as the VH:CD ratio in these segments. These results suggest that ε-PL may promote the digestion and absorption capabilities of the specific intestinal regions. The improved intestinal nutrient absorption can lead to fewer undigested and partially digested food particles reaching the liver through the portal circulation, thereby reducing the workload of the liver in processing these substances (Puzio, et al., 2021). Enhanced intestinal health and absorption can also improve the calcium utilization. This may have downstream physiological effects, such as improved eggshell formation. Previous studies have shown that increased intestinal absorption correlates with eggshell thickness and strength (Obianwuna, et al., 2022a, 2022b). Similarly, our findings align with previous reports revealing the VH:CD ratio as a key marker of gut health (Wilson, et al., 2018) and with evidence that optimizing intestinal morphology can improve systemic nutrient utilization.

Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 play central roles in the host's immune response to viral infections. They contribute to the disruption of intestinal tight junctions and increase mucosal permeability, ultimately leading to epithelial barrier dysfunction and intestinal inflammation. In contrast, IL-10 functions as a key anti-inflammatory mediator that suppresses excessive immune responses to pathogens and commensal microbiota, thereby preventing tissue damage and maintaining immunological homeostasis. TNF-α, although classically pro-inflammatory, exhibits a dual role in immune regulation, with evidence indicating both inflammation-promoting and tolerance-inducing effects, depending on the local immune context and its interaction with TNF-β (lymphotoxin α). In this study, we found that ε-PL supplementation significantly increased mRNA levels of TNF-α and IL-10 while significantly decreasing mRNA levels of IL-1β and IL-6 in the intestine. This result suggests that ε-PL may benefit intestinal health by modulating the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory pathways. For example, a shift in the cytokine profile may help preserve intestinal barrier integrity and reduce the risk of chronic inflammation. Excessive intestinal inflammation is known to increase epithelial permeability, allowing the translocation of microbial products and cytokines into the portal circulation. This can trigger immune activation in the liver, potentially leading to hepatic inflammation, fibrosis, or the development of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Our findings align with previous studies that have demonstrated the roles of IL-1β and IL-6 in disrupting tight junctions and promoting inflammation (Al-Sadi and Ma, 2007; Kaminsky, et al., 2021; Keyel, 2014; Villar-Fincheira, et al., 2021), as well as the protective, inflammation-modulating functions of IL-10 and TNF-α under certain conditions (Carlini, et al., 2023; Islam, et al., 2021; Assas, et al., 2017; Buhrmann, et al., 2013; van Loo and Bertrand, 2023). Increased intestinal inflammation can elevate intestinal permeability, leading to the translocation of microbial products and inflammatory factors to the liver via the portal vein (Maestri, et al., 2023; Pan, et al., 2020). Consequently, this can activate immune cells in the liver, potentially resulting in hepatic inflammation, fibrosis, or non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) (Pan, Wen, Kaminga and Liu, 2020).

Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, and it plays a critical role in maintaining intestinal mucosal health. Excessive ferroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells can lead to barrier dysfunction, increased inflammation, and heightened susceptibility to disease. Ferroptosis has been implicated in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis and other gastrointestinal disorders due to its contribution to epithelial cell death (Wu, et al., 2023). A study found that ferroptosis was more pronounced in aging D580 layers compared to peak-laying D280 hens (Yao, et al., 2023). This suggests that ferroptosis may influence the aging process in laying hens, potentially impacting their laying performance and overall health (Yao, Wang, Jiang, Guo and Li, 2023). Additionally, studies indicate that ferroptosis contributes to disrupting the intestinal mucosal barrier by promoting gut microbial dysbiosis, implying its role in intestinal health and disease progression (Zhou, et al., 2023). In our study, ε-PL supplementation significantly reduced the ferroptosis-promoting gene NCOA4 while significantly increasing the expression of anti-ferroptosis genes such as GCLC and NQO1 in the intestine of the laying hens. Intestinal ferroptosis can impact liver health through the gut-liver axis; increased intestinal ferroptosis can lead to higher iron accumulation and oxidative stress in the liver (Wu, et al., 2021). This may promote lipid peroxidation and elevate MDA and 4-HNE levels in the liver, potentially contributing to the development of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) (Cheng, et al., 2023; Ji, et al., 2023). Additionally, we also found that ε-PL addition significantly increased the mRNA levels of intestinal-barrier-related genes, which could help reduce the passage of undigested and partially digested food particles into the liver. These findings suggest that ε-PL may act as both an anti-inflammatory and anti-ferroptosis agent, enhancing the health of the intestine and liver in laying hens. Previous studies might not have considered ferroptosis as a pathway linking gut health to liver function. The observed reduction in hepatic MDA and increase GSH-Px levels could reflect novel anti-ferroptosis mechanisms unique to ε-PL.

Fatty Liver Hemorrhagic Syndrome (FLHS) is a common metabolic disorder in laying hens, characterized by excessive fat deposition in the liver and often resulting in sudden death. This condition is particularly prevalent in birds fed high-energy diets combined with restricted physical activity and can severely impair egg production. FLHS is primarily caused by a positive energy balance and is exacerbated by elevated estrogen levels, which together promote hepatic lipid accumulation (Trott, et al., 2014). Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) is a crucial enzyme in the liver that protects cells from oxidative damage. Malondialdehyde (MDA) is a marker of oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in the liver. The levels of MDA and GSH-Px are closely linked to fatty liver disease (Arroyave-Ospina, et al., 2021; Pan, et al., 2006). In our breeding experiment, a high rate of fatty liver was observed; this could be a typical finding for commercial egg-laying chicken farming. We considered that laying hens with severe fatty liver will be most likely to develop into FLHS and cause birds’ sudden death. In the present study, the results also indicated that ε-PL addition significantly increased the level of GSH-Px while decreasing the MDA level. Therefore, we speculated that ε-PL may suppress the development of fatty liver by regulating the levels of MDA and GSH-Px.

Gut microflora plays a crucial role in the health and production performance of hens (Abd El-Hack, et al., 2022; Reda, et al., 2021). The composition of the gut microbiota in livestock can be influenced by dietary changes and the time period (Lan, et al., 2021). In our present study, we observed higher beta diversity and a more distinct ASV classification in the ε-PL 0.01 % and 0.05 % groups, suggesting that ε-PL supplementation alters the cecum flora composition. The gut microflora can impact the hepatic immunity and inflammation through translocation of bacteria, bacterial products and metabolites through the intestinal barrier into the liver (Li, et al., 2023a). Additionally, research indicates that gut microflora can improve the eggshell thickness and quality. Multi-strain probiotics have been shown to improve egg quality parameters such as eggshell strength and albumen height (Li, Gao, Guo, He and Ma, 2023a; Macit, et al., 2021; Mikulski, et al., 2020). These findings support the hypothesis that dietary manipulation of the gut microbiota, including through agents such as ε-PL, may contribute to both improved intestinal health and productive performance in laying hens.

We further analyzed the composition and taxa in cecum flora. At the phylum level, the abundance of Patescibacteria was significantly increased following ε-PL supplementation. This phylum comprises a group of intestinal microorganisms that play multifunctional roles in host health. Additionally, they can degrade polysaccharides, which is critical for the host's energy harvest (Backhed, et al., 2005; Wang, et al., 2023). Patescibacteria are also known to regulate the immune system and enhance resistance to pathogenic microorganisms (Wang, Gallagher, Andrade, Liu, Humphreys, Turkarslan, Cutler, Arrieta-Ortiz, Li, Radey, McLean, Cong, Baker, Baliga, Peterson and Mougous, 2023). They produce short-chain fatty acids, such as butyric acid and acetic acid, which are beneficial for intestinal health (Backhed, et al., 2004). The observed enrichment of Patescibacteria following ε-PL supplementation suggests that ε-PL may contribute to gut health not only by reshaping the microbial community structure but also by enhancing host immunity and nutrient utilization through the promotion of beneficial taxa. Changes in gut microbiota can have broad implications for host physiology, including modulation of immune response (Hu, et al., 2018b; Zhang, et al., 2015), as well as digestion and metabolism (Rothschild, et al., 2018; Sonnenburg and Sonnenburg, 2014). To date, no study has specifically investigated the effects of ε-PL on digestibility in hens; previous research has shown that lysine deficiency or excess can affect nutrient digestibility in pigs (Elsbernd, et al., 2017; Wen, et al., 2017; Zeng, et al., 2013). At the genus level, ε-PL 0.01 % supplementation significantly increased the abundance of Lactobacillus followed by Saccharimonadales, Clostridia_UCG-014, RF39, Bacilli, Prevotellaceae, etc. Lactobacillus, a genus of probiotic bacteria, plays a crucial role in intestinal digestion. Species such as L. acidophilus help break down food and enhance nutrient absorption in the gut. Additionally, Lactobacillus spp. promotes mucus secretion, which helps strengthen the intestinal barrier. Lactobacilli produce lactic acid, which may inhibit colonization of harmful bacteria in the intestines (Dempsey and Corr, 2022). This study used advanced microbiome/metabolome profiling, which prior studies did not have employed. Discrepancies in microbiota findings, such as increased Patescibacteria and Lactobacillus, might reflect differences in sequencing depth, bioinformatics pipelines, or primer selection. Shifts in gut microbiota baseline composition over time (due to feed additives, or seasonal variations) could alter ε-PL’s efficacy. For instance, the prominence of Lactobacillus in this study might reflect a unique microbial community not present in earlier experiments.

Many studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus, a widely recognized probiotic, positively impacts egg quality and gut health (Hong, et al., 2021; Zhou, et al., 2022). Lactobacillus inhibits the growth of harmful bacteria while promoting the proliferation of beneficial ones, thus maintaining a balanced intestinal microbiome. Additionally, Lactobacillus produces beneficial substances, such as short-chain fatty acids, which support gut health (Hu, et al., 2018a; Yang, et al., 2018; Yin, et al., 2018). Dietary supplementation with Lactobacillus and Bacilli can improve intestinal barrier function and helps maintain immune homeostasis. These effects are likely linked to changes in the intestinal microbiome and metabolites (Chen, et al., 2023). For instance, Lactobacillus coryniformis subp. torquens T3 has been shown to reduce obesity in mice induced by high-fat diets, demonstrating its anti-obesity potential (Song, et al., 2021). In addition to maintaining intestinal health, Lactobacillus has been found to inhibit fatty acid absorption (Jang, et al., 2019), regulate lipid metabolism, and improve insulin sensitivity (Tang, et al., 2022). Some research indicates that Lactobacillus can reduce the liver enzyme levels in NAFLD patients and improve their liver function (Meroni, et al., 2019). Correlations between intestinal microbes and metabolites revealed that Lactobacillus was negatively correlated with Fagomine and 1,2-Cyclohexanedicarboxylic, and positively correlated with Lysyl-glycine. Fagomine, a polyhydroxylated alkaloid, has several important functions in the intestinal tract. It has been shown to stabilize populations of beneficial gut bacteria, such as those from the Bacteroidetes phylum, Bifidobacteriales order, and Prevotella genus (Hereu, et al., 2019). Fagomine can counteract the reduction in SCFA-producing bacteria and total SCFA levels caused by a high-fat diet, exhibiting a "fiber-like" action that promotes the growth of beneficial bacteria and improves gut microbial balance (Ramos-Romero, et al., 2022). Additionally, Fagomine has been found to reduce the excretion of Enterobacteriales (a group of potentially harmful bacteria) in rats fed a high-fat, high-sucrose diet, potentially preventing or mitigating the obesity associated with gut microbiota imbalance (Ramos-Romero, et al., 2014). Studies have shown that lysylglycine, a dipeptide containing the amino acids lysine and glycine, can be absorbed intact from the lumen of the intestine into the portal blood (Asatoor, et al., 1971). The absorption of lysylglycine is more rapid compared to the absorption of an equivalent mixture of free amino acids (Hellier, et al., 1972). Supplementing the diet with the lysylglycine dipeptide has been shown to positively affect growth, muscle proteins, and the expression of the PepT1 gene (involved in peptide transport) in juvenile yellow perch (Kwasek, et al., 2012).

Saccharimonadales can improve intestinal health in rats. Zhao et al. found that Saccharmonadaceae has a significant positive correlation with anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 and tight junction protein Occludin1 (Zhao, et al., 2020). Correlations between intestinal microbes and metabolites found that Lactobacillus was negatively correlated with 5-Hydroxy-6‑methoxy-1 h-indole-2-carboxylic acid, 2,8-Quinolinediol, and 20-oxo-leukotriene E4; positively correlated with Hydroxycotinine. 5-Hydroxy-6‑methoxy-1H-indole-2-carboxylic acid is an organic compound with the molecular formula C10H9NO4; the function of the compound in the intestinal tract is still unclear till now. 20-Oxo-leukotriene E4 is a metabolite of leukotriene E4, which is an inflammatory mediator. The concentration of 20-oxo-leukotriene E4 in the intestinal tract has been proposed as a potential marker of inflammation in conditions like inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in dogs (Hof, et al., 2012). In a study on growing pigs, the group supplemented with copper glycinate (Cu-Gly) had a lower concentration of 20-oxo-leukotriene E4 compared to the group supplemented with copper sulfate (CuSO4) (Lei, et al., 2023). The lower concentration of 20-oxo-leukotriene E4 in the intestinal tract was associated with higher levels of beneficial short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate and propionate, which are important for intestinal health. 2,8-Quinolinediol is a metabolite produced by the gut microbiome and can serve as a marker of gut microbial imbalances (Hubbard, et al., 2019; Zhu, et al., 2020). Hydroxycotinine can be absorbed and excreted through the intestinal tract, either as the free compound or as a glucuronide conjugate (Kanamori, et al., 2022); the function of the compound in intestinal health needs more studies. Taken together, Saccharimonadales is a beneficial bacterium to intestinal health.

Clostridia, a class of bacteria that includes species like Clostridium perfringens, play significant roles in various biological processes, both beneficial and harmful. Clostridia_UCG-014 belongs to Clostridiae, and UCG-014 has a significant negative correlation with liver injury in mice. The abundance of intestinal Clostridia_UCG-014 was lower in the PCOS cohort than in the healthy control cohort. The function of Clostridia_UCG-014 on hens is still unclear (Liu, et al., 2022; Zhu, et al., 2021). Clostridia_UCG-014, a specific type of bacteria, plays a significant role in the intestinal system by positively influencing gut health and maintaining the gut barrier function. Studies have shown that Clostridia_UCG-014 is associated with bacterial diversity and barrier function in healthy individuals, indicating its importance in gut homeostasis (Leibovitzh, et al., 2022). Furthermore, this bacterium has been reported to alleviate the accumulation of amino acids, bile acids, and indoles in the large intestine, highlighting its role in modulating intestinal metabolites and promoting gut health (Guo, et al., 2023). Additionally, Clostridia_UCG-014 has been linked to tryptophan metabolism in colitis mice, suggesting its potential involvement in improving gut barrier function and mediating protective effects against conditions like ulcerative colitis (Yang, et al., 2021). In this study, we found Clostridia_UCG-014 was negatively correlated with N—Carboxyethyl-gamma-aminobutyric acid, d-Digitoxose, 2-amino-3,7-dideoxy-D‑threo‑hept-6-ulosonate, 14-oxo-DoHE, N6-Acetyl-5S‑hydroxy-l-lysine, etc. and positively correlated with l-DOPA n-Butyl Ester, 4-Hydroxybenzaldehyde, TG (8:0/8:0/10:0), etc. N—Carboxyethyl-gamma-aminobutyric acid (also known as gamma-aminobutyric acid, or GABA); GABA supplementation has been shown to modulate intestinal immunity by inhibiting the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-18, IL-22) and promoting anti-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-10) (Chen, et al., 2019). GABA supplementation has been found to increase the concentrations of acetate, propionate, butyrate, and total SCFAs in the colonic and cecal contents of mice; GABA supplementation can decrease the pH values in the colonic and cecal contents, which is beneficial for maintaining a healthy intestinal environment (Xie, et al., 2017). d-Digitoxose can interact with the colon mucosa, which may be considered as putative preneoplastic lesions. This suggests that d-Digitoxose, as a component of digitalis glycosides, may have some influence on the intestinal mucosa and potentially contribute to the development of precancerous changes (Jiang, et al., 2010). N6-Acetyl-5S‑hydroxy-l-lysine is a modified form of the amino acid lysine; the acetylation and hydroxylation of the lysine residue appears to facilitate its intestinal absorption. 14-oxo-DoHE is an oxylipin, a class of bioactive lipid mediators derived from dietary polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). As an oxylipin, 14-oxo-DoHE is structurally related to other anti-inflammatory lipid mediators like resolvins and protectins (Gabbs, et al., 2015). TG(8:0/8:0/10:0) belongs to the class of triacylglycerols, which are glycerolipids containing three fatty acid chains; the key role of TG(8:0/8:0/10:0) in the intestinal tract is its direct absorption and metabolism as a medium-chain triacylglycerol without forming chylomicrons (Watanabe and Tsujino, 2022). 4-Hydroxybenzaldehyde has been shown to accelerate acute wound healing, including in the intestinal tract, through the activation of focal adhesion signaling in keratinocytes. This suggests 4-Hydroxybenzaldehyde may have beneficial effects on intestinal mucosal healing and repair. As a naturally occurring benzaldehyde compound, 4-Hydroxybenzaldehyde has been reported to have therapeutic effects in various diseases, including potential antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities (Kang, et al., 2017). The n‑butyl ester of l-DOPA can be absorbed more efficiently from the intestinal tract compared to l-DOPA itself. The ester modification improves the lipophilicity and permeability of l-DOPA, facilitating its absorption in the gastrointestinal environment, leading to enhanced bioavailability compared to the unmodified l-DOPA compound (Hoon, et al., 2017). In sum, the function of Clostridia_UCG-014 is complicated; the bacterium is negatively correlated with some metabolites such as N—Carboxyethyl-gamma-aminobutyric acid and 14-oxo-DoHE, that were beneficial to intestinal health, and positively correlated with 4-Hydroxybenzaldehyde, a compound that is also conducive to intestinal health.

Clostridium perfringens is also a well-known pathogen responsible for causing necrotic diseases in humans and animals (Takehara, et al., 2022; Zhang, et al., 2022a). This bacterium produces toxins and enzymes that can degrade mucin, affect enterocytes, and lead to conditions like necrotic enteritis (Villagrán-de la Mora, et al., 2020). Clostridium perfringens has been identified as a foodborne pathogen in broiler production, highlighting its role in food safety and public health (Villagrán-de la Mora, Macías-Rodríguez, Arratia-Quijada, Gonzalez-Torres, Nuño and Villarruel-López, 2020)

Anaerotruncus, a bacterial genus within the family Oscillospiraceae, is known for its presence in the human vaginal flora and gut microbiota (Yang, et al., 2017). One species within this genus, Anaerotruncus colihominis, has been identified in stool specimens and has been associated with bacteremia in clinical cases (Bianchimano, et al., 2022; Raimondi, et al., 2021). In this study, we found that ε-PL 0.05 % addition significantly decreased the ratio of Slackia in the intestinal flora. This may be beneficial to the intestinal health of laying hens. Slackia, a type of gut microbe found in chickens, plays a significant role in chicken growth and fat metabolism. Research indicates that Slackia is associated with fat deposition in chickens, with higher levels of Slackia promoting the storage of fat in animals. Studies have shown that Slackia's relative abundance negatively correlates with certain factors related to fat metabolism, such as HDL-C, CPT-1, and P-AMPK (Zhang, et al., 2022b). Additionally, genetically selected chickens with higher abundances of Slackia tend to store more fat (Wen, et al., 2021).

Slackia bacteria, such as Slackia exigua, have been isolated from human wounds and infections, indicating their potential pathogenic role in oral and extraoral infections. These bacteria have been linked to periodontitis and periapical infections, showcasing their involvement in infectious diseases (Kim, et al., 2010). In this study, we found that ε-PL 0.05 % addition significantly decreased the ratio of Slackia in the gut microbiota. In this study, we found the genus Slackia was positively correlated with asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA); ADMA is a metabolic by-product formed by the methylation of arginine residues in proteins, catalyzed by protein arginine methyltransferases (Avci, et al., 2018). Studies have found that plasma levels of ADMA and symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA) are elevated in patients with inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD). Arginine metabolism, including the production of ADMA, is involved in the pathogenesis of IBD and can affect intestinal epithelial cell restitution and regeneration (Li, et al., 2023b). The inhibition of Slackia by ε-PL may be beneficial to the health of laying hens.

In the present study, ε-polylysine (ε-PL) supplementation significantly increased the average egg weight (AEW) and yolk percentage, while having no significant effect on feed-to-egg ratio (FER) or daily feed intake (DFI). These results suggest that ε-PL may enhance nutrient partitioning toward egg production without altering overall feed efficiency. This effect is likely due to improved amino acid and lipid metabolism, potentially mediated by ε-PL’s influence on gut microbiota and intestinal absorption. In addition, ε-PL supplementation significantly improved eggshell thickness. Enhanced shell thickness is critical for reducing egg breakage during handling and transportation, thereby increasing the proportion of marketable eggs and improving overall egg quality (Kim, et al., 2022). The beneficial effect of ε-PL on eggshell quality may be related to improved calcium absorption or altered mineral metabolism, although further investigation is needed. Albumen quality was also improved, as indicated by increased albumen height and Haugh unit values in the ε-PL-supplemented groups. This may be attributed to the antimicrobial properties of ε-PL, which could help preserve the structural integrity and freshness of the egg white during egg formation and storage. Taken together, these findings support the potential of ε-PL as a functional feed additive to enhance egg quality in laying hens. However, discrepancies between our results and those of previous studies may stem from differences in basal diet composition (wheat and soybean meal) and genetic background of the hens used. These variables should be considered in future investigations to clarify the scope and consistency of ε-PL’s effects.

Taken together, ε-PL undergoes limited degradation in the gut, primarily through proteases, releasing l-lysine, as well as microbial cleavage into homopolymer subunits of varying length. Its primary function in the gut is an antimicrobial peptide rather than a lysine source. Previous research has shown that 94 % of the administered dose of ε-PL passed unabsorbed through the gastrointestinal tract and was eliminated in the feces of rats (Hiraki, et al., 2003). Dietary supplementation with 0.05 % ε-PL improved egg quality traits more than 0.01 % ε-PL, but both had similar effects on intestinal morphology and immune gene expression, and both significantly upregulated intestinal barrier genes. Obviously, 0.01 % ε-PL was more effective in reducing severe fatty liver and promoting beneficial gut microbes, including Lactobacillus and Clostridia_UCG-014, while reducing Anaerotruncus and Slackia. It also showed better metabolite regulation. ε-PL’s antimicrobial effect is linked to its interaction with bacterial membranes, though higher concentrations may reduce efficacy due to site saturation (Hyldgaard, et al., 2014). These findings highlight ε-PL as a promising antibiotic alternative in poultry.

Conclusion

Our results suggest that supplementation of ε-PL in diets may improve egg quality, but it does not appear to have a direct effect on laying performance. The increased expression of anti-inflammatory genes and enhanced antioxidant indices indicate potential health benefits. Additionally, the cecal microecological environment was positively influenced, with an increase in the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus. The modulation of amino acid and lipid metabolism, along with alterations in arginine biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, bile secretion, and steroid hormone biosynthesis, suggests possible pathways through which ε-PL affects egg quality and liver health. Further studies are needed to clarify the precise mechanisms through which ε-PL modulates metabolic processes and to evaluate its long-term impact on laying performance and overall hen health.
