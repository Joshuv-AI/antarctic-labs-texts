Changes in Smoking Status in Depressed Patients and the Risk of Dementia
Authors: Yoo Jin Jang, Hyewon Kim, Jin-Hyung Jung, Kyungdo Han, Hong Jin Jeon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Smoking is a known risk factor for dementia, but the effects of changes in smoking behavior after a depression diagnosis remain unclear.Methods:We conducted a nationwide cohort study of 1,290,530 individuals newly diagnosed with depression in South Korea between 2009 and 2012. Participants were categorized by smoking status before and after diagnosis. Incident dementia, including Alzheimer disease (AD) and vascular dementia (VD), was tracked through 2018. Adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using Cox models.Results:Continued smoking showed the highest dementia risk (HR 1.338 for all-cause; 1.323 for AD; and 1.524 for VD). Quitting reduced risk but remained higher than in persistent non-smokers. Middle age was a key risk period for AD. For VD, men had consistently higher risk, while women had increased risk only in the cessation group.Conclusions:Changes in smoking behavior after depression diagnosis influence dementia risk, underscoring the need for cessation strategies.

Dementia is an escalating public health challenge worldwide, significantly impacting patients, their families, and health care systems. As global populations age, the prevalence of dementia, particularly Alzheimer’s dementia (AD) and vascular dementia (VD), continues to rise (“Estimation of the global prevalence of dementia in 2019 and forecasted prevalence in 2050: an analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019,” 2022). Identifying modifiable risk factors for dementia has been a key focus of research (Baumgart et al., 2015), with smoking emerging as one of the most significant contributors. Smoking is not only a major risk factor for cardiovascular (Ishida et al., 2024) and cerebrovascular diseases (Pan et al., 2019) but is also linked to increased risks of dementia, including AD and VD (Durazzo et al., 2014; Johnson et al., 2021; Zhong et al., 2015).

Depression, a well-established risk factor for dementia (Fernández Fernández et al., 2024), frequently coexists with smoking, with individuals suffering from depression being more likely to smoke and develop tobacco use disorder (Fluharty et al., 2017). The severity of depressive symptoms is often linked to higher smoking levels (Jeon et al., 2020), and depression increases the likelihood of smoking initiation (Patton et al., 1998; Pratt and Brody, 2010). Despite the known relationship between smoking and dementia, most research has treated smoking as a static factor, without considering how changes in smoking behavior—such as quitting or starting smoking after a depression diagnosis—affect dementia risk (Baumgart et al., 2015). Given the increased risk of dementia among individuals with depression, it is crucial to understand how changes in smoking behavior could modify this risk. However, there remains limited evidence on whether smoking cessation after a depression diagnosis can effectively reduce the risk of developing dementia.

Therefore, the aims of this nationwide population-based cohort study are twofold: (1) to determine whether changes in smoking status before and after a depression diagnosis are associated with the incidence of dementia and its subtypes (AD and VD), and (2) to investigate whether these associations vary according to sex, age, and other clinical factors. By focusing on the dynamic aspect of smoking behavior, our study seeks to provide new insights into how smoking cessation could serve as a key intervention in reducing dementia risk among individuals with depression.

METHODS

Study Population

This study utilized data from the National Health Insurance Sharing Service (NHISS) database in South Korea (Shin et al., 2016), which encompasses ~97% of the population. The database incorporates claims data for hospital admissions, emergency room visits, outpatient visits, pharmaceutical services, and health screenings. Privacy was protected through anonymization of NHISS data, and the study was exempted from review by the institutional review board due to its retrospective nature.

From the 4,805,617 individuals diagnosed with depression between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2016, we included 2,258,232 who had undergone general health screening within 2 years before their depression diagnosis. Depression was identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes F32 and F33, as recorded by physicians. Out of these, 1,547,928 individuals also completed a health screening within 2 years following their depression diagnosis. We excluded 37,458 subjects with incomplete health screening data and 162,275 individuals younger than 40 at the time of diagnosis. In addition, to identify the first occurrence of dementia, we excluded 40,803 individuals who had previously received dementia diagnostic codes from January 1, 2002, to the onset of depression. Furthermore, to prevent “reverse causality,” we excluded 16,862 individuals diagnosed with dementia within 1 year of the second health screening day. The final analysis included 1,290,530 individuals, with their medical records reviewed for outcome occurrences until December 31, 2020 (Fig. 1).

Selection of eligible study subjects.

Changes in Smoking Status

Participants’ smoking status was assessed twice via a self-administered questionnaire during health screenings, which occurred before and after their depression diagnosis. At each health screening, participants identified their smoking status as never smoked, current smoker, or former smoker who had quit. Current and former smokers provided details on their smoking duration (in years) and quantity smoked (in packs).

Participants were categorized into four groups based on their smoking status across the two health screenings. The “Nonsmoker” group comprised individuals who had never smoked or had quit before the first health screening (within 2 years before the depression diagnosis) and continued to report as nonsmokers or maintained abstinence at the second health screening (within 2 years after the depression diagnosis). The “Initiated” group included those who were nonsmokers (never or former smokers) at the first screening but became current smokers by the second screening. The “Ceased” group consisted of individuals who were current smokers at the first screening but had quit by the second screening. The “Sustained” group comprised participants who remained current smokers at both health screenings.

Dementia

The primary outcome was the incidence of all-cause dementia, determined through the assignment of ICD-10 codes for dementia (F00–03, G30–31) with antidementia medication prescription (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, or memantine) during the follow-up period. The secondary outcomes included the dementia subtypes, categorized according to the initial ICD-10 code at the time of diagnosis: AD identified by codes F00 and G30, and VD by code F01.

Covariates

Low income was defined as being below 25% of the national average, calculated from health insurance payments, which are income-based in South Korea, and individuals receiving medical aid. Alcohol consumption was assessed via a self-administered questionnaire during the postdiagnosis health screening, classifying participants as “yes” or “no” based on whether their daily intake exceeded 0 g of pure alcohol. Regular physical activity was classified as performing moderate exercise for ≥5 days per week or vigorous exercise for ≥3 days per week. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using weight and height measurements, defining obesity as a BMI of ≥25 kg/m2 and abdominal obesity as a waist circumference of >90 cm for men and >85 cm for women. In addition, data on fasting glucose, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and total cholesterol were collected. Comorbid physical illnesses included diabetes mellitus (ICD-10 codes E11–E14 with medication claims or fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL), hypertension (ICD-10 codes I10–I15 with medication claims or blood pressure ≥140/90 mm Hg), and dyslipidemia (ICD-10 code E78 with medication claims or total cholesterol ≥240 mg/dL). Hearing loss was defined as abnormal results in the audiometric test during the second health examination or a previously diagnosed hearing impairment.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study population were summarized based on changes in smoking status before and after depression diagnosis. Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups employed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Cox proportional hazards regression models were utilized to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for the relationship between changes in smoking status and the risk of incident dementia, including all-cause dementia, AD, and VD. Incidence rates were computed per 1,000 person-years.

Three models were constructed: model 1, adjusted for age and sex; model 2, adjusted for age, sex, low income, alcohol consumption, regular physical activity, and BMI; and model 3, adjusted for all variables in model 2 plus hearing loss, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. We used an age- and sex-adjusted model as the baseline (model 1) due to the significant differences in smoking rates between men and women in Korea, as well as variations in smoker characteristics across age groups (S. Kim et al., 2022). Smoking rates are much higher in men compared with women, and antismoking campaigns since the 1990s have led to generational differences in smoking behavior (Chang et al., 2019). These campaigns resulted in lower smoking rates and different smoking patterns in younger age groups compared with older individuals (M. Kim et al., 2023). By adjusting for age and sex, we accounted for these key demographic differences, providing a more accurate analysis of the relationship between smoking and dementia risk. The cumulative incidence of dementia during the follow-up period was calculated using Kaplan-Meier curves, stratified by smoking status.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the impact of cumulative smoking exposure before the depression diagnosis on dementia risk. Smoking exposure was quantified in pack-years (PY), defined as the number of packs of cigarettes smoked per day multiplied by the number of smoking years. Participants identified as current smokers at the initial health examination were categorized into groups of <20 PY and ≥20 PY. Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to determine HRs for each category, with adjustments similar to those in the main analysis. Subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate the consistency of the association across different subgroups stratified by sex, age, and other covariates. Interaction terms were included in the Cox models to test for potential effect modification. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics and Incidence of Dementia

Baseline characteristics of participants categorized by smoking status before and after depression diagnosis are detailed in Table 1. Significant differences were observed among the four groups (Nonsmoking, Initiated, Ceased, and Sustained) across several variables (p < 0.0001). Of the 1,290,530 participants, 1,073,517 (83.18%) were Nonsmokers, 26,811 (2.08%) Initiated smoking, 48,411 (3.75%) Ceased smoking, and 141,791 (10.99%) were Sustained smokers. The proportion of men was highest in the Sustained group (89.31%) and lowest among Nonsmokers (30.48%). The Sustained group had the highest percentage of men (89.31%) and the youngest average age (54.95 years), while Nonsmokers were older and more likely to be female. Income, alcohol consumption, and physical activity also varied significantly among groups. During a mean follow-up of 4.26 years, 58,885 dementia cases were recorded, with incidence rates varying by smoking status.

Association Between Smoking Status and Dementia Risk in Depression

Table 2 presents the associations between dementia risk and changes in smoking status before and after depression diagnosis, analyzed using three models with varying levels of adjustment. For all-cause dementia, adjusted risks of model 3 were higher for Initiated smokers (HR: 1.254; 95% CI: 1.175–1.337), Ceased smokers (HR: 1.261; 95% CI: 1.206–1.319), and Sustained smokers (HR: 1.338; 95% CI: 1.295–1.383) compared with Nonsmokers. For AD, adjusted risks were elevated for Initiated smokers (HR: 1.262; 95% CI: 1.175–1.356), Ceased smokers (HR: 1.262; 95% CI: 1.201–1.327), and Sustained smokers (HR: 1.323; 95% CI: 1.275–1.373). For VD, Initiated smokers exhibited a nonsignificant adjusted risk (HR: 1.145; 95% CI: 0.929–1.410), while Ceased smokers (HR: 1.469; 95% CI: 1.291–1.672) and Sustained smokers (HR: 1.524; 95% CI: 1.385–1.677) displayed higher adjusted risks. The cumulative incidence probability of all-cause dementia, AD, and VD over the follow-up period is depicted by Kaplan-Meier curves (Fig. 2). Sustained smokers consistently exhibited the highest incidence probabilities across all dementia types. Sensitivity analyses showed consistent trends, regardless of cumulative smoking exposure (Supplementary Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JNMD/A199).

Subgroup Analyses Stratified by Sex, Age, and Other Covariates

Subgroup analyses (Table 3) showed that Sustained smokers had a higher risk of all-cause dementia for both men (HR: 1.353) and women (HR: 1.246), with no significant sex difference. For VD, the risk was significantly higher in men (HR: 1.574) than in women (HR: 1.154, p for interaction = 0.0456). Age-wise, Initiated smokers in their 40s had the highest risk (HR: 2.143), while Sustained smokers generally showed elevated HRs across all age groups. Younger age groups showed higher dementia risks, which decreased with age, except for those aged 80 and above.

Effect Modification by Sex and Age on the Associations Between Dementia Risk and Smoking Status in Depression

Subgroup analyses were conducted using a model adjusted for age, sex, low income, alcohol consumption, regular physical activity, body mass index, hearing loss, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia; however, sex and age were not used as covariates in each subgroup analysis.

Further analyses by income, alcohol consumption, obesity, and other factors revealed significant interactions for all-cause dementia and AD. Low-income Sustained smokers had a higher dementia risk (HR: 1.472) than their non–low-income counterparts (HR: 1.297). Alcohol consumption increased dementia risk among Sustained smokers (HR: 1.438 for alcohol consumers vs. HR: 1.270 for nonconsumers), while obesity attenuated the risk (HR: 1.227 with obesity vs. HR: 1.398 without obesity). For VD, alcohol consumption and diabetes significantly elevated the risk (Supplementary Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JNMD/A199).

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide cohort study of individuals with depression, we found that continuous smoking presented a greater dementia risk than discontinuing smoking post-depression diagnosis. In addition, initiating smoking was associated with a higher risk compared with never smoking or abstaining postdiagnosis in depressed patients. These findings remained consistent regardless of the total prediagnosis smoking amount. Middle age was a key risk period for AD. For VD, men showed consistently higher risk, while women had elevated risk mainly in the ceased smoking group.

Our findings are consistent with existing literature, which highlights the detrimental effects of smoking on cognitive decline and dementia (Otuyama et al., 2020; Raggi et al., 2022; Whitsel et al., 2022; Zhong et al., 2015). Similarly, our patient sample with depression demonstrated the adverse impacts of smoking. Smoking and depression independently cause vascular damage through mechanisms such as endothelial dysfunction, increased arterial stiffness, and elevated thrombosis risk (Koola et al., 2012; Messner and Bernhard, 2014; Taylor et al., 2013), which can synergistically heighten the risk of dementia. In addition, both conditions promote systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, expediting neurodegenerative processes (Durazzo et al., 2014; Sipahi et al., 2023). Smoking may disrupt neurotransmitter systems, including nicotinic, N-methyl-d-aspartate, and kynurenine pathways (Donlon et al., 2024; Zakiniaeiz et al., 2023). These overlapping vascular and neurochemical mechanisms may underlie the elevated dementia risk observed in patients with depression who smoke. In this context, recent studies have proposed that combination therapies targeting shared pathways—such as the galantamine–memantine combination—may offer integrative treatment benefits for depression, tobacco use disorder, and neurocognitive disorders (Bai et al., 2021).

Our sensitivity analyses showed that the total amount of smoking before depression diagnosis did not alter dementia risk. Contrary to expectations, even light smokers with <20 pack-years did not show a reduced risk compared with heavy smokers. A review by Durazzo et al. (2014) observed a dose-dependent relationship between the amount and duration of smoking, although the results for former smokers were inconclusive. Methodological differences may have impacted these findings, as our analysis concentrated on the amount of smoking before depression diagnosis rather than over a lifetime. However, the critical insight remains that cessation of smoking significantly reduces the future risk of dementia, irrespective of the amount previously smoked. The significant reduction in dementia risk among individuals who ceased smoking following a depression diagnosis emphasizes the urgent necessity for smoking cessation programs.

The gender difference in dementia risk was not significant for all-cause dementia or AD, with both genders experiencing increased risk due to smoking, consistent with previous studies (Jeong et al., 2023). However, the impact of smoking on VD risk varied by gender. Among men, those who continued smoking had the highest risk of VD, whereas the highest risk for women was observed in those who had quit smoking following a depression diagnosis. One possible explanation is that women might have quit smoking due to early symptoms of physical problems and were subsequently diagnosed with dementia. Nevertheless, this trend was observed only for VD and not for all-cause dementia or AD. The “smoking cessation paradox” refers to situations where quitting smoking may result in seemingly paradoxical outcomes, especially in cardiovascular contexts (Ramotowski et al., 2019). Quitting smoking might increase thrombotic complications in certain patients, such as those taking clopidogrel (Ramotowski et al., 2019). Smoking cessation is also known to adversely affect vasospastic angina through autonomic dysfunction, endothelial dysfunction, smooth muscle hyperactivity, and genetic predispositions (Tran et al., 2021). However, this does not imply that our findings should discourage smoking cessation in women. A recent study reported that although current smokers with stroke might initially have better outcomes than ex-smokers, they ultimately endured twice the mortality rate from stroke and suffered from stroke-related disabilities for 11 years longer (Wang et al., 2020). While our findings indicate a higher risk of VD in those who quit smoking, smoking exacerbates the overall disease burden and severity, necessitating careful interpretation.

For all-cause dementia, the highest hazard ratio was observed in individuals in their 40s, with a clear trend of decreasing risk as age increased. In contrast, the risk of Alzheimer’s dementia (AD) was higher in the 50s compared with the 40s, which may be attributed to AD’s tendency to manifest later in life, resulting in lower diagnosis rates and hazard ratios for AD in the 40s. Smoking was found to have a greater impact on the occurrence of dementia in individuals under 65 years, underscoring the importance of midlife as reported in other studies (Jang et al., 2021; Malik et al., 2021). Vascular risk factors in midlife are strongly associated with increased dementia risk later in life (Gottesman et al., 2017) and are linked with changes in brain structure and function that can lead to cognitive decline and dementia (Knopman and Roberts, 2011). Midlife hormonal changes might also enhance the detrimental effects of smoking, where decreasing estrogen levels could exacerbate smoking-induced oxidative stress and inflammation (Heravi et al., 2022). Another plausible explanation is that survival bias and competing risks reduce the apparent risk of dementia from smoking at extreme ages. Survival bias suggests that individuals who have smoked throughout their lives but reached very old age may represent a relatively healthier subset of smokers who were resilient to the detrimental effects of smoking. Competing risks, such as mortality from smoking-related diseases (e.g., cardiovascular disease, lung cancer), may also result in fewer smokers surviving into older age, thus lowering the observable incidence of dementia in these populations (Zhong et al., 2015).

Beyond age and sex, several other covariates, such as alcohol use, socioeconomic status, obesity, and diabetes mellitus, also influenced the relationship between smoking and dementia risk. Individuals with alcohol problems exhibited a higher risk of all-cause dementia, AD, and VD, which aligns with previous research suggesting that alcohol consumption, smoking, and depression contribute to neurotoxic and vascular damage, escalating dementia risk (Alikunju et al., 2011; Hulse et al., 2005; Takahashi et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2019). The impact of smoking on dementia risk was more pronounced in individuals with lower income, which may be due to smaller brain volumes and increased vulnerability to smoking’s effects in lower socioeconomic groups (Raggi et al., 2022). Conversely, obesity appeared to buffer the effect of smoking on dementia risk, possibly due to altered nicotine metabolism and increased medical monitoring in obese individuals (Ferrea and Winterer, 2009; Sieber, 2012). Diabetes significantly amplified the risk of VD in smokers, with those initiating smoking after depression diagnosis facing particularly elevated risk. However, smoking cessation in diabetic individuals reduced their risk of VD, likely due to improvements in vascular health and glycemic control (Walicka et al., 2022). This suggests that managing vascular health and addressing comorbidities such as diabetes are critical in mitigating the combined effects of smoking and diabetes on dementia risk (Ninomiya, 2014).

Our study has several notable strengths. First, the large sample size and use of a comprehensive nationwide database enhance the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Second, by leveraging a longitudinal design with repeated health screenings, we were able to track dynamic changes in smoking behavior before and after a depression diagnosis, which provided a unique perspective on behavioral trajectories rather than static exposure measures. Third, the inclusion of various demographic and lifestyle factors allowed for detailed subgroup analyses and identification of high-risk populations who may benefit from targeted interventions.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the absence of a healthy control group without depression limits our ability to determine if the observed association between changes in smoking behavior and dementia risk is specific to individuals with depression. Second, while our cohort was large, it only included individuals who underwent health screenings before and after their depression diagnosis. This might represent a healthier group, potentially underestimating the association. Third, depression and dementia diagnoses were based on ICD-10 codes, which may introduce misclassification. Nevertheless, these codes are widely accepted in large-scale studies and have been validated as reliable proxies for clinical diagnoses. Fourth, smoking status was self-reported and only captured up to 4 years before and after depression diagnosis, which may not reflect ongoing smoking behavior (Connor Gorber et al., 2009). Given the high relapse rate in Korea (Eum et al., 2022), some individuals categorized as Nonsmokers or Ceased smokers may have resumed smoking. Despite these limitations, our results highlight the benefits of even short-term smoking cessation in reducing dementia risk, as shown in our sensitivity analysis. Fifth, we did not account for death as a competing risk for dementia, which could lead to an overestimation of dementia risk, as smoking and depression increase mortality. Lastly, genetic factors (e.g., APOE4) and psychiatric medications were not considered, which could modify the relationship between smoking and dementia (Zhang et al., 2021). Future studies incorporating genetic and pharmacological data would add valuable insights.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Our study highlights how changes in smoking status—continuing, quitting, or initiating smoking after a depression diagnosis—affect dementia risk. Continued smoking posed the highest risk, while quitting still resulted in elevated risks compared with nonsmokers. Middle age was a critical period, especially for AD, and gender differences were observed in VD, with men showing a consistent increase in risk and women showing higher risk, mainly in the ceased smoking group. These findings underscore the need for personalized smoking cessation strategies to reduce dementia risk in depressed individuals. Future research should incorporate clinical details such as depression severity, medication use, and genetic factors, and consider competing risk models and longer follow-up to better understand long-term outcomes.
