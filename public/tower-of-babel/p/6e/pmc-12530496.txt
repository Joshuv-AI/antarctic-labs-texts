Factors associated with willingness to accept pneumococcal vaccine based on the Health Belief Model among the elderly (≥60 years) living in Klang Valley, Malaysia
Authors: Muhammad Faiz Mohd Hisham, Chandrashekhar T. Sreeramareddy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnderstanding health behavior using the Health Belief Model (HBM) helps formulate strategies to improve the willingness, thereby increasing the acceptance of pneumococcal vaccination among the elderly. We aimed to assess the willingness to receive the pneumococcal vaccine and associated factors to inform pneumococcal vaccination policy for the elderly in Malaysia. We surveyed 402 elderly (60 years and above) selected from 14 Pusat Aktiviti Warga Emas (PAWE; Senior Citizen Activity Centers) in Klang Valley, Malaysia. Using a questionnaire, we gathered information on respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics, self-reported knowledge of pneumococcal diseases, behavioral determinants using the HBM, trust in healthcare, trust in vaccines, and willingness to receive the pneumococcal vaccine. The mean age was 66.4 years (SD = 5.6). Only 29% had received at least one dose of pneumococcal vaccine, almost all of whom (93%) had received it before the Hajj or Umrah pilgrimage of Muslims. Mean willingness score was 8.20 (95% CI 8.01–8.39). Multiple linear regression analysis showed that elderly individuals were more willing to receive the pneumococcal vaccine if they perceived the disease as severe, had fewer barriers to vaccination, were influenced by prompts or encouragement to take action, and had greater trust in healthcare providers and the vaccine itself. The survey found low coverage for pneumococcal vaccination. Our findings suggest that improving information about pneumococcal disease, its severity, and the pneumococcal vaccine through health care providers to the elderly and their families would improve willingness to uptake the pneumococcal vaccine.

Background

Streptococcus pneumoniae carriage is typically asymptomatic; pneumococcus can cause a variety of infections, including otitis media, sinusitis, and pneumonia.1 Streptococcus pneumoniae is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children and adults,2 but individuals aged ≥60 years have a higher risk due to waning immunity.3 Pneumococcal vaccination reduces the incidence of invasive pneumococcal disease among the elderly4 and adults.5 Malaysia has a high burden and mortality from pneumonia, mostly caused by pneumococcal disease,6 which is the third leading cause of death.7 Pneumococcal vaccination was launched under the National Immunization Program (NIP) for children in 2020. Pneumococcal vaccine for adult vaccination is available only at private healthcare facilities. Economic evaluation has shown that pneumococcal vaccination for adults would reduce the burden of disease and save healthcare costs of treating pneumococcal diseases.8 Yet, to date, there is no nationwide vaccination program in Malaysia targeting the adult and elderly groups. The Malaysian Society of Infectious Diseases and Chemotherapy (MSIDC) recommends that Hajj pilgrims, adults with comorbid conditions, and individuals aged 60 years and above receive two doses of pneumococcal vaccine. This should follow the appropriate timing and sequencing guidelines for administering both the 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13) and the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPV23).

Currently, pneumococcal surveillance studies are mainly on serotype prevalence through laboratory-based methods, with insufficient emphasis on the elderly population.9,10 Since pneumococcal vaccination reduces morbidity and mortality among the elderly, surveillance of pneumococcal vaccine coverage among the elderly is important to determine the current coverage and the willingness to accept pneumococcal vaccination among them. Although many high-income countries (HICs) report pneumococcal vaccine uptake as high and meeting desired coverage levels,11–14 contrasting findings have been observed in countries such as China15 and Hong Kong,16 where uptake remains relatively low. Studies from HICs have also reported contextual, individual, and social group, and vaccine-related and vaccination-specific determinants that influence older adults’ uptake of pneumococcal vaccination.17 Vaccine cost, knowledge about pneumococcal disease and perception of its severity, prior vaccination experience, and concerns about side effects are also reported to be associated with pneumococcal vaccine uptake.18–22 Considering that vaccine acceptance is influenced by cultural and contextual factors, findings from HICs may not be directly applicable to the Malaysian context. Therefore, utilizing a well-established theoretical framework such as the Health Belief Model (HBM), which encompasses key constructs including perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, and cues to action, is essential for gaining a deeper understanding of health-related behaviors and factors affecting pneumococcal vaccine uptake among the elderly population in Malaysia. Moreover, the HBM serves as a comprehensive tool for assessing the psychological factors involved in vaccine acceptance and has been applied broadly across studies of various vaccines.16,23

Understanding the factors influencing (facilitators) and barriers to pneumococcal vaccine uptake using the HBM model assists in formulating strategies to improve pneumococcal vaccine uptake.23 This study is timely and necessary, particularly in the wake of growing global vaccine hesitancy following the COVID-19 pandemic, which has reshaped public trust in vaccination programs. Research indicates that exposure to misinformation, risk perception, and confidence in government institutions have significantly influenced vaccine attitudes during and after the pandemic.24 To date, there are no published data on pneumococcal vaccination uptake among the elderly population in Malaysia, nor have any studies specifically investigated vaccine acceptance using the HBM approach. Moreover, there is no established system to monitor pneumococcal vaccine uptake among older adults, making it difficult to assess vaccine coverage, its efficacy, and the factors that influence their willingness for the vaccination. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the pneumococcal vaccine coverage and willingness to accept among elderly (≥60 years) in Malaysia.

Methods

Design, setting, and participants

A cross-sectional community-based interviewer-administered survey was conducted in the Klang Valley that comprised the Federal Territories of Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya, and the state of Selangor. Klang Valley, a predominantly urban region, has a total population of 8.8 million, including an estimated 554,808 elderly individuals.25 Due to limited resources, we chose to survey the elderly who were registered with the Department of Social Welfare (DSW) and actively participated in the Senior Citizen Activity Center, also known in Malay as Pusat Aktiviti Warga Emas (PAWE). DSW administers PAWE under the Ministry of Women, Family, and Community Development. All Malaysian citizens aged 60 years and above, residents of the Klang valley, and registered with PAWE were eligible. Those with psychological impairments affecting cognitive clarity and decision-making capacity were deemed ineligible.

Sample size and sampling

Yamane’s formula [n = N/(1 + Ne^2)], where n is the sample size, N is the population size, and e is the margin of error, was used for sample size calculation. For N of 554,808, 95% confidence limits and an allowable error of 0.05, the minimum number of respondents required for this study was determined to be 400. We purposively selected PAWEs in the Klang Valley due to limited resources. Of 29 PAWEs in the Klang valley, 14 were selected by simple random sampling.

Questionnaire development

The interview questionnaire was adapted from previous research23,26–28 and had six sections: A to F (Online Appendix B). Section A covered basic demographics such as age, sex, race, marital status, education level, and gross monthly income. Section B collected information about medical history, comorbidities, self-perceived health status, pneumococcal vaccination status, and smoking behavior. Section C assessed the respondents’ self-reported knowledge of pneumococcal disease. Section D assessed the five theoretical constructs of the HBM. The HBM construct consisted of five domains: 1) Perceived Susceptibility, 2) Perceived Severity, 3) Perceived Benefit, 4) Perceived Barrier, and 5) Cues to Action, with two, four, three, eleven, and four items, respectively, yielding a total of 24 items in this section. Section E assessed respondents’ perceived trust in healthcare services and the pneumococcal vaccine, comprising three and four survey items, respectively. Section F covered respondents’ willingness to accept the pneumococcal vaccine using three items. All response options in sections D, E, and F were measured using a 4-point Likert scale: 1 – Strongly Disagree, 2 – Disagree, 3 – Agree, and 4 – Strongly Agree. We did not use any reverse coding. The total score of each construct was the sum of responses to the items under each of the constructs. In a pilot study among 40 respondents, the Cronbach’s alpha value of ≥0.8 for constructs of HBM, the trust in healthcare and trust vaccine components, and the willingness to accept the vaccine indicates a good level of internal consistency.29

Variables

Willingness to accept the pneumococcal vaccination as a score was the dependent variable. The score was the sum of responses to three items on a four-point Likert scale (Strongly Disagree = 1 to Strongly Agree = 4). The three items were about readiness for vaccination for self, encouraging, family and friends, and commitment if the government initiates a vaccination program. The independent variables were socio-demographic variables, domains of the HBM model, and trust in healthcare and trust in the vaccine. The scores for each domain of HBM, trust in healthcare, and vaccine were the sum of responses to the items under each domain. A higher total score within each construct indicates a greater level of agreement with the measured variable. The demographic variables were age, sex, education, work status, marital status (grouped as married/have partner and single/no partner), self-rated health status, presence of co-morbid conditions, and awareness about pneumococcal disease (Online Appendix B).

Procedure

During August–December 2024, the face-to-face survey was carried out on the weekly PAWE sessions by the first author, who is a native Malay speaker, using a self-administered questionnaire via a paper-based or device-assisted online Google Forms. Consent was obtained from each participant. The respondents took 10–15 minutes to complete the questionnaire. For illiterate respondents, the first author interviewed and recorded their responses. Scripted responses and active listening practices were applied to minimize interviewer bias. In the event of a language barrier, the proxy, i.e., kin who was with the sampling site during the data collection, served as a mediator; however, most of the respondents were able to comprehend and complete the questionnaire independently without requiring such assistance.

Analytic strategy

All data were entered into the prepared Google Forms and then downloaded into the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26. The descriptive analysis, presented with means, standard deviations, and frequency distributions, was conducted. A simple linear regression was performed to examine the independent association of willingness score with the demographics, health-related factors, HBM domains, and trust factors. Factors that showed statistical significance of p ≤ .05 in the simple linear regression analyses were entered into the multiple linear regression analysis to identify the factors associated with the willingness to accept the pneumococcal vaccine after controlling for confounders such as demographics and health-related factors. We estimated unadjusted and adjusted beta coefficients and their 95% confidence intervals. A p-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Of these, 111 Muslim respondents received a pneumococcal vaccine based on the recommendation by doctors before Hajj and Umrah in addition to the mandatory meningococcal vaccination. Only 117 respondents (29.1%) were aware of pneumococcal disease. Discussion

Our survey among the elderly participating in PAWE in the Klang Valley of Malaysia showed that only a third had received the pneumococcal vaccine. The recipients were mostly those who also received mandatory meningococcal vaccination to obtain a visa to Saudi Arabia to perform the Hajj pilgrimage and the pneumococcal vaccine, as per healthcare providers’ advice. The remaining eight non-Muslim respondents chose to receive the vaccine on their own initiative. Awareness about the pneumococcal disease was rather low, and the source of information was mainly the mass media. The mean score for willingness to receive the pneumococcal vaccine was average, and a willingness score was positively associated with a higher perceived severity, cues to action, and trust in healthcare and trust about the vaccine, and was negatively associated with perceived barriers to the vaccine. Our survey revealed an important purpose for which the pneumococcal vaccine was received, and the determinants of willingness that would help disseminate information among the elderly and their family members to improve its uptake.

For unvaccinated Malaysian adults of age 60 years and above, the current recommendation is a sequential series of PCV13 followed by PPSV23, with the PCV13 dose first and PPSV23 at least one year after, irrespective of the presence of any underlying medical conditions.30 Pneumococcal vaccination was introduced into the national childhood immunization schedule in 2022 and is available free of cost at all public healthcare facilities, but for adults, the pneumococcal vaccine is to be taken from private healthcare facilities, whose cost is to be borne by the recipient. Under this scenario, it is critical to assess the coverage, willingness to receive, and factors that influence the willingness to receive the pneumococcal vaccine. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is no literature on pneumococcal vaccination coverage among the elderly in Malaysia. Studies from high-income countries,11–13 as well as those from China15 and Hong Kong,16 have shown that the coverage of pneumococcal vaccines is rather low, which is similar to the 29% coverage of at least one dose of PCV in our survey. Coverage rates of hepatitis and influenza vaccines were also low among the Malaysian elderly.31 It is interesting to note that most of the recipients of the pneumococcal vaccine did so for the Hajj pilgrimage, which is, as per the current recommendation in Malaysia.30 Vaccination coverage among the Chinese elderly was much higher for hepatitis B and influenza in previous studies,31 but most of the pneumococcal vaccine recipients were Malay Muslims for the Hajj pilgrimage. Though not a mandatory requirement by the authorities of Saudi Arabia for the Hajj pilgrimage, the Malaysian doctors recommend that the elderly undertake the pneumococcal vaccine according to the recommendation by the Malaysian Society for Infectious Diseases.30 Our findings support the current call for mandatory pneumococcal vaccination.32,33 The reason for low PCV coverage other than for pilgrimage could be the cost of the vaccine to be paid out-of-pocket in the absence of a national immunization program funded by the government, physicians not offering the elderly pneumococcal vaccination during consultations, low awareness of pneumococcal disease, and/or concerns about vaccine safety reported from the studies in high-income countries.17

The willingness score to accept PCV among the elderly was just above average. To identify the predictors of these preventive health behaviors, we used HBM34 and identified that perceived severity and cues to action were positively associated with a higher willingness for the pneumococcal vaccine, whereas barriers were negatively associated with willingness. Similar findings were reported from the Australian elderly toward self-reported influenza vaccination using the HBM model.23 Perceived susceptibility to pneumococcal disease and the benefits of the vaccine were not associated with willingness, maybe due to a lack of knowledge about diseases and the vaccine, as shown by the literature.17 Among the cues to action, recommendations from family members,19 and healthcare professionals were reported to be important factors in higher willingness to accept the pneumococcal vaccine.17 The positive association of perceived severity with willingness for the pneumococcal vaccine is consistent with previous studies.35,36 This finding suggests that most of the elderly population surveyed who had at least one chronic condition may have felt it necessary to receive the vaccine. Identifying any potential barriers to vaccination is an important issue to address, as well as addressing their concerns toward getting the pneumococcal vaccine via health education or health promotional strategies to improve vaccination coverage. Consistent with the literature, willingness was negatively associated with barriers.21,23,35–37 Financial cost, vaccine-related concerns such as safety and effectiveness, are reported to be important barriers toward willingness for pneumococcal vaccine among the elderly.17 The association of trust in healthcare and trust in vaccines with willingness is also consistent with the literature,38–40 suggesting that despite the concerns about the vaccine, trust in the healthcare provider and positive attitudes toward vaccination strategies may improve willingness.

Implications of our findings on policy, practice, and research

Low to moderate coverage except among those who had performed Hajj pilgrimage and average willingness to receive pneumococcal vaccination emphasize the need to disseminate vaccine availability, effectiveness, and safety to this risk group through mass media, since for most elderly, this was the main source of information. Our findings suggest that family members and healthcare providers should provide more information about the pneumococcal vaccine to the elderly to improve their willingness. For improved willingness and uptake of the pneumococcal vaccine, subsidies on vaccine costs, cost coverage under health insurance schemes, and, importantly, free-of-cost availability in public health facilities may improve the coverage. More importantly, findings suggest that advice by healthcare practitioners during the consultation and assurance to the elderly through health education about vaccine safety would help improve willingness irrespective of cost-deterrence.41 In addition to health education regarding pneumococcal diseases and potential benefits, reminders to uptake the vaccine and onsite vaccination during healthcare encounters would be beneficial to improve acceptance.41 Large-scale surveys shall be conducted to estimate pneumococcal vaccine coverage, willingness, and vaccine hesitancy across the country. Qualitative studies shall also be conducted to better understand the perceptions of the elderly toward the pneumococcal vaccine and obtain deeper insights into the barriers to vaccination.

Limitations

Firstly, this was a small-scale survey of participants selected from the PAWE of the Klang Valley, and is not representative of the Malaysian elderly. A sample of the elderly recruited from PAWE is likely to be those with better health literacy, leading to a selection bias. Therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to all Malaysian elderly. Our questionnaire was not formally validated for this survey, and it did not cover health motivation and self-efficacy measurement. The association was tested using cross-sectional data to limit inferences about the temporality of domains according to HBM and willingness. Despite the assurance about anonymity and confidentiality during the survey, we cannot rule out social desirability bias for self-reported health behaviors. Since Klang Valley is an urban community, the findings do not reflect rural Malaysia.

Conclusion

Our survey shows that coverage of pneumococcal and the willingness to receive the vaccine were moderate. Using HBM, our study confirms that perceived severity of pneumococcal disease, barriers to vaccination, and cues to action, such as advice from family members or healthcare providers, explain the willingness to accept the pneumococcal vaccine. Improved information campaigns and health education about the pneumococcal disease and vaccine are needed to improve the willingness. Nationwide studies are needed to better inform the formulation of the pneumococcal vaccination rollout for the Malaysian elderly.
