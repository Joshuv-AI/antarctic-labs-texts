Association between shoulder external rotation strength and real-world arm use in patients with mild post-stroke hemiparesis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Shuichi Sasaki, Atsuko Karube, Akari Kobayashi, Yutaka Ishii, Haruki Taguchi, Takuya Nakai, Sora Kurosaki, Takayuki Watabe, Tomonori Kenmoku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract [Purpose] This cross-sectional study investigated the association between shoulder external rotation strength and real-world arm use in patients with mild post-stroke hemiparesis. [Participants and Methods] Seventeen participants with ≥Brunnstrom stage IV and active shoulder flexion ≥110 degrees were assessed. External rotation strength was measured using a hand-held dynamometer. Real-world arm use was evaluated using the Motor Activity Log. [Results] External rotation strength was associated with Motor Activity Log scores and grip strength. Participants with movement-induced shoulder pain demonstrated lower external rotation strength compared with those without pain. [Conclusion] Reduced shoulder external rotation strength may contribute to decreased real-world arm use in mild hemiparesis.Key words: Stroke, Shoulder stability, Motor activity log

INTRODUCTION

Reduced spontaneous use of the affected upper limb is frequently observed after stroke, even in patients with mild hemiparesis who regain selective motor control. In such individuals, impairment-based measures may indicate relatively good recovery, even when real-world use of the affected arm remains limited1). Identifying modifiable factors associated with reduced arm use is therefore important for rehabilitation.

Shoulder dysfunction after stroke, including pain and altered movement, is common2,3,4,5) and may contribute to decreased upper limb activity in daily life. However, evaluation of external rotator function of the shoulder has received less attention in routine neurorehabilitation despite its potential importance for dynamic glenohumeral stability and coordinated arm elevation6,7,8). Weakness of the external rotators may lead to compensatory shoulder movements and increased mechanical stress around the shoulder complex9, 10), resulting in movement-induced pain and avoidance of affected arm use.

The purpose of this study was to examine the association between shoulder external rotation strength and real-world arm use in patients with mild post-stroke hemiparesis. In addition, we explored whether movement-induced shoulder pain was related to external rotation weakness and reduced shoulder motion. We also examined the relationship between grip strength and external rotation strength to consider the clinical usefulness of grip strength as a practical indicator when hand-held dynamometry is not available.

PARTICIPANTS AND METHODS

In this cross-sectional study, patients with post-stroke hemiparesis who were admitted to a convalescent rehabilitation ward were screened. Inclusion criteria were Brunnstrom stage IV or higher in the upper limb and active shoulder flexion of at least 110 degrees. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval No. B16-180), written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Shoulder external rotation strength was measured using a hand-held dynamometer (Mobie, Sakai Medical Co., Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Hand-held dynamometry has been reported to have good intra- and inter-rater reliability for measuring shoulder rotation strength in clinical populations. Measurements were performed twice on each side with the shoulder at neutral rotation and the elbow flexed at 90 degrees11). The mean values were used to calculate the affected to non-affected side external rotation strength ratio. Left and right grip strength was assessed, and the grip strength ratio (affected/non-affected side) was calculated.

Real-world upper limb use was assessed using the Motor Activity Log (MAL)12), including the amount of use (AOU) and quality of movement (QOM) subscales. Motor impairment and clinical characteristics were assessed using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment13) for the upper extremity (FMA-UE), Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), and active shoulder flexion range of motion (AROM). Pain during active shoulder motion was recorded.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were used to examine associations between variables. Group differences were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test and statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Partial correlation analyses were also performed to control for potential confounding variables (MAS and FMA-UE).

The external rotation strength ratio was positively correlated with MAL-AOU (r=0.65, p=0.009) and MAL-QOM (r=0.70, p=0.004), along with the grip strength ratio (r=0.73, p=0.001). In contrast, the external rotation strength ratio was negatively correlated with MAS (r=−0.70, p=0.002). Partial correlation analyses were conducted to control for potential confounding variables. After controlling for Fugl-Meyer Assessment for the Upper Extremity (FMA-UE), the external rotation strength ratio remained significantly correlated with MAL-AOU (r=0.590, p=0.026) and MAL-QOM (r=0.658, p=0.011).

Similarly, after controlling for Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), the external rotation strength ratio was still significantly correlated with MAL-AOU (r=0.758, p=0.002) and MAL-QOM (r=0.800, p<0.001).

Four of the 17 participants reported movement-induced shoulder pain. Comparisons between grouped pain and no-pain participants are shown in Table 2. Movement-induced shoulder pain (coded as present vs. absent) was associated with a lower external rotation strength ratio (r=−0.55, p=0.022), indicating lower external rotation strength in participants with pain. The pain group demonstrated a lower external rotation strength ratio and reduced active shoulder flexion AROM compared with the no-pain group. MAL scores were lower in the pain group than in the no-pain group, although the difference was not statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

This cross-sectional study examined the association between shoulder external rotation strength and real-world arm use in patients with mild post-stroke hemiparesis. The main findings were that the external rotation strength ratio was associated with MAL-AOU and MAL-QOM, and that participants with movement-induced shoulder pain had lower external rotation strength and a reduced shoulder flexion range of motion.

External rotator muscles contribute to dynamic stabilization of the glenohumeral joint9) and coordinated arm elevation. In patients with mild hemiparesis, selective movement may appear adequate, yet external rotator weakness may be associated with compensatory movement patterns and altered joint mechanics. Such compensation may increase mechanical stress around the shoulder complex, which may be related to movement-induced pain and reduced spontaneous use of the affected arm. The observed association between pain and lower external rotation strength supports the clinical importance of evaluating external rotator function even in patients with mild impairment. Notably, these associations remained significant even after controlling for motor impairment (FMA-UE) and spasticity (MAS), suggesting that external rotation strength may independently relate to real-world arm use. Since increased use of the affected arm promotes motor improvement after stroke14), reduced use may hinder functional recovery. Although the MAL is widely used to assess real-world arm use after stroke12), reductions in impairment do not necessarily translate into increased arm use in daily life1). The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the MAL has been reported in previous studies, suggesting that the observed differences may have clinical relevance12).

External rotation strength ratio was correlated with grip strength ratio. The measurement of grip strength is feasible in most clinical settings, whereas the assessment of external rotation strength using a hand-held dynamometer is not always feasible. The present finding suggests that grip strength is a practical screening indicator of overall upper-limb strength when the direct measurement of external rotation strength is not possible. However, grip strength should not be regarded as a direct substitute for external rotation assessment because it reflects global upper-limb performance and may be influenced by multiple factors.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, particularly in the pain subgroup, and therefore this study should be considered exploratory in nature. In addition, all participants were recruited from a single convalescent rehabilitation ward, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Third, movement-induced pain was assessed clinically and may have been influenced by subjective factors. In addition, although partial correlation analyses were performed, the influence of other unmeasured confounding factors cannot be ruled out. Future prospective studies are needed to clarify causal relationships and to determine whether interventions targeting external rotator function improve real-world arm use and reduce shoulder pain.

In conclusion, shoulder external rotation strength was associated with real-world arm use in patients with mild post-stroke hemiparesis. Movement-induced shoulder pain was related to lower external rotation strength and reduced shoulder motion. Evaluation and management of external rotator function may be important for optimizing upper limb use during rehabilitation.
