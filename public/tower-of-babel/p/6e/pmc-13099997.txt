Comparative evaluation of several models for forecasting hourly electricity use in a steel plant
Authors: Fuzhen Gu, Yanlin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The iron and steel industry belongs to the most electricity-intensive branches of manufacturing, and power expenses often account for a large portion of overall production costs. For this reason, reliable short-term forecasts of plant-level electricity consumption are essential for optimizing production planning, avoiding excessive demand charges, and supporting low-carbon operation. This study compares three representative approaches for hourly power-load prediction in a steel enterprise: eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM). Using one year of operating data from an integrated steel plant, we build a dataset of 8,760 hourly records and design a unified preprocessing procedure, including three-sigma outlier detection, time-indexed linear interpolation, and chronological division into training, validation, and test subsets. All three models are trained within a 24-hour sliding-window framework and assessed with RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. The results indicate that each model is capable of depicting the cyclical variation of the load, whereas BiLSTM provides the most accurate predictions, achieving the lowest errors in both absolute and relative terms. XGBoost demonstrates competitive performance and robust trend following. In contrast, the unidirectional LSTM exhibits larger relative errors, particularly during low-load periods. The findings underline the benefits of bidirectional recurrent structures for short-term electricity-consumption forecasting in steel plants and offer guidance for model selection in industrial energy-management practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43868-z.

Introduction

The global iron and steel sector consumes a huge amount of energy. Industry as a whole is responsible for roughly one-third of final energy use worldwide, and steelmaking is one of the dominant consumers within this sector. In many integrated steelworks, expenditures on electricity, coal, natural gas, and coke can reach 20–40% of the total cost of production, with electricity frequently being the main single cost item. As a consequence, improvements in energy-use efficiency and accurate anticipation of electricity demand have become crucial levers for enhancing competitiveness, reducing operating expenditure, and advancing decarbonization strategies in steel enterprises1.

Electricity usage in modern steel production fluctuates significantly over time. Hourly demand is shaped by production plans, the operating states of key equipment, maintenance activities, and calendar effects such as workdays, weekends, and shift changes. If short-term load forecasts are inaccurate, production scheduling may be suboptimal, time-of-use tariffs may lead to extra costs, and the plant may face difficulties in participating in demand-response programs or integrating renewable power2. Conversely, accurate hourly forecasts enable steelworks to shift loads, reduce peak demand, and organize production in an energy-aware fashion.

Short-term load forecasting (STLF) has been researched extensively in power-system and building-energy applications. Early work mainly relied on statistical time-series models such as autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), exponential smoothing, and multiple linear regression3. These methods assume linear relationships and usually depend on hand-crafted features. With the growth of high-resolution load data, machine-learning approaches—support vector regression, random forests, gradient boosting, and others—have become popular because they can better capture nonlinear interactions between the load and explanatory variables. In particular, XGBoost has been widely applied in STLF because of its strong performance, efficiency, and robustness to heterogeneous feature sets.

In parallel, deep-learning techniques, especially recurrent neural networks (RNNs), have emerged as powerful tools for modeling time-series data. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks alleviate the vanishing-gradient problem and are able to learn long-range temporal dependencies in electricity-demand sequences4,5. Many recent studies report that LSTM-based models often outperform both traditional statistical approaches and shallow neural networks in short-term load-forecasting tasks. Extensions such as the Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM), which processes the sequence in both forward and backward directions, further enrich temporal representation and have shown promising results in load and renewable-power forecasting.

Despite these advances, challenges remain when such data-driven methods are applied to plant-level electricity consumption in steel enterprises6,7. First, a large portion of the literature focuses on system-level, residential, or commercial loads, while fewer contributions address process-driven industrial loads whose behavior is strongly governed by complex production operations. Second, only a limited number of studies perform head-to-head comparisons between tree-based ensemble models and deep recurrent networks under the same dataset, preprocessing pipeline, and evaluation protocol. Third, the combined impact of simple but robust preprocessing methods (e.g., three-sigma outlier detection and time-based interpolation) and calendar-type variables (e.g., weekday/weekend, day-of-week) has not been systematically explored in the steel-plant context.

To bridge these gaps, this work carries out a comparative evaluation of multiple models for hourly electricity-consumption forecasting in a steel enterprise. Using real plant data, we construct a year-long hourly dataset augmented with calendar features, clean it using a three-sigma rule followed by time-axis interpolation, and divide it chronologically into training, validation, and test sets to avoid temporal information leakage. On top of this dataset we implement a unified one-step-ahead sliding-window forecasting framework, within which three representative models—XGBoost, LSTM, and BiLSTM—are trained and evaluated.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on short-term load forecasting and summarizes the research gap. Section 3 describes the data source and the preprocessing procedures, including outlier detection, interpolation, and chronological dataset partitioning. Section 4 presents the construction of the forecasting models and the unified forecasting framework. Section 5 reports the experimental setup and compares the predictive performance of the three models using multiple evaluation metrics. Finally, Sect. 6 concludes the paper and discusses limitations and future research directions.

Literature review

Short-term load forecasting is widely recognized as a key technology for safe and economical operation of modern power systems. Recent review articles generally classify STLF approaches into three groups: classical statistical methods, machine-learning techniques, and deep-learning models, with various hybrid or ensemble schemes built upon them. Statistical models—including ARIMA and its seasonal variants, exponential-smoothing schemes, and regression-type formulations—dominated early applications because of their transparency and modest data requirements. As finer-granularity load data and richer contextual information have become available, data-driven ML and DL methods have been increasingly adopted to capture complex nonlinear dependencies and temporal patterns, often delivering higher accuracy in practical applications.

Statistical and classical time-series approaches

Classical time-series approaches model load as a stochastic process driven by its own historical values and sometimes exogenous variables8. ARIMA-type models and their seasonal extensions have been widely used in STLF because they explicitly capture autocorrelation, trend, and seasonality in the load series. Double seasonal Holt–Winters exponential smoothing has also been shown to perform competitively for intraday and daily load forecasting, especially in systems exhibiting both daily and weekly periodicities. In addition, multiple linear regression models have been applied by incorporating calendar variables as explanatory features. These models often serve as benchmarks because of their simplicity and tractability, but they rely on linearity assumptions and may struggle to represent nonlinear effects and complex interactions among predictors9.

Machine learning methods for load forecasting

To overcome the limitations of linear models, a rich body of work has investigated machine-learning alternatives for STLF, such as support vector regression, k-nearest neighbors, decision trees, random forests, and gradient-boosting algorithms10. These models flexibly approximate nonlinear relations between the load and input features and can accommodate heterogeneous data types. Random-forest-based models, when combined with appropriate feature selection and hyperparameter optimization, have demonstrated improved robustness and accuracy across various load profiles and forecasting horizons11.

Among boosting-based approaches, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) has received particular attention due to its efficient implementation, regularization mechanisms, and ability to handle large-scale tabular data. In short-term power load forecasting, XGBoost has been reported to outperform traditional gradient boosting and random forest models by better capturing feature interactions and reducing overfitting12,13. Moreover, XGBoost has been successfully employed in hybrid frameworks where feature engineering, decomposition (e.g., empirical mode decomposition), or ensemble strategies are used to further improve predictive performance. However, most of these studies focus on system-level or distribution-level loads, and fewer works systematically compare tree-based methods with recurrent deep learning models on the same industrial dataset14.

Deep learning models: LSTM and BiLSTM

Deep learning offers powerful tools for extracting hierarchical temporal features in STLF. While feedforward and convolutional neural networks have been applied to load forecasting, recurrent architectures—especially LSTM networks—are now the most widely used for sequential data because their gating mechanisms alleviate gradient vanishing and allow information to persist over longer horizons15. Numerous studies have shown that LSTM-based models can achieve better performance than both classical machine-learning methods and shallow neural networks in predicting building loads, distribution-system loads, and microgrid demand16.

To further enhance temporal feature extraction, bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) networks process the load sequence in both forward and backward directions, enabling them to exploit information from past and future contexts within the input window17. BiLSTM architectures have been applied to short-term load forecasting, often within hybrid or improved training frameworks. For example, BiLSTM combined with empirical mode decomposition or variational mode decomposition has shown promising performance in decomposed load series. Feature-mining-based BiLSTM models have been proposed to capture complex relationships among load, weather, and calendar variables, improving prediction accuracy especially under fluctuating conditions. Other works enhance BiLSTM training with optimization or regularization strategies, such as stochastic weight averaging, to improve generalization18. Overall, the literature indicates that BiLSTM can outperform unidirectional LSTM in many STLF scenarios by providing richer temporal representations; nevertheless, direct comparisons between BiLSTM and non-neural models under unified experimental setups remain relatively scarce19.

Electricity consumption forecasting in industrial and steel settings

Compared with system-level or residential STLF, plant-level industrial load forecasting poses additional challenges, including strong dependence on production schedules, process configurations, and operational strategies20. Recent studies have explored machine learning-based forecasting for industrial energy consumption, such as predicting annual or monthly energy use in manufacturing facilities and small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), often considering industry-specific characteristics and process parameters. These works highlight the potential of ML models to support energy management and efficiency improvements in industrial sectors.

Within the steel industry, forecasting of electricity consumption has attracted growing interest. Early work often used regression or traditional neural networks to model energy use at aggregated time scales. More recent studies focus on plant- or process-level predictions. For instance, some researchers have combined linear regression with k-nearest neighbors to estimate power demand and load type in steel production, while others have used neural networks to model the electricity requirements of electric-arc furnaces or other major aggregates21. There are also case studies on short-term consumption forecasting for smelting and other energy-intensive processes22. These contributions confirm the importance of load forecasting in steel enterprises, but they differ considerably in terms of model choice, feature design, and evaluation methodology23.

Summary and research gap

In summary, existing studies show substantial progress in STLF, ranging from classical statistical models through machine-learning techniques to advanced deep-learning architectures such as LSTM and BiLSTM. Comprehensive reviews emphasize that no single approach consistently outperforms all others; the relative merits of different models depend on data characteristics, available explanatory variables, and the forecasting horizon. For steel enterprises, though several works have introduced ML and DL models for energy-consumption prediction, many of them concentrate on aggregated or long-term horizons, employ varying preprocessing routines, or examine only one modeling paradigm.

There is therefore still a lack of systematic comparative analyses that evaluate multiple model families—tree-based ensembles, unidirectional LSTM, and BiLSTM—on the same hourly plant-level dataset under a unified pipeline for preprocessing, feature construction, and evaluation. In particular, the joint use of simple statistical preprocessing (three-sigma outlier detection plus time-based interpolation), basic calendar indicators, and multi-model comparison has rarely been investigated for steel-plant electricity-consumption forecasting. This paper aims to fill this gap.

Data set partitioning

Because time-series data exhibit temporal dependence, random splitting may lead to information leakage between training and testing. Therefore, we partition the cleaned hourly series chronologically into three subsets with a 70%/15%/15% ratio for training, validation, and testing, respectively. The training set is used to fit the model parameters; the validation set supports hyperparameter tuning and early-stopping decisions; and the test set, which corresponds to the last part of the time series, provides an unbiased estimate of out-of-sample performance. XGBoost model

XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) is an ensemble method that constructs a set of regression trees and adds them sequentially. Suppose the final model consists of (K) trees fk(⋅); the prediction for the i-th sample is the sum of all tree outputs, as in Eq. (6). Each tree partitions the feature space and approximates the target with a constant value in each leaf, while the ensemble combines many such trees to approximate complex nonlinear functions. In our forecasting scenario, the feature vector xt is composed of normalized load values from the previous 24 h, possibly together with calendar features. LSTM model

Conventional RNNs struggle to capture long-term dependencies because gradients tend to vanish or explode as sequences lengthen. LSTM networks address this problem by introducing memory cells and gating mechanisms that regulate information flow across time steps. Given the input vector xt, the previous hidden state ht−1, and the previous cell state ct−1, an LSTM unit updates its internal states through the forget gate, input gate, candidate cell state, cell-state update, and output gate, as shown in Eqs. The sigmoid activation σ(⋅)and the hyperbolic tangent function tanh(⋅)are used in the gates and candidate states, while element-wise multiplication ⊙controls how much information is passed forward. Owing to the nearly linear update of the cell state, gradients can propagate over long horizons, enabling the network to retain relevant information.

BiLSTM model

Standard LSTM processes the sequence in a single forward direction, capturing mainly the influence of past information on the current state. However, in many sequence modeling tasks, the representation at the current time step can also be influenced by “future contextual information.” The Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory network (BiLSTM) addresses this limitation by adding a backward LSTM alongside the forward LSTM. The two networks encode the time series in forward and reverse directions, and their hidden outputs are then concatenated to fuse past and future context, yielding a richer and more informative temporal representation.

Benefiting from the simultaneous use of both “past” and “near-future” information within the time window, BiLSTM is able to capture a more comprehensive temporal dependency structure compared with the unidirectional LSTM. This advantage is particularly evident when the load sequence exhibits strong periodicity or localized patterns.

In summary, XGBoost excels at modeling nonlinear relationships between input features and load through additive ensembles of regression trees, second-order gradient optimization, and structural regularization. LSTM, through its memory cell and gating mechanisms, effectively mitigates the gradient vanishing problem inherent in traditional RNNs and is capable of capturing long-term dependencies in load sequences. Building upon this, BiLSTM further incorporates a bidirectional structure that fuses contextual information from both forward and backward directions within the time window, thereby enhancing the richness of temporal feature representations. A comparative evaluation of these three models on the same dataset provides valuable insight into the performance advantages and applicability of deep recurrent neural networks relative to traditional tree-based models in short-term power load forecasting tasks.

Conclusion

This paper conducted a comparative study of three representative models—XGBoost, LSTM, and BiLSTM—for hourly power consumption forecasting in a steel enterprise. A unified preprocessing pipeline was applied, including three-sigma–based outlier detection, time-axis linear interpolation, and chronological partitioning into training, validation, and test sets. On this basis, a 24-hour sliding-window forecasting framework was implemented for all three models, and their performance was evaluated using RMSE, MAE, and MAPE, complemented by visual comparison of prediction curves.

The empirical results show that all three models are able to capture the daily cyclical characteristics of the steel plant’s electricity consumption, but their forecasting accuracy differs significantly. Among them, the BiLSTM model achieves the best overall performance, with the lowest RMSE, MAE, and MAPE, indicating superior capability in modeling both absolute and relative error aspects. XGBoost attains competitive results and effectively tracks the overall trend, while the unidirectional LSTM exhibits larger relative errors, especially in low-load intervals, and shows noticeable underfitting in some peak–valley segments. Overall, the study demonstrates that, under a unified experimental framework, the bidirectional recurrent neural network (BiLSTM) is more suitable than the other two models for short-term hourly power consumption forecasting in the examined steel enterprise.

The comparative results obtained in this study have both practical and methodological implications. Practically, the superior performance of BiLSTM indicates that exploiting bidirectional temporal dependencies within a 24-hour input window can significantly improve forecasting accuracy for plant-level industrial loads. For steel enterprises, more accurate short-term power consumption forecasts can support refined production scheduling, peak-shaving strategies, contract capacity management, and participation in demand response programs. XGBoost, with its simpler implementation and higher feature-level interpretability, provides a robust and practical alternative or benchmark model when deep learning expertise or computational resources are limited. In contrast, the unidirectional LSTM, although conceptually straightforward, does not deliver sufficient performance gains over BiLSTM and XGBoost in this case and is therefore less attractive as a primary forecasting model.

Methodologically, this work underscores the importance of a unified preprocessing and evaluation framework when comparing different model families. The use of three-sigma outlier detection, time-based interpolation, and chronological data splitting helps ensure that performance differences mainly reflect model characteristics rather than inconsistencies in data handling. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged: the analysis relies on data from a single steel enterprise and a single year; the feature set is kept relatively simple, focusing on historical load and calendar information; and the comparison is restricted to one-step-ahead forecasting and three model types. Future research could consider multi-step forecasting horizons, incorporate richer process and environmental variables, and investigate more advanced architectures such as CNN–RNN hybrids, attention-based models, or transformer-based forecasters, as well as integrate cost-sensitive metrics and explainable AI techniques to better align forecasting models with the operational decision-making needs of steel enterprises.
