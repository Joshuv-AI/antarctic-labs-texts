A promising bacterium-based strategy to control dengue transmission in mosquitoes
Authors: Xiumei Song, Li Gao, Jingwen Wang
Published: 2024

Main text

Mosquito-borne viral diseases are caused by viruses transmitted by various mosquito species, including dengue, chikungunya, Zika, Japanese encephalitis, and West Nile fever. Among these diseases, dengue has become the fastest spreading, with reported cases increasing approximately 10-fold from 0.5 million in 2000 to 5.2 million in 2019.1 Due to the lack of effective vaccines and treatments, prevention efforts rely mainly on insecticide-based mosquito control. However, the rise of insecticide-resistant mosquitoes poses a major challenge to mosquito control efforts. Additionally, mosquito-borne diseases tend to exhibit seasonal patterns, with higher transmission rates during warmer, wetter months, when mosquito populations are more prevalent. Globalization and climate change have expanded the geographic range of mosquito habitats and altered disease transmission capabilities, complicating control efforts. Recently, gene-drive-based mosquito modifications have shown promise in lab settings for controlling pathogen transmission. However, before being deployed in the field, a comprehensive risk assessment is necessary to evaluate the safety and efficacy of these methods, considering the potential for unforeseen traits in genetically modified mosquitoes, unintended spread of the transgene across species, and unknown long-term environmental effects. Hence, there is an urgent need to develop effective mosquito control strategies.

Flaviviruses, including dengue virus (DENV), Japanese encephalitis virus, West Nile virus, and yellow fever virus, are positive-sense, single-stranded RNA viruses. For these viruses to be transmitted successfully among hosts by a mosquito, it must first colonize the midgut, then migrate into the hemolymph, enter the salivary glands, and ultimately be transmitted to a new host during blood feeding.2 The midgut is also home to diverse microbiota, which have been demonstrated to play a crucial role in regulating the microenvironment of the midgut by modulating the midgut’s physical barrier, immune reactivity, and metabolic status.3 Understanding the tripartite interactions between viruses, gut microbiota, and mosquitoes will facilitate the development of effective approaches for vector control.

The research conducted by Zhang et al. represents a significant advancement in our understanding of the influence of microbiota-mediated alteration in the mosquito midgut microenvironment on flavivirus transmission (Figure 1A).4 Previously, the laboratory identified that two proteins, SmEnhancin and lipase, secreted by commensal bacteria in the midguts of colony Aedes aegypti inhibit DENV infection.2 In this study, the researchers focused on characterizing the roles of gut commensal bacteria in field mosquitoes in Yunnan, China, where dengue is endemic. Next, they discovered that the bacterial secretion contains antiviral effectors. Based on the size partition results of the bacterial secretion, they postulated that bacteria-secreted proteins play a role in viral antagonization. To identify the key antiviral protein(s), we expressed the proteins with the highest scores from mass spectrometry analysis and administered them individually to Aedes mosquitoes to evaluate their ability to suppress the virus. Glucose dehydrogenase (RyGDH) was found to significantly reduce DENV2 infection. Colonization with Rosenbergiella sp. YN46 reduced the pH of the mosquito midgut, and a reduction in midgut pH inhibited viral invasion into the midgut epithelial cells (Figure 1A). The researchers administered polyclonal antibodies against recombinant RyGDH to mosquitoes and observed that the antiviral activity was inhibited. Similarly, oral administration of an RyGDH-deletion mutant strain (ΔRyGDH-Rosenbergiella_YN46) failed to inhibit viral infection. Collectively, the results indicate that the commensal bacterium Rosenbergiella sp. YN46 reduces midgut pH through the secretion of GDH, thereby inhibiting the infectivity of DENV (Figure 1A), although the effect of reduced pH on the conformation of the DENV E protein remains to be elucidated and requires further investigation.

The incidence of dengue in regions with similar geographical and climatic environments varies considerably in Yunnan. Zhang et al. postulated that this discrepancy may be attributed to variations in gut microbiota in mosquitoes. By analyzing the prevalence of Rosenbergiella sp. YN46 in field A. albopictus collected from the two dengue endemic regions (Xishuangbanna and Lincang) and two non-endemic regions (Wenshan and Puer), the researchers found that the higher prevalence of Rosenbergiella sp. YN46 in mosquitoes corresponded to the lower reported dengue incidences. However, investigations of the GDH activity in mosquitoes from areas with a high prevalence of dengue fever are still lacking. Whether other bacteria in mosquitoes from non-endemic areas, in addition to Rosenbergiella, also secrete GDH remains unknown and needs to be addressed in the future.

Considering the high antiviral activity and spreading efficiency of Rosenbergiella sp. YN46, the authors conducted a semi-field intervention assay to examine the potential of this bacterium for dengue control. They introduced Rosenbergiella sp. YN46 into A. albopictus, which is permissive to DENV, in a large cage in the field, called a “mosquito sphere,” which simulates the natural habitat of mosquitoes, and they examined the infection outcome of DENV. The researchers observed that Rosenbergiella sp. YN46 effectively colonized the mosquitoes at all life stages and significantly reduced DENV2 infection (Figure 1B).

In light of all this, the ability of Rosenbergiella sp. YN46 to disrupt viral entry through acidifying mosquito midgut opens up new possibilities for developing innovative biocontrol strategies against mosquito-borne diseases. Before the discovery of Rosenbergiella sp. YN46, another bacterium, Wolbachia, had gained much attention because of its ability to inhibit both the mosquito population and DENV infection. Aedes mosquitoes that are manually transinfected with Wolbachia have been released in multiple sites around the world, and preliminary results indicate that this approach may be effective in dengue control.5 Nevertheless, the long-term effect of Wolbachia-based dengue control remains unknown because of the Wolbachia-mediated fitness cost in mosquitoes. In addition, the potential effect of these organisms on the environment also needs to be considered. In contrast, Rosenbergiella is an indigenous bacterium, and its colonization has no deleterious effect on Aedes mosquitoes. Therefore, this bacterium is unlikely to exert significant selection pressures on mosquitoes. In contrast to the labor-intensive approach of releasing Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes, Zhang et al. demonstrated that Rosenbergiella_YN46 can be effectively disseminated in field mosquitoes through vertical and transstadial transmission. Consequently, the Rosenbergiella-based strategy emphasizes the potential for sustainable and long-term control of DENV transmission. Furthermore, this bacterium is commonly found in plant nectar and is a common member of the insect microbiota. This may help to allay public safety concerns about the Rosenbergiella-based dengue control strategy. However, a comprehensive risk assessment is still required.

Although the findings of this study are promising, further research is required to elucidate the broader ecological implications of introducing Rosenbergiella sp. YN46 into natural mosquito populations. For example, the potential ecological effects of this bacterium on non-target organisms, such as beneficial insects and mammals, remain to be addressed. The stability of Rosenbergiella sp. YN46 colonization in mosquitoes is determined by multiple factors, including mosquito-Rosenbergiella interactions, Rosenbergiella-gut microbiota interactions, and non-biotic environmental factors. Although Rosenbergiella can effectively spread in A. albopictus populations, as evidenced by the semi-field study, further research is required to discover the stability of the symbiosis, the efficiency in virus inhibition, and the scalability of biocontrol applications in diverse climatic and environmental conditions across multiple sites. Nevertheless, the discovery of Rosenbergiella sp. YN46 as a potential biocontrol agent represents a significant step toward the development of sustainable and environmentally friendly strategies for combating mosquito-borne diseases. A strategy based on Rosenbergiella may be used in conjunction with a strategy based on Wolbachia to enhance the control of mosquito-borne viral diseases.
