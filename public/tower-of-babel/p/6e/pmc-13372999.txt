A new classification system of superior and inferior gemelli muscles: a cadaveric study
Authors: Nicol Zielinska, George Triantafyllou, Andrzej Borowski, Bartosz Gonera, Bartłomiej Szewczyk, Adrian Balcerzak, Łukasz Olewnik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe superior and inferior gemellus muscles are small components of the deep gluteal region that contribute to lateral rotation and stabilization of the hip joint. Despite their functional importance, these muscles display notable morphological variability with potential clinical relevance. The aim of this study was to classify the morphological variations of the superior and inferior gemellus muscles observed during anatomical dissection.Materials and methodsA total of 60 lower limbs (28 male lower limbs and 32 female lower limbs; 30 right and 30 left) from 30 cadavers of a Central European population, fixed in a 10% formalin solution, were examined.ResultsUpon dissection, the morphology of the superior and inferior gemellus muscles was assessed. Based on their course and the number of muscle bellies, four types of the superior gemellus muscle and three types of the inferior gemellus muscle were identified. The superior gemellus muscle was present in 86.7% of cases and the following types were distinguished: Type 1 (65%) was characterized by a single belly proximally attached to the external surface of the ischial spine and distally attached to the trochanteric fossa. Type 2 (10%) consisted of a double belly arising as a common muscular mass from the external surface of the ischial spine; the upper belly was distally fused with the piriformis tendon and attached to the tip of the greater trochanter, whereas the lower belly was distally attached to the trochanteric fossa of the femur. Type 3 (11.7%) presented as a single belly proximally attached to the external surface of the ischial spine and fusing with the piriformis muscle tendon. Their common tendon was distally attached to the tip of the greater trochanter. Type 4 (13.3%) was characterized by the absence of the superior gemellus muscle. The inferior gemellus muscle was present in 80% of cases and following types were identified: Type I (75%) consisted of a single belly proximally attached to the upper part of the ischial tuberosity and distally attached to the trochanteric fossa. Type II (5%) showed a double belly proximally attached to the upper part of the ischial tuberosity, fusing into a common muscular mass and distally attached to the trochanteric fossa. Type III (20%) – characterized by the absence of the inferior gemellus muscle.ConclusionsThe superior and inferior gemellus muscles demonstrate considerable morphological variability. Knowledge of these variations is essential for clinicians involved in diagnostic imaging, surgical procedures, and the management of neurovascular compression syndromes.

Introduction

The group of thigh muscles responsible for lateral rotation consists of the piriformis (Pm), superior gemellus muscle (SGm), inferior gemellus muscle (IGm), obturator externus muscle (OEm), obturator internus muscle (OIm), and quadratus femoris muscle (QFm) [16]. The SGm is proximally attached to the external surface of the ischial spine, in turn the IGm arises from the upper part of the ischial tuberosity. Both muscles are distally attached to the trochanteric fossa of the femur (usually fusing with the OIm tendon (OIt)). These muscles are innervated by nerves to QFm (nQF) and OIm (nOI) (L5, S1) [8, 12].

The SGm and the IGm play a key role in externally rotating the thigh when the hip is extended. They also assist in abducting the thigh during hip flexion and contribute to maintaining hip joint stability by securing the femoral head within the acetabulum [8, 12].

Notable anatomical variations can be observed within these muscles. Although extremely uncommon, either the SGm or IGm may be absent, while the simultaneous absence of both is considered exceptionally rare. In some individuals, duplication of one or both muscles has been reported [6, 10, 14]. Additionally, the SGm has been shown to occasionally blend with the Pm or gluteus minimus muscle, whereas the IGm may merge with the QFm [8].

The observed variability of these muscles is supported by their embryological development: the SGm and IGm, together with the OIm, arise from a common muscle anlage on the ischial side of the hip joint, which is not fully differentiated until an embryonic length of approximately 20 mm. This delayed differentiation allows for a wide range of morphological variation, including absence, duplication, or fusion with adjacent muscles [7].

To evaluate the clinical relevance of these anatomical variations, it is essential to consider their location and morphological characteristics. Accessory muscles or structures are often associated with potential neurovascular compression. In some cases [2] duplicated SGm and Pm, with the sciatic nerve (Sn) traversing between the two heads of the Pm, may predispose individuals to piriformis syndrome, which manifests as persistent pain in the buttock and hip region, occasionally radiating along the path of the Sn [11].

However, most textbook descriptions of the SGm and IGm are based on a small number of cases and do not reflect the wide variation seen in the general population. Reports of these variations are rare and usually come from case studies or small cadaveric series. There is no systematic classification of these muscles. Because of this, the “normal” anatomy shown in textbooks may not represent most people. This lack of detailed information makes it harder to understand deep gluteal anatomy and can create difficulties for clinical assessment, imaging, and surgical planning.

The aim of this study is to present a detailed anatomical classification of both the SGm and the IGm based on their morphological variations observed during anatomical dissection. By systematizing these variants, the study seeks to enhance anatomical understanding of the deep gluteal region. Such knowledge is essential for clinicians involved in diagnostic imaging, surgical procedures, and the management of neurovascular compression syndromes.

Materials and methods

A total of 60 lower limbs (28 male lower limbs and 32 female lower limbs; 30 right and 30 left), representing paired limbs from 30 cadavers of a Central European population, fixed in 10% formalin solution, were analyzed. The average age at death was 72.5 years. The cadavers were donated to the university’s anatomical donation program and were under the care of the Department of Anatomical Dissection and Donation. Specimens with any indication of surgical alteration in the target region were omitted. Dissection procedures for the hip and thigh strictly followed a standardized protocol.

Dissection protocol

The dissection process commenced with the removal of the skin and superficial fascia from the posterior region of the hip and thigh. This allowed for the visualization of the gluteus maximus muscle, which was subsequently detached from its attachments to expose the underlying gluteus medius and the Pm. Once the gluteus medius was cleaned, the Pm was carefully dissected, and adjacent anatomical structures were examined.

Following this, the dissection proceeded to identify and isolate the SGm. After locating its superior and inferior borders, the OIm was exposed by gently reflecting the overlying fascial plane. The IGm was revealed by continuing the dissection inferiorly, taking care to preserve the surrounding neurovascular structures. Particular attention was given to the relationships among these muscles and the Sn, which runs in close proximity to the Pm and can be affected by any variations in the muscle’s structure. The positioning of the Sn relative to the muscles of the hip region was carefully mapped to ensure accurate anatomical understanding and avoid damage to the nerve.

Thorough documentation of the spatial relationships among these muscles and neurovascular elements, particularly the Sn, is essential due to their variability and potential clinical relevance in conditions such as piriformis syndrome and deep gluteal syndrome.

The morphometric measurements included the proximal and distal attachment width and thickness, as well as the length of each muscle belly and tendon, and width and thickness of myotendinous junction. These measurements are presented separately for the SGm (Table 2) and the IGm (Table 3).

All measurements were performed using an electronic digital caliper (Mitutoyo Corporation, Kawasaki-shi, Kanagawa, Japan). Each measurement was performed twice to ensure reproducibility: intra-observer measurements were repeated by the same investigator, and inter-observer measurements were performed independently by two investigators. The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Lodz (resolution RNN/297/17/KE). The cadavers belonged to the Department of Anatomical Dissection and Donation of the Medical University of Lodz, Poland.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for MacOS, Version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, United States). Nominal data between unpaired observations were compared using the Chi-square test, while McNemar’s test was applied for paired observations. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were analyzed based on measurement type: unpaired measurements were evaluated with an independent t-test if normality was met; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. A paired t-test was employed for paired measurements when normality was satisfied. Mean comparisons across more than two groups involved one-way ANOVA if the data were normally distributed; if not, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used. The Spearman correlation was used to investigate possible associations between morphological measurements. Results are presented as mean and standard deviation unless otherwise specified, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

This type was observed in 6 lower limbs (10%; 4 M, 2 F, 3 L, 3 R) (Fig. 2).Type 3 - characterized by a single belly proximally attached to the external surface of the ischial spine and fusing with the Pm tendon (Pt). From a statistical point of view, there was no statistically significant difference in SGm and IGm types between sexes or sides (Table 1). The SGm and IGm types were symmetrically identified in 24 cadavers and asymmetrically in 1 cadaver. No statistically significant predominance of SGm and IGm morphometric parameters was observed between sexes or sides; the results are summarized in Tables 2 and 3. The results of the Spearman analysis are presented in Table 4. SGm belly (SGb) proximal attachment width, SGm tendon (SGt) distal attachment width, and SGt distal attachment thickness showed positive correlation with the corresponding IGm measurements (Table 4). Moreover, the SGb and SGt length were positively correlated with the IGm belly (IGb) and IGm tendon (IGt) length (Table 4). It should be noted that the SGm second head and IGm second head were observed in a small sample, which did not allow for statistical analysis (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4).

Discussion

In the current study, we assessed the morphological variability of both SGm and IGm, while we proposed a novel classification system for both muscles based on their variants. This is the most important outcome of the current study because these two muscles, despite their clinical significance, have been barely studied in the current literature [8].

To understand adequately the morphological variability of the hip musculature, their embryological background is essential. The SGm and IGm belonged to a united group along with the OIm and the QFm on the ischial side of the hip joint’s anlage. However, the anlage of the SGm, IGm, and OIm seems to differentiate earlier than the QFm. Nevertheless, the QFm anlage is located more anterior than the other muscles it is the first to differentiate, occurring at an embryonic length of approximately 11 mm. At this stage of development, a nerve originating from the sacral plexus innervates the anlage. Until an embryonic length of 20 mm, the three muscles (SGm, IGm, and OIm) are not fully separated, and their boundaries remain indistinct [7]. This undifferentiated period provides a window during which variations such as partial fusion or atypical attachment patterns may arise. The close temporal and spatial development of these muscles, together with their shared innervation, may therefore account for the frequent anatomical variants observed in adults, including duplication of bellies, fusion with neighboring muscles, and variability in tendon formation. Understanding this embryological context helps to explain why the SGm and IGm display substantial morphological diversity and reinforces the relevance of a detailed anatomical classification.

Three main variations of the SGm were observed and classified into four types based on their proximal and distal attachments, as well as on their fusion with adjacent muscles. Accordingly, the morphological variability was classified, in decreasing frequency, as follows: a single muscle belly without (Type 1) or with fusion with the Pt (Type 3), double-bellied muscle (Type 2) and the absence (Type 4). Similarly, the IGm, which demonstrated fewer variations, was classified as: a single muscle belly (Type I), a double-bellied muscle (Type II), and complete absence (Type III). The IGm exhibited fewer variants than the SGm; however, absence of the IGm was more frequent than absence of the SGm (20% versus 13.3%). Similar to the current classification systems, both SGm and IGm could be absent, single or doubled. In contrast to the current findings, earlier reports described the IGm as being less frequently absent than the SGm. The absence of the gemelli muscles has been reported with a prevalence ranging from 0% to 12%, while the complete absence of both muscles has been considered a very rare variation and was not observed in the present study [16]. Only one previous case report described bilateral absence of both gemelli muscles leaving unfilled the deep gluteal space, which could potentially compromise the stability of a total hip replacement. This is particularly relevant when using the posterior approach for accessing the short lateral rotators of the hip [1].

Few reports of the SGm variants were previously described. Bajka et al. [6] reported both unilateral and bilateral absence. In contrast, in the present study bilateral absence was observed in 4 out of 30 cadavers (8 of 60 paired lower limbs; 6.67%). There were reports of double-bellied SGm. Arifoglu et al. [2] recorded a double SGb coexisting with a double Pm belly (Pb). We observed a two-bellied SGm in 10% of lower limbs; thus, it could be considered an infrequent variation. Previously, fusion between the SGm and Pt was identified in 29% [16] while the current study recorded a prevalence of 11.7%.

The published literature for the IGm is even more limited. Older manuscripts reported that the absence of the IGm is very rare [16], but we were able to identify it in 20% of the current sample. In the present study, 5% of lower limbs had a double-bellied IGm that was reported as an anatomical possibility into Bergman’s Comprehensive Encyclopedia of Human Anatomic Variations [16]. Zielinska et al. [17] described the presence of two accessory heads in the gluteal regions, whereas one of them seemed to be an accessory head of the IGm.

Assi et al. [4] conducted an interesting study about short external rotator muscles of the hip, in which described their morphology, insertions, anatomical relationships, and potential functional and surgical significance, emphasizing their role in dynamic hip stability and total hip arthroplasty. The authors demonstrated that both the SGm and IGm consistently insert onto the tendon of the OIm rather than directly onto the greater trochanter, forming a conjoint “hip triceps” tendon. This configuration suggests that the gemelli primarily act by stabilizing the obturator internus tendon during contraction rather than functioning as independent rotators. Furthermore, the length of the hip triceps complex showed a significant correlation with femoral neck length, indicating potential relevance for restoring soft-tissue tension during total hip arthroplasty. The authors also proposed that the consistent orientation of the conjoint tendon may serve as a reliable intraoperative landmark for anatomical repair of the posterior capsule and short external rotators [4].

Another interesting study conducted by Assi et al. [3] was about all 24 hip muscles, in which proposed a novel functional classification based on the ratio of muscle belly length to combined free and intramuscular tendon lengths, reflecting muscle compliance. Group 1 includes muscles with a ratio higher than 1.5, such as the QFm, sartorius, adductor brevis, and pectineus; Group 2 includes muscles with a ratio between 0.5 and 1.5, including the gluteus maximus, short head of the biceps femoris, Oem, gracilis, SGm and IGm, gluteus medius, psoas major, long adductor, and iliacus; and Group 3 includes muscles with a ratio lower than 0.5, such as the rectus femoris, semimembranosus, long head of the biceps femoris, tensor fasciae latae, semitendinosus, adductor magnus, psoas minor, obturator internus, gluteus minimus, and Pm. According to this system, SGm and IGm, together with the OIm, are included in an intermediate group characterized by balanced elasticity and force generation, contributing mainly to dynamic stabilization of the hip joint. This grouping suggests that muscles within the same compliance category share similar functional behavior, which may be relevant for biomechanical modeling and surgical reconstruction strategies, particularly in total hip arthroplasty [3].

The innervation patterns of the gemelli muscles present notable anatomical variability. The SGm frequently receives dual innervation from both the nOI and the nOF, with this arrangement observed in approximately 60.4% of specimens. In contrast, the IGm is predominantly innervated by the nOF, accounting for 98% of cases, while additional contribution from the nOI was identified in only 2% of cases. Communication between these two nerves is common, with shared roots or connecting branches present in 84.6% of dissections, occurring both within and outside the muscles. The nOF typically originates from the ventral branches of spinal nerves L4 to S1, whereas the nOI arises mainly from L5 to S2, with occasional input from S3, suggesting partially overlapping segmental origins. Furthermore, the study highlighted diverse intramuscular branching patterns, which may underlie individual differences in neuromuscular coordination and functional control of the hip region [5].

Anatomical variations of gemelli muscles may have clinical significance. For example, they may complicate the interpretation of MRI or CT imaging of the hip and pelvis. Unrecognized muscle variants can mimic space-occupying lesions or be misinterpreted as pathological findings. In surgical contexts - particularly in orthopedic, neurosurgical, and urological procedures - precise anatomical knowledge is essential to avoid iatrogenic injury or misdiagnosis [13].

Abnormal hypertrophy or anatomical variations of the gemelli muscles may contribute to Sn entrapment. In such cases, the clinical presentation may mimic piriformis syndrome, even in the absence of Pm involvement. Although all observed variants of the SGm were located inferior to the Sn, specific anatomical configurations—particularly Types 2 and 3—may predispose individuals to Sn entrapment or piriformis - like syndrome. In Type 2, the presence of a double-bellied SGm, with the upper belly fused to the Pt, results in a significant increase in muscular volume within the compartment, and the Sn courses between the SGm and the Pm. Similarly, Type 3, characterized by fusion with the Pt and a shared distal tendon, may alter biomechanical tension. Despite their subneural course, these variants may contribute to narrowing of the infrapiriform foramen or abnormal dynamic interactions during hip movement, potentially affecting the gliding or mechanical environment of the Sn. Such configurations could lead to chronic irritation, pain, or neuropathic symptoms mimicking classical piriformis syndrome, even in the absence of direct compression by the piriformis muscle itself [13].

Morphological variations of the IGm may also significantly influence the risk of Sn entrapment. In particular, Type II, characterized by an IGm with an additional belly has the highest potential for clinical relevance. This configuration increases muscular bulk within the infrapiriform space. In contrast, Type I, a single IGb represents the most common pattern and is less likely to contribute to pathology. Interestingly, Type III, defined by complete absence of the IGm, may reduce regional muscle volume and, consequently, the risk of mechanical impingement, however, it may also lead to functional compensation by adjacent muscles, such as the OIm or the SGm [13].

Conversely, complete absence of one or both gemellus muscles, particularly when bilateral, may lead to muscular imbalance, compensatory overuse of adjacent rotators, and biomechanical instability, potentially resulting in chronic lumbopelvic or even knee dysfunction. These anatomical variations are also crucial in interventional procedures, such as during sciatic or pudendal nerve blocks, where unrecognized muscular variants may compromise procedural accuracy or increase the risk of neurovascular injury. Thus, awareness of gemellus muscle morphology is essential not only for the differential diagnosis of deep gluteal pain syndromes but also for optimizing outcomes in surgical and anesthetic procedures involving the posterior hip region [9, 15].

A few limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. The current sample (n = 60 lower limbs from 30 cadavers) was considered adequate for this novel investigation; however, the population was restricted to a single region (Łódź, Poland), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. The main strength of the study was the systematic investigation of SGm and IGm variants and their respective prevalences, with the proposal of two classification systems. Nevertheless, functional implications of these anatomical variations were not assessed, and advanced imaging or 3D documentation of nerve relationships was not available. Future studies should aim to correlate these morphological findings with distal attachments, innervation patterns, and biomechanical or clinical outcomes to provide a more comprehensive understanding of SGm and IGm anatomy.

Conclusions

The SGm and IGm demonstrate considerable morphological variability, including differences in presence, number of bellies, and fusion with adjacent muscles. These variations should be considered in anatomical studies and when planning procedures in the gluteal region.
