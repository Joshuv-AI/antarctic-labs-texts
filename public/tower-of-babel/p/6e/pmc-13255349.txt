Predictive factors for forgotten joints after direct anterior approach total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Xingao Pu, Hong Yu, Yuxuan Hu, Yifan Yang, Zhijiong Lin, Chengyue Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch indicates that the Direct Anterior Approach (DAA) for Total Hip Replacement (THA) may achieve a higher degree of joint oblivion compared to other approaches. However, the factors predicting postoperative joint awareness following DAA-THA remain unclear.MethodsIn this retrospective research, 165 patients with hip osteoarthritis who underwent DAA-THA at our hospital between 2020 and 2022 were selected, with a minimum postoperative follow-up of 2 years. Patients were categorized into the Forgotten Joint Group and Non-Forget Joint Group based on their response to the Patients’ Joint Perception (PJP) anchor question. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed using preoperative and intraoperative factors as potential predictors. Postoperative patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were excluded from the final multivariate model to avoid circularity.ResultsA total of 165 patients were included in the data analysis. The Forgotten Joint Group comprised 29 patients, and the Non-Forgotten Joint Group 136 patients. Preoperative radiographic severity(Kellgren-Lawrence grade), bone mass, and change in femoral offset (ΔFO) showed significant difference (P < 0.001).No significant differences were observed in gender, age, surgical side, BMI, albumin, smoking, drink, ASA grade, or VAS score (P > 0.05). According to the multivariate logistic regression model, preoperative radiographic severity was associated with “Forgotten Joint” [OR = 1.720 (95% CI:1.110–2.696), p = 0.002], Osteoporosis was associated with a lower likelihood of “Forgotten Joint” [OR = 0.205 (95% CI: 0.075–0.523), p = 0.001] and ΔFO was positively associated with “Forgotten Joint” within a certain range [OR = 1.520 (95% CI: 1.225–1.891), p = 0.001].ConclusionThis study indicates that poorer preoperative radiographic scores were associated with lower joint awareness, whereas osteoporosis was associated with higher joint awareness. Furthermore, a greater femoral offset reconstruction within the normal range was associated with achieving a forgotten joint.

The direct anterior approach (DAA) has gained increasing popularity among surgeons in recent years as an alternative approach for hip arthroplasty. Studies have demonstrated that DAA-THA yields superior postoperative Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) compared to PA-THA [1–2], meaning patients are more likely to achieve a “forgotten joint.” As the ideal outcome in joint replacement surgery, the “forgotten joint” requires creating a pain-free, functionally normal hip for the patient [3]. Only then can the patient genuinely forget their artificial joint, signaling to the brain that it is a “natural, healthy joint.” However, the factors predicting the forgotten joint after DAA-THA remain unclear. The purpose of this study is to explore the factors associated with the forgotten joint after DAA-THA by analyzing the relationship between the forgotten joint score and demographic characteristics, surgical data, and radiographic parameters.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at our institution between January 2020 to January 2022. The study included 165 patients who underwent primary unilateral total hip arthroplasty for primary hip osteoarthritis and met the following inclusion criteria: 1. Primary unilateral THA; 2. diagnosed of primary hip osteoarthritis; 3. complete clinical data and willingness to attend follow-up appointments. Exclusion criteria: 1. History of prior hip surgery before initial replacement; 2.Severe psychiatric or psychological disorders, or intellectual disability; 3. Inability to complete follow-up on schedule or incomplete clinical data. Baseline patient data (age, gender, BMI, surgical side; ASA classification, operative duration, albumin levels, etc.) were collected from medical records. Osteoporosis was defined according to World Health Organization criteria based on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) measurements, with a T-score ≤ − 2.5 at the lumbar spine or proximal femur. All patients underwent a minimum 2-year follow-up. At baseline and 2 years postoperatively, all patients underwent weight-bearing full-leg X-rays and anteroposterior and lateral X-ray films of the acetabulum. Two investigators independently measured the following parameters:1.Femoral offset (FO): distance from the center of rotation of the femoral head to a line that bisects the long axis of the femur. The change in femoral offset (ΔFO) was calculated as postoperative FO minus preoperative FO.2.Lower limb length discrepancy (LLD): difference in distance from the ischial tuberosity to the most prominent medial point of the lesser trochanter.Interobserver reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with values > 0.85 considered excellent.Follow-up data were collected via questionnaires administered during outpatient visits or telephone interviews at 2 years postoperatively. The following instruments were used:1.Patients’ Joint Perception (PJP) [4]: anchor question: “How do you perceive your artificial joint in daily life?” Response options: (1) Like a natural joint; (2) Sometimes I feel it; (3) Often I feel it; (4) Always feel it. Patients answering “Like a natural joint” were classified into the Forgotten Joint Group; all others into the Non-Forgotten Joint Group; 2. Forgotten Joint Score-12 (FJS-12); 3. Harris Hip Score (HHS) [5].

Surgical procedure

The procedure was performed by the same orthopedic surgeon using tha DAA approach. Patient positioned in the lateral decubitus position under general or epidural anesthesia. A standardized DAA was performed: an incision was made starting 2 cm below the anterior superior iliac spine and extending distally. Dissection proceeded through the interval between the sartorius and tensor fasciae latae muscles. The joint capsule was exposed and incised in an inverted “T” pattern. After femoral head osteotomy and acetabular preparation, uncemented components were implanted. The femoral offset was restored to approximate the contralateral side. The anterior capsule was repaired, and the incision was closed in layers.

Postoperative care

Postoperative antibiotics and anticoagulation were administered according to protocol. Early rehabilitation, including hip abduction and extension exercises, was initiated under the guidance of physiotherapists. Patients were encouraged to walk with a walker as tolerated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent t-tests. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Univariate logistic regression was performed to identify potential predictors of forgotten joint status. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis and those deemed clinically relevant were included in a multivariate logistic regression model. To assess multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for all independent variables. A VIF>5 was considered indicative of significant collinearity requiring corrective measures. To avoid circularity, postoperative FJS-12 and HHS were excluded from the final model. The model’s discriminatory ability was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and its derived from the final multivariable model and reflects its predictive performance. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated based on the optimal Youden index. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the association between PJP and FJS-12/HHS, providing ancillary support for the grouping scheme. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated for radiographic measurements. The relationship between the change in femoral offset (ΔFO) and the Forgotten Joint Score-12 (FJS-12) was examined using Pearson correlation analysis. Normality was confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) and corresponding two-tailed P-value were reported. A scatter plot with a fitted regression line was constructed to visualize the association. All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 165 patients were included in the final data analysis. Of these, 29 patients were classified into the Forgotten Joint Group, while 136 were assigned to the Non-Forgotten Joint Group. The ROC curve demonstrated good discriminatory ability for the assessment (Fig. 1). Among the preoperative and intraoperative baseline variables compared between the two groups, the Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade exhibited a statistically significant difference. In the Forgotten Joint Group, 14 individuals (48.3%) were classified as KL grade 1, 11 (37.9%) as KL grade 2, and 4 (13.8%) as KL grade 3. In contrast, the Non-Forgotten Joint Group included 21 individuals (15.4%) with KL grade 1, 71 (52.2%) with KL grade 2, and 44 (32.4%) with KL grade 3 (P < 0.001). Regarding bone mineral density, 19 patients (65.5%) in the Forgotten Joint Group had normal bone mass, while 10 (34.5%) had osteoporosis. In the Non-Forgotten Joint Group, 48 patients (35.3%) presented with normal bone mass and 88 (64.7%) with osteoporosis (P = 0.003). The mean femoral offset was 7.61 ± 6.39 mm in the Forgotten Joint Group and − 1.72 ± 5.48 mm in the Non-Forgotten Joint Group, representing a significant intergroup difference (P < 0.001). The Forgotten Joint Group also demonstrated significantly higher FJS-12 scores (83.41 ± 5.36 vs. 74.77 ± 4.46, P < 0.001) and postoperative Harris Hip Score (HHS) values (81.07 ± 4.17 vs.73.24 ± 4.51, P < 0.001) compared to the Non-Forgotten Joint Group. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to gender, age, surgical side, body mass index (BMI), albumin levels, smoking status, alcohol consumption, ASA grade, or preoperative VAS score (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Multivariate logistic regression

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients between the PJP and the FJS-12 and HHS scores were 0.406 and 0.425, respectively (both P < 0.001), suggesting a moderate association between the anchor question and continuous joint awareness measures. The AUC of the final model was 0.800, indicating good discriminatory ability (Fig. 1). Following analysis, several factors most closely associated with the “Forgotten Joint” were selected for univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. According to the multivariate logistic regression model, preoperative radiographic severity emerged as a factor positively associated with a “Forgotten Joint” [OR = 1.720 (95% CI:1.110–2.696), p = 0.002], whereas osteoporosis was associated with a lower likelihood [OR = 0.205 (95% CI: 0.075–0.523), p = 0.001], and femoral offset (ΔFO) demonstrated a positive association with the “Forgotten Joint” within a certain range OR = 1.520 (95% CI: 1.225–1.891), p = 0.001. A significant positive correlation was observed between ΔFO and FJS-12 (Pearson’s r = 0.22, P = 0.001), indicating that greater increases in femoral offset were associated with higher FJS-12 scores (Fig. 2) (Table 2).

Discussion

Total hip replacement (THA), as one of the most successful orthopedic procedures, has been established as the preferred treatment for hip osteoarthritis, effectively improving hip function and alleviating pain [6]. Over time, joint replacement techniques have continuously evolved. In 2012, Professor Behrend and colleagues introduced the “Forgotten Joint” concept, proposing that the ultimate goal of joint arthroplasty is to enable the artificial joint to function painlessly and naturally, resembling a native joint. To support this concept, they developed the Forgotten Joint Score (FJS), a core assessment tool considered capable of distinguishing between good and excellent outcomes, particularly in scenarios where other scoring systems are limited by ceiling effects [7]. As the “forgotten joint” concept gained increasing clinical prominence, the FJS has been continuously applied and validated. Versions in multiple languages have been progressively developed, establishing the FJS as an essential instrument for the clinical evaluation of arthroplasty outcomes [8–10].

The direct anterior approach is currently a widely favored minimally invasive technique for hip arthroplasty, regarded as a true “neuromuscular-sparing” approach that aligns more closely with the principles of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) [11]. Studies indicate that, compared to traditional surgical approaches, the DAA offers advantages in reducing tissue trauma and dislocation rates [12, 13]. Kamath et al. found that patients undergoing the DAA had a nearly 20% higher rate of discharge to home compared to those undergoing other approaches [14]. Thaler et al. observed that DAA patients exhibited significantly different gait patterns compared to those undergoing anterolateral approaches at the two-year postoperative follow-up, a finding attributed to the muscle-preserving nature of the DAA [15]. Nevertheless, the predictive factors for joint awareness following DAA-THA remain unclear. The objective of this study is to analyse the factors associated with joint awareness after DAA-THA, thereby providing clinical guidance.

Research indicates that patients with higher preoperative radiographic scores may experience smaller postoperative improvements [16]. Valdes et al. suggest that patients undergoing THA with lower preoperative radiographic osteoarthritis (OA) severity are more likely to experience severe pain following THA [17]. In another study, Keurentjes et al. [18] reported that patients with severe radiographic OA had a significantly higher probability of achieving the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) in physical function within 2 to 5 years following THA. Our findings are consistent with these results, suggesting an inverse association between preoperative radiographic severity and residual joint awareness [19, 20].

Restoration of femoral offset (ΔFO) is crucial for functional outcomes and implant longevity following hip arthroplasty [21, 22]. Rüdiger et al. demonstrated that a 20% reduction in FO correlates with an average 15% decrease in abduction torque [23]. Similarly, a prospective study by Sarwar et al. reported comparable findings: an analysis of 222 patients undergoing THA for osteoarthritis revealed that an FO reduction exceeding 5 mm appeared negatively correlated with abductor muscle strength [24]. Furthermore, research has identified inadequate restoration of femoral offset as a risk factor for postoperative dislocation [25]. Driesman et al. observed that excessively small FO may cause impingement syndrome, while overly large offsets could lead to greater trochanteric prominence, potentially increasing lateral hip pain [26]. Clement et al. observed that increasing femoral offset within a certain range improved functional outcomes [27]. Our findings are consistent with these results, indicating that, within a defined range, increased ΔFO is associated with better patient-reported function.However, the correlation between ΔFO and FJS-12 was modest (r = 0.22), indicating that while offset restoration contributes to joint awareness, other factors also play substantial roles.

Our research indicates that osteoporosis is associated with higher joint awareness, which is consistent with the findings of Alexander et al. [28]. Studies demonstrate that patients with osteoporosis experience adverse events more frequently than those with normal bone density; Layson et al. observed a higher incidence of aseptic loosening of prostheses postoperatively in osteoporotic patients [29]. Another study identified a higher likelihood of postoperative periprosthetic fractures in osteoporotic patients [30]. However, Meyer et al. observed comparable improvements in reported outcomes between osteoporotic and non-osteoporotic cohorts [31]. In summary, the relationship between osteoporosis and reported outcomes in patients following arthroplasty remains inconclusive.

Since Lewinnek proposed the concept of the “safe zone” numerous studies have explored the acetabular component safe zone [32–34]. However, this study found no evidence that the acetabular component positioning influenced the “Forgotten Joint”. Lower limb length discrepancy (LLD) following THA is a known cause of patient dissatisfaction [35, 36]; yet, no difference in LLD was observed among the patients included in this study. Previous research has suggested that smoking, obesity, osteoporosis, and preoperative smoking may be associated with low FJS scores following THA [37–39]. However, we did not identify any statistically significant correlations between these factors and the outcomes examined. This may be attributable to the small sample size and the relatively short follow-up period, which may have been insufficient to detect such differences.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, particularly the forgotten joint group (n = 29), which may affect the stability of the regression model and limit generalizability. Second, the single-center, single-surgeon design may introduce selection bias and limit external validity. Third, the follow-up period of 2 years may be insufficient to capture long-term changes in joint perception or implant survival. Fourth, although we excluded postoperative PROMs from the final predictive model, the inherent subjectivity of patient-reported outcomes remains a limitation. Furthermore, the binary grouping based on a single PJP anchor question, although supported by validation studies, is inherently sensitive to the chosen cutoff and does not fully reflect the ordinal nature of the response. Fifth, potential ceiling effects of the FJS-12 at 2 years may have influenced the results. Finally, unmeasured confounders such as preoperative activity level, psychological factors, or detailed gait analysis could not be accounted for.Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into the factors associated with forgotten joint after DAA-THA. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings and explore additional factors.

Conclusions

This study suggests that poorer preoperative radiographic scores are associated with lower joint awareness after DAA-THA, whereas osteoporosis is associated with higher joint awareness. Furthermore, a greater femoral offset reconstruction within the normal range was associated with achieving a forgotten joint.These findings may help surgeons identify patients at risk for suboptimal joint perception and guide preoperative counseling and surgical planning.
