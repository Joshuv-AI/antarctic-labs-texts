Idiopathic hypereosinophilic syndrome: Potential pathologic somatic gene variants identified by exome sequencing
Authors: Alejandro Ferrer, Mrunal Dehankar, Saurabh Baheti, Mrinal M. Patnaik, Thanai Pongdee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) is a rare group of disorders characterized by eosinophilia in blood and/or tissues. The etiology of HES is largely unknown. Characterizing the molecular pathophysiology of HES may improve diagnostic and therapeutic methodology.ObjectiveWe sought to identify somatic gene variants associated with idiopathic HES using exome sequencing.MethodsExome sequencing (SureSelect capture kit, 80× average depth) was performed using either PBMCs or bone marrow cytogenetic pellets from 31 patients with idiopathic HES. We used Mutect2 in tumor-only mode with gnomAD as the germline resource and FilterMutectCalls for confident somatic variant calling. Variants selected had at least 20 supporting reads, variant allele frequency below 35%, high Clinical Annotation of Variants impact, and minor allele frequency of <1% in gnomAD and Mayo Clinic Biobank.ResultsA total of 332 unique variants in 310 genes were identified at least in 1 patient. Of these, 5 genes were found recurrently mutated (present in at least 10% of samples): PRTFDC1 (16%), TYRO3 (16%), TDG (12.9%), TYW1B (12.9%), and ZNF880 (12.9%). Pathway enrichment analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes and Gene Ontology databases utilizing all variants identified in the cohort found significant enrichment in pathways related to cell cycle and PI3K-Akt signaling (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) and RAS signal transduction (Gene Ontology).ConclusionsSeveral somatic mutations were identified in our cohort of patients with idiopathic HES. Increasing the number of patients through collaborative efforts and pursuing functional testing of these variants will help elucidate the importance of these genes in idiopathic HES.

Introduction

Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) is a rare group of disorders characterized by elevated eosinophil levels in blood and/or tissues.1 The clinical presentations of HES are heterogeneous with affected organs being highly variable among patients. The most common presenting manifestations involve dermatologic, pulmonary, and gastrointestinal systems, with cardiac and neurologic issues occurring later. Although HES has well-established diagnostic criteria, the etiology of HES is largely unknown. The majority of HES cases are classified as idiopathic and have no defined etiology.2 Characterizing the molecular pathophysiology of idiopathic HES may improve diagnostic, therapeutic, and prognostic methodology. Most prior published studies used targeted sequencing, a very sensitive approach to identify somatic variants, but limited to explore a reduced number of genes. Here, we performed a broader exploratory investigation to identify somatic variants in genes not covered by these previous approaches using exome sequencing.

Results and discussion

The current study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board. Consecutive patients who met diagnostic criteria based on consensus guidelines1 for idiopathic HES were retrospectively identified. The presence of hypereosinophilia-related end-organ damage and/or dysfunction was based on clinical, pathologic, and/or radiologic findings. DNA was obtained from either PBMCs (n = 22) or bone marrow cytogenetic pellets (n = 9) stored at –80°C. Genomic DNA quantity and quality were assessed using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Mass) and a Fragment Analyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, Calif).

Exome sequencing (SureSelect capture kit, 80× average depth; Agilent) was performed as previously described.3 Briefly, paired-end libraries were prepared from genomic DNA using the SureSelectXT Low Input Reagent Kit (Agilent). The concentration and size distribution were determined using the Fragment Analyzer and Qubit fluorometer. Adapter-ligated DNA was amplified with the SureSelect Post-Capture Forward and specific index reverse primers for 12 cycles. Whole exon capture was carried out following the protocol for SureSelect Human All Exon v5 + UTRs 75 MB kit (Agilent) along with xGen Universal blockers TS mix (1076154; Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, Iowa). The purified capture products were then amplified using Universal forward (5′-AAT GAT ACG GCG ACC ACC GAG-3′) and reverse (5′-CAA GCA GAA GAC GGC ATA CGA G-3′) primers (Integrated DNA Technologies) for 12 cycles. The concentration and size distribution of the completed captured libraries were determined using a Qubit fluorometer and a Fragment Analyzer. Libraries were sequenced with a range of 55 to 80 million read pairs following Illumina’s standard protocol using the cBot and HiSeq 3000/4000 PE Cluster Kit (Illumina, San Diego, Calif). The flow cells were sequenced as 150 × 2 paired end reads on a HiSeq 4000 using HiSeq 3000/4000 sequencing kit and HD 3.4.0.38 collection software (Illumina). Base-calling was performed using RTA version 2.7.7 (Illumina).

As additional tissue for germline testing was not available, we relied on computational methods to predict somatic status. We used Mutect2 in tumor-only mode with gnomAD as the germline resource and FilterMutectCalls for confident somatic variant calling. Variants selected had at least 20 supporting reads, variant allele frequency below 35%, high Clinical Annotation of Variants impact4 and minor allele frequency of <1% in gnomAD and Mayo BioBank. Variants reported as benign/likely benign in ClinVar, or with CADD score below 25 and REVEL score below 0.8 were filtered. A cohort of patients with acute liver failure but similar age range and gender distribution was analyzed using the same workflow5 and used to exclude variants not specific to HES.

There were 31 patients identified (age range 27-85 years, 45% females) with a mean age at HES diagnosis of 51 years old (range 20-77) and a median peak absolute eosinophil count of 6,030/mm3 (range 1,596-57,744). This study introduces one of the largest assembled cohorts of patients with idiopathic HES to date who underwent exome sequencing. The only prior study that used exome sequencing for this purpose was performed by Andersen et al6 and included 5 patients with idiopathic HES. This group demonstrated somatic mutations in eosinophils of 3 patients in the spliceosome gene PUF60; in CDH17, which is involved in tumorigenesis; and in LMLN, which encodes a zinc-metallopeptidase. Similar to our results, the functional consequences of these mutations are unknown in terms of hypereosinophilia.

All other published studies used high-depth targeted sequencing that did not include these genes or some of the ones identified in our cohort. Pardanani et al7 investigated 98 patients with idiopathic HES or idiopathic hypereosinophilia using targeted capture next-generation sequencing on 23 myeloid-relevant genes. Eleven patients (11%) had a known or predicted pathogenic variant, involving at least 1 of 7 genes (TET2, ASXL1, KIT, IDH2, JAK2, SF3B1, TP53). In addition, 15 patients (15%) had a variant of unknown significance in 1 of 6 genes (TET2, ASXL1, SETBP1, CALR, CEBPA, CSF3R).

Lee at al8 employed a targeted panel including 88 genes to investigate 30 patients with either idiopathic HES or idiopathic hypereosinophilia. Of patients, 53% were found to have a gene mutation, and the most frequently affected genes were NOTCH1, SCRIB, STAG2, SH2B3, ASXL1, EZH2, GATA1, NF1, and SF3B1. Subsequent pathway network analysis found that these mutations may be related to clonal proliferation of eosinophils.

Finally, Wang et al9 used a panel of 45 genes in 51 patients with idiopathic HES and found 1 or more mutations in 14 (28%) patients. Of these 14 patients, 7 (50%) had 1 mutation, 5 (36%) had 2 mutations, and 2 (14%) had 3 or more mutations. The involved genes in order of decreasing frequency were ASXL1, TET2, EZH2, SETBP1, CBL, NOTCH1, DNMT3A, NRAS, JAK2, and GATA2. Patients with idiopathic HES and gene mutations showed clinical, laboratory, and bone marrow findings with some similarities to chronic eosinophilic leukemia, not otherwise specified, suggesting clonality. Based on these results, the authors suggest that a subset of patients with idiopathic HES may have evidence of clonality, which could establish a diagnosis of chronic eosinophilic leukemia, not otherwise specified. Although patients carrying variants were older and had lower eosinophil counts compared with patients without variants, none of the differences were statistically significant, and the authors concluded that the identified alterations may not represent true driver mutations for idiopathic HES.

As mentioned above, we identified mutations in several of the genes reported in these studies, but most were discarded by our filtering strategy. The 2 variants that passed our filtering (in ASXL1 and DNMT3A) were found in individuals older than 60 years old, suggesting that they could represent clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, a phenomenon dependent on age that is common in elderly patients within the age range of the HES cohort studied, further suggesting that their presence may be independent of HES. Also supporting this idea, we found that these genes carried variants among the individuals in the control cohort as well. Therefore, it is possible that previous studies may have been confounded by clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, a possibility that is being addressed in more recent HES literature.10

The 5 recurrently mutated genes found in our study were distributed among different patients in our cohort. This may be explained by the heterogeneous clinical presentation of HES, which may involve different pathways. Owing to the rare nature of this disease, the limited number in our cohort is too small to identify any clear correlation between HES presentation and the mutation identified. This study is also limited by using bulk exome sequencing, which cannot elucidate the specific cell lineages carrying these mutations, which could also vary from patient to patient. Additionally, we cannot discard the presence of additional recurrent mutations below the detection threshold of exome sequencing (allele frequency >5%). Although the low sensitivity of exome sequencing is one of the main limitations of this study, our approach was able to interrogate additional genes not included in prior targeted sequence studies and identified possible new pathologic processes to consider.
