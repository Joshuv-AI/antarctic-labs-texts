Principles of Sociopolitical Competency in Psychological Practice, Research, and Teaching
Authors: Richard E Redding, Nina C Silander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearch shows that sociopolitical attitudes and values (SPAVs) often play an important role in people’s personal and social identity, life choices, psychological and behavioral functioning, and interpersonal relationships (including those between psychologists and their clients). Thus, SPAVs are frequently relevant to the work that psychologists do, whether as clinicians, researchers, or educators. After reviewing the research on the impact of SPAVs on people’s lives and relationships, we provide a set of evidence-based principles for effectively addressing SPAVs in clinical practice, psychological research, education and professional training, and professional ethics.Keywords: cultural competence, psychotherapy, ethics, research bias

Sociopolitical attitudes and values (SPAVs) are often relevant to the work psychologists do (Redding, 2020; Redding & Cobb, 2023). Sociopolitical issues frequently arise when conducting research on cultural issues or policy-relevant topics, when teaching about such topics, and when working with sociopolitically diverse clients and communities.

In clinical practice, neglecting the role of a client and clinician’s SPAVs can damage a therapeutic relationship and result in a lack of treatment, ineffective treatment, and a failure to serve diverse sociopolitical communities (Redding, 2020; Redding & Cobb, 2023; Silander et al., 2020). Clients often raise sociopolitical issues in the context of their presenting concerns (Redding, 2020; Solomonov & Barber, 2018, 2019), and many seek help dealing with familial, friendship, and work conflicts over political differences, or even their anxiety about national politics (Duffey, 2024; Prout et al., 2019; Redding, 2020). Studies (e.g., Redding, 2020; Solomonov & Barber, 2018) show that incompatibility between the SPAVs of a clinician and client often negatively affects the therapeutic relationship and clinical outcomes. Moreover, clinicians’ SPAVs have been shown to influence diagnoses, case conceptualization, and choice of therapeutic approach, which may not always be consistent with a client’s values (Bilgrave & Deluty, 2002; Norton, 2021; Redding, 2020). Therapists sometimes try to influence clients’ SPAVs, which may damage the therapeutic relationship and reduce treatment effectiveness (Redding, 2020; Redding & Satel, 2023).

In clinical training, students say that they want more mentoring and instruction on dealing with sociopolitical issues in psychotherapy (McCarthy et al., 2022). In teaching, professors’ SPAVs influence their choice of course content and the ideological perspectives they teach, and studies show that the degree of compatibility between professors’ and students’ sociopolitical views can affect both students’ performance and professors’ grading (see Del Giudice, 2023; Friedrich & Douglass, 1998; Rom & Musgrave, 2014). Significantly, very few politically conservative, libertarian, or centrist students pursue graduate study in psychology because of a variety of disincentives that operate in the culture of psychology departments as well as discriminatory practices against them (Inbar & Lammers, 2012; Redding, 2023a, 2023b). This lack of diversity has significant negative consequences for the science and profession of psychology (Redding, 2001).

In psychological research, numerous studies have shown that sociopolitical biases operate across a wide range of subdisciplines and topics vis-à-vis how psychologists design, conduct, and evaluate research (Frisby et al., 2023), but proven debiasing strategies are available. In addition, research is needed to develop measures of sociopolitical competency and evidence-based treatment adaptations based on clients’ SPAVs, along with more research that includes sociopolitically diverse participant samples or SPAVs as a variable of interest.

We provide comprehensive evidence-based principles for effectively addressing SPAVs in clinical practice, teaching and professional training, and research. These principles are needed for several reasons. First, studies (e.g., Inbar & Lammers, 2012; Redding, 2020) show that psychologists frequently overlook the impact of SPAVs on their work, are often sociopolitically biased and discriminate against those having SPAVs different from their own (particularly conservatives), and report difficulty working and empathizing with such clients. This suggests the need for principles designed to promote psychologists’ understanding of sociopolitically diverse communities, prevent discriminatory practices, and promote sociopolitical competence and diversity in the profession. Second, although some clinical and multicultural practice guidelines touch on cultural values, except for Vieten and Lukoff’s (2022) religious and spiritual competency guidelines, this is virtually always in the context of race or ethnicity (e.g., American Psychological Association [APA], 2017b), and none address sociopolitical values specifically. Third, the extant literature on sociopolitical bias in psychology is limited primarily to its impact on psychological research, and mainly with respect to social psychology (e.g., Duarte et al., 2015). The literature addressing SPAVs in clinical work (Silander et al., 2020) and teaching is sparse. Last, an integrated set of principles is needed to guide psychologists in grappling with sociopolitical issues ethically and effectively across contexts. Our approach is unique in relying on research in the fields of behavioral genetics, neuroscience, and social and personality psychology on the role of SPAVs in people’s lives to provide much of the empirical foundation for such principles.

The Role of SPAVs in People’s Lives

We begin by describing the psychological and behavioral functions of SPAVs, which are those attitudes and values relating to politically relevant social and cultural issues (e.g., abortion, gun control, crime control, the death penalty, parental vs. state rights in education, racial issues, immigration policy, gender and LGBTQ issues, gender-affirming policies, vaccine mandates, the role of religion in the public square), which at various times have been at the forefront of the ongoing culture wars (Redding & Cobb, 2023, p. 2) and are among the most divisive issues among people (Crawford et al., 2017). The impact of SPAVs has grown in recent years in the current climate of political polarization and conflict (Pew Research Center, 2024).

SPAVs carry a strong emotional valence for many people, with studies showing the religious-like function that they often serve (Greenberg et al., 2017). They are existentially significant because they are based on foundational moral values, with those on the left and the right differing in the moral values underpinning their political views. Liberals primarily prioritize the values of fairness, equity, and caring, whereas conservatives prioritize respect for tradition and authority, sanctity, loyalty, and liberty (Atari et al., 2023; Haidt, 2013).

Research over the last few decades in personality and social psychology, neuroscience, and behavior genetics demonstrates that people’s SPAVs can have a profound impact on their psychological functioning, life choices, relationships, and the ways in which others respond to them (Redding, 2020; Redding & Cobb, 2023). Moreover, people’s sociopolitical identities can be as central to them as their demographic identities. SPAVs impact the interpersonal and professional functioning of psychologists, their clients, and their students. SPAVs are often an essentialist feature of personality and are important not only to personal identity but also to social identity and relationships. SPAVs also drive interpersonal bias and discrimination. We discuss each of these characteristics in turn below.

SPAVs are an essentialist feature of personality

SPAVs are an essentialist feature of personality, as suggested by the fact that they have a strong genetic and neurobiological basis, are linked to underlying personality traits, and usually remain relatively stable throughout adulthood. The genetic contribution to people’s political attitudes, party affiliation, and specific SPAVs is quite significant—approximately 45% to 65% of these traits are heritable (Alford et al., 2005; Fazekas & Littvay, 2015; Funk et al., 2013). Such heritable traits tend to be affectively powerful because they develop early in life and are relatively resistant to change (Alford et al., 2005; Tesser, 1993). SPAVs become solidified by late adolescence or early adulthood and typically remain fairly stable thereafter (Huddy, 2002; Jennings et al., 2009). A meta-analysis of behavioral genetic studies including 12,000 twins suggested the genetic link to political ideologies operates via heritable personality, temperament, and cognitive-style traits (Hatemi et al., 2014). The behavioral traits correlated with political ideology include openness to experience, conscientiousness, cognitive flexibility, need for order, tolerance for ambiguity, and disgust and threat sensitivity (Inbar & Pizarro, 2014; Jost & Amodio, 2012; Jost et al., 2009, 2018). Neuroscience studies (e.g., DeYoung et al., 2010) show that such traits linked to political attitudes are associated with differences in brain structure and functioning (Jost & Amodio, 2012; Mendez, 2017; Panish & Nam, 2024).

As we discuss in the Principles of Sociopolitical Competency section, the insight that SPAVs are rather essentialist to people’s personality is useful to psychologists in a number of ways. It implies, for example, that they should not be biased against others on the basis of such essentialist characteristics. They should assess clients’ SPAVs as related to their biopsychosocial makeup and life experiences to obtain a comprehensive and empathic understanding of their clients, adopt therapeutic approaches that are consistent with their clients’ SPAVs, and consider whether it is ethically appropriate or even harmful to influence a client’s SPAVs.

The role of SPAVs in personal identity

Sociopolitical identity is intertwined with personal identity (Brandt et al., 2014; Mason, 2018; Panish & Nam, 2024; S. T. Stevens et al., 2015), contributing to a sense of meaning, security, and self-worth, making it deeply emotional (Greenberg & Arndt, 2012; Jost & Amodio, 2012; S. T. Stevens et al., 2015) and serving a religious-like function for many (Kay et al., 2008; Pyszczynski et al., 2015). “Terror management theory,” supported by more than 350 studies across many countries, conceptualizes political attitudes as providing psychological security by helping to cope with the terror of death-related anxiety (Chew, 2022; Greenberg et al., 2017; Weise et al., 2008). Studies show that reminders of death lead to a stronger commitment to one’s political and religious views (Burke et al., 2010) and increased favoritism toward those having similar beliefs but increased hostility toward those having opposing beliefs (McGregor et al., 1998). People respond defensively when their cherished ideologies are challenged (Hart, 2014), which can even lower self-esteem (Cohen et al., 2000). Once SPAVs “become central to the self, they require little cognitive effort to maintain, are driven primarily by affect, are experienced more intensely than other attitudes and beliefs, and are considered facts about the world” (S. T. Stevens et al., 2015, p. 69).

Importance of SPAVs to people’s social identity and relationships

“Partisanship may be one of American’s most salient group affiliations” (Iyengar & Krupenkin, 2018, p. 24). Nowadays, with people having fewer social connections and less involvement in organized religion (Putnam, 2020), they often turn to ideology as a source of meaning and to political groups (often online) as a source of belonging (Hart, 2014; Kachanoff et al., 2020). People’s political identity is often a key aspect of their social identity (Devine, 2015; Greene, 2004; Huddy, 2002; Iyengar & Krupenkin, 2018), with members of sociopolitical groups having similar psychological makeups (Jost et al., 2003). As studies show, an individual’s identity with a political group “becomes part of the self, thus acquiring social and emotional significance,” with opposing ideological groups taking on negative emotional significance (Smith et al., 2007, p. 431).

The fact that people’s SPAVs are often an important aspect of their personal and social identity implies, for example, that psychologists should view SPAVs as a key component of personal identity just as they do demographic characteristics (Redding, 2020; Redding & Cobb, 2023), which has implications for professional ethics, clinical practice, and research. For example, as we discuss in the Principles of Sociopolitical Competency section, psychologists should assess the centrality of clients’ sociopolitical identity and how it affects their self-concept, psychological well-being, life choices, and relationships.

SPAV differences are potent drivers of bias and discrimination

People are often prejudiced against those who differ from them ideologically, especially on sociopolitical issues (Crawford et al., 2017). People may be more apt to categorize others on the basis of their political beliefs than their race (see Pietraszewski et al., 2015). Accordingly, sociopolitical bias in interpersonal relationships may be stronger than racial or ethnic bias, remarkably as first suggested by studies conducted in the 1960s (Insko et al., 1983) during a time when racism was more prevalent in American society than it is today. Differences in attitudes and values were the key drivers of racial discrimination (Rokeach et al., 1960) because racists felt threatened by the values held by racial minorities (Sears, 1988). Numerous studies showed that, particularly when there was little social pressure to be racially biased, belief similarity had a much greater effect on attitudes toward African Americans than race itself (e.g., Byrne & Ervin, 1969; Rokeach & Mezei, 1966; Triandis & Davis, 1965).

Recent studies also suggest that SPAV differences may be a more potent driver of discrimination than racial or other demographic differences (Iyengar & Westwood, 2015; Pietraszewski et al., 2015; Westwood et al., 2017). People show greater prejudice against people of the same race who have a different political ideology than they do people of a different race who share their ideology (Chambers et al., 2013). People are more willing to date someone of a different race, or would approve of their child doing so, than someone of a different political persuasion (D. A. Cox et al., 2020). Haidt et al. (2003) found that fraternity admission decisions were more influenced by SPAV similarity between the pledge candidate and fraternity members than demographic factors such as race or ethnicity. Studies also demonstrate that implicit political bias is stronger than racial bias among most people (Iyengar & Westwood, 2015).

Unfortunately, people often do not feel any compunction about discriminating against those who are sociopolitically different as they would toward those of a different race because they see race as an essentialist feature of people (Ganzach & Schul, 2024; Iyengar & Westwood, 2015). As discussed above, however, sociopolitical identity is also an essentialist feature—although for some more than others to be sure.

Because SPAVs are often drivers of bias and discrimination, psychologists’ own SPAVs will often influence or bias their clinical practice, research, and teaching. Moreover, some clients’ presenting problems will include experiences of sociopolitical discrimination or interpersonal or workplace conflict driven by sociopolitical biases, experiences that may be shared by psychologists’ students and colleagues as well.

Principles of Sociopolitical Competency

Below we first provide foundational principles that include the prerequisite attitudes and knowledge necessary to support cultural competency (see Vieten & Lukoff, 2022) for SPAVs. We then present skill-based principles for addressing SPAVs in professional ethics, clinical practice, education, and research.

Attitudes and knowledge

Psychologists should value sociopolitical diversity, exercise humility about their own SPAVs, and strive to understand and be open-minded about diverse SPAVs, particularly those belonging to the communities they serve

Psychologists should value SPAVs as a key aspect of diversity that strengthens their research, teaching, policy work, and ability to serve diverse communities (Duarte et al., 2015; Redding, 2001). Valuing sociopolitical diversity is a prerequisite for understanding and empathizing with the sociopolitical other (see Graham, Nosek, & Haidt, 2012). In addition, positive attitudes about diverse values lead to interpersonal flexibility and the motivation to interact with diverse groups (see Tropp & Bianchi, 2006), better relationships (Chaby et al., 2019; Hodson, 2011), and improved outcomes when working with diverse communities (see Homan et al., 2007).

Psychologists should adopt a stance of intellectual and cultural humility (Hook et al., 2025) about differing sociopolitical values, which entails an awareness that one’s own values may be misguided or based on inaccurate assumptions. It also means “curbing the natural tendency to view one’s own values and culture as superior . . . and an openness and willingness to learn about the cultural orientation of other people” (Van Tongeren et al., 2019, p. 464). Being mindful of cognitive biases (e.g., motivated reasoning, myside bias, biased assimilation, confirmation bias, belief perseverance, bias blind spot, groupthink; see Korteling & Toet, 2020) and practicing a critical reflexivity that recognizes and challenges one’s own ideological assumptions provide an effective disposition for open-mindedness (Yanchar et al., 2008). Such humility leads to greater curiosity about opposing views along with more positive feelings and greater tolerance toward those having such views. It also facilitates empathy and agreeableness and thus, not surprisingly, often leads to better relationships (Sgambati, 2025).

Psychologists, who are better equipped than most do to so, should put themselves in the shoes of the sociopolitical other and consider what life experiences (e.g., family-of-origin values and upbringing, socioeconomic level, educational background, peer associations, communities in which they live, media exposure and political knowledge, experiences of discrimination) and possible genetic and personality factors likely led them to have the SPAVs that they do. Research on moral foundations demonstrates that eight fundamental moral concerns (i.e., care, loyalty, honor, authority, sanctity, equality, proportionality, and ownership) underlie the moral and sociopolitical decision-making of most people (Atari et al., 2023; Haidt, 2013), but conservatives and liberals weigh these moral foundations differently (Graham et al., 2009; Graham, Haidt, et al., 2012; Haidt, 2013). Appreciating this differential weighting of shared foundational values identifies common ground that facilitates empathy (Wolf et al., 2020) while reducing the likelihood of overestimating moral differences (Haidt, 2013).

Psychologists should appreciate how people’s SPAVs may bias their views of others, strive to overcome their own biases, and, when appropriate, help others overcome biases

Even psychologists may fail to recognize their own biases because research shows that people are better at identifying bias in others than in themselves (Pronin et al., 2002). They should consider how they may be biased against those who are sociopolitically different and the ways in which such biases may affect their professional relationships. Indeed, people often have explicit and implicit biases against those with opposing political attitudes (Iyengar & Westwood, 2015; Nosek et al., 2007). They are apt to view them as irrational, uninformed, or immoral and to engage in psychological splitting wherein they see members of their own sociopolitical group as “all good” and those of the opposing group as “all bad” (Hartz et al., 2026), all of which makes their biases seem reasonable and justified (Eberhardt, 2019; Griffin & Ross, 1991). Yet they frequently misperceive others’ views (Chambers et al., 2006; Yudkin et al., 2019), usually by overestimating the degree to which they differ from their own (Graham, Nosek, & Haidt, 2012; Yudkin et al., 2019). For these reasons, favoritism toward one’s own sociopolitical group is a particularly potent driver of discrimination (Greenwald & Pettigrew, 2014). This is especially the case in the current climate of political tribalism and polarization, in which many people see their political views central to their self and social identity (Iyengar & Krupenkin, 2018). Studies show that conservatives and liberals are equally intolerant of one another (Brandt et al., 2014; Frimer et al., 2017).

According to their own self-reports, however, psychologists are often biased against those having conservative SPAVs (Honeycutt & Freberg, 2016; Inbar & Lammers, 2012). Many clinicians report being hesitant to treat such clients, for whom they have less empathy (Ley, 2024; Redding, 2020), whereas they have greater empathy for liberal or progressive clients (Tublin, 2017). This is likely due in part to the liberal political tilt in the profession (see Redding, 2023b), making conservatives the sociopolitical out-group. Moreover, psychological research has painted the picture that conservatives are authoritarian, closed-minded, simple-minded, intolerant, threatened by uncertainty, and driven by fear and disgust (e.g., Jost et al., 2018). This has influenced how conservatives are viewed by psychologists (Redding, 2023b), who may pathologize or demonize conservative worldviews and values, although they should be aware that recent research findings challenge this narrative somewhat while also identifying positive traits common among conservatives (e.g., Burton et al., 2015; Costello et al., 2022). Although many psychologists report a reluctance to work with Trump supporters (Ley, 2024), they may have greater empathy for such clients if they appreciate that they may support Trump’s policy positions not necessarily because they are authoritarian, racist, or closed-minded.

Having the motivation to overcome bias is important for successfully doing so (Paluck et al., 2021). Effective debiasing strategies include being conscious of the negative stereotypes one holds about other groups, replacing negative stereotypes with less judgmental responses, seeing people as individuals, taking the perspective of the sociopolitical other, and focusing on shared values and identities (Devine et al., 2012). Recognizing that people’s SPAVs are an essentialist feature of their personality largely because of genetic predispositions, innate personality and temperament patterns, and early upbringing can also reduce bias (see Ganzach & Schul, 2024). In addition, interacting with sociopolitically diverse groups and learning more about them is a good way to reduce stereotyping and prejudice, as demonstrated by studies on the “contact hypothesis” (Devine et al., 2012; Lupton et al., 2015; Paluck, 2016; Zhou et al., 2018). The nature and quality of these interactions matters, however (Hewstone, 2015). Discussions should be undertaken with an attitude that is nonjudgmental, curious, and empathic (see Brannon & Walton, 2013; Gergen, 2009; Israel, 2020). Sharing life histories and experiences relating to sociopolitical discrimination and how and why one’s sociopolitical values developed as they did is especially helpful (Gergen, 2009; Gray, 2025).

Psychologists should be aware of the SPAVs implicit in psychological research, different therapeutic approaches, and standards of professional practice

Psychologists should appreciate how sociopolitical ideology affects psychological research, its interpretation, and its application to clinical and policy work (see Duarte et al., 2015; Frisby et al., 2023; Redding, 2001). Ideological bias has been well documented in virtually every area of research having sociopolitical implications, including psychotherapy, psychological assessment, parenting practices, intelligence and IQ testing, sexuality, gender, prejudice and stereotyping, moral psychology, political psychology, and evolutionary psychology (e.g., Duarte et al., 2015; Frisby et al., 2023; Jussim & Crawford, 2017). Moreover, on surveys psychologists admit that they would likely judge grant proposals and manuscript submissions less positively when they conflict with their own sociopolitical ideologies (Honeycutt & Freberg, 2016; Inbar & Lammers, 2012).

Numerous studies (Munder et al., 2013) show a robust independent relationship between researcher therapeutic allegiance and the treatment effects found in studies evaluating or comparing therapeutic approaches, perhaps reflecting the fact that therapeutic approaches differ in their inherent SPAVs. Cognitive-behavioral therapies, for example, are grounded in an ethos of individualism and personal agency (Iwamasa & Hayes, 2019; Redding et al., 2026). By contrast, social-justice counseling and related approaches such as decolonial therapy are grounded in progressive political ideologies concerned about the causal role of systemic oppression, structural inequality, Eurocentrism, and discrimination in client problems (Redding & Satel, 2023; Redding et al., 2026). Clinicians may prefer approaches consistent with their own SPAVs, yet they may not be suitable for all clients. Psychologists should also be aware of the values implicit in some standards of professional practice, which may be antithetical to those of some clients. For instance, the APA’s recent practice guidelines for men and boys (APA, 2018) reflect feminist and progressive values and perspectives on traditional masculinity, with the empirical foundation for some of its claims relatively weak (Ferguson, 2023). Some sociopolitical and religious communities disagree with those values; in addition, the APA’s guidelines may have iatrogenic effects with some populations of boys and men and, by failing to respect their chosen expressions of masculinity, may dissuade some from seeking treatment (see Barry, 2023; Ferguson, 2023; Mahalik et al., 2012). Thus, psychologists should consider whether their preferred treatment approaches and/or extant practice guidelines would be efficacious with a particular client given that client’s SPAVs.

Skills

The following sections discuss the ethical, clinical, teaching, and research skills that demonstrate sociopolitical competence.

Professional ethics

Psychologists should not discriminate on the basis of SPAVs, pathologize others’ SPAVs, inappropriately influence clients’ SPAVs, or use a treatment modality that is unsuitable for clients merely because it is consistent with the psychologist’s values

Because the psychological effects of discrimination can negatively impact physical and mental health (e.g., Chae et al., 2017; Hoggard et al., 2015; Pieterse et al., 2012), psychologists should not discriminate on the basis of people’s SPAVs, which are often essentialist features of their personality and identity. Professional ethics codes should prohibit such discrimination (Redding, 2020), as does the ethics code of the National Association of Social Workers (2017).

Yet discriminatory behavior is often not overt or intentional but can entail subtle slights, gestures, and dismissive, insensitive, or exclusionary behaviors (Sue & Spanierman, 2020). For example, psychologists may have items in their office that signal their political viewpoints (which may be off-putting to some clients), react with negative body language or demur when a client raises particular SPAVs, avoid discussing certain issues, or discount the relevance or validity of a client’s SPAVs, all of which can damage the therapeutic relationship and reduce treatment efficacy and adherence (see Redding, 2020; Vybíral et al., 2023). Psychologists should remove such potentially alienating features of their professional space and communicate with curiosity, open-mindedness, and empathy about clients’ SPAVs.

Psychologists also should not pathologize, denigrate (as necessarily reflecting undesirable personality traits), or dismiss the SPAVs of their clients, research participants, or colleagues. Nor should they try to influence clients’ values or life choices unless there is a compelling therapeutic reason for doing so. Yet 48% of clinicians say they find it appropriate to challenge a client’s SPAVs in treatment, and 40% say there is a risk that doing so might impose their own values on clients (Redding, 2020). This highlights the risk to clients when psychologists’ ideological preferences impinge on clients’ values and autonomy, which the APA (2017a) and American Counseling Association (2014) ethics codes warn against. Therapists should “bracket” their own values and remain “focused on the client’s agenda” during clinical assessment rather than their own (McWhorter, 2019, p. 205).

In recent years, there has been a trend toward analyzing clients’ problems from an a priori sociopolitical perspective along with politically activist therapeutic approaches that are explicitly designed to influence clients’ sociopolitical values (Redding & Satel, 2023; Thomas, 2023). This constitutes an ethical breach if the therapist does so without a client’s consent and/or if the treatment is ill suited to a client’s values or goals. To be sure, some SPAVs are pathological or harmful, such as those grounded in prejudice, bigotry, or violence, but it is probably better to challenge the underlying maladaptive cognitive distortions and harmful behaviors rather than the SPAVs themselves. Moreover, attempting to change a client’s SPAVs is unlikely to be successful because SPAVs are rather resistant to change. Moreover, it is likely to damage the therapeutic relationship and may lead to early termination. It also may be psychologically harmful because SPAVs are often an essentialist feature of a client’s identity.

Psychologists should recognize when they cannot competently serve particular communities or clients because of their own biases or lack of cultural competence and seek training or consultation, or refer clients to other psychologists

Psychologists should consider whether there are potential clients for whom they might have sociopolitical blind spots or biases, significant client values-treatment modality misalignment, difficulty establishing rapport, or diminished empathy. Tools to assess cultural humility, implicit bias, and the impact of one’s political identity on client relationships may be useful in this regard (e.g., Bayne & Hankey, 2020; Bayne et al., 2022; Foronda et al., 2020). Clinicians should evaluate whether such potential problems can be remedied through training or consultation, or by using bias-mitigation strategies such as those described previously. Psychologists can enhance their sociopolitical competence through continuing education, reading relevant literature about different sociopolitical communities and value systems (e.g., Haidt, 2013), and obtaining supervision from diverse professional peers. Of course, experience working with diverse populations leads to greater cultural competence with those populations (APA, 2022).

Psychologists should assure clients during the intake process of the importance they place on respecting their values and invite them to tell the therapist if they perceive otherwise during psychotherapy. Disclosing the values that inform a psychologist’s approach to care and inviting a conversation about possible client concerns can also facilitate a good working relationship. The APA ethics code (APA, 2017a) implies that referring a client to another psychologist is a last resort when other steps are insufficient or unworkable (Standard 2.01, Boundaries of Competence), and it prohibits discrimination based on client characteristics. However, referral is ethically required when psychologists cannot provide competent care because they lack cultural competence, are unable to maintain objectivity, or when their values differ from those of their clients to such an extent that a good therapeutic relationship would be at risk. Before referring, however, clinicians should consider whether seeking consultation or supervision would address the problem and whether referring is truly in the client’s best interests. Clinics should establish guidelines for making such referrals and a network of other clinicians to whom they can refer clients, monitor referral patterns to ensure appropriateness, and provide consultation to help clinicians when they have values that conflict with those of their clients.

Clinical practice

Psychologists should inquire about clients’ SPAVs when relevant and work to understand and empathize with clients’ SPAVs and the ways in which they are related to their presenting problems

Assessing clients’ SPAVs along with related biopsychosocial information during a clinical interview supports a comprehensive and empathic understanding of clients, who report better treatment outcomes and greater satisfaction when therapists broach issues relating to their cultural identity (Depauw et al., 2022). Indeed, 43% of clients report having discussed their SPAVs in psychotherapy (Redding, 2020). Sociopolitical values also may manifest themselves with clients in indirect or unexpected ways (e.g., parenting and marriage decisions) and may have clinical relevance beyond the SPAVs themselves. For example, political differences among family members “often serve as proxies for deeper relational wounds and unmet needs” (McCown & Chamberlain, 2025, p. 34), and political issues are a leading cause of familial conflict (Safer, 2019).

Psychologists should avoid presuming clients’ SPAVs on the basis of their demographic characteristics or the political group to which they apparently belong because particular SPAVs are not always tethered to such characteristics, and doing so may create the impression that the clinician is relying on stereotypes. Psychologists should ask clients about their SPAVs when potentially relevant to their presenting concerns (e.g., reproductive decisions, parenting practices, sexuality, gender issues, death and dying, interpersonal and family conflicts, experiences of sociopolitical discrimination, anxiety about current events such as climate change or war). The Cultural Formulation Interview, a 16-question guide from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (APA, 2022) for assessing the impact of clients’ cultural values on client problems treatment effectiveness, and the therapeutic relationship, is a useful tool for assessing clients’ SPAVs. Therapists can also use assessment approaches such as values-based motivational interviewing and SPAV mapping exercises (e.g., exploring the intersectional roles of family-of-origin influence, key life experiences, education) to obtain information about clients’ SPAVs.

Clients experience stronger therapeutic relationships when they feel comfortable disclosing their SPAVs without perceiving judgment by the therapist (Redding, 2020). Therapists should establish a rapport that signals openness to diverse perspectives, demonstrate sincere curiosity about clients’ SPAVs, use active listening techniques, and humbly acknowledge when they lack an understanding of clients’ SPAVs. (For example, a clinician might say, “I may not share your views, but I appreciate hearing about them and want to understand how they affect your life.”) Therapists are more likely to discuss political issues when there is a convergence of client-clinician SPAVs (Solomonov & Barber, 2019), but they should not shy away from doing so when there is not. Therapists can use empathy-building strategies such as relating to the client’s needs and acknowledging shared experiences to bridge SPAV differences (Yourman, 2018).

To be sure, however, psychologists need not agree with their clients’ SPAVs. Empathy does not require agreement or betraying one’s convictions. Rather, it means understanding clients’ worldviews, having unconditional positive regard for clients, validating their emotional experiences, and exploring how their SPAVs may impact their identity, relationships, and functioning. Psychologists should appreciate that clients’ SPAVs reflects their biopsychosocial makeup and life experiences rather than character deficits, understand and empathize with clients’ values while also finding shared values, and focus on the functional impact of clients’ values rather than their ideological correctness.

Psychologists should consider how their own and their clients’ SPAVs may affect the therapeutic relationship, perceptions of clinician credibility and treatment expectations, and treatment adherence and completion

Clinicians report that their clients’ political views are among the most important factors affecting their work (Redding, 2020; Solomonov & Barber, 2018, 2019), and even their implicit sociopolitical biases may negatively affect the therapeutic relationship. Thus, clinicians should monitor for SPAV-related transference or client resistance as well as their own countertransference (i.e., their emotional reactions to a client) or defenses against a client’s sociopolitical world (see Hendelman, 2018), all of which can adversely affect psychotherapy. Once again, research shows the value of clinicians being self-aware about the impact of their values on their perceptions of and feelings about others. Clinicians having such an awareness are better able to recognize and navigate countertransference, as are clinicians who reflect on their thoughts and feelings about a client after each session (Gelso & Hayes, 2007).

Value dissimilarities between a clinician and client pose a risk to the establishment and maintenance of a good therapeutic relationship (Hardy et al., 2007), a linchpin for effective treatment (Wampold & Imel, 2015). Studies show that psychologists and clients alike perceive stronger therapeutic relationships when they share similar SPAVs (Redding, 2020; Solomonov & Barber, 2018) and that clinicians have greater empathy for such clients (Redding, 2020; Solomonov & Barber, 2018). SPAV incompatibility between a therapist and client can damage the client’s trust in the therapist, inhibit the client’s willingness to disclose information or ask questions (out of fear that the therapist will not understand, judge the client, or take offense), reduce treatment adherence (Eubanks et al., 2018; Redding, 2020), or lead to dropout or premature termination (Hendelman, 2018; Vervaeke et al., 1997). Similarly, McCarthy et al. (2022) suggested that when there are SPAV mismatches between clinical supervisors and trainees, trainees feel less comfortable discussing them with their supervisors.

Psychologists can, however, show unconditional positive regard and leverage psychologist-client similarities to buffer the impact of conflicting SPAVs. When appropriate, therapists can disclose their own SPAVs to clients if doing so would strengthen the therapeutic relationship or signal to clients that they appreciate their values. In addition, adopting a stance of cultural humility about clients’ SPAVs contributes to a positive therapeutic alliance, fewer perceived slights in therapy, and better therapeutic outcomes. It also buffers the negative effects of any sociopolitical missteps (see Davis et al., 2018) by the therapist, as does being attuned to clients’ verbal and nonverbal cues regarding SPAVs.

Psychologists should consider how SPAVs influence case conceptualization, treatment goals, and choice of therapeutic approach and modify treatment plans to leverage clients’ SPAVs

Forty-nine percent of clinicians report that their political views influence their clinical choices (Bilgrave & Deluty, 2002). Psychologists’ SPAVs correlate with their preferred treatment approaches and may influence case conceptualization (Bilgrave & Deluty, 2002; Norton, 2021; Norton & Tan, 2019), including clinician perceptions of what motivates clients’ behaviors and their degree of personal responsibility for their problems, the presence of psychopathology, and which issues are clinically relevant. For instance, liberal clinicians rate gun access and tobacco usage as problems warranting clinical intervention more so than do conservative clinicians, who rate abortion decisions as a more pressing clinical issue (Norton & Tan, 2019).

Clinicians are likewise influenced by their clients’ SPAVs, which may bias their clinical decision-making. Thirty-four percent of clinicians reported that their inferences about a client’s SPAVs might bias their diagnosis, and 31% said that it might negatively impact their confidence and effectiveness as a therapist (Redding, 2020). Psychologists’ distaste for clients’ SPAVs may lead them to assign more serious diagnoses (Bornstein, 2015), with studies suggesting that therapists tend to see clients who have political values very different from their own as being more pathological (Tjeltveit, 2006; Weisskopf-Joelson, 1980). On the other hand, therapists are more likely to see a client as having improved during the course of therapy when the client’s values become more closely aligned with those of the therapist (Beutler et al., 1983).

Therapists can minimize the biasing effects of their own SPAVs on case conceptualization by ensuring a standardized application of diagnostic criteria, identifying potential personal biases, and practicing cultural humility. Therapists should monitor treatment progress by using measurement-based care for symptom tracking as well as measures that account for the influence of SPAVs, such as those that assess cultural humility and the therapeutic alliance (e.g., Cultural Humility Scale: Hook et al., 2013; Brief Revised Working Alliance Inventory: Mallinckrodt & Tekie, 2016).

At the same time, knowing about clients’ SPAVs can positively inform treatment planning (Redding, 2020). Therapies tailored to clients’ values and identities have been shown to produce better outcomes (Hall et al., 2016), and psychologists can select treatments and adjust treatment plans to leverage clients’ SPAV-related strengths and protective factors. Consider that conservative clients, who tend to have a relatively strong internal locus of control (Sweetser, 2014), may respond better to cognitive-behavioral treatment, whereas liberal clients may prefer psychodynamic treatment (Norton & Tan, 2019). Clinicians should also recognize how SPAV-related traits may have negative effects on a client’s well-being or treatment. For example, conservative clients’ strong internal locus of control or self-reliance values may cause distress if these clients blame themselves for problems beyond their control or if, for example, they fail to avail themselves of needed treatment or assistance programs partly because of their SPAVs.

Psychologists should consider the role that clients’ SPAVs play in their personal and social identities, behavior, relationships, and life choices and evaluate how discrepancies between clients’ SPAVs and those inherent in clients’ environments affect their relationships, occupational functioning, and psychological well-being

Psychologists should consider the role that clients’ SPAVs play in their personal and social identity, appreciating that it may be as salient for them as their demographic identities (Redding & Cobb, 2023), potentially marginalizing them in some contexts while privileging them in others. Clients whose SPAVS are central to their self-identity may be more likely to experience or perceive discrimination (see Chae et al., 2017), and their sense of self-efficacy, mental well-being, and interpersonal relatedness is often negatively impacted when others view their sociopolitical identity negatively (Kachanoff et al., 2020) or when they feel that they must conceal their identity and cannot authentically express it (Fletcher & Everly, 2021). Therapists can gauge the centrality of a client’s sociopolitical identity and its relationship to other identities, whether a client has suffered any identity disruptions, a client’s perceived marginalization or privilege, and any identity-management strategies a client uses. The Political Identity and Relationship Impact Scale, developed for use in psychotherapy, may be helpful in this process (Bayne et al., 2022).

Psychologists should evaluate how the match or discrepancy between clients’ SPAVs and those with whom they regularly interact may be related to clients’ presenting concerns (Redding, 2020), assessing the frequency and severity of interpersonal conflict and its impact on clients’ social engagement, decision-making, and emotional regulation. For instance, mismatches between a client’s sociopolitical values and those predominating in that client’s workplace can lead to work dissatisfaction, workplace conflict, or impaired job performance (Anantharaman et al., 2025; Bermiss & McDonald, 2018). By contrast, settings that support and affirm clients’ values can buffer stress and enhance well-being (see Creswell et al., 2005; Fulmer et al., 2010). Some clients engage in psychologically draining “identity management” (Jones & King, 2014) by concealing their sociopolitical identity from others, often leading to stress, anxiety (Hewlin et al., 2017), and feelings of inauthenticity (Swigart et al., 2020), and they may seek therapists’ help in navigating how to be their authentic sociopolitical selves (Duffey, 2024). Clients now often seek psychotherapy to help manage the sociopolitical conflicts that arise (Chen & Rohla, 2018; Holtermann, 2025) in their familial, personal, and professional relationships (Duffey, 2024; Prout et al., 2019; Redding, 2020). Therapists can address clients’ depression and anxiety over relational conflicts, help them to empathize with others’ sociopolitical identities and bridge differences by focusing on shared values and experiences, teach clients effective strategies for dealing with sociopolitical conflicts, and help them to repair damaged relationships (Duffey, 2024; McCown & Chamberlain, 2025; Yourman, 2018).

Education and training

Graduate education, internships, and continuing education programs should provide training on sociopolitical competency skills

Multicultural training, which increases cultural competence (Rogers & O’Bryon, 2014), is required of every graduate program accredited by the APA (APA, 2009) and the American Counseling Association (Council for Accreditation of Counseling and Related Educational Programs, 2016). Current training programs, which are focused almost exclusively on demographic, religious, or disability factors, should also include sociopolitical competency training. In a survey of clinical trainees by McCarthy et al. (2022), most trainees indicated that they had encountered differences between their own values and those of their clients, which they had discussed with their clinical supervisor, and they desired training on how to address sociopolitical issues in psychotherapy. In addition to equipping students with the necessary clinical skills to address sociopolitical issues, supervisors should model openness to diverse sociopolitical perspectives, and programs should provide trainees with practicum placements and other opportunities to work with sociopolitically diverse communities.

Instruments such as the Political Identity and Relational Impact Scale (Bayne et al., 2022) can be used by clinical instructors to help trainees understand the impact of their own political identity on their relationships with clients. Curricula for addressing values issues adjacent to SPAVs, such as spirituality and religion (Pearce et al., 2019), may serve as models for sociopolitical competency training along with programs that foster understanding across sociopolitical communities or attitudes of open-mindedness and cultural humility (e.g., Gergen, 2009; Heterodox Academy, 2023; Israel, 2020). Likewise, graduate and continuing education programs could teach these principles in relevant courses through didactic instruction, role plays, and case discussions (that illustrate the role of clients’ SPAV problems and clinical decision-making, how clinicians with different ideological perspectives might approach such cases, and how to adapt treatments to clients’ values) and other activities in which clinicians practice empathy, perspective taking, and SPAV-sensitive interventions. Such training has been shown to improve clinicians’ ability to manage value conflicts and enhance therapeutic outcomes (Hook et al., 2025; McCarthy et al., 2022).

To diversify the profession and ensure sociopolitical diversity among providers of psychological services, as well as researchers and teachers, graduate programs should strive to matriculate sociopolitically diverse applicants, and employers (including colleges and university faculties) should recruit and hire psychologists with diverse SPAVs

More sociopolitically diverse practitioners are needed to effectively serve sociopolitically diverse clients and communities (Redding, 2020), particularly because politically conservative and religious communities are less likely to seek needed psychological services because of their perception that psychologists will not understand or respect their values (Brody, 1994; Redding, 2020). Diversity among clinicians also helps ensure diversity with respect to therapeutic approaches, which can be laden with implicit sociopolitical values. Moreover, just as psychologists of color are more likely to conduct research vis-à-vis racial issues and with communities of color (Roberts et al., 2020), researchers are more likely to conduct research with their own sociopolitical community and on the issues of concern to them. A critical mass of researchers investigating issues from alternative perspectives helps guard against scientific groupthink (e.g., Ahamad, 2022) and the tendency to self-censor when it comes to expressing politically unpopular perspectives (see Clark et al., 2024). Research suggests that minority voices must comprise roughly a third of the group to have a meaningful impact (Gladwell, 2024).

At the same time, a more sociopolitically inclusive workforce in psychology is needed to support the success of sociopolitical minorities in the profession. Feelings of sociopolitical isolation and a climate unwelcoming to one’s sociopolitical identity can contribute to a lack of work satisfaction or career failure (Bermiss & McDonald, 2018; Swigart et al., 2020). Substantial progress has been made in diversifying the profession demographically. Yet surveys show that we are moving in the opposite direction when it comes to sociopolitical diversity and that the field is often not welcoming of sociopolitical diversity (Redding, 2023a, 2023b).

The best way to diversify the profession is to diversify who enters the profession in the first place, but few sociopolitically diverse individuals pursue graduate degrees because they perceive that the discipline is hostile to their values and that they must conceal their sociopolitical identity to ensure career success. Studies show that they are discriminated against in graduate admissions (Redding, 2023a, 2023b), in obtaining faculty positions, and in the peer evaluations of their work (Honeycutt & Freberg, 2016; Inbar & Lammers, 2012; Redding, 2023a, 2023b). Additionally, they often lack academic mentors and opportunities to conduct research on the topics of interest to them (Redding, 2023a, 2023b), feel a sense of alienation from their graduate program and profession (Bourne, 2025), and self-censor to fit in and succeed (Redding, 2023b), all of which greatly limits their prospects for career success and satisfaction. Diversifying faculties is especially important given their outsized role in clinical training, research, and policy work. Moreover, because professors are often relied on for their expertise by policymakers and the media, a more diverse professoriate will improve psychologists’ public credibility (Redding, 2001). Thus, psychology departments should strive for a sociopolitically inclusive environment in teaching, research, and mentoring; train faculty on how to avoid sociopolitical bias; conduct climate assessments to identify barriers to inclusion; and undertake initiatives to recruit diverse students and faculty. In addition to these recommendations, Redding (2023a) provided specific strategies that graduate programs can use to recruit sociopolitically diverse students and described early-career development programs that have been implemented to mentor diverse students.

In teaching and clinical training, psychologists should explore sociopolitical values as an important aspect of diversity; present diverse perspectives and a representative range of research and opinions, particularly on contentious topics; provide opportunities for students to voice their opinions without fear of censorship or discrimination; and avoid discriminating against students or trainees in admissions decisions or performance evaluations on the basis of their SPAVs

Teaching and clinical training is a “persuasive enterprise” (Friedrich & Douglass, 1998). Even if only in their choice of course content, instructors persuade students to accept their preferred perspectives (Redding, 2023a). Students will come away with a very different understanding of intelligence research, for example (Friedrich & Douglass, 1998), depending on whether the professor emphasizes test bias, behavior genetic studies, “g” theories of intelligence, multiple intelligences theory, or critical perspectives.

Professors should discuss different sides of issues and encourage students to pursue open inquiry and debate, and their failure to do so may constitute an ethical breach (O’Donohue & Fisher, 2023). Course assignments and examinations should be structured to allow students to argue for various sides of an issue or to explain the arguments supporting a particular perspective without having to endorse it (Friedrich & Douglass, 1998). Unfortunately, psychology courses and texts often expose students only to the prevailing ideological orthodoxies in the field, failing to present contentious topics in a balanced way (Brown & Ferguson, 2025; Del Giudice, 2023; Warne et al., 2018). Course content on controversies in psychology could substantially broaden and enrich students’ education and provide a conducive space for students to discuss unorthodox ideas (see Frisby et al., 2023). Relatedly, understanding and respecting diverse SPAVs and those who hold them should be a key pedagogical goal when teaching about cultural diversity.

Applicants and students should not be discriminated against on the basis of their SPAVs in program admissions or other decisions or feel any pressure to endorse their professors’ SPAVs. Students should not be evaluated on the basis of their commitment to particular causes, as some programs have done (see Felkner v. Rhode Island College, 2023; Redding & Satel, 2023; Ward v. Polite, 2012), particularly because surveys show that the interest in advocacy varies as a function of psychologists’ political ideologies (Forestieri et al., 2024). Professors should be mindful that they may grade students who share their political views more favorably than those who do not, which may encourage students to tailor their work to match the political views of their professors (Rom & Musgrave, 2014). Students perceive that those professors whose political views differ markedly from theirs are biased in their evaluations of them (e.g., Brow et al., 2014). Course and supervisor evaluations should ask whether students felt free to voice their opinions, whether students with controversial or divergent views received fair treatment, and whether different sides of controversial issues were discussed (Redding, 2023a).

Research

Psychologists should minimize sociopolitical bias by conducting research from diverse sociopolitical perspectives and be mindful of the potential misuse of research findings on sociopolitical issues and groups

The impact of investigators’ ideological biases has been documented in many areas of psychological research (see Frisby et al., 2023). Researchers’ values influence topic selection, problem conceptualization, hypothesis formulation, sample selection, experimenter effects on participant answers and performance, survey question construction, data collection and analysis methods, the interpretation of results, the rigor of peer review, and the implications drawn from research findings and how they are communicated to policymakers (see Ellsworth, 2021; Frisby et al., 2023; Jussim & Crawford, 2017; Redding, 2013).

Strategies that have proven effective for mitigating researcher bias include transparency practices such as study preregistration, interrogating research paradigms for hidden ideological assumptions, testing rival hypotheses and seeking evidence inconsistent with one’s hypotheses, assessing whether the study design is capable of producing data refuting one’s hypotheses, reappraising interpretations, and evaluating the ecological validity of study results (Ellsworth, 2021; Jussim & Crawford, 2017). Ellsworth (2021) provided a checklist of factors for researchers and peer reviewers to prevent bias. In particular, researchers should be cautious that their interpretations of policy-relevant research or characterizations of sociopolitical groups do not reflect their own biases (see Duarte et al., 2015; Frisby et al., 2023) or unfairly stereotype (see Costello, 2023). (To avoid stigmatizing, it may be better to refer not to “conservatives” or “liberals” but, rather, to those “having conservative views” or those “having liberal views.”)

Scientists tend to gravitate toward topics having personal significance, often selecting topics on the basis of their political views (Gross & Simmons, 2014). Thus, an important systems-level strategy for reducing researcher bias and ensuring a more representative psychological science is to have ideological diversity among those who conduct research on sociopolitically relevant issues (Redding, 2013). Researcher diversity reduces groupthink, brings to bear diverse perspectives, makes it professionally safer to give voice to unorthodox perspectives, and facilitates creative problem-solving (Redding, 2013; Sulik et al., 2022). “Adversarial collaborations” between researchers with different ideological perspectives is particularly effective (Clark & Tetlock, 2023; Cowan et al., 2020). Perhaps the ideological diversity of investigator teams should be considered when evaluating grant proposals (see K. R. Stevens et al., 2021).

Reforms to journal editorial and peer- and grant-review practices would help guard against sociopolitical bias with respect to politically unpopular research (see Frisby et al., 2023) while diversifying the perspectives presented. Journals should include ideological diversity in their diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) statements; provide training to editors on strategies for avoiding sociopolitical bias; encourage the submission of ideologically diverse articles and studies with participant samples that include diverse sociopolitical communities; seek sociopolitically diverse reviewers; and invite commentaries from sociopolitically diverse scholars (see Buchanan et al., 2021).

Researchers should develop measures of sociopolitical competency and evidence-based practices for work with sociopolitically diverse populations and conduct research addressing issues of concern to those populations, using participant samples having diverse SPAVs and including SPAVs as a research variable when relevant

Culturally adapted therapies tailor therapy to clients’ identities and values (Iwamasa & Hayes, 2019), and meta-analyses of studies on adapted therapies show that they are more effective than those that are not adapted (e.g., Hall et al., 2016). There is a need to develop adaptations consistent with clients’ SPAVs. Empirical support for the effectiveness of treatments adapted for clients’ religious views (Hook et al., 2009) illustrates the usefulness of adaptations based on client values. Religious clients who received religiously grounded cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) for depression improved significantly more than matched clients receiving traditional CBT (de Abreu Costa & Moreira-Almeida, 2022). Conservative clients and therapists may prefer cognitive-behavioral or religious-based approaches, whereas liberal clients may prefer humanistic, psychodynamic, feminist, or social-justice approaches (see Norton & Tan, 2019). How, for example, might the former be adapted to serve clients having liberal SPAVs and the latter adapted for conservative clients? In addition, measures for assessing clinicians’ sociopolitical competency are needed to complement the currently available measures of cultural humility and empathy.

Researchers should evaluate the generalizability of dominant theories and research findings vis-à-vis diverse sociopolitical groups, consider the possible impact of study participants’ SPAVs on study outcomes, and strive for sociopolitically representative samples. For example, many psychological treatments were validated using samples including urban or university-town populations likely falling predominantly on the liberal side of the political spectrum (see Norton & Tan, 2019). We also need more research on the nature of SPAVs, which in turn could inform research on adapting therapies and psychosocial interventions that account for clients’ SPAVs. A good example is the moral foundations theory, which accounts for the different moral underpinnings of conservative, liberal/progressive, and libertarian political ideologies and the worldviews of those who hold them (Haidt, 2009).

The Way Forward: A Call to Action for the Profession

A multilevel strategy is needed in psychology to ensure sociopolitical competence at the individual, institutional or programmatic, and professional organization levels. Individual psychologists should value sociopolitical diversity and practice the sociopolitical competence principles outlined here. Academic and professional training programs can instill these values in students and provide them with sociopolitical competency training and tools, as well as experiences working with diverse sociopolitical communities. In addition, academic programs should include sociopolitical diversity in their diversity and nondiscrimination statements, undertake efforts to diversify graduate student bodies and faculty, and create ideologically inclusive learning environments that expose students to diverse perspectives and communities. Health-care institutions and psychological clinics should commit to a mission of providing sociopolitically competent services, establish policies and accountability mechanisms that foster sociopolitical inclusiveness and prohibit discrimination, provide training and clinical supervision on sociopolitical competency issues, recruit providers who can serve sociopolitically diverse communities, and integrate SPAV-related competencies into performance evaluations and reward those who demonstrate sociopolitical competence. Professional and accrediting organizations in psychology should revise their ethics codes to explicitly prohibit sociopolitical discrimination, require training programs to provide education on sociopolitical competency as a component of their cultural competence training, and encourage and fund research on sociopolitical competency issues.

Such institutional and organizational statements, norms, and policies would create incentives to mitigate bias as well as accountability for discriminatory behavior; increase the utilization of psychological services among conservative populations and produce improved outcomes; attract more sociopolitically diverse individuals to the profession; stimulate research on understudied sociopolitical issues and more research representing diverse perspectives as well as research on sociopolitically adapted treatments; and enhance public trust in psychology.

Implementing such initiatives requires changing the institutional structures and practices within which psychologists work. Below we briefly outline what this process might entail, using an outpatient psychology clinic as an example, highlighting those steps proscribed by the literature on organizational diversity and change (e.g., Fletcher & Beauregard, 2022; Kotter, 1996, Lewin, 1947; Onyeador et al., 2021).

First, leaders must persuade stakeholders that there is a real and pressing need for change (Kotter, 1996; Lewin, 1947). This means presenting practitioners with evidence that a lack of sociopolitical inclusivity and competence has direct ethical and clinical consequences. For example, studies showing that conservative clients are avoiding or prematurely terminating mental-health treatment because of perceived bias, or that the therapeutic alliance or clinical outcomes are impeded when clinicians are not sociopolitically competent, can serve as powerful persuasive catalysts. Leaders should assemble a guiding coalition (e.g., supervisors, training directors, respected clinicians) of those committed to change who can bring colleagues along. The coalition must communicate a compelling vision: that psychological services should be equally welcoming and effective for clients across the political spectrum and that sociopolitical diversity is a legitimate dimension of the clinic’s diversity and inclusion commitments. The message should be communicated consistently and repeatedly—through staff meetings, supervision, training materials, websites, and community outreach—so that it becomes a recognized feature of the organization’s identity.

Implementing the changes requires simultaneous and complementary structural reforms and individual-level interventions (Onyeador et al., 2021). At the structural level, the clinic can change how diversity is defined in its statement and public materials, put in place accountability mechanisms and structures (e.g., giving particular administrators and supervisors the responsibility for monitoring progress and outcomes; see Onyeador et al., 2021) for ensuring the unbiased delivery of services and sociopolitical competence in its practitioners, and develop outreach initiatives directed at conservative and faith-based communities that are often underserved by mental-health providers. Individual-level interventions may include, for example, incorporating sociopolitical values and diversity into cultural competency training for clinicians, providing case-based curricula and clinical supervision services that help practitioners recognize and manage bias and countertransference when working with clients whose political values differ from their own, and establishing clear professional norms that the therapeutic relationship must remain free of ideological judgment.

To sustain momentum, early successes should be celebrated (Kotter, 1996), particularly because resistance to diversity-related organizational change can often be overcome through demonstrated successes (see Wiggins-Romesburg & Githens, 2018). Success might include measurable improvements in intake rates from previously underserved communities, positive shifts in client satisfaction data or clinical outcomes, or favorable responses to outreach materials. Such early wins can be leveraged to broaden and deepen the change efforts, which should be anchored in the organizational culture alignment across supervisory levels, continuous reinforcement, and integration into daily clinical work (Kotter, 1996; Lewin, 1947). This means, for example, embedding sociopolitical competence and inclusivity into hiring criteria, performance evaluations, supervision frameworks, and continuing education requirements so that the changes become self-sustaining features in the life of the clinic.

Yet, as Wiggins-Romesburg and Githens (2018) explained, “diversity resistance is unique from resistance to most other organizational change initiatives because the proposed change extends beyond an organization’s way of doing things to touch an individual’s deepest held values and motivations” (p. 182). Indeed, undertaking the institutional changes and initiatives outlined here requires psychologists to be open-minded about diverse SPAVs. But they may not welcome sociopolitical diversity if they consider certain sociopolitical values, even if held by wide swaths of the population, to be inherently wrong or harmful and thus not deserving of respect. They may also be averse to working with clients, students, or colleagues who hold such views. Even when psychologists are motivated to practice sociopolitical competency, they may fail to do so because they fear disapproval from colleagues, who may see them as being complicit in harmful sociopolitical agendas. Moreover, psychologists may respond defensively to organizational change aimed at fostering sociopolitical diversity and training efforts aimed at reducing sociopolitical bias, because of the natural human tendency to favor in-group members while overlooking or underestimating our own biases (Onyeador et al., 2021). Thus, having in place institutional accountability and reward mechanisms is especially important (Onyeador et al., 2021).

Attitudinal obstacles can be addressed through educational and training programs that frame the moral and professional case for sociopolitical diversity and competence. The framing matters enormously. Psychologists are more likely to acknowledge the problem and welcome change when presented as an important professional growth challenge (Fletcher & Beauregard, 2022) consistent with their existing commitments to multicultural competence, diversity, and ethical practice. The goal is to provide ethical and culturally competent services to all clients and maintain professional boundaries between one’s personal views and ethical obligations as a clinician, teacher, or researcher. In addition, a sociopolitically isolated and homogeneous field is likely to miss important perspectives and marginalize certain communities (including working-class and rural communities, which often are conservative). When research on ideologically relevant topics comes overwhelmingly from one perspective, it is more likely to be biased and myopic and, thus, less valid, reliable, and comprehensive. (Psychologists should consider the virtue of epistemic humility when it comes to their cherished ideological perspectives, mindful that the history of psychology includes examples in which the field’s scientific or policy consensus was later proved wrong, misguided, or incomplete.) Similarly, psychologists have the ethical and educational obligation to equip their students with an open-minded, curious attitude toward different perspectives and an understanding of them.

Sociopolitical diversity and competency do not conflict with psychologists’ social-justice or DEI commitments but only further those goals and values. Framed in this manner, psychologists should welcome sociopolitical diversity. Like demographic diversity, sociopolitical diversity furthers the goals of DEI, which assumes four things to be true about people’s demographic characteristics: that they are central to identity; that people are discriminated against on the basis of such characteristics; that accompanying demographic diversity is a diversity of knowledge, values, and life experiences; and, that members of minority demographic groups are victims of marginalization or exclusion (Redding, 2025). However, as the research on SPAVs demonstrates, the same is also true with respect to people’s SPAVs, and some groups (mainly conservatives) are often marginalized by the psychological profession.

Thus, the arguments for sociopolitical competency are grounded in professional ethics, DEI considerations, the effective delivery of clinical services to all populations, educational effectiveness, and scientific integrity. In addition, when psychologists and their institutions demonstrate sociopolitical competency, this enhances public trust in the profession. Surveys show declining public confidence in science, mainly among conservatives, who see scientists as partisan actors (Milkoreit & Smith, 2024). Many conservative and religious communities also shy away from seeking psychological services, fearing that psychologists will not understand or respect their values (Redding, 2020; Richards & Bergin, 2014).

Sociopolitical competence is not about forsaking one’s values or achieving ideological balance or neutrality. Rather, it entails a commitment to self-awareness, understanding, and empathy, as well as ethical and sociopolitically competent clinical practice, research, and teaching, all supported by institutional structures and professional standards that promote sociopolitical diversity. We are hopeful that psychologists will embrace sociopolitical competency, seeing parallels in recent efforts by the field regarding religious competency. For years, psychology largely ignored or pathologized certain religious beliefs (Cummings et al., 2009). Like sociopolitical diversity, training programs provided little education in working with religious clients, and practitioners often reported discomfort in doing so. There also was relatively little psychological research on religious issues (Cummings et al., 2009). Over time, the field came to recognize that religion is central to many people’s identities and psychological functioning and that a lack of cultural competence with religious clients or biases against their views impairs treatment efficacy. This led to treatments adapted for religious populations (e.g., Hook et al., 2009), religious competency guidelines (Vieten & Lukoff, 2022), training curricula for religious competency (Pearce et al., 2019), and measures to assess clinical competence in working with religious populations (Hook et al., 2013).

Conclusion

Research demonstrates the significant impact that SPAVs often have on people’s identities, psychological functioning, and relationships (including those between psychologists and their clients), having substantial implications for the work that psychologists do. We have provided the first comprehensive set of research-based principles for effectively addressing SPAVs in clinical practice, psychological research, education, and professional training. On the basis of these principles, we also provided an action plan for the profession to promote sociopolitical competence and diversity. We encourage further research on these principles, particularly regarding best implementation practices and the development of instruments for assessing sociopolitical values and competency.

These principles, which include many specific competencies and skills, require a fundamental shift in the professional culture of psychology to embrace sociopolitical diversity, even when doing so is morally challenging to psychologists’ own passionately held worldviews. Psychologists should commit to examining their own biases, valuing sociopolitical diversity in clinical practice, research, and teaching, and serving diverse sociopolitical communities in an unbiased and culturally competent fashion. Graduate programs should create sociopolitically inclusive climates and diversify their faculties and student bodies. Professional organizations should incorporate into their ethics codes prohibitions against sociopolitical discrimination and sociopolitical competency training into their multicultural training requirements. These efforts would improve access to care and provide more effective care for diverse sociopolitical communities, establish an inclusive professional culture, strengthen and broaden the scope of psychological research and teaching, and enhance public trust in the profession while positioning the field as a model for engaging constructively across political divides.
