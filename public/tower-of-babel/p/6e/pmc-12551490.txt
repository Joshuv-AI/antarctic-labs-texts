Association between sleep duration at 12 and 18 months and epilepsy risk at 2–3 years: the Japan Environment and Children’s Study
Authors: Yuto Arai, Tohru Okanishi, Toshio Masumoto, Hisashi Noma, Yoshihiro Maegaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep deprivation has notable effects on neurobehavioural functioning in children. However, the link between sleep duration and the risk of developing epilepsy in early childhood remains uncertain. The aim of this study was to explore the association between sleep duration at 12 and 18 months and the risk of epilepsy at 2 and 3 years of age.MethodsData from the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS), a largescale birth cohort, were analysed. We assessed the risk of epilepsy at 2 and 3 years of age among children sleeping under 11 hours per night, the minimum recommended by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine for 1–2 year olds.ResultsOverall, 86 441 children were retrospectively analysed. Of these, 8786 (10.2%) and 12 850 (14.9%) slept for less than 11 hours at 12 months and 18 months, respectively. Multivariate logistic regression showed that a sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months remained significantly associated with epilepsy incidence at 2 and 3 years (adjusted OR (aOR) 2.02, 95% CI 1.01 to 4.01, p=0.045; aOR 1.98, 95% CI 1.15 to 3.41, p=0.013). Furthermore, a sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 and 18 months was significantly associated with epilepsy incidence at 3 years (aOR 2.77, 95% CI 1.28 to 6.01, p=0.010).ConclusionsInsufficient sleep in infancy may be linked to epilepsy later in childhood. These findings highlight the importance of sleep interventions during early life to improve long-term neurological health.Keywords: Child, Health Policy, Infant, Sleep, Seizures

Introduction

Sleep plays a critical role in early infancy, contributing significantly to brain development.1 2 Sleep is divided into rapid eye movement (REM) and non-REM sleep, both essential for early brain maturation.1 3 In animal models, REM sleep deprivation has been shown to impair synaptic transmission by affecting long-term potentiation4 5 and reducing dendritic growth.6 7 Moreover, inefficient neonatal sleep, as quantified by increased time in quiet sleep, has been linked to poorer motor and cognitive outcomes at 18 months of age.8

Sleep deprivation has notable effects on neurobehavioural functioning in children.9 Touchette et al demonstrated that persistent short sleep or trends towards short sleep between ages 2.5 and 6 predict higher hyperactivity and impulsivity, along with lower performance on neurodevelopmental tasks at school entry (ages 5–6).10 Moreover, sleep onset difficulties in infancy have been associated with later autism diagnoses and reduced hippocampal volume.11

Research links sleep deprivation to cognitive and behavioural problems; however, the exploration of how childhood sleep duration influences the risk of epilepsy onset has been reported in only a few studies. Epilepsy results from abnormal neural networks, with some types showing specific network disturbances.12,15 These networks develop over the first few years of life, making early childhood critical for neural circuit formation.16 It is hypothesised that disrupted sleep might impair network development, contributing to epilepsy onset.

While previous studies have linked sleep deprivation to neurodevelopmental issues, the specific association between sleep duration and the onset of epilepsy has been explored in only a few, leaving a gap in understanding how early-life sleep patterns impact long-term neurological outcomes. The Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS), a large-scale birth cohort study funded by the Japanese Ministry of the Environment (N/A), investigates how environmental factors, including sleep duration, affect child development.17 18 This data set provides an opportunity to assess the relationship between early sleep duration and the onset of epilepsy. We hypothesised that shorter sleep in early childhood increases the risk of developing epilepsy, which we investigated using the JECS data.

Methods

Measurement of sleep duration

To assess sleep duration at 12 and 18 months, mothers were asked to complete a questionnaire indicating when their infants had slept the previous day. They recorded their infants’ sleep over a 24 hours period, from noon of the previous day to noon of the following day, by marking the corresponding 30 min intervals in which their infants had slept. In this study, we set a cut-off of 11 hours for sleep duration based on the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommendation, which suggests that infants aged 1–2 years should sleep for 11–14 hours within a 24 hours period.19 In several studies, the lower limit of the AASM’s recommended sleep duration has been recommended as a cut-off.20,22

Design and participants

In this study, we prospectively examined the association between total sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months, 18 months or both and the risk of epilepsy at 2 and 3 years of age using the JECS data set. Details of the JECS methodology have been previously published.17 18 The JECS recruited pregnant women nationwide between January 2011 and March 2014, and the study is ongoing, with plans to follow participants until the children are 18 years old. We included 104 062 records from the jeсs-ta-20190930 data set, released in October 2019 and revised in 2022. Cases involving abortion, stillbirth, missing sleep data at 12 and 18 months or epilepsy diagnosed at 12 months were excluded.

Outcome: epilepsy among 2- and 3-year-old children

Epilepsy incidence was estimated using data from the C-2- and C-3-year questionnaires, completed when the children were 2 and 3 years old. In the self-reported questionnaires, mothers were asked, ‘Has your child been diagnosed with epilepsy by a physician?’ Children whose mothers answered ‘Yes’ were classified as having a diagnosis of epilepsy.

Statistical analyses

Data were analysed to evaluate the association between total sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months, 18 months or both and the incidence of epilepsy at 2 and 3 years of age. We used univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to assess the risk of incidence of epilepsy; for the multivariate model, we included the variables of maternal academic and social history,23 24 history of maternal epilepsy,25 maternal alcohol consumption during pregnancy,26 maternal age at delivery,27 hypertensive disorders complicating pregnancy, labour abruption, premature birth, low birth weight, chromosomal abnormalities, milk nutrition at 1 month of age28 and neonatal jaundice requiring phototherapy.29 We present crude (cORs) and adjusted ORs (aORs) with 95% CIs. Multicollinearity, which can obscure the individual effect of each variable, was assessed using a multivariate analysis. Variance inflation factors were used to evaluate multicollinearity, with values greater than 10 indicating significant multicollinearity. Data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics V.25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Patient and public involvement statement

This study did not involve patients or public.

Results

Overall, 104 062 entries were provided in the data set, of which 3759 were excluded because of abortion or stillbirth; 9824 and 3952 owing to missing sleep data at 12 months and 18 months, respectively; and 86 because of a diagnosis of epilepsy by age 1. Consequently, data from 86 441 children were analysed (figure 1). The demographic information of the participants is presented in table 1. The number of children with a sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months and 18 months was 8786 (10.2%) and 13 172 (14.9%), respectively.

Characteristics of the study participant

The number of children diagnosed with epilepsy at age 2 was 54, while at age 3, it was 89. Among the 54 children who developed epilepsy at 2 years of age, 10 children (18.5%) had a sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months of age, 9 children (16.7%) had less than 11 hours at 18 months and four children (7.4%) had less than 11 hours at both 12 and 18 months. Among the 89 children who developed epilepsy at 3 years of age, 16 children (18.0%) had a sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months, 19 children had less than 11 hours at 18 months (21.3%) and seven children (7.9%) had less than 11 hours at both 12 months and at 18 months. Table 2 presents the results of the logistic regression analysis for total sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months, 18 months or both, and the incidence of epilepsy in 2 and 3 year olds. In the unadjusted analyses, a sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months was significantly associated with the incidence of epilepsy at 2 and 3 years of age (cOR 2.03, 95% CI 1.02 to 4.03, p=0.044; cOR 1.97, 95% CI 1.15 to 3.39, p=0.014). A total sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 and 18 months was significantly associated with epilepsy incidence at 3 years (cOR 2.77, 95% CI 1.28 to 6.01, p=0.010).

After adjusting for maternal risks, complications and child characteristics, a sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months remained significantly associated with epilepsy incidence at 2 and 3 years (aOR 2.19, 95% CI 1.10 to 4.38, p=0.026; aOR 2.11, 95% CI 1.20 to 3.70, p=0.010). Among children with epilepsy at ages 2 and 3, variance inflation factor values for maternal age at delivery (≥35 years), maternal academic history (junior high school), maternal alcohol consumption during pregnancy, household income (<4000 yen/year), maternal epilepsy, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, labour abruption, gestational age <37 weeks, birth weight <2500 g, chromosomal abnormalities, milk nutrition at 1 month, neonatal jaundice requiring phototherapy and sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months, 18 months or both were all below 10, indicating no multicollinearity among these variables.

Discussion

In this large-scale prospective cohort study, examinations were conducted on how sleep duration at 12 and 18 months affects the risk of developing epilepsy. The findings revealed that a total sleep duration of less than 11 hours at 12 months, which is the lower limit of the AASM’s recommended sleep duration for children aged 1–2 years, was associated with an increased risk of epilepsy at both 2 and 3 years of age. Furthermore, a total sleep duration of less than 11 hours at both 12 and 18 months was associated with an increased risk of developing epilepsy at 3 years of age.

Owing to ethical concerns, experimental data on sleep deprivation in children are limited. However, research indicates that sleep deprivation can lead to problematic behaviours,30 decreased attention,31 32 anxiety, depression33 and hyperactivity.34 In a few studies, however, a link has been explicitly demonstrated between sleep deprivation and the risk of epilepsy.

Epileptogenesis may be triggered by the failure of normal adaptive processes related to synaptic and homeostatic plasticity.35 The Synaptic Homeostasis Hypothesis proposes that sleep facilitates homeostatic downscaling of synaptic strength to optimal levels for associative plasticity during wakefulness.36 Additionally, the glymphatic system, activated during sleep, plays a key role in clearing excitotoxic substances, such as glutamate, from the central nervous system.37 38 Sleep deprivation may promote the accumulation of glutamate, which is associated with epileptic seizures.39 40 Therefore, since sleep is essential for maintaining homeostasis and stability in neural networks, insufficient sleep could disrupt synaptic plasticity and lead to glutamate accumulation, increasing the risk of epilepsy onset.

The findings of this study reveal that appropriate sleep during early childhood may play a crucial role in neurodevelopment and that early sleep interventions could contribute to preventing epilepsy. For instance, a responsive parenting (RP) intervention may help establish sleep rhythms in infants.41 This intervention guides parents on sleep-related behaviours, such as creating consistent bedtime routines, promoting self-soothing, encouraging early bedtimes and addressing night-time awakenings. In future research, intervention studies, such as RP, should be explored and long-term follow-up investigations should be conducted to confirm their relationship with epilepsy onset.

This study has several limitations. The presence or absence of epilepsy at ages 2 and 3 was determined based on caregiver reports, although verification through medical records would have been preferable. In addition, detailed clinical information on epilepsy, including age at seizure onset, seizure type, neuroimaging findings, EEG findings and aetiology was not available in this data set. Genetic predispositions42 could not be fully accounted for, and these underlying conditions may have influenced both sleep and epilepsy. Furthermore, we could not exclude the potential effects of family history of epilepsy, consanguinity43 or head trauma during infancy.44 Therefore, future studies should include these clinical characteristics in the analyses. Second, since the sleep duration assessed in this study was limited to a single day, it may not accurately reflect the typical sleep patterns of individual infants. In addition, we did not adjust for the potential effects of acute illnesses, such as fever, the use of medications that may affect sleep patterns or the neurodevelopmental characteristics of the children. Future studies should consider longer term sleep measurements while accounting for such factors. Finally, regarding our statistical analysis, the number of cases developing epilepsy in this study was limited, and the CIs were wide. Therefore, strong predictions cannot be made based on these results. Furthermore, while ORs derived from logistic regression can be used to assess risk, they do not establish causality, and the results should be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, children who slept less than 11 hours per day at 12 months were observed to have an increased risk of developing epilepsy by age 3. These findings suggest a potential association between shorter sleep duration in infancy and epilepsy onset, although causality cannot be established. However, these findings highlight the importance of sleep interventions during infancy, as adequate sleep during early childhood may play a preventative role in epilepsy onset.
