Nutritional supplementation of ahiflower seed and press cake in laying hens and its effect on production performance, nutrient digestibility, egg quality, and yolk omega-3 fatty acid enrichment during mid- to post-peak production phase☆
Authors: Roseline O Ogory, Greg Cumberford, Karen Schwean-Lardner, Rex W Newkirk, Deborah Adewole
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAhiflower seed (AFS) and ahiflower press cake (AFP) are rich in n-3 fatty acids and other nutrients that can be beneficial for laying hens or deposited in their eggs. A total of 288 36-week-old white leghorn hens were allocated into eight treatments with six replicates of 6 birds/cage for 12 weeks. The treatments included the control (CON), CON + 10% flaxseed (10%FX), 1, 5, or 10% AFS, or 5, 10, or 15% AFP. Overall, 10%AFP increased BW compared to 1%AFS. Diets did not affect n-6/n-3, apparent total tract digestibility (ATTD) of energy, and most of the egg quality parameters. In the egg yolk, stearidonic acid was increased (P<.0001) in 10%AFS (0.15mg/g) compared to CON (0.01mg/g), 10%FX (0.08mg/g), and all other treatment groups. The eicosapentaenoic acid was increased (P=0.0001) in 10%AFS (0.28mg/g) and 10%FX (0.29 mg/g) compared to CON (0.05mg/g). Compared to CON (1.49 mg/g) and other treatment groups, 10%FX (9.47mg/g) increased (P0.000) α-linolenic acid while docosahexaenoic acid was increased (P0.000) by 10%AFS (5.39mg/g) compared to CON (2.82mg/g). Hens fed 10%FX, 5% and 15%AFP had increased (P=0.0001) linoleic acid (34mg/g) compared to CON (26.6mg/g). In conclusion, 10%AFS increased n-3 FAs in egg yolk with no effect on egg quality, eggshell Ca and P levels, ATTD of energy, and n-6/n-3 ratio.Keywords: Ahiflower, Digestibility, Egg yolk fatty acid profile, Egg quality, Laying hens, Production performance

Introduction

The fatty acid (FA) profile of egg yolk is determined by the components and concentrations of fatty acids present in the dietary ingredients fed to the laying hens (Cartoni Mancinelli et al., 2022; Shahid et al., 2015). Therefore, changing the composition of diets by including ingredients that are rich sources of omega-3 fatty acids (n-3 FAs) will enrich egg yolks with such fatty acids, making them healthier for human consumption (Cartoni Mancinelli et al., 2022; Shahid et al., 2015).

Flaxseed is a source of energy and protein in poultry diets and is used in the poultry industry for enhancing the n-3 FAs profile of eggs due to its high n-3 FAs content. Flaxseed is also used in human diets due to its health benefits (Cherian and Quezada, 2016). The price of flaxseed is increasing rapidly in the market due to geopolitical issues that tend to influence the price of this oilseed (Tass et al., 2024; Tyagi, 2024). Therefore, poultry nutritionists seek alternative, non-conventional feed ingredients that can supply nutrients to laying hens and serve as sources of n-3 FAs in their eggs.

Ahiflower (Buglossoides arvensis (L.) seeds and press cake could be alternative choices due to their nutritional value (Cumberford and Hebard, 2015). Ahiflower is an annual plant that is indigenous to Europe and Asia but can be cultivated in temperate geographical locations (Cumberford and Hebard, 2015). Ahiflower seed (AFS) and ahiflower presscake (AFP) are derived from the ahiflower plant and they contain n-3FAs and several nutrients that qualify them as potential partial replacements for common feed ingredients such as corn, soybean meal, and flaxseed in laying hen diets (Ogory et al., 2025). AFS has been reported to contain 12% crude protein (CP), 3,031 kcal/kg of energy, 13% calcium (Ca), and 0.55% phosphorus (P) (Ogory et al., 2025). The fatty acid profile of AFS showed that it contains 0.86mg/g stearidonic acid (SDA), 0.32 mg/g gamma linoleic acid (GLA), 2.95 mg/g alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), and 0.10 mg/g docosahexaenoic acid DHA (Ogory et al., 2025). The AFP has 20% CP, 2,330 kcal/kg GE, 11% Ca, 0.58% P, 0.36 mg/g SDA, 0.98mg/g linoleic acid (LA), and 0.16 mg/g GLA (Ogory et al., 2025).

Our previous study (Ogory et al., 2025) showed that the inclusion of AFS (5 or 10% AFS ) in the diets of 64-week-old Lohmann Lite white leghorn hens enriched their eggs with n-3 FAs (ALA, SDA, EPA and DHA).We also demonstrated that AFS and AFP can be used as partial replacement for soybean meal, corn, and Ca sources, to reduce the cost of feeding laying hens without negatively affecting egg quality and production performance.

However, these have not been confirmed in the diet of hens during the mid- to post-peak production phase. Age has been reported to influence hens’ nutrient utilization (Drozdowski, 2006; Gu et al., 2021). As birds get older, nutrient digestibility is adversely affected due to a decline in physiological and morphological activities of the gastrointestinal tract (Drozdowski, 2006; Gu et al., 2021). Elnesr et al. (2024) confirmed that utilization of trace minerals varies with physiological factors, including the hen's age and production stage, which affects how nutrients are deposited into the egg. It has also been demonstrated that older hens require more Ca than younger ones (Pelicia et al., 2009). Additionally, studies have shown that older hens deposit more n-3 FAs in eggs than younger birds (Gonzalez-Esquerra and Leeson, 2001). Therefore, the objective of the current study was to evaluate the effect of dietary supplementation of AFS and AFP in the diets of laying hens during the mid-to post-peak production phase as alternative sources of Ca, protein, energy, and polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFAs) and their influence on production performance, egg quality, the mineral content (Ca and P) in eggshell, and egg yolk fatty acid profile.

Materials and methods

Birds, diets, and housing

Approval for this study was obtained from the University of Saskatchewan animal care committee (Animal Use Protocol # 20230103). Lohmann Lite white leghorn hens (36 weeks old) were obtained from the University of Saskatchewan Poultry Research and Teaching Unit, with 288 hens randomly selected and distributed in a Specht conventional cage consisting of 24 sets of 2 cages/set. Each cage housed 6 hens with a stocking space of 76 inches2 in an environmentally controlled poultry unit. The lighting, ventilation, and heat were controlled for the optimum performance of the birds, and the birds were provided with 16 hours of light, 8 hours of darkness, and incandescent bulbs as a source of light throughout the experiment. Each cage represented one replicate. The birds were randomly assigned to eight experimental diets representing various treatments. The trial was conducted for 12 weeks, which was divided into 3 periods with four weeks per period; the birds were 40, 44, and 48 weeks old at the end of each period. The diets were corn-soybean meal based (formulated to be isocaloric and isonitrogenous) in mash form and were formulated following the guidelines for Lohman LSL Lite hens. As presented in Table 1, the diets included control (CON), CON + 10% flax seed (10% FX), CON + 1% ahiflower seed (1% AFS), CON + 5% AFS (5% AFS), CON + 10% AFS (10% AFS), CON + 5% ahiflower press cake (5% AFP), CON + 10% AFP, and CON+15% AFP (15% AFP). The AFS and AFP (supplied by Nature’s Crop International) were incorporated in the respective formulations to replace energy, protein, and Ca rich ingredients in the basal diet. Acid-insoluble ash (1%) was added to the diet as an indigestible marker all through the trial. Birds had access to water ad libitum from a nipple drinker.

Production performance

The body weight of the individual hens and feed intake for each cage were measured at the start of the trial and on weeks 4, 8, and 12 of the trial for the determination of average BW for each period (ABWP) and average feed intake for each period (AFIP). The number of eggs produced daily was recorded every day to determine egg number (EN) per period (ENP) and weighed to determine the average egg weight (EW) per period (AEWP). Also, feed conversion ratio (FCR) and hen-day egg production (HDEP) were calculated per period as shown in our previous study (Ogory et al., 2025).

Egg yolk and diet fatty acid analysis

At the end of the trial, one egg was collected from each replicate cage after which the yolk was separated from the albumen, kept in a 50 mL tube and stored frozen. Egg yolk and diet samples were analyzed for fatty acids at the University of Guelph lipid analytical laboratories. A 40 mL mixture of chloroform and methanol at a 2:1 ratio was used to extract, homogenize, and filter the sample. Twenty (20) mL of filtered sample was combined with 4.5 mL of water, then homogenized and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 minutes. After discarding the supernatant, 1 mL of the solvent was collected into a test tube, followed by evaporation under a gently flowing nitrogen stream and dissolution in toluene (25 mg/mL). Then, 20 μL of the solution was combined with 160 μL of toluene and 20 μL of Meth-Prep II transesterification reagent; finally, 1 μL of this mixture was injected into the gas chromatograph to determine the fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) (Aguillón-Páez et al., 2020).

Determination of nutrient digestibility

Celite (acid insoluble ash; AIA) was included in the diet at 1% for the determination of apparent total tract digestibility of energy, Ca, and P. Two days before the end of the trial, aluminum pans were placed underneath the cages and fresh feces (free of feed and feathers) were collected every 3 hours to ensure adequate quantity for the various analyses. The feces were stored in a -20°C freezer and then freeze-dried, after which they were ground with the Retsch Ultra Centrifugal Mill ZM200 and stored at room temperature for AIA, dry matter (DM), GE, and minerals (Ca and P) laboratory analyses. The DM, GE, CP, Ca, and total P were determined in both fecal and diet samples.

Chemical analysis

The chemical analysis procedures for AIA, DM, GE in diets, and minerals (Ca and P) in diets and eggshell were conducted as described previously by Ogory et al. (2025). Briefly, AIA in fecal and diet samples was determined in triplicates using the method of Vogt Mann et al. (1975). The DM was determined in duplicates using AOAC (1990) method, while GE was determined in duplicates using the bomb calorimeter and the ICP -MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) was used to determine the concentration of Ca and P in feces, diets and eggshells.

Egg quality

Every 4 weeks, four eggs were collected from each cage to evaluate egg quality. The egg quality parameters measured in this study include the egg-breaking strength, specific gravity, egg weight, shell thickness, albumen height, Haugh units, and yolk color. Eggs were dipped into a salt solution with concentrations ranging between 1.060 to >1.100 at increasing concentrations of 0.005, and egg floatation per salt concentration was recorded as the specific gravity value. The Egg Tester Ultimate™ (ORKA Food Technology) was used to determine the other egg quality parameters except shell weight. This equipment uses the DSM Yolk Color Fan (graded from 1-16), also known as the Roche color fan, to determine the YC. The eggshells were washed and dried, and the dried shells were weighed, pooled to represent a cage, and finely ground using a coffee grinder, which was used for eggshell Ca and P analyses.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Analysis Software (SAS) Program v9.4 mixed procedure was used to analyze the data in a completely randomized experimental design. Mean values were compared using the Tukey test and are presented in the tables with the standard error of the means (SEM). A probability (P) value below 0.05 (P<0.05) was considered significant. Furthermore, data for HDEP and egg quality were transformed (log (+1) to normalize data distribution. The non-parametric method on the Minitab software was used to analyze the Ca: P levels and the median values are presented.

Results

Fatty acid profile of the diet

The AFS and AFP used in the current study were from the same batch as those used in our previous study (Ogory et al., 2025). Thus, the FAs profiles of AFS and AFP were reported in that study, while the nutrient composition and FA profiles of the diets are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. The 10% AFS diet had a higher content of SDA (3.35mg/g) and GLA (1.02 mg/g) than the control (0.00 mg/g SDA; 0.01 mg/g GLA) and the 10% FX diet (0.02 mg/g SDA; 0.02 mg/g GLA). Compared to CON (19.79mg/g LA and 19.99mg/g total n-6), the LA and total n-6 FA were higher in the 10% FX-supplemented diet (22.51 mg/g LA and 22.89 mg/g total n-6). In addition, the 10% FX-supplemented diet had increased content of ALA (22.96 mg/g), PUFA (46.00 mg/g), total n-3 (23.13 mg/g), total FAs (84.35 mg/g), and the lowest n-6: n-3 (0.99), compared to other treatments.

Egg quality and eggshell calcium and phosphorus levels

The external and internal egg quality parameters evaluated in this study include egg-breaking strength, specific gravity, egg weight, shell weight, shell thickness, albumen height, Haugh unit, and yolk color (Tables 3, 4). The yolk color was significantly influenced by dietary treatments in periods 1, 2 and 3. In periods 1 and 2, the yolk color was lighter (P=0.011 and 0.016 respectively) by the 5% AFS compared to 5% AFP and 15% AFP. In period 3, yolk color was made dark (P<0.001) by all AFP inclusion levels, compared to all AFS inclusion levels. Furthermore, 10% AFS increased egg weight (P=0.012) in period 1 and shell weight in period 3, compared to CON. An interaction was observed between period and treatment for shell weight such that it was significantly increased (P=0.015) by 10% AFS compared to CON and 1% AFS, in period 3. Overall, there was no significant effect (P>0.05) of diet on the egg quality and eggshell Ca and P levels, except the yolk color and eggshell Ca:P levels (Table 5). The eggshell Ca: P was significantly reduced (P= 0.036) by 5% AFS compared to all AFP, 1%, and 10% AFS but did not differ from CON.

Production performance

The production performance results are presented in (Table 7). Overall, a significant effect (P=0.021) of dietary treatment was observed on body weight (BW) but not on FCR, hen day egg production (HDEP), or feed intake (FI). The BW was increased (P=0.021) by 10% AFP compared to 1% AFS but did not differ from CON, 10% FX, 5 and 10% AFS, and 5 and 15% AFP. Also, BW was influenced (P<0.05) by dietary treatments in periods 1 and 2. In period 1, BW was increased (P=0.0035) by 10% AFP compared to CON, 1% AFS, and 15% AFP but did not differ from 10% FX, 5 and 10% AFS, and 5 and 10% AFP; while in period 2, the 10% AFP-supplemented diet also increased (P=0.020) BW compared to 1% AS but did not differ from CON, 5 and 10% AFS, and 5 and 15% AFP. The HDEP was significantly influenced by dietary treatment in period 1, as it was increased (P=0.018) by 1 and 5% AFS, and 10% AFP, compared to CON, but did not differ from 10% FX, 10% AFS, 5 and 15% AFP. A significant effect of period was observed on the production performance (Table 7). The BW increased (P=0.008) in periods 2 and 3, compared to period 1. Feed intake also increased (P=0.043) in period 2, compared to period 1, while the HDEP and FCR reduced (P<.0001) as birds aged.

Discussion

Egg quality involves the assessment of the internal and external quality of eggs within recommended standards (Dilawar et al., 2021). The egg quality parameters determined in this study include egg-breaking strength, specific gravity, egg weight, shell thickness, albumen height, Haugh unit, and yolk color. We observed that egg weight and shell weight were increased by dietary supplementation of 10% AFS in periods 1 and 3, respectively. Dietary protein has been observed to be one of the factors that increase egg weight and shell weight (Keshavarz and Nakajima, 1995). The increase in egg weight and shell weight in our study by dietary supplementation of 10% AFS could be because the 10% AFS diet had a CP content of 20% which was the highest among all the treatments, while other treatments have CP between 16.4 and 18.3%. Joseph et al. (2000) and Parsons et al. (1993) reported an increase in egg weight from increased dietary supplementation of CP and Keshavarz and Nakajima (1995) reported that increasing dietary protein levels to 21% increased egg weight and shell weight. In our previous study with older laying hens, dietary treatment did not affect egg weight and shell weight (Ogory et al., 2025). In that study, the CP content of all the dietary treatments was below 19%.

Dietary treatments affected the yolk color across all periods and overall. In every period and overall, the yolk color was darker with AFP than with AFS. Yolk color is an important parameter influenced by various pigments deposited in the egg yolk, which affects pigmentation and, in turn, consumer acceptance (Jiang et al., 1992; Nys, 2000). The DSM color fan ranges from 1 to 15, indicating light to dark color intensity, from light yellow to orange-red (Toomer et al., 2019). Xanthophyll and carotenoids, mainly sourced from corn, are the primary pigments responsible for yolk coloration, especially producing a yellowish hue. In the current study, corn was partially replaced by AFS and AFP as energy sources. This replacement likely affected yolk pigmentation due to changes in xanthophyll concentrations after substituting corn with AFS and AFP. However, we did not measure the xanthophyll and carotenoid levels in the diets. In our previous study (Ogory et al., 2025), we used the Commission Internationale de L’Eclairage method to measure the color of egg yolks from 64-week-old birds fed diets with the same levels of AFS and AFP. There, we observed that 5% AFP reduced yellowness (L*); 10% FX and 15% AFP reduced redness (a*) and yellowness (b*). Similarly, Cherian and Quezada (2016) used 48-week-old hens to assess yolk color with the Roche color fan and reported that 10% FX reduced yolk color, resulting in a paler yellow yolk. Panaite et al. (2021) found that dietary supplementation of soaked flaxseed meal at 6% and 12% increased yolk color (measured using the Roche color fan) in 30-week-old hens. It should be noted that the methods used for determining yolk color in all these studies varied, and their dietary compositions were different.

Regarding production performance, we observed the effect of period on production performance. The BW, FCR, and HDEP were reduced while FI increased as birds aged. At this production phase, birds were diverting energy into growth, resulting in increased BW and reduced HDEP. This further resulted in reduced FCR in period 3, which suggests that the birds were using more energy for growth than for egg production during this phase. In our previous study (Ogory et al., 2025), the effect of period on production performance was determined using 64-week-old white leghorn (Lohmann lite) hens fed diets supplemented with the same levels of AFS and AFP with a stocking density of 5 birds per cage which differs from the current study that used 6 birds per cage. We observed that as birds increased in age, FCR and BWG reduced, but FI and HDEP increased. Our observation with older laying hens suggests that they were diverting more energy into egg production rather than body weight (Ogory et al., 2025). Production performance can be influenced by age; however, this effect may vary depending on environmental conditions, such as stocking density. It has been observed that a higher hen number in a cage reduces FI and HDEP. Similar to our findings, Bovera et al. (2014) determined the effect of group size on production performance and egg quality in 20-36-week, Lohmann Brown laying hens fed a corn-soybean meal diet with 25 hens in a cage (749 cm2/hen) and reported that BW increased in week 36 while FI, HDEP and FCR reduced as birds aged. Additionally, Rakonjac et al. (2018) reported that the HDEP of Isa Brown hybrid hens was higher when birds were at 26 weeks than at 36 weeks. Şekeroğlu et al. (2014) reported that age influenced HDEP of hybrid ATAK-S laying hens with 5 birds per cage fed a typical isonitrogenous and isocaloric diet, such that HDEP was higher when birds were 36-42 weeks old compared to when they were 30-35 weeks old. Dietary composition can also influence production performance in terms of protein, fat, fiber, or energy level.

Furthermore, the 10% AFP increased hen BW and HDEP in period 1. The rationale behind dietary supplementation of 10% AFP improving BW could relate to increased metabolizable energy. Ogory et al. (2025) reported increased apparent metabolizable energy (AME) and nitrogen-corrected AME by dietary supplementation of AFS and AFP as partial sources of energy and protein in the diet of 64-week-old laying hens. Thus far, only one study (Ogory et al., 2025) has determined the effect of AFS and AFP on nutrient digestibility in laying hens. We reported that AFS and AFP contain high contents of NDF (24 and 32%, respectively) and NSP (180 and 232mg/g, respectively). Birds digest and utilize carbohydrates based on the dietary fiber and starch compositions, so a diet high in fiber and NSP decreases nutrient digestibility (Hughes et al., 2000; Jamroz et al., 2001; Khempaka et al., 2018). However, in the current study, the analyzed NDF values in the diets were similar (8.11 – 11.20%), which could have alleviated the negative effects of dietary supplementation of AFS and AFP on production performance.

The digestion and utilization of Ca and P are affected by factors such as age, Ca sources, quantity of Ca and P in the diet, concentration of phytic acid, Ca and P ratio, and vitamin D content of the diet (Adedokun and Adeola, 2013). Physiological conditions also regulate Ca absorption. Phosphorus exists in phytate form in cereal and grain crops and is not readily available for absorption by birds because they lack endogenous phytase enzyme (Adedokun and Adeola, 2013; Marounek et al., 2008). In the current study, hens fed 5% AFS had reduced ATTD of P compared to CON, 1 and 10% AS but did not differ from all AFP inclusion levels, while the ATTD of Ca was reduced by 5% AFS compared to CON, 10% FX, 1 and 10% AFS, and 10 and 15% AFP but did not differ from 5%AFP. Both AFS and AFP in this study were used to partially replace the Ca sources (limestone and dicalcium phosphate) in the diet, therefore the dietary treatments had the same quantity of Ca and P. Reduction in Ca and P digestibility by 5% AFS, respectively, may be due to the phytic acid levels in AFS (1.06%) and AFP (1.24%) (Ogory et al., 2025). Phytic acid forms insoluble complexes with minerals in the digestive tract making them unavailable for absorption by the animals (Hervo et al., 2023; Medeiros-Ventura et al., 2023). The phytic acid content of the diets used in the current study was not measured, so it is possible that the phytic acid content of other ingredients may have contributed to this effect. This is similar to what was observed in our previous study (Ogory et al., 2025), where 10% AFP reduced the ATTD of P and Ca.

Poor eggshell quality is one of the primary causes of economic losses in commercial egg production. The formation and quality of eggshells are dependent on several factors such as the source and concentration of Ca and P in the diet (Ahmad and Balander, 2003). The AFS contains 13.51% Ca and 0.55% total P, and the AFP contains 11.48% Ca and 0.62% total P (Ogory et al., 2025), resulting in a Ca level of 3.96 - 5.58% in the AFS and AFP supplemented diets. Eggshell quality in terms of egg-breaking strength and thickness can be improved when the dietary Ca level is increased but a dietary Ca level higher than 0.44% will not influence eggshell quality (An et al., 2016; Świątkiewicz et al., 2015). High egg-breaking strength and thickness are important for the egg to retain its form during transport and prevent cracking when handled (Mertens et al., 2006; Samiullah et al., 2017). We observed no significant effect of dietary treatments on eggshell Ca and P levels which can be due to low Ca and P digestibility likely resulting from the formation of complexes by phytic acid with these minerals. This agrees with the observations in our previous study where eggshell Ca and P contents were not affected by dietary treatments (Ogory et al., 2025). In the current study, we also observed that eggshell Ca: P was reduced by 5% AFS which did not differ from CON but was different from the other treatments. The non-significant effect of AFS and AFP diets which are rich in calcium (ranges between 3.97-5.58) on the eggshell Ca and P levels and the reduction of eggshell Ca: P by the 5% AFS diet containing 4.23 % Ca may also be an indication that hen regulates Ca absorption. It is possible that the hen's metabolic and homeostatic action caused this occurrence by regulating the absorption of dietary Ca into the plasma, which serves as a passage route for the deposition of Ca into the eggshell (Clunies et al., 1992; Diana et al., 2021). Previous authors have noted that a diet containing high Ca levels resulted in lower Ca absorption (Clunies et al., 1992; Diana et al., 2021). Additionally, high dietary Ca and P levels in eggshells can be boosted by enhancing their absorption into the plasma through dietary supplementation with inorganic P and exogenous phytase (Pongmanee et al., 2020; Selle et al., 2009). There is much information on the effect of dietary Ca and P on egg quality (Bar et al., 2002; Clunies et al., 1992; Diana et al., 2021; Keshavarz and Nakajima, 1993; Pelicia et al., 2009) but its effect on eggshell Ca and P levels is scarce. Furthermore, the reduced eggshell Ca:P may indicate weaker eggshell strength and reduced thickness, but in this study, dietary treatment did not influence these egg quality parameters. The correlation between egg-breaking strength, eggshell Ca and P levels, and Ca: P ratio was determined but we found no significance among them.

Flaxseed has been reported to effectively enrich eggs with n-3 FAs in several studies (Aguillón-Páez et al., 2020; Cherian and Quezada, 2016; Ehr et al., 2017). It has been recorded that the fatty acid profile of egg yolk can be modified by altering the dietary fat source and quantity (Jiang et al., 1992). We observed that palmitoleic acid composition in the yolk was reduced by 10% FX, and 10 and 15% AFP, compared to the control. The reduction of palmitoleic acid by 10%FX may be due to the high concentration of other fatty acids in the egg yolk. The dietary supplementation of 3, 6, and 9% flaxseed in the diet of 58-week-old laying hens did not affect palmitoleic acid concentration in the yolk (Huang et al., 2020). Similarly, Hudečková et al. (2012) reported that dietary supplementation of 0.3% flax oil in Isa Brown hybrid combination layers diet did not affect the yolk's palmitoleic acid concentration. However, El-Zenary et al. (2023) reported that dietary supplementation of 22.5 g/kg flax seed oil increased palmitoleic acid concentration in the egg yolk. In our previous study, we observed no significant effect of treatment on egg yolk palmitoleic acid concentration (Ogory et al., 2025). Different fat sources were used in varying inclusion levels and so the diets would differ in fatty acid composition.

In the current study, we observed that dietary treatments did not influence GLA, n-6: n-3 ratio, total SFAs, MUFAs, n-3 FAs, n-6 FAs, and PUFAs. This is an indication that younger hens are limited in the synthesis and deposition of these FAs into their egg yolk due to not fully developed organs responsible for lipid metabolism such as the liver which is the main location where lipids are synthesized in hens (Cartoni Mancinelli et al., 2022). El-Zenary et al. (2023) reported that dietary supplementation of ahiflower oil and flax oil at 7.5 and 22.5 g/kg in the diet of 53-week-old Hy-line W-36 White Leghorn hens did not affect total FAs, PUFAs, MUFAs, and SFAs but influenced GLA, and n6:n3 ratio. The authors further showed that the n6:n3 ratio was significantly reduced by dietary supplementation of ahiflower oil and flax oil at 22.5g/kg while GLA was increased by the supplementation of 22.5 g/kg ahiflower oil compared to the control, which can be due to the higher concentration of GLA in the 22.5g ahiflower oil supplemented diet, an indication that increasing the inclusion level of ahiflower oil would boost GLA enrichment in the egg yolk. In our previous study (Ogory et al., 2025), we used the same inclusion levels of FX, AFS, and AFP, as in the current study, and observed that the total SFAs and MUFAs were not influenced by the dietary treatments which agree with our observations in the current study. In addition, we reported that the n6: n3 FAs were reduced by the 10% FX and 10% AFS diet, and total PUFAs were reduced by the 1% AFS diet, which disagrees with our current observation. This aligns with our earlier hypothesis on the impact of age (and organ development) that limits the synthesis of PUFAs in the AFS and AFP diets. Aguillón-Páez et al. (2020) supplemented the diet of 27-week-old Babcock brown hen with 13.5% FX and reported that dietary treatment had no effect on SFA, MUFA, and PUFAs but reduced n6:n3 FAs compared to the control. Furthermore, in a study by Huang et al. (2020), the dietary supplementation of 3, 6, and 9% FX in the diet of 58-week-old laying hens reduced n6:n3 FAs compared to the control. The ratio of n-6:n-3 FAs was reduced due to the rich ALA composition in flaxseed, which is catalyzed by the fatty acid desaturase enzyme, to yield more n-3 FAs derivatives - the EPA and DHA.

PUFAs are categorized into n-6 and n-3 FAs based on the positioning of the double bond and each has its representative members. The representative members of n-6 include the LA and arachidonic acid while ALA, SDA, EPA, and DHA represent n-3 FAs (Gheorghe et al., 2019). Furthermore, the n-3 FAs are anti-inflammatory while the n-6 FAs are proinflammatory in function (Cumberford and Hebard, 2015; Kapoor and Huang, 2006; Lefort et al., 2016). The ALA concentration in the egg yolk was highest with 10% FX dietary supplementation. This is not surprising because FX contains 35-50% of oil, and its oil contains about 66% ALA. Therefore, FX is used in the egg industry for ALA enrichment purpose in table eggs. Despite the lower oil content in AFS (18-24%) and the lower content of ALA (45.4%) in the oil, it also resulted in a significant increase in ALA in eggs which is more than three times what was deposited in the control eggs. This agrees with our previous study (Ogory et al., 2025), where the supplementation of the same levels of AFS and FX into the diets of 64 weeks of white leghorn laying hens resulted in significant increase in ALA content. Furthermore, El-Zenary et al. (2023) reported an increase in egg yolk ALA concentration from dietary supplementation of 22.5g/kg flax oil in the diet of 53-week-old Hy-line W-36 White Leghorn hens. Additionally, Aguillón-Páez et al. (2020) observed that ALA concentration in egg yolk increased by supplementing 13.5% FX in the 27-week Babcock brown eggs diet.

The ALA undergoes desaturation and elongation to yield SDA and very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) as its immediate metabolite (Barceló-Coblijn and Murphy, 2009). The highest deposition of SDA in yolk was from hens fed 10% AFS, in which SDA concentration increased 15 times the control diet (0.01 mg/g vs. 0.15 mg/g) and almost 2 times the FX diet (0.08 mg/g vs. 0.15 mg/g). This is not surprising as AFS has been reported to have the highest SDA compared to other oilseeds rich in PUFAs such as flaxseed and camelina which contain no SDA (Cumberford and Hebard, 2015; Gheorghe et al., 2019). This result agrees with our previous study, in which we observed that dietary supplementation of 10% AFS to laying hens increased SDA composition in the egg yolk six times more than the control diet (Ogory et al., 2025). The AFS contains 0.86 mg/g SDA (Ogory et al., 2025) and the SDA content in the 10% AFS diet (3.35 mg/g) was higher than the other dietary treatments which ranged between (0-1.27 mg/g). Our observation also agrees with that of El-Zenary et al. (2022, 2023) who reported that dietary supplementation of 22.5 g/kg ahiflower oil increased the SDA content of egg yolk and thighs and breast of broilers. Also, Elkin et al. (2015) observed that 5% SDA-enriched soybean oil diet increased SDA composition of egg yolk compared to a diet containing conventional soy oil. Dietary SDA plays beneficial roles in human nutrition, including inhibition of inflammation and tumor growth (Baker et al., 2025; Whelan, 2009). Stearidonic acid is formed through the delta-6 desaturation of α-linolenic acid (ALA). Because of this, dietary SDA is considered a more effective precursor for EPA synthesis than ALA. Human studies (Baker et al., 2016; Walker et al., 2013) have shown that SDA is converted into EPA and DPA, but not DHA. Moreover, the increase in EPA levels in blood lipids or cells is greater with SDA intake compared to an equivalent amount of ALA (Baker et al., 2016). Therefore, SDA may have a stronger impact on health than ALA.

EPA and DHA are beneficial to human health and play roles in the prevention of heart disease and inflammation (Fraeye et al., 2012). In the current study, EPA concentration in the yolk was increased by dietary supplementation of 10%AFS and 10% FX while 10% AFS also increased DHA concentration. The AFS and FX are rich in ALA and there exists a competition for the fatty acid desaturase enzyme (FADS 2) involved in the synthesis of EPA and DHA and arachidonic acid via elongation and saturation of ALA and LA (Cartoni Mancinelli et al., 2022). Therefore, the higher the dietary concentration of either ALA or LA, the greater their respective members in the body tissues (Cartoni Mancinelli et al., 2022). Moreover, FADS2 tends to have a greater affinity for ALA, so with a higher concentration of ALA in the FX and AFS diets; there will be a higher deposition of EPA and DHA in the egg yolk. It has been reported that there is an increase in EPA and DHA in body tissues from SDA than from ALA, suggesting efficient elongation and desaturation of these VLCFAs from SDA, in birds fed AFS (Cumberford and Hebard, 2015; El-Zenary et al., 2023). The 10% AFS and 10% FX increased EPA equally compared to the control. This could in part be due to the oil content in FX being much higher than that of AFS at the same inclusion level. In the current study as well as in the study of Ogory et al. (2025), the 10% AFS significantly increased egg yolk DHA almost 2x more than the control. In the current study, 10% AFS increased egg yolk DHA significantly compared to 10% FX (5.39 vs 4.09 mg/g). It is important to note this significance considering FX containing nearly twice the oil content of AFS at the same 10% inclusion level (Johnson et al., 2019). The DHA is an essential omega-3 fatty acid that offers numerous health benefits, particularly for brain function, heart health, and inflammation reduction (Johnson et al., 2019). While the 5% AFS did not have effect on egg yolk DHA in the current study, Ogory et al. (2025) observed that DHA was increased by 5%. It is possible that the older hens utilized by Ogory et al. (2025) could more efficiently synthesize DHA from the 5% AFS than the younger hens used in the current study. This could likely be due to the development, maturity and increased functionality of the liver which is the main organ for fatty acid metabolism (Irawan et al., 2022). Similarly, El-Zenary et al. (2023) observed that EPA and DHA deposition in egg yolk was higher from dietary supplementation of 7.5 and 22.5g/kg ahiflower and flax oil compared to soybean oil. They stated that the soybean oil used in the diet had a low concentration of ALA and half of the fat composition was n-6 FAs resulting in the reduction of EPA and DHA deposit in the egg yolk. The mechanism behind EPA and DHA deposition is such that the higher the ALA content, the higher the deposition of EPA and DHA and the same is the case for LA deposition yielding more arachidonic acid in the yolk (Cartoni Mancinelli et al., 2022). Also, Elkin et al. (2015) observed that 5% SDA-enriched soy oil and 5% flax oil increased EPA and DHA deposits in the egg yolk while Huang et al.(2018) observed that dietary supplementation of extruded flaxseed at 7.5, 15, and 22.5% increased DHA deposition in egg yolk.

The n-6 FAs representative, LA, was increased by dietary supplementation of 10% FX and 5% and 15% AFP. It has been advised that the dietary inclusion of flaxseed for younger birds should be limited as it contains antinutrients, which can adversely affect the digestion and absorption of dietary nutrients (Shahid et al., 2020). The 15%AFP diet had the highest concentration of LA (33.95 mg/g), while the 1 and 5%AFS (23.8 and 26.4 mg/g) was a little lower than the control (26.6 mg/g) due to low fat concentration in the press cake after oil extraction, resulting in low n-3FAs deposition in the egg yolk. This is contrary to the findings from previous studies, where 10% FX in the diets of 64-week white leghorn laying hens (Ogory et al., 2025) and 24-week-old Lohman brown laying hens (Aziza et al., 2013) reduced LA deposition in the yolk. Similarly, Aguillón-Páez et al. (2020) observed that 13.5% FX in the 24-week-old Babcock brown hens diet also reduced LA deposition in the egg yolk. Furthermore, observations from Huang et al. (2020) showed that 3, 6, and 9% FX supplemented in the diets of 58-week-old white leghorn hens did not affect LA deposit in the yolk. This difference can be attributed to the age and strain of birds and FX inclusion level, which was higher in the studies of Aguillón-Páez et al. (2020) and Ogory et al. (2025) than in those of Huang et al. (2020). The n-6 FAs metabolite, arachidonic acid, was reduced as a result of the dietary supplementation of 10% FX. This is expected due to the greater affinity of FADS2 for ALA, which is high in the 10% FX diet, resulting in increased synthesis and deposition of VLCFAs into the egg yolk and reduced competition for this enzyme by LA with ALA, resulting in a reduction in the deposition of arachidonic acid in the egg yolk. This agrees with our previous study (Ogory et al., 2025), and those of Aguillón-Páez et al. (2020), Aziza et al. (2013), and Huang et al. (2018, 2020).

Conclusions

The concentration of the n-3 VLCFAs, DHA and EPA in the egg yolk was increased by dietary supplementation of 10% AFS, while 10% FX and 10% AFS increased ALA compared to the control. Overall, all the AFS and AFP inclusion levels had no negative effect on most egg quality parameters, production performance, and eggshell Ca and P levels. Also, none of the AFS and AFP inclusion levels influenced the ATTD of energy. At the same time, in comparison to the control, P and Ca digestibility were reduced by 5% AFS. In conclusion, the supplementation of 10%AFS in the diet of 36 week old laying hens increased egg yolk ALA and n-3 VLCFAs (DHA and EPA) without adversely affecting egg quality and production performance; therefore it can be used to produce n-3 FAs enriched eggs which can help to increase the competitiveness of the poultry industry because more emphasis is placed on egg n-3 VLCFAs composition due to their health benefits to humans. Therefore, 10% AFS can serve as an alternative to flaxseed which is rising in market price, for continuous availability of n-3 FAs enriched eggs and to meet the world's growing population and demand for n-3 FAs enriched eggs. The inclusion of AFP in laying hens diets could partially replace more expensive energy, protein, Ca, and P source for possible reduction of production cost (although the economics of production was not determined in this study).
