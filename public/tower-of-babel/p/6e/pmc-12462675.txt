Clinical Benefit From Molecularly Guided Cancer Care: A Matching-Adjusted Indirect Comparison of Larotrectinib Versus Standard of Care
Authors: Carsten Bokemeyer, Keith R Abrams, Jesus Garcia-Foncillas, Antoine Italiano, Ulrik Lassen, Louise Linsell, Marisca Marian, Noman Paracha, Sean D Sullivan, Nicoletta Brega, Juliette Thompson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEWidespread adoption of the tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) inhibitor larotrectinib has been hampered by limited comparisons against non–TRK inhibitor standard of care (SoC) regimens because of the rarity of TRK fusions. Cross-trial comparisons may be facilitated using matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC), a validated methodology that balances baseline population characteristics.MATERIALS AND METHODSMAIC was conducted using individual patient-level data from three larotrectinib trials, compared with aggregate real-world data (RWD) from adult patients with locally advanced/metastatic TRK fusion–positive cancer in the Hartwig Medical Foundation database. Patient populations were matched on available clinical characteristics. Estimates of overall survival (OS; defined as the time from start of first postbiopsy treatment [or larotrectinib] to death) were compared between larotrectinib and non–TRK inhibitor SoC.RESULTSThe analysis included 24 patients receiving nontargeted TRK inhibitor SoC and 120 receiving larotrectinib. After matching, median OS was 50.3 months (IQR, 23.3-not estimable) for larotrectinib versus 13 months (IQR, 6.4-18.3) for SoC; larotrectinib was associated with an 84% risk reduction of death (adjusted hazard ratio, 0.16 [95% CI, 0.07 to 0.36]). To account for the longer follow-up time in the larotrectinib group than in the SoC group (median: 56.7 v 23.2 months), a restricted mean survival analysis was conducted up to 26.2 months (largest observed event time in SoC arm), which showed median survival of 22.6 months for larotrectinib and 12.8 months for SoC (mean difference: 9.8 months [95% CI, 5.6 to 14.0]).CONCLUSIONIn patients with TRK fusion–positive cancer, larotrectinib was associated with a mean survival advantage of 9.8 months compared with non–TRK inhibitor SoC, corroborating favorable findings for larotrectinib from a previous analysis that used a different set of RWD.

INTRODUCTION

Molecularly guided approaches to cancer therapy have proven therapeutic benefit in many patients with cancer for whom standard-of-care (SoC) therapies were previously limited to nontargeted treatments, such as chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or surgery. Emergence of precision oncology, including tumor-agnostic therapies, has shifted the therapeutic landscape such that treatments can be targeted either to oncogenic-driven molecular alterations in specific tumor entities or to alterations that might be shared across multiple tumor types. However, given that many treatment-informing genomic/molecular alterations occur infrequently, the evaluation of treatment efficacy using conventional thresholds for evidence generation in the randomized clinical trial (RCT) setting is not always feasible for such small patient populations.

Larotrectinib is a highly selective, potent, central nervous system–active, orally administered, small molecule tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) inhibitor. Larotrectinib was approved in the United States and the European Union in 2018 and 2019, respectively, on the basis of findings from three multicenter, single-arm, clinical trials of larotrectinib in patients with locally advanced or metastatic TRK fusion–positive cancer.1,2 Pooled data from those studies showed an investigator-assessed objective response rate (ORR) of 79%, median duration of response (DOR) of 35.2 months, median progression-free survival (PFS) of 28.3 months, and median overall survival (OS) of 44.4 months. An expanded analysis of additional patients with longer follow-up showed independent review committee–assessed ORR of 69%, median DOR of 32.9 months, median PFS of 29.4 months, and median OS not reached.3 As a result of these positive findings, international consensus guidelines now suggest the use of diagnostic approaches and patient selection criteria to identify patients who may benefit from TRK inhibition.4 For example, in their recent precision medicine working group, the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) issued a recommendation for the use of multigene next-generation sequencing in patients with advanced cancers in countries where tumor-agnostic therapies, such as larotrectinib, are accessible.5 Larotrectinib and other TRK inhibitors in combination with genetic testing to identify tumors harboring NTRK gene fusions (which encode TRK fusion proteins) have the potential to be practice-changing. However, access challenges have limited the use of these lifesaving therapies.

Matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) is a population-adjusted method based on inverse propensity score weighting that can be used for the generation of comparative evidence on the basis of real-world clinical care,6-8 used to establish benefit of novel therapeutics when RCTs are not feasible. Such tools are particularly important for patients whose genomic or molecular profiling could inform treatments with potential for high magnitude of benefit. MAIC was previously applied to a comparison of larotrectinib with real-world SoC, using published aggregate data from patients with TRK fusion–positive cancers identified in the Flatiron Health/Foundation Medicine database—which was selected as a suitable comparator on the basis of available baseline characteristics and alignment of index date with larotrectinib studies.9,10 Compared with SoC, larotrectinib was associated with a 78% lower risk of death, with median OS of 39.7 months for larotrectinib versus 10.2 months for SoC.9 However, despite the favorable outcomes from clinical trials and from the initial MAIC analysis, testing for NTRK gene fusions is not widely used. Even patients whose genomic profiles indicate the presence of NTRK gene fusion tumors have continued to receive therapies that may be less active and/or more toxic than larotrectinib because of various reasons related to approval status in different health systems.

This study aimed to determine whether the survival benefit of larotrectinib observed in the Flatiron analysis could be validated by comparing matched patients from a separate and distinct database against patients in the larotrectinib studies, but with more mature survival data than from the initial study. We compared the survival outcomes from patients treated with larotrectinib with patients receiving SoC regimens used in routine health care practice, using a subcohort of the Hartwig Medical Foundation (HMF) database, which includes real-world data (RWD) collected from 44 hospitals in the Netherlands from 2012 to 2020.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Systematic Literature Review

To identify an appropriate SoC comparator for this analysis, a systematic literature review (Medline, Embase, and Cochrane Library) was conducted on 22 June 2024 to identify studies comparing OS in patients with NTRK fusion–positive versus NTRK fusion–negative solid organ tumors receiving systemic therapies. Of six studies meeting eligibility criteria for the analysis (Data Supplement, Fig S1 and Table S1), four were identified previously: three studies were eliminated because of limitations in available data (as previously described11-13) and one was selected (Flatiron) for the previous MAIC study.14 Of the two new studies published since 2021, one was eliminated because it was a retrospective analysis specific to patients with head and neck adenoid cystic carcinoma15; only the study by Santi et al16 provided sufficient data to conduct a MAIC analysis.

Data Sources and Sample Selection

Patient-level data from patients with TRK fusion–positive tumors were collected from an integrated population from three clinical trials, as previously described.17,18 All patients in the larotrectinib trials had a locally advanced or metastatic solid tumor with a recorded start date for treatment with larotrectinib. For the analysis, the data cutoff date was July 20, 2023.

For the non–TRK inhibitor SoC comparator, we used available data from the publication by Santi et al,16 which included whole-genome sequencing of tumor DNA for patients in the HMF data set (CPCT-02 study), encompassing 44 hospitals in the Netherlands from 2012 to 2020.16 Eligible patients for this study were age 18 years and older, had a locally advanced or metastatic solid tumor, and had an indication for a new line of systemic treatment with registered anticancer agents.

In the study by Santi et al, the following data were extracted from the HMF data set: age, patient sex, tumor type, year of biopsy, start date of first postbiopsy treatment, and number of previous lines of therapy. To increase population overlap between larotrectinib trials and the HMF data before matching on available baseline characteristics, the following inclusion criteria were applied (Fig 1): (1) treatment with larotrectinib commenced before 31 December 2019; (2) age 18 years and older; and (3) TRK inhibitor–naïve.

Data sources for comparator arms. For the standard-of-care arm, Santi et al16 used data from the HMF data set (CPCT-02 study), which was composed of 3,556 patients with a known tumor location; 24 were identified with NTRK gene fusions and included in the analysis. The three larotrectinib trials contributed a total of 302 patients: n = 13 from the phase I dose-escalation study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02122913), n = 100 from a phase I/II study in children/young adults age 21 years or younger (SCOUT, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02637687), and n = 189 from a phase II basket trial across multiple primary tumor types in adults and adolescents age 12 years and older (NAVIGATE, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02576431). To increase population overlap between patients from the larotrectinib trials and patients from CPCT-02 study before matching on available baseline characteristics, the following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) treatment with larotrectinib commenced before 31 December 2019; (2) age 18 years and older; and (3) TRK inhibitor–naïve. After applying these criteria, the number of larotrectinib patients was reduced to 120. Definition of OS

In the SoC arm, Santi et al16 defined OS using the date of first postbiopsy treatment to avoid potential immortal bias between the date of biopsy and the start of the treatment. For the larotrectinib data set, index date was defined as the date of first treatment postbiopsy identifying the presence of NTRK fusion.18 Date of death or last known date at which known to be alive was used as the censoring date.

Statistical Methods

MAIC methodology was used to compare larotrectinib versus non–TRK inhibitor SoC.19,20 MAIC adjusts for imbalances in the patient populations before performing a treatment comparison. Patients were weighted by the inverse of their propensity to be in the larotrectinib trial population compared with the comparator study using a selection of matching factors,16 to balance the covariate distribution with that of the RWD.

Individual patient data were matched to the available baseline characteristics from HMF database reported by Santi et al,16 including age group, number of previous lines of systemic therapy, NTRK1 fusion, and year of biopsy/treatment initiation (Table 1). Patient sex and primary tumor location were not included in the matching analysis because of correlation of sex with tumor location, which could result in overadjustment, and because the numbers of patients in each tumor category was insufficient to conduct a stable analysis. These variables were included in the sensitivity analysis only.

RESULTS

Patients and Matching

The analysis included 24 adult patients with solid tumors receiving non–TRK inhibitor SoC in the HMF database and 120 patients from the larotrectinib trials. The larotrectinib patients were selected after applying the sample selection criteria (Fig 1), which decreased the initial sample size from 302 (total number of larotrectinib patients) to 191 patients treated before 31 December 2019, to 122 patients age 18 years and younger, to 120 who were TRK inhibitor–naïve. The 120 patients who met all criteria were included in the larotrectinib population used in the analyses.

Before matching, median age and age range varied between SoC and larotrectinib patients (59.0 [range, 55.5-67.5] v 55.0 [18.9-84.0] years). The proportion of female to male patients was similar between arms (54.2% v 53.3% female in SoC v larotrectinib patients). Categorization by primary tumor location showed more patients receiving SoC than larotrectinib had primary tumors of the breast (20.8% v 5.8%), lung (16.7% v 11.7%) pancreas (16.7% v 1.7%), prostate (12.5% v 0.8%), skin (16.7% v 5.0%), head and neck (4.2% v 0%), and urinary tract (4.2% v 0%), whereas more patients on larotrectinib than SoC had primary tumors of bone/soft tissue (20.8% v 4.2%) and colon/rectum (6.7% v 4.2%). After matching, the effective sample size (ESS) was reduced to 36 (30% of the original sample size of 120), and the two populations were successfully matched (Table 2).

Overall Survival

The results from the unadjusted and matching-adjusted analysis of OS in patients receiving larotrectinib compared with SoC are displayed in Figure 2. There were 50/120 (41.7%) deaths in the larotrectinib trial compared with 16/24 (66.7%) in the SoC population. After matching, median OS was 50.3 months (IQR, 23.3-not estimable) for larotrectinib compared with 13 months (IQR, 6.4-18.3) for SoC, representing a 37.3-month median survival advantage. Larotrectinib was associated with an 84% risk reduction of death (adjusted HR, 0.16 [95% CI, 0.07 to 0.36]). This was comparable with the estimate obtained in the unmatched analysis (HR, 0.18 [95% CI, 0.10 to 0.34]), which was also statistically significant. Median follow-up time was 56.7 months in the larotrectinib group and 23.2 months in the SoC group.

Restricted Mean Survival

To account for the longer follow-up time in the larotrectinib group, a restricted mean survival analysis was conducted up to 26.2 months, representing the minimum of the largest observed event time in the SoC arm. Restricted mean survival was 22.6 months for larotrectinib and 12.8 months for SoC, representing a mean survival advantage of 9.8 months (95% CI, 5.6 to 14.0) with larotrectinib over SoC (Table 3). This suggests that, on average, a patient treated with larotrectinib who has 26.2 months of follow-up would be predicted to survive approximately 9.8 months longer than with non–TRK inhibitor SoC.

DISCUSSION

Clinical benefit of larotrectinib has been supported by three single-arm clinical trials. Despite being a pioneer in the new paradigm of histology-independent therapies, as well as one of the first approved therapies based on basket trials, the uptake of larotrectinib and other TRK inhibitors has been hampered by several barriers, including the inability to generate randomized data in the agnostic setting, together with the extreme rarity of NTRK gene fusion cancers whose identification requires large-scale testing. In the absence of a direct comparison between larotrectinib and SOC, the MAIC methodology was used to show a survival benefit of treatment with larotrectinib compared with SoC, on the basis of a comparison with patients from the Flatiron database. In this study, a different, independent data set was used to represent SoC and compared with a broader larotrectinib clinical data set covering a longer period of time than that available at the time of the Flatiron MAIC. After balancing patient characteristics, larotrectinib was found to be associated with an 84% reduction in risk of death, with a restricted mean survival time of 22.6 months, compared with 12.8 months for SoC. The findings were consistent in a sensitivity analysis with additional matching factors. In parallel to the present analysis, a retrospective, observational study (EPI VITRAKVI) was conducted and demonstrated therapeutic benefit of larotrectinib versus an external, historical, chemotherapy-based SoC regimen in pediatric patients with locally advanced or metastatic infantile fibrosarcoma.21

The findings from this study are consistent with a previous MAIC between larotrectinib and SoC from Flatiron/FMI.9 In this study, HR for survival was 0.16 (95% CI, 0.07 to 0.36), whereas the Flatiron comparison showed an HR of 0.22 (95% CI, 0.09 to 0.52).9 The restricted mean survival analysis showed a survival advantage with larotrectinib in both studies: 9.8 months (95% CI, 5.6 to 14.0) additional survival over a 26.2-month follow-up in this study and 10.8 months (95% CI, 7.0 to 14.6) over 30.5-month follow-up in the Flatiron comparison. Minor differences between the two MAICs may be attributed in part to differences in characteristics selected for balancing.9 For the Flatiron MAIC, available baseline characteristics for matching included NTRK1 status, age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, tumor types, number of previous lines of therapy, initial stage of diagnosis, and brain metastases. By contrast, the HMF database had available characteristics of age, sex, number of previous lines of therapy, NTRK1 status, and year of biopsy; primary tumor location was taken into account for the sensitivity analysis only. Both analyses clearly and strongly support the clinical benefit with largely improved median OS resulting from targeted therapy with larotrectinib.

Tumor-agnostic therapies, such as larotrectinib, are designed to target genomic mutations or alterations that drive tumor growth, independent of the tissue of origin. The rationale for using a tumor's genomic driver in making treatment decisions is based on the effectiveness of precision oncology therapies in directly targeting underlying causes of tumor growth. This approach underscores the need for broad genomic testing in most patients to realize a drug's benefits.22 RCTs for individual cancer types are lacking because of the rarity of some targetable alterations. However, historic classification of cancers according to organ of origin has also constrained use of molecularly targeted therapies. A commentary by Andre et al22 highlights the potential downfalls of continued organ-based classification of cancers, such as the lack of reimbursement in some countries when histology-specific cancers trials are unavailable, and the failure to realize the full clinical potential of tumor-agnostic therapies when guiding bodies, such as ESMO or ASCO, continue to issue treatment guidelines on the basis of organ of origin. Andre et al22 argue that this outdated convention has denied access to lifesaving drugs, and underscores that, with the evolution of molecular targeted therapies, the number of live years lost resulting from treatment delays may range from the hundreds of thousands to millions. Our study reinforces the survival advantage conferred by larotrectinib after successful identification of NTRK gene fusions in a vulnerable patient population that might not otherwise have access to an effective targeted therapy.

Limitations of this study include the small sample size and limited details on baseline characteristics and therapies from the SoC data set. An unanchored indirect treatment comparison requires that all prognostic factors and treatment effect modifiers are accounted for and have sufficient overlap between comparator groups. The MAIC was restricted to the small set of available baseline characteristics reported by Santi et al,16 therefore we cannot rule out an imbalance in other important characteristics that could introduced bias. Other challenges with matching include the wide age range in larotrectinib trials, large differences in distribution of primary tumors, and lack of information on severity/progression, requiring use of previous lines of therapy as proxies. Also, we were only able to adjust for NTRK1 type mutation (instead of NTRK1-3) because of the small number of patients with NTRK2 mutation in the larotrectinib trials, thus limiting further adjustment. Despite these limitations, our data add to the convincing and growing body of information supporting clinically and highly significant survival benefit from administering TRK inhibitors to their respective patient population.

Health technology assessment (HTA) bodies have a challenging task of making access decisions on the basis of limited patient numbers, cost of genomic/molecular profiling, use of nontraditional trials, and long-term uncertainties.23 Paradoxically, the challenges of designing large RCTs for small populations (eg, high costs, low enrollment numbers) are at odds with HTA standards for high value and cost-effective therapies. Embracing the added value of real-world evidence (RWE) and indirect treatment comparison approaches to access decision making offers greater opportunities to patients for improved outcomes. The Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technology and Health recently reversed its initial denial of reimbursement for larotrectinib (because of lack of RCT evidence of comparative benefit), providing a conditional positive recommendation with price reduction after submission of additional supportive clinical data, including RWE that supported the oncogenicity and mutual exclusivity of NTRK fusion in certain cancers.23,24 Likewise, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the United Kingdom has recommended funding for larotrectinib through the Cancers Drug Fund, with a managed access agreement to collect additional data, including RWE, to address uncertainties in clinical evidence. Although RWE is now taken into consideration for some HTAs during assessments of evidence, it will be increasingly important for stakeholders to adapt processes to implement RWE as a means for managing uncertainty.

In conclusion, the MAIC analysis comparing larotrectinib trial data against a matched independent data set from patients receiving SoC suggested longer OS with larotrectinib, compared with non–TRK inhibitor SoC, in adult patients with NTRK-positive cancer. These findings corroborate a previous MAIC conducted using RWD in a similar population.9 Larotrectinib data included a much broader clinical data set covering longer period of time than the previous Flatiron analysis. These findings support an expanded adoption of genomic testing to identify patients who will benefit most from precision oncology drugs.
