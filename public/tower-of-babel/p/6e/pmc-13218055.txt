Evaluating the influence of dental post diameter on treatment outcome: a systematic review
Authors: Ahmad Al Jaghsi, Dinesh Rokaya, Zaineb Amjed Al-Nuaimy, Maryam Rafea Abdulwahhab, Maryam Abdulla Shaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study systematically reviewed the impact of post diameter on treatment outcomes in endodontically treated teeth.Materials and methodsThe review followed the PRISMA guidelines, and a systematic search was conducted in the databases from 1975 to 2024. Publications were searched in PubMed, Wiley Online Library, EBSCOhost, Cochrane Library, and Scopus. A total of five studies were selected for inclusion after the screening process; three of which were cohort studies, one was prospective, and two case-control studies were included in the results. Data and outcome variables were extracted and checked for risk of bias using the ROBINS-I tool.FindingsStudies that reported a decreased risk for failure and an increased survival rate for restorations with posts of greater diameter had a moderate risk of bias. Two studies reported a greater risk for complications resulting from an increase in post diameter and a reduction in remaining dentin thickness, but had a serious risk of bias. One study, in particular, reported an improvement in the survival rate of abutments upon increasing the post diameter; however, after a certain point, any increase led to the survival rate dropping by almost half.ConclusionsCurrent evidence—largely from non-randomized studies—shows no clear association between post diameter and treatment survival, and remains limited by heterogeneity, risk of bias, and inadequate control of confounding factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-07773-1.

Introduction

Posts-and-cores provide intra-radicular retention when the remaining tooth structure of a root canal-treated tooth isn’t adequate for final restoration retention. Post-and-cores are used to improve the longevity of restorations [1, 2]; therefore, factors that affect their survival rate are to be considered. Several factors have been linked to the long-term prognosis of endodontically treated teeth and are inclusive of post length, diameter, remaining radicular dentin thickness, and post-adaptation [3]. Several studies have shown that post diameter exhibits an influence on fracture resistance, post retention, as well as protection of tooth structure [4, 5].

Three fundamental schools have been developed as criteria for the selection of post-diameter. These approaches are divided into conservationist, preservationist, and proportionist methods. The proportionist approach advises that the post diameter must be below one-third of the root diameter at its narrowest point. The preservationist approach suggests that a post thickness should be such that it is surrounded by one mm of dentin. Conservationist proponents advocated for minimal preparation of the post space and restriction of post diameter to maximize the remaining dentin bulk [6].

Various studies [7–10] compared posts of variable sizes in their experiments and compared posts of varying sizes to determine the influence of diameter on treatment outcomes. An in vitro experiment by Holmes et al. [11] compared the effects of smaller and larger post diameters in stress performance values that were essentially unchanged for all sizes and they concluded that changes in post diameter dimensions did not alter dentin stress patterns significantly. This complies with the results of the clinical studies reviewed in this systematic review. One of which concluded that the likelihood of tooth dentin fracture was insignificantly altered by the presence of a thicker or thinner post [12].

One major factor in the fracture resistance of any endodontically treated tooth is the amount of remaining dentin. The importance of the remaining bulk of tooth structure with regard to strength and resistance to root fractures has been emphasized in multiple studies [13, 14]. Correspondingly, any decrease in dentin bulk weakens the tooth and compromises fracture resistance [15–17]. When preparing the canal for a larger post-insertion, additional dentin gets removed in the process, and this lowers the fracture resistance [4]; nonetheless, increasing the diameter can also increase stiffness at the expense of remaining dentin and fracture resistance [14]. Thinner posts provide increased resistance to radicular fracture [14], thus, fabricating a post with a strength that exceeds that of the endodontically treated tooth can be detrimental in terms of clinical success [5]. Meanwhile, when using fiber posts, a wider diameter can demonstrate better fatigue performance [18]. One study suggests limiting the post diameter to one-third of the root diameter with at least 1 mm of dentinal wall remaining in order to achieve a good long-term prognosis [19]. Retention has been noted as the single most significant factor affecting the prognosis of a post-retained restorative treatment [20]. Several studies establish that there is no significant relationship between post diameter and its retention and it is not advisable to increase post diameter as a method of increasing restoration retention [17, 21, 22]. An increase in post diameter loses its retentive advantages once it exceeds one-third of the root diameter. It instead proposes that it is more effective to prioritize preserving the remaining radicular dentin thickness in order to decrease the chances of failure of the post-retained restoration [23]. Conversely, a more recent trial found evidence that in metal post systems, a post of a larger diameter yielded higher tensile retentive forces than posts of a narrower diameter [24]. Another factor studied in relation to varying post diameters is stress distribution within the radicular dentin. Minor changes in post dimensions, inclusive of length and diameter, had minimal effect on the predicted distribution of both compressive and tensile stresses within dentin [11]. A newer study utilizing finite element analysis found that when increasing post diameter, consequent stress formation in dentin decreased [25].

The traditional schools with regard to post diameter selection are proportionism, conservationism, and preservationism. The proportionist approach relies on measurement of root diameter at its narrowest point. The post diameter must not exceed one-third of this dimension. Conservationists advocate for minimizing post preparation diameter as much as possible in order to maintain adequate remaining dentin thickness. This is intended to provide sufficient retention without compromising the tooth’s fracture resistance. The preservationist method of treatment planning is to preserve at least 1 mm of radicular dentin surrounding the inserted post. Setting a minimum remaining dentin thickness to support a post restoration is recommended by proponents of this school [26].

Evidence in the literature is predominantly inclined toward the presence of a link between post diameter and post-and-core treatment success and long-term prognosis. Nevertheless, there has not been a consensus regarding the degree of effect post diameter has, as well as the optimum dimension for post selection. In order to set clear guidelines for dental practitioners to follow when fabricating posts according to certain dimensions, further investigation via a systematic review is warranted. This study systematically reviewed the impact of post diameter on treatment outcomes in endodontically treated teeth.

Materials and methods

This systematic review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42024542744). The review was conducted with adherence to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The eligibility criteria were defined in relation to the population, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO) question as follows (Table 1).

The studies included were randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective cohort studies, retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, and case series. The target population includes human participants of all age groups who have undergone endodontic treatment and subsequently received dental posts of any type. Furthermore, any study lacking clear reporting on the influence of dental post diameter on treatment success and survival rates over time will be excluded. The search scope extends from 1975 to 2024, and publications must be in the English language.

An online database search involved five databases, which are PubMed, Scopus, EBSCO host, Wiley Online Library, and Cochrane Library (Table 2). Records were identified using search strings tailored to each search engine and were imported into a citation manager (EndNote, version 20), followed by automated removal of duplicates. Screening was performed by two independent reviewers (MS and MR) and involved two phases. Initially, all titles and abstracts were screened, followed by full-text screening, and afterward, any record that met the eligibility criteria was included in the study. Any discrepancy between the findings was resolved in the presence of both reviewers. A third reviewer (Z) was responsible for the grey literature database screening as well as citation search. A similar approach to database search was used by formulating specific search strings for various grey literature libraries like WHO Global Index Medicus, Google Scholar, WorldCat, and Data Archiving and Networking Services (DANS). The results were imported into the citation manager and checked for duplicates using an automated feature of EndNote as well as manually for missed duplicates. For the citation search, an online tool (Citation-Chaser) [20] was used to conduct a forward and backward citation search in addition to a manual search to ensure no articles of relevance were missed. The third reviewer (Z) also conducted a search of trial registries in the WHO trial registry, the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ANZCTR), Clinicaltrials.gov, the Dutch Trial Register, the EU clinical trial Register, and the German Trial Register.

A total of 21,795 records were identified from 5 databases (PubMed n = 8,386, Wiley n = 3,625, EBSCO n = 3,563, Cochrane n = 1,143, Scopus n = 3078). After the removal of duplicate records (n = 2,269), the remaining 19,526 records were screened. The initial title and abstract screening yielded 191 records eligible for further full-text screening. In addition to these sources, a grey literature search was conducted (WHO Global Index n = 1,475, Google Scholar n = 978, DANS n = 47, WorldCat n = 27) as well as a citation search (2,642), and screened a total of 4,700 records after removing duplicates (n = 469).

Table 3 shows the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies falling within the subsequent categories were excluded from this review: case reports, conference abstracts, letters to the editor, non-peer-reviewed sources, reviews, laboratory studies, unpublished studies, and opinion articles. Duplicate studies or multiple reports from the same study were removed to eliminate any redundancy in data extraction. No registered trials were published except for one found in the German clinical trials register, but it was not eligible for inclusion. Searches in grey literature, citations, and trial registers were utilized to further enhance the inclusivity of our systematic review. Following exclusions based on title and abstract, non-English language, studies published before 1975, along with any manual duplicates that could not be identified using EndNote, 196 records were eligible for full-text screening. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, five clinical studies following full-text screening and removal of overlap between the main library and grey literature and citation library were included. Five studies were selected for inclusion after the screening process. To assess the risk of bias in the final selected studies, the ROBINS-I tool [27] was used. This tool uses seven domains to evaluate the risk of bias, from which an overall score can be determined. The final decision assigned to the studies after answering all relevant signaling questions in the seven domains includes low, moderate, serious, critical, or no information. Two independent reviewers, Z.A. and M.R., were responsible for this process. The effect of changing the post diameter on the treatment outcome was assessed.

The list of outcomes to be evaluated includes any complications or risks, whether mechanical or biological, resulting from an increase in diameter, the effect on the survival rate of the post-treated tooth and its restoration status, and the type of failure that results if the treatment fails due to post diameter changes. Data was extracted from the articles, including the year of publication, study design, sample size, recruitment dates, the comparator and intervention, outcome, as well as the numeric result relating to the outcome.

The overall judgment took into consideration the worst domain-level judgment, or if a certain judgment was repeated for more than half of the domains, a higher level of bias in the overall judgment was selected. No studies displayed a critical risk of bias, and all gave sufficient information to reach an overall judgment; therefore, all five studies were included. Findings

Five studies were analyzed in this report, three of which were cohorts [7–29] one was a prospective cohort [7], and the other two were case-control studies [9, 30]. The studies differed in their mention of the material used for the post, as two studies used titanium [8] and glass fiber-reinforced posts [7], while two studies did not mention the material of the post used [29, 30]. One study only mentioned the manufacturing technique but not the material of the post used [9]. Only three studies provided follow-up [7–9], with two of them being retrospective assessments of patients’ previous follow-up appointments [8, 9]. Findings of the studies

Two studies found that increasing the diameter of the post resulted in higher survival rates for post-and-core treatment [7, 8]. Naumann et al. [7] compared a 1 mm diameter coronal post with experimental interventions of 1.25 mm and 1.5 mm, with the 1.25 mm post reducing harm by 10%, and the 1.5 mm post reducing harm by 30% according to the calculated hazards ratio of 0.9 and 0.7, respectively. However, this finding was not statistically significant [23]. Caserío Valea et al. [8] studied titanium unimetric posts of 0.8 mm and 1 mm diameter at the apex and concluded that survival rates of restorations with a smaller post were less than those of a larger diameter, but the effect of post diameter on survival rate was deemed insignificant (P-value of 0.276). But Wegner et al. [9] mentioned that there is a benefit in increasing the diameter of the post, but that only applies to a certain point, after which any increase will cause harm. In terms of post-treated teeth used as abutments for a fixed prosthesis, particularly FPDs, the diameter had no significant statistical effect on the survival rate. However, that was not true for RPD abutments. All RPD abutments were restored with conical double crowns, and it was found that survival rates for posts of ISO size 90 were 60.4% after 51 months, ISO 50 were 22.9%, and ISO 110 were 35.8%. This shows that an increase in diameter from ISO 50 to ISO 90 increased survival rates of RPD abutments, but when the diameter was further increased to ISO 110, a significant drop in survival rates of almost half was witnessed.

Finally, the last two studies related the increase in post size to remaining dentin thickness [30] or described it in terms of post-to-root ratio [29] and both deemed an increase in post size unnecessary weakening of the tooth structure that leads to harm. Silva et al. [30] had CBCTs of cases of teeth with vertical root fractures and controls without vertical root fractures, where the presence of the post was determined in both to study the relation between dentin thickness and post presence with the risk of developing VRF. They concluded that lower dentin thickness (odds ratio = 4.24), along with the presence of an intraradicular post (odds ratio = 2.4), was linked to VRF. They mentioned that dentin thickness of ≤ 1.3 mm presented with a greater likelihood of fracture, while thicknesses of ≥ 1.4 mm showed less likelihood of fracture. Veloso et al. [29] compared post diameters of equal or less than a third of the root with posts greater than a third of the root. The authors deemed the use of posts larger than ⅓ of the root incorrect and did not follow recommendations. 31.1%, 20.20%, and 11.11% of teeth in coronal, middle, and apical thirds, respectively, did not follow the principle of ⅓ or less, causing unnecessary destruction of root dentin and, in turn, tooth weakening and liability of treatment failure. Finally, few studies that described benefit for the analyzed outcome had a moderate risk of bias [7, 8], while those that described harm had a serious risk of bias, either due to bias detected in confounding [9, 29] or having a moderate risk of bias in five of the seven domains [30].

Discussion

This systematic review studied the impact of the diameter of the post on endodontically treated teeth treatment outcomes within the determined inclusion and eligibility parameters. The results of this systematic review demonstrate that the clinical literature up to date states that a change in post-diameter as an intervention poses no significant impact on treatment outcomes. However, some studies show that wider posts have no significant effect on failure rates [7, 8]. Fernandes et al. [6] found that there is no significant effect of post diameter on its retention, which is similar to the findings of this review.

Similarly, Slutzky-Goldberg et al. [1] mentioned that increasing post diameter was less consequential to treatment outcome than other parameters, such as post length. The post diameter was considered insignificant to survival and success rate compared to other determinants, like the number of remaining proximal contacts. Furthermore, Standlee et al. [21] also mentioned that diameter does not significantly influence the retentive capacity of a post and core restoration. In addition, according to Velho et al. [18] post diameter significantly influences the fatigue performance of fiber-reinforced composite posts. In their in vitro experiment, wider posts performed better under fatigue testing compared to thinner posts. Although the results of this systematic review ultimately suggest that post diameter has no significant impact on treatment outcome, some clinical studies did demonstrate a slightly lower failure rate correlated with wider post samples that were tested [7, 8].

It was found that a wider stainless-steel post increases the maximum stresses on dentin [4]. Hence, a smaller dowel diameter is recommended for increased chances of treatment success [5]. Therefore, a more conservative approach in selecting post diameter is preferable to preserve the remaining dentin thickness would increase the chances of success. The stress on dentin decreased with increased dowel diameter [25]. Similarly, Goodacre et al. [19] found that increasing post diameter decreases the treated root’s fracture resistance. Reduced dentin thickness was associated with a higher risk of vertical root fracture [12].

An important contemporary consideration in restoring endodontically treated teeth is the ferrule effect. A circumferential ferrule of around 2 mm substantially increases fracture resistance and is regarded as a key determinant of the biomechanical performance and clinical longevity of endodontically treated teeth [31]. Studies have shown that a 2 mm ferrule markedly improves resistance to failure, and in some models, teeth with a 2 mm ferrule restored without a post performed as well under cyclic loading as teeth restored with a post, highlighting that an adequate ferrule can supersede the mechanical contribution of the post itself [32]. A recent systematic review and meta-analyses by Al-Dabbagh et al. [33] corroborate the strong protective role of a circumferential ferrule on survival outcomes, while also noting heterogeneity in study designs and reporting. Crucially, very few clinical studies evaluating post dimensions have reported or controlled for ferrule height/extent, making ferrule presence an important confounding variable when assessing the effect of post diameter [34]. Future clinical trials examining post diameter should therefore stratify or control for ferrule height (for example, ≥ 2 mm vs. < 2 mm) to determine whether post diameter remains a clinically relevant predictor of survival when an adequate ferrule is present.

There are some limitations in this study. In this systematic review, we have excluded the laboratory studies. The clinical trials are rare because ethical constraints often prevent dentists from placing intentional narrow or wide posts solely to observe failure rates. In this systematic review, we included fiber and metal posts, which can have differences in the elastic modulus. In addition, as we included both fixed and removable prostheses, the RPD abutments are subjected to torque and lateral forces more than single crowns. These can result in the skewness in the results. Furthermore, the post-diameter selection protocol in this study was not reported in detail. The majority of the studies that studied the impact of post diameter on treatment outcomes were retrospective; the algorithm used for post diameter selection was not reported. Further research is needed within this field is necessary to support the best approach to post diameter selection.

Conclusion

Current evidence indicates that post diameter may influence the survival of endodontically treated teeth, with potential benefits only up to a conservative threshold beyond which excessive dentin removal may increase the likelihood of failure. However, current limited evidence from non-randomized studies suggests no statistically significant association between post-diameter and treatment survival, though high heterogeneity and risk of bias limit this conclusion; these findings should be interpreted with caution. Well-designed clinical trials with larger samples and longer follow-up are needed to clarify the effects of post-diameter.
