Cervical cord atrophy correlates with intracranial lesion burden in tumefactive multiple sclerosis
Authors: Albert Aboseif, Caitlin S. Jackson-Tarlton, Christopher G. Schwarz, Paul A. Decker, Matthew L. Kosel, Jiye Son, B. Mark Keegan, Kejal Kantarci, Jeanette E. Eckel-Passow, Orhun H. Kantarci, Burcu Zeydan, W. Oliver Tobin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal cord atrophy associates with motor disability in multiple sclerosis (MS). The influence of intracranial lesion burden (ILB) on spinal cord atrophy requires further investigation. Tumefactive MS (TMS) offers a model for studying the contribution of ILB on spinal cord atrophy.ObjectivesDetermine the relationship between upper cervical cord (UCC) area, ILB, and progressive TMS.MethodsIndividuals with tumefactive demyelinating disease (TDD) undergoing UCC area analysis (C1-C3) were stratified into three groups based on ILB: single-lesion (SL-TDD), multiple-lesion (ML-TDD), and tumefactive MS (TMS). Descriptive characteristics and UCC area were compared across radiological and clinical phenotypes.ResultsOf 109 individuals, six (6%) were SL-TDD, 28 (26%) ML-TDD, and 75 (69%) TMS. All seven (6%) with progressive MS met TMS criteria. TMS had more spinal cord (63% vs. 26%; p = 0.003), and lateral tract lesions (54% vs. 14%; p = 0.001), and a higher final EDSS [median 2.5 (IQR 1.5,3.0) vs. 2.0 (0.0,2.0); p = 0.01] than ML-TDD. After excluding individuals with UCC lesions, there was an inverse trend between median C2 area and ILB across groups: SL-TDD, 56.3 mm2 (47.9,69.0); ML-TDD, 53.4 mm2 (37.2,63.2), and TMS, 50.8 mm2 (32.0,64.3); p = 0.08.ConclusionsDuring the course of TMS, early disability may be driven by a single tumefactive lesion while late disability is related to the accrual of intracranial lesions, spinal cord disease, and UCC atrophy. Across the TMS spectrum, there appears to be an inverse relationship between UCC area and ILB, partially independent of UCC lesions which trended towards statistical significance, warranting further investigation.

Introduction

Tumefactive demyelinating lesions occur in ~ 2% of individuals with multiple sclerosis (MS) and represent the initial symptomatic demyelinating event in most individuals with tumefactive MS (TMS) [1, 2]. Up to 24% of individuals with tumefactive demyelinating lesions experience a monophasic course characterized by a single clinical attack [1, 2].

Both spinal cord lesions and atrophy are associated with motor disability and a progressive MS disease course [3–5]. Spinal cord atrophy can occur early, often prior to the emergence of progressive MS symptoms, and is greater among individuals with progressive MS [3, 4, 6–9]. While spinal cord lesions contribute to spinal cord atrophy in MS, each can occur independently, and both are associated with intracranial lesion burden [10–12].

Within the context of TMS, spinal cord lesions similarly contribute to progressive motor disability [2], but the role of spinal cord atrophy, its relationship to a progressive disease course, and its association with intracranial lesion burden are less well understood. Given that TMS often presents with a single symptomatic tumefactive lesion at disease onset, it may offer an ideal model for identifying early disease and evaluating the relationship between intracranial lesion burden, spinal cord atrophy, and progressive motor disability.

This study aims to determine the relationship between upper cervical cord (UCC) area, intracranial lesion burden, and a progressive disease course in individuals with tumefactive demyelinating disease (TDD). We hypothesize that individuals with a higher intracranial lesion burden have greater UCC atrophy, an increased level of disability, and a higher likelihood of having a progressive disease course.

Methods

Study protocol and consent

This was a single-center retrospective observational study approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB 19–007888). All individuals consented to having their records utilized for research.

Participants

Individuals with TDD evaluated at our institution between January 1990 and December 2021 with available clinical data and MR imaging were included. Individuals were identified through the MS clinic and by searching the electronic medical record (EMR) for diagnostic codes relating to MS, optic neuritis, transverse myelitis, Balo concentric sclerosis, and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) using the Mayo Clinic Data Explorer (MDE) tool [2]. Key terms including “tumefactive”, “large lesion”, or “Balo” were queried within the MRI text.

Inclusion criteria

Individuals with 1) ≥ one tumefactive demyelinating lesion (defined as ≥ 2 cm in maximum transverse diameter) on T2-weighted MRI imaging established to be due to MS by a fellowship-trained MS neurologist, and 2) a 3 T brain MRI with available 3D T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) and 3D T2-Fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences were included. The first available 3 T brain MRI demonstrating tumefactive demyelination was utilized for diagnostic confirmation, while the last available 3 T brain MRI was utilized for the UCC analysis. MRI scans were reviewed by a radiologist and a neurologist (J.S. and B.Z.), and those deemed to be of insufficient quality for volumetric analysis of UCC were excluded. Tumefactive demyelinating lesions due to neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD), or another non-MS central nervous system tumefactive disease were excluded [2].

Clinical characteristics, definitions, and group stratification

Individuals were reviewed by a fellowship-trained MS neurologist, and demographic and clinical data were extracted retrospectively from the EMR. Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score [13] was obtained at the time of the index tumefactive event, at one-year follow-up, and at the last in-person assessment. Progressive disease was defined as an insidious and irreversible progression of clinical symptoms for greater than one year without recovery [14]. Clinical course was described as monophasic, relapsing–remitting, secondary progressive with and without activity, and primary progressive with and without activity. Modes of progression were described as: motor progression due to a spinal cord lesion (myelopathic motor progression), motor progression due to a supratentorial lesion (supratentorial motor progression), progressive ataxia (cerebellar progression), and progressive cognitive decline (cognitive progression). A UCC lesion was defined as ≥ 1 demyelinating lesion across the C1 – C3 spinal cord segments. A spinal cord lesion was defined as ≥ 1 demyelinating lesion within the cervical and/or thoracic spinal cord. A lateral tract lesion was defined as ≥ 1 demyelinating lesion located in the lateral tracts of the cervical and/or thoracic spinal cord.

To evaluate intracranial lesion burden, MRI imaging was reviewed by two fellowship-trained MS neurologists (A.A. and B.Z.), and patients were stratified into three groups using the 2005 dissemination in space (DIS) diagnostic imaging criteria, which offers high specificity for distinguishing clinically definite MS [15, 16]. Individuals who did not meet DIS criteria (3 of 4: ≥ 1 enhancing lesion or 9 T2 lesions, ≥ 3 periventricular lesions, ≥ 1 juxtacortical lesion, ≥ 1 infratentorial lesion) were classified as tumefactive demyelinating disease (TDD). Among TDD, individuals with a single tumefactive lesion on MRI were classified as single-lesion tumefactive demyelinating disease (SL-TDD), and individuals with > 1 demyelinating lesions on MRI (including ≥ 1 tumefactive lesion) were classified as multi-lesion TDD (ML-TDD). MRI upper cervical cord area acquisition

UCC area was obtained utilizing 3 T brain MRI, a feasible alternative to dedicated spinal cord imaging [11, 17–20]. Average template space and tissue-class priors were created from MPRAGE sequences from 200 cognitively unimpaired participants in the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging (50% female; mean age 45.1 years, SD 8.3; 50% scanned on GE 3 T scanners, 50% scanned on Siemens 3 T scanners). FSLView was used to draw the atlas of UCC regions on the template space by a radiologist. To determine the upper and lower limits of C1, C2, and C3 levels, horizontal lines were drawn from the end of the clivus, the lowest margin of the anterior arch of C1, the top of the C2-C3 and the top of the C3-C4 disc spaces on the template [20]. SPM12 Unified Segmentation was used to segment tissue classes and Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) was used to localize the C1, C2, and C3 atlas regions [18]. The mean value of all axial-plane segmental areas from C1-C2, C2-C3 and C3-C4 levels (around 19 axial slices per each level) were used to calculate mean C1, C2, and C3 areas. All measurements were adjusted for total intracranial volume.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were summarized using median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and Chi-Square or Fisher’s Exact test for categorical variables. Comparisons were performed between individuals with and without progression, as well as groups stratified by lesion burden with and without UCC lesions. Pairwise comparisons were performed excluding patients with UCC lesions, to determine the impact of intracranial lesion burden on UCC area utilizing C2, previously determined to be the most sensitive location for assessing differences in spinal cord area [19–21]. Two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

A total of 113 individuals with clinically confirmed tumefactive demyelinating disease and compatible MRI imaging were initially included in the study. Two individuals with incompatible MRI imaging were excluded, one due to having a syrinx with an expansile cervical spinal cord, and another due to poor image quality. Two additional individuals were later excluded after an alternative diagnosis was identified; one was diagnosed with biopsy-proven central nervous system lymphoma, and another developed MOG-IgG seroconversion. Thus, the final cohort undergoing statistical analysis consisted of 109 individuals, a majority of whom were white (95%), female (60%), with a median age of 46 years (IQR 35, 55) at time of MRI analysis (Table 1). Median expanded disability status scale (EDSS) was 4.0 (IQR 3.0, 4.8) at initial presentation and 2.0 (IQR 1.0, 3.0) at last evaluation, over a median follow-up time of 8.9 years (IQR 1.9, 14.8). Clinical characteristics across the radiological TMS spectrum

Of the total cohort, 75 (69%) met DIS criteria and were classified as TMS, 28 (26%) had > 1 demyelinating lesion but did not fulfill DIS criteria and were classified as ML-TDD, and six (6%) had a single tumefactive demyelinating lesion and were classified as SL-TDD (Table 2). Of the six individuals with SL-TDD, five (83%) had a confirmatory brain biopsy demonstrating demyelination and/or positive (≥ 2) cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) restricted oligoclonal bands (OCB). A single (17%) SL-TDD individual without supporting histopathology or CSF had hallmark clinical and radiological features consistent with clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) and serological exclusion of NMOSD and MOGAD, thus biopsy was deferred. Among the 28 individuals classified as ML-TDD, 23 (82%) had histopathologically confirmed demyelination and/or CSF positive OCBs, four (14%) had neither but fulfilled clinical dissemination in time, and one (3%) had clinical features consistent with CIS. There was no difference in age, sex, race, initial EDSS, or EDSS at 1 year follow-up between the three groups (Table 2). None of the individuals classified as SL-TDD had spinal cord lesions, while those with TMS were more likely to have spinal cord lesions (63% vs. 26%; p = 0.003) and lateral tract lesions (54% vs. 14%; p = 0.001) compared to ML-TDD. EDSS at last follow-up was significantly higher in the TMS cohort compared with the ML-TDD cohort [2.5 (IQR 1.3, 3.5) vs. 2.0 (IQR 0.0, 2.0); p = 0.009], despite a similar median age [46 years (IQR 37, 59) vs. 47 years (IQR 34, 54); p = 0.89] and follow-up duration [10.1 years (IQR 2.0, 17.1) vs. 8.5 (IQR 2.6, 13.3); p = 0.39]. After removing individuals with UCC lesions (n = 31), the difference in EDSS between TMS and ML-TDD remained significant (p = 0.008). Conversely, EDSS at last follow-up between the TMS and SL-TDD cohort were similar [2.5 (IQR 2.0, 3.0) vs. 2.5 (IQR 1.5, 3.0); p = 0.81], although the SL-TDD cohort had significantly less follow-up time [8.9 (IQR (2.1, 16.6) vs. 0.7 (IQR 0.3, 6.5); p = 0.03].

Clinical characteristics: Individuals with progressive TMS

Seven (6%) individuals were classified as having a progressive disease course, with clinical characteristics summarized in Table 1. All seven with progressive disease fulfilled DIS criteria and were classified as TMS. Three had a secondary progressive course with disease activity, three had a secondary progressive course without disease activity, and one had a primary progressive course without disease activity. None were experiencing an acute relapse at the time of final EDSS assessment. Mode of progression was classified as myelopathic (4/7; 57%), supratentorial motor (due to a demyelinating lesion of the motor cortex; 2/7; 29%), cerebellar (1/7; 14%), and/or cognitive (1/7; 14%). The progressive TMS cohort had a higher median EDSS at 1-year follow-up [4.5 (IQR 3.5, 5.5) vs. 2.0 (IQR 1.5, 3.0); p = 0.02] and at last follow-up [5.8 (IQR 5.0, 6.5) vs. 2.0 (IQR 1.0, 3.0; p = 0.003] compared with the non-progressive TMS cohort (Table 2).

On average, those with progressive disease had a longer median duration follow-up time [16 years (IQR 13.3, 24.0) vs. 7.9 years (IQR 1.8, 14.3); p = 0.003)] than the non-progressive cohort. There was an increased frequency of spinal cord lesions [5/6 (83%) vs. 41/86 (48%); p = 0.09] and lateral tract lesions [4/6 (67%) vs. 33/84 (39%); p = 0.19] in the progressive TMS cohort compared with the non-progressive TMS cohort, although neither reached statistical significance.

Comparison of UCC area in single tumefactive lesions vs. multiple lesions

Individuals with a single tumefactive lesion (SL-TDD) trended towards a larger C2 area compared to individuals with multiple lesions (ML-TDD, TMS): [median: 56.3 mm2 (range 47.9, 69.0) vs. 51.9 mm2 (range 32.0, 64.3); p = 0.05]. After excluding individuals with UCC lesions, the SL-TDD cohort trended towards having a larger C2 area [56.3 mm2 (47.9, 69.0) vs. 52.1 mm2 (32.0, 64.3); p = 0.06, Fig. 3A]. Neither median C1 or C3 area differed between the two groups.

Comparison of UCC area in tumefactive demyelinating disease vs. TMS

Individuals that did not fulfill DIS criteria (SL-TDD and ML-TDD) trended towards a larger C2 area compared with those who did fulfill DIS (TMS): [median: 53.7 mm2 (range 37.2, 69.0) vs. 51.1 mm2 (range 32.0, 64.3); p = 0.06, Fig. 3B]. This trend persisted after excluding individuals with UCC lesions (p = 0.07; Fig. 3B). There was no difference in median C1 area between the groups. The difference in median C3 area trended towards significance [46.1 mm2 (range 27.4, 66.0) vs. 43.6 mm2 (25.1, 63.4); p = 0.09] and reached significance following exclusion of individuals with UCC lesions [46.1 mm2 (range 27.4, 66.0) vs. 42.7 mm2 (range 25.1, 63.4); p = 0.03].

Comparison of UCC area across the spectrum of intracranial lesion burden

Following exclusion of patients with UCC lesions, median C2 area across the three groups (SL-TDD, ML-TDD, and TMS) were 56.3 mm2 (range 47.9, 69.0) in SL-TDD, 53.4 mm2 (range 37.2, 63.2) in ML-TDD, and 50.8 mm2 (range 32.0, 64.3) in TMS (p = 0.08; Fig. 3C).

UCC across progressive and non-progressive TMS cohorts

Comparing UCC area between individuals with progressive and non-progressive TMS, there was no statistically significant difference or trend in median C1, C2, or C3 UCC area across groups (Table 3). Only seven individuals had a progressive disease course, possibly limiting statistical power to identify a difference.

Discussion

This retrospective observational study revealed differences across the TMS spectrum, demonstrating that individuals later in the TMS course had a higher frequency of spinal cord lesions, increased overall disability, and a higher likelihood of progressive disease, compared to individuals earlier in the TMS course that did not fulfill radiologic DIS. Furthermore, evaluating UCC across the TMS spectrum suggested a possible inverse relationship between intracranial lesion burden and UCC atrophy, which trended towards statistical significance. Specifically, individuals with TMS had the greatest UCC atrophy, while individuals with SL-TDD had the least UCC atrophy. These findings remained consistent after excluding individuals with UCC lesions. Although these findings are exploratory, they suggest a possible relationship between intracranial lesion burden, UCC atrophy, and disability across the TMS spectrum, partially independent of upper cervical spinal cord lesions. Additional studies are required to confirm these findings and might investigate whether treating individuals earlier in the TMS course might delay clinical evolution, spinal cord atrophy, and related disability.

Interestingly, despite the SL-TDD cohort having the largest UCC area, they had a similar disability level to the TMS cohort. However, unlike the TMS cohort, none of the SL-TDD had a progressive disease course, suggesting that disability in this cohort may be related to the acute demyelinating attack with incomplete recovery, possibly further compounded by intracranial biopsy. This is supported by the shorter median follow-up duration in the SL-TDD cohort, with less recovery time from the sentinel demyelinating attack, and less time to develop UCC atrophy. These findings suggest that a single intracranial tumefactive lesion may be sufficient to cause disability in TDD.

Individuals with SL-TDD likely represent an early form of TMS with less UCC atrophy and absent spinal cord disease. The absence of a progressive disease course among the SL-TDD cohort coupled with the short comparative follow-up time suggests that these individuals may have already converted to TMS by the time of progressive disease course onset. Additional studies are needed to determine the rate and frequency of evolution from SL-TDD to TMS, and whether earlier treatment might delay or prevent this progression.

The relationship between radiological phenotype and clinical course yielded additional insights into TMS. A majority of our cohort met the 2005 DIS criteria and were stratified into the TMS cohort, including all seven patients with a progressive disease course. Importantly, there was a higher frequency of spinal cord and lateral tract lesions, and a higher final EDSS in both the TMS and progressive cohorts compared to the TDD and non-progressive cohorts, respectively. This finding is consistent with prior literature in progressive MS cohorts, underscoring the relevance of spinal cord lesions in disability accrual across the TMS spectrum [5, 10, 22, 23]. We did not appreciate a difference in UCC area between the non-progressive and progressive TMS cohorts. This may be due to the small sample size of the progressive TMS cohort, restricting the statistical power needed to detect a meaningful difference. Larger studies involving progressive TMS cohorts are needed to further investigate the relationship between UCC area and a progressive disease course.

Recent studies have examined the relationship between intracranial lesion burden, spinal cord disease, and spinal cord atrophy in MS. Several studies have demonstrated independence between spinal cord disease and spinal cord atrophy, with each separately contributing to disability [10, 12]. However, most of the literature thus far has focused on the relationship between intracranial lesion burden and the presence of spinal cord lesions, and prior studies have not studied TMS specficially [24, 25]. This exploratory study demonstrated an inverse trend towards significance between intracranial lesion burden and spinal cord atrophy in TMS, at least partially independent of spinal cord lesions. These findings are reflected by a recent study suggesting that chronic active cerebral lesions may influence spinal cord atrophy in non-tumefactive MS [26], indicating an association between spinal cord atrophy and intracranial disease related to network atrophy and Wallerian degeneration [27–29].

This study is limited by its retrospective nature and strict MRI inclusion criteria, limiting its sample size, especially in individuals with progressive TMS and single tumefactive lesions. Most individuals in the study were young, white, and female, limiting generalizability. Individuals with a progressive disease course were on average followed longer than those with a non-progressive disease course, and some were lost to follow-up, representing a possible selection bias. Similarly, individuals with a single tumefactive lesion had a much shorter follow-up duration, possibly also related to selection bias, although alternatively this may be due to the natural history of the disease.

Due to the small sample size and exploratory nature of this study, a correction for multiple comparisons was not performed. Thus, these findings should be noted as exploratory and future validation in larger cohorts is required. Importantly, despite a concerted effort towards diagnostic confirmation of tumefactive demyelination in this cohort, we acknowledge the small but valid possibility of an alternative diagnosis. As previously mentioned, all patients in this cohort had MRI imaging consistent with tumefactive demyelination, and a majority either had biopsy-proven demyelination and/or OCB in CSF. Finally, the stratification of individuals with TDD was qualitative rather than quantitative and thus would not account for differences in lesion quantity within the TMS cohort which may involve significant variability. Future quantitative studies involving intracranial lesion volume and continuous lesion count are needed to further support these findings.

Conclusion

Disability in early TMS may be driven by a single tumefactive lesion, with potential for disability recovery during the transition to multi-lesion disease. Late in the TMS course, disability is likely multifactorial, driven by intracranial lesion burden, spinal cord disease, and UCC atrophy. Across the radiological spectrum of TMS, there appears to be an inverse relationship between UCC area and intracranial lesion burden partially independent of upper cervical cord lesions, which trended towards but did not reach statistical significance. Future studies are needed to confirm these findings and should explore whether treating earlier in the TMS course might prevent spinal cord atrophy and accumulation of disability.
