A novel outer membrane vesicle adjuvant improves vaccine protection against Bordetella pertussis
Authors: Michelle Galeas-Pena, Allyson Hirsch, Erin Kuang, Joseph Hoffmann, Patrick Gellings, Jasmine B Brown, Vanessa M Limbert, Claire L Callahan, James B McLachlan, Lisa A Morici
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPertussis is a vaccine-preventable respiratory disease caused by the Gram negative coccobacillus Bordetella pertussis. The licensed acellular pertussis (aP) vaccines protect against disease but do not prevent bacterial colonization and transmission. Here, we developed and tested an intranasal vaccine composed of aP antigens combined with T-vant, a novel adjuvant derived from bacterial outer membrane vesicles, that elicits both mucosal and systemic immune responses. We hypothesized that immunization of mice with aP-T-vant would enhance mucosal immunity and eliminate B. pertussis in the respiratory tract. In contrast to mice immunized intramuscularly with the licensed aP vaccine, intranasal immunization with aP-T-vant eliminated bacteria in both the lung and nasopharynx. Protection was associated with IFN-gamma and IL-17-producing, non-circulating CD4 + T cells in the lung and nasopharynx, and sterilizing immunity in the nasopharynx was dependent on IL-17. Novel mucosal adjuvants, such as T-vant, warrant further investigation to enhance the efficacy of next generation pertussis vaccines.Subject terms: Bacterial infection, Adjuvants

Introduction

Between 1940 and 1980, the introduction and use of the whole-cell pertussis (wP) vaccine had a dramatic effect on the reduction of disease due to pertussis, also known as whooping cough, a respiratory disease of children and adults. Due to the reactogenicity of the wP vaccine, it was replaced with the less reactogenic acellular pertussis (aP) vaccine in 1991 that is now part of the routine childhood immunization schedule in the United States and elsewhere. While the aP vaccine is highly effective at preventing disease in vaccinated individuals, B. pertussis continues to circulate in humans leaving infants and younger children as well as individuals with waning immunity at heightened risk for pertussis. There is therefore a dire need for improved pertussis vaccines that can eliminate B. pertussis from the respiratory tract in vaccinated individuals and potentially eradiate the pathogen from the human population altogether.

There are several possible reasons why B. pertussis continues to cause disease; however, one reason for this is that distinct immunological differences between the aP and wP vaccine have been demonstrated in a number of studies1–3. Notably, wP appears to promote a Th1 and Th17 response in rodents and baboons leading to clearance of bacteria from the nasal mucosa4,5. In contrast, aP immunization elicits a Th1/Th2 response without any evidence of Th17 immunity and is unable to clear the bacteria from the nasal tract6–9. Th17 mediated immune responses in the mucosa recruit neutrophils which are essential for B. pertussis clearance in mice10,11. Therefore, it may be possible to improve the aP vaccine using adjuvants that polarize a Th1/Th17 immune response.

Bacterial outer membrane vesicle-based vaccines and adjuvants have received considerable attention in recent years due to their versatility and immunostimulatory capacities. We and others have published on the utility of OMV vaccines to prevent disease caused by respiratory pathogens, including but not limited to, Neisseria meningitidis, Burkholderia pseudomallei, Yersinia pestis, and Bordetella pertussis12–24. In the case of B. pertussis, intraperitoneal immunization with OMVs derived from B. pertussis were shown to protect against challenge with clinical isolates that express pertactin, as well as pertactin deficient strains, by inducing Th1 and Th17 tissue resident memory CD4+ T cells in the respiratory tract22. The use of OMV-based vaccines against B. pertussis and their potential as next generation pertussis vaccines are nicely described in a recent review25.

Here, we tested aP combined with a novel OMV adjuvant called T-vant to determine whether immunization with T-vant adjuvanted aP could enhance bacterial clearance in the respiratory tract. We have previously shown that this OMV-based technology drives robust humoral and cellular immune responses to multiple antigens when delivered mucosally or parenterally14,26–29. Intranasal immunization using a live attenuated Bordetella ssp strain with a mutation in one of the genes regulating TSS3 (btrS) was found to be an effective way to stimulate Th17 responses in the nasopharynx, leading to bacterial clearance in mice after 56 days post-infection30. Therefore, we hypothesized that addition of T-vant to an aP vaccine (aP biosimilar) would enhance cellular immunity, and potentially Th17 immune responses, when delivered intranasally. We found that unlike intramuscular immunization with DTaP, intranasal immunization with aP-T-vant promoted clearance of B. pertussis in both the lung and nasopharynx of mice. The protection was associated with increased numbers of non-circulating CD4 T cells at the infection sites. Furthermore, aP-T-vant elicited higher numbers of IFN-γ and IL-17 producing CD4 T cells in the respiratory tract and bacterial clearance from the nasopharynx was dependent upon IL-17 signaling. Finally, immunization with T-vant adjuvanted aP induced mucosal IgA and significantly higher serum IgG compared to DTaP immunization in mice.

Results

Intranasal immunization with T-vant-adjuvanted aP vaccine eliminates B. pertussis in the lung and nasopharynx

Current acellular pertussis (aP) vaccines are formulated with alum and administered intramuscularly. In rodent and non-human primate infection models, immunization with alum-adjuvanted aP fails to eliminate B. pertussis, particularly in the nasopharynx, which allows for bacterial persistence and transmission to naïve hosts2,3,31. To determine if a T-vant-adjuvanted aP biosimilar lacking alum (aP-T-vant) could reduce bacterial colonization in infected tissues, mice were immunized intranasally three times at three week intervals and then challenged with B. pertussis strain D420 three weeks after the last immunization. The experimental design is shown in Fig. 1a. Naïve C57Bl/6 mice infected with B. pertussis D420 were heavily colonized in the lung (Fig. 1b) and nasopharynx (Fig. 1c) at day 0 and remained colonized up to three weeks post-infection. As expected, mice immunized intramuscularly with DTaP steadily cleared B. pertussis from the lung (Fig. 1b) but remained heavily colonized in the nasopharynx three weeks post-infection (Fig. 1c), corroborating previous findings regarding DTaP vaccines by others2,3,31. In contrast, mice immunized intranasally with aP-T-vant eliminated bacteria from the lung by two weeks post-infection (Fig. 1b) and the nasopharynx by three weeks post-infection (Fig. 1c). The reduction in bacterial counts in both the lung and nasopharynx in aP-T-vant immunized mice was evident as early as 7 days post-infection compared to mice in other immunization groups (****p < 0.0001). As safety of adjuvants delivered intranasally is an important consideration, we performed an additional experiment to assess lung histopathology following immunization and challenge. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 1, there was no evidence of increased pathology in mice immunized intranasally with ap-T-vant compared to mice immunized intramuscularly with dTaP, whereas naïve mice displayed enhanced inflammation in the lung at 4 days post-challenge.

Intranasal immunization with aP-T-vant drives expansion of CD4+ T cells in the lung and nasopharynx

Next, we investigated whether intranasal immunization of mice with aP-T-vant enhanced cellular immune responses to B. pertussis in the respiratory mucosa. Both humoral and cellular responses are elicited during B. pertussis infection32–35. However, cellular responses have been shown to be most critical for clearance of B. pertussis in animal models, including non-human primates5,11,36–38. We first determined the total numbers of CD4+ T cells in the lung and nasopharynx three weeks post-immunization in all groups. To discriminate circulating from non-circulating leucocytes, we performed a dual stain of CD45+ cells using different clones of anti-CD45 antibodies conjugated to two different fluorescent reporters as others have described39. The first antibody (CD45-PE-Cy7) was administered intravenously three minutes prior to euthanasia. Mice were then sacrificed and lung and nasopharynx harvested to prepare single cell suspensions. The cells were then stained with anti-CD45-FITC to identify cells non-circulating cells present in the tissues but not in blood (Fig. 2a). The gating strategy is shown in Supplemental Fig. 2. Mice immunized intranasally with aP-T-vant displayed significantly more non-circulating CD4 + T cells in the lung compared to DTaP-immunized and control groups of mice (p < 0.0005) (Fig. 2b). In the nasopharynx, there was a significant increase in the number of non-circulating CD4+ T cells in aP-T-vant mice compared to naïve mice (p < 0.05) but there was no significant difference compared to DTaP- or T-vant-immunized mice (Fig. 2c). These results indicate that inclusion of T-vant adjuvant in the aP vaccine expands CD4+ T cells at the sites of B. pertussis infection. Furthermore, CD4+ T cells isolated from the lung and nasopharynx demonstrated higher expression of CD69 in mice immunized with aP-T-vant (Supplemental Fig. 3), indicating these populations may be conventional tissue resident memory T cells.

Intranasal immunization with aP-T-vant promotes Th1 and Th17 immune responses in the lung and nasopharynx

Natural infection and vaccination with whole cell pertussis vaccines (wP) promote the expansion of CD4+ tissue resident memory (TRM) T cells in the respiratory tract of mice3. Further, induction of Th1 and Th17 immune responses was observed in baboons infected with B. pertussis or vaccinated with wP and correlated with bacterial clearance from the nasal mucosa whereas aP-immunized baboons displayed Th1 and Th2 immunity and were not able to eliminate bacteria and subsequently transmitted the disease to naïve animals31. We therefore evaluated the phenotype of non-circulating CD4+ T cells in the lung and nasopharynx of immunized mice using production of IFN-γ, IL-4 and IL-17 as an indication of Th1, Th2 and Th17 immunity, respectively. Lung and nasopharynx single cell suspensions were restimulated with the aP vaccine antigens, pertactin, pertussis toxin and hemagglutinin filamentous, for 18 h, followed by intracellular cytokine staining and flow cytometry. Mice immunized with aP-T-vant demonstrated significantly higher (p < 0.0001) numbers of IFN-γ-producing CD4 + T cells in the lung compared to control groups of mice (T-vant, naïve) (Fig. 3a, c), as well as in the nasopharynx compared to DTaP-immunized and control mice (Fig. 3b, d). The number of IL-4-producing CD4 T cells were also enhanced in both the lung and nasopharynx of mice immunized with aP-T-vant compared to all other groups (Fig. 3c, d). In addition, the number of IL-17-producing CD4 T cells were significantly higher (p < 0.0001) in the lung and nasopharynx of aP-T-vant immunized mice compared to the other immunization groups (Fig. 3a–d). To determine if IL-17 was responsible for the sterilizing immunity observed in the nasopharynx of mice immunized with aP-T-vant, we repeated the immunization and challenge study using IL-17R deficient mice. IL-17R deficient mice immunized with aP-T-vant were unable to clear B. pertussis from the nasopharynx by day 21 (Fig. 3e) but were still able to clear bacteria from the lung (Fig. 3f). This indicates that IL-17 mediated responses are essential for aP-T-vant mediated protection in the nasopharynx of mice and corroborates the findings of others that Th17 responses in the nasal tract are critical11,22,30,35–38.

Intranasal immunization with aP-T-vant induces both mucosal IgA and serum IgG

We next measured the mucosal and systemic antibody responses following immunization with aP-T-vant. Mice immunized intranasally with aP-T-vant possessed significantly higher titers of aP specific IgA in the bronchoalvealor lavage (BAL) fluid (p < 0.005) and nasal washes (NW) (p < 0.0001) compared to naïve control mice (Fig. 4a, b). There was some IgA detected in mice immunized with DTaP, however the titers were not significantly increased compared to naïve control mice. Since the aP vaccine prevents toxin-mediated disease in vaccinated individuals due to the induction of neutralizing serum IgG against pertussis toxin40–44, we next measured the serum antibody response against aP. Mice immunized with DTaP or aP-T-vant displayed significantly higher titers of sera IgG compared to naïve mice (p < 0.0001), and the IgG titers in aP-T-vant immunized mice exceeded that of DTaP immunized mice (p < 0.0005) (Fig. 4c). The ability of aP-T-vant to induce high levels of circulating aP-specific IgG was encouraging because intranasal vaccine administration sometimes doesn’t achieve equivalent serum antibody responses as the same vaccine given intramuscularly45. Determination of IgG2:IgG1 ratios demonstrated that aP-T-vant elicited more IgG2 than IgG1 indicative of a Th1-skewed response, while DTaP induced more IgG1 than IgG2 in the sera and BAL as expected (Table 1).

Discussion

Vaccination against pertussis is part of the recommended childhood immunization schedule in most countries around the world. Although the global vaccine coverage for pertussis is over 80%46, the number of reported cases have increased following the replacement of the wP vaccine with the aP vaccine47. Waning immunity in vaccinated individuals and bacterial transmission from asymptomatic carriers to vulnerable individuals have been proposed as some of the factors contributing to this resurgence31,48–52. It is therefore imperative to develop new or improved pertussis vaccines that can eliminate or significantly reduce B. pertussis colonization in the upper respiratory tract.

To clear B. pertussis from the upper airway in animal models, it is well-established that Th1 and Th17 immune responses are critical5,11,53–55. Here we evaluated the protective efficacy of an aP vaccine adjuvanted with T-vant, a novel OMV adjuvant that can be delivered mucosally29. Intranasal immunization of mice with aP-T-vant promoted complete clearance of B. pertussis from both the lung and nasopharynx of mice. Mice immunized with aP-T-vant displayed a significant increase in CD4 T cells compared to control groups, however the total number of CD4 T cells were similar to DTaP immunized mice. Consistent with other studies1,4,56 our data showed that DTaP immunization did not drive significant increases in either IFN-γ- or IL-17-producing CD4 T cells, particularly in the nasopharynx, which corresponded with their inability to clear bacteria from the nasopharynx. Alum has been shown to block induction of IL-17 in the nasal mucosa, reducing the ability of CD4 + T cells to recruit neutrophils which are key for bacterial clearance in mice9. In contrast, our data demonstrate that aP-T-vant promoted the expression of IFN-γ and IL-17-producing aP- specific CD4 + T cells, establishing a mucosal environment more conducive to bacterial clearance. Using IL-17R deficient mice, we demonstrated that vaccine clearance of bacteria from the nasopharynx, but not the lung, is dependent upon IL-17-mediated responses, perhaps through the recruitment of neutrophils11,53. We did not assess for the presence of neutrophils in the present study, and future studies are warranted to investigate the precise mechanism(s) of sterilizing protection conferred by the aP-T-vant vaccine.

Parenteral immunization, particularly with alum-adjuvanted vaccines, often fails to induce a robust mucosal immune response necessary for pathogen clearance57. This issue has been exemplified by the Covid-19 pandemic and the inability of intramuscular SARS CoV-2 vaccines to block viral colonization in the upper respiratory tract which is needed to halt viral transmission and spread. Mucosal vaccination strategies are an active area of investigation, and the development of safe and effective vaccine adjuvants that can be administered intranasally is critical57. In addition to our work with T-vant, other adjuvants have shown promising results to improve vaccine protection against pertussis. An adjuvant composed of Bordetella colonization factor A (BcfA), an outer membrane protein from Bordetella bronchiseptica, elicited cellular and humoral immune responses to Bordetella pertussis antigens. Addition of BcfA to a commercial aP vaccine resulted in greater reduction of B. pertussis from the lungs of mice than that elicited by aP alone and was accompanied by increased Th17 and reduced Th2 immune responses58. Administration of BcfA intranasally, also demonstrated to significantly reduce nasal colonization by enhancing mucosal Th17 responses, effectively reducing disease transmission59. More recently, B. pertussis derived OMVs were shown to adjuvant heterologous immunogens and to better enhance the Th1 immune response compared to alum which is similar to the results presented in our work60.

In addition to new adjuvants, vaccine platforms utilizing OMVs are receiving increased attention61–63. Immunization with OMVs derived from B. pertussis were shown to protect against pertussis in mice and promoted an increased expression of IFN-γ in splenocytes compared to commercially available Tdap64. In another study, intranasal delivery of the B. pertussis OMV vaccine led to higher levels of IgA and improved bacterial clearance in the nasopharynx compared to the OMV vaccine delivered by the subcutaneous route65. The advantage of using OMVs derived from B. pertussis as a vaccine is that they express additional proteins absent from the traditional aP formulation and could provide broader or better coverage. A potential disadvantage of B. pertussis OMVs is the safety risks associated with a potent lipid A species66–69, necessitating genetic modifications for lipid A attenuation. Subsequent work used a genetically modified strain of B. pertussis to reduce lipid A toxicity and showed that immunization with OMVs derived from the genetically modified strain promoted bacterial clearance in the lung following challenge with the parent strain23,64. It should be noted that the OMVs in T-vant are produced from a Burkholderia species with a naturally attenuated lipid A and the OMVs have been shown to be non-toxic in multiple assays and animal models14,27. Collectively, these studies support the utility and versatility of bacterial derived OMVs for intranasal vaccination, either as an adjuvant or a platform, against B. pertussis and potentially other respiratory pathogens.

In conclusion, our work presents compelling evidence that T-vant elicits a breadth of adaptive immune responses that contribute to protection in the respiratory tract. By stimulating production of IgA, IgG, and Th1, Th2, and Th17 CD4 + T cells at the mucosal sites, aP-T-vant immunization better mirrors immunity seen in response to B. pertussis infection and immunization with wP vaccines. Use of T-vant adjuvanted aP, in place of alum-adjuvanted aP, could potentially improve vaccine efficacy by prevention of disease and elimination of B. pertussis from the upper respiratory tract, thereby protecting the most vulnerable populations and reducing the global burden of disease.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was performed in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health (NIH). The protocols were approved by the Tulane University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol number 1472). Tulane University School of Medicine is fully accredited by the Association for the Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care-International.

Bacterial strains and growth condition

Bordetella pertussis strain D420 was kindly provided by Dr. Tod Merkel (FDA, CBER). Prior to each experiment, bacteria were prepared from frozen glycerol stocks by plating on Bordet-Gengou plates (BD, Sparks, MD) supplemented with defibrinated 15% sheep blood (Remel, Lenexa, KS) and incubated at 37 °C for 5 days. Single, isolated colonies were used to prepare lawns of bacteria which were then harvested by gentle scraping and suspended in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS) to achieve a concentration of approximately 6 x 108 CFU/mL. Bacteria were pelleted by centrifugation and washed twice with PBS. The final bacterial preparation was resuspended in sterile PBS to achieve the desired challenge concentration. For determination of tissue bacterial burdens, tissue homogenates were serially diluted and plated on Regan-Lowe agar (BD, Sparks, MD) supplemented with 10% defibrinated sheep blood and 40 ug/ml of Cefalexin (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and incubated at 37 °C for 5 days.

Mice

Male and female C57BL/6 mice, 8–10 weeks of age, were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA). B6.Cg-Il17rctm1.1Koll/J mice were crossed to B6.FVB-Tg(EIIa-cre)C5379Lmgd/J mice (the Jackson Laboratory Strain #:003724) at Tulane University School of Medicine. Germline transmission and demonstration of homozygosity of recombined allele were verified by PCR as previously described in ref. 70. Animals were fed rodent chow and water ad libitum and maintained under pathogen-free conditions.

Immunizations

C57Bl/6 mice (n = 6 per group) were immunized three times, three weeks apart with the following: group 1) DTaP (1/5th human dose of Infanrix, GlaxoSmithKline Biologicals, Rixensart, Belgium); group 2) aP acellular pertussis (aP) biosimilar composed of 5 μg of pertussis toxin mutant (Creative Diagnostics, Shirley, NY), 1.6 μg of pertactin (List Labs, Campbell, CA), and 5 μg of filamentous hemagglutinin (FHA) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO); adjuvanted with T-vant, and group 3) T-vant, an OMV adjuvant that we previously described in refs. 27,29. Naïve, non-immunized mice were also included as a control. Mice immunized with DTaP received 1/5th of the human dose54 intramuscularly in the vastus medialis muscle of the hind leg in a maximum volume of 50 uL per leg. All other groups were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane gas then immunized intranasally with a maximum volume of 8 μL per nare. The dose of T-vant was 5 ug.

T-vant adjuvant

The T-vant used in this study was GLP grade material that was manufactured by Catalent Biologics under NIH Adjuvant Development contract number 272201800045 C. Briefly, Bp82, a genetically-attenuated select agent exempt strain of Burkholderia pseudomallei71, was grown in Terrific broth supplemented with 0.6 mM adenine and 0.0005% thiamine at 37 oC in a 100 L bioreactor. Upon reaching an OD600 6.0, the temperature was lowered to 25 oC and 5 mM EDTA was added to the culture to enhance vesiculation. After 3 h, the supernatant was clarified by passage through a 0.2um filter. Vesicles were harvested from the clarified material by tangential flow filtration (TFF) using a 100 kDa filter. The vesicle preparation was treated with Benzonase to remove contaminating nucleic acids and subjected to another round of TFF. Vesicles were then purified by mixed mode chromatography and concentrated 10X by TFF. The final T-vant formulation contains 100 mM Tris Base, 3% Sucrose, 110 mM NaCl, pH 7.4, with a protein concentration of 0.5 mg/ml and final endotoxin measurements of 14 EU/ml.

Challenge studies

C57Bl/6 and IL-17R deficient mice were anesthetized with isoflurane gas and inoculated intranasally with 1 x 106 cfu of B. pertussis strain D420 in a volume of 40 μL (20 μL per nare). To determine the amount of bacteria deposited in the lung and nasopharynx, naïve mice were sacrificed 2 h after infection by carbon dioxide (CO2) asphyxiation, and the number of colony-forming units in the lung and nasopharynx were used as the day 0 starting point for all groups. At days 7, 14, and 21, mice were sacrificed by CO2 to determine bacterial burdens in the tissues. For IL-17RO mice, only day 21 was assessed. Lung and nasopharynx were harvested in 1 mL of sterile PBS, homogenized, serially diluted, and plated for determination of CFU/tissue (Supplemental Fig. 4a, b). The limit of detection was 30 CFU. Challenge studies were performed at least twice to confirm reproducibility using equal numbers of males and females, and the results are presented using the cumulative data from the independent experiments.

In-vitro restimulation assay

Single cell suspensions from lung and nasopharynx were seeded at 1 × 106 cells per well in DMEM with GlutaMAX (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) and stimulated with pooled acellular pertussis antigens at a concentration of 1 μg/mL for 18 h. Golgi Stop (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) was added for 4 h prior to surface staining. Washes were performed using PBS supplemented with Brefeldin A (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). As a positive control, phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and ionomycin (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) were utilized at a final concentration of 60 ng/mL and 1 μg/mL, respectively. Unstimulated cells were included as a negative control.

Intravascular staining

To discriminate circulating from non-circulating CD4 + T cells, dual staining of CD45+ cells was performed as previously described in ref. 39. Three weeks after immunization, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane gas and administered 3 μg of CD45 antibody conjugated with PE-Cy7 (Biolegend, San Diego, CA) via retro-orbital injection. After 3 min, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation to harvest the lung and nasopharynx (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Minced tissues were thoroughly washed with PBS within 10 min of extraction to avoid circulating fluorophore leakage to the tissue. Harvested tissue was covered with aluminum foil during all downstream processing to protect the labeled cells from direct light. Subsequently, cells were stained with CD45 conjugated with FITC (Biolegend, San Diego, CA). The percentage of cells labeled in the blood with CD45:PE-Cy7 was 96.1%. Non-circulating cells were identified by gating on CD45:PE-Cy7-, CD45:FITC + .

Flow cytometry

Lung tissues were placed in 2 ml of Advanced RPMI media (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA), 100 U/ml of heparin (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO) and 100 U/ml of DNAse I (StemCell, Vancouver, BC). After mincing the lung, collagenase (Sigma-aldrich, Stain Louis, MO) was added at 75 mg/ml. The tissue was incubated at 37 °C in an elliptical shaker. After incubation, cells were passed through a 70 mm filter and incubated in ACK lysis buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), then washed before final resuspension in flow buffer composed of PBS with 2.5% heat inactivated newborn calf serum, 0.5 mM EDTA and 2 mM of sodium azide. To derive cells from the nasopharynx, the skull of the sacrificed mouse was sectioned in the sagittal plane, and tissues were removed encompassing the nasal cavity and septum using tweezers. Nasopharynx tissue from 2 to 3 mice were pooled to obtain enough cells for in-vitro re-stimulation assay.

To discriminate circulating from non-circulating cells, the following surface markers were used per manufacturer’s protocol: CD45:PECy7 clone I3/2.3, CD45:FITC clone I3/2.3, and CD4:BV525 clone RM4-5 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA). For intracellular staining, IL-17A:PE clone TC11-18H10.1, IL-4:BV604 clone 11B11 and IFN-γ:APC clone XMG1.2 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) were utilized. Stained cells were analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) and data were obtained and analyzed with FloJo v10.8 software (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA).

ELISA for IgG and IgA antibody detection

Blood from immunized was obtained 3 weeks post immunization. Microtainer collection tubes with serum separator additive (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ) were used to collect serum from each sample. 2 μL of serum was serially diluted 10-fold in a 96-well plate coated with 0.1 ug total of either pertussis toxin, pertactin, FHA or combined aP components. IgG, IgG1, and IgG2 antibodies were quantified by ELISA using HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA). Results are presented as the reciprocal endpoint titers. Mucosal IgA was measured from bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and nasal washes (NW). In brief, BAL was obtained by canulating the trachea of mice and infusing the lungs with 1 ml of sterile PBS that was retrieved immediately back into the syringe and stored in sterile 1.5 ml tubes. Nasal washes were obtained by canulating trachea in cranial direction and washing the nasopharynx with 500 μL of sterile PBS. Consistency in sample dilution was confirmed by measuring the protein concentration of each sample by Bradford assay. All BAL samples were within a total protein concentration range between 0.9 and 1.1 mg/mL. Nasal washes were in the range of 0.03–0.04 mg/mL. 50 μL of BAL and 100 μL of NW were serially diluted 2-fold and plated in 96-well plates coated with individual and combined aP components as described for IgG. aP-specific IgA was quantified by ELISA using HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgA secondary antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). HRP was developed in all ELISA plates with a 2-component TMB peroxidase kit (SeraCare, Milford, MA). Total IgG results are presented as reciprocal endpoint titers. The ratio of IgG2/IgG1 was calculated using the OD values of IgG2c divided by the OD values of IgG172.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software was used for statistical analyses. A 2-way ANOVA of log10 transformed CFU and cytokine-producing CD4+ T cells was used for statistical analysis of bacterial burdens and cytokine expression, respectively. An ordinary one-way ANOVA was used for total numbers of CD4 + T cells in the tissues and for antibody measurements. ANOVA tests were followed by Tukey’s post-test with a confidence level of 0.05 (95% confidence interval). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
