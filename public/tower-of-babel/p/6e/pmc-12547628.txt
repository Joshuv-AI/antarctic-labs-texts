Mitochondrial mRNA oligocitydylation occurs in Chlorophyceae and Ulvales but not Bryopsidales or Trebouxiophyceae
Authors: A Bruce Cahoon, Isabella J Maggard, Waleed N Pathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial mRNAs in the green algal class Chlorophyceae have non‐template 3' oligo‐cytosine‐rich additions, also known as oligo(C), polycitydylation, or poly(C) “tails.” These oligonucleotide additions are believed to be unique to green algal mitochondria, as none have been observed in any other organism. Among algae, oligocitydylation had only been observed in species within the Chlorophyceae, with no evidence of it occurring in other taxonomic classes. In this study, evidence is presented that mitochondrial mRNA oligocitydylation occurs in the genus Ulva, demonstrating its presence in the Class Ulvophyceae and Order Ulvales. Two other species in Ulvophyceae, Bryopsis plumosa and Codium fragile, from the Order Bryopsidales were also screened, but no evidence of oligonucleotide additions was observed. This demonstrates that oligocitydylation occurs in Ulvophyceae but suggests it may be limited to the Ulvales. Three species from Trebouxiophyceae were also screened, and there was no evidence of oligonucleotide additions, suggesting it does not occur in this clade. These results demonstrate that oligocitydylation does occur outside of the Chlorophyceae but only in the closely related Ulvales clade, suggesting this could be a biochemical synapomorphy shared by these groups.Keywords: algal organelle RNA processing, evolution of mRNA processing, evolutionary development of RNA processing, mitochondrial RNA processing, mRNA 3′ oligonucleotide addition, non‐template mRNA addition, Oligocitydylation, Polycitylylation

INTRODUCTION

Mitochondria are semi‐autonomous eukaryotic organelles that originated from an endosymbiotic association between a proto‐eukaryotic cell and a bacterium from a sister lineage of modern Alphaproteobacteria, giving rise to the modern Eukarya lineage (Roger et al., 2017). Mitochondria are fully integrated and indispensable to the modern eukaryotic cell, where they serve as the site of carbon and nitrogen metabolism and amino acid and lipid biosynthesis and are a crossroads for other metabolic processes (Scheffler, 2008). Within the cytoplasm, they are often referred to as existing semi‐autonomously, as they can divide independently of the nuclear cell cycle, have maintained an ancestral remnant of their proteobacterial genome (mitogenome), and have transcription and translation systems that are distinct from those in the nucleus and cytoplasm (D'Souza & Minczuk, 2018).

The size and gene content of mitogenomes vary considerably across the Eukarya. Vertebrates tend to be between 16 and 20 kb, containing 37 efficiently packaged mRNA, tRNAs, and rRNAs (Taanman, 1999); whereas embryophytic land plants can be over 11 Mb and split onto numerous chromosomes with large stretches of non‐coding intergenic regions (Kozik et al., 2019; Sloan et al., 2012). Algal mitogenomes are diverse in size and conformation, reflecting the polyphyletic nature of this group (Burger & Nedelcu, 2012). In the Ulvophyceae‐Trebouxiophyceae‐Chlorophyceae (UTC) clade of the core Chlorophyta, the focus of this study, mitogenome size can range from just under 13 kb in Polytomella capuana to over 241 kb in Ostreobium quekettii (GenBank, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/, accessed October 2024).

Mammalian model systems provide the most studied mitochondrial mRNA processing examples (reviewed in Rackham & Filipovska, 2022). In these mitochondria, a nuclear‐encoded single‐subunit RNA polymerase initiates transcription from a promoter on each strand, yielding two chromosome‐length polycistronic transcripts, called the heavy and light primary transcripts. These carry all protein‐coding mRNAs, most of which are flanked by tRNAs and/or rRNAs. Some nucleotides on these primary transcripts undergo pseudouridylation or methylation, which may serve a regulatory function (Boughanem et al., 2022). Non‐translated RNAs are removed by endonucleases to release mRNAs, which is followed by the addition of a non‐template 3' oligonucleotide adenosine tail. Oligo(A) tails play two roles in the maturation of mRNAs. First, its addition stabilizes the transcript by presumably protecting it from ribonucleases (Nagaike et al., 2005), and second, some mRNAs require the addition of one or two adenosines to complete stop codons that were truncated during their removal from the polycistronic transcript (Chang & Tong, 2012). There is evidence that fragments of some mRNAs are circularized (Mance et al., 2020). It is unknown if these circRNAs play a role in mitochondrial gene expression or physiology, but some are upregulated in certain cancer cells (Liu et al., 2020; Xia et al., 2018).

Embryophyte mitochondria have a distinctive and complex gene expression system that focuses control at the post‐transcriptional stages by an extensive array of RNA binding proteins (Hammani & Giege, 2014; Zmudjak & Ostersetzer‐Biran, 2018). Land plants have relatively large mitogenomes in which most genes are separated by intervening non‐coding sequences. This means each gene has its own promoter, and transcripts are produced individually rather than as a component of a polycistronic primary transcript. RNA maturation in most plants includes extensive RNA editing in which nucleotides undergo deamination or amination, which converts cytosine to uridine or adenosine to inosine or vice versa to restore ancestral sequences (Ichinose & Sugita, 2017; Tillich et al., 2006). Genes may also be split into exons, which must be assembled by cis‐ and trans‐splicing mechanisms (Schmitz‐Linneweber et al., 2015). RNA termini are then matured through the actions of endo‐ and exo‐ribonucleases. Mature translatable mRNAs lack 3' non‐template oligonucleotide additions, and oligoadenylation of 3' termini occurs only as a degradation signal in plant mitochondria (Lange et al., 2009). Some RNA fragments and full‐length mRNAs are circularized and polysome associated, suggesting they could be translated (Liao et al., 2022).

Our knowledge of algal mitochondrial gene expression has primarily come from the model system Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, whose mitogenome is among the smallest of the Chlorophyceae: 15.4 kb, carrying only eight protein coding genes (Gray & Boer, 1988). Transcription and processing of the polycistronic RNA proceeds in a manner similar to mammalian systems but with notable exceptions. These exceptions include the addition of oligo(C‐rich), oligo(A/U‐rich), and oligo(C/U‐rich) tails on 3' termini (Cahoon & Qureshi, 2018; Gallaher et al., 2018; Salinas‐Giegé et al., 2017; Zimmer et al., 2009), the absence of RNA editing (Cahoon et al., 2017), and the circularization of full‐length mRNAs, which are polysome associated (Cahoon & Qureshi, 2018). To date, these mRNA processing events have been described among other members of the family Chlorophyceae, with oligocytidylylation reported in Scenedesmus obliquus and Polytomella parva (Salinas‐Giegé et al., 2017) while oligocytidylylation plus full‐length circ‐mRNAs have been reported in Pediastrum duplex (Proulex et al., 2021) and Edaphochlamys debaryana (Cahoon et al., 2024). The role(s) of oligonucleotide additions is (are) unclear. These additions occur well downstream of stop codons and, therefore, play no role in the completion of stop codons as poly(A) does in vertebrate mitochondria. It has also been noted that they are only on a portion of the mRNAs for any given gene, which casts doubt on a role in mRNA stabilization. The inclusion of oligo(C) additions at the junction sites of circularized mRNAs led Cahoon and Qureshi (2018) to suggest they may play a role in the circularization process.

In the seminal study describing oligo(C‐rich) tails in Chlamydomonas, Salinas‐Giegé et al. (2017) used 3' RACE to screen mRNA termini of one to three genes from representative species belonging to an array of Archaeplastida families, including Cyanophoraceae, Fliridiophyceae, Bryophyta, Tracheophyta, Pedinophyceae, Prasinophyceae, Trebouxiophyceae, and Chlorophyceae. Their findings showed no evidence of oligocytidylation outside Chlorophyceae. Proulex et al. (2021) reported the absence of oligocytidylation in the streptophyte alga Chara vulgaris, reinforcing the idea that this process is most likely limited to the chlorophyte lineage.

The purpose of this study was to screen all mitochondrial mRNAs from members of the Trebouxiophyceae and Ulvophyceae classes for evidence of oligocitydylation. These classes form a tripartite clade with Chlorophyceae, called UTC (Gulbrandsen et al., 2021; Leliaert et al., 2012), so it was reasoned that if this phenomenon did occur outside of Chlorophyceae, then it would most likely be among species within these sister clades. To test this, RNA‐seq and circular reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (circRT‐PCR) methodologies were used to perform comprehensive screens of all mitochondrial mRNAs from an Ulva sp., Bryopsis plumosa, and Codium fragile from Ulvophyceae and Chlorella vulgaris, Diplosphaera sundellii, and a Coccomyxa sp. from Trebouxiophyceae.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Tissue and culture

An Ulva sp. was collected from the Los Peñasquitos Lagoon, California, United States (March 2018) and cultured in the lab of Kalina Manoylov at Georgia College and State University. Samples of this species were shipped to the Cahoon lab, where DNA and RNA were extracted.

Bryopsis plumosa and Codium fragile were collected from Masonboro Sound at the University of North Carolina Wilmington's Center for Marine Science, June 2021. They were transported to the University of Virginia's College at Wise in seawater and cultured approximately 16 h under artificial light prior to nucleic acid extraction.

Chlorella vulgaris strain UTEX259 was acquired from the UTEX Culture Collection of Algae (University of Texas at Austin). Diplosphaera sundellii strain CCMP3705 (Thermozier et al., 2024) and Coccomyxa sp. strain WPW3‐3 were provided by Karen and Marvin Fawley from their collection. Trebouxiophytes were cultured in liquid Proteose Medium (2.94 mM NaNO3, 0.17 mM CaCl2, 0.3 MgSO4, 0.43 mM K2HPO4, 1.29 mM KH2PO4, 0.43 mM NaCl, 1 g · L−1 proteose peptone) at 20°C, on a shaker‐incubator (150 rpm), with a 12:12 h light:dark photoperiod.

Mitochondrial genome sequencing

Ulva sp. was frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a powder using a mortar and pestle. DNA extraction buffer from ZymoResearch's Quick‐DNA miniprep kit (ZymoResearch, Irvine, CA, United States) was added to the frozen powder in the mortar and mixed as it thawed. DNA was extracted from the lysate by following the manufacturer's protocol. Trebouxiophyte cultures were pelleted at 3000 × g for 5–10 min, and the cell pellets were resuspended in DNA extraction buffer as described above. The cell suspension was transferred into BashingBead Lysis Tubes (ZymoResearch) and vortexed for 10 min to disrupt cells. The samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 min to pellet beads and remove detergent bubbles. The cell lysate was pipetted from the bead lysis tube, and DNA was extracted following ZymoResearch's Quick‐DNA miniprep kit protocol. DNA concentrations and 260:280 ratios were estimated using a Nanodrop Lite spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, United States).

Whole genome sequencing was completed by Azenta (Plainfield, NJ, United States) using their Illumina paired‐end short‐read non‐human whole genome sequencing service. The total read output for each species was Ulva sp. = 67,335,560; Diplosphaera sundellii = 5,484,998; and Coccomyxa sp. = 9,015,482. Raw sequences were de novo assembled into full‐length mitogenomes using Geneious Prime (2024.0.5 BioMatters, Auckland, New Zealand). The fully assembled and annotated circular mitogenomes are available in GenBank (Ulva sp.—PP908992, D. sundellii—PQ563290, Coccomyxa sp.—PQ563289).

RNA extraction and analysis

Ulva sp., Bryopsis plumosa, and Codium fragile were frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into powders with mortar and pestle. An RLT extraction buffer from Qiagen's RNeasy Plant Mini kit (Germantown, MD, United States) was pipetted into each mortar and mixed into the frozen powder with a pestle as it thawed. Each slurry was transferred to a microfuge tube, and RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer's protocol. Trebouxiophyte cultures (Chlorella vulgaris, Diplosphaera sundelli, and Coccomyxa sp.) were pelleted at 3000 × g for 5–10 min, and the cell pellets were resuspended in RLT extraction buffer from Qiagen's RNeasy Plant Mini kit. The cell suspension was transferred into ZymoResearch 0.1 & 0.5 mm BashingBead Lysis Tubes and vortexed for 10 min. The samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 min to pellet beads and remove detergent bubbles. The cell lysate was pipetted from the bead lysis tube, and RNA was extracted following the manufacturer's protocol, including the optional DNase treatment steps. The RNA concentrations 260:280 ratios were estimated using a Nanodrop Lite spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, United States).

Total RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) was completed by Azenta, which included ribosome depletion using Qiagen's (Germantown, MD, United States) FastSelect Plant rRNA depletion kit and library preparation with New England Biolab's (Ipswich, MA, United States) Ultra II RNA preparation kit for Illumina. Sequencing was conducted using Illumina 2 × 150 bp technology. RNA‐seq output (in total reads) for each species was Ulva sp. = 46,774,584, Bryopsis plumosa = 35,647,421, Codium fragile = 59,899,601, Chlorella vulgaris = 68,110,200, Diplosphaera sundellii = 34,414,823, and Coccomyxa sp. = 39,013,035. All sequence reads were imported into Geneious Prime, and pairs were merged using BBMerge (Bushnell et al., 2017). To screen for 3′ non‐template oligonucleotide additions, merged reads were analyzed using Geneious Prime using four strategies (Figure S1). (1) Merged reads were aligned to their respective whole mitogenomes using the Map to Reference function and Geneious' RNA‐seq option with a relatively high sequence mismatch of 20%. (2) Putative mRNAs were assembled into contigs representing hypothetical mRNAs from merged reads using the Geneious de novo assembler using the Map to Reference function and the following options—Geneious' mapper, 5% maximum mismatches, and trimming of paired read overhangs. (3) Merged reads were aligned to individual coding regions using the same methodology described for strategy 2. (4) Single merged reads were aligned to only the 3′ terminus of each gene. These were quantified to estimate the proportion of mRNAs with non‐template oligonucleotide additions. RNA‐seq analyses were conducted once for each species.

For circRT‐PCR, the protocol described in Cahoon and Qureshi (2018) and Mance et al. (2020) was used. Briefly, mRNAs were circularized using T4 RNA ligase (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, United States). cDNAs were made from circularized mRNAs using MMLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, United States) and gene specific primers (Table S1, primers labeled R1) and/or random hexamers and incubated at 42°C for 60 min. These were polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplified using R1 and F1 primer pairs, Phusion DNA Polymerase (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, United States), and a BioRad (Hercules, CA) C1000 thermal cycler, 95°C for 10 min (95°C for 30 s, 55°C for 15 s, 7°C for 60 s) × 40 cycles, 72°C for 10 min. Amplicons were diluted 10× with sterile water and used as templates for secondary PCR reactions using the R2 and F2 primer pairs and the PCR conditions as described above except the annealing temperature was raised to 65°C. Amplicons were cleaned using the GeneJET PCR purification kit (ThermoFisher) and sequenced by Azenta (Plainfield, NJ, United States) using their paired‐end Illumina Amplicon EZ service. Sequences were analyzed as described in Cahoon et al. (2024). The circRT‐PCR analyses were conducted two to four times (independent replicates) for each species.

RESULTS

Mitogenomes

Three mitogenomes were sequenced, assembled, and annotated. Ulva sp. was assembled from 283,076 paired reads, producing a contig that was 87,298 bp in size with 29 protein coding genes, 26 tRNA genes, and two ribosomal RNA genes (Figure 1a). All genes occurred on the same strand, suggesting a single polycistronic transcript is needed for gene expression. Nearly all of the genes occurred within a region approximately 58,000 bp in size. The remaining 29,000 bp portion of the genome carried only five recognizable genes, including cox3 and four tRNAs. The remaining genome also contained 12 hypothetical open reading frames.

Polycitydylation of mitochondrial mRNAs in an Ulva sp.

Total RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq) was performed on the Ulva sp. To screen for non‐template oligonucleotide additions, RNA‐seq reads were aligned to the complete Ulva sp. mitogenome, resulting in a total of 605,547 merged reads aligning with an average coverage of 1303.7 for each nucleotide. RNA‐seq reads were also aligned to individual coding regions and assembled de novo, as outlined in Figure S1. The mRNA 3' termini and frequency of each one from RNA‐seq data were mapped for each gene (represented in Figures 1, 2 and S3–, S7). Non‐template oligo(C) additions were observed at the 3' termini of eight of the 29 mRNAs (Table 1). Three patterns were noticed: on atp6 (Figure 1), cox2, and nd1 (Figure S3), the oligo(C) additions occurred among a group of adjacent termini differing by single nucleotides in an AU‐rich region; on nd6, the addition was observed on a single terminus (Figure S4); and on cox1(Figure 2), cox3 (Figure S5), cob (Figure S6), and nd4 (Figure S7), the oligo(C) additions were observed on several disparate termini.

Oligocitydylation of mRNAs in an Ulva sp.

Four independent circRT‐PCR screens were performed to analyze the 3′ termini of the Ulva sp. mitochondrial mRNAs. This technique confirmed the presence of poly(C) additions on the atp6, cox1, cox2, cox3, and nd6 genes at the same termini identified during the initial RNA‐seq screen (Table 1, Figures 1, 2 and S3–, S7). The frequencies of poly(C) additions, calculated as the percentage of termini with poly(C) at a location compared to all termini for that gene, were consistent between RNA‐seq and circRT‐PCR for atp6 and nd6. These proportions differed greatly for other genes (cox1, cox2, and cox3), with different proportions detected between the techniques and between circRT‐PCR replicates. For cob, nd1, and nd4 genes, poly(C) additions were not detected with circRT‐PCR, meaning their presence was only detected from a single RNA‐seq experiment.

The lengths of the oligonucleotide additions ranged from o to 13 nucleotides (Figures 1, 2 and S3–, S7) and were predominantly oligo(C) tails. Although sequence alignments occasionally detected adenine, uracil, or guanine within the poly(C) tails, these occurrences were so rare that they did not appear in nucleotide Logo plots (data not shown).

The same RNA screening methodologies used to detect poly(C) tails in the Ulva sp. (RNA‐seq and circRT‐PCR) were applied to Bryopsis plumosa, Codium fragile, Chlorella vulgaris, Diplosphaera sundellii, and Coccomyxa sp. Non‐template 3' oligonucleotide additions were not detected on the mitochondrial mRNAs of these species.

DISCUSSION

Our findings indicated that oligo(C) tails are added to the mitochondrial mRNAs of an Ulva sp., marking evidence of this phenomenon occurring outside the algal class Chlorophyceae. Oligocitydylation was first reported in the genetic model species Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Class Chlorophyceae, Order Chlamydomonadales) where it was detected in varying amounts on all eight of the mRNAs transcribed from this compact mitogenome (Cahoon & Qureshi, 2018; Gallaher et al., 2018; Salinas‐Giegé et al., 2017). It has also been reported on eight of the 12 genes from Pediastrum duplex (Class Chlorophyceae, Order Sphaeropleales; Proulex et al., 2021) and four of the eight genes of Edaphochlamys debaryana (Order Chlamydomonadales; Cahoon et al., 2024). In the Ulva sp., eight of the 29 protein‐coding mitochondrial genes had an oligo(C) addition. A comparison of the polycytidylated genes from these four species (Table 2) showed that the mitochondrial genes conserved between Ulva sp. and the three Chlorophyceae species—atp6, the cox group, and the nd group—were the ones with oligo(C) additions. According to the mitogenomes currently available in GenBank, the Chlorophyceae as a group have relatively few protein coding genes (Figure S8a). Of these genes, atp6,9 cox1,2,3, and nd1–6 are commonly present (Figure S8b, Table S3). The presence of poly‐nucleotide additions to a specific subset of mitochondrial genes in Ulva sp. has led us to hypothesize that this phenomenon is limited to mRNAs transcribed from this core group.

For the genes cox1, cox2, cox3, and nd6, the proportions of termini with a poly(C) addition were two to five times higher in circRT‐PCR experiments than in RNA‐seq. We attribute this difference to the nature of circRT‐PCR, which selectively amplifies mRNAs for which 5' and 3' termini have been covalently joined to form circular molecules. In our circRT‐PCR experiments, we used T4 RNA ligase to artificially circularize all linear RNAs, but we hypothesize that many full‐length circularized mRNAs for these four genes are naturally present. Cahoon and Qureshi (2018) detected full‐length, circularized versions of five mitochondrial mRNAs with oligonucleotide additions in Chlamydomonas reinhardtii. It was suggested that these additions prolonged the half‐lives of these mRNAs, enabling circularization, which is a relatively slow process. Thus, the higher proportion of circRT‐PCR products with oligonucleotide additions in the Ulva sp. supports the hypothesis that these additions may facilitate circularization.

The only non‐template oligonucleotide additions observed in Ulva sp. mitochondria were oligo(C) tails, which was consistent with Pediastrum duplex and Edaphochlamys debaryana (Cahoon et al., 2024; Proulex et al., 2021). We observed no evidence of oligo(A), oligo(U), or mixed oligo(U + C) as has been reported for Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Cahoon & Qureshi, 2018; Salinas‐Giegé et al., 2017; Zimmer et al., 2009).

The lengths of the 3’ UTRs in Ulva sp. varied considerably between genes and for individual genes. This variation was similar to the variations reported in Pediastrum duplex (Proulex et al., 2021), Edaphochlamys debaryana (Cahoon et al., 2024), and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Cahoon & Qureshi, 2018; Salinas‐Giegé et al., 2017; Zimmer et al., 2009). These data suggest there may be no cleavage site that creates a specific 3’ UTR terminus when these mRNAs are released from the primary transcript.

Two other Ulvophyceae, Bryopsis plumosa and Codium fragile, were screened for oligonucleotide additions, and none were detected, suggesting this phenomenon is not universally present across this class. Ulva sp. is in the order Ulvales, whereas B. plumosa and Co. fragile are in Bryopsidales. Morphological and molecular phylogenetic analyses suggest Ulvophyceae is a paraphyletic group, and the relationships between the different Ulvophyceae clades and Chlorophyceae are still being defined (Büdel & Friedl, 2024; Hou et al., 2022; Leliaert et al., 2012). Chloroplast‐based phylogenies made from 50 and 79 gene datasets placed Ulvales and Bryopsidales into two distinct clades (Leliaert & Lopez‐Bautista, 2015; Turmel et al., 2016, 2017). Some phylogenies in these studies have suggested Ulvales may be a sister clade to Chlorophyceae, while others have suggested Bryopsidales may be the closest sister clade. Nuclear phylogenies constructed with 95,539 and 884 transcriptomic markers have also separated Bryopsidales and Ulvales into distinct clades but have suggested Bryopsidales to be the closest sister clade to Chlorophyceae (Del Cortona et al., 2020; Gulbrandsen et al., 2021; Hou et al., 2022). Our study demonstrated the presence of oligo(C) additions in Ulvales, which we have interpreted as a synapomorphy shared with Chlorophyceae (Figure 3).

With the observation that polycitydylation occurs outside of Chlorophyceae in the closely related Ulvophyceae, we extended our analyses to include representatives of the Trebouxiophyceae, since it forms the final class of the core Chlorophyta tripartite UTC clade (Gulbrandsen et al., 2021; Leliaert et al., 2012). The Salinas‐Giegé et al. (2017) study, which first described polycitydylation, screened two mRNAs from Coccomyxa subellipsoidea and three mRNAs from Chlorella sorokiana and observed no evidence of oligonucleotide additions. Since that study screened only a few mRNAs, we thought a full screen of mito‐transcriptomes may uncover oligonucleotide additions in other mRNAs. We observed no evidence of 3′ oligonucleotide additions to mitochondrial mRNAs in Diplosphaera sundellii (Order Prasiolales), Coccomyxa sp. (core Trebouxiophyceae), or Chlorella vulgaris (Order Chlorellales).

CONCLUSIONS

In this study we have demonstrated that oligocitydylation of mitochondrial mRNAs occurs outside of the algal class Chlorophyceae. The evidence presented shows that oligo(C) tails are added to some mitochondrial mRNAs in a representative species from the genus Ulva in the algal class Ulvophyceae. The Ulva sp. analyzed in this study had more than twice the number of genes on its mitogenome than any of the Chlorophyceae species analyzed to date. However, only genes occurring in the core conserved group observed in Chlorophyceae mitogenomes were oligocitydylated. We conclude that oligo(C) tails occur in at least one Ulvophyceae lineage and hypothesize that this phenomenon is limited to a subset of mito‐genes shared between the Chlorophyceae and Ulvophyceae lineages.
