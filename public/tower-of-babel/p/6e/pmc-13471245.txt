Normalization of abuse and barriers to reporting among adolescents and young people in settings of displacement in Nigeria
Authors: Abuo James, Precious Chidozie Azubuike, Chimankpam Kingsley Ogbonna, Temidayo Akinreni, Uchenna Frank Imo, Prosper Ayenmo Kanu, Gina Marie Piane, Antor Ndep
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescence is a critical developmental stage marked by physical, psychological, and cognitive growth, increasing autonomy, social exploration, and risk-taking behaviors. However, these natural transitions in displacement settings are overshadowed by heightened vulnerabilities such as gender-based violence. In many displacement settings, recurrent exposure to violence leads to its acceptance as an unavoidable part of daily life. Sometimes, violence is normalized, leading to further exacerbation of the crisis. Our study explored the in-depth experiences and normalization of abuse and existing barriers to reporting within the internally displaced persons settlement in Cross River State, Nigeria.MethodsOur qualitative study included adolescents and young people aged 15–24 years residing in the Bakassi Internally Displaced Persons camp within the Bakassi Local Government Area. Participants were selected via purposive sampling. Data were collected through 12 individual interviews and 3 focus group discussions. Data were transcribed verbatim, and thematic analysis was conducted using NVivo 14 to identify key themes.ResultsFindings from the study reveal participants’ experiences with sexual exploitation, abuse, harassment, and gender-based violence, as well as their perceptions of abuse and how it is normalized. Contextual factors such as self-blame, societal pressure to accept advances from men, and limited accountability contribute to heightened vulnerability within the camps, which result in emotional and psychological impacts. Barriers to reporting and seeking help included fear of repercussions, lack of trust in authorities or services, and stigma associated with victimhood.ConclusionsOur study demonstrates that normalization of abuse, alongside systemic barriers such as fear of repercussions, stigma, and limited trust in authorities, significantly constrains reporting and help-seeking behaviors. Furthermore, our study identifies weak and largely absent accountability mechanisms as a key structural factor enabling continued abuse. To enhance accountability and safeguard vulnerable populations, appropriate legal penalties and enforcement measures should be applied to all forms of abuse. Additionally, future interventions should also address the underlying vulnerabilities that predispose individuals to abuse, ensuring a more comprehensive approach to prevention and protection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13031-026-00814-0.

Background

Globally, conflict and persecution-induced displacement has reached unprecedented levels further contributing to heightened vulnerability to Gender-Based Violence (GBV) [1]. GBV impacts both the physical and mental health of adolescents and young people (AYP) in displacement settings [1]. Defined as violence inflicted upon individuals based on their gender identity or societal norms, GBV leads to severe reproductive health consequences, including sexually transmitted infections (STIs), unintended pregnancies, and psychological trauma [1]. Adolescent girls are disproportionately affected due to the intersection of age and gender, especially in displacement and refugee settings [2]. The most common forms of GBV include child marriage, intimate partner violence (IPV), and domestic abuse [3]. Among displaced populations, these risks are amplified by conflict, insecurity, and the breakdown of social protection systems.

By the end of June 2025, the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) estimated that 117.3 million people globally had been forcibly displaced due to crises such as poverty, armed conflicts, and political instability [4]. Among them, 42.5 million refugees, defined as individuals who have crossed international borders and cannot safely return home, were recorded, with over half being under 18 [5]. In addition, 67.8 million were internally displaced persons (IDPs) who remained within their own countries [4]. Africa bears the heaviest burden, hosting about 35 million internally displaced individuals, making it the region with the highest number of IDPs due to conflict and violence [6–9]. In Africa, nearly 80% of IDPs are concentrated in just five countries: Sudan, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Somalia, Nigeria, and Ethiopia [10]. Sudan alone accounted for a record 11.6 million IDPs, the highest number ever recorded in a single country, and Nigeria accounts for 8 million [11, 12].

In many displacement settings, recurrent exposure to violence leads to its acceptance as an unavoidable part of daily life. Studies have documented that for many displaced adolescents, violence was no longer perceived as an anomaly but rather an expected and routine occurrence [13–16]. This normalization perpetuates cycles of abuse, silence, and impunity, further marginalizing survivors. In conflict-affected regions, including Northeast Nigeria and the South-south, the risks of GBV, including rape, early and forced marriage (EFM), IPV, and female genital mutilation (FGM) are further intensified by displacement [17]. Many adolescent girls in displacement settings across Nigeria report that their first sexual experience was forced [18]. The North-East region of Nigeria, for example, has had over 75% of women and girls in displacement camps report at least one form of GBV [19]. Similarly, as reported by Abbani (2021), the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) in 2021 found that over 80% of displaced women and girls in Borno, Adamawa, and Yobe States reported at least one form of GBV, with sexual assault and early marriage being the most common [20]. An assessment conducted by UNHR and the UNFPA in Cross River State, South-South Nigeria, revealed that women and adolescents in refugee-hosting communities such as Ogoja, Ikom, and Akamkpa face high risks of sexual exploitation, early marriage, and intimate partner violence, which are often compounded by economic dependency and limited access to protection services [21]. Also, Oko (2024) reported a total of 1,200 cases of GBV in resettlement areas and refugee camps across Cross River State [22]. Of these incidents, 74% involved females, 16% males, and 10% children. The reported forms of violence included rape, physical assault, sexual abuse, intimate partner violence, denial of resources, as well as psychological and emotional abuse [22].

The collapse of social protection structures during humanitarian crises limits access to reproductive health services, psychosocial support, and legal recourse, leaving survivors without essential avenues for care and justice [23, 24]. Despite the widespread prevalence of GBV in displacement settings, reporting remains exceptionally low due to deep-rooted systemic barriers embedded in displacement and humanitarian structures. Research identifies shame, stigma, fear of reprisal, and inadequate institutional support as significant deterrents [25]. Threats of death from partners, fear of stigma, retaliation, breaches of confidentiality, and lack of institutional support discourage survivors from seeking help in displacement settings [25]. For male survivors, barriers to reporting differ significantly due to societal expectations that reinforce notions of masculinity, strength, and self-reliance - as a result, many fear shame, stigma, and emasculation if they disclose their experiences [26]. Notably, the fear of retaliation remains a critical factor in sustaining silence around abuse. Many survivors are forced to return to environments where their perpetrators hold power over them, which makes reporting a life-threatening decision [13, 27]. The gaps in displacement and humanitarian responses, as well as the lack of coordination between gender-based violence and child protection divisions, often result in inadequate prevention and response strategies, particularly for adolescent girls [28]. Thus, improving the well-being of these vulnerable adolescents requires greater investment in research and the implementation of effective interventions to meet their specific needs, including secondary education, which is often neglected but has critical implications for life chances and the health of this age group.

Despite the high number of humanitarian and fragile settings globally, evidence documenting the effects of such crises on adolescent well-being is limited. This study seeks to examine the normalization of abuse and the barriers to reporting among AYP in displacement settings in Nigeria. By shedding light on the social and institutional factors perpetuating a culture of silence, the research aims to inform evidence-based interventions that can strengthen protection systems, improve survivor support mechanisms, and enhance accountability frameworks within humanitarian responses.

Methods

Study design

Our study employed a qualitative research approach to explore the in-depth experience and normalization of abuse and existing barriers to reporting within the IDP settlement.

Study settings

This study was carried out in the Bakassi Local Government Area (LGA) in the Cross River State, Nigeria. Bakassi LGA is a coastal region with a shared boundary with Cameroon and has a unique sociopolitical history in the country. The area was the subject of longstanding territorial disputes before its formal integration into Nigeria [29]. The area has been home to a significant population of IDPs who are settled following the ceding of the Bakassi Peninsula to Cameroon [30]. Our study was conducted in the Bakassi IDPs camp, located in Ikot-Effiom estate. The estate is the place where the government has built houses for IDPs and houses over 1,000 people [31, 32]. These sites accommodate displaced individuals and families who continue to experience challenges related to housing, social services, and healthcare services.

Study population

Our study included AYP aged 15–24 years, residing in the Bakassi IDPs camp. This population represents most of the dwellers in the camps and is characterized by unique challenges that may impact their well-being [33].

Sampling technique and sample size

We employed a purposive sampling technique to select participants who could provide in-depth and relevant insights for the study. As a team, following consultations with the literature and internal discussions, we agreed that eligibility would be determined by having lived in the camp for at least six months. The purposive sampling criteria included being between 15 and 24 years old, individuals of any gender identity without prejudice, having resided in the camp for at least six months (this duration ensured that individuals have interacted with social structures, support systems, and prevailing norms within the camp, which are central to the study’s focus), having experienced through self-reported recount or witnessed abuse or community responses to abuse, and the ability to communicate in English, Pidgin, Efik, or Ibibio. These criteria were chosen to ensure that participants had adequate exposure, insight, and communicative capacity to meaningfully contribute to the study. Individuals below 15 years and or those who showed signs of psychological distress, and unwillingness to participate were excluded from the study.

At the initial stage, the researchers held several preliminary meetings with the camp’s leaders to introduce the study objectives and implications. These meetings served as an opportunity to refine and secure community buy-in. These meetings helped refine participants’ selection based on predefined criteria and eligible subgroups within the camp population. Following these discussions, additional face-to-face meetings were held with the dwellers in the camps to further explain the study objectives, clarify concerns, and discuss their intention to participate in the study.

A total of twelve AYPs were selected for in-depth interviews (IDIs), as this number is widely recognized in qualitative research as adequate to reach thematic saturation for relatively homogeneous groups [34]. This was also our experience during the interviews. For the focus group discussions (FGDs), twenty-four participants were recruited to ensure diverse perspectives while maintaining manageable group sizes of eight participants each, which supports active engagement, participant comfort, and rich discussion [35]. Conducting three FGDs also aligns with recommendations for capturing variability across subgroups and enhancing credibility in qualitative inquiry [34, 36]. The three FGDs were organized to enhance homogeneity within each group: one with younger adolescents (15–17), one with older adolescents/young adults (18–24), and one mixed group to explore cross-age interactions. Participants represented a mix of AYP across the 15–24 age range, with both male and female participants drawn from different sections of the camp.

Data collection tools

For the IDIs and FGDs, we leveraged the existing literature to structure the question guides [37, 38]. The guides focused on key aspects and questions relating to lived episodes and experience of abuse and violence within the camps, stigma associated with the victim, perceptions of abuse and normalization, etc. The guides were pilot tested by the authors among similar study participants in different locations. Challenges related to ambiguity and the double-barred nature of some questions asked, and the overall length of time taken for the pilot, were noted throughout the pilot interview and transcript. Following the exercise, the research team held a debriefing meeting to refine the guides. This process allowed us to reduce the overall length of the guides, refine the wording, structure, and flow of the question sequence in alignment with the study objectives (See supplementary material).

Data collection procedure

Our study employed the services of trained Research Assistants. The Research Assistants had a rich blend of academic and professional experience in public health, social services, and qualitative research. The IDI and FGDs were conducted in a safe place allotted by the camp leaders and the participants. The IDI and FGDs were conducted in Pidgin and Efik/Ibibio, the participants’ local languages.

Before the data collection, the study objectives, duration, and modalities of the IDI/FGDs were explained to the participants. Additionally, the Research Assistants requested and obtained written informed consent from participants to participate and to audio-record the IDIs and FGDs from the participants (more information is described in the ethical considerations section). Each IDI averaged 23 min, while each FGD averaged 40 min. Although shorter than standard guidance (typically 60 min for IDIs and 90 min for FGDs) [39], these durations were sufficient to capture in-depth insights because the interview guides were focused and participants provided rich, detailed narratives.

The three FGDs were organized to enhance homogeneity within each group; one with younger adolescents (15–17years), one with older adolescents/young adults (18–24 years), and one mixed group to explore cross-age interactions. This structure aligns with qualitative research guidance indicating that 2–4 FGDs per homogenous participant category are generally sufficient to reach thematic saturation, provided the discussions are in-depth and the groups are purposively selected [34, 35]. Debriefing sessions were held at the end of each data collection day to reflect on the process and discuss any challenges encountered and strategies to improve subsequent sessions [40]. Data were collected until the available information had answered the study objectives or until no new information emerged from the participants [41].

Data analysis

We employed a thematic analysis to systematically identify, organize, and interpret patterns from participants’ narratives. The audio-recorded tapes from the IDIs and FGDs were transcribed verbatim by the Research Assistants with support from the authors. As the IDIs and FGDs were conducted in Pidgin and Efik/Ibibio, audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and then translated into English by research assistants fluent in both languages, with two authors cross-checking the translations against the recordings to ensure meaning, depth, and context were accurately preserved. This careful transcription and translation process ensured that the final English-language transcripts accurately reflected participants’ perspectives, allowing for credible and trustworthy thematic analysis.

Following the development of the transcription, the authors read and familiarized themselves with the major insights from the data, which formed the base for the code tree. The data were imported into NVivo 14 to facilitate coding, organization, and retrieval, supporting the thematic analysis conducted by the researchers. Two independent researchers among the authors coded the data and met to resolve conflicts, compared and merged their codes. On occasions where there were coding disagreements, the researchers resolved them through discussion and consensus between the two independent researchers. When initial codes differed, the researchers reviewed the data together, compared their interpretations, and agreed on the final codes. From the code tree, initial codes were derived from the data, while additional refinement was done through rigorous discussion and meetings among the authors [42]. In line with thematic approach, each participants’ narratives and quotations were directly presented to illustrate the depth of the insights. Our analysis considered not only what participants said, but also why and how they said it, with demographic factors such as age and gender used to contextualize and deepen interpretation.

Reflexivity

Reflexivity relates to practices and positions that may pose potential bias and perspectives in the research process [43]. For instance, AJ brings experience in AYP, which offered insights, while PCA has extensive experience working with AYP for over six years, which further enriched the research process. PCA’s experience in engaging AYP in diverse settings added depth to the analysis, particularly on the social, psychological aspects of their experiences. All authors in this study identified and took a neutral position in understanding and interpreting the findings from this study, ensuring that the themes and ideas from the data were generated through iterative readings and identification of themes.

Ethical considerations and consent to participate

We obtained ethical approval from the Research and Ethics Committee, Cross River State Ministry of Health, with approval number CRSMOH/HRP/REC/2024/492, in line with the ethical guidelines outlined in the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki [44]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their involvement in the study. Parents’ or caregivers’ consents were obtained in writing for participants below 18 years, alongside the child’s assent, with guidance from camp officials serving as gatekeepers. Each participant, regardless of age, was informed of the study objectives, modalities, and potential benefits. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that they would not suffer any risk or consequences if they decided to pull out of the study at any stage.

Given the sensitive nature of sexual exploitation, abuse, and gender-based violence, participants were considered a vulnerable group. To minimize potential distress, discussions were conducted in a safe, private setting, and participants were reminded they could decline to answer any question or withdraw at any time without consequence. One of the camp health workers, a trained mental health professional and counsellor, was made available during and after data collection to provide immediate support to any participant experiencing psychological distress or re-traumatization (although none of this was recorded) and participants were also encouraged and referred to the available support services within the camp. All collected data were stored safely in clou-based, password-protected server. Only the principal investigator of the study had access to the server. Participants were provided with a small, non-monetary token of appreciation to acknowledge their time and participation.

Experience with SEAH and GBV

Findings from both IDIs alone showed that young people in displacement settings shared experiences of GBV and its lasting effects. Participants emphasized fear, stigma, and underreporting that accompany such incidents, as the major challenges surrounding their experiences. The IDI provided more personal and emotionally detailed accounts of abuse and its long-term psychological effects. For instance, a 20-year-old female informant recounted the case of a friend who suffered sexual violence, highlighting the psychological toll and isolation survivors often experience. “Some girls have become pregnant and face GBV. For instance, I have a friend who recently got sexually assaulted by someone she said was her boyfriend. She lives in constant fear, isolating herself at home and battling self-hatred.” – (20-year-old Female, In-depth Informant).

Violence in these settings is not limited to sexual assault but also includes physical and verbal abuse. A 21-year-old female interviewee described how domestic violence and verbal abuse were prevalent within the camp environment, with some perpetrators facing legal consequences. However, she noted that such violence remains widespread. “The cases are there, some people may have been arrested, but I know that violence and use of vulgar and bad language on people is what happens here. I know. On some occasions, some men beat women.” – (21-year-old Female, In-depth Interviewee).

While GBV is often framed as an issue affecting women, a 23-year-old male informant pointed out that men also experience abuse, including physical violence, verbal harassment, and sexual coercion. However, male survivors often remain silent due to societal expectations and fear of being perceived as weak. “I want to note that this is two-way – we should not only focus on it if only women get abused. Yes, they may be the ones mostly, but there is male battery by women – physical abuse, verbal abuse, and pressure for sexual moments when the man may not be ready; these things happen, and no one wants to talk about them. Especially, men who feel they will be considered weak if they talk.” – (23-year-old Male, In-depth Informant).

The FGD, on the other hand, reinforced these experiences while highlighting broader community dynamics that discouraged reporting. Participants discussed how fear of judgment, retaliation, and stigma prevented survivors from seeking help. A 20-year-old female participant emphasized how social pressures discourage survivors from seeking justice, leading to an underreporting of GBV cases. “Although reports may be given to authorities, I am sure that they get less than what happens because some fear being seen reporting these cases because the community will see them differently. They may also be scared by the person who assaulted her.” – (20-year-old Female, FGD Participant).

Contextual factors influencing vulnerability

IDIs and FGDs consistently identified poverty and economic insecurity as major drivers of vulnerability to exploitation and abuse. IDIs emphasized how material deprivation pushed young women into relationships with older men who promised financial or material support. A 23-year-old male informant highlighted how financial deprivation forces some young women into relationships with older men who offer material or financial support. “The primary reason for some of these occurrences [is] involvement with men who made promises of providing the things females here lacked because of the challenging living conditions we face. Poverty is a pervasive issue that contributes to these circumstances.” – (23-year-old Male, In-depth Informant).

Participants in IDI also described how the desire for material possessions intensifies vulnerability, as individuals navigate economic insecurity and social expectations. A 22-year-old male informant noted that the lack of access to basic needs and personal desires fosters relationships driven by financial dependency. “The desire for material possessions often leads girls to seek relationships with boys, expressing their wishes for such things. Poverty in the camp is the main contributing factor. When individuals do not have access to the things they desire, such as nice shoes or material possessions, it can create a sense of longing and confusion.” – (22-year-old Male, In-depth Informant).

FGDs expanded this understanding by linking vulnerability to adolescence, bodily changes, and social pressure. Female participants described how puberty and attention from boys increased exposure to coercion and abuse. A female adolescent in a focus group discussion described how bodily changes during puberty are sometimes accompanied by external pressures and coercion, leading to situations of abuse. “I consider these things as almost flowing with growth. They are wrong. When kids reach adolescence and notice changes in their bodies, like growing breasts and bigger hips, they start feeling mature. When boys show interest in them, they might not refuse; if they do, some persons may pressure them, and that is abuse.” – (21-year-old Female Adolescent, FGD Participant).

A 17-year-old female participant in a focus group discussion also elaborated on how financial instability influences decisions around sexual relationships, often leading to unintended consequences such as pregnancy and sexual abuse. “The failure of receiving financial support, such as when someone offers money, can sway people’s decisions, even when it is related to sexual encounters. Unfortunately, this often leads to unintended pregnancies and other sexual abuse as these relationships can be ongoing.” – (17-year-old Female, FGD Participant).

Emotional and psychological impacts

IDIs and FGDs revealed substantial emotional and psychological consequences associated with abuse and displacement. Across the IDI, participants cited personal accounts of loneliness, mistrust, regret, and emotional withdrawal. Data from the FGDs further complemented these narratives by placimg emotional distress within the broader realities of camp life. Beyond general distress, a key narrative that emerged was the progressive withdrawal of victims from social relationships and community life. Experiences of abuse, particularly when perpetrated by trusted individuals, were described as deeply destabilizing, eroding trust and fostering feelings of betrayal, loneliness, emotional isolation, and social withdrawal. Participants reported intentionally distancing themselves from others as a coping mechanism to avoid further harm, which in turn reinforced their social exclusion. A 21-year-old female interviewee recounted how a shift in a close friendship due to sexual coercion led to deep emotional pain and a desire for isolation. “Do you know how lonely life can get? When you consider someone your best friend from school, and the person suddenly becomes sexually interested in you. I often sit alone, reflecting on all of these. I regret things and want to stay all alone throughout life and not want to trust anyone.” – (21-year-old Female, In-depth Interviewee).

In tandem with the findings from IDI data, participants in the FGD further described how stigma, poverty, insecurity, and repeated exposure to abuse collectively affected mental health and reduced quality of life. For some, living in displacement settings significantly affects mental health, emotional well-being, and overall quality of life. Participants’ accounts suggest that psychological distress is not driven by a single factor, but rather by the cumulative burden of stigma, resource scarcity, and recurrent exposure to abuse. A 20-year-old female focus group participant described how the compounded effects of stigma, resource shortages, and experiences of abuse contribute to psychological distress and a diminished sense of life fulfillment. “Life alone itself in this camp has a great impact on one’s mental health, experiences, and life. You know, you already are battling with the stigma of living here and the shortages of provisions here. Now, you also have to struggle with abuse from people and the fear of reporting. It makes a person not experience life to its fullest. This is the reality of most persons here.” – (20-year-old Female, FGD Participant).

Barriers to reporting and seeking help

Barriers to reporting and seeking help emerged only emerged in the FGD session. The discussions provided especially rich discussions on barriers to reporting abuse, including fear of retaliation, shame, stigma, and distrust in authorities. Participants explained that survivors often remained silent because reporting could expose them and their families to danger, judgment, or humiliation.

Fear of repercussions

A significant barrier to reporting abuse in displacement settings is the fear of retaliation or negative consequences. Young people shared concerns about the potential fallout from speaking out, especially after witnessing the outcomes for others who had attempted to report abuse. One 18-year-old female participant expressed the emotional weight of staying silent to avoid further harm: “Sometimes, these people may want to speak out, but the fear of what might happen next hinders them. When they see what happened to others who spoke up, it’s better to keep quiet, even if it feels like carrying a heavy weight alone.” – (18-year-old Female, FGD Participant). An 18-year-old male participant similarly described how the fear of bringing shame or danger to their family prevented individuals from seeking help: “People know about these mechanisms, but they feel like reporting could mean trouble and shame for their family. Most times, their families depend on them, and they can’t afford to put their families in danger or shame. So, we keep quiet, hoping things would improve by themselves.” – (18-year-old Male, FGD Participant).

Lack of trust in authorities or services

Another significant barrier to disclosure of abuse is a lack of trust in the systems that are meant to provide support. Participants described feeling let down by authorities or service providers who appeared indifferent or ineffective in addressing incidents of violence. An 18-year-old female focus group participant recounted how an instance of harassment involving a friend went unaddressed despite medical checks being provided: “Sometimes, the people who should respond and address these issues around here act somehow indifferent. So, a friend of mine was harassed by her boyfriend, and we spoke to someone in the facility, who provided medical checks and all. But we watched, and it was like nothing went beyond there; the person was not called to order or arrested. So, how do we go back again in the next instance?” – (18-year-old Female, FGD Participant).

Stigma associated with victimhood

The stigma surrounding abuse victimhood also plays a crucial role in deterring young people from reporting abuse. Participants reported that societal pressures to be strong and self-reliant often lead to self-blame and silence. A 21-year-old female participant described the burden of societal expectations that view victimhood as a sign of weakness, which leads to a reluctance to report: “This is so wrong, people are told to be strong, not victims. So, when they are abused or harassed, they have that mentality that they are expected to be strong and not victims, and maybe they cannot report. Since reporting and admitting what happened feels like admitting defeat. The shame and stigma are plenty, so people stay silent and bury their trauma.” – (21-year-old Female, FGD Participant). An 18-year-old male participant also discussed how victimization by women was met with disbelief or mockery: “In the camp, being a victim meant being weak; it brings pity or judgment. Anyway, I did not care. When I reported that my babe hit me, everyone who heard about it, even close friends, mocked me. You know, nobody wants to believe or hear that a woman can physically abuse a man.” – (18-year-old Male, FGD Participant).

Support systems

Data from IDIs and FGDs identified formal and informal support systems within the camp. IDIs highlighted the availability of some GBV services, particularly for severe cases such as rape and intimate partner violence, although participants noted that less visible forms of abuse often received less attention.

Availability and accessibility of support services

Support services for GBV and abuse within displacement camps are reported to be more readily available for severe incidents such as rape and intimate partner violence, but there are challenges in addressing less obvious forms of abuse, like unwanted touching. As a 20-year-old female participant stated: “So, the thing is that these services that are there for abuse are mostly for more serious things like rape or intimate partner abuse, not necessarily for small issues like touches and all.” – (20-year-old Female, In-depth Informant).

FGDs also provided more detailed accounts of how support systems operated in practice. Participants described the importance of GBV focal people, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), peer educators, youth advocates, and safe spaces that encouraged disclosure and emotional healing. One 18-year-old male participant noted: “In the camp, we have people who are dedicated to handling issues of GBV and sexual abuse like rape, and although there are times of the day when they do not work, there is someone who is like a GBV focal person in the health centre and with the NGOs here, and it is easy to talk to them.” – (18-year-old Male, FGD Participant). Additionally, the emotional support offered by these focal people helps young people regain confidence after experiencing abuse. A 17-year-old female participant shared: “The time I went to talk to one of the focal persons about an issue I had with a boy, I was ashamed initially, but then, they were very open to me and encouraged me not to feel bad. Their words helped my mental health, and I regained my self-esteem.” – (17-year-old Female, FGD Participant).

Community responses and solidarity

There were also community-led initiatives to address GBV and sensitize others on SEAH. A 22-year-old female youth representative explained: “When we agreed and led a meeting to sensitize people on GBV and all forms of SEAH, it was as one. Our voices were loud, and we requested that everyone needed to be respected. We resonated how no one should be afraid and live like a victim, but we should be a community united against violence for a future where no one has to live in fear.” – (22-year-old Female Youth Representative, In-depth Interviewee).

FGDs also emphasized community solidarity, with participants describing the camp as a close-knit environment where survivors were supported collectively. In the camp, community solidarity plays a crucial role in addressing abuse and offering mutual support. Participants emphasized the tight-knit nature of the camp community, where instances of abuse were met with collective grief and action. A 19-year-old female participant shared: “When one of us reported being abused and it was confirmed, we all felt the pain. We were close-knit in the camp like a family because we knew that we must take care of each other. It wasn’t just about surviving but making sure that none of us would feel so alone in these struggles.” – (19-year-old Female, FGD Participant). Furthermore, the formation of safe spaces in the camp allowed participants to speak freely and support one another. A 21-year-old male participant reflected on his role in these safe spaces: “We created safe spaces where we could talk freely, away from people. In those moments, we were like one family, supporting one another and taking initiatives to ensure that no one becomes a victim anymore. It was weird as a guy to be part of this, but I am happy that I was. I always have been!” – (21-year-old Male, FGD Participant).

Additionally, several NGOs and peer educators within the camp empowered adolescents to report abuse and access support services. A 20-year-old male participant explained: “There are NGOs and other organizations here that conduct activities related to adolescent sexual and reproductive health (ASRH) in the camp. They provide education through peer educators we all receive these lessons. So, they encourage people who have experienced any form of abuse or harassment can reach out to them and seek assistance.” – (20-year-old Male, FGD Participant). Lastly, youth advocates were instrumental in representing adolescents’ concerns and reporting issues to authorities. One 19-year-old female participant noted: “We also have some young people who report our conditions to the authorities, they actively engage with us to understand our preferences and aspirations, including our desire to continue schooling, and we also report some of these issues to them as we have trusted them over time.” – (19-year-old Female, FGD Participant).

Perceptions of abuse and normalization

Cultural and societal influences on perceptions

Across both methods, participants described how cultural norms and displacement conditions contributed to the normalization of abuse. IDIs provided reflective accounts of how repeated exposure to violence across generations shaped acceptance of abusive behaviour. Overall, both methods demonstrated that displacement intensified normalization by creating environments marked by insecurity, poverty, and weakened protection systems, where harmful behaviours became embedded within everyday survival. Participants explained that young people who witnessed abuse without consequences often came to see it as normal. A 23-year-old male informant highlighted how generational exposure to abuse creates a cycle of normalization. He noted that witnessing abuse without intervention reinforces acceptance of violence among younger individuals, making it difficult to challenge societal norms. “When people grow up to see their mothers and sisters being abused, they grow to see it as normal. Mostly, since their aunties and mothers did not report or speak against it, it has a way of making other younger girls and adolescents communize and normalize abuse and harassment.” – (23-year-old Male, In-depth Informant).

These findings were also elaborated in the FGD session. Among participants, abuse was frequently viewed as an ordinary part of life, particularly in rural and displacement contexts. A 20-year-old female participant in a focus group discussion emphasized how abuse is seen as part of life rather than a violation, particularly in rural settings. “This is Nigeria, especially in rural settings, where there is a way communities and cultural norms tend to normalize abuse, and this is wrong. So, people tend to abuse and get abused and not report it as they think it is normal.” – (20-year-old Female, FGD Participant).

Impact of displacement on normalization

Participants also described how displacement exacerbates the normalization of abuse, as young people in camps face multiple disadvantages that make them more accepting of harmful situations. Living in conditions marked by economic hardship, insecurity, and limited protection mechanisms appeared to lower thresholds for what is considered unacceptable behaviour. In this context, abuse becomes embedded within everyday survival strategies, with individuals more likely to tolerate harmful situations they might otherwise resist.

A 22-year-old male informant highlighted the compounded vulnerability of displaced individuals, explaining that displacement often forces people to tolerate situations they would otherwise reject. “Life happens sometimes and puts people twice at a disadvantage. So, when people are displaced, they tend to see some things as normal that are not, and abuse is one of them.” – (22-year-old Male, In-depth Informant).

This normalization was further reinforced through social dynamics within the camp, where collective acceptance of certain behaviours creates pressure to conform. A 21-year-old female interviewee further elaborated on how various forms of abuse, ranging from unpermitted touching to intimate partner violence, are often dismissed in camp settings. She noted that individuals who challenge this normalization are often met with resistance. “I would say this very firmly: sometimes, people here see these things as normal and not as abuse. Unpermitted touches, sexual violence, verbal abuse, intimate partner violence – people here in the camp see these as normal and look at you as weird when you call it abuse.” – (21-year-old Female, In-depth Interviewee).

Discussion

The study explored the in-depth experiences of abuse, the normalization of such abuse, and the existing barriers to reporting within the IDP settlement in Bakassi, Cross River State, Nigeria. Findings revealed several interconnected themes that collectively illustrate the realities of respondents. These include perceptions and normalization of abuse, contextual factors such as limited accountability heighten vulnerability, emotional and psychological impacts, barriers to reporting and seeking help, and the availability and effectiveness of support systems. Together, these themes present comprehensively how abuse is perceived, experienced, and navigated within the resettlement.

Findings from our study showed that societal norms within displacement settings contribute to the normalization of abuse. Adolescents were often compelled to perceive harassment and sexual abuse as acceptable, particularly towards girls, reflecting entrenched patriarchal attitudes common in rural Nigerian contexts. Most rural Nigerian settings perceive abuse toward the feminine folk to be normal in a male-domineering, stereotyped community [45]. Such norms, transmitted across generations, reinforce tolerance for abusive behaviors, which may be exacerbated by the vulnerabilities induced by displacement [16]. Various forms of sexual abuse, including unwanted touching, intimate partner violence, and verbal harassment, were often treated casually in the camp and largely went unpunished. Similar trends have been reported in South Sudan, where displacement increased non-partner sexual violence (NPSV) and IPV among adolescents [46], as well as in North America and Haiti, where abuse of displaced adolescent girls was also observed [16]. Findings imply that programmes and interventions targeting adolescents in settings of displacement must not rely solely on mechanisms but must also address social and cultural norms that legitimize abuse. Addressing these perceptions calls for community-level behavioural change strategies, structured adolescent-based GBV prevention programmes and adopting gender-transformative programming, which are evidence-based to reduce the acceptance of violence in several other humanitarian settings [47].

Victims in the camps experienced repeated exposure to prolonged distress, compounding their psychological burden over time. This is consistent with findings from Alves et al. (2024), who reported that adolescents exposed to various forms of abuse frequently suffer mental health disorders [48]. These psychological impacts may outlive the abusive events themselves and increase adolescents’ risks of such chronic mental health conditions as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Systematic reviews of internally displaced populations in sub-Saharan Africa show consistently high PTSD prevalence, demonstrating how displacement amplifies trauma exposure and weakens coping resources [49]. Notably, abuse was not limited to females; males, particularly younger adolescents, were also victims. Societal expectations of male strength often discouraged reporting, causing male victims to endure abuse in silence. A study in India by Malik & Nadda (2019) reports abuse of males by females [50]. Evidence from other African contexts, including Libya, Ghana, the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), and South Sudan, similarly shows male adolescents as vulnerable to abuse [14–53]. Thus, mental health interventions in displacement settings must be gender-responsive and inclusive, recognizing that both male and female adolescents face unique psychological risks.

Economic and societal pressures were key drivers of adolescent vulnerability to abuse. Adolescents faced societal pressure to accept advances from men upon reaching puberty, and economic hardship compounded their vulnerability. Scarcity of financial resources, relief items, and dependence on external support created conditions that rendered young people, particularly girls, more susceptible to abuse [54]. Similar patterns were observed in Uganda, where lack of material resources and unemployment were linked to increased sexual and gender-based violence among adolescent girls and women [55, 56]. These pressures also reinforced inequitable gender norms, often leading to early and forced marriages.

Fear of repercussions, stigma, and previous experiences of indifference by authorities were significant barriers preventing adolescents from reporting abuse. In some cases, reporting resulted in negative consequences for complainants, which discouraged others from coming forward. Perpetration of sexual abuse in displacement camps is often unchecked, particularly when authorities themselves are complicit [57, 58]. Social stigma was especially pronounced for male victims, who are expected to maintain a façade of strength and may face ridicule if abused [59]. Similar findings were found in Uganda, where adolescents and youths who were victims of sexual abuse experienced social stigma [56].

These patterns demonstrate structural weaknesses in mechanisms that should safeguard adolescents and suggest that, without adolescent-friendly, private, and confidential pathways, abuse in most cases will remain under-reported and normalized. Limited accountability further enhances a culture of impunity and highlights the need for strengthened community sensitization, protective systems, and tailored psychosocial and other support services for adolescents in settings of displacement. Evidence from several settings shows that improving survivor-centered reporting mechanisms significantly increases disclosure and uptake of services [60, 61].

Despite these challenges, support services within the camp offered some relief, particularly for severe cases such as rape. Community solidarity emerged as a crucial protective factor, providing victims with social support and facilitating the effectiveness of group activities, including enlightenment and sensitization programs. Such support systems play a vital role in mitigating self-isolation and promoting mental health recovery [62, 63]. The presence of these formal and informal support mechanisms further demonstrates the need for community-based interventions in settings of displacement. Evidence from humanitarian contexts shows that peer support groups, survivor-safe spaces, and targeted psychosocial programs significantly improve coping, resilience, and help-seeking behaviors among adolescents [64, 65]. Strengthening these systems, through integrations of professional social and mental health services as well as continuous funding, can enhance identification of abuse, lessen the burden of trauma, and enhance long-term wellbeing.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study adopted a qualitative approach, which offers deep insight into the perspective of the respondents, by considering their lived experiences. While adolescents in displacement settings have been studied largely in Nigeria, previous studies have mostly focused on the broad GBV experiences, service gaps or humanitarian needs. This study contributes new knowledge by showing how normalization occurs, how structural and economic vulnerabilities reinforce exploitation, how male victimization remains hidden, and how community dynamics both enable and hinder reporting. Our study provides evidence that can directly inform policy design, adolescent-focused GBV prevention, and survivor-centered interventions in humanitarian camps. FGDs were not stratified by sex or gender, which may have constrained participants’ willingness to express gender-specific perspectives in a group setting. However, this limitation was partially mitigated by IDIs, which were conducted in separate sessions and allowed for more gender-specific insights to emerge. Limited integration of the IDI and FGDs were conducted in the result section, which may have limited the overall depth of the findings from this study. We encourage future similar studies to integrate findings across the methods to provide deeper contextual understanding of the phenomenon under study.

Conclusion and recommendations

AYP in displacement settings shared their experiences of GBV, sexual exploitation, abuse, and harassment, and their lasting effects within the camps. Despite their experience, most of our participants feared repercussions, coupled with their lack of trust in authorities or services, which posed as barriers for reporting. Many struggled with self-blame and remained silent despite the recurring experience of abuse. Due to this, prosecution of abuse perpetrators is remarkably lax in displacement settings in Nigeria. There is an urgent need to strengthen and upscale support services within the camp in a bid to provide essential aid to abuse victims and ensure a safer environment for all. GBV and displacement policies should prioritize adolescents, including implementing prevention strategies that question the cultural and social norms that drive violence among young people. Adolescent-oriented, gender-transformative approaches are critical to reducing social acceptance of violence. Mechanisms for accountability need to be created in IDP camps to compensate for a culture of impunity; this is especially when the system fails to act. Also, policies should consider male adolescents as a vulnerable group. Scaling up social protection measures such as livelihood support, cash transfers, and education subsidies can help to relieve economic pressures that make adolescents vulnerable to exploitation. Humanitarian agencies should create safe and confidential systems for adolescents to report abuse without prejudice or fear of reprisal. Psychosocial service provision at the community-level, including survivor safe-spaces, peer support groups, and targeted mental health care, should be strengthened. Continued training of front-line workforce and camp leadership in trauma-informed survivor-centered care is critical to ensuring trust. Furthermore, combining gender-transformative initiatives such as mentoring and life skills programmes can help change negative norms. Together, these efforts will enhance protection, reporting, and recovery for adolescent girls in displacement.
