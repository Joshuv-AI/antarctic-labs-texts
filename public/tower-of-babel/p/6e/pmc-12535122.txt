Breastfeeding difficulties and maternal mental health: role of social support in rural northwestern China
Authors: Jie Yang, Yunjie Liu, Xu Zhang, Yihan Zhang, Yuchen Meng, Junhao Wu, Yongchang Duan, Hongyu Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, escalating mental health issues among postpartum women underscore their significant impact on individuals, children, and society. This study investigates the associations between breastfeeding difficulties and maternal mental health in rural areas of northwestern China. Additionally, we examine how mothers’ perceived family support and home visits by community health workers (CHWs) are associated with variations in maternal mental health outcomes.MethodsData for this study were collected in March and December 2021. Descriptive statistics and multivariate linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the prevalence of maternal mental health problems and the association between breastfeeding difficulties and maternal mental health. Maternal mental health was assessed using Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21), while breastfeeding difficulties were measured based on mothers’ self-reported experiences with common breastfeeding challenges. Perceived family support for breastfeeding and a community-based home visit intervention were analyzed to explore differences in associations across subgroups.ResultsGreater overall breastfeeding difficulty was significantly associated with higher maternal stress (β = 2.391, P = 0.001), and showed marginally positive associations with depression (β = 1.186, P = 0.102) and anxiety (β = 0.953, P = 0.082). Among specific categories, feeding process difficulties were significantly associated with increased anxiety (β = 1.173, P = 0.031) and stress (β = 2.031, P = 0.008). Among mothers with lower practical support, breastfeeding difficulties were significantly associated with higher depression (β = 1.782, P = 0.009), anxiety (β = 1.577, P = 0.002), and stress (β = 2.722, P < 0.001), whereas associations in the higher support group were not significant. For emotional support, a stronger association with stress was observed in the lower support group (β = 2.748, P = 0.008) but not in the higher support group. Stratification by community health worker home visits showed minimal subgroup differences. ConclusionsIn essence, this study underscores the impact of breastfeeding challenges on maternal mental health and highlights family support’s pivotal role, calling for targeted intervention to support rural mothers.Trial registrationThe study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shaanxi Normal University and Xi’an Jiaotong University of China (No: 2020 − 1240) on 08/03/2019. Background

Maternal mental health has become a critical threat to public health because of its robust associations to peripartum or postpartum complications [1] adverse birth outcome [2], stunted growth [3], as well as compromised children’s cognitive and noncognitive development [4, 5]. According to the World Health Organization [6], more than 10% of prenatal women and 13% of postnatal women have tendencies of mental health problems globally, such as depression, Anxiety, and stress. Numerous empirical studies have reported an even higher prevalence of mental health problems in low- and middle-income countries, with approximately 15–57% [7, 8].

China, like other low- and middle-income countries, faces significant mental health challenges among perinatal and postnatal women. Previous studies have found an overall maternal depression prevalence of 16.3%, with prenatal depression at 19.7% and postnatal depression at 14.8% [9]. Notably, rural areas exhibit higher incidences of maternal depression, ranging from 23 to 32%, compared to about 15.5% in urban settings [10–12]. However, due to the lack of routine screening of mental health problems in rural areas, this prevalence is still considerably underestimated. The relative shortage of mental health specialists and medical resources in rural China makes the exploration of preventive interventions more urgent, and this requires a comprehensive understanding of the potential risks to women’s mental health in these regions.

Breastfeeding is one of the most important practices for women after delivery, but the influence of breastfeeding difficulties over maternal mental health is often overlooked when identifying risk factors for maternal mental health. Existing studies of risk factors of maternal mental health mainly focus on individual behaviors or social factors [13]. For instance, smoking during pregnancy [14], unhealthy dietary habits [15, 16], inadequate or poor-quality sleep [17], and lack of exercise [18] are highly associated with the incidence of postnatal depression. At the social level, women’s mental health is influenced by factors such as family economic status and spousal relationships [19, 20].

Yet, it is well documented that postpartum depression is associated with lower rates of breastfeeding [21, 22], but few studies have focused on the potential influence of difficulties during breastfeeding practice on maternal mental health. A previous study suggests that physical discomfort during breastfeeding can be linked to an increased risk of postpartum depression [23]. This creates a troubling cycle: breastfeeding difficulties intensify depression or anxiety, complicating the feeding process further, leading to longer feeding times, and ultimately harming the infant’s nutritional intake and growth [24].

Breastfeeding difficulties are prevalent worldwide, including in China. In Denmark, up to 40% of postpartum women experience breastfeeding problems [25]. In Sweden, 26.9% of mothers reported issues such as sore nipples, poor sucking technique, and perceived insufficient milk supply [26]. Although studies on breastfeeding difficulties are rare in China, one study in Beijing indicated that nearly one-third of mothers experienced moderate or severe of breastfeeding difficulties [27]. These challenges are often overlooked by families and society when understanding their mental health status.

Another gap in the existing literature in understanding the role of family support, which may help mitigate the impact of breastfeeding difficulties on maternal mental health. According to the stress buffering model, social support can mitigate the psychological impact of a stressful event by attenuating the stress appraisal response [28]. That is, social support may provide an individual emotional support or instrumental help to better cope with their breastfeeding difficulties and thus reduce its impact on their mental health. Thoits [29] further suggests that support from close family members may have potential distinct effects on mental health. Although there were plenty of studies theorizes that family support is a potential buffer between disadvantaged situations like food insecurity and poor mental health [30] studies on whether family support protects maternal mental health under conditions of breastfeeding difficulties remain underexplored in rural China.

In addition to family support, community-based interventions offer an alternative source of social support that may mitigate the impact of breastfeeding difficulties on maternal mental health. In our study, we incorporated community support by introducing home visits conducted by community health workers (CHWs), who provided both nutritional and health education support as well as emotional support. A systematic review has demonstrated that such community support—by delivering practical information on nutrition and breastfeeding alongside emotional assistance—can help mothers manage negative emotions during periods of breastfeeding difficulties. Moreover, a range of personalized, community-delivered support interventions have proven to be particularly effective in enhancing both psychological well-being and breastfeeding outcomes [31].

To address these research gaps and contribute to the limited literature on breastfeeding difficulties and maternal mental health in rural China, we examined the association between breastfeeding difficulties and maternal mental health outcomes. Notably, we tested whether this association differed across levels of perceived family support and participation in a community-based home visit intervention.

Methods

Data and sample

This study utilized baseline and endline data from a randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted by Shaanxi Normal University in rural Shaanxi Province, which focused on maternal and infant health. The study employed a multilevel cluster random sampling strategy to identify participants through a three-step procedure. First, ten counties with relatively low levels of economic development were randomly selected from five prefecture-level cities in the province. Second, within each county, ten townships were randomly chosen from a list provided by the local Health Bureau, excluding the township that housed the county seat due to its relatively higher urbanization and affluence. In counties with fewer than ten townships, all townships were included, resulting in a final sample of 79 townships. Third, within each selected township, a list of pregnant women between 8 and 32 weeks of gestation was obtained from the local Township Health Center, from which ten women were randomly selected. In townships with fewer than ten eligible women, all were included. Eligible participants met the following criteria: aged between 18 and 45 years, had resided locally for at least one year, and were within the first six months of pregnancy.

Two rounds of data collection were conducted using the same structured questionnaire in the same sample sites. The baseline survey was administered in March 2021, and the endline survey followed in December 2021, approximately eight months later. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained enumerators recruited from Xi’an university students. Enumerators underwent extensive training and completed a pilot survey with 20 participants prior to formal data collection. Interviews were conducted privately, with efforts made to avoid interruptions from other family members. The survey gathered information on infant and household characteristics, maternal demographics, breastfeeding practices, and maternal mental health. A total of 597 eligible mothers were enrolled at baseline, and 527 completed the endline survey (response rate: 88.3%). After excluding 30 cases with missing outcome or key covariate data, the final Analytic sample included 497 mothers, representing 83.2% of the baseline cohort.

The intervention was delivered from May to December 2021. It consisted of monthly home visits and telephone follow-ups delivered by trained community health workers (CHWs), who were recruited from among women and child health specialists at township health centers. A total of 39 CHWs participated in the study, with An average of 8.7 Years of work experience. On average, CHWs were 33 Years old, and 80% of them held a college degree or higher. Each home visits lasted 40–60 min and focused on maternal nutrition, breastfeeding techniques, and early childhood development. In the meantime, participants in the control group families received no additional intervention.

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shaanxi Normal University and Xi’an Jiaotong University of China (No: 2020 − 1240). Each eligible participant received a consent form with information regarding program objectives, procedures, potential risks, benefits, and an explanation of privacy protection. Participants provided informed consent for inclusion in the study before engaging in a face-to-face interview with a single enumerator.

Measures

Maternal mental health

Maternal mental health (including depression, stress, and anxiety) was assessed using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21), which is a quantitative measure of depression, anxiety, and stress symptomatology (7 statements each) during the past week. Participants were asked to decide how Much the statements apply to them using a scale from 0 to 3 where 0 refers to “did not apply to me at all”, 1 refers to “applied to me to some degree”, 2 refers to “applied to me to a considerable degree”, and 3 refers to “applied to me very much”. The score of each subscale is Multiplied by 2 to lie within a 0 to 42 scale where higher scores indicate worse outcomes [32]. Each subscale score is Multiplied by 2 to obtain the final score that corresponds to the grading criteria of DASS. Based on the final scores of each subscale, the severity of negative emotions is classified into five levels: normal, mild, moderate, severe, and extremely severe [32, 33]. In this study, the levels were further consolidated, with the “normal” level classified as having no corresponding risk of negative emotions, while the other four levels were classified as having corresponding risks. Specifically, a depression subscale score > 9 indicates a risk of depression; an anxiety subscale score > 7 indicates a risk of anxiety; and a stress subscale score > 14 indicates a risk of stress [33]. The Chinese version of the DASS-21 was validated in a previous study and Cronbach’s alpha for its subscales was 0.78, 0.66, and 0.83, respectively [34].

Breastfeeding difficulty

Breastfeeding difficulties were measured based on maternal reports of 17 challenges experienced within the first two weeks postpartum (e.g., baby not latching, nipple pain, insufficient milk, cesarean-related issues; full list in Supplementary Table A1). The item pool was adapted from the validated Breastfeeding Experience Scale (BES), with modifications to better reflect the rural Chinese postpartum context. Emotionally oriented items (e.g., feeling tired or embarrassed) were excluded to avoid conceptual overlap with psychological outcomes, and culturally specific barriers (e.g., cesarean/episiotomy-related issues, time constraints) were added.

Guided by prior research, we grouped the items into three theoretically defined domains: infant-related difficulties, physical feeding difficulties, and maternal difficulties [24, 35, 36]. To construct composite indicators, we applied exploratory factor analysis using polychoric correlation matrices and principal-axis factoring, extracting one factor per domain. Standardized factor scores were computed using the regression method and used as continuous predictors in subsequent models. In addition, we extracted a total breastfeeding difficulty score to capture the overall level of challenges experienced. As the purpose was to generate summary scores rather than test latent structures, factor loadings are not reported.

Breastfeeding family support

Breastfeeding family support was measured using a 9-item scale developed by Zhu Xiu [37]. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.886. Participants rated their level of agreement with each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” The scale consists of two dimensions: practical support, comprising 2 items (e.g., “My family often prepares food that is good for lactation”), and emotional support, comprising 7 items (e.g., “I think my family wants me to exclusively breastfeed my child”). Practical support in this study refers to tangible help provided by family members, such as caring for the baby or assisting with housework, while emotional support reflects the recognition and encouragement received from family members regarding breastfeeding.

For the analysis, we calculated the mean scores for each support dimension and constructed binary subgroup variables to capture variation in support levels. Participants were classified into “higher” and “lower” family practical support and emotional support groups based on the distribution of scores. To enhance the distinction between groups, those with moderate but not consistently high support levels were grouped into the “lower” category.

Home visits by community health workers

Home visits by community health workers (CHWs) were similarly used to construct a binary subgroup variable. This variable captures whether a participant received the childbirth support home-visit intervention delivered by CHWs. Participants were classified into two groups: “received home visits” (coded as 1) and “did not receive home visits” (coded as 0).

Covariates

In all multivariate models, we controlled for potential confounding factors based on prior literature. These included maternal characteristics (age, education, self-rated health), infant characteristics (gender, birth order, preterm status, underweight), and family context (husband co-residence, conjugal relationship quality, family asset level, and grandparental caregiving) [38–41]. We also included a binary indicator for receipt of home visits and risk of maternal mental health at baseline to adjust for potential intervention exposure. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were below 5 for all covariates, indicating no multicollinearity concerns.

Statistical analyses

The data processing and statistical Analyses in this study were conducted using STATA 17.0. Frequency and percentage were used to describe maternal characteristics, infant characteristics, family characteristics, and specific maternal breastfeeding difficulties. Additionally, we performed descriptive analyses of postpartum mental health indicators using frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, as appropriate.

Multiple linear regression models were employed to examine the associations between breastfeeding difficulties and maternal mental health outcomes, including depression, anxiety, and stress. To further assess whether these associations differed by levels of family support, we conducted stratified regressions by family emotional support and family practical support. Similarly, to examine differences by exposure to community support, we conducted stratified analyses based on receipt of home visits from community health workers (CHWs). Multicollinearity was assessed, and all variance inflation factors (VIFs) were below 5, indicating no multicollinearity concerns.

The role of social support in the association between breastfeeding difficulties and maternal mental health

To further explore the potential buffering role of social support, we conducted stratified regressions by levels of family support and community support (Table 5). Across all three mental health outcomes—depression, anxiety, and stress—breastfeeding difficulties were more strongly associated with adverse outcomes among mothers with lower levels of family practical support. For example, among mothers with lower practical support, breastfeeding difficulties were significantly associated with higher depression (β = 1.782, P = 0.009), anxiety (β = 1.577, P = 0.002), and stress scores (β = 2.722, P < 0.001), while the corresponding coefficients in the higher support group were negative but not statistically significant. These patterns suggest that practical assistance from family members may buffer the negative psychological effects of breastfeeding difficulties.

A similar pattern was observed when stratifying by family emotional support. The association between breastfeeding difficulties and maternal stress was stronger and statistically significant among mothers reporting lower emotional support (β = 2.748, P = 0.008), while the corresponding coefficient in the higher support group did not reach statistical significance. Notably, our additional models including interaction terms further confirmed a significant moderating effect of emotional support, with negative and statistically significant interaction effects for depression, anxiety, and stress, suggesting that emotional support plays a particularly important role in mitigating the mental health impact of breastfeeding difficulties.

In contrast, stratification by home visits from community health workers revealed relatively small and statistically marginal differences. While the association between breastfeeding difficulties and stress remained significant in both subgroups (received vs. not received), the effect sizes were similar, and no clear moderating pattern emerged. This finding implies that, compared to family-based support, community-level interventions such as home visits may have a more limited role in buffering maternal mental health risks associated with breastfeeding.

Discussion

Utilizing survey data from rural households in northwestern China, we examined the prevalence of maternal mental health challenges encompassing depression, anxiety, and stress. Our findings highlighted breastfeeding practice difficulties as a potential contributing factor to maternal mental well-being. Notably, our study highlighted the influence of perceived family support, showing that it plays a significant role in mitigating the impact of breastfeeding difficulties on maternal mental health.

In our sampled regions, the incidence rates of depression, anxiety, and stress among women within six months post-delivery markedly surpassed national averages [42]. However, despite this high prevalence, psychological healthcare resources in these rural areas remain limited, impeding immediate screening and treatment access. Given the transient nature of postpartum depression, investing in preventive interventions becomes imperative. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of potential risk factors for maternal mental health problems assumes greater importance for more effective and efficient preventive measures.

Our investigation revealed that overall breastfeeding difficulties were significantly associated with increased maternal stress, and showed positive associations with both depression and anxiety symptoms. This aligns with previous research that has linked factors like maternal sleep difficulties, breastfeeding concerns, and negative feeding attitudes to the onset of depression or anxiety [43–45].

Moreover, our study delineated varying impacts of different difficulty types on maternal mental health. Notably, difficulties during feeding process exhibited a more pronounced influence on maternal mental well-being compared to difficulties related to infant (such as baby not waking up for feedings on schedule or choking on milk) or maternal difficulties (like insufficient time for breastfeeding or doctor advising against breastfeeding). Previous studies have indicated a correlation between physical discomfort and an increased risk of depression [46]. Another survey reported that women experiencing negative sentiments about breastfeeding and reporting significant pain during nursing shortly after childbirth were more likely to encounter postpartum depression within two months, as evidenced in a large-scale study of U.S. women [23].

However, given the cultural context of rural China, where it is common for postpartum women to live in multigenerational households with parents or in-laws, we incorporated perceived family support as a key stratification variable during the design phase. This decision was guided by previous studies highlighting the role of kin-based support in shaping maternal well-being [47]. Categorizing this support into emotional and practical dimensions, following Emmott’s framework [48], we discovered that both forms of support from family members could alleviate the adverse effects of breastfeeding difficulties on maternal mental health. Notably, the adverse impact of breastfeeding difficulties on maternal mental health was more pronounced among women with lower levels of family support, suggesting that supportive family environments may play a buffering role. Family emotional support was more strongly associated with a buffering pattern, which might be attributed to its ability to enhance social connection, self-esteem, and coping strategies, thereby reducing stress [49].

In addition to examining the role of family support in breastfeeding, we also explored whether community support modifies the relationship between breastfeeding difficulties and maternal mental health. However, our findings indicate that community support did not yield substantial subgroup differences. One possible explanation is that, in rural areas of China, child-rearing is predominantly the responsibility of the family, while community interventions tend to be limited in scope and depth. Moreover, support from family members is often more continuous and emotionally intimate [50]. Mothers may also place greater trust in the advice and comfort offered by close relatives, whereas community support may be perceived as external, lacking the individualized care that mothers need [51]. Another possible explanation concerns the implementation features of the community-based intervention. Although community health workers (CHWs) delivered structured monthly home visits, the relatively low frequency and brief contact duration may have constrained their capacity to build trust and offer sustained emotional support. Furthermore, the intervention focused primarily on technical knowledge (e.g., nutrition, breastfeeding) rather than therapeutic interaction, which may explain why CHW support did not appear to buffer the adverse effects of breastfeeding difficulties on maternal mental health. These findings suggest that, in low-resource rural settings, family-based support may be more impactful than externally delivered interventions unless the latter are intensified or redesigned to foster deeper emotional engagement.

In advancing current research, our study contributes novel insights in three important ways. First, we developed and validated a culturally adapted tool to assess breastfeeding difficulties in rural China, incorporating structural and procedural barriers often omitted in Western-developed instruments. Second, we conceptually differentiated and empirically tested three subtypes of breastfeeding difficulties, revealing that difficulties during the feeding process have the strongest association with maternal mental health outcomes. This nuance helps clarify the potential mechanism through which physical discomfort may translate into psychological distress. Third, we compared the differential associations across levels of family and community support, demonstrating that family emotional support is more effective in addressing mental health risks than community-based interventions. These findings extend the stress-buffering framework to the specific context of rural maternal health in China and offer guidance for designing culturally grounded support strategies.

This study also has some limitations that warrant consideration. While it reveals a higher incidence of maternal mental health issues and strong associations with breastfeeding challenges, its conclusions are specific to our sampled regions due to the relatively small sample size. Thus, generalizing these findings to all rural areas in China requires caution. In addition, the breastfeeding difficulty scale was developed based on a combination of adapted and context-specific items, rather than a standardized instrument, which may introduce measurement error or limit cross-study comparability. Maternal mental health outcomes were also based on self-reported DASS-21 scores, which, despite being widely used, may be subject to reporting bias.

Conclusions

We utilized survey data from rural households in northwestern China to examine the relationship between breastfeeding difficulties and maternal mental health. Our analysis revealed a significant association between breastfeeding difficulties—particularly those related to the physical aspects of feeding—and increased levels of maternal stress. An important finding of this study is the variation in this association across levels of family support. Specifically, both practical and emotional support from family members appear to play a protective role in maternal mental health, especially when compared to community support, underscoring the importance of family member involvement.

Our findings offer valuable insights for policymakers, highlighting the importance of not only promoting breastfeeding but also providing support for mothers facing various breastfeeding difficulties, and ensuring that women with difficulties in breastfeeding have access to social support. Thus, efforts to address the difficulties encountered during breastfeeding are crucial to promoting maternal health in rural areas. Combining such interventions with emotional and practical support from close relatives may be particularly effective.

In summary, given the persistent high incidence of difficulties in breastfeeding in rural China and the tendency of postpartum women to experience depression, anxiety, and stress, we believe that it is necessary to conduct more research on postpartum women’s mental health and breastfeeding experiences in rural China.
