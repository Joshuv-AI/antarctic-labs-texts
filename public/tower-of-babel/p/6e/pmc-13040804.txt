Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio as a correlative factor of severe SARS-CoV−2 infection in neonates: associations with lymphocyte subsets alterations
Authors: Guangliang Bi, Qingyan Xiao, Jie He, Shuzhe Xiao, Jie Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe SARS-CoV-2 remains prevalent, and neonates remain susceptible. This study aims to explore the potential of the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) as a valuable tool for assessing the severity of SARS-CoV-2 infection in term neonates in the late neonatal period (8–28 days of life). Additionally, it seeks to examine the underlying immune characteristics associated with this ratio.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted from November 1, 2022, to February 1, 2023 in four hospitals. Baseline characteristics and laboratory results were collected and analyzed. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of NLR and lymphocyte subsets analysis were performed.ResultsThe study enrolled 143 term neonates in the late neonatal period with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, comprising 54 severe cases and 89 non-severe cases, along with 59 age-matched uninfected controls. The NLR was identified as an associated factor for severe SARS-CoV-2 in term neonates in the late neonatal period. Furthermore, the levels of CD4+CD45RA and CD8+CD28+ T cells were significantly reduced in neonates with SARS-CoV-2 compared to healthy neonates, and a significant decrease in CD8+CD28+ T cells was observed in the severe SARS-CoV-2 group compared to the non-severe group. ConclusionsNLR is an associated factor of severe SARS-CoV-2 in term neonates in the late neonatal period. Introduction

Since the World Health Organization (WHO) declared SARS-CoV-2 a global health emergency in January 2020, the virus has undergone significant evolution, with emerging variants exhibiting enhanced transmissibility and immune evasion capabilities [1]. Certain recently emerged sublineages within the Omicron family, characterized by key mutations such as L455S in the spike protein, have demonstrated unprecedented immune escape from neutralizing antibodies and a 1.5- to 2.0-fold higher effective reproduction number compared to earlier variants [2, 3]. By early 2024, these variants accounted for a dominant proportion of global SARS-CoV-2 cases and have been classified as a “variant of concern” (VOC) by WHO due to its rapid dominance in multiple regions, including China [4]. This variant’s heightened affinity for human ACE2 receptors and enhanced fusogenicity in nasal epithelial cells may explain its accelerated spread, particularly in pediatric populations [5].

Compounding this threat, China’s 2024 spring season witnessed a convergence of respiratory pathogens, including influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and mycoplasma pneumoniae, creating a syndemic scenario that strains neonatal healthcare systems [4]. Neonates, with their immature immune systems and limited maternal antibody transfer, face amplified risks of severe complications such as multisystem inflammatory syndrome (MIS-N) and persistent immune dysregulation [5]. In studies involving adults and older children, multiple research findings indicate that elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) levels correlate positively with COVID-19 disease severity [6–7]. However, neonates’ immune systems remain underdeveloped, resulting in significant differences in immune response mechanisms compared to adults and older children. While adult studies have established NLR as a robust prognostic marker for COVID-19 severity [5], the immunological landscape in neonates remains poorly characterized. Preliminary evidence suggests that SARS-CoV-2 infection in newborns may trigger atypical cytokine profiles, including elevated IL-6 and IL-10 levels, potentially exacerbating lymphopenia and neutrophil hyperactivation [8].

This study analyzes clinical and hematological data from SARS-CoV-2-positive term neonates in the late neonatal period (8–28 days of life) in four Guangdong province hospitals during China’s initial post-liberalization wave (Nov 1, 2022 to Feb 1, 2023). We aim to elucidate whether NLR is associated with disease severity in this vulnerable population and explore the alterations of lymphocyte subsets in neonates with severe infection.

Methods

Study design and population

The current retrospective cohort study included patients with SARS-CoV-2 in Nanfang Hospital in Guangzhou, Dongguan Children’s Hospital, Dongguan Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital in Dongguan, Zhongshan Women and Children’s Hospital in Zhongshan, China, from Nov 1, 2022, to Feb 1, 2023. The patient inclusion criteria were: (1) Term neonates in the late neonatal period (8–28 days of life) with COVID-19 nucleic acid test positive as well as blood culture negative; (2) Availability of laboratory data within the first 24 h of admission. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Lack of laboratory test results; (2) Presence of other pathogen infections; (3) Presence of severe congenital diseases; (4) Parental disagreement to participate.

Term neonates in the late neonatal period were selected because the ratio of neutrophils to lymphocytes in newborns undergoes a reversal within the first week after birth (from day 1 to around day 6, neutrophils are higher than lymphocytes; from around day 6 up to 6 years old, lymphocytes are higher than neutrophils) [9]. Moreover, their immune systems are more developed compared to early neonates, allowing for a clearer observation of clinical progression and laboratory changes during recovery.

In order to compare the NLR and lymphocyte subgroups of neonates after SARS-CoV-2 infection with normal baselines, we also included neonates during the same period who had no infections, abnormal delivery history, or congenital diseases as controls. The healthy neonates recruited as matched controls were selected based on several parameters including gestational age, postnatal age, birth weight, and gender, and were matched to the SARS-CoV-2-positive neonates (case group). Controls were selected from the same hospital cohort as the COVID-19-positive neonates to ensure comparability. They were healthy outpatient neonates without any perinatal infections, delivery complications, or congenital anomalies. The blood sample collection time for the control group differed from that of the case group by no more than 24 h. The matching process was conducted manually, with careful consideration of the aforementioned parameters to ensure optimal matching between cases and controls.

Neonates were diagnosed with COVID-19 following the Seventh Edition of the Interim Guidance from the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China. This guidance was based on a combination of epidemiological history (such as close contact with confirmed COVID-19 cases), clinical symptomatology (including fever, cough, dyspnea, or other respiratory symptoms), and positive RT-PCR test results. Supportive imaging criteria, such as chest X-ray or CT scan findings consistent with COVID-19 pneumonia, were also considered when available. Epidemiology, demography, clinical manifestations, laboratory examinations were extracted from the electronic medical records. The clinical data were collected from the first day of admission, unless otherwise noted in the current study.

There are several definitions for severity used by pediatricians [10–12]. For our study, we adopted a definition for severe illness that’s been applied to neonates and covers both severe and critical outcomes [11–13]. We classified severe illness as cases exhibiting clinical symptoms such as apnea, tachypnea, respiratory distress or respiratory failure, and a requirement for supplemental oxygen; and at least one of the following two categories: laboratory findings of a low white blood cell count (< 5 × 10^9 per L), a low lymphocyte count (< 1 × 10^9 per L), or an elevated C-reactive protein concentration (CRP) (> 5 mg/L); and/or radiological evidence of an abnormal chest x-ray. The current study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University (NFEC-2022-446).

Data collection

All data were collected according to the electronic medical records. The collected data from enrolled neonates included the general conditions of the neonates and their mothers, risk factors and complications during the mother’s pregnancy, comorbidities of neonates, clinical laboratory tests. All patients had laboratory data (including blood routine data for calculating the NLR and CRP value) upon the first day of admission.

For the subgroup with reexamination blood routine data, samples were collected during the recovery period, which was defined as when clinical symptoms (such as fever, cough, respiratory distress) completely disappeared and two consecutive nucleic acid tests (interval ≥ 24 h) were negative. This subgroup was selected to dynamically observe the changes in NLR from the acute infection phase to the recovery phase, and all neonates who completed the recovery phase follow-up and had complete blood routine data were included. For the subgroup with flow cytometry data, samples were collected within 24 h of admission (acute infection phase). This subgroup was selected based on the availability of residual blood samples after routine clinical tests and parental informed consent for immune function analysis. Due to the limited volume of neonatal blood samples and the high cost of flow cytometry, only 27 neonates (11 severe, 8 non-severe, 8 healthy controls) met the criteria for this analysis.

Flow cytometry was employed to analyze lymphocyte subsets. Blood samples were collected in EDTA - anticoagulated tubes. For sample storage, the collected blood samples were processed within 2 h of collection. If immediate processing was not possible, the samples were stored at 4 °C for a maximum of 4 h. The following fluorescent-conjugated monoclonal antibodies were used: CD3-PE-Cy7 (clone SK7, BD Biosciences), CD4-FITC (clone RPA-T4, BD Biosciences), CD8-APC (clone RPA-T8, BD Biosciences), CD19-PE (clone HIB19, BD Biosciences), CD45RA-APC-Cy7 (clone HI100, BD Biosciences), CD28-PerCP-Cy5.5 (clone CD28.2, BD Biosciences), and CD56-PE (clone B159, BD Biosciences). Briefly, 100 µL of whole blood was incubated with 5 µL of each antibody panel in the dark at room temperature for 20 min. After incubation, 2 mL of 1× red blood cell lysing solution (BD FACS Lysing Solution) was added and incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were then centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and resuspended in 500 µL of PBS. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a FACSAria III instrument (BD Bioscience) with BD FACSDiva software (version 8.0.1). A minimum of 10,000 lymphocyte events were acquired per sample. Data analysis was conducted using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1, Treestar) [14].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyzes were performed using SPSS version 25.0 and GraphPad Prism version 9.0. First, collinearity diagnostics were performed to calculate the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for all variables, and VIF values < 5 indicated no significant collinearity. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and comparisons between two groups were performed using the Student’s t-test; comparisons among three groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test.

A multivariate logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with severe COVID-19, with disease severity (severe vs. non-severe) as the dependent variable. Covariates included gestational age, birth weight, maternal gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), white blood cell (WBC) count, lymphocyte count, NLR, and CRP; these covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and previous literature [15]. Stepwise regression (entry criterion: p < 0.10, removal criterion: p > 0.15) was used to screen for significant predictors [16].

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic value of NLR for severe COVID-19, with severe infection as the outcome variable. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics of neonates

This study enrolled 143 neonates with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection after excluding 18 cases (12 due to incomplete white blood cell count data and 6 for parental refusal). Additionally, 59 healthy neonates without perinatal infections, delivery complications, or congenital anomalies were recruited as matched controls during the same observation period. They all had blood routine data, among which 36 neonates had blood routine data in early infection and recovery stages, and 8 neonates had data of cellular immunity (Table 1).

We first compared the clinical characteristics of neonates with severe and non-severe cases of COVID-19 infection, including basic clinical features, mother complications, pre-existing comorbidities, and clinical laboratory results (Table 1).

In terms of basic clinical features, our observations indicate that neonates in the severe group had a significantly lower gestational age (GA) (37.93 ± 3.09 vs. 38.82 ± 1.18, p < 0.0001) and birth weight (2.99± 0.77 vs. 3.14 ± 0.48, p = 0.004). We also found that infants born to mothers with gestational diabetes are more susceptible to developing severe cases of COVID-19 infection (Table 1).

Regarding the clinical laboratory results, neonates in the severe group had significantly higher levels of CRP (3.956± 11.25 vs. 1.050± 0.91, p = 0.000), NLR (2.567±1.685 vs. 1.117±1.557, p = 0.037), and lower lymphocytes (2.116±1.239 vs. 3.657±1.776, p = 0.031) (Table 1).

Screening for associated factors

A multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that two factors were independently associated factors of neonates with severe COVID-19, per the following results: NLR (p = 0.001, odds ratio [OR] 4.45, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.421–13.938), CRP (p = 0.015, odds ratio [OR] 1.79, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.119–2.866) (Table 2).

ROC curve analysis of NLR

To investigate the correlation between NLR and the severity of COVID-19 infection, ROC curve analysis was performed with severe infection as the outcome variable. The results demonstrated that the NLR values showed a decreasing trend with the reduction of disease severity: severe group (2.001 ± 1.111) > non-severe group (0.8136 ± 0.5951) > healthy control group (0.6299 ± 0.4438), with significant differences among the three groups (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1A). In addition, self-comparison of 36 neonates with both infection phase and recovery phase CBC data showed that the NLR value in the recovery phase (0.807 ± 0.9984) was significantly lower than that in the infection phase (2.218 ± 2.431, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1B).

Layered and ROC curve analysis of NLR. (A) NLR values in neonates with severe COVID-19, non-severe COVID-19 and healthy neonates; (B) NLR values during early and recovery phase of COVID-19 infection in neonates; (C) ROC curve of NLR in neonates

Based on the ROC curve analysis (Fig. 1C), the optimal cut-off value of NLR for predicting severe COVID-19 was 1.1452, with a sensitivity of 78.9%, a specificity of 81.4%, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.836 (95% CI 0.755–0.917; p < 0.001).

Preliminary research [17–19] indicates that the incidence of COVID-19 in newborns ranges from 1.5% to 15.8% across various regions and time periods. To calculate the predictive values of this cut-off, we first determined the incidence of severe COVID-19 in our study population: 54 severe cases among 143 infected neonates, with an incidence of 37.76%. Based on this incidence, the positive predictive value (PPV) was 68.2% (the probability that neonates with NLR ≥ 1.1452 actually have severe COVID-19) and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 89.5% (the probability that neonates with NLR < 1.1452 do not have severe COVID-19).

Immunological features of non-severe and severe groups

Since neonates with severe COVID-19 infection experienced a significant decline in lymphocytes, we further examined the changes in their subpopulations. Blood samples were collected from 11 neonates with severe COVID-19 infection, 8 neonates with non-severe COVID-19 infection and 8 healthy neonates for immune cell analyzes. Although our sample size is small, we still found that, except for natural killer (NK) cells, all other lymphocyte subsets exhibited a decline in absolute count as the disease severity worsened (Fig. 2A). Specifically, the absolute count of T lymphocyte (2130 ± 1404 vs. 3639 ± 1231, p = 0.038) and T helper (CD4+) cells (1398 ± 801 vs. 2614 ± 910, p = 0.013) in neonates with severe COVID-19 infection was significantly lower compared to the control group (Table 3; Fig. 2A), indicating that a predominant decrease in the CD4+ T lymphocyte subset is the main reason for the lymphocyte decline in neonates with severe COVID-19 infection. We then further analyzed the distribution of T cell subsets, including CD4+CD45RA, CD4+CD45RO, CD8+CD28+ and CD8+CD28−. Table 3 showed that percentage of CD4+CD45RA and CD8+CD28+ T cells were significantly lower in the COVID-19 infection group than in the control group, and percentage of CD8+CD28+ T cells were significantly lower in the severe group than in the non-severe group (Fig. 2B). However, percentage of B cells, CD4+CD45RO and CD8+CD28− showed no differences between severe, non-severe, and control cases. Surprisingly, the percentage of NK cells increased in neonates infected with COVID-19, which is different from the adult study [17].

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic continues to affect the population, including neonates who are particularly vulnerable. Given the distinctive attributes of neonates, there is currently no available vaccine specifically designed for them, making them more prone to severe symptoms and potential immune dysfunctions upon severe infection. Devin, J [18] found that neonates who suffered from severe COVID-19 required more respiratory support, medications, and had a longer length of stay in a hospital. Most of these neonates were premature and needed respiratory support for other complications besides COVID-19.

In this study, we identified several independent associated factors for term neonates in the late neonatal period with severe COVID-19. Considering that CRP and CBC are routinely measured in laboratory tests, this model was a user-friendly method to identify neonates at a high risk of severe COVID-19 [19].

Regarding the diagnostic utility of NLR, our study demonstrated that with an optimal cut-off value of 1.1452, NLR achieved a sensitivity of 78.9% and specificity of 81.4%, accompanied by an AUC of 0.836, indicating good discriminative ability for severe neonatal COVID-19. The predictive values of NLR are closely associated with the incidence of severe disease in the target population. In our study population, where the incidence of severe COVID-19 was 37.76%, the PPV was 68.2% and NPV was 89.5%. This high NPV is particularly clinically valuable, as it suggests that neonates with NLR below the cut-off have a low probability of developing severe disease, which can help clinicians reduce unnecessary intensive monitoring and intervention for this subgroup. However, the moderate PPV indicates that approximately one-third of neonates with NLR above the cut-off may not develop severe disease, highlighting the need for combination with other clinical indicators (e.g., clinical symptoms, CRP levels) for comprehensive assessment.

Notably, the PPV of NLR will vary significantly in settings with different incidences of severe neonatal COVID-19. For example, in regions or periods with a high incidence of severe disease (e.g., 50%), the PPV of NLR at the same cut-off is estimated to increase to approximately 76.5%, while in settings with a low incidence (e.g., 10%), the PPV may decrease to around 34.3%. This variation emphasizes that when applying NLR in different clinical contexts, clinicians should consider the local epidemiological characteristics of severe neonatal COVID-19 to avoid overinterpreting the results. In addition, the dynamic change of NLR from the acute infection phase to the recovery phase (significantly decreased to near healthy control levels) further supports its role as a potential monitoring indicator for disease progression and treatment response, which can help track the effectiveness of clinical interventions and adjust treatment strategies in a timely manner.

NLR plays an important role in determining the severity of the infection, increased NLR values are often associated with more severe COVID-19 infections and worse outcomes [20]− [21] At a recovery period following the COVID-19 infection, the follow-up CBC examination revealed that the NLR value also decreased and approached the level of the control group consisting of neonates without any infection. Neutrophils are the first responders in the immune system and their numbers rapidly increase in response to viral infection [22], whereas lymphocytes play an important role in adaptive immune response and a decrease in their numbers can lead to impaired viral clearance. Therefore, an imbalance in the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio during viral infections indicates an inadequate immune response to the virus, which can potentially lead to severe outcomes [23].We acknowledge the wide confidence interval of NLR in the multivariate analysis, which indeed indicates some uncertainty in the effect estimate. This could be attributed to several factors, including the relatively smaller sample size, the low event rate, or the high variability in NLR values among neonates. Despite the statistical significance observed in our study, we concur that these findings should be validated in larger studies with more precise estimates.

We also evaluated the changes in cellular immunity of neonates after COVID-19 infection by observing the changes in various lymphocyte subsets. Similar to adults [17], neonates have a decrease in lymphocytes, with a mainly decreased T lymphocyte count, after being infected with the COVID-19. However, adults with severe COVID-19 have a decrease in CD45RO T cells, which is different from what we found in our study of severely ill newborns, where CD4+CD45RA T and CD8+CD28+ T cells were the primary decrease, indicating that neonates infected with COVID-19 may exhibit a unique immune response that differs from adults.

Possible causes of these decreased peripheral T-cell levels include direct damage from SARS-CoV-2, inhibited T-cell expansion, and increased apoptosis triggered by autoimmune antibodies due to the virus [24, 25]. This study represents the first clinical evaluation of decreased CD4+CD45RA T, CD8+CD28+ T cells in neonates with COVID-19 infection, and the potential connection between decreased CD8+CD28+ T cells and severe cases.

Following antigenic stimulation in vitro, naive cells tend to acquire CD45RO and lose CD45RA expression [26]. It’s highly probable that this switch from CD45RA to CD45RO also occurs in newborns in vivo, after being stimulated by an infectious organism. Inflammatory processes such as infection activate “naive” CD45RA+ T lymphocytes, resulting in the upregulation of CD45RO and downregulation of CD45RA [26, 27]. These findings are consistent with the study conducted by Aygun et al.[31], which shows that neonates with culture-proven sepsis have lower amounts and percentages of CD45RA + T cells.

CD8+CD28+ T cells play a critical role in the immune response to viral infections [28].These T cells are able to recognize viral antigens presented on infected cells through their T cell receptors (TCRs), and subsequently become activated and proliferate to mount an effective immune response. However, some viral infections can lead to the progressive loss of CD28 expression on CD8+ T cells, impairing their function and contributing to disease progression. Therefore, maintaining a robust population of CD8+CD28+ T cells may be crucial in the control and clearance of COVID-19 infections in neonates [29].Given the relatively small sample sizes in each group, it is important to note that this finding indicates a potential area for further investigation, rather than serving as a definitive diagnostic or prognostic marker. Further validation through multi-center studies with larger sample sizes is required. Additionally, it is recommended that subsequent research focus on the dynamic relationship between cellular immune markers and the severity of the disease.

However, this study has limitations, such as a small sample size; larger multi-center studies are needed to confirm these findings. Additionally, immune cell analyzes were not performed in the early stages of the illness, limiting the dynamic monitoring of immune cells. The study mainly focused on cellular immunity and did not simultaneously test humoral immunity, so it does not reflect the overall immune status of neonates infected with COVID-19. Further research and clinical practice are needed to understand the immune responses in neonates with SARS-CoV-2 infection. CD4 + CD45RA T cells, CD8 + CD28+ T cells (cell subsets) and NLR may contribute to the prognosis of neonates with severe COVID-19. Studying the changes in lymphocyte subpopulations may provide new insights into understanding the pathogenesis of neonatal COVID-19.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the NLR serves as an associated factor for identifying severe SARS-CoV-2 infection in term neonates in the late neonatal period. An NLR threshold of 1.1452 offers significant diagnostic utility, with certain sensitivity and specificity. Furthermore, the pathogenesis of severe COVID-19 in this vulnerable population is associated with distinct alterations in lymphocyte subsets. Critically, neonates with severe infection exhibit significant reductions in both naïve CD4 + T cells (CD4 + CD45RA+) and cytotoxic effector CD8 + T cells expressing CD28 (CD8 + CD28+), with the decrease in CD8 + CD28+ T cells being particularly pronounced compared to non-severe cases. While our findings suggest NLR is associated with disease severity in neonatal COVID-19, the study design does not allow for evaluation of its accuracy as early predictive biomarkers. The current data primarily support its role as monitoring indicators during acute infection phases rather than preclinical predictors. The critical determinant of disease severity appears to be the collapse of specific T-cell subsets responsible for viral clearance and immune homeostasis. NLR should be combined with other clinical parameters through multimodal assessments to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Further validation through multi-center studies with larger sample sizes is required.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This retrospective study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University (approval number: NFEC-2022-446) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. For the retrospective analysis of existing clinical data, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the aforementioned ethics committee due to the non-interventional nature of the study and minimal risk to participants. However, for the prospective component involving flow cytometry analysis of lymphocyte subsets in neonates, written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians of all participants under the age of 16 prior to sample collection and analysis. Similarly, for the healthy control group, written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians for the collection and use of clinical data and biological samples. All consent procedures were conducted in accordance with the regulations outlined in the Chinese Ethical Review Methods for Biomedical Research Involving Humans (2016) and approved by the institutional ethics committee.
