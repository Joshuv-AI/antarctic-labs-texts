Population-level cardiovascular risk prediction models including biochemical predictors in 800 000 individuals
Authors: Bruno Batinica, Suneela Mehta, Jingyuan Liang, Rod Jackson, Katrina Poppe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsMost countries lack the clinical cohort data required to create cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk prediction models and rely on models developed elsewhere. However, individual-person linkage of health administrative databases enables the creation of local models.ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore whether the addition of routine biochemical tests could improve the performance of ‘administrative data-based’ models.Methods and resultsEstimated glomerular filtration rate, haemoglobin A1c, total cholesterol/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio, and triglyceride tests performed on one-third of New Zealand adults were identified and linked to national administrative health datasets (11 564 665 tests). Sex-specific Cox models estimating 5-year CVD in people aged 30–74 without prior CVD were developed, internally and temporally validated, and compared, including and excluding the four biochemical predictors. 805 817 individuals were included, of whom 64% had all four examined test results available. 1.8% of women and 3.4% of men experienced a CVD event during 4.7 years mean follow-up. All biochemical predictors except triglyceride level in men were statistically significant independent predictors of CVD risk. The addition of these predictors improved global model performance metrics over models without biochemical predictors and identified 34% of men and 28% of women in the cohort who accounted for 75% of all CVD events, respectively.ConclusionBiochemical predictors improved the performance of administrative data-based CVD risk prediction models, illustrating the untapped potential of widely available databases. Similar models could be developed in many countries and healthcare organizations if health administrative databases were linked. These models could be remotely applied across populations to inform CVD prevention policy and practices.Condensed abstractNovel CVD risk prediction models have recently been developed in New Zealand and Denmark from predictors available in health administrative data, but their performance is limited by their parsimony. Biochemical predictors are now linkable to New Zealand administrative datasets and we show that they improve the predictive ability of previous ‘administrative data-based’ models. These new models were remotely deployed across a large New Zealand region, identifying 27% of individuals who accounted for 74% of subsequent CVD events. Similar models could be created in many countries and healthcare organizations to aid population health planning efforts, investigate equity gaps, and pre-screen populations who experience difficulties accessing traditional health services.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Risk prediction, Biochemical markers, Health administrative data

Introduction

A key challenge in preventing cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the timely identification and management of high-risk individuals.1 CVD risk prediction models (also known as risk prediction equations) derived from clinical cohorts are an established tool for quantifying an individual’s risk of developing CVD and guiding clinical decisions regarding CVD prevention.2 Unfortunately, few contemporary large-scale clinical cohort studies are available for deriving robust CVD risk prediction models. Only a few countries, notably the UK and New Zealand, have access to large primary care databases that can be linked at the level of the individual to national hospitalization and mortality data, making it possible to affordably recruit the representative cohorts required to derive robust country-specific models.3,4 Elsewhere, large cohorts have been created by pooling multiple research study populations to derive generic models, which require recalibration when transported to specific local populations.5 The recalibration process is typically done indirectly due to the paucity of relevant data in the new populations where generic models are to be applied, and the performance of these models is sub-optimal in terms of discrimination and calibration.4

Alternative CVD risk prediction models, derived entirely from the limited set of predictors available in administrative health datasets, have been developed and deployed in New Zealand and Denmark.6–8 These ‘administrative data-based’ models can predict risk across entire populations to help inform population-based CVD prevention efforts and can be easily retrained on more up-to-date administrative data from the same local population to combat data and concept drift. Administrative data-based models have generally shown good calibration and discrimination but performed less well in certain high-risk subpopulations, potentially due to the limited number of predictors at their disposal.7

As healthcare embraces the ‘big data’ revolution, the pool of predictors routinely available across large populations will grow.9 A natural choice of new predictors to add to administrative data-based models is laboratory test results. These biochemical markers are routinely collected, stored in large centralized databases, and commonly used in models derived from clinical cohorts.10 TestSafe is a data-sharing programme between four health districts in northern New Zealand, allowing primary and secondary care clinicians to access their patients’ laboratory results remotely.11 This database contains all results collected in hospitals and community laboratories in a catchment zone encompassing approximately 1.9 million of the country’s 5.2 million people.

We sought to examine the potential of using this laboratory database to improve previously developed administrative data-based models. This study aimed to develop and validate Cox models incorporating laboratory results to predict 5-year CVD risk among individuals without prior CVD and compare the performance of these new models to current models that do not incorporate laboratory results.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study is part of a programme of research originally approved by the Northern Region Ethics Committee in 2003 (AKY/03/12/314), with subsequent approval by the National Multi-Region Ethics Committee in 2007 (MEC07/19/EXP) and with annual re-approval since as part of a vascular research programme (2024 PR 18564). Individual patient consent was not required as all data were de-identified at source.

Study design and participants

The study cohort was created by de-identified individual-level linkage of eight national health administrative datasets using encrypted national health index (NHI) numbers assigned to every person using the New Zealand health system.12 Inclusion criteria for the study cohort were (i) New Zealand residents, (ii) aged 30–74 years, (iii) alive at the study index date of 1 January 2013, (iv) living within the catchment zone of the four Northern Region health districts (Auckland, Counties Manukau, Northland, and Waitematā), (v) enrolled with or having at least one recorded contact with publicly funded health services in 2012, and (vi) without missing demographic data.

To balance predictor availability and temporal proximity to the study index date relevant laboratory tests performed on the study cohort between 1 January 2010 and 1 January 2015 were extracted from the TestSafe database for analysis. While results after the index date were included, a 3-month masking period was used prior to any CVD event or death to ensure that no laboratory predictors measured after or around the time of disease were included as predictors. Individuals were subsequently excluded if they were not eligible for CVD risk assessment according to national primary CVD prevention guidelines.13 Exclusion criteria included (i) a history of prior CVD or heart failure (based on a hospital admission in which an ICD10-AM code for CVD or heart failure was recorded or if the individual had previously been prescribed an antianginal medication or loop diuretic three or more times) and (ii) extreme biochemical results that warrant treatment regardless of CVD risk (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, total cholesterol/high-density lipoprotein ratio > 8, triglycerides > 11 mmol/L).13 Additional information is provided in the Supplementary Methods. The specific ICD-10 codes and medications used to classify prior CVD and heart failure are described in Supplementary material online, Table S1–S5.7

Outcome

Time in days to first fatal or non-fatal CVD event was identified using national hospitalization and mortality datasets over the 5 years following the study index date.13 Additional information is provided in the Supplementary Methods, and the specific ICD-10 codes used to define a CVD event are provided in Supplementary material online, Table S6.7 Individuals who died from other causes or who ceased to have recorded health contacts were censored at their death or last recorded contact date, respectively.

Predictors

Predictors were pre-specified based on prior evidence regarding CVD risk factors, their availability in the dataset, and to facilitate comparison of our models to published administrative data-based models.6,7 Predictors included in both the new and comparison sex-specific models were (i) age, (ii) ethnicity (as per the New Zealand Ministry of Health Ethnicity Data Protocols),14 (iii) level of deprivation, (iv) diabetes status, (v) previous hospitalization for atrial fibrillation, and (vi) baseline dispensing of blood pressure-lowering, lipid-lowering, or anti-thrombotic medications. More information about these predictors is available in the Supplementary Methods.7 New predictors were (i) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), (ii) haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), (iii) total cholesterol/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TC:HDL), and (iv) triglycerides.

Missing data

Biochemical predictor information was extracted from the result collected closest to the study index date within the stated cut-off period. Missing biochemical results were imputed using the MissForest package, which utilizes random forest imputation.15 This approach was selected due to the combination of continuous and categorical variables in our dataset, and the need for a single imputation methodology that could be used to impute values when performing inference with the model. As a sensitivity analysis, complete case analysis and multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE) was conducted (see Supplementary material online, Methods). Biochemical predictor availability and patterns of missingness are also reported.

Statistical analysis

Two sets of sex-specific, multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression models were created. The first set of models used the exact same predictors as models previously developed by our team but was retrained in this cohort to be used as a comparison.6,7 The second set of models utilized all the predictors from the comparison models plus the laboratory result variables.

The proportional hazard assumption was assessed using the global test of scaled Schoenfeld residuals and visual inspection of Schoenfeld residual plots.16 The linearity of association between the new continuous variables and outcome was assessed with visual inspection of Martingale residual plots17 and fractional polynomial analysis.18

First-order interactions were partly pre-specified to facilitate model comparison; interactions involving new predictors were assessed using fractional polynomials and included if they met a strict prespecified P-value threshold of <0.001 and were clinically plausible.

Age was centred at the mean value and deprivation at quintile 3. eGFR (in mL/min/1.73m2) was found to have a non-linear association with CVD risk so was categorized (30–45 = G3b, 45–60 = G3a, >60 = normal) according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KD-IGO) chronic kidney disease (CKD) classification to preserve model assumptions while maintaining clinical interpretability.19 HbA1c, TC:HDL, and triglycerides were centred at healthy average values (40 mmol/mol, 3.5, and 1.5 mmol/L, respectively). HbA1c was scaled by 10 mmol/mol.

Absolute 5-year risk estimates were derived from the models by estimating the baseline 5-year survival of the training cohort. This baseline survival was then raised to the power of the exponentiated linear predictor of a particular individual to estimate their 5-year CVD risk.

To assess whether the additional laboratory variables were important predictors, over and above the original administrative data-based predictors, the linear predictor (prognostic index) from the baseline administrative model was calculated and included as a single covariate in subsequent Cox models alongside the new laboratory predictors. This method summarized the baseline risk information into a single term, preventing the re-estimation of individual baseline coefficients while testing the independent contribution of the biochemical predictors.20

Model performance

Predictions from the two sets of sex-specific models were generated using 10-fold cross-validation. These predictions were quantitatively evaluated using Royston and Sauerbrei’s R2 and D statistics21,22 and Harrell’s C statistic.23 The R2 value estimates the proportion of total variance explained by the model, while the D and C statistics measure model discrimination. Reclassification of individuals between models was assessed at risk thresholds of 5% and 15% to align with current national management guidelines.13 The models were qualitatively assessed through calibration (comparing Kaplan-Meier estimates of event rates to predicted event rates in risk deciles) and discrimination (displaying Kaplan–Meier estimates of event rates in risk deciles) plots.7 Qualitative assessment of calibration and discrimination plots was also performed in relevant New Zealand sub-populations, specifically those stratified by (i) age, (ii) ethnicity, (iii) quintiles of deprivation, (iv) health district, and (v) baseline medication dispensing.

Temporal validation

A temporal validation of model performance was undertaken by creating a new cohort with the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as the study cohort but using a study index date of 1 January 2016. All predictors and inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied identically, accounting for this 3-year shift, except for the laboratory result inclusion window, which was shifted to include only results prior to the index date (1 January 2011 to 31 December 2015). Baseline and new model performance were examined in the complete case cohort, and model discriminative ability and calibration were compared.

Analyses were conducted in R 4.1 and Stata software version 14.1. This study followed the TRIPOD + AI recommendations for reporting new prediction models.24

Results

Participants

The study cohort included 805 817 individuals (Figure 1), of whom 515 921 (64%) had results for all laboratory tests of interest to this study (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1–SS2). The mean age of the whole cohort was 48.1 years, and approximately half (53%) of the cohort were women (Table 1). Europeans were the largest ethnic group, followed by Pacific Peoples, Māori, Indian and Chinese. The prevalence of diabetes was approximately 7%. 19.5% of women and 21.8% of men were on at least one of the three examined pharmaceutical agents (lipid-lowering, blood pressure-lowering, anti-thrombotic) at baseline, while 3.0% of women and 4.5% of men were on all three. In women and men, respectively, the mean eGFR level was 93.4 and 91.1 mL/min/1.73m2, the mean HbA1c was 40.4 and 41.0 mmol/mol, TC:HDL was 3.6 and 4.2, and triglycerides were 1.4 and 1.7 mmol/L. 1.8% of the women and 3.4% of men experienced a CVD event during a mean follow-up time of 4.7 years. Multivariable models

The adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for predictors in the new (with biochemical predictors) and comparison (without biochemical predictors) sex-specific multivariable models are shown in Figure 2 and Supplementary material online, Table S11. The HRs for most predictors included in the comparison models were not significantly affected by the addition of the laboratory test results, except for diabetes, Māori ethnicity, and Pacific Peoples ethnicity, which were attenuated.

HbA1c was a statistically and potentially clinically significant predictor of CVD risk with a HR of 1.14 (per 10 mmol/mol increase, approximately one SD in the population) in women and men. The HR for TC:HDL was 1.27 in women and 1.25 in men (per one unit increase, approximately one SD in the population). Compared to the reference eGFR category (>60 mL/min/1.73m2), the two lower eGFR categories were also statistically significant predictors, with HRs ranging between 1.1 and 1.3. Triglycerides had an HR of 1.09 in women and 1.02 in men and were statistically significant, however when examining model coefficients in the complete case cohort as a sensitivity analysis, Triglycerides ceased to be a statistically significant predictor in men (see Supplementary material online, Table S12). A further difference observed in the complete cases analysis was that the lowest eGFR category (30–45 mL/min/1.73m2) had higher HRs (1.4–1.5). All hazard ratios were similar, but slightly attenuated when performing MICE (see Supplementary material online, Table S13). An interaction term between age and TC:HDL was identified by fractional polynomial analysis but was not statistically significant in the final assessed model.

Model performance

The new and comparison models had excellent calibration in both sexes (Figure 3). The new models had modestly improved discrimination, illustrated by the greater horizontal spread of points in the calibration plots and the higher proportion of total events captured in the highest predicted risk deciles in the discrimination plots. The magnitude of improvement in discrimination was higher in subpopulations stratified by age and baseline medication use. Visual inspection of all sub-population plots revealed equivalent or improved calibration and discrimination with the new models. Calibration plots for sub-populations stratified by age are presented in Figure 4. All other sub-population plots are presented in Supplementary material online, Figures S3-S10.

The new models reclassified 1.4% and 2.9% of all individuals with CVD events into more appropriate clinical risk groups across current recommended management thresholds (15% and 5%), in women and men, respectively (Table 4). Women who were reclassified as high risk by the new model (<15% risk in the comparison model and >15% risk in the new model) were 55% more likely to have a CVD event than those reclassified as not high risk (>15% risk in the comparison model and <15% risk in the new model), and women reclassified as low risk (>5% risk in the comparison model and <5% risk in the new model) were 10% less likely to have a CVD event compared to those reclassified as not high risk (<5% risk in the comparison model and >5% risk in the new model) (see Supplementary material online, Table S17–S19). At a 3.4% risk threshold, the new model identified 34% of men who account for 75% of CVD events, and at a 2.0% threshold, it identified 28% of women who account for 75% of CVD events (see Supplementary material online, Figure S11).

Temporal validation

Supplementary material online, Table S20 presents the baseline characteristics of the 318 067 individuals in the temporal validation cohort, the mean age of the cohort was 56.7 and 56.1 in the 51.6% who were female and 48.4% who were male. Over a median follow-up of 4.9, 4.0% of women and 6.6% of men experience a CVD event. In temporal validation, both the new and the comparison model remained well calibrated, and a similar modest but statistically significant increase in Harrell’s C and Royston’s R2 was observed with the inclusion of biochemical predictors (see Supplementary material online, Table S21 and Supplementary material online, Figure S12).

Discussion

This study developed novel sex-specific Cox models to predict the 5-year risk of fatal or non-fatal CVD events in individuals without prior CVD using routinely available national and regional health datasets in New Zealand. Models utilizing biochemical results and administrative data outperformed those using only administrative data in measures of explained variance, calibration, and discrimination. All examined biochemical predictors, except for triglyceride levels in men, had statistically significant and potentially clinically significant HRs.

A key strength of the study was its size, with 805 817 participants, it captured almost every eligible adult in a large regional population (approximately one-third of the national population). Model performance was validated internally (i) using 10-fold cross-validation, (ii) in policy-relevant sub-populations, and (iii) in a temporal validation cohort to assess robustness.7,25 The new models demonstrated good calibration and discrimination in all assessed subpopulations. Using only widely available predictors, these models identified over 75% of CVD events in less than 33% of the population. They could be applied to every individual in a population to identify at-risk sub-populations, quantify inequities in CVD treatment, and assess the effectiveness of planned future public health interventions.26

Predictors and outcomes were prespecified to facilitate unbiased confidence intervals for model coefficients.27 Of the newly examined biochemical predictors, TC:HDL was the strongest, based on the magnitude of the HR (approximately per SD), further supporting the efficacy of TC:HDL as a predictor of CVD.28 Controversy surrounds the accuracy of ‘normal’ eGFR values and the efficacy of eGFR as a predictor of CVD in populations not enriched with CKD.29 Despite not being as statistically significant as HbA1c and the TC:HDL, the relatively large HRs (∼1.1–1.3) of categories of eGFR indicate that these predictors can significantly alter an individual’s estimated 5-year CVD risk. Although it is implicated in the causal pathway for atherosclerotic disease,30 serum triglyceride level was the weakest of the examined predictors and varied the most between men and women. Triglyceride levels exhibit marked daytime variation, largely due to fasting status, potentially limiting the predictive ability of the triglyceride level predictor.31

While the biochemical predictors impacted individual risk, as reflected by the magnitude of their HRs, the effect of these additional predictors on traditional metrics of model performance was statistically significant but modest in magnitude. However, these standard model performance metrics are known to be insensitive to the potential importance of additional predictors.32 Exploring novel metrics that more effectively compare models is an important methodological challenge for the future.

Limitations

A possible weakness of the current study relates to the handling of missing data, with imputed results biasing the models’ coefficients for each of the examined laboratory test results. However, random forest imputation is widely used and well-studied, and MICE and complete case cohort sensitivity analyses were performed, showing minimal differences in predictor coefficient estimates. In our cohort, 64% of individuals had all four test results available. HbA1c was the most frequently missing test; however, recent recommendations for HbA1c testing as part of a complete CVD risk assessment in New Zealand will help address this problem in future models developed from more recent data.13 Even as our dataset coverage increases, individuals who have trouble accessing health care would be less likely to undergo laboratory tests, potentially biasing a model that includes laboratory results as predictors and limiting its ability to accurately measure current management inequities. Missing results will also always be an issue due to some individuals not requiring, and thus not undergoing, testing.

One other possible source of bias in the model is that by relying on hospitalization records, individuals who experience non-fatal cardiovascular events but do not present to hospital will not appear to have had an outcome. This will artificially lower the risk of groups who struggle to access healthcare services, and be a source of algorithmic bias in our model.33 Additionally, this study utilized standard Cox models, which do not formally account for the competing risk of non-CVD death. While this approach aligns with current clinical guidelines, it may lead to a slight overestimation of risk in older individuals where non-CVD mortality is more prevalent.13

Only a single result for each laboratory test was used for each individual, increasing the variance of the data and, by extension, the model. As these administrative data-based models are not primarily designed for ‘point-of-care’ use, more computationally intensive approaches that include multiple tests per person could be utilized in the future. Novel deep-learning approaches, which can handle higher-dimensional predictor information, are natural extensions to this current work but require careful implementation to prevent miscalibration and overfitting.6

Clinical implications and future directions

International external validation of these models is not relevant as they are designed to be New Zealand-specific, and if another country has equivalent data, they could derive their own models. Validation in the whole of New Zealand and temporal validation will soon be possible as more laboratory data becomes available to our team.

‘Administrative data-based’ models will gradually become more powerful as the amount and quality of predictor information routinely collected and stored increases. Given the difficulty of collecting the up-to-date cohort information required for traditional clinical models, it seems likely that hybrid models (utilizing clinical results which are widely collected) will eventually see use in clinical practice. However, extensive work in data-linkage, model creation, and validation is required to bring this to fruition.

With consideration of the potential biases discussed above, these improved models could be used by policymakers to identify and target interventions to vulnerable high-risk sub-populations and to assess the likely benefit of planned health policies.26 The data required to create similar models (health-administrative and laboratory test results) are routinely collected in many countries and sub-national healthcare organizations, but are currently underutilized in risk prediction due to a lack of linking identifiers, bureaucratic challenges, and concerns over patient privacy.34 The substantial potential health gains from making these administrative and laboratory data more accessible must be balanced against the potential theoretical harms of linking individual-level health data.

Conclusion

Routinely collected laboratory test results enable improved 5-year CVD risk prediction in a large proportion of the general population of New Zealand. The models presented here are readily available for strategic planning and decision-making regarding population-level CVD prevention efforts, and similar models could be replicated in many settings.
