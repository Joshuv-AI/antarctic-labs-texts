Investigation of Isthmin-1 level in patients with polycystic ovary syndrome
Authors: Burcu Özata, Ahmet Burak Gürpinar, Büşra Şahin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine-metabolic disorder among women of reproductive age, associated with hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology. Insulin resistance and lipid metabolism abnormalities are common in PCOS, increasing the risk of metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Isthmin-1 (ISM1) is a novel adipokine that regulates glucose and lipid metabolism, but its role in PCOS remains unknown. This study aimed to investigate whether serum ISM1 levels are altered in women with PCOS compared to healthy controls and whether ISM1 is associated with key metabolic (e.g., insulin resistance, homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance [HOMA-IR]) and hormonal parameters (e.g., follicle-stimulating hormone [FSH], luteinizing hormone [LH], estradiol [E2], testosterone, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate [DHEA-S]). Emerging evidence highlights the role of novel adipokines in the metabolic dysregulation associated with PCOS. Among these, ISM1 has gained attention due to its insulin-mimetic effects and its role in glucose and lipid metabolism. Given the central role of insulin resistance and obesity in PCOS pathogenesis, investigating whether ISM1 is altered in women with PCOS could provide new insights into its potential role in disease mechanisms. Furthermore, exploring its association with insulin resistance markers may help determine whether ISM1 can serve as a supportive metabolic biomarker. Therefore, this study aimed to compare serum ISM1 levels between women with PCOS and healthy controls, and to examine the relationship between ISM1 levels and key metabolic and hormonal parameters.MethodsThis case-control study included 60 women aged 18–45 years: 30 women with PCOS diagnosed using the Rotterdam 2003 criteria and 30 age-matched healthy controls. Anthropometric measurements (body mass index [BMI], waist circumference), metabolic parameters (fasting/postprandial glucose, fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c], lipid profile), and hormonal markers (FSH, LH, estradiol, testosterone, DHEA-S, prolactin, thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH], free thyroxine [FT4]) were assessed. Serum ISM1 concentrations were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Statistical analyses included independent t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, Spearman correlation, and ROC analysis.ResultsMedian ISM1 concentration was significantly higher in the PCOS group (1.60 ng/mL) compared to the control group (1.34 ng/mL, p = 0.03). ISM1 levels positively correlated with fasting insulin (r = 0.26, p = 0.04) and HOMA-IR (r = 0.29, p = 0.03). No significant correlation was found between ISM1 and reproductive hormones or lipid profile components. ROC analysis demonstrated that ISM1 had moderate diagnostic performance in identifying PCOS, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.658 (95% CI: 0.52–0.77, p = 0.026). At an optimal cut-off value of 1.85 ng/mL, the sensitivity was 43.3% and specificity was 90.0%.ConclusionElevated serum ISM1 levels and their correlation with insulin resistance suggest that ISM1 may contribute to the metabolic pathophysiology of PCOS. Although its diagnostic performance is limited due to low sensitivity, ISM1 may serve as a supportive biomarker in identifying metabolic risk in women with PCOS. Further large-scale and mechanistic studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify the functional role of ISM1.Keywords: Polycystic ovary syndrome, Isthmin-1, Insulin resistance, Metabolic dysfunction, Adipokine

Background

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder [1]. It is characterized by menstrual cycle irregularities, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and the presence of polycystic ovarian morphology [2]. It affects 6–20% of women of reproductive age [3]. The exact etiology and pathology of PCOS is not comprehensively known. Evidence suggests the role of different external and internal factors, including insulin resistance, hyperandrogenism, environmental factors, genetics and epigenetics [4]. The increased frequency of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in women with PCOS results in a high ratio of luteinizing hormone (LH) to follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). It is not known whether hypothalamic dysfunction is the primary cause of PCOS or whether it is due to an abnormal steroid feedback mechanism, but in both conditions LH levels are elevated. In 60% of patients, LH/FSH exceeds 2/1 [5]. According to the Rotterdam 2003 criteria for the diagnosis of polycystic ovary syndrome, 2 of the following criteria must be present; clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, chronic oligo/anovulation, and the presence of polycystic ovaries on ultrasonography. Congenital adrenal hyperplasia, hyperprolactinemia, and androgen-secreting tumors may also lead to these findings and these should be excluded. Therefore, PCOS is actually a diagnosis of exclusion [6]. Women with PCOS show a higher degree of insulin resistance compared to unaffected women. Insulin resistance is a reduced glucose uptake response to the amount of insulin given. PCOS is associated with impaired glucose tolerance and an increased risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [7]. Even after correction of body mass index (BMI), women with PCOS are more likely to develop T2DM later in life and have β cell dysfunction independent of obesity. Women with PCOS have increased levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides, total cholesterol/high-density lipoprotein and decreased levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL). Women with PCOS are more prone to be obese. There is a pattern of android or central obesity, thick waist circumference and thick subcapsular skin folds, which are also markers for insulin resistance [8, 9].

Isthmin 1 (ISM1) was first identified in 2002 as a protein structure secreted in Xenopus embryos and involved in early brain development. Subsequent studies have shown the presence of ISM1 in different cells and organs in mice, including the brain, lung, vascular structures, skin and immune system. ISM1 belongs to the isthmin gene family and is expressed on chromosome 20 in humans. Studies have shown that ISM1 is an adipokine that promotes glucose uptake and improves glucose tolerance and hepatic steatosis. In mouse models, ISM1 has been shown to suppress lipid synthesis in hepatocytes and increase glucose uptake in adipocytes, thus ameliorating hyperglycemia while reducing lipid accumulation in the liver. ISM1 ablation results in impaired glucose tolerance, decreased adipose tissue glucose uptake and decreased insulin sensitivity. Therapeutic dosing of recombinant ISM1 ameliorated diabetes in diet-induced obese mice and hepatic steatosis in a diet-induced fatty liver mouse model [10, 11]. Recent literature highlights the pleiotropic role of ISM1 in metabolic regulation, including insulin-mimetic properties and adipokine activity [12, 13].

Given the central role of insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction in the pathogenesis of PCOS, and in light of the emerging insulin-mimetic and lipid-regulating properties of ISM1, we hypothesized that serum ISM1 levels may differ between women with and without PCOS [14]. Given the central role of insulin resistance in the pathogenesis of PCOS and the emerging insulin-mimetic properties of ISM1, there is a need to clarify whether ISM1 levels are altered in women with PCOS and whether ISM1 is associated with key metabolic and hormonal parameters. However, the potential role of ISM1 in PCOS remains largely unexplored, and its diagnostic value has not yet been systematically evaluated. Therefore, this study was designed to compare serum ISM1 concentrations between women with PCOS and healthy controls and to investigate its associations with insulin resistance, reproductive hormones, and lipid profile parameters.

Methods

The study was conducted between September 2023 and February 2024 and included a total of 60 women aged 18–45 years. Among them, 30 women diagnosed with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) formed the study group, while 30 women who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for PCOS comprised the control group. All participants applied to the Gynecology and Obstetrics outpatient clinic of Ordu University Training and Research Hospital, voluntarily agreed to participate, and signed informed consent forms. Demographic and obstetric data (age, height, weight, BMI, gravida, parity, number of live births) were obtained through structured interviews and a review of medical records at the time of enrollment. To ensure hormonal consistency, fasting blood samples were collected on the third day of the menstrual cycle. For participants with irregular menstrual cycles, the third day after spontaneous or progesterone-induced withdrawal bleeding was used as a reference point.

PCOS was diagnosed based on the Rotterdam 2003 criteria, which require at least two of the following: clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, chronic oligo/anovulation, or polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasonography. Women with chronic diseases, pregnancy, or active smoking status were excluded from the study.

To ensure consistency in metabolic parameters, participants were instructed to avoid caffeine intake and strenuous physical activity before sample collection. Fasting blood samples were collected on the 3rd day of menstruation, including for participants with irregular menstrual cycles.

Although no age-based subgroups were defined, the PCOS and control groups were age-matched. The mean age of the control group was 24.3 years, and the mean age of the PCOS group was 24.1 years. BMI matching between the PCOS and control groups was intentionally not performed. This decision was based on the fact that elevated BMI and central adiposity are intrinsic features of the PCOS phenotype and contribute to its pathophysiology. Matching for BMI could have limited the generalizability of the findings by selecting an atypical subgroup of PCOS patients with normal weight. Instead, BMI and waist circumference were measured and reported separately, and their potential influence on ISM1 levels was considered in the interpretation of results. Future studies with larger sample sizes may include statistical adjustments or stratified analyses to further clarify the independent role of ISM1 in relation to adiposity.

Approximately 8 mL of venous blood was collected from each participant into two anticoagulant-free biochemistry tubes after an overnight fast of at least 8 h. The samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Part of the serum was used for routine laboratory analysis, and the remaining serum was transferred to Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent measurement of ISM1 concentrations.

Routine biochemical analyses were performed to assess metabolic and hormonal parameters associated with PCOS. Metabolic assessments included fasting blood glucose, 2-hour postprandial blood glucose, fasting insulin, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), which was calculated using the formula: [fasting insulin (µU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)]/405. The lipid profile included total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels. Hormonal assessments comprised FSH, LH, estradiol (E2), total testosterone, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S), prolactin (PRL), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and free thyroxine (FT4). All biochemical and hormonal analyses were performed at the Ordu University Biochemistry Laboratory using an automated immunoassay analyzer (cobas 6000 c 501 and e 601, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). HbA1c was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using the Arkray Adams HA-8180T system (Arkray Inc., Japan).

Serum Isthmin-1 (ISM1) levels were measured using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Human ISM1 ELISA Kit, BT Lab, Jiaxing Korain Biotech, Zhejiang, China; Lot No: E7643Hu), with a sensitivity of 0.17 ng/mL and a measurement range of 0.38–24 ng/mL. All analyses were performed in duplicate according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and average values were used for statistical evaluation.

The Ethics Committee of Ordu University Training and Research Hospital approved this study (Ethics Committee Decision No: 2023/232, Date: 15.9.2023). The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analysis

Power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.2 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany) to determine the minimum required sample size. Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.80), an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired statistical power of 80% (1–β = 0.80), it was calculated that a minimum of 30 participants per group would be necessary to detect significant differences between the PCOS and control groups. Based on this, a total of 60 participants (30 per group) were included in the study.

Statistical analyses were performed with MedCalc (version 20.009; Ostend, Belgium) statistical package program. Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine whether the data were normally distributed. Descriptive statistical methods (mean, standard deviation, median, 25th and 75th percentile values, frequency, ratio) were used to evaluate the study data. Independent t-test was used to compare two groups with parametric distribution. Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare two groups with nonparametric distribution. The direction and level of the relationship between numerical variables were determined by correlation analysis. Pearson correlation was used for groups with parametric distribution and Spearman correlation was used for groups with nonparametric distribution. To evaluate the diagnostic performance of ISM1 in distinguishing individuals with PCOS from healthy controls, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted. In this analysis, anthropometric parameters such as BMI and waist circumference—potential confounders affecting ISM1 levels—were considered. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. While the ROC curve analysis revealed a high specificity (90.0%) at the optimal ISM1 cut-off value of 1.85 ng/mL, its sensitivity was relatively low (43.3%), indicating that ISM1 may be more suitable as a complementary biomarker rather than a standalone diagnostic tool in the context of PCOS. In the pairwise comparison tables, data that fit the normal distribution are shown as mean and standard deviation (SD) and data that do not fit the normal distribution are shown as median (25.p - 75.p). Statistical significance was accepted as p < 0.05 for all values.

Discussion

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common endocrine and metabolic disorders in women. It is characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology [15]. PCOS is a systemic syndrome that affects not only the reproductive system but also has profound effects on glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and lipid profile [16]. In recent years, adipokines, particularly the newly identified Isthmin-1 (ISM1), have emerged as potential biomarkers in these metabolic disturbances.

ISM1 was first described by Jiang et al. as a molecule that increases glucose uptake, improves hepatic steatosis, and suppresses lipid accumulation [16]. Their study showed that ISM1 acts independently of insulin receptors and suggested that it could be a therapeutic target in metabolic disorders. In contrast, Wang J. et al. reported that ISM1 levels were decreased in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), suggesting a protective role of ISM1 against disease development [17]. However, another study by Wang C. et al. in the same year identified a positive association between ISM1 and the severity of diabetic nephropathy, suggesting that ISM1 may reflect disease progression rather than protection [18]. These conflicting findings indicate that ISM1 may have variable effects depending on the stage and context of metabolic disease.

In a 2023 study, Liao et al. found that ISM1 concentrations were significantly higher in individuals with T2DM compared to healthy controls [19]. Similarly, in pregnant women with gestational diabetes, ISM1 levels were also found to be elevated [20]. In both studies, ISM1 showed strong associations with BMI, waist circumference, HOMA-IR, oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results, and HbA1c levels. A 2024 study further reported elevated ISM1 levels in patients with fatty liver disease associated with metabolic syndrome, suggesting that ISM1 could reflect worsening metabolic status [21].

However, a 2024 study conducted in Kuwait found significantly lower ISM1 levels in obese individuals and a negative association between ISM1 and T2DM [22]. Such opposing findings may be due to differences in sample size, ethnicity, study design, or inadequate control for confounding variables. It is also possible that differences in ISM1 regulation arise from population-specific factors, including genetic backgrounds and lifestyle variables such as diet and physical activity, which were not controlled in our study.

In our study, serum ISM1 levels were significantly higher in women with PCOS compared to healthy controls, as expected. Additionally, ISM1 levels showed a positive correlation with fasting insulin and HOMA-IR, supporting the potential involvement of ISM1 in insulin resistance and metabolic dysregulation. Although ISM1 showed a lower diagnostic performance compared to postprandial blood glucose, its strong association with insulin resistance may position it as a potential supportive marker, rather than a replacement, in the diagnostic assessment of PCOS. The high specificity observed in the ROC analysis (90% at a cut-off of 1.85 ng/mL) further suggests that ISM1 may assist in diagnosis when interpreted alongside conventional metabolic markers. However, differences in BMI and waist circumference were not statistically adjusted, representing a limitation. Although ISM1 showed correlation with insulin resistance, its causal role remains speculative without mechanistic validation. The ROC curve’s AUC value of 0.658 and low sensitivity (43.3%) limit its diagnostic performance.

On the other hand, no significant correlation was found between ISM1 levels and lipid profile components (LDL, HDL, triglycerides, and total cholesterol) or reproductive hormones such as LH, FSH, estradiol, total testosterone, DHEA-S, prolactin, TSH, and FT4. This may indicate that ISM1 is more closely related to insulin resistance mechanisms than to dyslipidemia or hormonal alterations in PCOS. These findings suggest that ISM1 may function more specifically as a marker of metabolic impairment rather than reflecting broader endocrine dysfunction.

This study has some limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; it remains unclear whether increased ISM1 levels contribute to the pathogenesis of PCOS or reflect a compensatory response to metabolic stress. The sample size was relatively small, and the single-center design limits generalizability. In addition, potential confounding variables such as physical activity, dietary patterns, inflammatory markers, and fat distribution were not assessed.

Most notably, anthropometric variables such as BMI and waist circumference—both of which were significantly higher in the PCOS group—were not statistically adjusted. Since ISM1 is an adipokine potentially influenced by adiposity, the lack of standardization for these parameters represents a critical limitation and may have confounded the observed associations. Although our sample size restricted the feasibility of multivariable modeling, future studies with larger cohorts should include appropriate statistical adjustments (e.g., ANCOVA or multivariate regression) to control for adiposity-related confounding and to more accurately assess the independent relationship between ISM1 and PCOS.

Furthermore, other potential confounding factors such as physical activity level, dietary intake, inflammatory markers and regional fat distribution (e.g., visceral vs. subcutaneous adiposity) were not assessed or statistically adjusted in this study. While participants with chronic illnesses, smoking, and pregnancy were excluded to reduce bias, the lack of comprehensive control for internal confounders limits the ability to isolate the specific role of ISM1 in PCOS pathophysiology. Future research should incorporate standardized assessments of lifestyle and metabolic variables, and apply multivariable statistical models to minimize internal bias and improve causal inference.

Based on all these findings, future prospective, multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to confirm the role of ISM1 in PCOS and related metabolic diseases. Furthermore, experimental studies are required to clarify the direct effects of ISM1 on insulin signaling, steroidogenesis, ovarian function, and lipid metabolism at the molecular level.

Conclusion

Our study provides new insights into the role of ISM1 in the pathophysiology of PCOS. Based on our study findings and the available literature, we can hypothesize that high ISM1 levels in participants are a risk factor for PCOS. This study suggests that combinations of ISM1 levels with other metabolic parameters, as well as PCOS, provide a better understanding of the pathogenesis of diseases associated with metabolic and lipid disorders. However, detailed studies with larger sample sizes are needed to determine the role of ISM1 in PCOS. Given its modest sensitivity and lack of causal evidence, ISM1 should be interpreted as a complementary marker pending further validation.
