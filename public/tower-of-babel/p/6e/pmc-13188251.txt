Low-dose radiotherapy remodels the tumor immune microenvironment via the cGAS–STING pathway: mechanisms, challenges, and combination therapy strategies
Authors: Yongze He, Xianhu Zeng, Qianyi Liu, Linsen Zhou, Ying Tang, Shuotong Liu, Xiaobin Wang, Shiyan Shen, Jialin Ji, Zhen Liu, Jiangping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT) has emerged as a promising immunomodulatory strategy by activating the cyclic GMP–AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway, thereby reprogramming the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). LDRT induces DNA damage and cytosolic dsDNA accumulation, leading to cGAS-STING activation and subsequent production of type I interferon and proinflammatory cytokines. Consequently, LDRT promotes dendritic cell maturation, enhances CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and cytotoxicity, repolarizes macrophages toward an anti-tumor, immunostimulatory phenotype, and suppresses myeloid-derived suppressor cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs). However, sustained cGAS–STING activation may paradoxically induce immunosuppression through PD-L1 upregulation, T cell exhaustion, and enrichment of inhibitory cells. Combining LDRT with immune checkpoint inhibitors, STING agonists, chemotherapy, or CAR-T cell therapy synergistically amplifies antitumor immunity by overcoming TIME suppression and fostering long-term immune memory. Challenges such as radiotherapy heterogeneity, dose optimization, and STING pathway mutations require precise strategies including image-guided radiotherapy, nanocarrier-based delivery, and biomarker-driven patient stratification. This review highlights the dual role of LDRT-mediated cGAS–STING signaling in TIME remodeling and provides a foundation for developing novel combinatorial immunotherapies.

Introduction

The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, and response to therapy. The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is composed of tumor cells, immune cells (such as T cell subsets, natural killer cells, and macrophages), stromal cells (fibroblasts, endothelial cells), and the extracellular matrix (collagen, glycosaminoglycans). These components communicate through soluble factors (cytokines, chemokines, growth factors) that enable intercellular signaling crosstalk. Despite some success, conventional treatments like surgery, chemotherapy, and high-dose radiotherapy often face challenges including tumor recurrence, metastasis, and significant damage to healthy tissues.While advances in understanding the TIME have established immunotherapy as a promising strategy, its broad application is hindered by limited response rates in many patients [1–3].

Radiotherapy, a cornerstone of cancer therapy, directly eliminates tumor cells and modulates the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in a dose-dependent manner. High-dose radiation primarily induces DNA damage and tumor antigen release but may concurrently impair immune cells within the TIME. In contrast, low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT), generally defined as a total dose below 10 Gy with per-fraction doses under 2 Gy, achieves immune reprogramming without causing substantial direct tumor killing. LDRT promotes immunogenic alterations in tumor cells, activates immune effector cells, and reshapes immunosuppressive components of the TIME, thereby stimulating systemic anti-tumor immunity. Growing evidence supports the role of LDRT as an immune adjuvant capable of synergizing with immunotherapy to enhance treatment outcomes [4–6].

A key mechanism by which low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT) reprograms the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. This essential innate immune cascade detects cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), which is released from tumor cells following radiation. The DNA sensor cGAS binds to this dsDNA and catalyzes the synthesis of the second messenger cGAMP. cGAMP then activates STING, triggering downstream signaling that induces the production of type I interferons (IFN-I) and other proinflammatory cytokines. This response promotes dendritic cell (DC) maturation and antigen presentation, ultimately enhancing T cell-mediated antitumor immunity [7, 8]. Studies have revealed that LDRT can effectively activate the cGAS–STING pathway in both tumor cells and immune cells, thereby modulating the tumor immune microenvironment and enhancing systemic antitumor immunity.​

Therefore, elucidating how LDRT remodels the tumor immune microenvironment via the cGAS-STING pathway is crucial for developing optimized therapeutic strategies. This review synthesizes recent advances in this area, providing insights and a rationale for novel combinatorial approaches in clinical oncology.

Signal transduction of the cGAS–STING pathway

The cGAS-STING pathway constitutes the central signaling axis for cytosolic DNA-induced immune responses [9, 10]. The sensor cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS) recognizes aberrant double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) from sources such as infection, genomic instability, or therapy-induced DNA leakage. This recognition induces conformational changes that activate cGAS, enabling it to synthesize the second messenger cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) from ATP and GTP. cGAMP then binds to and activates the endoplasmic reticulum-resident protein stimulator of interferon genes (STING), initiating its downstream signaling cascade [11–15].

It is noteworthy that ENPP1 (Ecto-nucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1) functions as the dominant hydrolase for extracellular 2′3’_cGAMP. It suppresses STING activation by specifically cleaving the phosphodiester bond of 2′3’_cGAMP, degrading it into AMP and GMP. Critically, certain tumor cells can secrete ENPP1 via exosomes, thereby degrading extracellular cGAMP within the tumor microenvironment to facilitate immune evasion.

Under resting conditions, STING resides on the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, anchored by its interaction with the calcium sensor stromal interaction molecule 1 (STIM1). Its cytosolic domain forms a V-shaped pocket that faces the cytoplasm [16], which accommodates cGAMP [17, 18]. Upon cGAMP binding, triggered by cGAS-dsDNA sensing, STING undergoes a major conformational shift: its C-terminal domain rotates inward, transforming the pocket from a V-shape to a U-shape. This is accompanied by the formation of an anti-parallel β-sheet "lid" over the pocket and a 180°rotation of the ligand-binding domain relative to the transmembrane region [19]. These rearrangements promote C-terminal oligomerization, transitioning STING from an open, inactive state to a closed, active conformation [20].

Following activation, STING undergoes higher-order oligomerization to form a tetramer and is trafficked from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus via the coat protein complex II (COPII) vesicular system and ADP-ribosylation factor (ARF) GTPases [19, 21–24]. At the Golgi, STING is palmitoylated at cysteine residues Cys88 and Cys91 by DHHC-family palmitoyltransferases. This lipid modification enhances the membrane anchoring stability of the STING tetramer and induces conformational changes that expose its intracellular signaling platform, thereby enabling the recruitment of downstream kinases and the initiation of immune signaling [25].

Following palmitoylation, the conformationally activated STING recruits downstream regulators, including TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), via its exposed signaling platform. This recruitment simultaneously initiates two major signaling axes: the interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3)–type I interferon (IFN-I) transcriptional pathway and the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)–driven proinflammatory cytokine expression pathway [26, 27]. The IRF3–Type I Interferon (IFN-I) Signaling Axis: Upon platform assembly, TBK1 binds to palmitoylated STING and autophosphorylates at Ser172, forming an active STING-TBK1 complex [26, 28]. This complex recruits monomeric interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), enabling TBK1 to phosphorylate a specific serine cluster (e.g., Ser385, Ser396) within IRF3's C-terminal region. Phosphorylation relieves IRF3's autoinhibitory conformation, triggering its homodimerization [26, 29]. The dimerized IRF3 exposes its nuclear localization signal and is transported into the nucleus via the Importin-α/β heterodimer. Within the nucleus, the IRF3 dimer binds to interferon-stimulated response elements (ISREs) in the promoters of type I interferon genes (IFN-α/β) and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs). By recruiting the histone acetyltransferase coactivators CREB-binding protein (CBP)/p300, IRF3 facilitates chromatin remodeling and the assembly of RNA polymerase II, thereby initiating the transcription of IFN-I and ISGs to drive antiviral and antitumor immune responses [30].

The NF-κB Signaling Axis: Following activation, the C-terminal tail of STING recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) [31], which catalyzes K63-linked polyubiquitination on STING. This ubiquitin chain serves as a scaffold for recruiting and activating the IκB kinase (IKK) complex (comprising IKKα, IKKβ, and the regulatory subunit NEMO) [32, 33]. The activated IKK complex phosphorylates the inhibitory protein IκBα at Ser32/Ser36, marking it for K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This releases the canonical NF-κB heterodimer (primarily p65/p50), enabling its nuclear translocation [34, 35]. In the nucleus, NF-κB binds to κB motifs in promoter regions, driving transcription of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), chemokines (e.g., CXCL9, CXCL10), and other immune mediators. Notably, a positive feedback loop exists: NF-κB upregulates microtubule-stabilizing proteins that delay STING degradation [36]. In summary, the cGAS-STING pathway functions as a central signaling hub that translates cytosolic DNA anomalies into immune responses. This process is initiated by cGAS-mediated detection of aberrant DNA, followed by cGAMP signal relay, culminating in STING activation—via conformational and post-translational changes—and the subsequent induction of both IRF3-IFN-I and NF-κB signaling axes,thereby mounting a comprehensive host defense program.

Low-dose radiation therapy: definition, evolution, and clinical positioning

Low-dose radiation therapy (LDRT) is defined by the delivery of radiation at very low doses per fraction (typically 0.5–1.0 Gy), with a total dose significantly lower than that used in conventional regimens. This distinguishes it fundamentally from conventional radiotherapy, as its therapeutic rationale is not direct cytotoxicity but radiation hormesis. In this paradigm, low-dose exposure induces adaptive biological responses, primarily anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, marking a shift from using radiation as a cytotoxic agent to a biological response modifier [37].

LDRT occupies a distinct niche within the modern radiotherapy spectrum, standing in stark contrast to modalities such as SBRT, particle therapy, and FLASH radiotherapy [38–41]. While these advanced techniques aim to maximize tumor ablation through physical dose concentration—via hypofractionation, the Bragg peak, or ultra-high dose rates—their goal remains direct cytotoxic eradication. In contrast, LDRT operates on a fundamentally different principle: it utilizes low-doseradiation not as a destructive force, but as a biological signal to modulate immune and inflammatory responses. The conceptual and clinical evolution of LDRT has progressed through distinct phases, closely aligned with advancements in radiobiological science. Its origins are empirical, dating to early 20th-century observations of symptomatic improvement in inflammatory conditions such as arthritis and pneumonitis following minimal radiation exposure [42]. A significant theoretical milestone was reached in the 1980 s with T.D. Luckey's systematic articulation of the "radiation hormesis" hypothesis, which provided a framework for understanding stimulatory or beneficial effects from low-level stressors [43]. The 1990 s marked a shift toward experimental validation, as work by researchers like Takai Y demonstrated immunomodulatory effects of very low-dose radiation in preclinical models, advancing the field from phenomenology to mechanism [44].

In the twenty-first century, LDRT achieved modern clinical validation. Its efficacy for refractory benign conditions like osteoarthritis was established through prospective studies and incorporated into guidelines such as those from the German Society for Radiation Oncology (DEGRO) [45]. Most recently, exploratory trials during the COVID-19 pandemic provided proof-of-concept for its systemic anti-inflammatory effects in mitigating cytokine storms, opening a new avenue in critical care [46–48]. Mechanisms of low-dose radiotherapy in modulating the TIME via the cGAS–STING pathway

Low-dose radiotherapy induces dsDNA release: a triggering signal for cGAS–STING Activation

LDRT activates the cGAS–STING pathway through a dual damage cascade. It induces nuclear DNA double-strand breaks, releasing genomic dsDNA into the cytosol, while concurrently triggering mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) opening and the collapse of membrane potential, leading to the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) [49].

These mislocalized nuclear and mitochondrial DNA together serve as endogenous ligands for cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) [50, 51]. In the cytosol, cGAS recognizes this mislocalized DNA, triggering its activation and the synthesis of the second messenger cGAMP. Additionally, cGAMP can diffuse directly to adjacent cells through gap junctions [52, 53] or be exported extracellularly via transporters such as ABCC1 [54, 55], followed by uptake into neighboring cells through importers like SLC19A1 [56]. Furthermore, cGAMP can be packaged into virus particles and delivered to new host cells during infection [57, 58]. The antimicrobial peptide LL-37 can also bind to cGAMP and facilitate its delivery to target cells [59]. It is noteworthy that the stability of extracellular cGAMP is tightly regulated: classical hydrolases such as ENPP1 and ENPP3 restrict its diffusion range through enzymatic degradation [60, 61], whereas SMPDL3A—potentially induced by LXR-mediated lipid metabolism—may also participate in this process, collectively preventing excessive immune activation [62]. This molecular cascade achieves core activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, establishing a key foundation for initiating local antitumor immunity and coordinating subsequent immunoregulatory networks.

Coordinated modulation of the Cytokine network

The biological significance of the cGAS–STING pathway extends not only to intracellular signal transduction, but more importantly, to its capacity to modulate the cytokine network, thereby bridging signal sensing and immune response within the tumor microenvironment. The following section will detail how this pathway orchestrates antitumor immunity by shaping cytokine dynamics.

In antitumor immunity, the cGAS–STING pathway converts cytosolic dsDNA signals into an interconnected cytokine regulatory network—comprising key mediators such as IFN-β, CXCL9/10, TNF-α, and IL-6—via the IRF3 and NF-κB signaling axes, thereby orchestrating multi-layered immune responses (Fig. 1).

Phosphorylated IRF3 translocates to the nucleus and drives the transcription and secretion of IFN-β. This cytokine then acts in a paracrine manner, binding to the IFNAR receptor on tumor and immune cells. This engagement activates the JAK-STAT (Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription) pathway—a central signaling cascade that transduces cytokine signals into the nucleus, leading to the upregulation of a broad suite of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) [63]. Notably, TRAIL and Fas ligand, encoded by ISGs, induce apoptosis indirectly by binding to death receptors and recruiting adaptors like FADD to form the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC), which activates Caspase-8 [64–67]. Activated Caspase-8 then cleaves Caspase-3 to initiate apoptosis and processes Bid into tBid. tBid translocates to mitochondria, triggering cytochrome c release and amplifying the apoptotic signal [68–71]. ISG-encoded p21 and p27 bind to and inhibit cyclin–CDK complexes, preventing phosphorylation of the retinoblastoma protein (Rb), a key tumor suppressor and cell-cycle regulator. Unphosphorylated Rb thus remains bound to E2F transcription factors, arresting the G1/S transition and suppressing tumor cell proliferation. [72–74]. Furthermore, IFN-β also upregulates CXCL10 (C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10). This chemokine recruits CXCR3 (C-X-C chemokine receptor type 3)—expressing immune cells like NK and CD8+ T cells into the TIME [75, 76], and concurrently inhibits VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor)—driven angiogenesis. Thereby, it disrupts tumor neovascularization, induces metabolic stress, and suppresses tumor proliferation.

Following ISG expression, both tumor cells and dendritic cells (DCs) upregulate surface MHC-I molecules (which present tumor antigenic peptides) and the costimulatory molecule CD80. Antigen-presenting DCs activate CD8+ T cells through dual signals: TCR engagement by MHC-I—peptide complexes (signal 1) and CD80-CD28 binding (signal 2), driving their differentiation into cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [77–79]. CTLs eliminate target cells via the perforin–granzyme pathway, where perforin pores facilitate granzyme B-mediated apoptosis and directly disrupt membrane integrity [80–82]. Furthermore, tumor cell-derived type I interferon drives the activation of a CD11b + conventional dendritic cell subset (ISG + DCs), which acquire and present intact tumor-derived peptide-MHC class I complexes-a process known as MHC class I dressing—alongside upregulated CD80 expression, thereby enhancing antigen presentation and establishing an immunostimulatory amplification loop within the "tumor cell-DC-T cell" axis [83].

Synergizing with the IRF3 axis, NF-κB activation promotes TNF-α secretion [84]. TNF-α binding to TNFR1 triggers receptor trimerization and the recruitment of adaptor proteins (e.g., TRADD, RIPK1) [85–87], forming a complex that—when pro-survival signals are suppressed—activates Caspase-8. Activated Caspase-8 then initiates apoptosis through dual routes: by cleaving Bid to tBid, leading to mitochondrial cytochrome c release, and by directly activating Caspase-3, mirroring the apoptotic cascade induced by TRAIL/Fas ligands.

In parallel, NF-κB activation also drives IL-6 secretion. Upon binding to the IL-6R/gp130 receptor complex on immune cells, IL-6 activates the JAK-STAT3 pathway, leading to STAT3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation [88, 89]. In Treg precursors, nuclear STAT3 promotes Foxp3expression, driving their differentiation. These Tregs suppress immunity through multiple mechanisms: secreting IL-10 and TGF-β, engaging CTLA-4 with CD80/CD86 on antigen-presenting cells to block co-stimulation, and promoting macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype. Collectively, this reinforces a localized immunosuppressive microenvironment [90, 91].

Conversely, in memory T-cell precursors, STAT3 binds to the Bcl-2 promoter, upregulating its expression. Elevated Bcl-2 inhibits mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, preventing cytochrome c release and conferring apoptosis resistance to central memory T cells (TCM) [92, 93]. Furthermore, IL-6 cooperatively sustains STAT1 signaling induced by IFN-γ through the STAT3 pathway, collectively maintaining high T-bet expression in central memory T cells (TCM). This preserves their stem-like properties and ensures rapid antigen recognition upon tumor recurrence, enabling swift differentiation into effector T cells that secrete IFN-γ and granzyme B to reinitiate anti-tumor immune responses.

The IRF3 and NF-κB pathways do not operate in isolation but function through crosstalk and complementarity, forming an integrated regulatory network. Phosphorylated IRF3 synergizes with NF-κB to induce CXCL9 and CXCL10 expression, which recruit CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, and DCs into the tumor microenvironment via the CXCR3 receptor [94, 95]. In the tumor microenvironment, recruited NK cells recognize stress-induced ligands—MICA/B (MHC class I chain-related proteins A/B) and ULBP (UL16-binding proteins)—via the activating receptor NKG2D (natural killer group 2D). Simultaneous NKp30 (natural cytotoxicity receptor 3)engagement with B7-H6 (B7 homolog 6) delivers co-stimulatory signals, triggering IFN-γ (interferon-gamma) secretion that amplifies ISG expression and TNF-α (tumor necrosis factor-alpha) release that polarizes macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype [96, 97]. Through cGAS-STING activation, LDRT converts cytosolic DNA signals into a coordinated cytokine network that recruits and activates key immune effectors—including T cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells—within the tumor microenvironment. This process dynamically balances pro-inflammatory antitumor responses with regulatory mechanisms to prevent excessive immune activation, thereby shifting LDRT’s role from direct cytotoxicity to systemic immunomodulation.

Remodeling the tumor microenvironment: low-dose radiotherapy reverses immune desertification and stimulates immunogenic phenotypes

TIME is classified as either "cold" or "hot" based on immune cell infiltration and function [98]. Cold tumors are fundamentally characterized by a lack of immune cell infiltration, manifesting in two subtypes: the "immune-desert" type, where low tumor antigen expression and weak chemotactic signals prevent immune cell recognition and recruitment into the tumor; and the "immune-excluded" type, in which immune cells are present around the tumor but cannot effectively infiltrate the tumor parenchyma due to stromal barriers, among other factors. In contrast, hot tumors belong to an "immune-inflamed" microenvironment, marked by robust infiltration of functionally active cytotoxic T cells, NK cells, and others [99, 100]. These cells sustain a persistent anti-tumor immune response through the release of effector molecules such as interferon-gamma and TNF-α, rendering such tumors generally more responsive to immune checkpoint inhibitors and similar therapies [101].

The "cold" and "hot" tumor phenotypes are not fixed but are dynamically evolving entities. Therefore, a central therapeutic aim is to remodel cold, immunosuppressive tumors into hot, immune-responsive ones. In this context, activating the cGAS-STING pathway represents a key mechanistic strategy [102]. Acting as a key intracellular DNA sensor, this pathway responds to damage-induced cytosolic DNA, such as that generated by LDRT [49–51]. This activation drives strong expression of type I interferons and chemokines like CXCL10, which effectively counteract immunosuppression in the TIME by recruiting and activating dendritic cells and T cells, thereby systematically reshaping the microenvironment from a "cold" to a "hot" state [103] (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Tumor immune microenvironment phenotypes. a Classification of tumors based on immune infiltration patterns: immune-inflamed, immune-excluded, and immune-desert. Central to this shift is the orchestration of various TIME cells, including MDSCs, B cells, TAMs, NK cells, DCs, and T cells.

MDSCs

MDSCs represent a highly heterogeneous population of immature immune cells that are actively recruited into TIME [104]. As documented in, they suppress T-cell function through secretion of nitric oxide (NO) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [105], while reference demonstrates their role in promoting tumor survival, angiogenesis, metastasis, and pre-metastatic niche formation [106]. On one hand, this pathway suppresses Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3), a key oncogenic transcription factor, thereby reducing tumor cell secretion of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF). Consequently, it inhibits both the differentiation and infiltration of MDSCs. Consequently, it inhibits both differentiation and infiltration of MDSCs [107]. On the other hand, STING agonists (e.g., cGAMP, c-di-AMP) directly target MDSCs to suppress their production of NO and ROS, thereby impairing their immunosuppressive function against T cells. As evidenced by in vitro experiments, cGAMP suppresses NO and ROS production in MDSCs isolated from spleens of B16 melanoma-bearing mice, consequently diminishing MDSC-mediated T-cell immunosuppression [105]. Moreover, combinatorial strategies can potentiate regulatory control over MDSCs. Studies demonstrate that the STAT3 inhibitor HJC0152 and STING agonist c-di-AMP act synergistically to significantly reduce MDSC infiltration while concurrently enhancing T-cell accumulation in the TIME [108].

We acknowledge that TIME harbors heterogeneous myeloid populations with distinct phenotypic and functional states. While we use the term "MDSCs" to denote immunosuppressive immature myeloid cells, we recognize their plasticity and overlap with other lineages such as tumor-associated macrophages and neutrophils. Throughout this work, MDSC identification adheres to established phenotypic criteria (lineage markers and functional assays) per field consensus guidelines, with subsets specified where appropriate to reflect biological diversity.

B cells

B cells, as key mediators of humoral immunity, support LDRT-driven cGAS-STING signaling through antigen presentation, antibody secretion, and cytokine modulation [109], while cGAS-STING activation in turn potentiates B cell effector functions. Pathway-generated cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs) directly activate B cells in a STING-dependent manner, leading to caspase-dependent cell death. In mouse models of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), the STING agonist 3′3’-cGAMP inhibits tumor growth and induces apoptosis of malignant B cells through this mechanism [110]. Furthermore, activated B cells upregulate CD86 to enhance antigen presentation and T cell cross-priming, a process that synergizes with LDRT to supply potent antigenic signals for adaptive immunity [111].

On the other hand, adaptive resistance can occur via cGAS-STING hyperactivation, which drives pathological regulatory B cells (Bregs) expansion; these Bregs suppress NK cell function through IL-35, reinforcing immunosuppression and impairing the efficacy of LDRT-combination therapies [112]. To counter this, targeting the STING-Breg-NK network holds promise. In pancreatic cancer models, combining LDRT with cGAMP and an IL-35-neutralizing antibody synergistically blocks immunosuppression. This preserves beneficial STING-driven B cell functions while inhibiting Breg-mediated escape, offering a novel paradigm to improve combination therapy [113].

NK cell

LDRT fine-tunes the chemokine network via the cGAS-STING pathway to enhance immune cell tumor infiltration. It critically augments both the recruitment and cytotoxicity of natural killer (NK) cells. Unlike antigen-specific T cells, NK cells possess the unique capacity to directly recognize and eliminate target cells without prior antigen exposure or major histocompatibility complex (MHC) restriction. This intrinsic property establishes NK cells as pivotal targets in tumor immunotherapy, and LDRT precisely amplifies this advantage through cGAS-STING pathway activation [114–116].

Pathway stimulation induces tumor cells to secrete key molecules like CXCL9 and IL-2. This aligns with findings in hepatocellular carcinoma, where eEF2K silencing or inhibition activates STING, driving robust chemokine secretion that enhances NK cell chemotaxis via the CXCR3 axis [117]. Concurrently, LDRT also activates the cGAS-STING pathway intrinsically within NK cells, driving IFN-γ secretion that in turn amplifies their cytotoxic function [118]. LDRT induces the surface expression of stress signals like NKG2D ligands on tumor cells, thereby enabling more precise recognition and elimination by NK cells [96]. As experimentally demonstrated by the Chen team using the Ncr1iCre STINGf/f mouse model (featuring NK cell-specific STING knockout), ablation of STING signaling results in a marked increase in metastatic burden in melanoma lung metastases. This finding provides compelling evidence for the core role of intrinsic STING signaling within NK cells in mediating their anti-tumor functionality [119].

Furthermore, LDRT may potentially inhibit mitophagy, leading to the leakage of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)–a mechanism analogous to that observed with rocaglamide in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). This mtDNA release subsequently amplifies the targeted recruitment of NK cells in a STING-dependent manner [120].

TAMs

M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) constitutes a core immunosuppressive feature within the TIME [121]. Conversely, LDRT can precisely activate the cGAS-STING pathway to drive the repolarization of TAMs towards an anti-tumor M1 phenotype which depends on the coordinated interplay of multiple molecular mechanisms.

Functioning as vital professional scavengers, macrophages phagocytose pathogens, process antigens, and present them via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules [122, 123]. Upon migration into the TIME, they differentiate into TAMs, playing a pivotal role in both initiating and regulating anti-tumor immune responses [124]. Their phenotypic plasticity is tightly regulated by the cGAS-STING pathway.

LDRT promotes M₁ polarization by activating the cGAS-STING pathway [125], which drives the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, TNF-α) and upregulates inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and interleukin-12 (IL-12). iNOS enhances the phagocytic capacity of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [126], while IL-12 recruits and activates T cells and NK cells to form a cytotoxic network [127]. This is evidenced in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), where cGAS-STING activation repolarizes TAMs to M1, enhances T cell infiltration, and suppresses tumor progression [128].

Conversely, STING activation directly suppresses the M₂ phenotype by transcriptionally downregulating its signature marker CD206 (mannose receptor), while also reducing the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β [129, 130]. The reduction in IL-10 alleviates its suppression of T cell activation, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immunity [131]. Concurrently, decreased TGF-β impedes the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process and tumor angiogenesis [132]. Critically, cGAS-STING activation drives the repolarization of M2-type TAMs toward an anti-tumor phenotype, which in turn alleviates tumor burden [133]. For instance, in murine models of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), researchers successfully repolarized bone marrow-derived M2 macrophages towards the M1 phenotype using the STING agonists DMXAA and 2′3’-cGAMP. This reprogramming notably induced tumor site-specific vascular disruption and reduced tumor burden in the mouse models [134].

Notably, in glioblastoma (GBM) models, M₁-polarized TAMs activate T cells through efficient antigen presentation. Concurrently, secreted IFN-γ enhances the cytotoxicity of natural killer (NK) cells. This establishes a coordinated "TAMs-NK-T cells" immune axis that synergistically enhances systemic anti-tumor immune responses [135]. In models of colorectal cancer liver metastasis, STING activation further upregulates the expression of immune-responsive gene 1 (Irg1). This promotes the nuclear translocation of transcription factor EB (TFEB). We recognize that the traditional M1/M2 dichotomy does not fully capture the heterogeneity and plasticity of macrophages in the tumor microenvironment.In this review, we employ these terms as a conceptual framework—"M1-like" referring to anti-tumor, immunostimulatory functions, and "M2-like" to pro-tumor, immunosuppressive functions—while acknowledging that these represent functional extremes along a continuum of activation states. Future studies using higher-resolution technologies will further elucidate macrophage phenotypic remodeling under LDRT and cGAS-STING signaling.

DCs

LDRT activates the cGAS-STING pathway in dendritic cells, enhancing antigen presentation and T cell priming. This is amplified by induced type I interferon, which broadens immune activation and strengthens effector T cell responses [137, 138]. As central mediators bridging innate and adaptive immunity, DCs represent highly specialized tissue surveillance cells within the natural anti-tumor immune response. Equipped with an array of receptors to detect damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) [139–141], DCs enable LDRT to selectively amplify this recognition capacity, thereby enhancing immune priming.

Mechanistically, this process is initiated when LDRT causes tumor DNA damage and dsDNA release. The extracellular dsDNA is then internalized by DCs, leading to cGAS-STING activation and IFN-I production [142]. In conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC₁s), this process induces high expression of MHC-I [143, 144] and costimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86), significantly enhancing the cross-presentation of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs). Similarly to the STING agonist PolySTING, which specifically activates the STING–IFN-I axis in cDC₁s, LDRT enhances their tumor infiltration and antigen-presenting capacity, thereby promoting CD8⁺ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity [145].

Concurrently, IFN-I promotes IL-12 secretion by DCs via the JAK-STAT pathway, driving CD4⁺ T cell differentiation toward the Th1 phenotype. This provides essential help for CD8⁺ T cell activation [145]. Meanwhile, under LDRT modulation, conventional type 2 dendritic cells (cDC₂s) synergize with Tim-3 immune checkpoint blockade to upregulate MHC-II and costimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86), further promoting Th1 differentiation of CD4⁺ T cells and amplifying the anti-tumor immune response [146, 147]. Similarly, in murine tumor models, combining the STING agonist ADU-S100 with anti-Tim-3 therapy enhances cDC₂ maturation and differentiation, which promotes T cell activation and tumor infiltration, ultimately improving survival [146].​

Furthermore, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) exhibit a relatively limited capacity to prime naive T cells. However, LDRT engages the cGAS-STING pathway within pDCs, driving robust production of type I interferons (IFN-I) [148]. Relevant studies indicate that the cGAS-STING pathway is central to DNA sensing and IFN-I induction in pDCs. T cell

LDRT can enhance CD8+T cell cytotoxicity through the cGAS-STING pathway and coordinate the modulation of T cell subsets to improve anti-tumor efficacy [151]. STING activation enhances CD8⁺ T cell anti-tumor immunity through IFN-β-mediated mechanisms: it upregulates cytotoxic molecules like granzyme B (Gzmb) and perforin (Prf1); suppresses the pro-survival Akt pathway to drive effector T cell (Teff) differentiation; and induces the transcription factor TCF1 to maintain a stem-like precursor (Tpex) state for long-term memory [152]. The activation of the cGAS-STING pathway can drive the differentiation of CD8+T cells into a distinct "exhausted-like effector" (CD8+Tex) population. Although expressing inhibitory receptors like PD-1, these cells maintain potent cytotoxic function. Notably, their combination with PD-1 blockade significantly enhances anti-tumor immunity [153].

CD4+T cells are critical in antitumor immunity, with evidence showing cGAS-STING signaling plays an indispensable role in regulating their development and effector functions [154, 155]. Previous studies have established that low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT) enhances CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and antigen recognitionthrough STING activation. However, dynamic immune monitoring by Martin's team revealed time-dependent remodeling of the tumor microenvironment post-radiation: Early infiltration of B cells and CD4⁺ T cells precedes CD8⁺ T cell recruitment. This spatiotemporal dynamic reshapes the topological architecture of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Specifically, B cells facilitate cross-presentation of neoantigens to activate CD4⁺ T cells, which subsequently secrete interleukin-2 (IL-2) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ). Consequently, these cytokines establish a pro-inflammatory milieu that provides the molecular foundation for deep CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and clonal expansion, ultimately enabling coordinated control of both primary and metastatic lesions [156].

Further studies revealed that CD4+T cells engage in bidirectional crosstalk with cDC1s via the STING-IFN-β axis. The IFN-β produced by T cells upregulates MHC-I and costimulatory molecules (e.g., CD80/86) on cDC1s, establishing a feedforward activation loop that enhances their cross-presentation capacity. This process enables effective priming of CD8+T cells, thereby bridging innate and adaptive immunity [157]. In translational clinical research, studies by He and colleagues have demonstrated the pivotal role of CD4⁺ T cells in enhancing the efficacy of in situ vaccines. As primary responders in the immune cascade, CD4⁺ T cells induce robust Th1 polarization upon STING-based vaccine administration. Critically, this Th1 polarization suppresses regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation, thereby reversing the immunosuppressive landscape of tumors [158].

γδ T cells provide early interferon-γ (IFN-γ) production [159], and their effector functions are stringently regulated by the cGAS–STING signaling pathway. LDRT enhances antitumor immunity by activating the cGAS-STING pathway in γδ T cells, coordinating their dual roles in tumor progression. In early-stage tumors, LDRT promotes tumor-derived cGAMP to activate the TBK1-Eomes axis, driving IFN-γ production and cytotoxic function. Conversely, in advanced tumors, LDRT counteracts TGF-β-induced epigenetic silencing (e.g., STING promoter methylation) to prevent functional exhaustion, thereby sustaining their long-term immunomemory and surveillance capacity. [160].

Notably, research by Serrano's team further demonstrates that combining LDRT with STING ligands precisely modulates the activation threshold of human γδ T cells and enhances tumor cell lysis efficiency through coordinated regulation of the STING pathway. This provides compelling experimental evidence for optimizing in vivo γδ T cell immunotherapy to amplify both cytotoxic activity and immunomemory effects [161]. Moreover, LDRT harnesses post-translational modifications (PTMs) to not only directly regulate γδ T cell effector functions, but also orchestrate cytokine networks of innate immune cells for maintaining immune-specific homeostasis. Remodeling tumor vasculature

Tumor pathological angiogenesis, marked by hyperpermeability and disorganization, creates a physical barrier that impedes immune cell infiltration [163]. STING-driven IFN-I release downregulates vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGFA) to inhibit aberrant angiogenesis while enhancing pericyte coverage, thereby improving vascular stability. This normalized vasculature reduces the diffusion of immunosuppressive factors such as TGF-β, ultimately fostering a more immunologically active TIME [164].

At the stromal level, functional heterogeneity of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) is finely modulated by cGAS-STING pathway: it exerts bidirectional regulation on the differentiation of pro-tumoral (pCAFs) and anti-tumoral (rCAFs) subpopulations, where enrichment of rCAFscrucially alleviates stromal barrier impedance against immune cells [165]. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) models, cGAS-STING dual-positive cohorts exhibited elevated expression of rCAF-specific markers, concomitant with sustained CD8⁺ T cell infiltration from stroma into tumor parenchyma. Transwell coculture assays further demonstrated that LDRT-mediated STING activation reverses CAF-imposed blockade of immune cell trafficking, significantly enhancing tumor cell killing efficiency [166].

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), as pivotal stromal components, engage in cGAS-STING-regulated crosstalk with tumor cells. Studies reveal that MSCs mediate bidirectional IFN-β shuttling via the cGAS-cGAMP-STING axis, which upregulates HLA-I expression on tumor cells to evade NK cell-mediated killing, thereby promoting metastatic dissemination [167]. But LDRT-activated STING signaling may counteract this immunosuppressive axis to restore NK cell recognition sensitivity against tumor cells. Concurrently, engineered MSCs can deliver STING agonist-loaded extracellular vesicles (STINGa-EVs), which amplify cGAS-STING pathway activation in monocyte-derived cells, thereby potentiating innate immune responses [168].

At the soluble mediator level, pathway activation-induced IFN-I drives secretion of chemokines (e.g., CXCL9/CXCL10), which are essential for directional trafficking of CD8+T cells [169]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), STING activation remodels distal tumor microenvironments via IFN-I and CXCL9/10 release, potentiating T cell neoantigen reactivity [170]. Concurrently, Jiang et al.'s STING-activating nanovaccine demonstrated that CXCL9 synergizes with T cell-derived IFN-γ to establish a forward-feeding circuit, markedly amplifying T cell-mediated cytolytic activity [171]. Conversely, the dual modulation of IL-6 by this pathway requires precise balancing: STING activation rapidly induces cancer cell-derived IL-6, which activates STAT3 signaling to drive pro-survival programs and PD-L1 upregulation, establishing an immunosuppressive loop [172].

Critically, although cGAS-STING activation stemming from chromosomal instability may propagate therapeutic resistance through IL-6/NF-κB-mediated pathways [173], the spatially and temporally refined modulation by LDRT preferentially activates the IRF3/IFN-β signaling branch. This selective engagement is mechanistically achieved via depletion of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) pools or inhibition of mtDNA extrusion within tumor cells, consequently sensitizing malignancies to PD-1 inhibitors.

The dichotomous impact of LDRT-Triggered cGAS-STING axis on Tumor Immune Microenvironment (TIME)

LDRT activates the cGAS-STING pathway, which dually modulates the tumor immune microenvironment through both immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive mechanisms.

Positive immune activation effect

The core of positive immune activation begins with the initiation of immunogenic cell death (ICD) [174–176]. LDRT-induced ROS trigger ER stress, activating the PKR-like ER kinase (PERK)/eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) pathway. This promotes surface exposure of calreticulin, an "eat me" signal, which is recognized by the CLEC12A receptor on DCs, facilitating DC phagocytosis of tumor cells [177].The cGAS-STING pathway drives ATP release via PANX1 channel opening. Extracellular ATP binds the P2X7 receptor on DCs, acting as a chemoattractant to guide DC recruitment to tumors [178]. Additionally, nuclear membrane rupture accompanying nuclear DNA damage leads to the release of high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) into the extracellular space. HMGB1 binds the TLR4-MD2 complex on DCs and upregulates antigen-processing molecules such as Tapasin, thereby enhancing DC-mediated processing of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) [179].

Building upon this, the release of type I interferon (IFN-I) further amplifies the immune response. STING activation induces type I interferon (IFN-I) secretion. Secreted IFN-I binds IFNAR on DCs [180], activating the JAK1/STAT1 pathway to upregulate MHC-I and co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/86). Specifically, MHC-I presents TAAs to the TCR of CD8⁺ T cells, while CD80/CD86 binds to CD28 on T cell surfaces to deliver a “second activation signal.” Concurrently, IFN-I directly acts on CD8⁺ T cells via IFNAR, boosting granzyme B and perforin production to amplify cytotoxicity [181].

Through cGAS-STING signaling, LDRT activates NF-κB, inducing p65/p50 nuclear translocation and dose-dependent upregulation of MHC class I/II. This enhances endogenous tumor-associated antigen (TAA) loading onto MHC-I and surface presentation, shortening the CD8⁺T cell recognition window and sharpening adaptive immunity [182].

The establishment of the inflammatory microenvironment and the recruitment of immune cells represent another crucial dimension of positive effects. NF-κB-driven IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α upregulate ICAM-1 on tumor endothelium [183], enhancing vascular permeability and immune extravasation. Simultaneously, induced CXCL9-11 bind CXCR3 on CD8⁺T cells, navigating them through stromal collagen toward the tumor core. This synergistic recruitment of DCs, macrophages, and T cells establishes a positive feedback loop—aggregation fuels cytokine secretion, further amplifying infiltration and local immunity [94, 95].

Notably, the cGAS-STING pathway exerts functionally opposing yet therapeutically synergistic effects. NF-κB upregulates PD-L1 on tumor cells—an immunosuppressive step that, paradoxically, enriches targets for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, thereby potentiating T-cell de-repression in combination therapy [184]. Concurrently, NF-κB drives FAS expression [185]; ligation by FASL on CD8⁺T cells triggers caspase-8-mediated apoptosis, directly killing tumor cells. This dual regulation amplifies local cytotoxicity while propagating systemic responses to distant metastases via the abscopal effect, highlighting the integrated, systemic nature of LDRT-driven immunity [186–188].

Negative immunomodulatory effect

However, sustained cGAS–STING activation—resulting from cumulative LDRT doses or extended irradiation fields—leads to persistent IRF3 phosphorylation and continuous IFN-I/ISG expression. Prolonged IFN-I desensitizes IFNAR via phosphatase-mediated dephosphorylation, impairing JAK–STAT signaling and T cell responsiveness. Concurrently, IRF3 and NF-κB cooperatively upregulate tumor PD-L1, which engages PD-1 to suppress TCR signaling and accelerate T cell exhaustion—manifested by reduced Ki67, perforin, and granzyme B, alongside increased PD-1 and TIM-3 [189, 190].

Synergistic upregulation of inhibitory molecules further constrains immunity. IFN-I and IFN-γ from CD8⁺T cells activate IRF1 via JAK-STAT1; nuclear IRF1 binds the IRF response element on the PD-L1 promoter, driving PD-L1 expression [191].Concurrently, IFN-I induces indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) in DCs via STAT1. IDO converts tryptophan to kynurenine, depleting an essential amino acid for T cell proliferation and activating AhR to drive regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation and function [192].

cGAS–STING–NF-κB signaling drives TGF-β secretion from tumor and stromal cells, recruiting circulating Tregs to TIME—a key negative effect [193]. These Tregs suppress effector T cell IL-2 signaling via IL-10 and TGF-β, blocking proliferation and activation. Meanwhile, STING signaling promotes MDSC differentiation from myeloid progenitors. MDSCs express iNOS and NOX2, generating NO and ROS: NO modifies TCR cysteine residues, disrupting MHC–TAA binding; ROS oxidizes the CD3ζ chain, inhibiting TCR signaling [194].

Additionally, LDRT upregulates TGF-β via cGAS–STING [195], driving TAMs toward M2 polarization. M2 TAMs highly express CD206 and IL-10, promoting angiogenesis via VEGF and suppressing T cell activity via IL-10—forming an immune sanctuary for tumor growth [196].

Ultimately, LDRT directly depletes lymphocytes in peripheral blood and the TIME. The exploration and implementation of a low-dose radiotherapy combined treatment strategy in clinical research

Synergistic mechanisms of Low-Dose Radiotherapy (LDRT) and Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors (ICIs)

LDRT combined with ICIs (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1) synergistically reshapes the TIME from immunosuppressive to immunologically activated via precise cGAS–STING pathway activation [198, 199]. Simultaneously, ICIs remove the final immune brake on T cells, thereby closing a complete regulatory loop from pathway initiation to effector consolidation [200]. LDRT initiates cGAS-STING signaling indirectly via cytosolic dsDNA from nuclear DNA repair defects and mitochondrial DNA release. cGAS binds dsDNA and produces cGAMP, which activates STING [18, 20]. Activated STING undergoes Golgi translocation and palmitoylation-dependent TBK1 recruitment [26, 27], culminating in IFN-I and chemokine transcription.

cGAS-STING activation primes ICIs efficacy in TIME via three dimensions. First, IFN-I drives cDC1 maturation, upregulating MHC-I/CD80/86 to enhance antigen cross-presentation and provide essential activation signals [143, 144]. Second, NF-κB-induced CXCL9/10 guide CD8+T cell infiltration via CXCR3, overcoming the infiltration barrier in cold tumors [151, 155]. Third, STING signaling attenuates immunosuppression—suppressing Treg-derived IL-10/TGF-β and inhibiting MDSC STAT3 activity to reduce NO/ROS production [105, 108].

Crucially, LDRT activation via the cGAS-STING pathway also provides ICIs with a precise target—the upregulation of PD-L1 expression [201]. STING activation drives PD-L1 transcription and surface expression on tumor cells and DCs via the IRF3-STAT1 signaling axis. While seemingly immunosuppressive, this process in fact provides recognizable targets for immune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD-L1 antibodies [202].

Notably, this synergy extends beyond PD-1/PD-L1. Studies demonstrate that tumor clearance rates are significantly reduced in STING-deficient mice when treated with CTLA-4 antibodies combined with radiotherapy. In prostate cancer models, multi-target combination therapy (e.g., anti-CTLA-4 antibody/anti-PD-1 antibody/anti-4-1BB antibody combined with the STING agonist CDG) elevated cure rates from 40 to 75%, further confirming STING's pivotal role in integrating innate and adaptive immune responses [203]. In a metastatic tumor model simulating coexisting tumors in the flank and tongue, researchers delivered a STING agonist to the flank tumor and administered systemic treatment combining anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies. The results showed that 71% of mice experienced sustained tumor regression [204]. ICIs not only relieve T cell suppression but also consolidate LDRT-induced immune memory. LDRT activates the cGAS–STING pathway to produce IFN-I, which inhibits Akt signaling and drives naive CD8⁺ T cells toward a stem-like effector phenotype with both cytolytic function and self-renewal capacity [152].

In terms of clinical trials, Wang et al. established the immunological basis for LDRT-ICI synergy, showing in 198 lung adenocarcinoma specimens that CD8 + T cell density correlated with TLS score (r = 0.79) and predicted favorable prognosis (HR = 0.23); animal studies confirmed whole-lung LDRT plus anti-PD-1 promoted TLS formation and enhanced ICI efficacy [205]. Li et al. then demonstrated in 9 stage III NSCLC patients that LDRT (10–30 Gy/5–15 fx) with durvalumab/chemotherapy enabled 77.8% surgical conversion, with 33.3% grade 3–4 AEs and no surgical delays or mortality [206].

Yin et al. first showed in murine models that LDRT + HFRT + anti-PD-1 improved 40-day survival (55.5% vs. 7.5%) and metastatic control; transcriptomics revealed immune gene upregulation in irradiated metastases. A subsequent single-arm trial (n = 9) reported 33.3% PR, 22.2% SD, and 28% metastatic volume reduction [207]. Zhou et al. added LDRT (4 Gy/2 fx) to SBRT (30 Gy/3 fx) and sintilimab in 29 stage IV NSCLC patients, elevating abscopal response from 38 to 50% and achieving ORR 60.7%, DCR 78.6%, and mPFS 8.6 months [208]. Patel et al. confirmed in a controlled trial that HDRT + LDRT yielded 23% abscopal response vs. 11% with HDRT alone [209].

Subgroup also analyses identified predictive biomarkers. Zhou et al. reported that PD-L1-low patients (< 1%) achieved 50% ORR with triple therapy versus 26–27% with ICI monotherapy, while PD-L1-positive patients derived even greater benefit (80% vs. 50%), indicating molecular heterogeneity. [208]. Li et al. observed that responders exhibited higher baseline TMB, suggesting TMB as a complementary selection criterion [206]. Patel et al. further demonstrated in a phase II trial (59 patients, 38 NSCLC) an overall DCR of 42% and ORR of 19%; immunohistochemistry confirmed significantly increased T cell and NK cell infiltration in LDRT-irradiated lesions, providing mechanistic correlates [209].

However, Schoenfeld et al. reported a negative randomized trial in ICI-resistant NSCLC: LDRT plus dual PD-L1/CTLA-4 blockade failed to improve ORR, PFS, or OS, likely due to prior treatment-induced immune exhaustion and suboptimal sequencing (LDRT post-ICI) [210]. Collectively, these studies establish LDRT as an immunomodulatory enhancer in NSCLC, though optimal patient selection and regimen await larger trials.

Synergistic Mechanism of Low-Dose Radiotherapy (LDRT) and STING Agonists

The synergy of LDRT and STING agonists relies on a cascade—endogenous injury, exogenous ligand enhancement, and immune microenvironment remodeling—that amplifies cGAS–STING activation, yielding antitumor immunity superior to monotherapy.

LDRT generates cytosolic dsDNA via nuclear/mtDNA damage, serving as an endogenous cGAS ligand. This process may be potentiated by LDRT-induced Mn2+ release or metal ion redistribution, enhancing cGAS sensitivity and activity [211, 212]. When combined with exogenous STING agonists (e.g., diABZI, 2′3'-cGAMP), dual ligand engagement synergistically amplifies STING activation, prolongs phosphorylated STING duration, and drives downstream IRF3/NF-κB signaling more potently than either agent alone [213]. LDRT not only activates the cGAS-STING pathway via DNA damage but also activates NF-κB signaling through bypass pathways such as ATM kinase [214]. It forms a transcriptional regulatory network with the STING agonist-induced IRF3 pathway, synergistically promoting the secretion of type I interferons (IFN-I) and chemokines (CXCL9, CXCL10).

Studies also show that LDRT activates the IFN-γ/STAT1 axis and DNA damage-induced cGAS–STING signaling in colorectal cancer, collectively driving the polarization of an interferon-licensed CAF subset (ilCAFs) characterized by IRF1 upregulation and CCL4/CCL5 secretion. These ilCAFs recruit CD8⁺ T cells and cDC1 via CCR5, establishing a positive feedback loop of antigen presentation and T cell activation. STING agonist SR717 amplifies this cascade: combined RT + SR717 significantly enriches ilCAFs, enhances CCL4-dependent immune infiltration, suppresses tumor growth, and prolongs survival in multiple CRC models, offering a promising strategy to overcome RT resistance through CAF-centric immunoreprogramming [215].

Interferon-stimulated DNA (ISD) targeting the cGAS-STING pathway represents a highly promising strategy in cancer immunotherapy. However, ISD-based cGAS-STING activation is limited by nuclease degradation, poor uptake, and inefficient cytosolic delivery [216]. Garland et al. developed NanoISD, a nuclease‑resistant nanoparticle engineered for cGAS targeting and endosomal escape, enabling robust STING activation and increased NK/T cell infiltration upon intratumoral injection [217]. LDRT-induced vascular permeability further synergizes with this platform: nanoparticles achieve tumor-enriched STING agonist delivery via radiotherapy-primed TME features, enhancing local efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity.

Notably, the transient injury effect of LDRT (lasting 48–72 h) [218] can synergistically complement the sustained-release properties of STING agonists (maintained for 7–10 days via nanocarriers).This coordination triggers early IFN-I burst for immune initiation, followed by sustained activation driving stem-like CD8+T cell expansion, multiplying memory T cells and suppressing distant metastases [219].

Clinical evidence confirms this synergy. In lung metastases, inhaled nanoliposomal cGAMP (NP-cGAMP) enables lung-targeted STING activation; combined with fractionated RT (8 Gy × 3), it suppresses both irradiated and non-irradiated metastases [218]. SimilarlyIn colon cancer, intratumoral STING agonist 2′3'-cGAMP (10 μg) enhances local RT (20 Gy) in a STING-dependent manne [50]. Further studies on pancreatic cancer have validated this synergistic effect, in pancreatic cancer, RT (10 Gy) plus STING agonist RR-S2-CDG (10 μg) disrupts immune tolerance and drives T-cell responses controlling local and distant tumor growth [220].

Synergistic antitumor effects of Low-Dose Radiotherapy (LDRT) and conventional chemotherapy

Conventional chemotherapy induces genomic instability and mitochondrial stress, leading to nuclear DNA fragmentation and mtDNA release into the cytoplasm. LDRT amplifies this cytosolic dsDNA burden by triggering DNA double-strand breaks and micronucleus formation. This dual endogenous signal synergistically supplies abundant substrates for cGAS activation [50, 221].

LDRT and chemotherapy synergistically amplify cGAS-STING signaling. Enhanced IFN-I drives cDC1-mediated antigen cross-presentation [198], while CXCL9/10 direct CD8+T cell recruitment into tumors [94, 95]. Simultaneously, LDRT also increases vascular permeability, improving chemotherapeutic penetration and damage product release to sustain STING activation via a positive feedback loop.

At the TIME remodeling level, LDRT synergizes with chemotherapy to remodel TIME from immunosuppressive to immune-activated via two dimensions. Reversing immunosuppression: cGAS-STING suppresses Treg function (IL-10, TGF-β) and reduces MDSC-derived NO/ROS; chemotherapy acts similarly. Combination amplifies this, decreasing Treg accumulation and MDSC-mediated T cell inhibition [105, 107]. Optimizing effector function: Synergistic STING activation drives M2 → M1 TAM reprogramming, enhancing phagocytosis and pro-inflammatory secretion [128]. Concurrently, IFN-I and chemokines boost CD8+T cell infiltration, differentiation, and cytotoxicity, elevating effector T cell numbers and killing efficiency [151].

LDRT's transient DNA damage burst rapidly ignites cGAS–STING to prime immunity, while chemotherapy's sustained half-life continuously fuels DNA damage—together, their temporal complementarity synergistically amplifies and prolongs STING pathway activation.

Clinical evidence for the synergistic strategy of LDRT combined with chemotherapy and immunotherapy has been established in extensive-stage small cell lung cancer through peer-reviewed, high-level prospective data. The multicenter, single-arm phase II MATCH trial, led by Lu You’s team, enrolled 56 treatment-naïve ES-SCLC patients who received LDRT (15 Gy/5 fractions) concurrently with atezolizumab and etoposide–platinum chemotherapy [222]. The primary analysis published in Med in 2024 reported a confirmed objective response rate of 87.5% (95% CI: 75.9–94.8) and a median progression-free survival of 6.9 months (95% CI: 5.4–9.3), and mechanistically revealed that this regimen recruits TCF1⁺ PD-1⁺ CD8⁺ stem-like T cells into the tumor microenvironment. The 3-year follow-up update published in International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics in 2025 further demonstrated a median overall survival of 16.9 months (95% CI: 14.0–32.9), with 3-year PFS and OS rates of 20.7% and 35.1%, respectively—representing the highest survival outcomes reported in this setting. Grade 3–4 adverse events were primarily neutropenia (60.7%) and leukopenia (58.9%), with no increased pulmonary toxicity signal [223]. These findings provide the first prospective, translationally validated evidence integrating efficacy and mechanism for LDRT-chemotherapy-immunotherapy combinations.

Synergistic Mechanism of Low-Dose Radiotherapy (LDRT) and CAR-T cell therapy

CAR-T cell therapy is one of the most promising anti-cancer strategies, utilizing genetically engineered T cells to express chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) that precisely target and eliminate tumor cells [224]. However, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and intratumoral heterogeneity significantly limit the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-T cells in clinical applications [225].

The chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) consists of three domains: extracellular single‑chain variable fragment (scFv), transmembrane, and intracellular signaling regions [226]. CAR‑T cells have evolved over five generations [227]: first‑generation CARs carry only CD3ζ signaling, showing limited efficacy; second‑generation CARs add a co‑stimulatory domain (CD28 or 4‑1BB), markedly enhancing T cell activity; third‑generation CARs incorporate dual co‑stimulatory signals for amplified activation; fourth‑generation CARs (TRUCKs) are engineered to secrete cytokines, enabling autonomous immune modulation; fifth‑generation CARs feature a cytokine receptor domain (e.g., IL‑2Rβ) that activates the JAK–STAT pathway, combined with precise knock‑in into loci such as TCRα or PD‑1 to augment functionality while minimizing off‑target effects [228–230].

Despite these technological advances, CAR-T cell therapy remains challenging in solid tumors due to three core bottlenecks [231–233]. First, target deficiency and antigen heterogeneity—solid tumors often lack truly tumor-specific antigens, and heterogeneous expression of existing targets leads to incomplete eradication and immune escape. Second, physical and biological barriers within the TIME—profound immunosuppression, hypoxia, and dense fibrosis collectively impede CAR-T cell infiltration and penetration, rendering even systemically infused effector cells incapable of reaching tumor nests. Third, limited CAR-T cell persistence and functional exhaustion—poor in vivo expansion and truncated survival, exacerbated by chronic antigen stimulation and metabolic stress, culminate in progressive loss of cytolytic function [234, 235].

The core of the synergistic antitumor mechanism lies in LDRT precisely reshaping the TIME by activating the cGAS–STING signaling pathway [236]. This orchestrated intervention directly counters all three CAR-T bottlenecks: STING-driven chemokine secretion (CXCL9/10) promotes CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, overcoming physical barriers; IFN‑I-mediated reprogramming alleviates immunosuppression and rescues effector function; and LDRT-induced upregulation of tumor PD‑L1 paradoxically enriches targets for combination checkpoint blockade [237].

LDRT activates the cGAS-STING-NF-κB axis to promote CXCL9/10 secretion [94, 95]. These chemokines recruit CAR-T cells via CXCR3, significantly enhancing their intratumoral infiltration density [238]. Concurrently, LDRT increases tumor vascular permeability, reducing endothelial barriers and facilitating CAR-T penetration into the stroma. This dual mechanism—chemokine-guided trafficking and barrier attenuation—directly overcomes the “poor infiltration”bottleneck in solid tumors. LDRT-activated cGAS-STING signaling enhances CAR-T recognition and killing via IFN-I [239]. IFN-I upregulates tumor MHC-I and CAR-targeted antigens (e.g., CD19, HER2), improving antigen presentation, binding efficiency, and reducing off-target effects [240]. Concurrent LDRT-induced damage exposes additional tumor-associated antigens, further broadening CAR-T killing range.

Other cGAS-STING activation strategies beyond LDRT further validate its core role in optimizing CAR-T therapy and addressing key clinical challenges. For instance, studies indicate that the STING agonist DMXAA promotes secretion of chemokines CXCL9/10. These chemokines recruit Th/Tc17 CAR-T cells expressing CXCR3 to tumor sites, thereby reducing immune suppression and enhancing CAR-T cell expansion [238]. In a mouse pancreatic tumor model, co-delivery of CAR-T cells with the STING agonist cyclic di-GMP was also demonstrated to stimulate immune responses, eliminate tumor cells resistant to adoptive transfer of lymphocytes, while simultaneously reversing the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) and preventing immune escape [241]. These strategies resonate with the functional logic of LDRT, collectively pointing toward the core direction of “reprogramming TIME by activating the cGAS-STING pathway.”

cGAS-STING-driven interferon secretion also correlates with CAR-T clinical outcomes. Tumor cell sensitivity to IFN-γ governs CD4+CAR-T activity [242]; CAR-T-derived IFN-γ boosts endogenous T/NK cells and sustains CAR-T cytotoxicitye [243]. This IFN family synergy complements LDRT-mediated IFN-I regulation, forming an integrated STING-dependent immune network. Challenges and optimization of LDRT combined therapy

Radiotherapy heterogeneity limits LDRT efficacy in modulating TIME via cGAS-STING across spatial, cellular, and functional dimensions. Spatially, hypoxic tumor cores exhibit radioresistance, yielding dsDNA release and cGAS-STING activation only one-third that of oxygen-rich peripheries. Hypoxia and perfusion disparities cause spatial variation in radiation response; peripheral areas are more susceptible to DNA damage, while hypoxic cores fail to activate cGAS-STING [244].

Cellularly, tumor cells display differential radiosensitivity. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are highly radioresistant—even at high doses, they suppress IFN-γ and TNF-α secretion, directly impeding cGAS-STING-mediated immune activation [245, 246]. Immune cells are more sensitive; 0.5 Gy suffices to promote CD3⁺ T cell infiltration [247]. This “dose requirement disparity” complicates uniform dosing for optimal responses across cell types.

Functionally, a paradox emerges: high-dose regions upregulate MHC-I but also induce PD-L1, accelerating T-cell exhaustion [248, 249]. Low-dose zones facilitate immune extravasation but fail to trigger sufficient immunogenic cell death. This contradiction constrains cGAS-STING synergy. Furthermore, STING gene mutations reduce pathway activation capacity, leading to resistance—even optimal doses cannot initiate effective signaling.

To address the aforementioned issues, multiple countermeasures can be implemented. For instance, image-guided radiotherapy (such as MRI-Linac) can optimize dose distribution [250]. By incorporating the principles of spatial fractionation radiotherapy (SFRT), a “high-medium–low” dose gradient can be established within the tumor [251]: while the medium-dose zone (8–12 Gy) targets tumor parenchyma to activate the cGAS-STING pathway. The low-dose zone covers the tumor periphery to promote T-cell and NK-cell infiltration [41, 252]. Research confirms that SFRT achieves local and distant tumor control by irradiating 20% of tumor volume (e.g., lattice radiotherapy), accompanied by increased tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes and enhanced immunogenic cell death characteristics, fully validating the advantages of this heterogeneous dose distribution.

For patients with STING mutations, strategies that enhance immune responses—such as radiopharmaceutical therapy (e.g., ⁹⁰Y-NM600) [253]—can be used in combination with anti-PD-L1 inhibitors. Radiopharmaceuticals can enhance the immune-activating effects of the cGAS-STING pathway by increasing CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and reducing regulatory T cells (Tregs) through heterogeneous dose distribution when used in combination with anti-PD-L1 inhibitors [254]. By integrating single-cell sequencing with spatial transcriptomics, we can correlate microdose distribution with immune cell infiltration and gene expression profiles to identify patients exhibiting the “STING-high + hypoxic”subtype. Concurrently, microdose detection technologies such as iQID enable assessment of local dose distribution, providing precise evidence for personalized treatment strategies [255].

Optimizing dose and timing is also fundamental to enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of LDRT. Doses too low (< 1 Gy) may fail to induce sufficient dsDNA release, making it difficult to effectively activate the cGAS-STING pathway; conversely, doses too high (> 10 Gy) may damage immune cells and suppress immune function. Regarding timing, combining LDRT with other therapies must align with the activation cycle of the cGAS-STING pathway. STING activation reaches peak IFN-I secretion within approximately 24–48 h. Therefore, administration of ICIs or STING agonists should occur within 3 days after LDRT to fully capitalize on the immune activation window. Additionally, different tumor types exhibit varying dose requirements. For instance, melanoma is sensitive to 2 Gy doses [256], whereas pancreatic cancer may require 4 Gy to activate the cGAS-STING pathway [257]. Future treatment plans should incorporate patient-specific factors such as tumor type and TIME status to develop individualized dose and timing regimens, thereby avoiding insufficient or excessive immune activation resulting from inappropriate treatment protocols.

Simultaneously, expanding precision treatment strategies represents a key direction for enhancing the regulatory efficacy of the LDRT-cGAS-STING pathway. These strategies encompass biomarker-guided patient selection based on STING pathway status and tumor hypoxia, optimization of radiotherapy dose and fractionation according to tumor type and immune contexture, and combination with targeted agents that modulate STING signaling. For instance, developing specific drugs targeting upstream and downstream molecules such as TBK1 and ENPP1 can further amplify the pathway's immunomodulatory effects[258]. While TBK1 inhibitors may suppress pathway activation, they can be employed under specific circumstances—such as when excessive activation leads to immunopathological damage—to precisely regulate pathway activity, thereby balancing immune activation with tissue injury. Similarly, ENPP1 inhibitors sustain STING activation by preventing degradation of the endogenous STING agonist 2′3'-cGAMP, offering another avenue for fine-tuning the immune response. Such personalized approaches, guided by tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental factors, aim to maximize therapeutic benefit while minimizing off-target effects.

The development of novel formulations also holds significant importance. For instance, STING agonist nanocarriers and radiation-responsive smart delivery systems (such as radiosensitizing liposomes) show promise in achieving spatiotemporal coordinated release of LDRT and drugs. This approach enhances local drug concentration and amplifies the activation effect of the cGAS-STING pathway [259]. Additionally, gene editing technologies such as CRISPR-Cas9 can be employed to repair STING mutations and restore pathway function, offering new therapeutic options for drug-resistant patients. The exploration of these novel strategies will open new avenues for the clinical application of LDRT [260].

Future research should further elucidate the interactions between the cGAS-STING pathway and other signaling pathways (such as NF-κB and JAK-STAT) in different combination therapy regimens, identify key regulatory nodes, and lay the foundation for developing more effective combination treatment strategies. Concurrently, by integrating treatment response data from clinical patients, we should continuously optimize combination regimens, advance the translation from basic research to clinical application, and ultimately enhance the overall efficacy of cancer therapy.

Conclusion

In summary, research on LDRT modulating the TIME via the cGAS-STING pathway offers a novel perspective for tumor immunotherapy. Leveraging its unique immunomodulatory properties, LDRT induces tumor cells to release dsDNA, thereby activating the cGAS-STING pathway. This pathway then remodels the cytokine network via the IRF3-IFN-I and NF-κB signaling axes, facilitating the transformation from an immunosuppressive “cold tumor” to an immunologically activated “hot tumor”.—not only promoting dendritic cell maturation, enhancing T cell infiltration and killing functions, and driving macrophage M1 polarization, but also synergizing with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and STING agonists to amplify antitumor effects, laying a solid foundation for sensitizing immunotherapy in patients with “cold tumors”.Nevertheless, significant challenges persist in this field: precise control of dosage and timing requires further optimization based on tumor type and TIME characteristics; individual variations (such as cGAS-STING pathway status and STING mutations) demand more refined stratified treatment approaches; and issues like radiotherapy heterogeneity and STING agonist delivery efficiency urgently require breakthroughs. Looking ahead, deeper insights into the cGAS-STING pathway's interaction with TIME, combined with multimodal imaging techniques and molecular biomarker screening, will enable the development of novel strategies like responsive nanocarriers and gene editing. This approach holds promise for achieving spatiotemporal synergy between LDRT and targeted/immunotherapies, ultimately driving the clinical translation of personalized combination treatment regimens. Such advancements will provide stronger theoretical and practical support for enhancing tumor treatment efficacy and improving patient outcomes.
