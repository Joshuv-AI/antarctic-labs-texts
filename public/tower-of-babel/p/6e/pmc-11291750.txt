Adsorptive removal of anticarcinogen pazopanib from aqueous solutions using activated carbon: isotherm, kinetic and thermodynamic studies
Authors: Degirmenci Mustafa, Bulduk Ibrahim, Akbel Erten
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Pazopanib, which is dangerous for aquatic environments due to its toxic and bioaccumulation potential, has been detected at different concentrations in oncology hospital wastewater, sewage, and surface waters. This study aimed to remove pazopanib from wastewater by activated carbon adsorption technique. The effect of the main variables such as initial concentration, pH of pazopanib solution, adsorbent dose, contact time of the phases, and temperature on the adsorption process was evaluated and the optimum adsorption conditions were determined. The experimental data were applied to Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubinin-Radushkevich adsorption isotherm models to describe the adsorption behavior. The experimental data were applied to pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and intra-particle diffusion kinetic models to describe the adsorption kinetics. Isotherms were established in the 20–50 °C temperature range to study the adsorption equilibrium. According to the results, the highest removal efficiency of pazopanib (95.87%) was obtained at initial concentration (100 mg L−1), adsorbent dose (0.30 g L−1), temperature (20 °C), contact time (120 min) and pH (7.0). The adsorption kinetics was well described by the pseudo-second-order kinetic model (R2 = 0.9998) and the adsorption isotherm by the Langmuir model (R2 = 0.9999). In thermodynamic studies, the negative values of standard enthalpy (ΔH°), standard free enthalpy (ΔG°), and free entropy (ΔS°) indicate that the adsorption process is spontaneous and favorable, i.e. the disorder is reduced. These results indicate that the developed adsorption process can be efficiently and spontaneously applied for the removal of pazopanib from aqueous solutions.

Introduction

Due to the increasing incidence of cancer, the use of anticancer drugs has increased exponentially in recent years and is expected to increase further in the coming years1–3. Anticancer drugs used in cancer treatment are a group of drugs developed to prevent cellular proliferation by interacting with DNA function and cell signaling4. Although cancer drugs have been developed to kill fast-growing cells in cancerous tumors, most of them lack selectivity5. In any living organism, they can attack tumor cells as well as healthy cells and cause genotoxic, cytotoxic, teratogenic, and mutagenic effects6,7. Therefore, anticancer drugs have become an important environmental issue due to their potential risks8.

Due to their persistent nature, the discharge of anticancer drugs into the environment may result in the spread of genes associated with antimicrobial resistance through selective pressure on microorganisms9,10. Many papers have been published reporting the presence of anticancer drugs in hospital wastewater11–14. Since hospital wastewater is usually discharged to wastewater treatment plants without any pretreatment, hospitals are one of the sources of entry of these drugs into the environment15. In addition, many anticancer drugs are nowadays consumed by outpatients and consequently, these drugs are also discharged directly to wastewater treatment plants16,17. Wastewater from factories where anticancer drugs are produced can be listed as another source of these drugs18,19. Many anti-cancer drugs have been detected in wastewater, groundwater, river water and even drinking water1,20–28.

Various methods such as physical, chemical, and biological have been used to remove these pollutants from wastewater. Studies have shown that conventional treatment methods cannot effectively and acceptably remove pharmaceutical pollutants and bring effluent concentrations of these pollutants to standard values. For example, biological methods have been poor at removing these pollutants, which may be due to the toxicity of these pollutants to microorganisms. On the other hand, biological methods require a long time for the biodegradation of these pollutants. Coagulation and flocculation methods have low efficiency in the removal of organic compounds due to their high solubility and low molecular weight. Membrane systems also have disadvantages such as high energy costs and the need for pretreatment. The use of advanced oxidation processes as an alternative option for the treatment of hazardous and biodegradable pollutants has increased significantly29–31.

The adsorption method has been described as superior to the other mentioned methods due to its effective pollutant removal and simple applicability. Above all, it is easy to use in treatment systems and field conditions. The literature review revealed several studies investigating pharmaceutical adsorption on activated carbon, clay, carbon nanotubes, silica, and zeolite. Activated carbon is the adsorbent with the highest efficiency for pharmaceutical adsorption as it is highly porous and has a large surface area with the capacity to adsorb various organic and inorganic elements even under a wide range of conditions32,33,38.

Pazopanib (PZP) is an anticarcinogenic drug used in the clinical treatment of soft tissue and advanced renal cell carcinoma tumors. PZP (Fig. 1) is an oral multi-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor with activity against platelet-derived growth factor receptors, vascular endothelial growth factor receptors, stem cell receptors, and fibroblast growth factor receptors. Since the drug is given orally, patients can receive chemotherapy at home, which is an important advantage in terms of quality of life26,34.Figure 1The chemical structure of Pazopanib.

The chemical structure of Pazopanib.

Anti-cancer drugs are the only group of medicines explicitly classified as 'hazardous' under the European Commission's Waste Framework Directive. Although anti-cancer drugs are designed to kill the rapidly growing cells found in cancer tumors, because many of them lack selectivity, they can also attack healthy cells and cause cytotoxic, mutagenic, teratogenic, and genotoxic effects, leading to adverse effects in any living organism. Anti-cancer drugs are therefore recognized as an important environmental issue due to their potential risks for healthy individuals, pregnant women, children, and especially the elderly. While there are a minimal number of studies in the literature investigating the removal of anticancer drugs from wastewater, there is no study on the removal of PZP from wastewater. Therefore, comprehensive studies on the removal of this anticancer drug from wastewater are needed. In this study, it was aimed to remove pazopanib from wastewater by activated carbon adsorption technique. The equilibrium, kinetic, and thermodynamic modeling of the PZP adsorption process on activated carbon is performed for the first time. In this study, the adsorption of pazopanib on activated carbon was investigated, the effects of pH, phase contact time, initial concentration, and activated carbon dose on the process were investigated, adsorption kinetics were evaluated by pseudo-first order, pseudo-second order, and intraparticle diffusion models, and adsorption equilibrium was evaluated by constructing equilibrium isotherms at 293 K, 308 K, and 323 K. Furthermore, thermodynamic parameters were determined by fitting the experimental data to Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubinin-Radushkevich isotherm models.

Material and method

Instruments

The concentration of PZP in aqueous solutions was determined using an HPLC system (Agilent 1260, USA), an ultraviolet detector, and Chemstation software. pH measurements were performed using a pH meter (Mettler-Toledo, Switzerland) with a glass electrode. Ultrapure water used in the experimental studies was produced using a water purifier (Merck-Millipore Milli-Q, USA). Batch adsorption studies were carried out using a shaking water bath (WITEG WSB-30, Germany).

Chemicals

PZP (≥ 98.0%) and tablets (Vopazzi, 200 mg) were obtained from Deva Holding Pharmaceutical Company (Istanbul, Turkey). Acetonitrile (≥ 99.9%), methanol (≥ 99.0%), ethanol (≥ 99%), NaOH solution (0.1 N), HCl solution (0.1 N), H3PO4 (85%), KH2PO4 (≥ 99.0%), and activated carbon were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemie (Istanbul, Turkey).

Determination of pHpzc value of activated carbon

First, 500 mL of 0.1 M NaCl solution was prepared. Then, 50 mL of the salt solution was taken into 250 mL volume flasks and the pH of the solutions was adjusted with 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH solutions to pH 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. The initial pH of each solution was recorded. To each flask 0.050 g of activated carbon was added. The bottles were placed in a shaker and shaken for 48 h at a temperature of 25 °C and a shaking speed of 150 rpm. At the end of the shaking, each mixture was filtered through white tape filter paper and the pHf values of each sample were measured with a pH meter. The pHf values were plotted against pHi values35.

Batch adsorption experiments

To prepare the stock PZP solution (500 µg mL−1), 250 mg of PZP was precisely weighed and transferred to a 500 mL flask, and 100 mL of ultrapure water was added. The contents of the volumetric vial were sonicated for 30 min to dissolve the contents. The volume was then made up to the mark with ultrapure water. Batch adsorption experiments were carried out in 250 mL capped conical flasks using 50 mL of PZP solutions. In the batch adsorption study series, the effects of solution pH, initial concentration, adsorbent dose, and temperature on PZP removal efficiency were investigated. PZP solutions used in the experimental series were prepared at the desired concentrations by diluting from the stock solution. After adjusting the pH values of the prepared solutions, certain amounts of activated carbon were added to the solutions and placed in a thermostat water bath (WITEG, WSB-30). The solutions were shaken at 150 rpm in a water bath at the specified temperature. The samples were then filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe tip membrane filter, and PZP concentrations in the filtrates were determined by HPLC.

The effect of pH on PCP removal efficiency was investigated for different pH values in the range 2–12, keeping all other parameters constant. The pH of the solutions was adjusted to the appropriate value using 0.1 N HCl or 0.1 N NaOH.

In the second step, the effect of activated carbon dosage on the removal efficiency was investigated for different activated carbon dosages in the range of 0.2–0.6 g L−1, keeping all other parameters constant. The pH of the solutions was adjusted to the pH value determined in the previous step where the best removal efficiency was obtained before the addition of activated carbon.

The effect of initial concentration on the removal efficiency was investigated for different concentrations of PZP in the range 25–100 mg L−1, keeping all other parameters constant.

The effect of temperature on the removal efficiency was investigated for different temperature values in the range 20–50 °C, keeping all other parameters constant.

In each experiment, only one parameter was varied and the other parameters were kept constant. After shaking for a predetermined period, 1 mL of sample was taken, filtered through an injector tip filter (0.22 μm membrane filter) and then the concentration of PZP in the filtrate was determined using an HPLC. All experiments were performed in triplicate and the results were averaged. Equilibrium modeling of the adsorption process

Adsorption isotherms provide qualitative information about the nature of the interaction between the solute and the surface, in addition to the exact correlation between the adsorbate concentration and the amount of surface deposition at a given temperature. When an ideal correlation is obtained for equilibrium curves that can describe the relationships between the PZP adsorbed at a given temperature and the PZP concentrations remaining in solution, a suitable sorption system design is obtained. In this study, Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models, which are the three most commonly used models for equilibrium modeling of PZP adsorption on activated carbon, were used. The Langmuir isotherm is based on the physical and/or physical interactions between the solute and vacant sites on the adsorbent surface. In this equation, KL is the Langmuir adsorption constant (L mg−1) and qm is the monolayer adsorption capacity of activated carbon (mg g−1). The affinity between adsorbate and adsorbent can be determined by the dimensionless separation factor (RL). Analytical method and validation

An HPLC method was developed for the quantification of PZP in aqueous solutions. The conditions of the developed chromatographic method are presented below: In the chromatographic method, an Extend C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm i.d. 5 µm) was used for separation at a constant temperature of 25 °C. The mobile phase was 10 mM KH2PO4 solution (pH: 2.0 with orthophosphoric acid) and acetonitrile (20:80, V/V). Isocratic elution with a flow rate of 1.2 mL min−1 and eluent detection was performed at a wavelength of 253 nm using an ultraviolet detector. The retention time of PZP was determined as 10.65 min. The developed analytical method was validated for the parameters "specificity, system suitability, linearity, precision, accuracy, sensitivity, and robustness" according to ICH Q2 (R1) guidelines43,44.

Standard solutions (the range of 5–30 μg mL−1) were injected into the HPLC system to test its linearity into the HPLC system on three consecutive days. The chromatographic responses (peak areas) from the instrument were recorded for each concentration of the standard solutions. A calibration curve was drawn with concentrations on the x-axis and chromatographic responses (peak areas) on the y-axis. Data from the analytical method were used for regression analysis using the least squares method. The linearity of the method was determined by the absolute mean recovery, relative standard deviation, and R2 of the calibration curve. The sensitivity of the HPLC technique was assessed by determining the limits of detection (LOD) and limits of quantification (LOQ).

Determination of zero adsorbent charge point (pHpzc)

The zero adsorbent charge point (pHpzc) refers to the pH value at which the surface charge of the adsorbent is zero. A pH value lower than pHPZC causes protonation of the surface functional groups of the adsorbent, while a pH value above pHPZC causes deprotonation of the surface due to the presence of OH− ions in the solution. In short, the pH level at which the surface charge of the adsorbent is equal to zero is known as the zero charge point. Determination of the zero charge point helps to better understand the electrostatic interactions between the adsorbent surface and adsorbate at a given pH. Figure 3 shows that the pHpzc value for activated carbon is 8.0. The adsorbent surface is positively charged and conditions are favorable for anion adsorption if the final pH is lower than pHpzc. When the pH level is above the pHPZC value, the negatively charged adsorbent surface becomes favorable for the adsorption of cations. Due to their surface charge, activated carbon exhibits a negative zeta potential at pH levels higher than 8.0.Figure 3pH zero charge point of activated carbon (pHpzc).

The effect of pH on adsorption performance

The adsorption efficiency (%) of PZP on activated carbon at different pH values is given in Fig. 4. The removal percentage was 83% at pH:2 and 96% at pH:7. A continuously increasing removal efficiency was obtained. The removal percentage was 92% at pH:10 and 90% at pH:12. A gradually decreasing removal efficiency was obtained starting from pH:7. The best removal efficiency was obtained at pH:7. Since the wastewater is generally discharged in the neutral pH range, the pH of the study was determined as 7 and the subsequent experiments were carried out at pH:7. From this adsorption parameter screening study, it can be concluded that the adsorption of PZP is mainly affected by pH.Figure 4The influence of pH on adsorption performance.

The effect of adsorbent dose on adsorption performance

To study the effect of adsorbent dosage on PZP adsorption, the proportion of activated carbon was varied from 0.10 to 0.50 g L−1, keeping other parameters constant (Fig. 5). When the results were analyzed, the adsorption efficiency increased rapidly from 87.14% to 97.14% from the adsorbent dosage of 0.10–0.30 g L−1. Adsorption is fast at the beginning of the process due to the presence of active free sites. This is because increasing the amount of activated carbon increases the surface area and hence the number of sites available for adsorption. It is observed that the adsorption efficiency progresses slowly at adsorbent dosage values greater than 0.30 g L−1. The slowdown can be explained by the coating of the activated carbon surface, making it difficult for PZP molecules to access the remaining active sites. The adsorption efficiency was 97.14% when the adsorbent dosage was 0.30 g L−1 and 99.32% when it was 0.50 g L−1. There is not a big difference between both adsorption efficiencies. An adsorbent dosage of 0.30 g L−1 is sufficient to reach our target of PZP removal above 95%. The use of more than this amount of activated carbon will increase the treatment costs. Therefore, the adsorbent dose was determined as 0.30 g L−1.Figure 5The effect of adsorbent dose on adsorption performance (C0 = 100 mg L−1; pH = 7.0; T = 20 °C).

The effect of initial concentration on adsorption performance

The influence of initial concentration on adsorption performance was investigated for pazopanib concentrations ranging from 50 to 150 mg L−1. Parameters such as activated carbon dose (0.30 g L−1), pH (7.00), temperature (20 °C), and contact time (120 min.) were kept constant. Figure 6 shows the removal versus initial PZP concentration. It is observed that PZP removal decreases continuously with increasing C0 from 50 to 150 mg L−1. However, a sharp decline was observed at larger concentrations after an initial concentration of 100 mg L−1. This behavior can be explained by the presence of high concentrations of C0 on the surface. Moreover, PZP molecules tend to form agglomeration which creates steric hindrance and causes difficulty in penetration of PZP molecules into activated carbon. The formation of a higher PZP concentration gradient on the surface of the adsorbent than in the solution also explains this and leads to dominant desorption. An initial concentration of 100 mg L−1 is suitable for a removal efficiency above 95%.

The influence of contact time of phases on adsorption

Figure 7 shows the removal of PZP during the contact time of the phases. According to this, PZP removal occurs rapidly in the first 15 min and continues slightly in the range of 20–120 min. The removal process is completed in 120 min. When the mass transfer between the phases was analyzed, a PZP concentration gradient was formed between the PZP solution and the activated carbon surface. This concentration gradient created a driving force. Initially, all active sites on the activated carbon surface were abundant, which can be said to lead to a rapid mass transfer. As the available free space decreases, the system approaches equilibrium. The removal percentage reaches close values starting at 120 min and increases from 95.87 to 96.24% at 150 min. Statistical analysis (ANOVA, confidence interval 95%) revealed no significant difference in removal times between 120 and 150 min. The adsorption process' kinetics

From Fig. 6, it can be seen that the initial adsorption rate was quite high, 91.28% of adsorption occurred in the first 15 min of the process, which was caused by the high concentration gradient and the large number of adsorption sites on the adsorbent at the beginning of the process. From 15 to 60 min, a moderate adsorption rate was observed, which increased with a slight slope, and no significant adsorption was recorded after 60 min. This is a result of the decrease in adsorption sites. Using adsorption kinetics, the reaction rate and pathways can be understood and they depend on the physical and chemical properties of the adsorbent. Three models were used to describe adsorption kinetics: pseudo-first-order kinetics (PFO), pseudo-second-order kinetics (PSO), and intra-particle diffusion (IPD) models. The graphs obtained from the kinetic studies are given in Fig. 9A, B and C and the kinetic parameters and correlation coefficients are given in Table 3. The table shows that the correlation coefficient of the pseudo-second-order kinetic model of PZP is higher than that of the pseudo-first-order model. This indicates that the adsorption of PZP molecules on the adsorbent surface follows a pseudo-second-order kinetic model. Moreover, the error coefficients for the PSO model are lower than those of the PFO kinetic model. Regeneration of activated carbon

Desorption processes were performed to determine the reusability of activated carbon. Ethanol and isopropyl alcohol are very good solvents for pazopanib45. Since ethanol has the advantages of being easily available and volatile and evaporates easily, ethanol was used as a solvent for the desorption process in the solid–liquid extraction method. For regeneration, the adsorption process was carried out under optimum adsorption conditions and activated carbon, which was filtered and separated after adsorption, was used in adsorption/desorption cycles. For desorption, the filtered activated carbon was washed with 50 mL ethanol at 180 rpm for 30 min and then dried. The adsorption/desorption cycle was repeated five times. Figure 11 shows the percentage removal of pazopanib by activated carbon in different cycles. The decrease in active sites can be attributed to the decrease in adsorption efficiency during the regeneration cycle. In conclusion, activated carbon shows promise for pazopanib removal from wastewater and potential for use in environmental remediation.Figure 11The removal of pazopanib by activated carbon in different cycles.

PZP removal studies in hospital wastewater samples

Real wastewater containing PZP contains many impurities that can hinder the adsorption process. In this study, a real wastewater sample was obtained from Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University (AFSU) Oncology Hospital. The hospital wastewater sample was analyzed by HPLC for the presence of PZP. Since the wastewater sample did not contain PZP, a certain amount of the PZP standard was spiked. Under optimum conditions, the removal efficiency in the hospital wastewater sample (81.25%) was lower than the efficiency determined from the synthetic samples (95.88%). Physical and chemical impurities as well as biological contaminants in the wastewater also caused a decrease in the removal efficiency compared to the synthetic samples. However, quite high efficiencies were achieved even in real hospital wastewater.

Conclusion

Anti-cancer drugs are the only group of medicines explicitly classified as 'hazardous' under the European Commission's Waste Framework Directive. Although anti-cancer drugs are designed to kill the rapidly growing cells found in cancer tumors, because many of them lack selectivity, they can also attack healthy cells and cause cytotoxic, mutagenic, teratogenic, and genotoxic effects, leading to adverse effects in any living organism. Anti-cancer drugs are therefore recognized as an important environmental issue due to their potential risks for healthy individuals, pregnant women, children, and especially the elderly. While there are a minimal number of studies in the literature investigating the removal of anticancer drugs from wastewater, there is no study on the removal of PZP from wastewater. Therefore, comprehensive studies on the removal of this anticancer drug from wastewater are needed. The activated carbon used in adsorption studies was characterized by scanning electron microscopy and Brunauer, Emmet, and Teller analysis. The optimum adsorption conditions were determined by evaluating the effect of the main variables such as initial concentration, pH of pazopanib solution, adsorbent dose, contact time of the phases, and temperature on the adsorption process. The results of the parameter studies showed that the percentage of PCP removal increased with increasing activated carbon dose and contact time. However, the removal percentage decreased as the initial PZP concentration and temperature increased. The removal rate at optimum values was 95.87%. Five-stage recycling studies showed that the percentage removal decreased from 94.94 to 70.69 for a concentration of 100 mg L−1. Equilibrium data were analyzed by Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherms. PFO and PSO, and IPD models were also discussed. The Langmuir and PSO kinetic models were more consistent with the equilibrium data, where R2 was above 0.998 and the error function was lower. Thermodynamic studies showed that the adsorption process was spontaneous and exothermic. Overall, the activated carbon adsorbent had excellent adsorption performance and achieved effective removal of PZP from aqueous media.
