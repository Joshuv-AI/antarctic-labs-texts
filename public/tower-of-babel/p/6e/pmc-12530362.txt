Evaluation of atypical antipsychotic-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in patients with schizophrenia: a randomised controlled trial protocol
Authors: Amiya Shaju, Debadatta Mohapatra, Biswa Ranjan Mishra, Archana Mishra, Anand Srinivasan, Rituparna Maiti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTIntroductionMitochondrial dysfunction and disrupted cerebral energy metabolism have been increasingly linked to the pathophysiology of schizophrenia. Preclinical studies suggest that certain atypical antipsychotics may exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction by inhibiting respiratory complex I, contributing to adverse effects. Risperidone, a dopamine D2/serotonin 5-HT2 antagonist, and aripiprazole, a partial dopamine D2 agonist, were selected because of their contrasting pharmacological profiles and reported differential effects on mitochondrial complex I activity. Despite these findings, the clinical implications remain poorly understood. This randomised controlled trial aims to evaluate and compare the biochemical and clinical effects of risperidone and aripiprazole on mitochondrial dysfunction in patients with schizophrenia.Methods and analysisIn this randomised, open-label, parallel-arm clinical trial, 60 patients with schizophrenia who were drug-naïve or had not taken any antipsychotic drugs for at least 4 weeks before recruitment were randomly assigned to risperidone or aripiprazole treatment for 12 weeks, with inclusion of an additional group of 30 healthy individuals for baseline comparisons. The primary outcome is the change in mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I concentration in platelets, and secondary outcomes include serum lactate, pyruvate, creatine kinase levels and Newcastle Mitochondrial Disease Adult Scale (NMDAS) scores. Clinical efficacy and safety are evaluated using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) and monitoring treatment-emergent adverse events. Intention-to-treat analysis will be done for all parameters using suitable statistical tools.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee and the study conformed to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki and ICMR’s national ethical guidelines for biomedical and health research involving human participants (2017). Written informed consent will be obtained from the legally authorised representative of the patients. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conferences to inform safer treatment strategies for schizophrenia.Trial registration numberNCT06236451.Keywords: Schizophrenia & psychotic disorders, CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY, Clinical Trial

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a complex psychiatric disorder which affects approximately 1% of adults globally.1 2 The aetiopathogenesis of schizophrenia is primarily associated with dysfunction in dopaminergic, glutamatergic, serotonergic and GABAergic neurotransmission.3,9 However, the recent evidence suggests that a compromised cellular energy state resulting from mitochondrial dysfunction plays a vital role in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia.10,13 Cognitive impairment and aberrant behaviours might result from altered neural plasticity caused by impaired mitochondrial function.14 There are several indicators that suggest an underlying mitochondrial dysfunction in schizophrenia, including altered mitochondrial respiration, altered mitochondrial structure, an increase in polymorphisms and mutations in mitochondrial DNA, a decrease in high-energy phosphates and a decrease in pH in the brain.15 Wang et al also revealed complex immune-brain interactions and inflammation as a mediating effect for peripheral mitochondrial dysfunction and cognitive impairment.16

Pharmacotherapy for schizophrenia is primarily based on the monoamine hypothesis, with most treatments targeting dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways.17 Treatment with antipsychotics has been demonstrated to alter brain energy metabolism and impact mitochondrial function. Previous preclinical studies have shown that certain typical and atypical antipsychotic drugs can reduce mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I activity in brain.18,24 In one cross-sectional observational study, Casademont et al reported a significant decrease in the activity of complex I with haloperidol and risperidone compared with drug-naive patients.25 Moreover, the mitochondrial damage associated with schizophrenia is not ameliorated by antipsychotics.15 Additionally, there is a growing body of evidence linking antipsychotic-induced extrapyramidal adverse effects with impairment of mitochondrial energy metabolism mediated by inhibition of complex I of the electron transport chain.26 27 (A detailed review of evidence has been presented in the online supplemental material)

In the realm of psychopharmacology, a notable knowledge gap persists regarding the clinical implications of antipsychotic-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Our literature search could not find any clinical trial or prospective observational study evaluating the effect of antipsychotic drugs on mitochondrial functions. Knowledge of the extent and implications of this interference is crucial for a thorough understanding of the long-term effects of antipsychotic medications. Unravelling the specific mechanisms, quantifying the degree of mitochondrial dysfunction and delineating any associated clinical outcomes remain critical gaps in current knowledge that demand further investigation. Bridging this gap could significantly enhance our understanding of antipsychotic-induced mitochondrial alterations and aid in the development of more targeted and safer treatment strategies for patients with schizophrenia.

We hypothesise that treatment with atypical antipsychotic drugs may influence the emergence of adverse effects due to their impact on energy metabolism by disturbing the mitochondrial respiratory chain along with the psychopathology and prognosis of schizophrenia. Therefore, the present randomised controlled trial has been planned to evaluate and compare biochemical and clinical parameters of mitochondrial dysfunction between risperidone and aripiprazole in patients with schizophrenia.

Methods and Analysis

Study design and study site

The proposed study will be conducted as a randomised, open-label, parallel-arm, clinical trial. The study protocol has been prepared following the Standard Protocol Item Recommendations guidelines for Interventional Trials and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06236451). The present study is a single-centre study and will be conducted in the departments of Pharmacology and Psychiatry of our institute.

Study population and eligibility

Patients attending the outpatient psychiatry department of our institute with schizophrenia will be screened, and enrolment will be done as per predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. After direct communication with the principal investigator, detailed information regarding interventions, assessment methods, benefits and potential adverse reactions will be explained, and a patient information sheet will be provided to the participants. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants before they participate in the study (participant informed consent form has been provided under online supplemental material).

Sample size calculation

A sample size of 30 participants per group is considered sufficient to detect an intergroup difference of 4 nmol/min/mg in mitochondrial complex I activity using a SD of 4.7, a significance level of 0.05 and an allocation ratio of 1:1, as informed by previous findings from Casademont et al25. Although literature evidence is available for activity, our primary outcome will be mitochondrial complex I concentration . The sample size estimation is based on an anticipated moderate-to-large effect (approximately 0.8 for the standardised difference in means), for which calculations show that 26 subjects per group provide 80% power at the specified significance level of 0.05. To account for an expected attrition rate of 10%, we plan to enrol 30 participants per group. A group of 30 healthy individuals will be recruited to assess biochemical parameters of mitochondrial dysfunction for comparison with schizophrenia patients at baseline.

Randomisation and allocation concealment

By employing computer-generated codes and block randomisation, all the patients who are enrolled will be divided into two treatment groups with an allocation ratio of 1:1. The investigator, who will not be involved in patient recruitment, will generate the random allocation code for the participants. The sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelope (SNOSE) technique will be followed for allocation concealment.

Aripiprazole group: Aripiprazole will be started at a dose of 10 mg/day and will be increased to the stable dose of 20 mg/day over 2–3 weeks and will be continued for 12 weeks.

Study procedure and data collection

Patients with a diagnosis of schizophrenia presenting to the outpatient department of Psychiatry, AIIMS, Bhubaneswar, will be screened. After considering the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria and receiving signed informed consent from LRA, enrolment will be done. A thorough medical history will be taken, and basic anthropometric information will be documented. PANSS and NMDAS scores will be recorded at baseline. We will also evaluate the biochemical parameters (mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I concentration in platelets, serum lactate, serum pyruvate, lactate: pyruvate ratio and serum creatine kinase) at baseline. The enrolled patients (n=60) will be randomised into two groups using computer-generated algorithms. Patients in one group will be prescribed aripiprazole (n=30), while those in the other group will be prescribed risperidone (n=30) for 12 weeks. A group of healthy individuals (n=30) will be recruited to assess biochemical parameters of mitochondrial dysfunction for comparison with schizophrenia patients at baseline. After 6 weeks, a telephonic follow-up will be done to ensure compliance and check for any adverse events (if any). Following a 12-week study period, all patients will have their post-treatment PANSS and NMDAS scores, and all biochemical parameters will be reassessed (figure 1, table 1). There can be unscheduled visits due to deterioration of symptoms or adverse drug reactions. Adverse drug reactions will be noted down and managed according to severity. Causality assessment will be done for adverse drug reactions by using the WHO-UMC system.

Evaluation of medication adherence

At the 12 week follow-up, all patients will be instructed to return the medication strips, and compliance will be evaluated using the pill-count method. If a patient’s medication compliance is found to be ≥80%, they are considered to be adherent.

Plan for statistical analysis

Continuous data will be presented as a mean and SD or median and IQR. Categorical variables will be expressed as percentages or proportions. The statistical analysis will be done using the R software.32 A p-value of 0.05 or below will be considered for determining statistical significance. The intention-to-treat (ITT) principle will be used for analyses. Multiple imputations will be employed to deal with missing values, and the pooled data will be used for analysis. To find out the group difference of categorical variables, the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test will be used (χ2 test, when <20% of cells have expected frequency <5 and Fisher’s exact test when>20% of cells have expected frequency of <5). For comparing means/medians, the unpaired t-test / Mann-Whitney U test (between the groups) and two-sided paired t-test/ Wilcoxon signed-rank test (within the group) will be used. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient will be used to examine the relationship between two parameters. A linear regression will be done to analyse the factors affecting the primary outcome parameter, and a multivariable logistic regression model will be used to analyse the factors affecting the treatment response.

Data management

A case report form (CRF) (presented in the online supplemental material) will be used to record all clinical and demographic information, and each recruited patient’s sequence code will be used for data entry. Once a week, one investigator will check the clinical data. Only authorised investigators will have access to the signed consent forms, which will be appended to each individual’s CRF. A designated investigator will access and examine the final datasheet.

Discussion

Emerging evidence indicates that mitochondrial dysfunction and disturbed cerebral energy metabolism may play a role in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that certain antipsychotic drugs, particularly those targeting respiratory complex I, induce mitochondrial toxicity, leading to reduced cellular ATP levels and viability. Given the critical role of mitochondrial dysfunction in neurodegenerative processes, including neuronal pathways involved in movement, this mechanism is a significant contributor to drug-induced extrapyramidal symptoms. Understanding the relationship between antipsychotics and mitochondrial dysfunction is vital for improving schizophrenia treatment and prognosis. While preclinical evidence supports this association, clinical validation is limited to a single cross-sectional study to date. This randomised controlled trial aims to address the knowledge gaps on the effect of atypical antipsychotic drugs on mitochondrial dysfunction in schizophrenia. This study intends to assess and compare the effect of both risperidone and aripiprazole on mitochondrial dysfunction in treatment-naïve patients or patients who had not been on antipsychotics for 4 weeks.

The present study will be the first to conduct a clinical trial to systematically evaluate platelet mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I concentration in schizophrenia patients treated with risperidone or aripiprazole. While preclinical findings, such as those by Hardy et al, demonstrate that antipsychotics can inhibit mitochondrial complex I, the clinical relevance of these findings remains uncertain. The comprehensive approach of the trial, combining clinical scales with biochemical markers of mitochondrial function, offers a robust framework for elucidating these relationships.

By including a baseline comparison with healthy controls, this study also seeks to evaluate schizophrenia-associated mitochondrial dysfunction in treatment-naïve patients or patients who had not been on antipsychotics for 4 weeks. The evaluation of secondary outcomes, including serum lactate, pyruvate and creatine kinase levels, will provide further insight into the impacts on biochemical markers. Additionally, the study incorporates a safety evaluation to monitor treatment-emergent adverse events, enabling a better understanding of the correlation between mitochondrial dysfunction and adverse drug reactions.

Despite its strengths, this trial has certain limitations. The single-centre design may limit the generalisability of findings to broader populations. Furthermore, the 12 week follow-up period, while sufficient for assessing short-term effects, may not capture long-term mitochondrial or clinical outcomes. Adherence to treatment, monitored through pill counts, may not fully account for variations in patient compliance. Additionally, the lack of blinding could introduce bias, although objective biochemical markers serve to mitigate this risk.

The anticipated findings of this trial have significant implications for clinical practice and future research. Should mitochondrial dysfunction be confirmed as a mediating factor in antipsychotic-induced adverse effects, this would provide a rationale for developing therapeutic strategies to mitigate such effects. For example, adjunctive treatments aimed at enhancing mitochondrial function could potentially improve patient outcomes. Furthermore, our findings could guide the selection of antipsychotics based on their mitochondrial toxicity profiles, enabling more personalised treatment approaches in schizophrenia.

In conclusion, this randomised controlled trial represents a significant step forward in understanding the complex relationship between antipsychotic medications and mitochondrial function in schizophrenia. Addressing a critical gap in literature has the potential to inform clinical practice, improve treatment outcomes, and guide future research directions. Future studies should build on these findings by incorporating longer follow-up periods, larger sample sizes and a broader range of antipsychotic medications. Such efforts would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the long-term impacts of antipsychotic treatment on mitochondrial health, ultimately contributing to the development of safer and more effective therapeutic strategies for schizophrenia.
