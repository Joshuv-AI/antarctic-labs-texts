Machine learning for predictive risk stratification in recurrent miscarriage: a systematic review
Authors: Maryam Imani, Fateme Sadeghi-Nodoushan, Safiyehsadat Heydari, Somaye Dehghani-Sanij, Farzaneh Fesahat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent miscarriage (RM), affecting 1–2% of couples, often lacks a clear etiology, complicating prognosis and care. Machine learning (ML) offers a potential paradigm shift by enabling robust risk stratification. This systematic review synthesizes the current landscape of ML applications for predicting RM, evaluating methodological quality, performance, and clinical translatability.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science until 9 December 2024. Studies developing or validating ML models for predicting RM or related pregnancy loss were included. Data on study characteristics, datasets, predictive features, ML methodologies, validation approaches, and performance metrics were extracted. Quality was assessed using the CHARMS tool.ResultsTwenty-six studies were included. Most were retrospective (n = 24) and originated from China (n = 14). Dataset sizes varied widely (n ≈ 78 to 338,904). Models incorporated diverse data, from clinical demographics (85% of studies) to embryological morphokinetics and omics biomarkers (26%). Tree-based ensemble algorithms, particularly Random Forest and XGBoost, demonstrated consistently high performance. While reported discriminative performance was often promising (AUC frequently > 0.80), critical methodological gaps were identified. There was a pervasive lack of robust external validation; most studies relied solely on internal validation. Reporting of calibration and handling of missing data was frequently inadequate, hindering assessment of clinical reliability.ConclusionML shows substantial promise for transforming RM prediction through multi-factorial risk stratification. However, the field’s current evolution from proof-of-concept to clinical tool is hampered by a significant validation and reproducibility gap. Background

Recurrent miscarriage (RM), defined as the loss of two or more clinically recognized pregnancies before the 20th week of gestation, represents a profound clinical challenge and a deeply personal tragedy for affected couples [1]. It affects approximately 1–2% of women of reproductive age, signifying a significant reproductive health issue with multifaceted etiologies, including genetic, anatomical, endocrine, immunological, and thrombophilic factors [2]. Despite extensive diagnostic workups, a definitive cause remains elusive in nearly 50% of cases, often termed “unexplained RM,” which complicates prognosis and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies [3], and the ability to provide accurate, personalized prognostic information.

The traditional approach to managing RM has largely been reactive. Clinicians often rely on statistical models based on limited clinical parameters, which may lack the complexity and predictive power to accurately stratify individual risk and identify individuals at highest risk for subsequent pregnancy loss. This limitation underscores the pressing need for a paradigm shift towards more predictive, personalized, and preventive care in reproductive medicine, enabled by robust risk stratification tools [4].

In this context, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have emerged as transformative technologies with the potential to revolutionize healthcare. Unlike conventional statistical methods, ML algorithms can autonomously learn from and identify intricate, non-linear patterns within large, high-dimensional datasets [5]. This capability is particularly suited to complex conditions like RM, where the interplay of numerous factors contributes to the outcome. By integrating diverse data types—from clinical history and laboratory results to advanced embryological imaging and molecular biomarkers—ML models can develop robust predictive tools. These tools aim to stratify patients based on their individual risk, potentially enabling earlier interventions, personalized treatment plans, and improved counseling [6].

Recent years have witnessed a surge in research applying ML to various aspects of reproductive medicine, including embryo selection [7, 8], prediction of IVF success [9], and obstetric complications [10]. The application of ML for predicting miscarriage and RM is a rapidly growing field of research [11, 12]. Studies have investigated a wide array of predictive features, such as maternal characteristics, endocrine profiles, immunogenetic markers, and embryo morphology, utilizing algorithms ranging from logistic regression and support vector machines to advanced ensemble methods and deep learning [13, 14].

However, this rapidly evolving field remains fragmented, lacking a comprehensive synthesis of the evidence. To address this gap, this systematic review provides a critical appraisal and consolidation of existing studies on ML-based prediction models for RM. The specific objectives were to: map the characteristics of the datasets and predictive features; identify and categorize the employed ML algorithms; evaluate methodological aspects including model validation and handling of data imbalance; synthesize reported performance metrics; and discuss clinical translatability and future research directions.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline. A protocol for this review was registered with Prospero registration: CRD420251176304.

Search strategy and study selection

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was performed in three electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science (ISI). The search aimed to identify all relevant studies published from inception until 9 December 2024. The search strategy combined keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to the core concepts of “machine learning” (e.g., “Artificial Intelligence,” “Deep Learning,” “Supervised Machine Learning”) AND “recurrent miscarriage” (e.g., “Recurrent Pregnancy Loss,” “Abortion, Habitual,” “Early Pregnancy Loss”). The complete search syntax for each database is detailed in Table 1, and retrieved results can be accessed via Supplementary File S1. To ensure literature saturation, the reference lists of all included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened.

Exclusion criteria encompassed review articles, editorials, animal studies, case reports, non-English publications, and studies where the full text was unavailable or where miscarriage was not a primary outcome.

The study selection process was managed using EndNote reference management software. After removing duplicates, two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all identified records against the eligibility criteria. The full texts of potentially relevant articles were then retrieved and assessed independently by the same two reviewers. Any disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included prediction modeling studies were critically appraised using the Checklist for critical Appraisal and data extraction for systematic Reviews of prediction Modelling Studies (CHARMS) [16].

The CHARMS tool was applied independently by two reviewers to assess key domains of potential bias, including participant selection, source of data, outcomes, predictors, sample size, handling of missing data, and model evaluation (particularly optimism in model performance). Discrepancies in the quality assessment were resolved by consensus. The results of this appraisal are narratively summarized in the results and discussion sections to contextualize the findings.

Following consultation with the third reviewer, one study (Hiba Asri et al., [15]) was excluded from the final list of included studies.

Results

Overview of included studies and dataset characteristics

The present systematic review included 26 studies that utilized a variety of study designs and data sources to develop machine learning models for miscarriage prediction. The vast majority of these studies employed a retrospective design [7–14, 17–22, 25–34], while a smaller number utilized prospective [24] or cross-sectional approaches [25] design. Geographically, research efforts were predominantly concentrated in China [9–11, 14, 17, 21, 23, 26–30, 32, 33]. Other contributing countries included the United States [23], Italy [12], Japan [7], and several others, with one study providing a multi-country analysis across six East African nations [25].

The scale of the datasets varied widely across studies. Several investigations utilized very large datasets comprising tens of thousands of samples [9] analyzed 31,030 cases and Wang et al. [10] analyzed 25,709 pregnancies, while the study by Setegn et al. [25] included 338,904 instances from demographic health survey data. In contrast, studies focusing on specific molecular biomarkers employed smaller, yet more deeply characterized cohorts, such as Mora-Sánchez et al. [17] with 95 couples and Shen et al. [30] with 78 plasma samples. A notable trend was the increasing use of public omics databases (e.g., GEO) to discover novel diagnostic biomarkers for RPL, as seen in the studies by Wei [11], He [28], and Chu [29] and their colleagues.

Regarding data sources, a significant portion of the studies focused on populations undergoing ART. Data from In Vitro Fertilization–Embryo Transfer (IVF-ET) [9, 20, 30], Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection (ICSI) [18, 25], Single Thawed Euploid Embryo Transfer (STEET) [23], and Preimplantation Genetic Testing for Aneuploidy (PGT-A) cycles [26] were commonly used. Conversely, a number of studies focused on more general natural pregnancy populations [7, 26, 31–34] or utilized large-scale national survey data [13, 25]. This heterogeneity in study design, sample size, and population source reflects the complex nature of miscarriage prediction and underscores the importance of contextualizing model performance within its specific dataset characteristics. Furthermore, the temporal scope of the included studies spanned from 2019 to 2024, with data collection periods ranging from single-year cohorts to comprehensive multi-year datasets covering up to five years. The complete dataset characteristics are available in Supplementary File S3. The research landscape, as visualized in Fig. 2 (Sankey diagram), illustrates the predominant flows from specific countries to particular data types and sample sizes, highlighting the geographical and methodological concentrations within the current evidence base.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria of the reviewed studies

The inclusion and exclusion criteria across the reviewed studies reflected a focused effort to define homogeneous patient populations for model development, primarily centered on women with a history of RM or those undergoing ART. The most common inclusion criterion for RM studies was a history of two or more [12, 21, 28] or three or more [16] unexplained pregnancy losses, often adhering to established guidelines like those from ESHRE [12]. Studies focusing on miscarriage in ART populations typically included women with confirmed pregnancies following procedures such as IVF-ET [9, 21, 30], ICSI [28], or PGT-A cycles [26], with a confirmed positive biochemical pregnancy or fetal heartbeat being a frequent requirement [8, 18].

A consistent theme in the exclusion criteria was the meticulous effort to rule out known confounding causes of miscarriage. The majority of studies explicitly excluded participants with anatomical uterine anomalies, parental chromosomal abnormalities, endocrine disorders, and autoimmune diseases [22, 27, 29]. Other commonly applied exclusion criteria encompassed multiple gestations [10, 27, 34], iatrogenic or medically indicated pregnancy terminations [10, 18, 27], the presence of systemic illnesses (e.g., severe cardiovascular, liver, or renal diseases) [21, 28, 31, 33], and incomplete medical records or loss to follow-up [10, 13, 17, 20]. Furthermore, studies often restricted their cohorts to singleton pregnancies [9, 18, 32, 33] and specified an upper age limit for female participants to minimize age-related confounding factors [17].

The inclusion and exclusion criteria applied across the reviewed studies are summarized in Fig. 3, while the complete detailed criteria for each individual study are available in Supplementary File S3. The inclusion and exclusion criteria across the 26 studies followed the PICOS framework. The predominant inclusion criteria were: women with ≥ 2 recurrent miscarriages (85% of studies), ART populations (59%), and confirmed pregnancy (100%). Major exclusion criteria encompassed known causes of miscarriage (78%), multiple gestations (67%), and systemic comorbidities (74%).

The application of these stringent criteria (summarized in Fig. 3) ensured that the developed ML models were trained on well-defined populations, thereby increasing the internal validity of the findings for specific patient subgroups. As illustrated in Fig. 3, this focus on homogeneous populations also suggests that the generalizability of the models to more heterogeneous populations or those with known, complex etiologies of miscarriage may be limited and requires further external validation.

Predictive features and model outcomes

The features utilized for model development demonstrated remarkable heterogeneity, ranging from basic clinical and demographic variables to deep phenotyping of embryos and molecular biomarkers. The initial feature sets varied widely, from a single immunogenetic score [17] to over a thousand transcriptomic features [11]. Among clinical predictors, maternal age [7, 12, 20, 21, 30], Body Mass Index (BMI) [7, 12, 25, 30], and reproductive history were commonly employed across multiple studies [14, 20–22, 27]. A significant number of investigations leveraged specialized data, such as morphokinetic parameters from time-lapse imaging [19], and molecular markers including specific hormone levels and gene expression profiles [9, 11, 18, 28, 29].

To manage this high dimensionality and enhance model generalizability, a variety of feature selection methods were employed. Classical statistical techniques like t-tests and chi-square tests were common for initial filtering [20, 22, 34], while advanced methods like LASSO regression [11, 13, 27] and Random Forest-based importance ranking [10, 11, 28] were also widely used. Furthermore, model-agnostic interpretation tools like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) were utilized not only to select features but also to provide post-hoc interpretability for the model predictions [18, 25, 32].

The predicted outcomes were clearly defined and aligned with clinical endpoints. The primary outcome for the majority of studies was a form of pregnancy loss, most notably Recurrent Pregnancy Loss (RPL) or Recurrent Miscarriage (RM) [12, 17, 22]. Other studies predicted specific types of loss, such as Biochemical Pregnancy Loss (BPL) [26, 30], first-trimester miscarriage [8, 19], missed abortion [14, 21], or spontaneous abortion in the second trimester [31]. Several models were also developed to predict related obstetric complications, including spontaneous preterm birth (sPTB) [32, 33] and preeclampsia (PE) [10]. In the context of ART, common outcomes included live birth (LB) [7] and clinical pregnancy [24]. This diversity in both predictive features and target outcomes underscores the multi-faceted approach of ML in addressing the complex challenge of predicting adverse pregnancy events. Clinical and demographic parameters constituted the most frequently utilized feature category (85% of studies), followed by laboratory biomarkers (48%) and omics data (26%). The predominant feature selection methods included LASSO regression (33%) and tree-based importance analysis (26%). The primary predicted outcome was recurrent pregnancy loss (37%), with early miscarriage (30%) and biochemical pregnancy loss (15%) also being commonly investigated. Machine learning algorithms and software platforms

A diverse array of ML algorithms was employed across the reviewed studies, ranging from traditional statistical models to advanced ensemble and deep learning methods. The most frequently utilized algorithms included Logistic Regression (LR) [9, 18, 20, 21, 27, 31, 32], Random Forest (RF) [9, 11, 14, 18, 19, 25–27], and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [9, 18, 19, 25–27, 31, 33, 34]. Other commonly applied models were Support Vector Machines (SVM) [9, 12, 18, 26, 28, 29, 34] and various forms of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) including Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) [18, 26] and more complex architectures like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [7, 13, 23]. A notable trend was the comparative evaluation of multiple algorithms within a single study to identify the best-performing model for a specific prediction task [10, 18, 26, 31]. The study by Mora-Sánchez et al. utilized a specialized immunogenetic matching algorithm (IMMATCH) based on peptide-binding affinity and AI-based classification [17].

When the “best” algorithm was reported, no single model universally outperformed all others, though tree-based ensemble methods demonstrated consistently high performance. Random Forest was identified as the top-performing model in several studies for predicting outcomes like pregnancy loss [9, 14] and biochemical pregnancy loss [26], as well as in large-scale demographic studies [24]. Similarly, XGBoost was frequently reported as the best or one of the best models for predicting miscarriage in various contexts [5, 18, 26, 30, 32, 33]. In other instances, AdaBoost [10] and specialized deep learning models like DRIM-Net [13] or proprietary algorithms such as iDAScore [7] and ERICA [8] achieved the highest predictive accuracy for their respective tasks.

The software environment for model development was predominantly based on Python and R, reflecting their extensive ecosystem of data science and ML libraries. Python was often used with libraries such as scikit-learn, XGBoost, TensorFlow, PyTorch, and interpretation tools like SHAP [10, 18, 19, 25, 27, 32, 33]. R was commonly employed for statistical analysis and specific bioinformatics pipelines, utilizing packages like glmnet, randomForest, and caret [11, 23, 24, 29, 30]. Additionally, proprietary software and platforms, including SPSS [20, 21, 26, 29, 31–33] and specialized clinical AI tools like the PGTai 2.0 platform [23], were also utilized, particularly for clinical data analysis and validated commercial applications (Table 3).

Data preprocessing and model validation methods

The methodological approaches to data preprocessing and validation varied significantly across the studies, revealing a concerning lack of standardization and comprehensive reporting in key methodological areas. For model validation, internal validation techniques were commonly employed, with k-fold cross-validation (particularly 5-fold or 10-fold) being a prevalent strategy to mitigate overfitting and provide a more reliable estimate of model performance [12, 14, 18, 20, 22, 25, 27, 32]. However, a critical finding was the scarcity of robust external validation; only a small subset of studies strengthened their findings by validating models on temporally [7] or geographically [10, 31, 33] distinct cohorts. Many studies relied solely on a simple hold-out train-test split [5, 9, 28, 30, 34] or did not explicitly report their validation method [8, 11, 23, 24].

Similarly, the reporting of data preprocessing steps was often inadequate. The handling of missing data was frequently underreported, with complete-case analysis being a common approach [7, 9, 24, 30, 32–34]. When imputation was used, methods were typically basic [21, 26, 32]. Feature normalization—a critical step for many ML algorithms—was explicitly mentioned in only a few studies [10, 11, 23, 30, 33].

Furthermore, model calibration was infrequently evaluated, and when reported, was primarily assessed visually using calibration curves [27–29, 31, 33, 34]. This collective under-reporting of validation rigor, preprocessing, and calibration highlights a significant methodological gap that must be addressed to ensure the clinical reliability and interpretability of predictive risk models. Model evaluation metrics

The evaluation of model performance utilized a wide spectrum of metrics, reflecting the need to assess different aspects of predictive power, particularly in the context of imbalanced datasets common in miscarriage prediction. The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC/ROC) was the most frequently reported metric, employed by the vast majority of studies [9–11, 17, 18, 20, 21, 26–34]. This metric provides a comprehensive measure of a model’s ability to discriminate between classes across all possible classification thresholds.

Beyond the AUC, a core set of classification metrics was commonly used to describe performance at a specific decision threshold. Accuracy was widely reported [9, 10, 12, 17, 18, 20–27, 29–32], though its value can be misleading in imbalanced datasets. More informative metrics for clinical settings, such as Sensitivity (Recall or True Positive Rate) and Specificity (True Negative Rate), were also frequently presented [10, 14, 17, 19–21, 26, 27, 29–33], as they quantify the model’s ability to correctly identify cases with and without the outcome, respectively. To provide a balanced view between precision and recall, the F1-Score was reported in several studies [9, 10, 20, 21, 25, 27, 31, 33].

A smaller subset of studies employed more advanced metrics suitable for nuanced performance assessment. The Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), which is a robust metric for binary classification on imbalanced data, was used in a few instances [14, 27, 32]. Metrics like the Positive Predictive Value (PPV/Precision) and Negative Predictive Value (NPV) [9, 19, 26, 27, 33], which are highly relevant to clinical decision-making, were also reported. Some studies complemented the ROC curve with a Precision-Recall Curve (PRC) [17, 27], which is often more informative than the ROC curve when dealing with significant class imbalance. The variety of metrics used underscores the multifaceted nature of evaluating predictive models intended for a clinical context, where both overall discrimination and specific operational characteristics are critical (Table 5).

Discussion

Positioning ML in the clinical spectrum of RM care

In contrast to the expanding role of ML in diagnostic imaging and refined clinical prediction in other obstetric domains [35, 36], yet a critical need remains for precise, individualized prognostic guidance, especially in unexplained RM cases [3, 12]. Concurrently, ML has evolved from a research tool to an integrated component of obstetric and gynecologic practice, providing data-driven support for risk assessment in areas such as preeclampsia screening and fetal imaging [36–38]. Of the 26 reviewed studies, the vast majority focus on prediction, operating within the preventive domain of care and addressing two primary clinical needs: risk assessment prior to pregnancy and monitoring during ongoing gestation. The methodological challenges identified are not unique to RM but reflect broader issues in clinical AI, highlighting predictive modeling as a cross-cutting approach in modern women’s health.

Principal findings

This systematic review synthesized the evidence from 26 studies on the utilization of ML for predicting RM and related pregnancy loss outcomes. The principal finding is a pronounced dichotomy: ML demonstrates considerable technical feasibility for RM risk stratification, with studies reporting strong discriminatory performance (AUC often > 0.80). However, when compared to benchmarks now being set in more advanced obstetric ML applications [34–36], this technical capability needs robust external validation and standardized reporting guidelines (e.g., TRIPOD-ML) and the integration of explainability (XAI) frameworks into both research code and commercial platforms (as seen in the use of platforms like the Coulter DxAI in one of the reviewed studies [26]). Consequently, the field remains in a critical translational phase toward clinical integration.

Translating evidence into action for diverse stakeholders

To maximize the practical impact of this synthesis and guide the field toward clinical utility, we present our findings as an actionable roadmap tailored to three distinct stakeholder groups. This stratification reflects the translational phase of ML in RM prediction, where the path from proof-of-concept to early clinical adoption requires targeted guidance.

An evidence-based starter framework for new investigators

A researcher embarking on an ML project for RM prediction can optimize their approach by adhering to the most consistently reported and successful practices in the current literature. The foundational step should involve assembling a structured dataset of clinical and demographic variables (e.g., maternal age, BMI, reproductive history), as this was the most common and accessible data type, used in 20 of the 26 reviewed studies. For data preprocessing, handling missing values through case exclusion or central value imputation (mean/median) were the predominant methods. Feature selection should employ established techniques; LASSO regression and tree-embedded importance metrics (e.g., from Random Forest) were among the most effective, successfully identifying predictive features in studies of immunologic, endocrine, and morphokinetic data. At the core of model development, our synthesis strongly indicates that tree-based ensemble methods, particularly XGBoost and Random Forest, are the most robust and high-performing algorithms in this domain. They were the top-performing model in 16 of the 26 studies. Model evaluation must mandate robust internal validation using k-fold cross-validation (employed in 18 studies), and performance reporting should extend beyond area under the curve (AUC) to include sensitivity and specificity, due to their direct clinical relevance for risk stratification. However, the new investigator must be acutely aware of the field’s most critical translational gap: only 6 studies (23%) performed external validation and model calibration. Thus, the immediate output of this framework is a technical research-grade model. Figure 4 provides a visual roadmap for the field. Also, a detailed, step-by-step flowchart is provided (Supplementary File S4).

A Roadmap for machine learning in recurrent miscarriage Prediction

Lessons from current best practices for implementing teams

For teams seeking to implement models with the strongest current evidence, our review identifies optimal data-algorithm combinations contextualized by clinical scenario. For general RM prediction, XGBoost applied to structured clinical data has the most frequent record of high performance (AUC > 0.80 in studies [9, 18, 24, 26, 27]). Within the specialized context of ART and embryo selection, deep learning models, specifically CNNs applied directly to morphological or time-lapse images (e.g., iDAScore), represent the current technological frontier for raw image data. For biomarker discovery using transcriptomic or other omics data, a hybrid strategy of rigorous feature selection via LASSO or WGCNA followed by a powerful classifier like XGBoost or an ANN proved successful in several studies (e.g., studies [11, 15, 19]). The technical ecosystem is dominated by Python (scikit-learn, XGBoost, PyTorch/TensorFlow), with R used complementarily for omics and statistical analysis.

Future frontiers for advanced developers

The most urgent methodological priority is to address the translational chasm. This requires mandating prospective external validation in multi-center cohorts, rigorously evaluating and reporting model calibration, systematically addressing class imbalance (e.g., with SMOTE, used in 5 studies), and formally integrating explainability (XAI) tools like SHAP (used in 7 studies) into the development lifecycle. Furthermore, a critical observation from our synthesis is the prevailing imbalance between technical and clinical model evaluation. While discrimination metrics (e.g., AUC) are routinely reported, calibration (reported in 6 studies) and formal clinical utility analysis (e.g., decision curve analysis) are notably absent. For predictive models to responsibly inform monitoring or intervention decisions in RM, assessing the reliability of their probability estimates and their net benefit over standard care is fundamental [39, 40]. Beyond these steps, future technological frontiers include exploring multi-modal data fusion (clinical, imaging, omics) and reinforcement learning for modeling risk across sequential pregnancies, which could directly address the profound uncertainty in unexplained RM.

Conclusion

This systematic review maps the progress of ML toward integration into the clinical management of RM. The current literature positions ML primarily as a tool for enhanced risk stratification and monitoring during ongoing pregnancy, addressing a key prognostic need after initial diagnosis. Mirroring the established adoption of ML in other obstetric domains, the field has converged on effective technical standards: tree-based ensembles like XGBoost for structured clinical data, and deep learning models for embryo imaging analysis.

To align with the implementation readiness now expected in applied obstetric ML, future research must consolidate these advances by prioritizing key translational benchmarks. These include the adoption of multi-center external validation protocols, comprehensive model calibration, and formal assessments of clinical utility. By focusing on these established next steps, ML for RM prediction can solidify its place within the routine clinical toolkit, ultimately contributing to more informed counseling and improved outcomes for affected individuals and couples.
