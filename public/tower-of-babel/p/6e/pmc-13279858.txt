Piezoelectric Surface Charge and Dynamic Stimulation Synergize to Promote Cardiac Myoblast Alignment and Maturation
Authors: Rafaela M Meira, Sylvie Ribeiro, Senentxu Lanceros‐Mendez, Clarisse Ribeiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis work investigates the role of surface charge and synergistic mechano‐electrical stimulation of piezoelectric poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) on the behaviour of H9c2 cardiac cells. H9c2 cells preferentially adhered to poled PVDF samples, regardless of polarity, indicating that increased average surface charge creates a favourable environment for initial attachment. Proliferation assays showed that negatively charged PVDF films promote the highest early growth, while dynamic mechanical stimulation (1 h/day) further enhanced proliferation, particularly on positively poled PVDF, highlighting a synergistic effect between charge and mechanical cues. Differentiation assays demonstrated that stimulation applied during either the proliferation or differentiation phase promoted myotube formation, increasing length, width, and alignment, with negatively poled PVDF supporting the most advanced maturation. At the molecular level, qPCR confirmed upregulation of the early cardiomyogenic marker actc1, whereas the late marker Tnnt2 remained unchanged, indicating partial but incomplete cardiomyocyte differentiation. These results reveal that both static polarization and dynamic piezoelectric stimulation cooperatively modulate cell fate decisions, integrating electrical and mechanical signalling pathways. Together, these findings demonstrate that surface polarization and mechano‐electrical cues of piezoelectric biomaterials act in concert to regulate cardiomyoblast adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, unveiling charge‐dependent design principles for multifunctional electroactive materials aimed at cardiac tissue regeneration.Keywords: bioreactors, cardiac tissue regeneration, mechano‐electrical stimulation, piezoelectric poly(vinylidene fluoride) biomaterials, surface charge

Introduction

The heart, as a central component of the circulatory system, is responsible for pumping blood throughout the body [1]. In addition to delivering nutrients, hormones, and oxygen to cells, circulated blood also collects carbon dioxide and other cellular waste products for elimination [2]. Despite breakthroughs in the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of cardiovascular diseases, these conditions remain the leading cause of death and disability worldwide [3, 4]. Furthermore, cardiac tissue has limited regenerative capacity; after injury or disease, lost or damaged tissue is progressively replaced by fibrotic scar tissue, compromising cardiac function and often leading to heart failure [5]. For patients with end‐stage heart failure, heart transplantation remains the only therapeutic option [6]. However, limited organ availability and complications related to immunosuppression pose significant challenges [7]. Therefore, new therapeutic strategies are urgently needed, with tissue engineering (TE) representing a promising approach [8, 9].

Through the combination of scaffolds, cells, and bioactive factors, TE allows the development of biological substitutes to restore, maintain, or improve tissue function, enabling tissue regeneration instead of its replacement [10, 11, 12]. As scaffolds play a crucial role in TE strategies, there is a growing interest in designing advanced functional materials, particularly stimuli‐responsive ones, for TE applications. Scaffolds made from these materials provide structural support and active stimuli, mimicking the natural environment to which cells are responsive [11, 13].

Among stimuli‐responsive materials, piezoelectric polymers have garnered increasing attention from the scientific community due to their ability to generate and deliver electrical signals to cells [14, 15]. These signals are particularly beneficial for cells with inherent piezoelectric properties, such as those in bone [16, 17], tendon [18], and skin [19, 20], or cells like muscle [21, 22] and neurons [14, 23, 24], which rely on electrical or mechano‐electrical stimuli for normal function. The most widely used piezoelectric polymers in TE applications are poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) and its copolymer poly(vinylidene fluoride‐co‐trifluoroethylene) (P(VDF‐TrFE)), due to their strong piezoelectric response, biocompatibility, and biostability [25].

PVDF is a semicrystalline polymer exhibiting polymorphism, and depending on processing conditions, it can crystallize into four main phases: α, β, γ, and δ. The electroactive properties of PVDF are governed by the molecular conformation within these crystalline domains. The β‐phase, characterized by an all‐trans (TTTT) planar zigzag configuration, possesses the highest net dipole moment due to the parallel alignment of –CF2 dipoles along the polymer backbone, conferring superior piezoelectric and pyroelectric properties. In contrast, the α‐phase adopts a trans–gauche–trans–gauche′ (TGTG′) conformation, in which consecutive dipoles orient in opposite directions, resulting in dipole cancellation and a non‐polar structure [26]. The crystalline phase composition of PVDF is strongly dependent on processing conditions. The α‐phase is typically obtained by crystallization from the melt over a broad temperature range, whereas the β‐phase is not directly formed from melt crystallization but can be induced through mechanical stretching, electrical poling, solvent casting, or electrospinning. These treatments promote chain alignment and dipole reorientation, often leading to the coexistence of multiple crystalline phases. Consequently, the electroactive performance of PVDF depends on β‐phase content, microstructure, and overall degree of crystallinity [27, 28]. In electrically poled PVDF, the application of a high electric field further aligns dipoles within crystalline domains, stabilizing macroscopic surface polarization and generating persistent surface charges that modulate the local electrical microenvironment at the cell–material interface [26].

Beyond this, these polymers can be fabricated into fibers, films, or porous structures with tailored microstructures and physical properties, allowing the development of biomimetic scaffolds [29]. In TE, PVDF‐based materials have been engineered to enhance bioactivity and electroactivity through strategies such as plasma surface activation [30], incorporation of conductive nanofillers [11], and surface functionalization with extracellular matrix proteins [31].

Previous research has highlighted the influence of PVDF's surface charge on the biological responses of different types of cells. For instance, positively charged PVDF films were shown to enhance MC3T3‐E1 osteoblast adhesion and proliferation [32], while similar effects were observed in SH‐SY5Y neuroblastoma cells [14]. Positively charged PVDF films not only improved neuron‐like cell adhesion and proliferation but also significantly promoted neurite extension and differentiation [14]. Nonetheless, this response is cell‐type specific. For instance, C2C12 myoblast cells adhered and proliferated more on negatively charged surfaces than on neutral (non‐charged) or positively charged PVDF samples. Moreover, both poled PVDF surfaces were found to enhance C2C12 myoblast differentiation [33].

Despite the well‐documented influence of PVDF surface polarization in osteogenic, neurogenic, and skeletal muscle models, its polarity‐dependent and dynamically coupled role in cardiac myoblast behaviour and maturation remains largely unexplored, particularly considering the intrinsic mechano‐electrical coupling that governs cardiac physiology [34]. Therefore, this work investigates how the surface charge of piezoelectric PVDF films influences H9c2 myoblast adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation. PVDF samples with different surface charges – positive (“poled +”), negative (“poled –”), and neutral (“non‐poled”) – were used to isolate the contribution of polarization polarity. H9c2 myoblasts were selected as an established in vitro model for cardiac muscle, owing to their skeletal‐muscle origin and cardiac‐like phenotype upon differentiation. They exhibit spontaneous expression of cardiac‐specific markers and are highly responsive to electrical and mechanical cues, making them particularly suitable for studying electroactive and mechano‐responsive biomaterial effects on cardiac cell behaviour [35]. Unlike previous studies that focused either on compositional modification or static polarization alone, the present work combines controlled surface charge polarity with temporally defined mechanical stimulation of a piezoelectric substrate, enabling the dissection of static versus dynamically generated electroactive cues in a cardiac cell model.

Additionally, the mechano‐electrical effects of the poled samples were assessed using a home‐made mechanical bioreactor. This apparatus applies controlled mechanical deformation to the piezoelectric materials, which in turn induces transient piezoelectrically generated electrical potentials [14], enabling the assessment of the direct impact of mechano‐electrical stimulation on cardiac cell behaviour. “Non‐poled” PVDF films were employed to isolate the effects of surface charge and mechano‐electrical stimulation in the absence of piezoelectric polarization on H9c2 cell response.

Experimental Section

Materials

Tissue culture coverslips were acquired from Sarstedt. Poled and “non‐poled” PVDF films were acquired from Measurement Specialties (thickness: 120 µm). The polarized films were supplied with a through‐thickness poling state, resulting in opposite surface charges on each side of the film, with the polarization direction indicated by the manufacturer. In this study, the films were positioned such that either the negatively charged surface (“poled –”) or the positively charged surface (“poled +”) was exposed to the cells. The polarization direction of each surface was experimentally confirmed using a d33 meter (model 8000, APC Int Ltd), yielding piezoelectric coefficients in the range of 24–27 pC.N−1, ensuring consistency with the supplier specifications. According to the manufacturer's specifications and previous characterization studies of the same commercial β‐PVDF films [16], the material exhibits a dominant electroactive β‐phase crystalline structure. The films present a degree of crystallinity of approximately 46%, supporting stable electroactive behavior. The “non‐poled” PVDF films exhibited no macroscopic dipole alignment and therefore no net surface polarization. Importantly, poled and non‐poled films share identical chemical composition, thickness, crystallinity, and bulk mechanical properties, differing exclusively in dipole orientation and resulting surface charge state. Their most relevant properties are shown in Figure 1.

Most relevant properties of poled and “non‐poled” PVDF films. The provided data were obtained from [14, 21, 26, 36].

Material Sterilization

“Non‐poled” and poled circular samples with 13 mm in diameter were sterilized by exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light for 1 h (30 min on each side). After UV sterilization, the samples were washed thrice with sterile phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS 1x, pH 7.4) for 5 min each wash.

Cell Culture

H9c2 rat cardiomyoblast cells (ATCC CRL‐1446) were seeded in a 75 cm2 cell culture flask containing Dulbecco´s Modified Eagles Medium (DMEM) high glucose (4.5 g.L−1) (PAN‐Biotech) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Biochrom) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (P/S, Biochrom), at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. The culture medium was replaced with fresh growth medium every two days until H9c2 reached approximately 70% confluency. At this point, the cells were trypsinized using 0.05% trypsin‐EDTA (Biochrom, Berlin, Germany).

Focal Adhesions

The focal adhesions of H9c2 cells seeded at a density of 4000 cells per sample on different PVDF samples were analysed by immunofluorescence staining after 24 h of cell seeding. The H9c2 cells were first washed with sterile PBS 1x (pH 7.4) for 5 min, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Panreac) for 15 min at 37°C. After this, the cells were rinsed with PBS solution for 5 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 10 min at room temperature. Next, the cells were incubated with anti‐vinculin−FITC antibody (Sigma–Aldrich) (1:50 in PBS 1x) for 1 h, followed by two washes with PBS 1x solution (5 min/wash). The cells were then stained with 0.1 µg.mL−1 of tetramethylrhodamine B isothiocyanate (TRITC, Hello Bio) for 45 min. After two additional PBS 1x washes (5 min/wash), the cells were stained with 1 µg.mL−1 of 4',6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI, Sigma–Aldrich) for 5 min. Finally, the samples were washed twice with PBS 1x (5 min/wash) and once with distilled water before being mounted on glass slides with Fluoroshield (Sigma–Aldrich). All staining procedures were conducted at room temperature in the dark. The images were acquired using an Olympus BX63 microscope, and the cell aspect ratio was calculated for over 50 cells using ImageJ software from the fluorescence images. The results are expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (S.E.M).

Cell Proliferation Assays Under Static and Dynamic Conditions

H9c2 myoblast cells were seeded on top of the films (cell density = 2.5 × 104 cells/mL) and incubated at 37°C in 95% humidified air containing 5% CO2 for 24 h. After the incubation time (T0), one 24‐well plate was maintained under static conditions (cell culture without stimulation), while the other was transferred to a home‐made bioreactor system for dynamic culture, containing an equal number of “poled +”, “poled –,” and “non poled” PVDF samples to ensure all surface charge conditions were simultaneously subjected to mechanical stimulation. In the bioreactor, cells were subjected to mechano‐electrical stimulation at a frequency of 1 Hz for 1 h per day, for up to 72 h (Figure 2). These specific stimulation parameters were selected based on previous studies showing that a frequency of 1 Hz (≈ 60 beats per minute) reproduces the physiological human heart rate and efficiently promotes cardiac cell alignment and maturation without excessive stress. Although the heart rate of rodents is typically higher (2–3 Hz, ≈ 120–180 bpm), 1 Hz is widely adopted in vitro to maintain standard, reproducible conditions and to emulate the human cardiac cycle under controlled mechanical and electrical cues [34]. Moreover, the stimulation was applied for 1 h per day over 72 h to allow daily activation–recovery cycles that mimic the intermittent mechanical environment of the myocardium and to prevent cellular fatigue or apoptosis associated with continuous loading. Such short, periodic stimulation has been shown to promote cytoskeletal organization, sarcomeric alignment, and early differentiation of H9c2 cells while maintaining high cell viability [37].

The 48 and 72 h time points were selected to evaluate early proliferation dynamics and to capture surface charge‐ and stimulation‐dependent effects before reaching high confluence levels. After 48 and 72 h of cell culture under both static and dynamic conditions, the proliferation of the H9c2 cells was evaluated by resazurin assay. At each time point, the samples were transferred to a new 24‐well plate and incubated with 10% (v/v) resazurin solution for 2 h and 30 min at 37°C. After the incubation time, 100 µL from each well was transferred to a 96‐well plate, and fluorescence was measured (λex 560 nm/λem 590 nm) using a microplate reader (Biotech Synergy HT). Cell Morphology

After 48 h of cell culture under static and dynamic conditions, the morphology of H9c2 myoblast cells cultured on the different PVDF samples was analysed through immunofluorescence staining. The H9c2 cells were first washed with sterile PBS 1x (pH 7.4) for 5 min, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Panreac) for 15 min at 37°C. After this, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 10 min at room temperature. The cells were subsequently stained with 0.1 µg.mL−1 of TRITC for 45 min and briefly washed with PBS 1x before DAPI staining. The DAPI staining (1 µg.mL−1) was performed for 5 min, followed by two washes with PBS 1x (5 min/wash) and one wash with distilled water. Finally, the samples were mounted on glass slides with Fluoroshield. All staining procedures were performed at room temperature in the dark. Morphological analysis was carried out using an Olympus BX63 microscope.

Cell Differentiation Assays Under Static and Dynamic Conditions

H9c2 myoblast cells were seeded on top of the different films (“non‐poled”, “poled +” and “poled –” PVDF samples) at a cell density = 5 × 104 cells/mL and incubated at 37°C in 95% humidified air containing 5% CO2. As illustrated in Figure 3, three dynamic culture conditions were tested in this study: 1 – stimuli on proliferation: cells were stimulated 1 h per day initially and then maintained under static conditions until the end of the experiment; 2 – stimuli on differentiation: cells were cultured under static conditions until confluence was reached (approximately four days), after which they were stimulated 1 h per day throughout the end of the experiment; and 3 – stimuli on proliferation and differentiation: cells were stimulated for 1 h per day throughout the entire experiment. All conditions were conducted over an 8‐day culture period. For comparison, parallel cultures were maintained entirely under static conditions. In all experiments, the cell culture medium was replaced every two days.

Schematic representation of the H9c2 cell differentiation assays conducted under static (no stimulation) and the different dynamic conditions (mechano‐electrical stimulation).

Immunofluorescence Staining of Myosin Heavy Chain

To evaluate the differentiation of H9c2 cardiomyoblasts into cardiomyocytes, cells cultured on the different PVDF samples were stained with DAPI and myosin heavy chain (MHC), a cardiac differentiation marker, at the end of the 8 days of differentiation experiments, and visualized by fluorescence microscopy. The H9c2 cells were first washed with sterile PBS 1x for 5 min before undergoing fixation with a solution of 70% ethanol, 37% formaldehyde, and glacial acetic acid (20:2:1) for 10 min at 4°C. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with a blocking solution containing 5% goat serum and 0.3% Triton X‐100 in PBS 1x for 1 h at room temperature. Following this step, the cells were incubated with a monoclonal mouse IgG2B Clone MF20 antibody (R&D Systems, Catalog No. MAB4470) diluted 1:50 in 5% goat serum in PBS 1x for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The cells were then washed thrice with 0.5% PBS‐Tween 20 and labelled with an anti‐mouse IgG‐NL557 secondary antibody (R&D Systems) diluted 1:200 in 5% goat serum in PBS 1x, at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After this, the cells were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg.mL−1) for 5 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by washing with 0.5% PBS‐Tween 20 and distilled water. Finally, the samples were carefully detached from the 24‐well plate, mounted on glass slides with Fluoroshield, and visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX63).

Calculation of Fusion and Maturation Indexes, Myotube Length, Width, and Orientation

Fusion and maturation indexes were calculated in H9c2 cells immunostained with DAPI and MHC. Myotubes were defined as differentiated cells containing more than two nuclei. The fusion index was determined as the ratio of nuclei in myotubes with two or more nuclei to the total number of nuclei. The maturation index was defined as the proportion of myotubes containing five or more nuclei. For all quantifications, approximately twelve images were analysed for each condition. Myotube length, width, and orientation were also measured in more than 125 myotubes per condition using ImageJ software from fluorescence images. Data are presented as mean ± S.E.M.

Ribonucleic Acid Isolation and Gene Expression Analysis

After 8 days of cell culture, H9c2 cells were washed with sterile PBS 1x for 5 min before being harvested with TRIzol (Invitrogen), and ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted from the cell pellet of each condition using the RNAqueous Micro Kit (Invitrogen). The quality and quantity of the isolated RNA samples were assessed with an Infinite M nano plate reader (Tecan) by measuring absorbance at 260–280 nm. Reverse transcription was performed using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems) with the following conditions: step 1: 25°C for 10 min, step 2: 37°C for 120 min, step 3: 85°C for 5 min, and step 4: 4°C hold. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was conducted using a Bio‐Rad real‐time thermal cycler CFX96 with the SYBR Green method (PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix from Applied Biosystems). The primers used for the amplification are listed in Table 1. The target genes actc1 and Tnnt2 were selected as representative markers of early and late stages of cardiomyogenic differentiation, respectively, based on their well‐established roles in sarcomeric organization and contractile function of cardiomyocytes [38]. Primer sequences were designed according to rat gene sequences available in the NCBI database or adapted from previously validated studies on H9c2 differentiation. The following amplification protocol was used: UDG activation at 50°C for 2 min, Activation (Dual‐Lock DNA polymerase) at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s (Denature) and at 60°C for 1 min (Anneal/Extend). The quantification of gene expression was performed using the comparative 2‐ΔCT method, with 18S rRNA as a housekeeping gene. The results are expressed as mean ± S.E.M.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent samples per condition unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± SD for proliferation assays and as mean ± standard S.E.M. for morphological, differentiation assays, and gene expression analyses. Prior to statistical testing, data were visually inspected for consistency and normality assumptions inherent to parametric testing. No data transformation was applied. Outliers were not excluded unless technically justified.

For comparisons involving one independent variable, one‐way ANOVA was performed, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons post hoc test. For experiments involving two independent variables (surface polarization and stimulation condition), two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used. Statistical significance was considered at a confidence level of 95%. Exact p‐values or significance levels are indicated in the corresponding figure captions. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version v9.2.0).

Results and Discussion

“Non‐poled” PVDF films were used as control substrates throughout the study. As these samples share identical chemical composition, crystallinity (46%), thickness (120 µm), and mechanical properties with the polarized films, differing exclusively in dipole orientation, this approach enables the isolation of polarization‐dependent effects without introducing confounding material‐related variables.

As summarized in Figure 1, all PVDF films exhibit comparable crystallinity, dielectric constant, and surface roughness, confirming that microstructural parameters are equivalent across samples. Differences between groups are primarily associated with surface potential and contact angle, which arise from the polarization state of the films.

H9c2 Cell Adhesion

After 24 h of cell culture under static standard conditions, H9c2 myoblast cells seeded on “non‐poled” and poled (positively and negatively charged) PVDF samples were stained to evaluate cell morphology and vinculin expression. The staining included anti‐vinculin FITC for vinculin (green), TRITC for the actin cytoskeleton (red), and DAPI for nuclei (blue). Fluorescence microscopy was used for visualization, and the acquired images are presented in Figure 4a. Fluorescence images reveal that H9c2 cells seeded on “poled +” and “poled ‐” PVDF samples appear more spread and exhibit a more elongated morphology compared to those on “non‐poled” PVDF samples. Regarding vinculin expression, vinculin—a cytoplasmic actin‐binding protein—is observed throughout the cytoplasm of H9c2 myoblast cells across all samples, regardless of the material's surface charge (Figure 4a). To quantify these observations, the cell aspect ratio (Figure 4b) was calculated as a quantitative measure of cell elongation, and it is represented in Figure 4c. The data show that H9c2 cells cultured on positively and negatively charged PVDF samples exhibit a higher aspect ratio, reflecting greater elongation, compared to those on neutral (“non‐poled”) samples. No significant differences were observed in the aspect ratio between “poled +” and “poled ‐” PVDF samples. These findings suggest that, after 24 h of cell culture, H9c2 cells exhibit enhanced elongation on PVDF surfaces with average surface charge, independently of the charge type, indicating that the surface charge of the biomaterial plays a role in modulating cell morphology.

H9c2 Cell Proliferation

To assess the influence of surface charge alone and in combination with mechano‐electrical stimuli on cell proliferation, H9c2 cells were cultured on “non‐poled”, “poled +” and “poled ‐” PVDF samples under static and dynamic culture conditions for up to 72 h. Proliferation was evaluated using the resazurin assay at 48 and 72 h, as shown in Figure 5a.

Dynamic conditions involved mechano‐electrical stimulation at a frequency of 1 Hz for 1 h per day over three consecutive days. The results are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 4). b) Representative immunofluorescence images of H9c2 cells seeded on different PVDF samples after 48 h of cell culture with nuclei stained with DAPI (blue) and actin cytoskeleton stained with TRITC (red). The scale bar (100 µm) is valid for all images. Statistical analysis was performed using Two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test: * p < 0.0332, ** p < 0.0021, *** p < 0.0002 and **** p < 0.0001.

Across both evaluated time points, H9c2 cells proliferated on all PVDF samples under both static and dynamic conditions, though differences in proliferation rates were observed depending on surface charge and mechanical stimulation. After 48 h, under static conditions, “poled ‐” PVDF samples showed significantly higher proliferation rates (≈ 120 ± 5%) compared to “poled +” (≈ 96 ± 2%) and “non‐poled” (≈ 91 ± 7%) PVDF samples. Under dynamic conditions (1 h/day stimulation), an increase in the proliferation rate was observed for poled samples, with no significant differences between positively (≈ 115 ± 14%) and negatively (≈ 138 ± 9%) polarization. In contrast, “non‐poled” PVDF samples showed no significant difference in proliferation between static (≈ 91 ± 7%) and dynamic (≈ 93 ± 6%) conditions. These findings indicate that mechanical stimulation alone does not affect cell proliferation unless accompanied by the macroscopic piezoelectric response (surface charge variation, whether positive or negative) enhanced by the poling of the samples.

At 72 h, variations in proliferation across PVDF samples were maintained under both static and dynamic conditions. Under static conditions, H9c2 cell proliferation was higher on “non‐poled” (≈ 165 ± 11%) and “poled ‐” PVDF samples (≈ 152 ± 3%) compared to “poled +” PVDF samples (≈ 140 ± 9%). This result contrasts with the findings at 48 h, where cells showed a preference for negatively charged PVDF samples. Under dynamic conditions, 1 h/day stimulation further enhanced H9c2 cell proliferation on all PVDF samples except “nonpoled” PVDF samples (≈ 175 ± 27%), which was consistent with the results obtained at 48 h of cell culture. Among polarized samples, “poled +” PVDF samples (≈ 201 ± 9%) significantly promoted higher proliferation than “poled ‐” PVDF samples (≈ 169 ± 9%).

In addition to the proliferation assay, immunofluorescence staining was performed at 48 h to analyse cell morphology (Figure 5b). Fluorescence microscopy images revealed higher cell density on poled PVDF samples under dynamic conditions compared to static conditions, which agrees with the resazurin assay results (Figure 5a). With respect to the effect of surface charge and mechano‐electrical stimulation on H9c2 cell morphology, no significant morphological changes were observed.

Previous characterization of the same β‐PVDF films demonstrated that surface polarization generates measurable and stable electrical potentials. Kelvin Probe Force Microscopy (KPFM) revealed average surface potentials of approximately +6 V for “poled +” and −4 V for “poled –” samples, whereas “non‐poled” PVDF showed near‐neutral potential values [36]. Under mechanical stimulation at 1 Hz (1 h/day), the poled films generate transient piezoelectrically induced potentials of approximately 8.5 mVpp, as previously quantified for equivalent samples under comparable loading conditions [16]. The difference in magnitude and polarity between “poled +” and “poled –” surfaces indicates that the bioelectrical microenvironment experienced by adhering cells depends not only on the sign of polarization but also on the strength and orientation of the induced electric field. Such asymmetry helps explain the distinct biological responses observed between both polarities, confirming that the combined mechanical and electrical cues, rather than mechanical stimulation alone, govern H9c2 cell behaviour.

H9c2 Cell Differentiation

To facilitate interpretation, it is recalled that three dynamic stimulation conditions were tested: stimulation applied during proliferation, during differentiation, or during both stages of culture. This experimental design aimed to distinguish whether the timing of mechano‐electrical cues differentially influences the early and late stages of H9c2 cardiomyogenic differentiation. To evaluate the influence of PVDF surface charge, both alone and in combination with mechano‐electrical stimuli, on H9c2 cell differentiation, the cells were stained with DAPI and myosin heavy chain (MHC), a cardiac differentiation marker, after 8 days of cell differentiation. Immunofluorescence imaging was used for visualization, and representative fluorescent images of H9c2 cells seeded on the different PVDF samples are shown in Figure 6a. The fluorescence images (Figure 6a) confirm that all PVDF samples, regardless of surface charge, support the cardiomyogenic differentiation of H9c2 cells. This is evidenced by MHC expression in all samples after 8 days of cell differentiation under both static and dynamic conditions.

Figure 6 also shows the fusion index (percentage of myotubes with two or more nuclei) (Figure 6b) and the maturation index (percentage of myotubes with five or more nuclei) (Figure 6c) of H9c2 cells. Under static conditions, all PVDF samples exhibited similar fusion index values (Figure 6b). However, when PVDF samples were subjected to mechanical stimulation using a home‐made mechanical bioreactor during the proliferation phase, the fusion index decreased for all samples. Mechanical stimulation during the differentiation phase decreased H9c2 cell fusion in “non‐poled” PVDF samples, while poled PVDF samples showed no significant differences in the fusion index between static and dynamic (stimuli during differentiation) conditions. When mechanical stimulation was applied for 1 h/day during both proliferation and differentiation phases, the fusion index increased in “non‐poled” and “poled +” PVDF samples but decreased in “poled ‐” PVDF samples.

Regarding the maturation index (Figure 6c), no significant differences were found between “non‐poled” and “poled” PVDF samples under static conditions. When mechanical stimulation was applied during the proliferation phase, the maturation index increased for all samples, with “poled ‐” PVDF samples showing the highest value (≈ 44 ± 3%). A similar trend was observed when stimulation was applied only during the differentiation phase. Notably, mechanical stimulation during the differentiation phase enhanced the maturation of H9c2 cells across all PVDF samples, again with “poled ‐” PVDF samples achieving the highest value (≈ 47 ± 2%). In contrast, applying stimulation during both proliferation and differentiation phases did not enhance maturation in poled PVDF samples (values remained similar to static), and even reduced maturation in “non‐poled” samples. This suggests that premature stimulation may compromise the proliferative pool of myoblasts, leading to reduced coordinated maturation.

These results indicate that mechanical stimulation in the differentiation phase promotes both fusion and maturation, whereas stimulation in the proliferation phase primarily enhances maturation while reducing fusion.

A deeper analysis reveals an inverse relationship between fusion and maturation indexes under specific conditions. When mechanical stimulation was applied only during the proliferation phase, a reduction in the fusion index was observed, but the maturation index significantly increased across all samples, particularly in “poled ‐” PVDF. Conversely, when mechanical stimulation was applied during both proliferation and differentiation, an increase in the fusion index was detected in “non‐poled” and “poled +” PVDF samples, but this condition did not further enhance maturation, resulting in values similar to static conditions.

This pattern aligns with the findings of Zevolis et al. [39], who reported that mechanically stimulated H9c2 cells can exhibit a low fusion index coupled with a high maturation index. Their study demonstrated that low‐strain/frequency mechanical loading enhances MHC expression, myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs) such as MyoD and Myf6, and hypertrophic markers, promoting maturation. Additionally, it was observed that mechanical stimulation downregulates apoptotic and atrophic markers, supporting a favourable environment for maturation without excessive fusion into multinucleated structures [39].

Thus, mechanical stimulation can effectively promote H9c2 cell maturation without significantly inducing cell fusion, depending on the time at which the stimulus is applied. Our findings further demonstrate that, independently of PVDF surface charge, mechanical stimulation enhances cell maturation, particularly when applied during the proliferation or differentiation phase, rather than both. Moreover, mechanical stimulation of “poled ‐” PVDF samples during these phases promotes advanced stages of H9c2 cell maturation, reinforcing the role of negative surface charge and the synergistic contribution of piezoelectric (mechano‐electrical) stimulation. In poled PVDF samples, the applied mechanical deformation induces localized surface potentials that modulate cell–substrate interactions and intracellular signaling, amplifying the biological response beyond what is achieved by mechanical stimulation alone. Conversely, stimulation on “non‐poled” PVDF represents a purely mechanical condition, where no electrical component is generated, resulting in less pronounced effects on differentiation and myotube organization. These comparisons clearly indicate that many of the observed cellular responses require the concurrent mechanical and electrical cues provided by the piezoelectric material.

Myotube length is shown in Figure 6d. Under static conditions, both “poled +” and “poled ‐” piezoelectric PVDF samples promote longer myotubes compared to “non‐poled” PVDF samples. Furthermore, it is verified that myotube length is significantly improved when PVDF samples are mechanically stimulated.

A more pronounced increase in myotube length was observed in “poled +” and “poled ‐” PVDF samples under mechanical stimulation, with no major differences between the different stimulation conditions. In “poled +” samples, the longest myotubes were observed when stimulation was applied during both proliferation and differentiation, while in “poled ‐” samples, the greatest length was achieved when stimulation occurred during the proliferation phase. However, the variations between stimulation conditions were relatively small, suggesting that the presence of mechanical cues, rather than the specific phase of application, is the key factor influencing myotube elongation. Nonetheless, the elongation effect was consistently stronger in poled samples, confirming that the additional electrical potential generated by the piezoelectric response of PVDF reinforces cytoskeletal organization and myotube extension, beyond the effect of mechanical deformation alone.

Relatively to myotube width (Figure 6e), it is verified that, under static conditions, “non‐poled” PVDF samples present larger myotubes than “poled +” and “poled ‐” PVDF samples. Furthermore, results indicate that the width of myotubes was increased following mechanical stimulation of PVDF samples, with the exception of the “non‐poled” PVDF samples. Mechanical stimulation of “non‐poled” PVDF samples during the proliferation phase shows no significant effect on myotube width, while stimulation during the differentiation phase increases myotube width, but when applied during both proliferation and differentiation phases, it results in a decrease in myotube width. The pronounced increase in myotube width observed for poled samples under dynamic conditions further highlights the role of piezoelectrically induced potentials in driving late cytoskeletal maturation, whereas purely mechanical stimulation is insufficient to trigger comparable structural reinforcement.

These findings align with the trends observed for cell maturation, reinforcing that “poled ‐” PVDF is the most favorable condition for promoting both advanced differentiation and structural organization of H9c2 myotubes. The results also indicate that mechanical stimulation during the differentiation phase effectively enhances both myotube elongation and width without significantly increasing cell fusion, supporting its role as a key factor in engineering functional muscle‐like tissues.

The orientation of H9c2‐derived myotubes was also determined, and the obtained results are displayed in Figure 7. Under static conditions, “non‐poled” PVDF samples displayed a broad and random distribution of orientations, whereas poled PVDF samples showed a tendency toward preferential alignment. Mechanical stimulation, especially when applied during either proliferation or differentiation phases separately, significantly improved the alignment of myotubes. This effect was most pronounced when stimulation occurred during differentiation, leading to sharper orientation peaks and a higher degree of anisotropy. These findings are consistent with the enhanced maturation and increased myotube width previously observed in Figure 6, reinforcing that surface charge, combined with mechano‐electrical cues, promotes organized myotube architecture.

The gene expression of cardiac α‐actin (actc1), which is an early‐stage differentiation marker for cardiomyocytes [40], and cardiac troponin T (Tnnt2), which is characteristic of mature cardiomyocytes, indicating functional sarcomeric assembly and contractility capacity [35], were evaluated at day 8 in cell cultured under both static and dynamic conditions through qPCR (Figure 8). Concerning actc1 expression, the results indicated that mechanical stimulation improved actc1 expression in all PVDF samples, except for the “poled +” PVDF samples, where mechanical stimulation during the differentiation phase resulted in decreased actc1 expression. This reduction may reflect a shift from early cytoskeletal activation toward later differentiation stages, consistent with the higher morphological maturation observed in “poled +” samples. In the case of Tnnt2, mechanical stimulation did not significantly affect Tnnt2 expression in most PVDF samples, showing similar values to those under static conditions. However, in “poled +” PVDF samples, mechanical stimulation during the differentiation phase increased Tnnt2 expression, while in “poled ‐” PVDF samples, stimulation during both proliferation and differentiation phases also increased Tnnt2 expression. This suggests that surface polarization modulates the responsiveness of late cardiac markers, with “poled –” supporting broader stimulation windows, while “poled +” requires differentiation‐specific cues.

Overall, these findings reveal that H9c2 cells are in an early or partial differentiation stage of cardiomyocytes, in line with the known expression kinetics of these cardiac markers: actc1, an early structural and mechanosensitive gene that responds rapidly to mechanical stimuli [39], while Tnnt2, a marker of sarcomeric maturation and functional contractility, typically requires stronger differentiation cues and longer culture periods to show significant expression [40]. These molecular results are coherent with the morphological analysis (Figures 6 and 7), where enhanced myotube alignment and increased maturation were observed despite limited late marker expression, indicating structural but not fully functional cardiomyocyte differentiation.

To enhance Tnnt2 expression and promote cardiomyocyte maturation toward a more functional cardiac phenotype, further studies should consider using reduced serum content (1% FBS) combined with retinoic acid during H9c2 cell culture [35, 40]. High serum (10% FBS) alone without serum reduction and retinoic acid supplementation maintains H9c2 cells in a proliferative and less differentiated state [35, 40]. Short daily mechanical stimulation may enhance early cytoskeletal gene expression, such as actc1, but alone is insufficient to trigger full cardiomyocyte‐like differentiation.

The combined effect of surface polarization and mechano‐electrical stimulation suggests that transient electrical cues generated at the material–cell interface may influence membrane‐associated signaling processes involved in early cardiomyogenic activation. Although ion channel activity was not directly assessed in this study, the observed modulation of cytoskeletal organization and actc1 expression is consistent with electro‐mechanical regulation of cell membrane dynamics.

Altogether, these findings complement the morphological and structural data, confirming that surface charge and mechano‐electrical cues of the piezoelectric biomaterial accelerate early cardiomyogenic events but remain insufficient to induce full maturation, highlighting the need for complementary biochemical and/or optimized temporal stimulation strategies.

From a translational perspective, the present results provide relevant design guidelines for electroactive substrates in cardiac tissue engineering. Controlled surface polarization, particularly negative polarity (′poled –′), combined with temporally defined mechano‐electrical stimulation, enhances structural maturation and alignment of cardiac myoblasts. Such insights may guide the development of implantable piezoelectric cardiac patches, electroactive coatings, or dynamically stimulated in vitro platforms capable of delivering synchronized mechanical and electrical cues. Importantly, modulation of cell behavior through polarization‐dependent mechanisms without altering the chemical composition of the material represents an advantage in terms of scalability, reproducibility, and regulatory compliance for future device development.

Despite these advances, some limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The biological evaluation was conducted using the H9c2 cardiomyoblast model, which, although widely established for mechanistic studies, does not fully recapitulate the electrophysiological and contractile behaviour of mature cardiomyocytes. In addition, the differentiation period and stimulation conditions were selected to isolate early mechano‐electrical effects, which may partially explain the limited expression of late‐stage markers such as Tnnt2. Furthermore, the study focuses on a well‐defined material platform to decouple surface charge and dynamic stimulation effects, rather than introducing compositional complexity. While this approach enables mechanistic clarity, it does not explore multifunctional material optimization.

Future work should therefore address these aspects by extending the biological validation toward more physiologically relevant models, including primary cardiomyocytes, human induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived cardiomyocytes, and ultimately in vivo cardiac injury models. In parallel, systematic optimization of stimulation parameters, including frequency, amplitude, and temporal profiles, as well as integration with biochemical cues, will be essential to promote full cardiomyocyte maturation. From a materials perspective, combining controlled polarization with multifunctional strategies, such as conductive or bioactive fillers, may further enhance electro‐mechanical coupling and therapeutic performance. These developments will be critical to translate the present findings into clinically relevant electroactive cardiac platforms.

Conclusions

The present work demonstrates the significant influence of PVDF surface charge and mechano‐electrical stimulation on H9c2 cardiomyoblast behaviour. Adhesion assays revealed that cells preferentially adhered to poled PVDF samples, independently of surface charge, indicating that macroscopic surface creates a more favourable electrostatic microenvironment for initial cell attachment. Proliferation assays further confirmed that negatively poled PVDF promoted higher early growth under static conditions, while dynamic stimulation enhanced proliferation on positively poled films, underscoring the synergistic interaction between surface charge and mechanically induced electrical cues.

Differentiation analyses showed that the timing of mechano‐electrical stimulation plays a critical role in modulating myotube formation and maturation. Stimulation applied during either the proliferation or differentiation phase enhanced myotube length, width, and alignment, with negatively poled PVDF consistently supporting the most advanced structural maturation. At the molecular level, upregulation of the early cardiomyogenic marker actc1 confirmed stimulation‐induced activation of early differentiation pathways, whereas the limited modulation of Tnnt2 indicates partial but not fully mature cardiomyocyte differentiation.

Overall, these findings reveal that static surface polarization and dynamically generated piezoelectric signals act cooperatively to regulate cardiomyoblast fate decisions. By demonstrating that surface charge polarity and temporally controlled mechano‐electrical stimulation can modulate cardiac cell behaviour without altering material chemistry, this work provides mechanistic insights and practical design parameters for the development of electroactive substrates and devices aimed at cardiac tissue engineering and regenerative applications.
