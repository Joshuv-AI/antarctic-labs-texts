Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry as a sensitive non-radioactive alternative for quantitative analysis in drug metabolism studies based on fluorine detection
Authors: Sam Wouters, Filip Cuyckens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work details how to obtain quantitative results in drug metabolism studies through fluorine detection, utilizing an optimized capillary liquid chromatography setup coupled with inductively coupled plasma tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ICP-MS/MS). This approach eliminates the need for authentic standards or specific labeling with radioactive tritium or carbon-14, enabling targeted and quantitative metabolite identification in much earlier stages of drug discovery and development. Eluent composition was optimized towards selectivity and sensitivity, with correction for gradient effects achieved through both counter gradient application and model-based data correction. The setup allows a limit of detection below 1 ng of fluorine injected on the column for a chromatographic peak to be achieved. High mass spectrometry sensitivity, combined with the ability to inject volumes up to 400 µL via a trap-elute injection, enables selective and sensitive detection of fluorinated drugs and their metabolites at relevant levels for in vitro and in vivo samples in various matrices. The workflow was validated via direct comparison with radioactivity detection, making use of 14C-radiolabeled drugs. This approach holds significant potential to shape future study designs in absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion studies.Graphical abstract

Introduction

The golden standard to study in vitro and in vivo drug metabolism remains radioactivity detection. The detection principle is based on beta radiation upon radioactive decay of tritium (3H) or radiocarbon (14C) and detection of photons emitted by a scintillant. By synthesizing the drug with a 3H or 14C label, the parent drug and metabolites can be easily distinguished from all endogenous substances present in any biological matrix, allowing for selective detection. Furthermore, it provides a uniform response that is independent of the chemical structure of the molecules analyzed. This enables the determination of the total drug-related material in each sample and facilitates mass balance calculations. More recent methods, such as accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS), allow for the direct measurement of carbon isotope ratios in the presence of low amounts of 14C. This technique is much more sensitive than scintillation counting and enables the quantification of lower 14C concentrations, permitting microtracer or microdose studies [1–3].

The ideal technique would, however, be one in which no radiolabel needs to be administered at all, eliminating radioactivity exposure to subjects participating in a human mass balance or hADME (human absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion) study. This would allow for generating truly quantitative data for drug metabolism-related experiments at much earlier stages of discovery and development, while omitting the need to synthesize labeled parent drug or metabolite standards. It would also facilitate the profiling of samples within other preclinical or clinical studies, such as those from first-in-human studies. For instance, steady-state metabolite profiling could be conducted much more easily, while multiple dosing with a radiolabel is often very challenging or unfeasible. Studies in specific populations or with formulations that are currently very difficult to dose with a radiolabel could greatly benefit from approaches that do not require specific radiolabeling of the drug with 3H or 14C.

Conditions for an alternative technique to radioactivity detection are that the technique must be capable of analyzing elements that are not omnipresent in endogenous matrices and that these elements can be analyzed with a response that is independent of the chemical structure. This draws attention to fluorine, an element that is widely used in therapeutic drugs and is scarcely found in biological matrices and is estimated to be present in around 30% of small-molecule drugs approved over the past decade [4–6], and present in up to 50% of drugs under development. There are already promising examples of the use of alternative techniques for radiolabeling and detection such as fluorine nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (F-NMR) in preclinical [7, 8], and first-in-human [9] studies. Recently, its potential was shown in the human mass balance study of alpelisib [10] and Paxlovid® (nirmatrelvir/ritonavir) [11].

An alternative technique capable of quantifying halogen-containing drugs is inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). As molecules break down into their constituent atoms in the plasma, an ICP-MS response is obtained that is independent of the chemical structure in which these atoms were originally present. Meermann et al. demonstrated its use in a human metabolism study for bedaquiline based on bromine quantification [12], as a study design with 14C-compound administration was challenging due to the very long residence time of bedaquiline in the body. Instead of a dedicated mass balance study, patient samples from an ongoing study were utilized. Various other studies have further demonstrated the potential of this technique for drug metabolism studies, for drugs containing halogens such as iodine, chlorine, and bromine, yielding comparable results to traditional radioactivity detection without the requirement to introduce a specific isotope label [13, 14]. In addition to metabolite profiling, the potential as an alternative for quantitative whole-body autoradiography has also been demonstrated for a bromine-containing drug [15]. To the best knowledge of the authors, we here detail the determination of fluorine via ICP-MS for the first time in the context of quantitative drug metabolite profiling, at relevant concentrations and in relevant matrices.

In contrast with halogens such as iodine and chlorine which can be measured directly using ICP-MS, extremely low sensitivity is obtained for fluorine to limited F+ formations, as the ionization potential exceeds that of argon. This is addressed by providing an excess of barium salt. When interacting with Ba2+, F− can form the polyatomic cation 138Ba19F+ in the plasma or post-plasma, as detailed in other publications [16–18]. In a MS/MS system, allowing two mass filtering steps, m/z 157 is selected in Q1 and oxygen gas is introduced in the octopole reaction cell to remove isobaric interferences at m/z 157 (138Ba18O1H+, 138Ba16O1H3+, and 138Ba17O2H+) formed in the plasma. In Q2, m/z 157 is again selected, which is in turn detected. The concept of BaF+ detection has previously been demonstrated, however, mostly applied in the field of environmental or food analysis [19, 20].

The primary advantages of ICP-MS over F-NMR are its higher sensitivity and the ability to couple it online with a liquid chromatographic separation. This enables the metabolite profiling of in vitro or in vivo samples to be measured within the same timeframe as a determination using LC/radioactivity detection. In contrast, for F-NMR, the LC eluent must be collected in fractions (offline detection), which are analyzed separately, a process that can be very time-consuming, costly, and oftentimes negatively impacts LC resolution as this is dependent on the number of fractions collected per chromatographic peak.

In this study, we describe the steps taken to optimize LC-ICP-MS/MS towards sensitive and accurate detection of BaF+, to achieve levels relevant for drug metabolism work. The liquid chromatography setup and conditions used play a significant role in this process. We are building upon our multidimensional LC experience with setups that allow for very large volume injections, thereby drastically increasing the sensitivity for LC with online radioactivity detection and micro-LC with electrospray-MS detection [21, 22]. We demonstrate the application of ICP-MS/MS for detection of fluorine through BaF+ formation on samples obtained both from in vitro and in vivo drug metabolism studies, validated by comparison with data obtained via offline radioactivity detection of precisely the same samples. While the focus of this manuscript is primarily on the use of ICP-MS/MS for drug metabolism studies, the methodologies and developments described could also potentially be extended to other applications where fluorine quantification in an unbiased manner, i.e., without authentic standards [23], is important, such as in environmental analyses of perfluorinated compounds like PFOS and PFAS.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

Acetonitrile (ACN, electronic grade 99.999%), water (inorganic trace analysis grade), ammonium acetate (trace metal analysis grade, 99.9999%), ammonium formate (trace metal base, 99.995%), barium nitrate (Ba(NO3)2, trace metal analysis grade, 99.9999%), and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 99%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Formic acid (FA, 98–100% trace analysis) was obtained from Avantor (Radnor, USA). High purity methanol (MeOH, 99.95%) was sourced from Biosolve (Valkenswaard, The Netherlands). Water (MilliQ system, Millipore-Merck, Burlington, USA), and LC-MS grade acetonitrile (Biosolve, 99.95%) and methanol (Supelco, 99.9%) were also used to compare noise levels.

As trapping column, a 1.0 × 50 mm (i.d. × L) Triart Phenyl (3 µm) column was custom packed by YMC (Kyoto, Japan). As analytical column, a 0.3 × 150 mm Triart Phenyl (1.9 µm) UHPLC column was used (YMC), with a 0.3 × 5 mm guard column (Triart Phenyl, 3 µm). A filter cartridge (2.1 mm i.d., 0.3 µm, Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany) was installed directly after the T-piece where the sample is diluted online.

Sample preparation

Samples from metabolism studies of a proprietary small-molecule drug, referred to as drug X, have been investigated to demonstrate the potential of the approach. Rat hepatocytes were incubated at 2 µM, using 14C-labelled compound X, and quenched by adding 1 volume of DMSO. After vortexing and centrifugation (15 min, 4 °C, 15,871 rcf), the supernatant was analyzed.

Wistar Hannover rats were obtained from Charles River Germany and treated at our facilities. Rat plasma and feces were collected at 4 timepoints, after oral administration of compound X at 10 mg/kg (40 µCi/kg 14C-X). Another group of rats was dosed intravenously at 5 mg/kg (40 µCi/kg 14C-X) for bile duct cannulation (BDC). Urine was also collected from these animals. Plasma was pooled across animals and timepoints following the AUC method [24], 100 µL DMSO was added per mL of plasma, vortexed, and precipitated by adding 3 volumes of ACN. After vortex mixing and centrifugation, the supernatant was subjected to vacuum centrifugation (Martin Christ, Osterode am Harz, Germany) to remove all organic solvent (1 mBar for 3 h). Feces samples were extracted with methanol using a procedure described elsewhere [25] and pooled across subjects; organic solvent is removed as described above. Bile and urine were subjected to centrifugation.

Liquid chromatography setup

A Dionex Ultimate 3000 RSLC Nano system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germering, Germany) was modified using off the shelf options. The WPS3000 TPLRS autosampler is fitted with suitable combinations of a 100 or 500 µL syringe; 100, 500, or 1000 µL buffer loop; 20, 50, or 125 µL sample loop; and 2.4 or 15 µL needle. This is combined with a NCS3500 pump/column compartment module and expanded with a 2nd NCS3500 module to have access to two high-pressure gradient binary pumps serving as analytical pump (capillary flow meter) and modulator pump (Proflow nanoflow meter), respectively, and two micro-flow ternary low-pressure gradient pumps serving as loading and make-up flow pumps. The two column compartments were fitted with three low-dispersion 10-port-2-position switching valves, and one 6-port-2-position valve. One valve serves as a UV diverter valve, another as MS diverter valve. In selected experiments, an ultraviolet detector (45 nL flow cell) was used as a detector (Vanquish Neo, Thermo). Glassware was replaced with plastic eluent bottles from the ICS5000 ion chromatography system (Thermo, Sunnyvale, USA). The system was operated in Chromeleon 7.3 (Thermo). LC conditions including flow rate, solvent composition, and gradient are optimized in this work and detailed in the manuscript.

Mass spectrometry and offline radioactivity detection

An 8800 tandem ICP-MS/MS (Agilent Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) was used with a total consumption nebulizer with a single pass spray chamber (Agilent). The system was optimized towards the use of organic solvents, by installing a 1-mm injecting torch, platina inlet and skimmer cones, and an S-lens. While applying argon (99.998%) at 20 L/min, plasma conditions and settings were tuned to maximize signal: RF power: 1500 V, sample depth: 7.0 mm, argon nebulizer gas: 0.8 L/min, Ar/Oxygen 80/20 make-up gas: 0.35 L/min, extract 1: −25 V, extract 2: −200 V, omega bias: −100 V, omega lens: 6 V, plate bias: −115 V, octopole bias: −57 V, energy discrimination −15 V. Reaction gas oxygen (> 99.999%): 80%. All gases were sourced from Messer (Zwijndrecht, Belgium).

To perform offline radioactivity detection, samples were collected in 384-well Lumaplates (Revvity, Waltham, USA) using a MFx fraction collector (Trajan, Victoria, Australia), and analyzed using a Microbeta microplate reader (Revvity). During fraction collection, a make-up flow (50% MeOH, 650 µL/min) was provided using a positive displacement pump (VICI AG Int., Schenkon, Switzerland). For further details on offline radioactivity detection we refer to recent work [25]. ICP-MS data was corrected using an in-house developed model applied using Biovia Pipeline Pilot. ICP-MS and offline counting data was processed in Laura software (v6.4.4.41, Lablogic, Sheffield, UK).

The efficiency of BaF+ formation is highly sensitive to plasma conditions and temperature [16, 17, 19]. In gradient LC, the stability of these parameters is challenged as the LC eluent physicochemical properties are gradually changing throughout the experiment. In HPLC-ICP-MS, using a classic nebulizer and spray chamber, solvent composition affects nebulization efficiency, thus influencing the portion of the flow going into the MS. In this study, however, a total consumption nebulizer is used, operating at capillary flow rates. As such, here it is easier to understand solvent-related effects on plasma stability, as nebulization efficiency is close to 100% (no droplet formation was observed in the spray chamber at the selected flow rate). Some precipitation may be observed in the spray chamber when using high amounts of buffers or some fouling of the sample matrix; this does however not seem to affect system stability, which is very satisfactory especially concerning the large amounts of feces, bile, and urine injected throughout our optimization and validation process.

Figure 3 shows the effect on MS signal intensity when applying an LC gradient. To focus on the contribution of solvent conditions on plasma and ionization efficiency, the amount of Ba(NO3)2 and F (0.025% TFA solution applied post column) entering the system is kept constant in this experiment. As the amount of organic solvent increases throughout the gradient, signal intensity is increasing, to reach an apex at a certain percentage of organic, depending on the physicochemical properties of the solvents used (Fig. 3A). Different solvent compositions were tested, revealing significant differences in sensitivity for TFA. The use of acetonitrile shows a dramatic decrease in response compared to methanol, likely due to the increased carbon load on the plasma. Effects of organic modifier or buffer used were also observed (Fig. 3B). Ammonium formate (10 mM with 0.02% formic acid, pH 3.5) provides a higher response compared to ammonium acetate (10 mM), again suggesting that limiting the amount of carbon entering the system is beneficial. Running a gradient up to 99% organic also seemed to be detrimental for plasma stability, which could even be visually observed (blue central plume when using methanol, blackened cone with a red burning ring with pure acetonitrile). The effect of flow rate is shown in Fig. 3C and corroborates the inverse relation of carbon load with signal intensity. For ICP-MS sensitivity it is beneficial to reduce the flow rate. However, this is limited by the choice of analytical column, where too low flow rates would result in excessive dispersion and long run times. A further reduction in column dimensions to allow flow rate reduction was not investigated, as this would extend the run time too much and limit loadability, which is also paramount here to achieve high sensitivity.

This illustrates that careful consideration of chromatographic conditions and compatibility with ICP-MS is required. To obtain adequate chromatographic selectivity and peak shape while not suffering too much loss in MS response, the use of mixtures of MeOH and ACN was investigated. As different drug substances were evaluated in a variety of in vitro and in vivo matrices, oftentimes presenting complex separations, Table 1 shows the solvent conditions which were eventually found to provide high sensitivity and good chromatography in a variety of cases.

A significant sensitivity drift is observed as a function of the gradient composition. As a result, peaks of equal concentration would be either under-expressed when eluting under more aqueous conditions, or over-expressed when later-eluting. Considering this effect, it is of interest to modulate the solvent composition prior to entering the MS source [28]. The modulator pump, used to provide Ba(NO3)2 may be exploited to apply a counter gradient during the LC run. By programming an organic-to aqueous gradient, this allows to increase the % organic at the gradient start, and lower it at the gradient end (ramp A: 99/1 H2O/MeOH + 60 ppm Ba(NO3)2 to B: 1/99 H2O/MeOH + 60 ppm Ba(NO3)2). Despite only adding 20% of flow rather than 1:1 flow addition, a much more stable response throughout the run could be achieved, avoiding extreme points in the sensitivity plot, where the response is low. The effect is shown in Fig. 4A and B. A 1:1 flow of the counter gradient may fully correct the sensitivity drift, resulting in a flat line throughout the gradient. However, this approach was not chosen in order to maintain maximum sensitivity, by not further diluting the flow, apart from what is already added for the addition of Ba(NO3)2, also bearing in mind sensitivity is inversely proportional to flow rate in LC-ICP-MS (Fig. 3C).Fig. 4Relative response (applying a constant concentration of TFA and Ba(NO3)2) throughout the LC run (applying optimized solvents from Table 1: 1–95% in 50 min at 5.5 µL/min), without (A) and with (B) organic-to-aqueous counter gradient on the modulator pump (1.1 µL/min)

Despite this improvement, the remaining small deviation in response especially at higher organic content, can still be corrected post measurement to obtain truly quantitative data. Vanhaecke et al. [29] previously also reported the gradient-sensitivity relation in LC-ICP-MS, although it was mostly attributed to variations in nebulization efficiency, which are ruled out here by using the total consumption nebulizer. They developed a model to correct MS response throughout the gradient. In this work, we determine a relation between %B and sensitivity and using a lookup script and the gradient parameters (t0 and %B versus time), a sensitivity correction value can be obtained for any given timepoint and any given gradient (using the same solvents) to correct the data. As an example, a late-eluting peak (e.g., 80% B) is over-expressed by a factor 1.2; thus, the correction factor applied to these datapoints is 0.8. As a result, peaks of equal concentration are equally represented, regardless of where they elute in the gradient. The correction process has been simplified by making use of Pipeline Pilot in which the model is stored, and raw data files are uploaded and corrected files are returned. Figure 4B shows the relative response (applying the same TFA concentration as previously) throughout an LC run (for a selected gradient steepness and time).

Under these optimized conditions we are able to obtain a limit of detection (S/N or 2H/6.6σ = 3) of 0.65 ng F on column (6.5 ng/mL), presenting the lowest LOD for fluorine using LC-ICP-MS/MS to the knowledge of the authors [16]. This considers that sharp chromatographic peaks are obtained, as shown for 20–100 µL injections (Fig. 5). Quantitative comparison with radioactivity detection

With the promise of obsoleting radioactive dosing to animals and human subjects (healthy volunteers or patients), it is paramount to show equivalent quantitative results between the two techniques. Figure 6 shows in vitro and in vivo metabolite profiling data in various matrices for compound X as corrected fluorogram (A, C, I, G) and offline radiochromatogram (B, D, J, H). The ICP-MS data have been corrected with the response determined in Fig. 4B. Metabolites from Phase I and Phase II metabolism are observed, and as is common in drug metabolism, are eluted earlier than the parent drug. As such, the molecules used in this comparison span a range of polarity (influencing solvent composition at the point of elution) and volatility. For in vitro incubations using only 14C-labelled material with high specific activity (Fig. 6 A/B), the sensitivity of the technique positions between online and offline radioactivity detection. In in vivo studies (animal and human), the administered dose commonly only contains a small percentage of radioactive material. Therefore, only a small part of the compound and its metabolites can be detected using radioactivity detection, while when determining F as BaF+ via ICP-MS/MS, the entire dose is measured. This also explains the difference in the response ratio between ICP-MS and offline counting for the 3 different groups of samples (in vitro 2 µM, low dose BDC rat and high dose rat). As peaks tend to be broader in offline counting (additional dispersion, and limited fractions/run), the height of certain peaks may appear different. Therefore, area percentages should be compared. Results for parent and metabolites exceeding the 5 area% limit are all within 82–112% of the results obtained via offline radioactivity counting. As the agreement with radioactivity data is not different for metabolites and parent, presenting different physicochemical properties (and potentially a different ionization behavior), LC-ICP-MS clearly provides a response that is independent of the matrix and chemical structure of the components being determined. Considering that there is also a margin of error on offline radioactivity counting data (matrix effects, quenching, recovery, etc.) [25] the two datasets are in very close agreement and show the quantitative accuracy of ICP-MS/MS is fit-for-purpose for drug metabolism studies.

Given the strong correlation of the radiochromatograms and quantitative results obtained via BaF+ detection using LC-ICP-MS/MS, we can conclude that the technique is a viable alternative to LC-radioactivity detection. In drug metabolism studies, this technique can serve as a valuable complement to ESI-MS, which is essential for structural identification but cannot be used quantitatively without the use of authentic standards due to matrix and structure-dependent responses.

Concluding remarks

Careful optimization of a capillary-scale trap-elute LC-ICP-MS/MS system results in a robust state-of-the-art platform, unlocking the potential to achieve a limit of detection of 0.65 ng F on column (6.5 ng F/mL). The setup allows injecting high volumes of up to 400 µL, whilst maintaining adequate separation selectivity and efficiency. To achieve these high injection volumes without excessive dispersion and minimizing sample loss, it is important to tune and optimize the trapping conditions (trap dimensions and loadability, loading time, loading solvent, dilution ratio). Optimization of LC solvents and MS tuning results in a technique which provides adequate sensitivity to serve as a viable alternative to radioactivity detection. Importantly, ICP-MS also provides a structure-independent response, and thus allows for absolute quantitation without the need for authentic standards (cfr. ESI-MS). The further development and commercialization of different types of ionization sources or MS systems could potentially further improve sensitivity [18, 30–32].

The optimized setup allows to characterize in vitro samples at concentrations typically used in early discovery work, where radiolabeled compounds and standards are often not available. The approach is more quantitative than a classical discovery UHPLC-UV-HRMS workflow, and the process is greatly aided by a selectivity which is only rivaled by radioactivity detection or accelerator mass spectrometry. To provide structural information/identification, the capillary LC system can be coupled to a HRMS system, possibly making use of a split flow to the ICP-MS.

When considering in vivo studies, the approach allows to eliminate radioactive burden for animals and human subjects, as mass balance data can be obtained without conducting a separate radiolabeled mass balance study. We have demonstrated that even for low turn-over compounds, good quality data could robustly be obtained from various complex matrices including, but not limited to, feces, urine, bile, and plasma. Results were in good agreement with the results obtained by means of radioactivity detection, enabling the acquisition of data critical for filing with health authorities, via cold (unlabeled) studies. The approach is currently robustly used to support preclinical and clinical studies. It should be noted that when conducting human or patient studies, a detailed overview of comedication will be required, and collection of pre-dose samples for the different matrices under investigation should be included in the ICP-MS study to prevent false identification of comedication (and related metabolites) as actual drug metabolites, as other fluorinated drugs will confound the data.

As drugs are metabolized, it is plausible that the fluorine-containing parts of the molecule are split off and thus not detected, e.g., cleavage products, downstream metabolites [33]. This, however, also occurs in radiolabeled studies, despite a careful selection of the position of the label with respect to metabolic hotspots (a region of the molecule where metabolism is expected, which is likely to undergo major transformations). In this sense, ICP-MS can also provide complementary information to radioactivity detection (and vice-versa), as cold/unlabeled metabolites can be observed, if a fluorine atom is still present on the metabolite where the radiocarbon or tritium is metabolically cleaved off and thus no longer present.
