Arthroscopic All‐Inside/All‐Suture Repair of Complete Radial Tear of the Lateral Meniscus Using a Percutaneous Single‐Needle Technique
Authors: Diego Sánchez Cruz, Ricardo Castro Gaona, Gabriel Larrota, Néstor Sánchez Dicelis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadial tears of the lateral meniscus have limited healing potential and unfavorable biomechanics, and current suture constructs that use capsular rip‐stop stitches may increase meniscal extrusion and constrain native motion. This technical note describes an all‐inside, crossed, meniscus‐based repair for complete or near‐complete radial tears of the lateral midbody using a percutaneous needle. Key steps, indications, and rehabilitation are detailed. This construct aims to balance stability with preservation of meniscal mobility and vascularity; prospective clinical studies are required to confirm its long‐term efficacy.

Arthroscopic visualization is obtained with a 30° scope through a high anterolateral portal, with instrumentation through a standard anteromedial portal. With the knee in the figure‐of‐four position and varus stress applied, a Tuohy needle is introduced percutaneously proximal to the meniscal rim, anterior or posterior to the lateral collateral ligament depending on the tear location. A No. 2/0 ultra‐high‐molecular‐weight polyethylene suture is passed side‐to‐side through the anterior segment (femoral‐to‐tibial) and then through the posterior segment to create a horizontal stitch bridging the radial defect; suture limbs are retrieved and parked untied. The needle is then loaded with a looped No. 1 PDS to shuttle each ultra‐high‐molecular‐weight polyethylene limb from tibial‐to‐femoral through the opposite meniscal segment, creating a crossed configuration. The ultra‐high‐molecular‐weight polyethylene limbs are retrieved on the femoral surface and tied with sliding arthroscopic knots backed by alternating half‐hitches, restoring meniscal contour while avoiding overtensioning. Radial tears of the lateral meniscus are common injuries, reported in up to 14% of knee arthroscopic procedures. 1 , 2 Biomechanically, when these lesions are complete, they markedly reduce the resultant force carried by the meniscus under axial load 3 and create unfavorable dynamic contact mechanics that are not different from those observed after meniscectomy. 4 In view of these alterations and their clinical implications, there is currently a trend toward surgical management of such tears. 5

Numerous suture configurations with favorable biomechanical and clinical results have been described. 6 , 7 However, constructs that incorporate rip‐stop sutures show the best mechanical behavior in terms of gap reduction, stiffness, and ultimate load to failure. 8 The drawback of vertical rip‐stop sutures is that, because they are anchored to the capsule, they generate not only compressive forces but also peripheral traction forces, which may lead to meniscal extrusion 9 and reduce natural meniscal motion during knee flexion‐extension.

The aim of this technical note is to describe an all‐inside, crossed, meniscus‐based repair technique for radial tears of the lateral meniscus body. This approach is designed to exploit the previously reported biomechanical advantages 8 , 9 while providing a stable construct with minimal peripheral tension, avoiding meniscal extrusion and preserving native meniscal mobility throughout the knee's arc of motion.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Indications and Contraindications

The indications are complete or near‐complete radial tears of the lateral meniscus midbody with adequate meniscal tissue allowing suture capture on both sides of the tear; the contraindications for this procedure are as follows: advanced lateral compartment chondrosis (Kellgren‐Lawrence grade ≥ 3) or an irreparable meniscal degeneration with insufficient tissue for fixation.

Patient Positioning and Setup

The patient is placed supine with the knee in the figure‐of‐four position (Figure 1). A tourniquet is not used. Two portals are established: a high anterolateral and an anteromedial working portal.

Left knee. Patient positioned in figure‐of‐four; the LCL is palpated and a Tuohy needle is introduced percutaneously anterior or posterior to the LCL (depending on the tear location) and proximal to the meniscal rim. (LCL, lateral collateral ligament.)

Diagnostic Arthroscopy and Tear Assessment

A standard diagnostic arthroscopy is performed with a 30° arthroscope through the anterolateral portal. The radial tear of the lateral meniscus is identified, and its location, completeness, and tissue quality are assessed using a probe. Concomitant chondral lesions and ligamentous injuries are documented. The edges of the tear and the adjacent synovium are rasped to promote healing.

Step 1: Preparation and Landmarks

With the knee in the figure‐of‐four position, the lateral compartment is opened by applying a varus stress. A small bump may be placed under the ankle to increase the working space. The lateral collateral ligament is palpated and identified. A Tuohy needle is then inserted percutaneously either posterior or anterior to this structure, depending on the tear location, and proximal to the meniscal rim (Figure 1).

Step 2: Placement of the First Horizontal Suture

The arthroscope will be positioned in the anteromedial portal, and the anterolateral portal will be used as the working portal. Using the Tuohy needle loaded with a No. 2/0 ultra‐high‐molecular‐weight polyethylene suture (FiberWire, Arthrex, Naples, FL), the first side‐to‐side stitch is passed. The needle penetrates the anterior meniscal segment from the femoral surface at the red‐red zone and exits on the tibial surface (Figures 2A and 3A). To avoid tibial chondral scraping, a metal cannula may be used to protect the articular surface during needle passage, although this is not necessary in all cases. Through the anterolateral portal, a probe is introduced to retrieve the suture loop, which is positioned in the intercondylar notch and subsequently grasped with a suture retriever. Without withdrawing the needle, the same sequence is repeated in the posterior meniscal segment (Figures 2B and 3B). At this step, a grasper is used to elevate and counterpress the meniscus, facilitating passage of the needle and suture. The sutures are retrieved and left untied and parked anteriorly.

Step 3: Crossing Sutures

The Tuohy needle is loaded with a looped No. 1 PDS (Ethicon, NJ). Through the same percutaneous entry point, the needle is passed again through the anterior meniscal segment, 3 to 4 mm from the first stitch, and the suture limb from the posterior meniscal segment is captured with the loop and brought from the tibial to the femoral surface (Figures 2C,D and 3C). The same maneuver is repeated through the posterior meniscal segment, this time capturing the anterior suture limb and transferring it from the tibial to the femoral surface, thereby creating a crossed‐suture configuration (Figures 2E,F and 3D).

Step 4: Knot Tying and Final Assessment

The crossed sutures are retrieved (Figures 2G and 3E) and tied (Figures 2H and 3F) using sliding arthroscopic knots backed by 3 alternating half‐hitches. Care is taken to avoid overtensioning and to restore the native width of the meniscus. Final probing confirms construct stability and restoration of the lateral meniscal contour (Video 1). Postoperative Rehabilitation

Protected weight‐bearing with crutches is recommended for the first 6 weeks. Range of motion is typically limited to 0° to 90° during the first 4 weeks, avoiding deep squatting and pivoting. Running is usually permitted after 4 months, and return to pivoting sports is allowed after 6 to 9 months, depending on concomitant procedures and clinical progression.

DISCUSSION

Radial tears of the lateral meniscus have a modest healing prognosis. Several authors have reported complete healing rates between 22% and 35% for these tears in stable knees. 10 , 11 The literature remains limited regarding prognostic factors specifically associated with nonhealing in this setting; however, from an anatomical perspective, the meniscal vascular hilum in the region adjacent to the popliteal hiatus is very narrow, and there is no femoral capsular insertion along the cranial border, which severely limits vascular ingrowth. 12 As this represents a nonmodifiable prognostic factor, surgeons have focused on optimizing repair techniques to reduce gap formation and improve stiffness and ultimate load to failure. Multiple techniques have been described with favorable biomechanical results. 6 Among the various constructs, 2 features are likely to enhance biomechanical performance: the use of rip‐stop sutures and incorporation of crossed suture patterns. 6 , 13

In a biomechanical analysis, Stender et al. 14 showed that constructs incorporating rip‐stop sutures significantly reduced gap opening under cyclic loading compared with a simple crossed construct, 15 although no significant differences were found with respect to ultimate tensile strength or failure rate.

Despite their advantages under cyclic loading, constructs that include rip‐stop sutures may have at least 2 potential drawbacks. First, because these sutures are anchored in the capsule, they may restrict physiological meniscal motion and promote meniscal extrusion, 9 and second, vertical sutures knotted in the capsule may narrow the meniscal vascular hilum and further compromise its already limited blood supply. The main advantage of the technique described here (see Table 2) is that it seeks to balance an adequate biomechanical profile with a predominantly meniscal‐based fixation, thereby preserving both the physiological excursion of the meniscus and its scarce but crucial vascularity.

This technique requires adequate tissue quality on both sides of the tear. As with all radial meniscus repairs, overtensioning may alter meniscal morphology. The needle entry point must respect the integrity of the lateral collateral ligament and remain proximal to the meniscus to avoid injury to the meniscal vascular hilum and the inferior genicular artery. During needle passage, care should be taken to avoid chondral scraping; the use of a cannulated probe or protective sheath is recommended for this purpose. Finally, the presence of suture material on the articular surface raises concern about potential chondral damage. 16 Although some authors have reported that nonabsorbable meniscal repair sutures become incorporated into the meniscus without causing detectable cartilage injury meniscal, 17 we consider it essential to use low‐profile sutures and avoid bulky knots. This report describes the technique only; systematic clinical outcome data are required to validate its long‐term efficacy.
