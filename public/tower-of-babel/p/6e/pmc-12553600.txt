Effects of sulfur on nitrogen transformation in malodorous rivers with different remediations
Authors: Yunyi Zha, Chenxi Yu, Yao Huang, Jin Zhu, Gongming Zhou, Yan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractHigh levels of total nitrogen (TN) in urban rivers remain a significant environmental concern due to their contribution to malodor, which needs an urgent solution. Considering the critical role of sulfur (S) in river habitats, how and to what extent S is involved in nitrogen (N) transformation deserves attention, especially in the context of river remediations. In this study, sediment incubation and river simulation showed that denitrification had a higher tolerance to sulfide than anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox) and outcompeted anammox in substrate competition. Moreover, appropriate levels of sulfide (40 mg·L−1) stimulated S-driven autotrophic denitrification (SAD) activity (from 0.010 to 0.021 meq·g−1 VSS·h−1), with the relative abundances of Thiobacillus-like bacteria increasing from 0.46% to 21.51%. However, 40 mg·L−1 sulfide inhibited anammox activity (from 0.010 to 0.0032 meq·g−1 VSS·h−1), with the decrease in both ammonium removal (from 55.25% to 22.32%) and anammox bacteria abundance (from 0.45% to 0.18%) in the incubation. A significant correlation (P < 0.01) between TN and sulfate was observed in calcium nitrate added and aerated river simulations, which indicated that sulfide was involved in the N-transformation through SAD. This finding was further confirmed by the relatively high abundances of Thiobacillus (1.15% and 1.12%, respectively) in both sediment aeration and calcium nitrate addition systems. Notably, S-driven autotrophic partial denitrification coupled with anammox was discovered under calcium nitrate addition. This study can fill the gap in understanding the interactions of S and N biogeochemical processes with river remediations and can help establish an effective approach for TN removal.Key points• Anammox showed higher sensitivity to 40 mg·L−1 sulfide than denitrification.• Coupling mechanisms between S and N were studied based on real river scenario.• Calcium nitrate induced the coupling of SAPD with anammox.• Denitrification showed relatively high tolerance to sulfide in malodorous rivers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13612-0.Keywords: Urban river, Denitrification, Anammox, Aeration, Calcium nitrate, Sulfide exposure

Introduction

The “malodor” in urban rivers has attracted ever-increasing attention after the Implementation Strategy for Urban Black-Odor Water Management Guidelines was carried out (Cao et al. 2020). Relatively high-level total nitrogen (TN) is recognized as one of the key factors for the algal blooms in urban rivers, further contributing to the malodor in waterbodies (Yin et al. 2019a; Huang et al. 2022; Weng et al. 2022). Apart from nitrogen (N), sulfur (S) is another equally vital biogenic element and contributes directly to malodor formation in waterbodies. Moreover, the contents of S in urban rivers are increasing due to effluent discharge from wastewater treatment plants, which further necessitates consideration of its role in N-transformation processes in malodorous rivers. Therefore, an in-depth insight into the S–N coupling is helpful to understand the elemental cycles in malodorous rivers, so as to provide theoretical support for the effective control of malodor.

The role of S in N-transformation in natural aquatic ecosystems was first recognized by Canfield et al. (2010), who found that nitrogen cycling processes were associated with the “cryptic sulfur cycle” in marine sediment. After that, some related studies about the effects of S on N-transformation have been done in marine ecosystems or estuaries (Rios-Del Toro and Cervantes 2016; Caffrey et al. 2019), constructed wetlands, and meromictic lakes (Wenk et al. 2013; Roland et al. 2018). In particular, Rios-Del Toro and Cervantes demonstrated that S-driven denitrification (SAD) coupled with anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox) could significantly change the nitrogen and sulfide fluxes in marine sediments. Notably, although this linkage has been studied, the impact of reduced sulfur on denitrification and anammox remains controversial regarding whether it is an inhibition effect or a stimulation effect. For example, Wenk et al. (2013) showed that hydrogen sulfide (H2S) addition could stimulate anammox and verified the coexistence of S-related and N-related bacteria in a lacustrine environment, while Roland et al. (2018) maintained that H2S did not affect anammox in a meromictic lake.

Moreover, although scholars generally agree that reduced sulfide inhibits denitrification to some extent, disparate observations are found concerning the inhibition threshold and reversibility of reduced sulfide on denitrification and anammox (Dapena-Mora et al. 2007; Zhu et al. 2018). What is more, the effects of sulfur on nitrogen transformation in malodorous rivers may be different from those findings in marine ecosystems, lakes, constructed wetlands, and wastewater treatment plants, since malodorous rivers are characterized by high pollution loads and oxygen deficiency. Additionally, it remains unknown regarding the N-related microbial adaptation behavior against reduced sulfur. Therefore, identifying the effects of S on denitrification and anammox is of great significance for providing new insights into N- and S-cycling and guiding the remediation of malodorous river ecosystems.

Aeration and calcium nitrate addition are two effective measures for malodorous river remediation (Cao et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022; Li et al. 2019; Cheng et al. 2023). Existing research has documented its individual roles in N-transformation and has tentatively explored the conversion of S. For example, Chen et al. (2019a) preliminarily observed the coupling between N and S with the increasing aeration disturbance in the malodorous river, and Mai et al. (2021) investigated the coupled oxidation of S and N compounds with different N/(Fe + S) ratios under calcium nitrate addition in serum bottles. However, the interaction between S and N with different remediation measures, especially in more real scenarios, remains largely unknown. Further investigation is required to unravel the underlying mechanisms and provide a comprehensive understanding of the N-S biogeochemical cycling in malodorous rivers.

In this research, based on sediment incubations and river simulation experiments, we systematically investigated the following: (1) the impact of sulfide on denitrification and anammox in incubation systems; (2) the dynamics and interactions between S and N in simulated malodorous river systems separately with aeration and calcium nitrate addition; and (3) the coupling relationship of denitrifying bacteria, anammox bacteria, and S-related bacteria in N-transformation. This study will provide a new perspective on the response of denitrification and anammox to sulfide in river remediation scenarios, as well as theoretical support for effective regulation on the N cycling coupled with S in malodorous rivers.

Materials and methods

Sampling

The sediments and overlying water were all collected from the Lidianpu in Taopu town, Shanghai. Lidianpu is characterized by poor hydrodynamic conditions and is polluted by wastewater and sewage discharged from surrounding outlets. The fundamental physiochemical characteristics were as follows: dissolved oxygen (DO) 0.36–0.42 mg·L−1, ammonium-nitrogen (NH4+-N) 12.90–13.93 mg·L−1, TN 20.80–32.26 mg·L−1, sulfide (S2−) 0.78–0.84 mg·L−1, sulfate (SO42−) 18.30–24.52 mg·L−1, and pH 7.20–7.53, sediment acid volatile sulfur (AVS) 122.74–133.24 μmol·g−1, and total iron 19.68–21.41 g·kg−1.

Sediment incubations

The basal medium and trace element solutions I and II were adopted following the method described by Rios-Del Toro and Cervantes (2016). The compositions of the basal medium were as follows (mg·L−1): KHCO3, 500; KH2PO4, 27.2; MgSO4·7H2O, 60; CaCl2·2H2O, 180. The compositions of trace element solution I were as follows (mg·L−1): EDTA, 1500; H3BO4, 14; MnCl2·4H2O, 1000; CuSO4·5H2O, 250; ZnSO4·7H2O, 430; NiCl2·6H2O, 190; CoCl2·6H2O, 240; NaMoO4·2H2O, 220. The trace element solution II comprised (mg·L−1): EDTA, 5000 and FeSO4, 5000. Each liter of basal medium was amended with 1 mL of trace element solution.

Denitrification incubation experiment was conducted in serum bottles (250 mL) and cultivated sediments with basal medium (described before) in a ratio of 1:10. To analyze the effects of reduced sulfur on denitrification activity, varying levels of sulfide (S2−: 0, 8, 40, 64, and 96 mg·L−1) were separately added to five different serum bottles with sodium nitrate of 28 mg·L−1. To explore the impact of a sudden sulfide shock on microorganisms and TN removal, the supplied reduced sulfide levels escalated to 160 mg·L−1 at the 6th cycle for the incubation formerly supplied with 8 mg·L−1 sulfide. The sulfides required for the experiment were obtained by diluting the concentrated Na2S·H2O stock solution. In addition, the basal medium was pre-purged with argon to remove oxygen. Sealed the serum bottles and purged the headspace with argon for 15 min. The pH in the serum bottle was maintained at about 7.5 with 1 mol·L−1 HCl solution and cultured in a constant temperature chamber at 26 ℃.

Similar incubation systems were also done to initiate anammox with NH4+-N of 28 mg·L−1 and nitrite (NO2−-N) of 36.96 mg·L−1 supply. Control anammox sediment incubations were run with NO2−-N (36.96 mg·L−1) alone, and NH4+-N (28 mg·L−1) alone, as well as sediment sterilization with NH4+-N (28 mg·L−1) and NO2−-N (36.96 mg·L−1) addition, respectively. After the anammox reaction was initiated, reduced sulfide (40 mg·L−1) was introduced to investigate its inhibition or promotion to the anammox process. The activities of denitrification and anammox processes were adapted from the previous study with modifications (González-Blanco et al. 2015). Specifically, the specific activity was determined through stoichiometric analysis of substrate consumption and product formation rates, and then converted to electron equivalents for comparability. The detailed information is presented in Table S1. All treatments were run in duplicates.

River simulation

To further investigate the dynamics of endogenous S and N and their possible correlations in a more real scenario, the simulated river systems (Wang et al. 2017) were set up with control, the addition of 140 g NO3−-N·m−2 calcium nitrate, intermittent overlying-water aeration (DO 4 ± 1 mg·L−1, duration 12 h), and intermittent sediment aeration (0.1 mg·L−1, duration 12 h), respectively. Among them, calcium nitrate was added as the nitrate source. Each simulated system covered by gobo was built with a 2:1 volume ratio of overlying-water and sediment in a homemade plexiglass reactor, and let stand for about 2 days before running. The reactor is 30 cm in diameter and 100 cm in height, which is detailed in Fig. S1.

Microbial analysis

To investigate the dynamic variations in microbial diversity and abundance after artificial regulations for long-term incubations and river simulations, samples were gathered from sediment incubation and river simulation during the final stages, respectively. These samples were assigned to Magigene Company (Guangdong, China) and Personalbio Company (Shanghai, China) for the high-throughput sequencing and PCR analysis, respectively. Four pairs of primers were applied in this study, including cd3aF (5′-GTSAACGTSAAGGARACSGG-3′) and R3cd (5′-GASTTCGGRTGSGT CTTGA-3′) targeted denitrifying function gene (nirS) (Murdock and Juniper 2017), DSRp2060F (5′-CAACATCGTYCAYACCCAGGG-3′) and DSR4R (5′-GTGTAGCA GTTACCGCA-3′)–targeted sulfate reducing function gene (dsrB) (Wang et al. 2014), Anammox-1F (5′-GTGTAGCAGTTACCGCA-3′) and Anammox-2R (5′-TCTGTATT ACCGCGGCT-3′)–targeted anammox bacteria (Wang et al. 2014), and 341 F (5′-CCTACG GGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′)–targeted bacterial communities (Wang et al. 2017).

Analytical methods and statistical analysis

The measure of TN, NH4+, NO3−, NO2−, S2−, and SO42− followed standard methods (APHA 2012). The measurements of DO, pH, and temperature of the overlying water were obtained by portable meters.

The data analysis and visualization were conducted using Origin 2018, R (version 4.0.3), Mothur software (version 1.40.5), and Cytoscape (version 3.6.0). Prior to heatmap generation, bacteria abundance in each sample was log2-transformed to minimize the impact of extremum and then normalized scores for optimal visualization. To find co-occurrence or co-exclusion interaction modes of community members and infer the possible “cooperative” or “competitive” relationship between different microbial groups, the Mothur software (version 1.40.5) was utilized to determine the Spearman rank correlation coefficient according to the abundance of species at the genus level from incubation sediment. Afterward, the results were imported into Cytoscape (version 3.6.0) to obtain the community network for visualization (Shannon et al. 2003). Microbial groups that show a positive or negative correlation with each other can be found directly, and an association network of dominant microbial groups can help explore their ecological significance (Faust and Raes 2012).

Results

The impact of sulfide on denitrification and anammox in sediment incubations

Considering sulfide is the dominant form in river sediment and present at different levels in different rivers, the impact of different sulfide concentrations on denitrification activity was first explored by sediment incubation experiments (Fig. 1; Table S1). It was found that moderate sulfide could promote nitrate reduction, and the denitrification activity exhibited a corresponding increase from 0.021 to 0.025 meq·g−1 VSS·h−1 as sulfide level rose from 40 to 64 mg·L−1 compared with control (0.010 meq·g−1 VSS·h−1) (Fig. S2). However, as the level continued to increase from 96 to 160 mg·L−1, denitrification activity rapidly decreased from 0.012 to 0.006 meq·g−1 VSS·h−1.

Profiles of potential denitrification activity and anammox activity with different sulfide levels (Note: anammox activity is inhibited with sulfide of higher than 40 mg·L−1)

Additionally, the denitrification rates with different sulfide levels were also assessed by the time needed for achieving 98% nitrate removal (Table S2). It took 9–10 days, 6 days, and 3 days for incubation to remove over 98% of nitrate with the addition of sulfide of 8, 40, and 64 mg·L−1, respectively, while the control group required a longer period (9–14 days) to achieve comparable nitrate removal. As the sulfide level exceeded 96 mg·L−1, inhibitory effects on the denitrification process were observed, with nitrate removal failing to reach 98%. Taken together, it was concluded that suitable levels of sulfide (40–64 mg·L−1) could stimulate the SAD and thus enhance nitrate removal, while high-level sulfide (96–160 mg·L−1) exerted a toxicity effect on the denitrification process.

Besides, the effects of sulfide on anammox potential in malodorous river sediments were also explored by incubation experiments (Fig. 1; Table S1; Fig. S3). Different from the effects of sulfide on denitrification, anammox activity was markedly inhibited (from 0.010 to 0.0032 meq·g−1 VSS·h−1) with the average ammonium reduction rate decreasing from 29.64% to 13.36% at 40 mg·L−1 sulfide. Taken together, it was concluded that 40 mg·L−1 sulfide inhibited anaerobic ammonium oxidizing bacteria (AnAOB) activity but stimulated SAD.

S and N dynamics in river simulation with different remediations

To explore the interplay and coupling of N and S in river simulation under different remediations, this study evaluated the dynamic variations of endogenous N and S species (Figs. 2 and S4). As shown in Fig. 2b, the ammonium levels increased initially (0–10 d) owing to the endogenous release and then decreased for all runs in the overlying water. The ammonium levels in both overlying-water and sediment-aeration groups decreased to 5 mg·L−1 within 30 days, which was mainly due to the effective nitrification from aeration.

Nitrogen dynamics in the overlying water of river simulation with different remediation techniques

In contrast, the calcium nitrate addition group exhibited a significant accumulation of ammonium between days 16 and 30, possibly attributed to the induction of the dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonia process. Subsequently, the ammonium concentrations decreased from 21.92 ± 0.66 to 13.81 ± 0.41 mg·L−1, possibly consumed by the nammox process. Additionally, the continuous nitrite accumulation was observed from days 19 to 38 under calcium nitrate treatment (Fig. 2d), and then gradually declined to 4.76 mg·L−1 over the period from days 38 to 74. These observations suggested the potential occurrence of a partial denitrification process, and its coupling with anammox further consumed the nitrite.

Compared with calcium nitrate addition (13.23% TN removal), both overlying-water-aerated and sediment-aerated regulations performed better in TN removal (82.15% and 67.52%, respectively), which was due to the facilitation of nitrification–denitrification coupling. It was also found that low S2− (0.018 to 0.042 mg·L−1) and high SO42− (97.75 to 160.49 mg·L−1) were present in all river simulations (Fig. S4). However, the generation of SO42− resulted from the sulfide oxidation under aerated conditions, whereas calcium nitrate stimulated SAD and produced SO42− (Liu et al. 2017a).

Correlation analysis was conducted using R (version 4.0.3) to explore possible interactions of S with N in river simulation with different regulations (Fig. 3). An extremely significant negative correlation was observed between TN and SO42− for overlying-water aeration (coefficient: − 0.88 (P < 0.01)) and sediment-aerated river simulation (coefficient: − 0.86 (P < 0.01)). Similarly, for the calcium-nitrate-addition group, TN also showed a negative correlation with SO42− (−0.59 (P < 0.01)). These observations showed the involvement of sulfide in the nitrogen removal process, e.g., SAD. With calcium nitrate added as the electron acceptor, the abundant AVS and nitrate in the sediment stimulated the growth of SAD to remove TN and produce SO42−, which can be released and migrate to the water (Yin et al. 2019a). Comparatively, both overlying-water and sediment aeration had better TN removal than calcium nitrate addition. Although S2− concentration in the overlying-water was low (Fig. S3), malodorous river sediment is quite abundant in reduced sulfur species such as iron sulfides (e.g., FeS and FeS2), which could be used as electron donors for SAD (Torrentó et al. 2011; Hayakawa et al. 2013).

Correlation analysis of nitrogen and sulfur in river simulations. Control (a), calcium nitrate addition (b), overlying-water aeration (c), and sediment aeration (d)

To further assess the effects of S on N-transformation under calcium nitrate addition, SAD is defined by stoichiometric analysis based on material balance (α = ∆NO3−/∆SO42−). The theoretical stoichiometric ratio in SAD (5S2-+8NO3-+8H+→5SO42-+4N2+4H2O) is 1.6, and the ratio of S-driven autotrophic partial denitrification (SAPD) (S2-+4NO3-→SO42-+4NO2-) is 4. The actual ∆NO3−/∆SO42− ratio of the calcium nitrate addition group was about 3.09 (1.6 < ∆NO3−/∆SO42− < 4). It showed that SAD and SAPD coexisted in the calcium nitrate addition group, and calcium nitrate may induce more SAPD and thereby promoted the coupling of SAPD and anammox, consistent with the variation trend of nitrite depicted in Fig. 2d.

Microbial community analysis

The S- and N- cycling processes are involved in numerous complex physical, chemical, and biological processes. Amongst these processes, the microbial community is recognized as playing a dominant role. Hence, it is essential to comprehensively understand the coupled relationship between S and N by analyzing its microbial community.

The genus-level relative abundances of bacterial communities with different incubations and river remediation were shown in Fig. 4. It was found that Thiobacillus was present in all groups (0.36%–21.51%), which demonstrated the occurrence of sulfur autotrophic denitrification in malodorous rivers. Among these groups, Thiobacillus was the most abundant in denitrification incubation (21.51%), which further confirmed the fact that SAD was promoted with 40 mg·L−1 of sulfide. Additionally, the Thiobacillus maintained a relatively high abundance (6.98%) even in anammox incubation, indicating that SAD was promoted to some extent under sulfide exposure in anammox incubation (Table S3). Although the anammox activity was inhibited with sulfide addition (Fig. 1), anammox-related Chloroflexi (44.35%) as well as Planctomycetes-like bacteria (35.26%) were observed with 40 mg·L−1 sulfide (Fig. S6). These observations could be attributed to the partial inhibition and the restoration of AnAOB activity with the decrease of sulfide level. Besides, there might be sampling errors due to the existence of extracellular DNA from inactivated bacteria during the microbial analysis (Carini et al. 2017).

Relative abundance of microbial communities at genus level. (a) Heatmap of the top 15 bacterial genera (column-standardized relative abundance) under different river remediation treatments, where CK, CA, WA, and SA represent control, calcium nitrate addition, water aeration, and sediment aeration, respectively; (b) denitrification incubation; (c) anammox incubation

It was found that the abundances of denitrification-related gene nirS (0.11%–0.14%) and sulfate reduction–related gene dsrB (0.12%–0.16%) were relatively high in all river remediation groups, confirming the important role of S in N-cycling in urban rivers (Fig. 5; Table S4). Additionally, the abundances of AnAOB gene (0.36%–0.72%) were also relatively high under all remediations, demonstrating the key player of anammox in urban rivers. However, no anammox bacteria at genus level were detected, due to the selected gene marker targeting different anammox specificity groups (Chen et al. 2019a, b), and the corresponding low background abundance of sediment samples.

The relative abundance of functional gene (nirS, dsrB, and AnAOB) in sediment incubations (a) and river simulations (b) (Gene abundant ratio represents the ratio of functional gene abundance to 16S rDNA abundance)

Notably, the relative abundances of AnAOB gene (0.45% → 0.72%) and Thiobacillus (0.36% → 1.12%) both showed increases in the calcium nitrate addition system (Fig. 4 and Table S4). In addition, the relative abundance of sulfide-oxidizing bacteria Sulfuritalea (0.12% → 0.78%), as well as sulfate-reducing bacteria Desulfococcus (0.08% → 1.37%) and Dechlormonas (0.07% → 0.67%) all increased compared with the control, which demonstrated that the S was involved in N-transformation through the coupling between SAPD and anammox in the calcium nitrate addition group. Additionally, Thiobacillus was relatively more abundant in sediment aeration (1.15%) compared with other remediation groups (0.36%–1.12%), indicating the resuspension of sediment via sediment aeration facilitated more involvement of reduced sulfur (e.g., AVS) in denitrification. Furthermore, to elucidate the interspecies interactions linked with N and S cycling, network analysis of co-occurrence or co-exclusion patterns for the bacterial community was constructed at the genus level based on the Spearman (r > 0.60 and p < 0.01), showing that anammox bacteria affiliated with Planctomycetes presented a “cooperative” relationship with Desulfococcus and a “competitive” relationship with Thiobacillus (Fig. S7). This finding provided supportive evidence for the complex effects of S on N transformation in malodorous rivers.

Discussion

Urban rivers are affected by intense anthropogenic perturbation, especially in highly populated urban areas, and are characterized by high-level reduced sulfur (AVS) (He et al. 2013). Considering that S is generally acknowledged as a pivotal element associated with malodorous rivers and the contents of S in overlying water are increasing due to discharges from wastewater treatment plants, the effects of S on N-transformation in malodorous rivers deserve further attention. Although some research has been done to ascertain the role of S in denitrification and anammox in some aquatic ecosystems such as estuaries and lakes, relevant information is unavailable regarding the effects of S on N-transformation in urban rivers under different remediations (Weng et al. 2022).

Denitrification and anammox are two main N-removal pathways in aquatic environments. Existing investigations have not reached a consensus on the response of denitrification and anammox under sulfide exposure, and the promoted or inhibited effects of reduced sulfide on these two pathways are still under debate (Zhu et al. 2018; Deng et al. 2022). Some scholars maintain that low sulfide levels (even at 3 mg·L−1) could inhibit the denitrification rate (Wu et al. 2016), while other scholars argue that relatively high levels of sulfide can still promote denitrification (Dolejs et al. 2015). These disparities can be attributed to the differences in inhabitants, microbial diversity, and metabolic adaptation. Our study showed that denitrification activity can be markedly promoted as the sulfide level increased from 40 to 64 mg·L−1 but decreased when the sulfide concentration exceeded 96 mg·L−1. Microbial analysis revealed that Thiobacillus, a typical anaerobic sulfur autotroph, was the most abundant in denitrification incubation with 40 mg·L−1 of sulfide. Additionally, Sulfuritalea (0.12% → 4.85%) and Longilinea (2.26% → 2.45%), which can utilize reduced sulfur as electron donors to drive denitrification, also showed increases in their relative abundances. These phenomena revealed that SAD was promoted with moderate sulfide addition in denitrification incubation. Moreover, the relative abundances of denitrification-related gene nirS also increased from 0.16% to 1.42%, further confirming the role of sulfide in promoting SAD. However, denitrification was not completely inhibited at 160 mg·L−1 sulfide. The relatively high tolerance of denitrification may be explained by the fact that these organisms in polluted rivers experience extreme sulfur-like stress and thus develop adaptive metabolism to sulfide.

The impact of sulfide on anammox has been studied, and diverse observations have been noted regarding the promoting or inhibiting effects of sulfide on anammox. Some studies suggest that sulfide may have direct toxic and irreversible effects on anammox bacteria by destroying the protein structure or directly competing for electron acceptors (e.g., nitrite) for denitrification with anammox (Chen et al. 2008), whereas other scholars maintain that anammox bacterial activity can gradually recover with decreasing sulfide concentration (Jin et al. 2013; Chen et al. 2024). Based on the actual sulfide levels in pore water in malodorous rivers, 40 mg·L−1 sulfide was added to further explore the impacts of S on anammox. The results show that although anammox activity was inhibited with 40 mg·L−1 sulfide, the anammox-related Chloroflexi (44.35%) and Planctomycetes-like bacteria (35.26%) were still observed. In addition, Thiobacillus maintained a relatively high abundance (6.98%), and nirS levels also increased from 0.16% to 1.48%, indicating the promotion of SAD in anammox incubation with 40 mg·L−1 sulfide. These suggest that the occurrence of SAD leads to a decrease in sulfide levels and promotes the coupling of SAPD and anammox, thus reducing sulfide toxicity to anammox bacteria. Similarly, Russ et al. (2014) find that S transformation-related bacteria can mitigate the toxicity of sulfide to allow anammox bacteria to stay active.

Calcium nitrate addition is often considered an effective remediation technology and makes significant contributions to the S and N interaction in rivers (He et al. 2021; Mai et al. 2021). For instance, Sheng et al. (2021) find that calcium nitrate facilitates the rapid startup of the anammox process. In our research, calcium nitrate addition greatly promotes the process of SAPD coupled with anammox. The nitrate provided by calcium nitrate facilitates the oxidation of reduced sulfur (e.g., AVS), which can be used to conduct partial denitrification by S-related bacteria (Thiobacillus) (Deng et al. 2022). Consequently, the accumulated nitrite can be utilized in the anammox process. Moreover, the calcium nitrate addition system exhibited a concurrent increase in sulfide-oxidizing bacteria (Thiobacillus from 0.36% to 1.12%; Sulfuritalea from 0.12% to 0.78%), sulfate-reducing bacteria (Desulfococcus from 0.08% to 1.37%; Dechloromonas from 0.07% to 0.67%), and anammox bacteria (AnAOB gene abundance from 0.45% to 0.72%). These observations highlight the important coupling between S-cycling and N-transformation. Previous research has also shown that partial denitrification can take place in high sulfide conditions (e.g., influent concentration of 300 mg-S·L−1) (Hayakawa et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2017b). Interestingly, Deng et al. (2021) find that anammox has a higher tolerance of sulfide in the SAPD coupled anammox system than in the simple anammox system, and anammox plays a primary role in N removal in this coupling system, which can be further confirmed by the relatively high gene abundance of AnAOB under calcium addition in our study.

Aeration has a substantial impact on the intrinsic N-transformation in urban rivers (Yin et al. 2019b). Notably, during our experiment, aeration in the overlying-water exhibits better TN removal (82.15%) compared to sediment aeration (67.52%). This observation could be supported by the fact that a moderate concentration of reduced sulfide released by overlying-water aeration promotes SAD. In contrast, sediment aeration releases a large amount of reduced sulfide, which inhibits denitrification and the anammox process. Previous research also demonstrates that different aeration depths can cause significant changes in bacterial communities related to S and N (Chen et al. 2019a). Hence, the appropriate aeration intensity and method should be taken into consideration when treating malodorous rivers through aeration remediation in the future.

In conclusion, S plays a multifaceted role in N-transformation in urban rivers through SAD, SAPD, and anammox. The impact of sulfide on denitrification and anammox is concentration-dependent. Anammox has a lower tolerance for sulfide and is inhibited under a 40 mg·L−1 sulfide level. Meanwhile, SAD could be stimulated under the same condition. The river simulation showed that calcium nitrate addition could promote SAPD, producing nitrite for anammox and thus enhancing their coupling. This coupling is mediated by S-driven autotrophic denitrifiers (Thiobacillus) and AnAOB. Additionally, overlying-water aeration exhibited relatively high TN removal through nitrification–denitrification promoted by released reduced sulfur. A significant correlation (P < 0.01) between TN and SO42− was also observed, which indicated that sulfide was mainly involved in the N-transformation through SAD. This study can fill the gap in understanding the interactions of S and N biogeochemical processes with river remediations and can help establish an effective approach for TN removal in practical application.
