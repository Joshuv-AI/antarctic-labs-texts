Interregional reimbursement reform in China lowers administrative barriers to healthcare utilization
Authors: Yueying Cui, Ruihua Feng, Hui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeographic segmentation of health insurance creates administrative barriers that limit patients’ access to out-of-network healthcare, particularly in underserved regions. We assess the impact of a reimbursement reform removing interregional administrative barriers on patient healthcare utilization.MethodsWe conduct a quantitative quasi-experimental study using longitudinal administrative data from a representative city with limited high-tier medical resources. The causal effects of the reform are estimated based on patient visit records from January 2021 to November 2022 using a regression discontinuity of time (RDiT) design.ResultsThe reform significantly increases intercity healthcare visits by up to 11.2%, especially among low-income patients and women. While patients with severe conditions benefit, increased utilization for minor ailments also occurs. This surge in demand strains medical resources, leading to increased readmission rates without reducing hospital stays.ConclusionsRemoving administrative barriers enhances equity but may reduce healthcare efficiency if demand management is neglected. Policymakers should combine expanded access with investments in local healthcare capacity and demand-side controls to ensure sustainable and equitable service delivery.Subject terms: Health services, Public health

Introduction

The uneven geographical distribution of healthcare resources is a persistent issue, with high-quality medical facilities often concentrated in economically advanced areas. Patients in regions with underdeveloped healthcare infrastructure must often travel to access superior medical services when faced with significant health needs. In theory, such spatial disparities in healthcare supply need not translate into unequal access if patients can move freely across regions. In practice, however, these patients frequently encounter administrative and economic barriers to seeking care1–3 due to geographically segmented insurance coverage.

China provides a salient example of how insurance-induced frictions interact with spatial inequality in healthcare resources. Public medical insurance reimbursement has historically been tied to the insured individual’s home city, reflecting city-level fund pooling and localized administration. While medical insurance expenditures incurred locally are typically reimbursed through instant settlement, patients seeking care in other cities are required to pay the full medical expenses upfront and subsequently apply for reimbursement in their home city. This institutional arrangement resembles the experience of “out-of-network” patients in other healthcare systems4,5 but is amplified by additional procedural requirements and reimbursement penalties. As a result, insurance segmentation generates substantial administrative barriers to intercity healthcare utilization6,7

Specifically, reimbursement for intercity care was conditional on obtaining pre-authorization, usually in the form of referrals from local physicians. But the approval were uncertain and often discretionary, partly because local providers faced weak incentives to refer patients to hospitals in other cities. Even when authorization was granted, reimbursement was delayed and often provided at lower effective rates than for local care. For patients, especially those facing high treatment costs, this system created significant ex ante uncertainty regarding coverage eligibility and substantial financial liquidity risk due to delayed and partial reimbursement. These risks could dominate relatively modest travel costs and deter patients from seeking care outside their home city, even when higher-quality treatment was unavailable locally.

Since 2016, China has gradually pursued interregional integration of its public medical insurance system through a series of reimbursement reforms aimed at reducing these institutional frictions. In this paper, we focus on one such reform implemented on September 1, 2021, in our sample city. The policy standardized reimbursement rates and deductibles for intercity healthcare to match those applied in the patient’s home city and enabled real-time settlement without any pre-authorization. As a result, insured patients could freely seek healthcare services across cities within the province without facing approval uncertainty, delayed reimbursement, or financial penalties. We refer to this policy change as the Interregional Reimbursement Reform. Supplementary materials provide a detailed institutional background.

Reforms that eliminate such frictions can significantly improve access and equity, yet their effects are often underappreciated. Our sample city is medically underserved and exhibits strong unmet demand for higher-quality care in nearby cities, making it an ideal setting to study how easing administrative frictions affects healthcare utilization and distributional equity. Our analysis examines not only the intended equity-enhancing effects of the reform but also its implications for patient behavior and resource allocation. While demand-side reforms can deliver immediate gains in accessibility and equity, they may also generate unintended behavioral responses. By making it substantially easier for patients to seek care in higher-tier hospitals outside their home locality, the policy raises the possibility of patients’ over-utilizing intercity higher-quality services for conditions that could be treated locally8,9 Such behavioral shifts could strain already scarce high-level medical resources and potentially reduce system-wide efficiency.

Policies aimed at improving healthcare equity typically follow two broad strategies. One approach focuses on the demand side, reducing patient-level barriers so individuals can travel to obtain higher-quality care; the reform we study fall into this category. The alternative approach targets the supply side, redistributing or expanding high-quality medical resources to better match local demand, but this approach is relatively costly to implement. Both strategies are widely discussed in the global health economics literature, particularly in multi-jurisdictional systems where administrative boundaries shape access to care10–14 Demand-side access reforms are fast and low-cost, but without supply-side capacity expansion, they may reallocate congestion rather than improve overall system performance.

From a policy perspective, we underscore the necessity for a systematic approach that carefully considers the multiple conflicts inherent in healthcare delivery. Promoting healthcare equity should not rely solely on increasing patient mobility, as this approach may unintentionally overload high-quality facilities. Instead, a sustainable long-term strategy requires strengthening local healthcare capacity, ensuring that patients can receive appropriate care without unnecessary intercity movement. This supply-side perspective better aligns resource distribution with patient needs and reduces efficiency pressures created by high cross-region demand.

Methods

We exploit the Interregional Reimbursement Reform as a natural experiment to identify its effects on healthcare utilization and patient behavior. The detailed definition of variables is listed in the Supplementary Table B1.

Data

We use large-scale anonymous administrative data from a representative city with limited high-tier medical facilities. This study was approved by the Ethical Review of Research of Institutional Review Board of Institute of Medical Information, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College (IMICAMS/04/25/HREC). The original dataset spans January 1, 2021, to November 31, 2022, and covers all patient visits recorded through medical insurance card usage, including both inpatient and outpatient records, totaling 952,716 entries. We exclude pediatric visits (4.9%) and out-of-province care (10.7%), and focus only on a 60-day window before and after the reform, yielding a sample of 100,525 records. As shown in Supplementary Table B2,94.39% of local visits occurred at secondary hospitals, while 77.23% of intercity visits were to tertiary hospitals, highlighting the disparity in care levels and the relevance of studying patient mobility under the reform.

Each entry includes patient demographics, vulnerable identity tags (e.g., low-income, severe disability), visit details, ICD-10 diagnoses, length of stay, and medical expenses. It is particularly important to explain that the low-income group consists of individuals receiving government subsidies for basic living needs. In addition to enjoying basic medical insurance, they also have other medical subsidy benefits.

Regression discontinuity in time (RDiT)

To identify policy impacts, we apply a Regression Discontinuity in Time (RDiT) design, using time as the forcing variable15,16, This method is widely used in health economics17–19 and sociology20,21 RDiT is particularly suitable given our single-city setting, where Difference-in-Differences (DID) is infeasible due to the lack of a comparable control group.

The forcing variable we use is the calendar day, measured as the number of days before (negative values) and after (positive values) the official implementation date of the Reform. We use a standard nonparametric approach, and the specific equation is as follows:

We use visit-level data without aggregation, where each observation represents the attributes of patient i’s visit on date t. The outcome variable Yi,t includes IntercityHealthcarei,t, which equals 1 if the visit occurred outside the patient’s home city, and 0 otherwise. The key treatment variable, Policyt, equals 1 after the implementation of the reform, and 0 otherwise. The control function f(Dayt) is modeled as a flexible polynomial. Bandwidth selection follows the MSE-optimal method by Cattaneo and Titiunik (2022)22, using a first-degree polynomial to minimize overfitting. The error term is denoted as μi,t.

To validate the RDiT assumptions, we perform balance test, manipulation test, exogeneity test, and a series of falsification analyses in Supplementary materials. We also address potential calendar effects—such as weekday, monthly, or seasonal patterns—that may bias estimates. Following Hausman and Rapson (2018)15, we adopt an augmented local linear approach. In the first step, we use a two-year window to regress outcomes on date fixed effects (month, day of month, day of week) and extract residuals, removing cyclical variation. In the second step, we apply local linear estimation to these residuals within a ± 60-day window around the policy cutoff. This two-stage procedure ensures that results are not driven by temporal patterns unrelated to the reform.

Results

Prior to formal analysis, we summarize patient characteristics by healthcare location in Supplementary Table B3. Low-income and female patients are less likely to travel, while intercity patients are younger on average. Intercity visits involve more high-risk diagnoses, longer hospital stays, and lower readmission rates. Supplementary Table B4 documents that the monetary cost of intercity travel is relatively small compared with the cost of medical treatment. We also conduct an OLS regression in Supplementary Table B5 to identify factors associated with intercity healthcare utilization, using a binary indicator for intercity visits as the outcome and controlling for fixed effects. Results are consistent with the descriptive patterns.

Figure 1 visualizes the impact of the Interregional Reimbursement Reform on intercity healthcare visits. The x-axis denotes days relative to the policy date, with scatter points representing two-day averages. Linear trends fitted before and after the cutoff, along with 95% confidence intervals, reveal a clear discontinuity: daily intercity visits rose sharply from ~250 to over 400. The confidence intervals do not overlap across the breakpoint, indicating a statistically significant jump in patient mobility. While a modest decline appears about a month post-reform, the visit level remains well above pre-reform averages. These patterns suggest that the policy meaningfully increased access or reduced frictions in intercity healthcare utilization.

The formal estimates in Supplementary Table B6 and plots in Supplementary Fig. B1 reveal important institutional heterogeneity stemming from China’s dual insurance system. China operates two major public medical insurance schemes: Urban–Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI), which covers informal workers and residents, and Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI), which serves formally employed workers. Prior to the reform, both groups faced identical administrative frictions when seeking intercity care. However, the two schemes differ substantially in their benefit designs, especially for outpatient care. URRBMI features low outpatient reimbursement caps and narrow coverage for intercity outpatient services, so even after the reform removes administrative barriers, outpatient financial protection remains limited. As a result, URRBMI enrollees exhibit significant increases only in inpatient intercity utilization (0.079 in Panel A; 0.086 in Panel B), with no detectable outpatient response. In contrast, UEBMI provides broader outpatient coverage and higher reimbursement ceilings. Once administrative frictions were eliminated and reimbursement rates standardized across jurisdictions, UEBMI enrollees experienced significant increases in both outpatient and inpatient for intercity care.

We conduct another subgroup analysis based on patients’ economic status, gender, and age to examine the effects of the reform on intercity medical utilization. The results are presented in Table 1. Panel A shows that although intercity utilization increased for both low- and non-low-income patients, the effect for low-income individuals receiving government living subsidies is more than twice as large. This pattern aligns with the fact that low-income patients faced more binding administrative constraints prior to the reform (as shown in Supplementary Table B3). Consequently, the reform disproportionately relaxes constraints for low-income patients, leading to stronger behavioral responses. Panel B reports heterogeneity by gender and shows that female patients experience significantly larger effects. The female is more sensitive to information about policies related to family life such as healthcare services, implying higher opportunity costs of obtaining public service access. Removing restrictions on intercity healthcare reimbursements therefore reduces more monetary and non-monetary burdens for female. Panel C groups patients by physical mobility, generally considering those over 60 as “Old” and those over 75 as “Senior,” who are often seen as less mobile. While seniors show lower sensitivity to the reform with an RDiT estimate of 0.091 compared to 0.109 for young and middle-aged patients, the differences across age groups are not statistically significant. Overall, the findings highlight the policy’s equity-enhancing role. By removing administrative and economic barriers, the reform disproportionately benefited disadvantaged groups and broadened access across demographic lines.

Next, we examine potential provider-side responses, such as overcharging or differential treatment of intercity patients who may not be subject to local DRG budget constraints23. We conduct the analysis separately for local and intercity patients and compare how treatment intensity and medical expenses change before and after the reform within each group. This approach allows us to assess whether intercity patients experience differential changes relative to local patients following the policy.

In Table 2, panel A, we examine the impact of the reform on inpatient stays using RDiT estimation for both local and non-local patient groups. If differential treatment by medical staff were present, we would expect to see significant differences in hospitalization intensity between these groups, as such treatment could influence the duration of hospital stays. Columns (1) and (2) use the natural logarithm of the raw length of stay as the dependent variable. In contrast, columns (3) and (4) adjust for disease severity using disease type fixed effects, analyzing residuals from these regressions. This process involves several steps. First, we include ICD-10 code as a factor in the regression to account for cost variations due to different conditions. Next, we run a regression of inpatient stays on these ICD-10 fixed effects and calculate the residuals. These residuals, then used as the dependent variable, represent medical expenses adjusted for disease type, essentially isolating the impact of other factors on costs. The findings indicate that the reform has virtually no significant impact on the length of stay for either local or non-local hospitalizations.

We then observe the effects of the reform on medical expenses in Table 2, panel B, categorized by local and intercity treatments. Columns (1) and (2) use raw medical expenses as the dependent variable, while columns (3) and (4) adjust for disease severity. Interestingly, the reform shows no significant impact on local medical expenses, as indicated by the negligible coefficients in columns (1) and (3). However, for intercity treatments, there is a counterintuitive reduction in average medical expenses post-reform, as seen in column (2), suggesting that the reform may have led to cost savings or more efficient billing practices for intercity patients. Yet, when controlling for disease severity with ICD-10 fixed effects, this significant reduction disappears in column (4). This indicates that the initial observed decrease in expenses might be attributed to changes in the case mix or severity of conditions treated rather than purely cost-reducing behaviors. Together, these findings suggest that healthcare providers do not manipulate treatment costs or hospitalization duration in response to insurance policy changes.

However, the observed decrease in average costs for non-local medical treatments, which disappears after controlling for disease severity, may indicate a potential behavioral responses at the patient level. This involves patients overestimating their healthcare needs and overutilizing medical services post-reform. The reform improved reimbursement rates for non-local treatments, granting patients the freedom to choose their treatment locations. This change may lead patients, even those with mild conditions, to seek care at higher-quality non-local hospitals.

In Table 3, we provide empirical evidence supporting this hypothesis. In Panel A, we analyze non-local medical visits by illness severity to assess the impact of the reform. The RDiT estimates are significant for both Common illnesses and Hard-to-treat illnesses, indicating that patients not only seek more treatment for serious conditions but also increase utilization for less severe ailments. In contrast, the analysis of local medical visits shows no significant impact from the reform for either group, indicating that the observed increase in non-local medical visits for both categories reflects additional demand rather than a substitution away from local care, as shown in Panel B. These findings suggest that patients are addressing previously unmet needs for serious conditions and are also overutilizing services for minor ailments and highlights the need for policies to manage overutilization while ensuring necessary care is accessible.

The surge in intercity healthcare utilization following the reform may have important implications for healthcare system capacity and efficiency. A primary concern is the strain on medical resources and personnel, which may compromise healthcare quality and efficiency. Specifically, doctors require time to become familiar with patients’ medical histories and conditions, which can be challenging with an influx of new patients. Additionally, the healthcare system may become overwhelmed, leading to misallocation of resources or delays in treatment24,25, To empirically assess the impact of the reform on healthcare quality, we use a subset of non-common illnesses and adopt the 30-day readmission rate as a proxy measure for medical quality. As shown in Table 2, panel C, the RDiT estimates support our hypothesis: there is an increase in the readmission rates for intercity patients post-reform, whereas local patients do not exhibit a similar change. This may suggest that the quality of care for intercity patients may have declined due to the increased patient load and associated challenges.

Discussion

The Interregional Reimbursement Reform in China led to a significant rise in intercity care by removing pre-authorization requirements and eliminating reimbursement penalties for intercity care, and disproportionately benefited individuals who were previously constrained by administrative complexity, uncertainty, and liquidity risk, indicating a meaningful improvement in access equity.

The reform also generated unintended challenges. While we find no evidence of provider-side overcharging or strategic behavior, some patients increasingly sought higher-tier care for relatively minor conditions, expanding total healthcare demand without substituting away from local services. These patterns are consistent with a setting in which administrative barriers previously served as a coarse demand-management device. By granting patients full geographic and institutional flexibility, the reform weakened demand discipline and facilitated large-scale patient mobility across cities. In the short run, medical capacity and follow-up systems did not fully adjust to the surge in non-local patients. As a result, increased demand likely strained hospital resources and disrupted care continuity, leading to higher readmission rates without a corresponding reduction in length of stay.

Equity and efficiency operate on different margins in this reform. Equity gains arise from the removal of administrative and financial barriers that had disproportionately excluded disadvantaged groups from accessing higher-quality care. Efficiency losses, in contrast, reflect transitional capacity constraints and the absence of complementary demand-management mechanisms, rather than a reversal of equity improvements. The coexistence of these effects highlights the need for policy packages that combine expanded access with investments in referral coordination, triage protocols, and capacity expansion in high-demand destinations26–29 to ensure that broader access does not come at the cost of care quality.

Our study contributes to several strands of literature. First, we enrich the health insurance deregulation literature by focusing on how removing both administrative and financial barriers reshapes patient behavior. Unlike prior studies that emphasize provider-side moral hazard23,30–32 we center on patient-level responses, especially ex post moral hazard8,9,33–35, providing suggestive evidence on how expanded coverage and reduced geographic frictions can unintentionally increase demand and strain care quality.

Second, we contribute to the literature on financial frictions and liquidity sensitivity in healthcare2,36, From the perspective of institutional economics, the administrative barriers such as mandatory ex ante approvals or record-filing procedures, represent non-price constraints that distort optimal resource allocation37,38, We discuss how removing upfront costs and authorization requirements enhanced mobility by easing liquidity constraints, especially for lower-income patients and women, consistent with literature that emphasize higher barriers for these group to obtain public services39–44

Finally, we inform the literature on out-of-network billing and segmented insurance systems24. Unlike U.S.-based studies centered on provider incentives4, China’s government-run universal coverage allows us to isolate the effects of administrative accessibility without price renegotiation. This offers insight into how public systems manage cross-regional access and patient mobility under uniform pricing.

Conclusion

Administrative barriers distort healthcare allocation and are common in multi-jurisdictional systems globally. Removing them improves access and equity but risks overloading top-tier facilities without demand management. Policymakers should combine coverage expansion with local capacity building and behavioral tools like triage protocols to balance fairness and efficiency. These insights offer practical guidance for health reforms in diverse countries facing similar institutional challenges.
