Balancing in Virtual Reality: Motor Learning and Functional Improvement in Down Syndrome
Authors: Evelyn Ardai, Tibor Vámos, Gabriella Papp, Andrea Berencsi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMotor learning in individuals with Down syndrome (DS) may be influenced by syndrome‐specific motor and cognitive characteristics. Virtual reality (VR) systems are becoming increasingly popular and offer a valuable opportunity to study motor learning. To date, only a few studies have examined motor learning to improve balance in DS. This study aimed to investigate motor learning and functional motor performance in young adults with DS during VR‐based training.MethodsParticipants with DS (n = 31) and typical development (TD = 28) between 18 and 30 years of age took part in the study. Participants were randomly allocated into training (DS Wii and TD Wii) and control (DS C and TD C) groups. Training groups participated in a 4‐week‐long balance training with two Nintendo Wii balance games, whereas their performance was monitored. All participants underwent pre‐ and posttraining assessment of functional motor performance.ResultsDuring the VR training, the DS Wii and TD Wii groups' game scores show significant performance improvements in both games. Learning curves showed great individual differences, especially in DS. Although TD groups outperformed DS groups in the functional motor tests, DS Wii group's pre‐ and post‐training results indicated a significant improvement in functional motor performance.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that individuals with DS are able to learn in a VR environment and successfully transfer the acquired skills to functional abilities. TD individuals also improved their functional performance or reached a plateau. We conclude that VR is an effective system for engaging DS individuals in motor training for improving balance skills.Keywords: balance, Down syndrome, motor learning, virtual reality

Introduction

Down syndrome (DS) is the most common chromosomal disorder associated with intellectual disability (ID) and can occur across any ethnicities. The global prevalence of DS is approximately 1/800. It is primarily caused by trisomy of chromosome 21 (Bull 2020). The motor profile of individuals with DS is characterized by generalized muscle hypotonia, ligamentous laxity and hypermobility, reduced muscle strength (Galli et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2012), lower limb orthopaedic conditions such as pes planus (Galli et al. 2008) and individuals with DS also present difficulties in agonist and antagonist muscle cocontraction (Alesi and Battaglia 2019; Bieć et al. 2014; Giustino et al. 2021). These biomechanical characteristics can affect postural control (Bieć et al. 2014; Giustino et al. 2021; Massó‐Ortigosa et al. 2024; Silva et al. 2017) that is essential for performing everyday activities (Leite et al. 2018; Villarroya et al. 2012).

Postural control requires two distinct processes, the integration of multisensory inputs and the organization and execution of an adequate motor response (Villarroya et al. 2012; Vuillerme et al. 2001). These processes may show deficits from early childhood and continue to affect postural control in later life (Rigoldi et al. 2011; Vuillerme et al. 2001), resulting in balance dysfunction in individuals with DS (Galli et al. 2008; Rigoldi et al. 2011). Moreover, difficulties in postural control can negatively affect the independence and activities of daily living of individuals with DS (Horvat et al. 2013; Rigoldi et al. 2011). Therefore, studying the characteristics of postural control and its improvement is essential for the development of rehabilitation strategies in DS (Villarroya et al. 2012).

Virtual reality (VR) technology has gained attention in the field of assessment in motor learning (Cavalcante Neto et al. 2021; de Mello Monteiro et al. 2017; Smits‐Engelsman et al. 2015) and real‐word skills (Franze et al. 2024; Michalski et al. 2023). VR‐based active video game systems, such as the Nintendo Wii, are widely available on the commercial market. These systems simulate real‐world games and sports activities and promote interaction and active engagement (Mouawad et al. 2011; Silva et al. 2017; Wuang et al. 2011). They also allow for controlled manipulation of practice conditions and offer features for monitoring performance over time (Smits‐Engelsman et al. 2015). Additionally, the continuous feedback provided to users makes VR technology a potentially applicable tool in the field of motor learning (Cavalcante Neto et al. 2021; Menezes et al. 2015; Smits‐Engelsman et al. 2015). Furthermore, active video game systems offer a multifaceted learning environment for individuals with disabilities, where acquired skills can be transferred to real‐world contexts (Franze et al. 2024; Michalski et al. 2023).

Motor learning is a complex process, ideally involving improved performance on the task and retention and transfer of skills over time (Smits‐Engelsman et al. 2015). The characteristics of motor learning in individuals with ID may differ from those observed in TD individuals (Berencsi et al. 2016, 2017). However, limited research has specifically examined motor learning in individuals with DS in a VR environment (de Mello Monteiro et al. 2017). Although there have been studies focusing on functional skill improvement following VR‐based interventions (Berg et al. 2012; Lin and Wuang 2012; Silva et al. 2017; Wuang et al. 2011), a focus on the characteristics of motor learning in terms of performance improvements throughout the training process (Schmidt et al. 2019) is still lacking in the literature. A further important question is whether performance improvement in a VR environment is associated with improvement of functional abilities, which reflects the transfer of acquired skills (Franze et al. 2024; Michalski et al. 2023; Vámos et al. 2018).

Therefore, the primary aim of the study is to determine the learning characteristics during balance training using VR technology in DS and TD. The main focus is on examining group‐level performance changes during practice without assessing game performance retention. A secondary objective is to explore whether improvement in VR environment transfers to improvement in functional balance skills after VR training, which refers to the short‐term changes in functional motor abilities following a 4‐week training intervention. To address these questions, we investigate learning performance and pre‐ and posttraining functional motor performance in young adults with DS and age‐matched TD individuals in a course of a VR balance training programme. We hypothesize that the pretraining performance of TD participants would be superior to that of participants with DS in balance, mobility and muscle strength (Wang et al. 2012). Moreover, we hypothesized that both DS and TD participants' game performance would improve over the sessions of VR‐based balance training (Cavalcante Neto et al. 2021; Smits‐Engelsman et al. 2015). In addition to game performance, we also expected improvements in functional balance (Smits‐Engelsman et al. 2015) and a potential indirect improvement in muscle strength (Lin and Wuang 2012; Silva et al. 2017).

After the analysis of homogeneity of variances, the parametric and nonparametric tests indicated significant difference only with regard to age between the groups (p = 0.006).

DS participants were recruited from a convenience sample from three special educational centres for children with intellectual disability. TD participants were recruited from Eötvös Loránd University Bárczi Gusztáv Faculty of Special Needs Education. Inclusion criteria were (a) diagnosis of DS based on the analysis of the karyotype for DS participants; (b) age ranging between 18 and 30 years; (c) being able to stand and walk independently; (d) being able to understand and follow simple instructions; and (e) parent(s)/legal tutors and/or participants willing to provide written consent (information adapted for DS participants). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) severe musculoskeletal problems (e.g., hip, knee, foot or spinal deformities that limit exercise); (b) severe or not corrected visual or hearing problems (not able to follow visual image or instructions of the game); (c) previous history of neurological disorders such as traumatic brain injury, dizziness and epilepsy; and (d) uncontrolled cardiovascular disease. Excluded were participants who carried coexisting autism, ADHD or cerebral palsy. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (KEB/2021/022), and all the participants or their parents/legal tutors provided written informed consent.

DS and TD participants were randomly allocated to the training groups: Wii‐based exercise groups [DS Wii group (n = 16) and TD Wii group (n = 15)] or control groups [DS control (DS C) group (n = 15) and TD control (TD C) group (n = 16)]. Three participants from the TD Wii group did not complete the Wii training because they were unable to attend all training sessions. Thus, 59 participants completed all the training sessions and assessment procedures (Figure 1).

Procedure and Materials

Procedure

All data were collected at the beginning and after the training programme by a certified special needs education therapist. The investigator was not blinded. The procedure was carried out in an isolated room, for the DS participants in their school and for the TD participants at the university. Participants were tested individually. Participants familiarized themselves with the experimental setup and DS participants were supported by total communication. First, height, weight, foot physical examination and randomly ordered functional motor assessment were performed. The training groups then participated in a 4‐week Wii Balance training programme. During the training period, control groups continued with their usual daily activities without modifications (Figure 2).

The evaluation and the procedure.

Motor Learning Paradigm and Performance Measurement With Nintendo Wii

Nintendo Wii Balance Board (WBB) is an accessory designed for the Nintendo Wii gaming console and Wii Fit balance activities. The balance board contains four pressure sensors, which allow the user to control an avatar while playing balance games.

In Penguin slide game, the player tilts his/her body left and right to guide the penguin catching as many fish as possible. The result is determined by the number of the collected fish (game score). In Soccer heading game, the player guides the goalkeeper to head soccer balls by tilting into the path of them. The result is determined by the number of the headed balls (game score). The movement patterns of these games allow to facilitate a complex development of balance.

The Nintendo Wii allows for individualized rates of progression. The games operate on a staircase principle whereby task difficulty increases in response to individual performance. One of the selection criteria was that participants were able to understand verbal and visual instructions. All participants familiarized themselves with the games prior to the start of the training. Verbal and physical assistance was provided to all participants to support them in controlling their Mii characters. Task comprehension and execution were further supported by continuous visual feedback from the Wii and the immediate on‐screen display of scores. Throughout the practice sessions, feedback was provided to all participants, and assistance was offered to help them interpret their game scores.

DS Wii and TD Wii participants followed the same Nintendo Wii Balance training using a projected screen and a WBB placed 1.5 m from the wall. Each game was practiced five times per session with 1‐min rests. Participants stood on the WBB in sneakers, guided by on‐screen instructions and an investigator who was responsible for operating the console and recording data. Participants completed two individual sessions per week, individually. The training programme consisted of eight sessions under 4 weeks, integrated into school PE lessons for the DS Wii group and university activities for the TD Wii group. The environmental conditions were identical for both groups. Control groups (DS C and TD C) continued regular PE and their daily activities. Participants were asked to report their usual physical activity level by a questionnaire during the study. Group differences in physical activity levels were examined using chi‐square tests of independence based on the distribution of participants across usual activity level. The distribution of physical activity levels did not differ significantly between groups (Table S17).

Functional Motor Assessment

Functional motor performance was determined using the following tests: (1) Berg Balance Scale (BBS) assesses balance and risk of falls through 14 functional tasks, each scored on a scale from 0 to 4, with 56 as the maximum total (Berg 1989). (2) Five times sit‐to‐stand test (FTSST) measures lower limb strength along with balance and postural control. The time taken to complete the test is recorded and expressed in seconds (Guerrero et al. 2023; Whitney et al. 2005). (3) Timed up and go test (TUG) maps a person's mobility, functional balance and gait speed. The total time taken is recorded and expressed in seconds (Guerrero et al. 2023; Podsiadlo and Richardson 1991). (4) Plank Test assesses core muscle endurance in a functional manner. The time, participants hold in plank position, is recorded and expressed in second (maximum 30 s) (Boyer et al. 2013). (5) 30‐s sit up test measures core strength and endurance, particularly in the abdominal muscles. The total number of sit ups are recorded for 30 s (Skowroński et al. 2009).

Statistical Analysis

The learning effect was investigated by analysing Nintendo Wii training outcomes (game scores in each training session) of the DS and TD training groups. A two‐way mixed model of analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on each dependent variable with repeated measures on training sessions (training sessions × two intervention groups). Multiple comparisons with LSD were performed when a significant main effect or interaction was present.

Functional motor assessment outcomes (BBS, FTSST, TUG, Plank Test and 30‐s sit up test) were analysed and compared between the four study groups in the means of pretraining and posttraining performance. A two‐way mixed model ANOVA (pre‐ and posttraining × four study groups) was performed on each dependent variable. Multiple comparisons with LSD were performed when a significant main effect or interaction was present. The significance level was set at p < 0.05, and SPSS Version 20.0 (IBM Corporation, New York, NY, USA) was used for the analysis.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to investigate the characteristics of motor learning and map the functional transfer effect of a balance training in a VR environment in young adult individuals with DS and TD. Our findings indicated a clear learning effect during training for both groups. Furthermore, the training was followed by a positive transfer effect to functional balance, mobility and an indirect transfer effect to core and lower limb muscle strength in young individuals with DS and TD.

Our findings demonstrate for the first time, that VR balance training enhances gaming performance in individuals with DS with a differential learning curve from TD. In the Penguin slide game, the DS group mean performance exhibited a consistent and significant improvement across training sessions, with no evidence of a ceiling effect up to the eighth session (Figure 3, Tables S1 and S5). TD participants displayed higher baseline performance, showed a significant improvement during training sessions, but their performance plateaued in the final three sessions, indicating a possible ceiling effect (Figure 4, Tables S3 and S6). In the Soccer heading game, the DS group did not exhibit significant changes in mean performance during the first three sessions, although the individual performance variability showed a decreasing trend. This lack of improvement at group level during the first three sessions may indicate a prolonged fast phase of motor learning (Dayan and Cohen 2011), potentially caused by the cognitive load of the task or the difficulty of identifying effective postural strategies. Despite the lacking significant difference between the first and last sessions, significant improvement from the third to the eighth session confirmed learning effect in the DS Wii group (Figure 5, Tables S2 and S7). TD participants started from a higher level and showed a continuous improvement without a ceiling effect (Figure 6, Tables S4 and S8). Children with developmental coordination disorder (DCD) and TD demonstrated similar learning pattern with Nintendo Wii (Smits‐Engelsman et al. 2015). After participants completed 100 repetitions of Ski Slalom game, no ceiling effect was observed that indicates potential for further improvement. Similar to our balance training, young adult individuals with DS gradually reduced the number and the variability of errors and improved performance during anticipatory timing task (de Mello Monteiro et al. 2017) in VR environment and finger force production task (Latash et al. 2002). We found high level individual differences in baseline performance and improvement not only in ID but also in TD in the present study. In ID, fine motor learning was characterized by high variability in both baseline performance and learning trajectories with Williams syndrome (WS). Nonetheless, task‐specific practice led to measurable learning effects, even though WS participants typically did not reach the performance levels of TD participants by the end of the training (Berencsi et al. 2016, 2017) similar to our present findings. Our results support that Nintendo Wii VR games can effectively facilitate motor learning (Cavalcante Neto et al. 2021; Smits‐Engelsman et al. 2015) both in DS and TD. Furthermore, DS and TD group‐level differences in learning curves (i.e., prolonged fast phase and ceiling effect) and individual variability in both groups suggests that stages of motor learning and individual speed of learning need to be considered when designing motor training programmes in VR environment.

The present findings indicate that a 4‐week VR‐based balance training using commercially available gaming systems may represent a feasible and effective intervention for improving motor learning. This is consistent with evidence from special needs education, where interactive and feedback‐rich virtual environments have been shown to support skill development and provide continuous feedback that enhances learning and motivation across diverse user groups, including individuals with intellectual disabilities (Ardai et al. 2022; Standen and Brown 2005). Moreover, there is an indication that skills acquired in virtual environments can transfer to real‐world tasks, reinforcing the practical relevance of such interventions for everyday functional outcomes (Franze et al. 2024; Michalski et al. 2023).

In our study, functional skill improvement was also present after VR training that resulted in enhanced lower limb and core muscle strength, as well as improved balance and mobility function in the DS training group. These results are consistent with transfer effect of VR training with Nintendo Wii to core muscle strength (Silva et al. 2017), to lower limb muscle strength in adult individuals with DS (Lin and Wuang 2012), to improvement in balance in children (Wuang et al. 2011) with DS. We also demonstrated that transfer to various functional skills was also present in TD that has been partly previously showed by Smits‐Engelsman et al. (2015) following the VR training.

The observed improvement in functional motor skills in the training groups supports the use of VR‐based balance exercises. These findings are especially relevant for clinical practice because improvement in functional outcomes such as mobility and balance is more directly reflected in participation and independence of the participants in everyday tasks. Similar findings have been reported in clinical rehabilitation settings, where VR‐based training was shown to induce indirect improvements in functional skills such as balance and ambulatory skills, after practicing VR‐based balance games. Improvements in postural control and mobility are particularly relevant because these domains directly affect participation and independence in everyday activities such as self‐care and mobility (Hung et al. 2014; Karasu et al. 2018).

The interactive nature of commercially available VR applications requires active and immersive participation from the user and provides real movement experiences and clear performance goals. The pronounced differences observed in individual performance and learning patterns highlight the need for individual progression strategies that account for varying baseline/initial skill levels and learning rates. In populations characterized by developmental heterogeneity, such as individuals with DS or other intellectual disabilities, flexible modulation of task difficulty and progression may be essential to ensure optimal skill acquisition when implementing VR‐based training into clinical or educational settings. Fixed training protocols may be less effective than those dynamically customizable to individual performance profiles, particularly in individuals with ID.

In addition, our findings indicate that monitoring individual response patterns across sessions may be essential for identifying performance decline, stagnation or rapid improvement, allowing timely adjustments in task difficulty. Systematic reassessment of functional motor skills alongside the game performance measures may further support decision‐making of the therapists or investigators regarding progression and task variability. Furthermore, monitoring performance improvement can align with functional goals relevant to everyday activities and participation.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, although clear group‐level improvements were observed in game performance in the training groups, the absence of follow‐up retention measurement limits conclusions regarding long‐term changes due to motor learning. Although the primary aim of the study was to examine short‐term performance changes and functional transfer during a structured training period rather than to assess long‐term retention.

Second, modest improvement in 30‐s sit up test results of TD control group may reflect alternative explanations, like the test–retest effects, increased familiarity with the assessment procedure, natural performance variability or heightened motivation. These factors cannot be fully excluded due to the nature of the experimental design.

Third, the study consists of a commercially available VR‐based tool that simultaneously engages multiple balance‐related games such as weight shifting, postural control and dynamic stability. Although this supports generalized balance improvements, it limits the ability to identify which specific balance subcomponents were most strongly affected by the training.

Additionally, baseline differences between the DS and TD groups may have influenced the results, e.g., in terms of the potential for improvement, but at the same time, the results clearly answered our research question in the sense that learning occurs in DS, and this has a transfer effect on functional abilities. The relatively small sample size and characteristics, as well as the short follow‐up period, may also limit the generalizability of the results. Finally, although the low cost and the accessibility of the tool support its potential clinical implications, the results could not be widely generalized, because other VR‐based interventions may use different task structure, different type of feedback or level of the immersion. Future research should include larger and more diverse samples, as well as a structured follow‐up period to assess the retention and the long‐term clinical relevance of practice in various VR‐based environments.

Conclusion

During balance training in the VR environment, participants with DS and TD exhibited learning that transferred to improvements in functional abilities of muscle strength, mobility and balance. Our study clearly demonstrated that the VR environment is effective in engaging individuals with DS in motor learning tasks, whereas differential learning curves suggest the need for individual adjustment of task difficulty.
