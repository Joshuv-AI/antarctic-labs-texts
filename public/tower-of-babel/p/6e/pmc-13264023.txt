Dataset of temperature and relative humidity measurements in domestic refrigerators in Bangkok Thailand
Authors: Yvanne Paviet-Salomon, Nattawut Chaomuang, Cheeraphat Potisuwan, Evelyne Derens-Bertheau, Methawee Nukunudompanich, Onrawee Laguerre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset provides detailed measurements of internal air temperature, product temperature, and relative humidity in domestic refrigerators in households in Bangkok, Thailand. Data were collected from 123 households using Freshliance TagPlus-TH data loggers installed both inside and outside the refrigerators.For each refrigerator, air temperature and relative humidity were monitored at five internal locations: top shelf, middle shelf, vegetable drawer, door shelf, and freezer compartment. In addition, the temperature of a meat-like test product placed on the middle shelf was recorded. Ambient air conditions surrounding each refrigerator were also measured to characterize the external thermal environment. Prior to deployment, all sensors were prepared and activated by the research team, with time zero (T0) defined at this stage. Participants were only required to place the sensors according to the provided instructions while minimizing disturbances to their usual refrigerator usage habits. The sensors remained inside the refrigerators for seven consecutive days, recording measurements every 5 minutes before being removed.Data collected during the first and seventh days of monitoring were excluded from the analysis to account for the uncertainty regarding the exact times at which consumers placed and removed the sensors. This exclusion also helped eliminate transient effects caused by the introduction of heat loads during sensor installation and removal, which are not representative of normal refrigerator operating conditions. Consequently, only the five-day dataset corresponding to stabilized operating conditions is presented.The dataset is provided as tab-delimited.txt files. File names identify both the refrigerator number (1–123) and refrigerator configuration, including single-door, two-door top-freezer, and two-door bottom-freezer models. An accompanying R script enables the generation of temperature and humidity profiles for each refrigerator over the 5-day monitoring period. A README document is also included and provides detailed descriptions of column names, sensor locations, and metadata associated with each measurement campaign.Temperature sensors have a resolution of 0.1°C over a range of −30°C to +70°C, and an expanded uncertainty of ±0.5°C (k = 2). Relative humidity sensors have a resolution of 0.1 % and an expanded uncertainty of ±3 % (k = 2). These uncertainty values are based on the manufacturer’s specifications, additional calibration could not be performed within the scope of this study due to the lack of suitable calibration equipment. This high-resolution dataset provides valuable insights into the thermal and hygrometric conditions of domestic refrigeration systems under real household usage conditions. The data may support a wide range of applications, including the assessment and optimization of refrigeration performance through analysis of temperature heterogeneity and thermal behavior inside refrigerating compartments, the evaluation of food safety and risks associated with domestic storage temperatures, the identification of potential temperature abuse conditions in challenge-test studies, the development of consumer recommendations, the development and validation of simplified thermal models and CFD simulations for predicting airflow and heat transfer within domestic refrigerators, studies related to energy consumption and food preservation, and the optimization of domestic refrigerator design parameters using CFD and artificial neural network approaches.The dataset is expected to provide valuable insights for researchers, stakeholders, and policymakers working on refrigeration technologies, food safety, and cold-chain management.Keywords: Domestic refrigeration, Food Storage, Monitoring, Consumer

For the uncertainties of all sensors, the coverage factor (k) is equal to 2. This factor is used to calculate the expanded uncertainty of a measurement, which defines an interval around the measurement result. For k = 1, the interval corresponds to 68.27% confidence level. When k = 2, the confidence level increases to approximately 95.45%, which is commonly used in metrology practicality. For k = 3, the confidence level is about 99.73%. The concept of the coverage factor is detailed in the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement [1].

Background

Domestic refrigeration, where food spends the majority of its self-life, is essential for preserving the organoleptic and sanitary qualities. While domestic refrigerators are widely used, detailed field data on their internal/external conditions in tropical environments remain scarce. High ambient temperatures and humidity as in Bangkok, and diverse consumer practices can strongly influence refrigerator performance.

This data paper focuses on air temperature/relative humidity at the top, middle, door, vegetable drawers, freezer compartments, and product temperature at the middle shelf. The neighbouring ambient temperature and relative humidity were also recorded. Measurements were taken at 5-minute intervals over 7 days. However, only data of 5 days were extracted, while the ones of the first and the seventh days were excluded to assure the data under quasi-steady state condition.

This data paper is related to the research article by providing the complete set of raw measurements, processed time series, and metadata describing sensor positions and refrigerator types. The dataset can be used for model development, comparative analyses, planning of future experiments or field studies and risk evaluation for food preservation at home.

Data Description

The dataset is organized into three main directories. The first directory, "00_script", contains an R script used to visualize the raw data in graphical form. The second directory, "01_src", includes all measured values of air temperature, product temperature, and air relative humidity every 5 minutes for 5 days. The third directory, "02_res", includes all the curves obtain thanks to script and data. The additional directory "03_figures" contains the main figure to show the measurements positions in each refrigerator type. Each text file corresponds to the measurement for one refrigerator, which can be identified by a number assigned at the beginning of the study. As 123 domestic refrigerators were measured, it results in 123 individual data files. The dataset includes three refrigerator types (Fig. 1): single-door (n = 41), two-door / top-freezer (n = 77), and two-door / bottom-freezer (n = 5).

File names follow the structure "RefrigeratorNumber_RefrigeratorType". Only the refrigerator type is provided in the metadata file, other technical information is not available.

The format of all raw data files are tab-delimited .txt files with the following structure (Fig. 2):

Structure of the raw data files with tab-delimited .txt format. The first column presents Time (min), the other columns represent the measured parameter, for example, temperature (T,°C), relative humidity (RH, %) at a given position (AMB, MS, TS….).

By applying the script "script_fridge.R" on a raw data file of one refrigerator, the curves of temperature and humidity evolution over 5 days at all positions can be generated. Temperature curves have the type name "RefrigeratorNumber_RefrigeratorType_TEMP", while humidity curves have the type name "RefrigeratorNumber_RefrigeratorType_HUM".

A readme file provides the metadata in a raw data file (Fig. 3). It describes the signification of each column name such as the measured parameter (T, RH) and the sensor position (AMB, MS, TS, VD, DS and FC).

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Participant recruitment was conducted using an online survey form (Google Forms), which included an invitation to participate in an on-site refrigerator investigation. The survey was initially distributed to acquaintances residing in Bangkok, who were subsequently encouraged to share it with other potential participants. All individuals were first informed about the objectives of the study, the planned activities, and their rights regarding anonymity and withdrawal from participation. Participants who agreed to take part completed an online consent form, and all responses were recorded anonymously.

Following acceptance, appointments were scheduled for on-site visits. Prior to deployment, all sensors were prepared and activated by the research team, with time zero (T0) defined at this stage. Participants were only required to place the sensors according to the provided instructions while minimizing disturbances to their usual refrigerator usage habits. The sensors remained inside the refrigerators for seven consecutive days, recording measurements every 5 minutes before being removed. A total of 145 participants agreed to participate in the investigation; however, only 123 datasets were retained for the final analysis. Excluded datasets corresponded to participants residing outside Bangkok, measurement errors caused by instrument malfunction, and/or accidental interruptions during data collection. The investigated refrigerator types included single-door (n = 41), two-door/top-freezer (n = 77), and two-door/bottom-freezer (n = 5) models. To characterize the surrounding environmental conditions, an additional sensor equipped with a magnetic support was attached to the external wall of the refrigerator door. This configuration enabled continuous recording of ambient temperature and relative humidity in the kitchens or dining areas where the refrigerators were installed. To simulate food temperature inside the refrigerator, a meat-like test product was developed using an aqueous non-ionic polymer hydrogel. The formulation consisted of 164 g of hydroxyethyl-cellulose (Shin-Etsu, Japan), 32 g of distilled water, and 2.8 g of phenoxyethanol as an antifungal agent (Knanze, Thailand), corresponding to a total mass of approximately 200 g. The mixture was mechanically homogenized to ensure uniform polymer hydration and gel formation.

The resulting gel exhibited a thermal conductivity of 0.45 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹, a density of 1032 kg·m⁻³, and a specific heat capacity of 4.82 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹ [2]. In addition, it showed stable viscoelastic behavior, low syneresis, and high thermal diffusivity stability. The test product was placed in a cylindrical aluminum container (83 mm diameter and 38 mm height), with a temperature data logger positioned at its center to represent the product core temperature (Fig. 10). This This configuration ensured reproducible heat and mass transfer conditions during data acquisition across all surveyed refrigerators.

Cross-sectional (top) and side (bottom) views of the meat-like test product used for data acquisition. Reproduced from Chaomuang et al. [2025].

Data collected during the first and seventh days of monitoring were excluded from the analysis to account for the uncertainty regarding the exact times at which consumers placed and removed the sensors. This exclusion also helped eliminate transient effect [7], caused by the introduction of heat loads during sensor installation and removal, which are not representative of normal refrigerator operating conditions. Consequently, only the five-day dataset corresponding to stabilized operating conditions is presented in this data paper.

Limitations

Nowadays, almost all families in Thailand (72 millions population) have a refrigerator. According to the project duration and the grant, only 123 refrigerators were surveyed, thus, the obtained data may not be representative. In general, the residents in Bangkok have higher education level and higher income in comparison to the rural ones. Their refrigerator might be more performance with higher technology, which may lead to better temperature control. The monitoring methodology developed in this study would be served in the future to include the consumer participants in suburban and rural areas. So, this would provide a more comprehensive understanding of domestic refrigeration practices across Thailand.

No additional calibration, sensor drift assessment, or long-term stability tests were performed during or after the 7-day measurement period. Consequently, the reported accuracy and uncertainty values rely solely on the manufacturer’s specifications for the TagPlus-TH sensors. Although this approach is commonly adopted in large-scale field studies involving multiple domestic appliances, these limitations should be considered when interpreting the reported uncertainties. Users of the dataset are encouraged to account for these constraints depending on their specific applications.
