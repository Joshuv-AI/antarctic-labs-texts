Bacterial Glutamine Synthetases as a Novel Metabolic Selection Marker to Improve CHO Cell Culture Performance Through Selection Stringency Modulation
Authors: Daniel Heinzelmann, Nicholas Michelarakis, Franziska Reuss, Benjamin Lindner, Anne R Karow‐Zwick, Joschka Bauer, Benjamin Renner, Simon Fischer, Patrick Schulz, Moritz Schmidt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe use of metabolic selection markers has advanced stable cell line generation, increasing productivity while simultaneously eliminating the need for antibiotic reagents. This study explores the potential of bacterially derived glutamine synthetases (GS) as a novel generation of metabolic selection markers to further enhance CHO cell culture performance. GS‐I proteins were extracted from the genomes of enterobacterial and actinomycetes species. Three of these enzymes demonstrated functionality when stably transfected into GS‐deficient CHO cells, leading to a 3‐ to 4‐fold increase in antibody titer compared to endogenous GS from Cricetulus griseus. This study indicates that the functionality of bacterial GS enzymes in mammalian cells is determined by solvent accessibility and the geometry of the catalytic binding pocket. Dysfunctional variants showed a less accessible bifunnel. Bacterial GS were evaluated for their bioprocess performance leading to superior stable pool and clone performance. Transcriptome analysis further revealed that regulatory cellular mechanisms were decoupled in a cross‐species set‐up, reinforcing the suitability of repurposing bacterial enzymes as selection markers in mammalian cell lines. By modulating the selection stringency, an increase in expression performance was achieved without impairing the bioprocess behavior or long‐term cell line stability.Keywords: Chinese hamster ovary cells, cell culture performance, cell line stability, glutamine synthetase, homology modelling, metabolic selection marker, molecular simulation

Introduction

The number of approvals and sales of biopharmaceuticals is steadily increasing, with monoclonal antibodies leading this class of therapeutics [1]. The majority of marketed products are manufactured using Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell lines, favored for their suitability for large scale suspension bioreactors, ability to thrive in chemically defined media, resistance to viral infection, ease of genetic modification, and human‐like glycosylation [2 , 3]. To ensure a sustainable market supply, CHO cell lines are continuously optimized for higher productivities and more versatile applications [4 , 5]. The generation of stable, high‐producing recombinant CHO cell lines requires the stable integration and long‐term maintenance of transgene expression constructs into the host cell's genome. Selection markers are an efficient tool and allow for the identification and isolation of cells that have successfully integrated the desired gene stably into their genome [6 , 7]. Antibiotic selection markers function by providing resistance to specific antibiotics, enabling only the successfully transformed cells to survive in the presence of these supplements. This resistance can induce extensive transcriptomic changes beyond the cellular mechanisms targeted by the antibiotic. Therefore, the use of antibiotics has recently been advocated with caution [8]. Metabolic selection markers function by complementing a metabolic deficiency in the host cells, enabling only transfected and high producing cells to grow in a specific, restrictive growth medium. One of the most prominent metabolic selection systems for stable cell line generation relies on the essential metabolic enzyme glutamine synthetase (GS, EC 6.3.1.2) [9 , 10]. During the first implementation of the GS selection system, addition of the GS inhibitor methionine sulfoximine (MSX) enabled the selection of productive stable cells even in cell lines producing sufficient levels of GS [7 , 9]. Although this approach is still valid today, selection can result in a high fraction of non‐producers due to high MSX tolerance of CHO cell lines [11]. Thus, knockout host cell line completely devoid endogenous GS were established to allow for a more stringent selection process [10 , 12 , 13].

GS is an essential enzyme involved in nitrogen metabolism. It catalyzes the conversion of glutamate, ATP, and ammonia into glutamine, an amino acid that plays a crucial role in many biosynthetic pathways within the cell [14 – 18]. The GS superfamily comprises three subclasses: GS‐I, GS‐II, and GS‐III [14]. Prokaryotic enzymes form the GS‐I and GS‐III branches of the evolutionary tree, while GS enzymes from eukaryotic species are categorized as GS‐II type. Structural analysis has revealed that enzymes originating from the bacterial branches predominantly assemble as functional dodecamers, but they can adapt tetradecameric structures, particularly in complex with regulatory proteins [19]. GS enzymes from eukaryotic species have been observed to form octamers or decamers [20 – 23]. GS typically assembles into homo‐oligomeric complexes, where each complex consists of four to seven monomers arranged in a ring‐like structure and two such ring structures stacking on top of each other [21]. The dimerization between each pair of monomers creates a catalytic binding pocket, adopting a bifunnel‐like structure [20 , 24]. Thus, the number of active sites correlates with the complexity and abundance of dimeric interfaces within a functional oligomer. The GS system, as applied in the selection of CHO production cell lines, effectively leverages the metabolic function of mammalian GS‐II and has been significantly advanced since its discovery [25]. Modulating selection stringency has emerged as a pivotal approach to enhance cell line performance. Tuning the selection stringency using attenuated or weakened promoters, resulted in more stringent selection markers as measured by decreasing survival rates and longer recovery of cell pools during selection. However, the decreased transcriptional activity of the selection markers, simultaneously led to increased specific productivity and overall titers in recovered cell pools [26 , 27]. Similar strategies to modulate selection stringency have been employed, including the generation of attenuated selection marker variants using mutagenesis [28 , 29]. This approach allows for significant advancements in transgene expression. Thus, time‐consuming amplification of the selection process through the supplementation of MSX can be omitted. The application of advanced genetic tools for cell line engineering provided additional enhancements of the GS system [12 , 13], and identified novel target genes that can adapt the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle) for improved growth in glutamine‐free medium [30]. Moreover, to expand the options for orthogonal selection, multi‐auxotrophic CHO cell lines have been created by combining deficiencies in multiple metabolic genes and selection markers [31 – 33].

In this study, we present the utilization of bacterial GS‐I enzymes as metabolic selection markers in mammalian cells. While the prokaryotic subclass of GS‐I enzymes shares a similar catalytic mechanism and essential structural features with the eukaryotic GS‐II system [21 , 23 , 34 – 36], the use of bacterial enzymes in mammalian expression systems as a metabolic selection marker has previously remained unexplored. We evaluated the activity of bacterial GS variants in CHO cells and examined their structural variations using homology modeling and molecular dynamics simulations. Our analysis relates structural attributes to functionality of GS enzymes in mammalian cellular environments. Furthermore, we conducted bioprocess evaluations and long‐term stability tests using cell pools and clonal CHO cell lines that expressed these novel bacterial selection markers. Overall, the presented study not only demonstrates the successful application of bacterial GS as a metabolic selection marker in CHO cells, but it also unveils a novel approach that significantly outperforms traditional mammalian GS systems with regards to cellular performance of recombinant production cell lines. Hence, this method offers a powerful tool to modulate selection stringency and dramatically enhances cell line performance.

Results

Identification of Bacterial Glutamine Synthetases for the Use in Mammalian Cells

Although the homo‐oligomeric state differs between bacterial GS‐I (Figure 1A) and eukaryotic GS‐II (Figure 1B), the catalytic site and mechanism remain highly conserved (Figure 1C) [23]. Despite that bacterial GS enzymes exhibit a higher number of catalytic sites per oligomer, we hypothesized that structural features and evolved adaptations in bacteria may have led to nuanced functional profiles of GS. Consequently, this could potentially result in modulated selection stringency in mammalian cells. In a BLAST search, a set of GS‐I enzymes were selected, containing a highly sequence‐homologous enterobacterial enzyme cluster and a more distantly related sequence from actinomycetes species Corynebacterium glutamicum (Figure 1D). Amino acids forming the catalytic bifunnel are highly conserved among the selected variants, cross‐species and domains of life (Figure S1). The selection of enzymes included a GS from Providencia vermicola (ProvGS), which has already been well characterized regarding its structure and enzymatic activity [37]. Further, GS from C. glutamicum (CoryGS), Budvicia aquatica (BudGS), Arsenophonus sp. (ArsenoGS), Brenneria roseae (BrennGS), and Photorhabdus luminescens (PhotoGS) were further selected for functional characterization. These selections were made to represent a diverse range of living environments and temperatures, as well as to include Actinobacteria for significant bacterial diversity within the GS cluster. All selected bacterial GS‐I enzymes exhibit low levels of sequence homology (∼18 %) compared to the GS‐II enzyme found in Cricetulus griseus (C. griseus) (Figure S2). All selected bacterial GS were classified as GS‐Iß, harboring a 25 amino acid insertion at position 151–178 and a reported distinct adenylation site at tyrosine 408 (Figure S1) [21].

Functional Testing of Bacterial GS‐I Enzymes in CHO Cells

All six selected bacterial GS‐I enzymes were subjected to functional testing in GS‐deficient CHO cells. C. griseus codon optimized DNA fragments encoding for the GS were cloned into PiggyBac‐ITR expression plasmids under the control of a SV40 promoter. In addition, the transposon carried a light and heavy chain of an IgG1 antibody under the control of a CMV promoter (Figure 2A). Stable cell pools were generated by co‐transfection of the GS‐based antibody transposon and a PiggyBac‐transposase encoding mRNA. A transposon encoding the C. griseus GS‐II selection marker was used as a positive control. For each GS variant, a total of six cell pools were generated. After transfection, selection was conducted in cell culture medium devoid of L‐glutamine. Starting at day 5 after the transfection, viable cell density (VCD), viability, and L‐glutamine concentration were monitored.

Cell pools transfected with ProvGS, PhotoGS, and BudGS successfully recovered from selection, comparable to the control group expressing the C. griseus GS‐II selection marker. On day 12 post‐transfection, these cell pools showed a viability close to or even exceeding 90% (Figure 2C). The PhotoGS pools demonstrated the fastest growth, while the ProvGS pools had the lowest viable cell density (VCD) with 2.7 × 106 cells/mL and 1.1 × 106 cells/mL (Figure 2B), respectively, indicating varying levels of activity and thus selection stringencies for these bacterial GS enzymes. Furthermore, CoryGS, ArsenoGS, and BrennGS failed to rescue the survival of the GS‐deficient CHO cells, resulting in a continuous decline in viability from approximately 80% on day 5 to 30%–50% on day 12, with no increase in VCD throughout the observed cultivation period. In addition to the VCD and viability data, glutamine was directly measured from the cell culture supernatant during the selection phase to indirectly assess the catalytic activities of the GS selection markers (Figure 2D). In accordance with phenotypic selection behavior, CoryGS, ArsenoGS, and BrennGS failed to generate L‐glutamine throughout the observed cultivation period. Even 12 days post‐transfection, no significant alteration in L‐glutamine concentration was observed, confirming the dysfunctionality of these bacterial GS enzymes in CHO cells. Cells transfected with either CriGS, ProvGS, PhotoGS, or BudGS on the other hand, exhibited accumulating levels of L‐glutamine ranging from 7.5 to 10.3 mg/L. Once recovered from selection, the rate of L‐glutamine synthesis of bacterial GS varied between d9 and d12 with cell‐specific L‐glutamine productivities ranging from 1.4 to 24.5 ng of L‐glutamine per cell and day, confirming differences in the activity levels of the different enzymes. After completion of the selection phase, recovered cell pools were transferred into shake flasks and IgG protein production was determined at the end of a 3‐day batch cultivation (Figure 2E). Strikingly, the antibody titer in cell pools transfected with bacterial GS was 3‐ to 4‐fold higher, ranging from 29.1 to 34.2 mg/L, compared to cells transfected with the CriGS control, which showed an IgG concentration of 11.3 mg/L. Copy number of stably integrated transgenes ranged from 4 to 9, with CriGS having the highest genomically integrated copy number of nine copies (Figure S3).

To check whether the GS enzymes initially identified as dysfunctional under the tested conditions—ArsenoGS, BrennGS, and CoryGS—may have been affected by issues in transcription or translation, we analyzed mRNA and transcript as well as protein levels by dPCR and Western blot. Thus, cell pools stably overexpressing all the evaluated GS proteins under control of a CMV promoter and selected by G418 as an antibiotic selection marker were generated during an additional round of transfection with constant codon usage. Whilst an overexpression of transcripts was detectable for all genes (Figure S4), the Western blot showed that no protein was detectable for the GS enzyme from C. glutamicum (Figure S5), suggesting a challenge with the translation of this distinct enzyme. However, also the non‐functional GS enzymes originated from Arsenophonus sp. and B. roseae can be expressed by CHO cells. Conclusively, the dysfunctionality of these two bacterial GS proteins in CHO cells is likely not caused by a lack of expression or translation.

Structural Analysis of Bacterial GS‐I Enzymes

Structural analysis of bacterial GS enzymes was conducted using molecular modeling to investigate the root cause of functional differences between the tested bacterial GS variants in CHO cells, despite the high sequence homology observed in the cluster of bacterial GS‐I enzymes. We employed homology modeling techniques to construct a dimeric structure of the GS protein to focus on the active bifunnel formed at the dimeric interface using the GS structure from Salmonella typhimurium (PDB ID: 1F52) as a template for the Arsenophonus sp. sequence. The constructed dimer was meticulously designed with both the Mn and Ti ions in their respective positions, along with the ADP ligand, given their importance for catalytic activity. This process was systematically repeated for the remaining GS variants under investigation. Following the construction of these structures, we proceeded to conduct molecular dynamics simulations to further refine the models generated as well as explore the dynamics and structural conformations of the proteins. Initially, to ascertain the stability of the protein structures during the simulation, we evaluated the Root Mean Squared Deviation (RMSD) of the atomic distances across all atoms within the structures to gain an unbiased assessment of structural stability. This approach ensured that the dynamics of all regions of the protein were examined, thereby providing a comprehensive view of the protein dynamics.

Our analysis revealed that all protein structures maintained stability throughout the simulation, with RMSD values not exceeding 0.4 nm and reaching a plateau around the 60‐ns mark (Figure S6). An exception was the structure derived from the C. griseus sequence, which displayed a higher RMSD value of approximately 0.6 nm. We also assessed the Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) for each monomer within the protein structures (Figure S7). Similar to the RMSD analysis, the C. griseus structure displayed a higher RMSF value of around 1.0 nm, indicating a model that required more extensive rearrangement. However, the remaining six structures exhibited uniform stability, reinforcing the robustness of our modelling approach.

We further evaluated the native contacts within the protein structures (Figure S8). Consistent with previous observations, C. griseus GS structure displayed a lower percentage of remaining native contacts. Despite these variations, a plateau was quickly reached in the RMSF and native contact analyses, suggesting that the protein structures had sufficiently equilibrated during the simulation.

Finally, we extracted the protein structures from the final frame of each simulation to compare the RMSD of atomic distances between the different structures (Figure S9). The results indicate no significant conformational changes across the different prokaryotic GS structures. These findings provide additional confidence in the stability and reliability of our models and suggest that our simulation approach is capable of accurately capturing the dynamics of these protein structures.

Having established the robustness of our models, our analysis focused on discerning the differences between functional and non‐functional variants by examining the Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) of the ADP binding site. By doing this, we found a notable distinction between the GS variants: non‐functional GS variants, specifically those from Arsenophonus, Brenneria, and Corynebacterium, exhibited a lower SASA of the ADP binding site (Figure 3A—red circles), while functional GS variants displayed a higher SASA area (Figure 3A—green circles). The C. griseus (Figure 3A—purple circle) showed the highest SASA, aligning with our previous observations.

To understand these differences, we examined the residues constituting the binding site. Here, we observed a distinct tilt and geometry in the orientations of ADP‐binding‐site‐forming residues in the non‐functional GS variants (Figure 3B). Specifically, the residues ARG 322, HIS 272, and PHE 226 exhibited an altered orientation in non‐functional GS variants (shown in red, Figure 3B) compared to the functional GS variants (shown in green, Figure 3B).

This altered orientation of key residues in non‐functional variants could potentially modulate the tunnel accessibility, impacting the catalytic activity of the GS. For instance, in our analysis of the ADP‐binding site using Arsenophonus (red) and P. vermicola (green) as representative examples of non‐functional and functional variants, respectively (Figure 3C), we found these residues directly located on the ADP‐bifunnel. This suggests that structural differences in these residues could play a controlling role in modulating the tunnel accessibility, thereby influencing the functionality and overall selection stringency of the GS enzyme.

Bioprocess Assessment of Stable Cell Pools Selected With Bacterial GS Selection Markers

To assess effects of bacterial GS selection markers on cell growth, productivity, and metabolic profiles post selection in comparison to the native CriGS in a controlled bioprocess, the previously generated stable CHO cell pools were used to inoculate in a small‐scale bioreactor system. Cell pools were examined using six biological replicates per GS selection marker. After inoculation, all cell pools showed an exponential growth phase (Figure 4A), and a constantly high viability close to 100% (Figure 4B). The peak VCD of the cell pools was reached at day 9 or 10, respectively, with BudGS reaching the peak VCD slightly earlier compared to CriGS, PhotoGS, and ProvGS. Absolute values ranged from 22 × 106 cells/mL up to 32.7 × 106 cells/mL. Peak VCD of the cell pools can be ranked from high to low: ProvGS > CriGS > BudGS > PhotoGS. After reaching peak VCD, all cell pools exhibited a decrease in viability (Figure 4B). Still, all bacterial GS exhibited a similar behavior to the endogenous CriGS, with harvest viabilities exceeding 85% at the end of the fed‐batch process (day 14). The antibody titer at the end of the bioprocess ranged from 1.0 to 4.2 g/L with CriGS showing the lowest and ProvGS exhibiting the highest titer, respectively (Figure 4C). Considering the cell growth, PhotoGS pools showed the highest cell‐specific productivity (Qp) with 17.2 pg/vc/d, while CriGS ranked the lowest with only 3.9 pg/vc/d (Figure 4D). Although cell pools generated with GS from different species showed differences in growth and productivity, they exhibited comparable behavior in glucose and lactate consumption (Figure S10).

RNA sequencing and subsequent transcriptome analysis was conducted on samples collected during the exponential growth phase (day 5) of the small‐scale bioprocess to assess the influence of the cross‐species application of GS selection markers on the transcriptomes of CHO cells. Principal component analysis (PCA) among the GS groups showed no major differences between the different GS species, while showing good comparability between biological and technical replicates (Figure S11A–C). This indicated that the introduction of prokaryotic GS does not interfere with the cell's transcriptomic behavior or the GS‐specific regulatory feedback mechanisms. Furthermore, gene expression values of the GS enzymes and the LC and HC genes of the expressed antibody were also analyzed (Figure S11D). Interestingly, GS expression was comparable between all pools and found to be approximately at the same level as the housekeeping gene Eif3i. The LC and HC genes on the other hand were far more overexpressed in all pools with the CriGS pools exhibiting the lowest overall expression values among the cell pools. Lastly, a differential gene expression analysis only identified minor differences when comparing the evaluated groups of GS enzymes (Figure S12). With seven genes, the highest number of differentially expressed genes was identified between the ProvGS and BudGS groups. Comparison between all other groups showed an even lower number of genes (2–4 genes), with PhotoGS and BudGS groups showing no single differentially expressed gene at all.

Long‐Term Stability of CHO Cell Lines Transfected With ProvGS for Recombinant Production of Complex Biologics

To investigate the long‐term phenotypic and genetic stability of cell lines generated using prokaryotic metabolic selection markers, clonal cell lines generated using the glutamine synthetase from P. vermicola (ProvGS) were evaluated. ProvGS was selected based on its superior performance in the previous cell pool assessment with regards to high titers and favorable growth behavior, especially compared to CriGS. Moreover, structural features like a low SASA score and a narrower catalytic‐bifunnel suggested a putatively higher level of selection stringency and thus may also lead to individual clones with enhanced performance. To examine this hypothesis, phenotypic and genetic stability assessment was performed with clonal cell lines expressing a difficult‐to‐express (DTE) recombinant therapeutic protein candidate (IgG‐ScFv fusion protein; Figure 5A) [39]. After single‐cell deposition and scale up, clonal cell lines were selected for cryopreservation based on productivity and growth performance. Subsequently, eight clones were thawed and cultivated in continuous stock culture in shake flasks for a cumulative number of 54–60 generations. Subsequently, these cells were used to inoculate a small‐scale bioreactor. In summary, a cell age of 60 days counted from the thaw of the cell lines to the end of the bioprocess was reached for a final stability assessment. During the small scale fed‐batch process, the evaluated clones showed peak VCDs ranging from 18.0 to 29.2 × 106 cells/mL (Figure 5B). At the end of the fed‐batch process all clones showed high viabilities of ≥86% (Figure 5C). Concentration of the DTE molecule was measured at the end of the fed‐batch process and product titers of up to 5.9 g/L as well as a cell specific productivity of up to 31.4 pg/vc/d (Figure 5D) were achieved. Overall, the evaluated cell clones consistently demonstrated a strong and high‐performing bioprocess performance in terms of growth, productivity, and harvest viability, even after extended cultivation periods. At the genomic level, long‐term stability was evaluated by copy number determination and Southern blot analysis. To allow a read‐out on stability from genetic characterization experiments, cryopreserved samples from each clone were included in the genetic analysis to provide a day 0 reference sample. Copy numbers in the range of 2–10 integrated heavy and light chain gene copies of the IgG‐ScFv molecule per cell were measured (Figure 5E). The data showed that the number of heavy and light chain copies remained balanced in each clone. Furthermore, this balance is maintained even during long‐term cultivations, providing evidence of the genetic stability of the ProvGS based expression constructs. Genomic integrity over the prolonged cultivation time was subsequently evaluated by Southern Blot analysis. Hybridization band pattern for the light and heavy chain of the IgG‐ScFv molecule (Figure 5F) was comparable between the stability sample and the day 0 reference for all characterized clones.

Methods

Plasmid Construction

Codon optimized DNA sequences encoding for the identified bacterial GS genes were synthetized (Twist Bioscience, USA) and directly cloned into Boehringer Ingelheim (BI) proprietary transposon plasmids already encoding for the light and heavy chain genes. PiggyBac‐encoding mRNA was synthetized (GenScript, USA) and directly used for transfection.

Cell Culture

CHO‐K1 cells were purchased from ATCC. Suspension adaption and generation of bi‐allelic GS knock out was performed at BI. Resulting BI proprietary glutamine synthetase deficient (GS−/−) CHO‐K1 host cell system (BI‐HEX CHO‐K1GS−/−) was cultured in suspension in BI proprietary animal‐component free and chemically defined cell culture medium. Cell culture maintenance was performed every 2–3 days with seeding cell densities of 0.3 × 106 viable cells/mL. The Vi‐CELL BLU (Beckman Coulter, USA) or Cedex Analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland) were used for cell counting. Cultures were incubated at 36.5°C, 5% CO2 and with agitation at 120 rpm (50 mm orbit) in an orbital shaker incubator (Infors, Switzerland).

Generation of Stable CHO Cell Pools

BI‐HEXCHO‐K1GS−/− host cells were co‐transfected with a PiggyBac‐transposase encoding mRNA and the transposon plasmid carrying the genetic information for the heavy chain and light chain genes of the antibody as well as the different GS genes. The Neon transfection system in combination with the 100 µL Neon Kit was used for electroporation (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany). Stable CHO cell pools were generated via metabolic selection in BI proprietary selection medium without L‐glutamine and without methionine sulfoximine (MSX).

Selection of cell pools overexpressing the GS enzymes was performed in presence of 200 mg/L G418 and 850 mg/L L‐glutamine.

Generation of Clonal Cell Lines

Single cell cloning (SCC) of stable mAb producing cell pools was performed using the CellCelector (Sartorius, Germany) device in combination with the nanowell‐based cloning technology was performed for the deposition of single cells. Cells were plated out in nanowells and the verification of single cell deposition was performed by microscopic imaging. Cell productivity was determined by bio‐layer interferometry using an Octet HTX system (FortéBIO, USA). Best growing and producing clones were selected for expansion into shake flasks.

Determination of Antibody Concentration

Antibody concentration was determined by bio‐layer interferometry using an Octet HTX system (FortéBIO, USA) with protein A sensors. Prior to analysis, the protein A biosensors were hydrated in assay buffer to ensure optimal performance. Samples from the cell culture supernatant were diluted in cultivation medium prior to analysis and a standard curve was generated using a series of known concentrations of a reference antibody and cultivation medium was used as blank sample. Final antibody concentrations were calculated based on the obtained linear regression from the standard material. Protein A biosensors were regenerated between sample measurements.

Determination of Glutamine Concentration

Glutamine concentration from the cell culture medium was determined on the BioMajesty (DiaSys Diagnostic Systems, Germany) using the Glutamine Assay Kit (4BioCell, Germany). Briefly, cell culture supernatants were collected and clarified by centrifugation to remove cellular debris. The samples were then diluted appropriately to ensure that the glutamine concentration fell within the dynamic range of the assay from 73 to 7450 mg/L. For quantification, glutamine calibrators provided with the kit was used to generate a standard curve.

Fed‐Batch Bioprocess

Bioprocess performance of stable pools and clones was assessed in a small scale bioreactor system (ambr15, Sartorius, Germany). At the start of the process, cells were seeded with a target value of 0.7 × 106 viable cells/mL with a culture start volume of 13 mL. Starting at day 2 of the process, automatic feeding of 40 mL/L/day of nutrient feeds was started and glucose concentration was adapted manually to constantly exceed a concentration of 4 g/L. The pH was maintained at 6.95, and the partial pressure of oxygen was consistently controlled at 50%. Cell growth and metabolites were measured daily.

Isolation of Genomic DNA and Total RNA

The QIAsymphony DSP DNA Midi Kit, a commercially available kit, was used to isolate and purify gDNA on the QIAsymphony SP (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. During the extraction procedure, an RNase treatment was performed to remove contaminating RNA. The QIAsymphony RNA Kit (Qiagen, Germany) was used to isolate and purify total RNA from animal and human cells and tissues according to the manufacturer's protocol. During the extraction procedure, a DNase treatment was performed to remove contaminating DNA. The concentration of the DNA and RNA samples was determined by UV scan with a NanoPhotometer N120 (Implen, Germany) at A260 nm.

RNA Sequencing and Transcriptome Analysis

For RNA sequencing, total RNA was isolated as described above and samples were analyzed with an internally qualified Illumina‐based next generation sequencing approach. After isolation, total RNA was subjected to mRNA isolation using NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB, Germany) following library preparation using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Directional Library Prep Kit (NEB, Germany) with Beads following the manufacturers protocol. After quantification and quality control of the library a sequencing run on NextSeq 500/550 Mid Output Kit v2.5 (Illumina, USA) was conducted.

Sequencing data was analyzed with Genedata Selector (Basel, Switzerland) Sequence Workflow software (Version 9.5). Briefly, sequencing adapters and low quality bases were trimmed off with Trimmomatic (v0.39) [40] before reads were mapped to the CriGri_PICRH‐1.0 hamster genome [41] using STAR (v2.7.9.a) [42]. Reads mapping to annotated genes were counted and exported. Using the Selector Analyst, genes with an average read count below 1 were excluded and a PCA was calculated. Further, a differential gene expression analysis using DESeq2 was performed using the GS groups as factor [43]. Genes with fold changes > 2 and Benjamini–Hochberg corrected p values < 0.001 were considered significant differentially expressed.

Transgene Copy Number Determination

Transgene Copy Number Determination was performed via droplet digital Polymerase Chain Reaction (ddPCR) on a QX200 system (BioRad, Germany). Primer‐probe sets (see Table S1) were designed to specifically prime to an endogenous control (e.g., Eif3i) and the target genes (e.g., LC and HC). Each hydrolysis probe was labeled with a reporter fluorescence dye (FAM for target genes and HEX for endogenous control).

For the separation of potential tandem copies of the expression vector, a restriction digest of the sample gDNA was performed for copy number determination according to the manufacturer´s instructions (NEB, Germany). After formation of droplets using a QX200 AutoDG (BioRad, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions, cycling was carried out in a standard PCR cycler under the following conditions for ddPCR and Two‐Step RT‐ddPCR: 95°C for 10 min followed by 50 cycles at 94°C for 30 s and 59°C for 60 s and a final step of 98°C for 10 min. For One‐Step RT‐ddPCR, the protocol was initiated by a single step of 50°C for 10 min followed by the previously described cycling program with adaption of the annealing temperature to 56°C. Amplified samples were analyzed using a QX200 droplet reader (BioRad, Germany) for the determination of total fluorescent droplet counts.

Based on the absolute quantification of target molecules per reaction, the gene copy number per cell was determined as the ratio between transgenic and endogenous copy number per reaction (corresponding to the copy number per haploid genome) and then multiplied by 2 to express the copy number per cell. Normalized gene expression values were determined as the ratio between transgenic and endogenous copy number per reaction.

Gene Expression Analysis

Gene expression analysis was also performed via ddPCR. Primer‐probe sets (see Table S1) were designed to the bacterial GS genes and TaqMan Gene Expression Assay Cg04597581_g1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) was used to specifically prime to an endogenous control (e.g., Eif3i). Each hydrolysis probe was labeled with a reporter fluorescence dye (FAM for GS and VIC for endogenous control).

After formation of droplets cycling was carried out under the following conditions: 50°C for 10 min, an initial denaturation step of 95°C for 10 min followed by 50 cycles at 94°C for 30 s and 56°C for 60 s and a final step of 98°C for 10 min. Normalized gene expression values were determined as the ratio between transgenic and endogenous target molecule concentration per reaction.

Southern Blot Analysis

Prior to Southern Blot analysis, gDNA and vector DNA were digested with restriction enzymes according to the manufacturer´s instructions (NEB, Germany). Digested gDNA samples were separated on TAE‐agarose gel by electrophoresis. For size determination, a DIG‐labeled DNA Molecular Weight Marker VII (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland) was loaded as well. After depurination and a neutralization, transfer of DNA onto positively charged nylon membrane (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland) was carried out by capillary forces during overnight incubation.

For Southern blot hybridization, the hybridization solution from the DIG Easy Hyb Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland) was used according to protocol. Labeling of DNA probes with Digoxigenin‐Deoxyuridine Triphosphate (DIG‐dUTP) was performed utilizing the PCR DIG Probe Synthesis Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland). The PCR probes were generated by using the respective expression vector as template in the PCR reaction. Hybridization with the DIG‐labelled probe was then performed over night at 42°C. Washing and blocking of membranes was done using the DIG Wash and Block Buffer Set (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland) according to protocol. For immunological detection the membrane was incubated with an anti‐digoxigenin alkaline phosphatase conjugate (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland). The CDP‐Star Detection Reagent (GE Healthcare, USA) was used for detection of the hybridization signals according to protocol. Subsequently, images of the blot membranes were acquired on a ChemiDoc Touch system (Bio‐Rad, Germany) using the optimal auto‐exposure function. Processing and labeling of the resulting images was performed in the Image Lab Software (Bio‐Rad, Germany) Version 6.1.0.

Western Blot Analysis

For Western blot analysis, proteins were extracted from stable transfected cell pools after metabolic selection. For this, 1 × 106–3 × 106 cells were treated with a lysis buffer containing Pierce RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and cOmplete Mini EDTA free protease inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cells were incubated at 4°C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 8 min at 4°C to isolate the supernatant. The supernatant was reduced by adding NuPAGE 10× reducing agent (Invitrogen, USA) and incubated for 5 min. at 95°C. A total of 20 µL of the reduced soluble lysates treated with NuPage LSD Sample Buffer 4x (Invitrogen, USA) were separated on a 4%–12% Bis‐Tris NuPAGE gel (Invitrogen, USA). The Chameleon Duo Pre‐stained Protein Ladder (LI‐COR Biosciences, USA) was used as a marker. Blotting to nitrocellulose iBlot 2 NC Mini Stacks membranes (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) was performed using method P0 of the iBlot dry blotting system. The membranes were then blocked for 1 h at RT in blocking buffer. This was followed by a 4°C overnight incubation with primary anti‐FLAG (Cell Signaling Technology, Netherlands) and anti‐beta actin antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, Netherlands). The following day, blots were probed with the corresponding secondary antibodies. Detection of the target proteins was performed using the ODYSSEY Infrared Imaging System (LI‐COR Biosciences, USA).

Data Analysis

Data analysis and visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism Version 9 (GraphPad, USA). Mean values and standard deviations from biological replicates were directly calculated in GraphPad Prism.

Homology Modeling and Molecular Simulations

The homology models for this study were generated using the protein builder from Molecular Operating Environment (MOE—Chemical Computing Group ULC, Canada). The protein sequences were obtained from NCBI, and the template structures were identified from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) [44]. The models were protonated at a physiological pH of 7.2 with an ionic strength of 0.13 M NaCl using Protonate3D [45]. They were then run through the structure prep protocol of MOE to resolve any structural issues and were then relaxed using molecular mechanics to resolve any atom clashes.

All simulations were carried out with the GROMACS code version 2023.2 [46]. The CHARMM36‐ljpme [47] force field was used for the protein and water, and the glycan moieties were described with the GLYCAM06j force field [48]. To set up the simulation systems, each protein was placed randomly inside a cubic simulation box, ensuring there was at least 1 nm distance from the edges of the box to avoid any periodic boundary condition issues. The boxes were then solvated using the TIP3P water model [49], and the total charge of the simulation box was neutralized by adding sodium and chloride ions at a concentration of 0.13 M.

Prior to the production simulations, the systems were energy‐minimized using the steepest descent algorithm and equilibrated for 10 ns at 298 K and 1 bar with harmonic position restraints on all protein heavy atoms (force constants of 1000 kJ/mol/nm2). Virtual site hydrogen were used for the protein together with hydrogen mass repartitioning (HMR) between the H atoms and heavy atoms of the glycan, allowing integration of the equations of motion with 4 fs time steps. Bond lengths were constrained using the LINCS algorithm [50]. The number of matrices in the expansion for matrix inversion was set to 6 (“LINCS‐order” parameter in GROMACS) to describe the contributions of the constraint forces more accurately to the virial and thus to the pressure. SETTLE [51] was used to constrain all internal degrees of freedom of the water molecules. Constant temperature (298 K) and pressure (1 bar) during the simulations were ensured via a Bussi velocity rescaling thermostat [52] and a Parrinello–Rahman barostat [53], respectively, with coupling time constants of τ T = 0.5 ps and τ p = 10 ps (a Berendsen barostat was used during the first 300 ps of the 2 ns position‐restrained equilibration). A buffered Verlet neighbor list [54] was used for the pairwise nonbonded interactions. Lennard–Jones 6,12 interactions were smoothly shifted to zero at the 1.0 nm distance cutoff. An analytical correction was added to the energy and pressure to correct for this truncation of the Lennard–Jones interactions. The 1.0 nm distance was also used to switch between the short‐range and the long‐range Coulomb interactions, which were treated with the smooth particle‐mesh Ewald (PME) method [55] with a 0.12 nm grid spacing and cubic B‐spline interpolation.

Each of the structures was subjected to an energy minimization followed by NVT and NPT equilibrations, 10 ns each, finishing with a 100 ns production simulation. This extensive simulation period was chosen to ensure that the structures had sufficient time to equilibrate and reach a stable conformation.

Discussion

The application of bacterial GS enzymes as metabolic selection markers in mammalian cells, particularly CHO cell lines, presents a novel approach to achieve nuanced levels of selection stringency and significantly enhance overall expression performance. Out of six tested bacterial GS, three were identified as inherently functional. Although mRNA and protein levels were assessed for the putatively non‐functional proteins, the observed dysfunctionality may not necessarily reflect intrinsic inactivity. The lack of activity in the three expressed variants from Arsenophonus sp., B. roseae, and C. glutamicum may result from misfolding, post‐translational modifications, or CHO‐specific inhibition mechanisms. It thus is important to consider the cellular context in evaluating enzyme functionality and suggests that further investigation in alternative expression systems or under modified selection conditions may reveal latent activity of these three variants GS enzymes in eukaryotic cell lines.

Recent advancements of selection systems for recombinant CHO production cell lines either focused on the attenuation of the endogenous Chinese hamster GS or modulated the selection stringency by reducing the transcriptional activity of the selection marker [26 , 27 , 29]. However, these approaches, especially when in combination with MSX amplification, often impaired the selection behavior in a way that viabilities dropped <20% post transfection and recovery of cell pools took up to 30 days, in addition to retarded doubling times and cellular growth during the post selection phase [10].

Although a cross‐species exchange of the endogenous C. griseus with other eukaryotic proteins (e.g., Mus musculus or Homo sapiens) is an already described strategy [56], the presented approach differs from these strategies. We leverage a cross‐species application of prokaryotic enzymes for biopharmaceutical applications rather than engineering eukaryotic GS proteins. Harnessing the activity of bacterial enzymes in mammalian expression systems has been described to eliminate certain glycosylation pattern or preventing proteolysis [57 , 58], but not on the context of selection marker engineering. Bacterial GS enzymes have evolved to reliably function even in diverse environmental conditions, such as varying temperatures, selective sources of nutrients to drive the metabolism and pH levels [59 , 60]. However, these bacterial GS may not function optimally in mammalian cell culture conditions and this slightly sub‐optimal level of functionality provides an opportunity to modulate selection stringency and enhance capabilities of mammalian cell lines in the context of biopharmaceutical production. Interestingly, only a subset of the tested GS enzymes demonstrated functionality in CHO cells to a degree that allowed cells to recover from selection and to produce and maintain sufficient levels of L‐glutamine for survival. However, the absence of detectable protein for the GS enzyme from C. glutamicum, despite successful mRNA expression, raises intriguing questions about post‐translational modifications that might be necessary for the stability and functionality of heterologous expressed proteins in CHO cells. It highlights the need for a deeper understanding of how host cell machinery interacts with foreign proteins, particularly those from bacterial sources, which may differ significantly in their translation efficiency and stability in mammalian cells which may directly have affected the selection stringency of the tested bacterial GS. The varying outgrowth behavior and L‐glutamine accumulation of functional bacterial GS enzymes in cell pools during the selection further suggests such different catalytic activities of the enzymes, albeit not to an extent where the time in selection was significantly impaired. Most importantly, the expression performance of all CHO pools fueled by functional bacterial GS exceeded that of endogenous CriGS by three‐ to four‐fold. While the absolute genomic copy numbers and also the GS expression values were within a comparable range in all pools, we observed that expression values of the antibody genes were increased for the pools generated with bacterial GS enzymes. This observation suggests that pools using bacterial GS as a selection marker might consist of cell populations in which transgenes have integrated into more transcriptionally active loci, potentially as a compensatory mechanism for lower GS activity during selection. Given the comparable transgene copy numbers, GS expression and the absence of direct evidence for enzyme activity differences, this hypothesis warrants further investigation. However, our analysis of the structural geometry revealed that the catalytic bifunnel may play a significant role in determining the selection stringency and functionality of GS enzymes in mammalian cells.

One potential advantage of a cross‐species approach could be that specific mammalian feedback mechanisms might not apply to bacterial GS [61 – 63]. Such regulatory networks that can influence GS functionality in mammalian cells include end‐product regulation by glutamine‐derived metabolites [64], post‐translational modifications such as nitrosylation or nitration by nitric oxide leading to loss of function [65], and differential activation through metal cofactor binding [64 , 66], as eukaryotic GS enzymes are Mn2⁺‐dependent metalloproteins whose activity and pH optima can be modulated by divalent cations. A decreased sensitivity of bacterial enzymes against these mechanisms potentially makes them more inert in an eukaryotic environment and thus more robust in mammalian cells. As a result, we can speculate that fewer factors might be able to inhibit or negatively regulate bacterial GS resulting in more stable cell lines. This could be a potential explanation for the superior bioprocess behavior observed in CHO cell pools selected with bacterial GS compared to those selected with the endogenous GS enzyme, while maintaining a similar metabolic and transcriptomic profile. The putatively most stringent functional bacterial GS, based on SASA score and structural features, also demonstrated the best pool performance and hence the GS enzyme derived from P. vermicola was finally selected for long‐term cell line stability assessment of clonal cell lines. While these structural characteristics may correlate with observed functionality, no direct evidence currently links them to enzymatic activity, and confirming such a relationship would require more detailed structural and biochemical investigations. Taken together the structural information and the small scale fed‐batch results, this suggests a mechanism of action which combines more than one aspect, with more work needed to elucidate on the relation between structural features and selection stringency.

In addition to the overall improved bioprocess performance of CHO pools using bacterially derived GS enzymes, long‐term stability of clonal cell lines up to 60 days in culture was demonstrated in small scale bioprocesses, and genomic characterization suggests a robust and stable integration of the selection marker and associated transgenes into the host cell genome. As part of future development and considering a potential implementation into commercial manufacturing, the potential clearance behavior of bacterial GS enzymes during downstream processing should be considered, particularly given that these proteins may not be detected by conventional CHO host cell protein assays.

In summary, the presented cross‐species application of bacterially derived metabolic selections markers provides an avenue to modulate selection stringency in mammalian cells, and thereby dramatically enhances cell line performance with a promising potential to further extend the toolbox for cell line engineering in the future.
