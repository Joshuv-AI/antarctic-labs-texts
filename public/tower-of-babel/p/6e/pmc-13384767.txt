Flexible laser-induced graphene (LIG)/ZnO-nanorod (NR) heterostructure for efficient photodegradation
Authors: Megha Megha, Deepak Deepak, Sayani Das, Sekhar Chandra Ray, Susanta Sinha Roy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this work, a flexible laser-induced graphene (LIG)/ZnO nanorod heterostructure was developed for the photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue (MB) under UV exposure. Oxygen plasma treatment was employed to alter the LIG surface wettability from hydrophobic to hydrophilic, thus facilitating the uniform deposition of ZnO nanorods on the LIG substrate. The successful formation of crystalline ZnO nanorods on the LIG surface was confirmed using X-ray diffraction and SEM studies. Photocatalytic performance studies were performed under UV light for 120 minutes. Among the developed composites, the LIG/ZnO10 composite exhibited the highest degradation rate of 96.7%. The high rate of degradation was maintained over multiple cycles, thus further accentuating the stability of the developed composites for real-world application. This enhanced photocatalytic activity can be ascribed to enhanced dye adsorption, increased interfacial LIG/ZnO contact and more effective charge separation and transportation. To validate the porous nature of the synthesized LIG, BET surface area analysis was performed, which identified abundant active sites for dye adsorption and enhanced photocatalytic degradation. The reaction mechanism was validated using radical scavenging experiments. These results suggest that oxygen plasma surface engineering is an efficient technique for increasing the performance of LIG-based photocatalysts and highlight the potential of flexible LIG/ZnO heterostructures for dye degradation applications.

Introduction

Over the past several decades, expanding industrial activities, specifically in the paper, cosmetics and textile sectors, have significantly degraded environmental quality, leading to acute water contamination and the accumulation of hazardous toxic waste. The disposal of dye waste from these industries is a major cause of water pollution. Millions of tons of dyes are estimated to be produced worldwide, with more than 15% released into water bodies as pollutants.1–4 Hence, the development of effective and eco-friendly methods for the removal of these dyes is a major focus among scientists globally. Photocatalysis is an important emerging technology to decompose organic dyes and inorganic pollutants in water bodies to less complex molecules. Due to the high stability and high composition ratio of aromatic rings in their molecular structure, dyes are very hard to be removed or degraded, and most of them are non-biodegradable and photochemically active.5,6

The photocatalytic process is a quick, promising, and environmentally friendly technology based on advanced oxidation processes, capable of oxidizing a wide range of micropollutants. Utilizing highly reactive radicals, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) effectively treat wastewater by rapidly and non-selectively oxidizing a broad spectrum of micropollutants. In particular, the photocatalytic process is quickly becoming a promising, environmentally friendly technology. While common photocatalysts, such as ZnO, TiO2 and WO3, have low production costs and are environmentally friendly, their practical applications are limited by their large bandgap (Eg), which can only be effectively excited by high-energy blue light. ZnO serves as an effective photocatalyst for degrading pollutants due to its wide bandgap (∼3.37 eV), high electron mobility and ability to generate reactive oxygen species under ultraviolet (UV) light. By absorbing UV light, ZnO generates electron–hole pairs that drive the oxidation reactions necessary to decompose organic dyes and contaminants, such as methylene blue. Its advantages over TiO2 include stronger UV absorption and better performance against bacteria; however, rapid charge recombination limits its efficiency.7,8 Rapid electron–hole recombination after photoexcitation further reduces its quantum efficiency as photogenerated carriers recombine before reacting with pollutants. Surface defects and photocorrosion exacerbate losses under prolonged irradiation. Strategies such as doping (e.g., oxygen vacancies for bandgap narrowing), graphene coupling for charge separation and heterojunction formation extend the visible-light response and boost degradation rates by up to 6-fold.9

Combining ZnO with other materials in composites merges the strengths of each component, yielding functional materials with superior properties to the individual parts.10,11 Among these, nanostructured carbon forms such as graphene and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) stand out, enabling diverse applications in biosensors, photovoltaics, supercapacitors and photocatalysts. Laser-induced graphene (LIG) acts as an electron sink, separating photogenerated charges in ZnO to suppress recombination. The high surface area of LIG improves pollutant adsorption and light harvesting.

In metal oxide/LIG nanocomposite systems, LIG functions beyond merely serving as a cocatalyst. As a substrate, LIG enhances the effective surface area and enables the higher loading of photocatalytic materials. Furthermore, its robust, self-supporting nature enables the nanocomposite to function as a free-standing photocatalyst for the photodegradation of organic contaminants in aqueous media. This structural advantage simplifies the separation of the catalyst from the reaction solution and promotes its convenient recovery and reuse.12,13

In this work, we investigated the integration of hydrothermally synthesized ZnO nanorods (ZnO NRs) onto plasma-treated LIG substrates. The plasma treatment of LIG was carried out to enhance surface wettability and promote improved adhesion between the ZnO NRs and the conductive graphene framework. The loading of ZnO was systematically optimized by varying the drop-casting ratio to achieve uniform coverage while maintaining effective electrical pathways. The resulting ZnO/LIG nanocomposites were subsequently characterized, and their functional performance was evaluated to determine the influence of the nanorod loading on photocatalytic efficiency.

Experimental

LIG preparation

LIG was fabricated on a 125 µm-thick Kapton polyimide sheet through direct laser irradiation under ambient conditions (Fig. 1). A CO2 laser (10.6 µm wavelength) operated at a fixed power of 8 W and a scanning speed of 110 mm s−1 was used to convert the polyimide surface into a porous and electrically conductive graphene network. The active electrode area was defined as 4 × 4 cm2, forming a continuous conductive LIG film without an interdigitated pattern.

Schematic of the synthesis process of the LIG/ZnO nanorod composites for photodegradation.

To improve surface wettability and interfacial adhesion, the as-prepared LIG was exposed to oxygen plasma for 2 minutes using a Diener Electronics plasma surface treatment system. This treatment introduced oxygen-containing functional groups on the graphene surface, transforming its intrinsic hydrophobic nature into a hydrophilic one. As a result, uniform solvent spreading and enhanced adhesion of the subsequently deposited materials were achieved.

ZnO nanorod preparation

ZnO NRs were synthesized via a hydrothermal route using zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) as the base in deionized water. The process began by dissolving 0.1 M Zn(NO3)2·6H2O in water, followed by the addition of a 3 M NaOH solution dropwise with constant stirring to maintain the pH value at 7–10. This mixture was then transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave filled to 60–80% capacity. The sealed autoclave was heated at 150 °C for 12 hours to promote anisotropic rod growth, after which it was allowed to cool naturally, and the precipitate was collected via centrifugation, washed repeatedly (5 to 6 times) with water and ethanol, and dried at 80 °C to produce a white powder of nanorods.14,15 The as-prepared nanorods were dispersed in an appropriate solvent (DI water) and subsequently deposited onto the plasma-treated LIG substrate via drop-casting. To optimize surface coverage and device performance, different deposition volumes of 5 µL, 10 µL and 20 µL were systematically investigated. The drop-casting ratio was carefully controlled to obtain a uniform nanorod distribution while preserving effective electrical conduction pathways across the LIG substrate.

Characterization

The structural properties of the fabricated heterostructures were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) in a coupled θ–2θ geometry with a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer employing Cu Kα radiation as the X-ray source.

Raman spectroscopic measurements were performed using an STR micro-Raman spectrometer equipped with a 532 nm argon-ion laser (2.5 mW) and a 20× objective lens to probe vibrational characteristics. The surface morphology and microstructural features of the samples were examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, JEOL JSM-7600 PLUS). Additionally, wettability studies were conducted through contact angle measurements using a software-controlled needle-based goniometer system (APEX, Delhi, India). Surface chemical states and elemental compositions were analysed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα (1486.7 eV) X-ray source (Kratos Analytical Supra Instrument).

Results and discussion

Structural and morphological analyses

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The phase and crystallinity properties of LIG and ZnO-drop-cast LIG were analysed by XRD analysis, as shown in Fig. 2. LIG and plasma-treated LIG (LIG O2) display detectable broad peaks at 22.42° (100) and 26.34° (002), showing amorphous carbon features.16,17 Additionally, the typical curves of ZnO drop-cast in volumes of 5, 10 and 20 µL are labelled as LIGZnO5, LIGZnO10 and LIGZnO20, respectively. No ZnO diffraction peaks are observed in LIGZnO5, likely because the coverage is below the XRD detection limit. For the LIGZnO10 and LIGZnO20 samples, the diffraction peaks at 31.88°, 34.47°, 36.24°, 47.51°, 56.57°, 63.02°, 67.87° and 69.09° are ascribed to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200) and (201) planes, respectively, corresponding to hexagonal wurtzite ZnO, in accordance with the standard pattern (JCPDS card no. 36-1451).18 Aside from the characteristic LIG peaks at 22.24° and 26.34°, no additional peaks are observed in either the LIG or ZnO-drop-cast LIG samples, confirming the high purity of the materials.

Photoluminescence (PL) analysis

As shown in Fig. 5, the photoluminescence (PL) spectra of the ZnO NRs exhibit characteristic emission features consisting of multiple peaks centered at ∼392 nm and ∼411 nm, along with a broad deep-level emission at ∼678 nm. The near-band-edge (NBE) emissions at 392 nm and 411 nm are attributed to excitonic recombination processes, including free exciton transitions and shallow defect-related states, which are commonly observed in ZnO nanostructures at room temperature.20,21 The broad emission in the visible region (∼678 nm) is associated with deep-level defects such as oxygen vacancies, zinc interstitials and surface-related states, which act as recombination centers within the bandgap.22–24 Such defect-induced emissions are widely reported in ZnO systems and are particularly prominent in nanostructures due to their high surface-to-volume ratio.25

PL spectra of ZnO and LIG/ZnO (10 µL).

Upon integration with LIG, a significant modification in the PL response is observed. The LIG/ZnO composite displays a dominant near-band-edge emission at ∼377 nm, while the visible emission at ∼615 nm appears significantly suppressed and broadened. The blue shift of the NBE peak and its increased prominence indicate improved crystallinity and more efficient excitonic recombination in the presence of LIG.26 Simultaneously, the suppression and shift of the deep-level emission suggest a reduction in defect-related radiative recombination pathways. These changes can be attributed to strong interfacial interactions between ZnO and LIG. The observed PL peaks are summarised in Table 1. The highly conductive LIG facilitates efficient charge transfer at the interface, where photogenerated electrons from ZnO are partially transferred to the LIG network, reducing electron–hole recombination via defect states.27 Additionally, LIG contributes to the passivation of surface defects by interacting with oxygen-related vacancies and functional groups, thereby decreasing the density of deep-level trap states responsible for visible emission. The remaining broad emission at ∼615 nm indicates the presence of residual defects, which may still contribute to surface-mediated processes.28,29

Furthermore, the enhanced NBE emission in the LIG/ZnO composite is attributed to the increased carrier density near the ZnO surface, facilitated by LIG's superior electrical conductivity and photon absorption. This promotes excitonic recombination while simultaneously suppressing nonradiative pathways. The observed PL behavior suggests that defect passivation and bidirectional charge transfer between ZnO and LIG occur, reducing deep-level emissions while enhancing band-edge transitions. Such synergistic effects are consistent with previous reports on ZnO-carbon composites, which displayed that graphene-based materials improve optical and electronic properties by modulating surface states and facilitating charge separation. This PL evolution directly correlates with enhanced –OH generation and selective MB photodegradation.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS analysis was performed on the LIG and ZnO-NR-drop-casted LIG (LIGZnO10 samples) to determine their elemental chemical composition. Fig. 6(a and d) confirms that LIG and LIGZnO10 samples show a primary C 1s peak at 284.7 eV and 283.77 eV, respectively, corresponding to sp2-bonded carbon. The C 1s spectrum of LIG displays three peaks: peak I (∼284.7 eV) corresponds to C–C/CC bonds, peak II (∼285.88 eV) corresponds to C–O bonds, and peak III (288.62 eV) corresponds to O–CO bonds.

XPS spectra of LIG (a) C 1s and (b) O 1s; LIG/ZnO (10 µL) (c) Zn 2p, (d) C 1s and (e) O 1s and (f) LIGZnO10 survey scan.

The C 1s spectrum of LIGZnO10 reveals ZnO-induced electronic restructuring within the LIG framework. Specifically, the peak at 283.76 eV (Δ = −1.01 eV relative to LIG sp2) indicates Zn–C electron donation. Furthermore, the significant downshifts in C–O (284.10 eV, Δ = −1.78 eV) and CO (285.56 eV, Δ = −3.06 eV) peaks confirm interfacial bonding and defect passivation. These modifications enable the efficient charge separation required for photocatalytic –OH generation. The selective degradation of methylene blue (MB) is attributed to defect engineering, wherein passivated LIG facilitates electron transfer to O2 and ZnO holes drive MB oxidation.30 The Zn 2p spectrum of LIGZnO10 (Fig. 6(c)) displays the two main peaks of Zn 2p1/2 and Zn 2p3/2 states centred at 1044.55 eV and 1021.50 eV, respectively, with a 23.05 eV splitting, confirming Zn2+ in the ZnO lattice.31,32 The high-resolution O 1s XPS spectrum (Fig. 6(b and e)) reveals distinct spectral evolution upon ZnO decoration. Pure LIG exhibits CO (531.98 eV), C–O–C/H2O (533.34 eV) and surface –OH (538.0 eV) peaks, characteristic of laser-induced defects. LIGZnO10 shows the emergence of Zn–O lattice oxygen at 530.31 eV, confirming successful ZnO formation, alongside shifted CO (531.88 eV), indicating electron donation from ZnO to LIG defects at the interface.33,34 The deconvoluted XPS parameters of C, O and Zn species are presented in Table 2.

Photocatalytic activity

The photocatalytic performance of LIG/ZnO was assessed via MB degradation under UV light. Fig. 7(a) shows that the MB decomposes continuously throughout the irradiation time on LIG/ZnO films. Samples with dimensions of 2 cm × 2 cm were placed in a beaker containing 10 mL of a 5 ppm MB solution. The solution was stirred using a small magnetic stirrer bar at the bottom of the beaker, which rotated freely without damaging the samples. Further, the solution was kept in the dark for 30 minutes as a conditioning step. Then, the system was irradiated by a mercury UV lamp placed at a distance of 15 cm from the solution containing the sample films. UV-VIS absorbance spectra were recorded at 30 minute intervals over 120 minutes to monitor the maximum degradation. The spectra were collected in the 400–800 nm range in microcuvettes. Fig. 7 displays the absorption spectra of LIG and LIG/ZnO composites (with ZnO drop-cast in volumes of 5, 10, and 20 µL) recorded over a 120 minute degradation cycle. The LIG/ZnO10 exhibits the highest MB adsorption. However, further increasing the ZnO percentage during drop-casting drastically reduces adsorption due to the blockage of LIG substrate pores.35

UV-vis spectra of LIG, LIGZnO5, LIGZnO10 and LIGZnO20.

In addition, the water contact angle (ϕ) of the bare LIG is measured to be ϕ = 106.57° (Fig. S1). Following oxygen plasma treatment, the ϕ decreases significantly to approximately 0°, as demonstrated in SI Video V1. This treatment transforms the inherently hydrophobic LIG surface into a superhydrophilic one, as evidenced by enhanced wettability. Improved hydrophilicity enhances catalyst–dye interactions, increasing the adsorption of dye molecules from the aqueous solution. Furthermore, the enhanced interfacial contact promotes the uniform dispersion and strong adhesion of ZnO NRs drop-casted onto the LIG surface. As a result, efficient charge transfer between ZnO and the conductive LIG network is achieved, reducing electron–hole recombination and enhancing the generation of reactive oxygen species. These combined effects contribute to the improved photocatalytic degradation performance of the ZnO/LIG system.

The rate of degradation varies according to the duration of irradiation. The photocatalytic efficiency (γ) was determined from the following equation:1γ (%) = (C0 − Ct)/C0where C0 and Ct are the concentrations of MB dye at times t = 0 and t, respectively. The photodegradation of MB can be fitted by pseudo-first-order reaction kinetics, as shown in Fig. 8.

The ln(C/C0) and −ln(C/C0) changes over time as per the irradiation time for LIG and LIG/ZnO composites (Fig. 8(a and b)). The linearity of the data indicates that degradation occurs according to pseudo-first-order kinetics (Fig. 8(b)). Degradation efficiencies at the end of 120 min of UV radiation are compared in Fig. 8(c), and the corresponding apparent rate constants are shown in Fig. 8(d). Among all the samples, the LIG/ZnO (10 µL) composite exhibits the highest degradation efficiency (96.96%) and the highest apparent rate constant, indicating that it has the best photocatalytic properties compared to the other composite materials prepared. LIG/ZnO (5 µL) exhibits a degradation efficiency of 88.03%, while that of LIG/ZnO (20 µL) is 90.15%. The superior photocatalytic activity of this sample is likely due to the fact that the ZnO NRs are optimally loaded on the porous LIG framework, enabling good charge separation and electron transport as well as the availability of many sites for photocatalytic activity. Conversely, the photocatalytic properties of the LIG/ZnO (20 µL) composite are lower than expected, possibly due to the excess loading of ZnO blocking some of the available pores in the LIG and the aggregation of the ZnO NRs.

Cycling stability results

Additional reusability experiments were performed over six consecutive photocatalytic degradation cycles to evaluate the stability and recyclability of the free-standing photocatalyst. The subsequent degradation efficiencies are summarized in Table 3. The photocatalyst retains approximately 88.6% of its initial activity after six cycles, demonstrating good operational stability and reusability. Fig. 9 demonstrates the reusability of the LIGZnO10 sample after 6 cycles. The slight decrease in degradation efficiency upon repeated use may be attributed to the accumulation of reaction intermediates on the catalyst surface and the partial blockage of active sites. The presented LIG-ZnO (LIGZnO10) photocatalyst performs in accordance with the previously reported free-standing photocatalysts with an 85.6% degradation efficiency after six cycles, consistent with the range reported in the literature.36,37 This indicates that the catalyst possesses good long-term operational stability and exhibits only a slight decrease in photocatalytic activity. The SEM images recorded before and after the degradation of MB (Fig. 10) to evaluate structural stability show no significant change in the morphology of the photocatalyst, with only minor surface changes, such as slight dye residue deposition, indicating that the photocatalyst maintains good morphological integrity after the degradation process, supporting stable performance and possible reusability. This stability is beneficial for photocatalytic applications for sustained degradation efficiency. Furthermore, EDX analysis has been included in the SI (Fig. S5), which shows negligible changes in the atomic percentages of Zn, O and C because of MB dye adsorption, confirming that the elemental composition remains almost unchanged. The detailed analysis of the XRD patterns of the LIG-ZnO photocatalyst before and after photocatalytic degradation has also been provided in the SI.

Radical scavenging experiments

Scavenging tests were performed utilizing isopropyl alcohol (IPA) and p-benzoquinone (p-BQ) as specific quenchers for hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) and superoxide radicals (O2˙−), respectively. The removal efficiency was approximately 97% before treatment, but dropped to approximately 82% in the presence of IPA. However, in the presence of p-BQ, the removal efficiency dropped significantly to approximately 3% (Fig. 11). These results suggest that O2˙− is the dominant reactive species initiating the degradation, whilst ˙OH plays a secondary role in the degradation process.

Detailed mechanism

ZnO NRs facilitate efficient charge transport owing to their inherently high electron mobility. This high mobility enables rapid electron migration along the axial direction of the nanorods, thereby significantly enhancing the overall electron transfer rate. Furthermore, growing ZnO directly on LIG sheets to form ZnO/LIG nanorod composites significantly enhances the interfacial contact between the two materials. This strong interfacial coupling promotes accelerated electron transport across the ZnO/LIG interface, thereby facilitating more efficient charge separation and transfer within the composite structure. LIG-ZnO NRs photocatalysis leverages a type-II heterojunction between LIG, a conductive carbon matrix, and ZnO NRs, dramatically enhancing charge separation and pollutant degradation efficiency over pristine ZnO.38–40

Under UV irradiation (hν ≥ 3.2 eV), ZnO NRs generate electron–hole pairs according to the following reaction:ZnO + hν → e− (CB) + h+ (VB)

As shown in Fig. 12, the photogenerated electrons are efficiently transferred from ZnO to the LIG network (eZnO− → eLIG−), which suppresses electron–hole recombination and enhances charge separation, as supported by photoluminescence (PL) analysis. The sharp near-band-edge emission observed at ∼378 nm in LIG-ZnO indicates improved crystallinity and reduced recombination. In contrast, the defect-related emissions in pure ZnO at 393 nm and 410 nm and the broad band near 679 nm correspond to oxygen vacancies and deep-level states. Additionally, the broad visible emission (500–700 nm) of LIG-ZnO confirms the presence of surface defects, which act as active sites for oxygen adsorption and electron trapping.41–43 The transferred electrons react with dissolved oxygen to form reactive oxygen species via the following equations:e− + O2 → O2˙−

Additionally, plasma treatment renders LIG superhydrophilic by introducing oxygen-containing functional groups. This promotes dye adsorption and strengthens the interaction between the catalyst and pollutants.

The photocatalytic performance is highly dependent on the ZnO loading on the LIG base. In LIGZnO5 (5 µL), the ZnO loading is limited and even below the detection limit, as seen in XRD (Fig. 2), providing an insufficient number of photocatalytically active sites and reduced light absorption, resulting in lower photocatalytic activity. Upon increasing the ZnO loading to 10 µL (LIGZnO10), the ZnO layer gets uniformly well-dispersed on the porous LIG surface, providing efficient interfacial charge transfer and an optimum balance between active site availability and light harvesting. The LIG conductive network facilitates rapid electron transport, thereby improving charge separation and enhancing photocatalytic performance. However, a further increase in the ZnO loading in LIGZnO20 (20 µL) results in the agglomeration of ZnO, partially blocking the porous LIG surface, which reduces the effective area, limiting light penetration and impeding the interaction between the catalyst surface and dye molecules, ultimately leading to a reduction in photocatalytic efficiency. Thus, the optimized 10 µL ZnO loading provides the most favorable balance between surface coverage, charge transport and accessibility of active sites, resulting in the highest photocatalytic degradation performance.

Improvements and advantages

The achieved degradation efficiency for the LIG/ZnO composite is 96.7% after 120 minutes of UV irradiation, which is better than that of most reported ZnO-based and graphene-based photocatalytic systems.42–49Table 4 compares the photocatalytic activity of the LIG/ZnO heterostructure produced as part of this work and that of previously reported photocatalysts for MB degradation. The LIG/ZnO heterostructure shows an improvement in photocatalytic activity compared to previously reported ZnO NRs grown on ITO (73.7%) and secondary silicon substrates (56.9%). The LIG/ZnO heterostructure utilizes a conductive and porous structure, providing a conducive environment to enhance charge separation and pollutant porosity. The LIG/ZnO heterostructure shows efficiency equal to that of some of the advanced composite photocatalysts, such as PAN/β-CD/TiO2/GO, GO/g-C3N4/ZnO, ZnO/WO3, and ZnO/CuO. The enhancement in degradation efficiency is due to the synergistic interaction of ZnO NRs with oxygen-plasma-treated LIG, which facilitates efficient electron transport, inhibits electron-hole recombination, and increases the number of available photocatalytically active sites. Thus, the flexible LIG/ZnO heterostructure developed in this work is a highly efficient photocatalyst for wastewater treatment applications.

Conclusions

The present work described the fabrication and characterization of a composite material composed of laser-induced graphene (LIG) and ZnO NRs for the photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue (MB) under UV light. Oxygen plasma treatment was used to hydrophilically modify the LIG surface such that the ZnO nanorods were uniformly deposited upon it and had significantly improved interfacial contact. Structural characterization by X-ray diffraction and SEM validated the successful fabrication of crystalline ZnO NRs, while surface area analysis by the BET method indicated that the composite had a porous structure, thus providing a large number of active sites for the adsorption of dye molecules. The optimized LIGZnO10 composite demonstrated high effectiveness for degrading MB (equal to 96.7%) after 120 minutes of UV irradiation, obeying pseudo-first-order reaction kinetics. The degradation was sustained over multiple cycles, demonstrating the stability of the developed composites for real-sample applications. These results support the improved photocatalytic activity of the LIG/ZnO composite due to the effective separation and transport of charge carriers at the LIG/ZnO interface. The results presented herein suggest that the use of oxygen plasma surface modification is an effective approach for improving the photocatalytic activity of flexible LIG-based composites for application in dye treatment and environmental remediation.
