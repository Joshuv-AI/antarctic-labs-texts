Standardization of CD30 immunohistochemistry staining among three automated immunostaining platforms
Authors: Masafumi Seki, Akira Satou, Renji Funato, Tomoko Tamaki, Naoki Wada, Norihiro Nakada, Hirofumi Matsumoto, Iwao Nakazato, Eriko Wada, Kaneko Sakurai, Toyonori Tsuzuki, Kennosuke Karube
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe identification of CD30 expression by immunohistochemistry is essential for the treatment of lymphomas using an antibody‐drug conjugate targeting CD30. However, no standardized protocol for CD30 staining has been available. In this study, we compared three common automated immunostaining platforms {Bond III (B III), Dako Omnis (DO) and Ventana BenchMark ULTRA (VBMU)}. A primary antibody for CD30, the Ber‐H2 clone, was diluted 50‐ to 400‐fold for B III and DO, and ready‐to‐use antibody was used for VBMU. An enhancement step using a linker was introduced in all protocols. First, several candidate dilutions were selected for each platform by staining six cases. These candidate conditions were then confirmed with 60 cases of various types of peripheral T‐cell lymphomas (PTCLs). The concordance rates of CD30 expression among platforms differed depending on cutoff values and antibody dilutions, except for anaplastic large cell lymphoma. The concordance rates among three platforms in the evaluation of “positive” or “negative” were 100% and 97% when the cutoff values were 1% and 10% respectively, if using 400‐diluted antibody in B III and 100‐diluted antibody in DO. This study demonstrated the feasibility of equalizing CD30 staining of PTCLs among different platforms by adjusting protocols.

INTRODUCTION

CD30, first identified in 1982 as a protein recognized by a monoclonal antibody against Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) cell lines, 1 is a transmembrane glycoprotein (120 kDa) that belongs to the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily. 2 This protein is variously expressed in a variety of lymphomas such as angioimmunoblastic T‐cell lymphoma (AITL), adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL), peripheral T‐cell lymphoma (PTCL), not otherwise specified (PTCL‐NOS), anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL), diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma, and classic HL (CHL), 3 as well as in activated B‐ and T‐lymphocytes. 3

Brentuximab Vedotin (BV), recently introduced as a new treatment for lymphomas with CD30 expression, is composed of an anti‐CD30 monoclonal antibody and four units of monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) microtubule inhibitor. 4 BV is taken up by tumor cells via CD30‐mediated endocytosis followed by the release of MMAEs, leading to the cytotoxicity. 4 , 5 The effectiveness of BV for CHL and CD30‐positive PTCL has been demonstrated in several clinical trials. 6 , 7 , 8

The identification of CD30 expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC) is an essential step in the treatment of patients with PTCL using BV. Although most clinical trials specified the use of the “Ber‐H2” clone, no standard protocol for IHC has been provided. 6 , 7 , 9 Differences in staining platforms and protocols caused variation in positivity rate, even though automated procedures outperformed manual procedures regarding interlaboratory reproducibility. 10 In fact, the CD30 positivity concordance rate among laboratories was not high when each institution used variable protocols. 11 Uniformization of CD30 IHC staining across platforms and laboratories is considered necessary to reduce inequalities in treatment opportunities among patients with PTCLs. In the present study, we analyzed staining performance among different immunostaining platforms from three major manufacturers (Leica Biosystems, Agilent Technologies, and Roche Diagnostics) and examined staining protocols with higher concordance among them.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Specimens

Samples of formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) specimens from 62 cases of PTCL, three cases of CHL, and one case of tonsillitis diagnosed between 2006 and 2022 at Nagoya University Hospital, Aichi Medical University Hospital, University of the Ryukyus Hospital, Nakagami Hospital, and Okinawa Prefectural Nanbu Medical Center and Children's Medical Center were collected. Specimens from Aichi Medical University Hospital were fixed in 10% unbuffered formalin, while specimens from four other hospitals were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. The histological types of PTCL included 30 cases of ATLL, 10 cases of AITL, six cases of ALCL (primary cutaneous and systemic) and 16 cases of PTCL‐NOS. All FFPE specimens were stored at room temperature and cut at a 3–5 µm thickness in each institution, and IHC staining was then performed within 3 months as previous reports confirmed that there was no serious effect on immunohistochemistry if the staining was performed within 3 months after the generation of unstained slide glasses. 12 , 13 We did not obtain written informed consent because this study was conducted by using the stored FFPE samples. Instead, an opt‐out method was adopted to give subjects choices of participation.

Reagents and platforms

Bond III (B III) of Leica Biosystems (Nussloch, Germany), Dako Omnis (DO) of Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, CA), and VENTANA BenchMark ULTRA (VBMU) of Roche Diagnostics (Rotkreuz, Switzerland) were used for automated immunostaining systems. A prediluted antibody (Roche Diagnostics, clone: Ber‐H2, catalog number: 790‐4858) was used as primary antibody for VBMU, and a concentrated primary antibody from Agilent Technology (Agilent Technologies, clone: Ber‐H2, catalog number: M0751) was diluted for B III and DO. The detection system was the Ventana OptiView DAB IHC detection kit (Roche Diagnostics, catalog number: 760‐700) for VBMU and the BOND Polymer refine detection (Leica Biosystems, catalog number: DS9800) for B III. The Ventana OptiView DAB IHC detection kit and BOND Polymer refine detection use an enhanced method with a linker, which combines enzyme polymer and primary antibody. Both EnVision FLEX without a linker step (Agilent Technologies, catalog number: GV800) and EnVision FLEX + Mouse LINKER with a linker step (Agilent Technologies, catalog number catalog number: GV821) were used to compare the CD30 positivity for DO.

Verification

Each specimen was evaluated by two pathologists (MS and KK) and classified into six groups according to CD30 positivity (<1%, 1≤ and <5%, 5≤ and <10%, 10≤ and <20%, 20≤ and <50%, ≥50%). The two pathologists examined each slide together using a multi‐head microscope and discussed to reach a consensus. When heterogeneous staining within a section was observed, we estimated the mean CD30 positivity of the whole slide. The positivity rate was calculated as the proportion of CD30‐positive tumor cells to all tumor cells when lymphoma cells were evident, such as with large and strongly atypical nuclei. When tumor cells were difficult to identify, specifically in the case of the combination of small‐ to medium‐sized tumor cells and prominent background inflammatory cells, the proportion of CD30‐positive cells to all mononuclear cells was calculated. For each case, the CD30 positive rates in DO and B III were compared to the positive rate in VBMU to determine the concordance rate. In addition, thresholds were set at five levels (1%, 5%, 10%, 20%, and 50%), and the concordance rate of positivity and negativity based on each threshold was examined. We considered the positive rate in VBMU as a standard because a prediluted antibody was used for CD30 IHC for VBMU, and the dilution rate could not be changed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Excel and R (version 4.3.1). CD30‐positivity agreement between VBMU and other platforms was evaluated by calculating Cohen's kappa coefficients. A kappa coefficient <0 was judged as poor, 0.00–0.20 as slight, 0.21–0.40 as fair, 0.41–0.60 as moderate, 0.61–0.80 as substantial, and 0.81–1.00 as almost perfect. A correlation between CD30 staining results and fixatives was analyzed using Fischer's test. P‐values less than 0.05 were regarded as indicating statistically significant differences.

RESULTS

Determination of staining conditions

IHC staining of CD30 by VBMU, B III, and DO was performed for three cases composed of CHL, PTCL‐NOS, and tonsillitis using the Ventana OptiView DAB IHC detection kit for VBMU and the 60‐fold diluted primary antibody (3.13 µg/mL), empirically decided, for B III and DO. Both protocols with and without a linker in DO were compared. Staining intensity and positivity in DO without a linker were lower than in DO with a linker, B III, and VBMU (Figure 1). Therefore, the enhancement step with a linker was adopted for DO in subsequent experiments.

CD30 immunohistochemistry staining in tonsilitis using clone Ber‐H2 (60x diluted, 3.13 µg/mL) by four different protocols. (a) Dako Omnis without a mouse linker; (b) Dako Omnis with a mouse linker; (c) Bond III; and (d) Ventana BenchMark ULTRA.

Comparison of the three platforms using a small number of cases

IHC for CD30 was performed with stepwise dilutions of the primary antibody to select several conditions showing high concordance among the three platforms using a total of six cases, including two CHL cases and one ALCL case, in addition to the three cases above. Staining performances in B III and DO with 50‐, 100‐, 200‐, and 400‐fold antibody dilution (0.47–3.76 µg/mL) were compared to VBMU. In all cases except ALCL, staining intensity and positivity tended to decline as antibody concentration decreased in B III and DO. B III with 200‐fold (B III 200x) or 400‐fold (B III 400x) and DO with 100‐fold (DO 100x) or 200‐fold (DO 200x) dilutions were comparable to the results of VBMU (Figure 2). Therefore, we considered these four conditions to be candidate protocols for equalizing CD30 staining. On the other hand, one case of ALCL showed strong and homogenous positivity, regardless of antibody concentrations and platforms (Figure 3).

Comparison of the three platforms using 60 cases of PTCL

To confirm whether standardization was actually accomplished, 60 new PTCL cases (ATLL, 30 cases; AITL, 10 cases; ALCL, five cases; PTCL‐NOS, 15 cases) were stained with five protocols (VBMU, B III 200x, B III 400x, DO 100x, and DO 200x) which showed relatively similar staining performance in the previous analysis using the six cases. Marked non‐specific background staining was not observed in any staining protocols. As mentioned above, when tumor cells were difficult to identify, the proportion of CD30‐positive cells to all mononuclear cells was calculated. Consequently, the percentage of positive cells among all mononuclear cells was calculated in 25 of 60 PTCL cases. Of these 25 cases, seven were ATLL, eight were AITL, and 10 were PTCL‐NOS (Figure S1). Number of cases with PTCL classified by CD30‐positivity rate was summarized (Table S1, Figure 4).

Number of cases with each peripheral T‐cell lymphoma according to CD30‐positive rate. Cases were classified into six levels (<1%, 1≤ and <5%, 5≤ and <10%, 10≤ and <20%, 20≤ and <50%, ≥50%) of CD30 positivity by four different protocols.

The concordance rates of CD30 positivity among the three platforms were compared. For all cases, the combination of VBMU, B III 200x, DO 100x and VBMU, B III 400x, DO 100x showed the highest agreement rate of 85%. In contrast, the combination of VBMU, B III 200x, and DO 200x had the lowest concordance rate of 68% (Table 1). In particular, the concordance rate was only 60% in AITL. When all histological subtypes were included, the combination containing DO 100x had a concordance rate greater than 80% regardless of the dilution factor of B III. Further, ALCL showed 100% agreement regardless of protocol.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we compared IHC staining for CD30 using a Ber‐H2 clone, which was used in the clinical trials on BV, with the aim of standardization in the three major automated immunostaining platforms, VBMU, B III, and DO. IHC staining harmonization between automated immunostaining platforms has already been tried for programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD‐L1). 14 , 15 In PD‐L1 staining, combinations of antibody and platform are fixed as companion diagnostics depending on the therapeutic agent. The various protocols were examined for their ability to reproduce results with the companion diagnostic in these studies. However, no companion diagnostic test for BV has been approved. The Ber‐H2 clone was the designated primary antibody, but no established staining protocol was specified in clinical trials. 7 IHC staining for CD30 by VBMU had a single staining protocol with the prediluted antibody and the Ventana OptiView DAB IHC detection kit. We attempted to standardize IHC staining for CD30 by tuning protocols of B III and DO based on VBMU as the standard. The results showed that DO requires the enhancement step with a linker in addition to the default protocol to standardize staining performance among the three platforms. According to the kappa coefficients, CD30 positivity rates showed good concordance between VBMU and the other four protocols examined, especially B III 200x, B III 400x, and DO 100x. Moreover, B III 400x, DO 100x, and VBMU had the best positive and negative concordance rates in the three models of 100% and 97% when the cutoff value was set at 1% and 10%, respectively. Use of the protocol described above will allow a reduction in the inequality of treatment opportunities, because the 1% cutoff is frequently used to determine the indication for BV in clinical practice. On the whole, we recommend the protocols of B III 400x and DO 100x.

Four‐step dilution of the primary antibody was examined for various types of lymphoma and reactive tonsillitis in B III and DO for comparison of VBMU in combination with the Ventana OptiView DAB IHC detection kit, a ready‐to‐use pre‐diluted antibody. In all cases except one case of ALCL, staining intensity and positivity paralleled the concentration of primary antibody. In contrast, there was no difference in staining intensity within the examined concentrations in ALCL, which was attributable to the higher level of CD30 expression in ALCL compared to other PTCL and tonsillitis. 16 This suggests that lymphomas other than ALCL and reactive lymphoid tissue are more appropriate positive controls than ALCL for the detection of detailed staining sensitivity.

There is no definitive cutoff value or condition of CD30 that actually predicts the effectiveness of BV. Although B III 400x and DO 100x showed the best concordance to VBMU in the present study, the higher primary antibody concentration might enable more sensitive IHC staining, especially for B III with 50‐fold dilution. Increased sensitivity also results in more false positives. No previous study has shown a correlation between CD30‐positivity and treatment response of BV, and there was no significant difference in overall response rate or median duration of response between patients with CD30 expression <10% and ≥10%. 6 , 9 The effect of BV is composed not only of a direct cell‐killing effect on CD30‐expressing tumor cells, but also of various aspects such as immunostimulatory effect, suppressive effect on regulatory T cells, and the cytotoxicity of released MMAE from cells engulfing BV on neighboring CD30‐negative cells. 17 , 18 , 19 The development of IHC staining protocols for molecules besides CD30 that correlate with the therapeutic response of BV is necessary for the safe and efficient use of the drug and is a topic for future research.

The calculation method of CD30 positivity is a limitation in this study. Various inflammatory cells such as lymphocytes, histiocytes, and eosinophils sometimes accompany lymphomas, in particular AITL. Among inflammatory cells in the tumor microenvironment, activated lymphocytes are often positive for CD30 and have nuclear atypia. Therefore we found it difficult to distinguish CD30‐positive tumor cells from CD30‐positive lymphocytes in the backgorund, especially in cases with mild tumor cell atypia, a small proportion of tumor cells, and/or abundant reactive lymphocytes. 3 , 20 In such cases, the proportion of CD30‐positive cells to total mononuclear cells was calculated as the CD30‐positivity rate instead of to total lymphoma cells (Figure S1), following the proposal of a previous study. 3 However, the discrepancy between CD30‐positivity rate among total mononuclear cells and among tumor cells might be substantial in such cases.

The type of fixative varied according to the hospital that provided specimens in this research. Seven cases with PTCL‐NOS (three cases) or AITL (four cases) fixed with unbuffered formalin were compared to those with neutral buffered formalin (12 PTCL‐NOS and six AITL) for the CD30 positivity stained by VBMU. If the threshold is set at 1%, all cases (three PTCL‐NOS and four AITL) fixed in unbuffered formalin and 17 cases (11 PTCL‐NOS and six AITL) fixed in buffered formalin were positive for CD30. If the cutoff is 10%, no specimen fixed in unbuffered formalin and 3 cases (one PTCL‐NOS and two AITL) fixed in buffered formalin were positive for CD30. No significant difference was observed between the two fixatives by Fisher's test at either threshold (p = 1.00 for the 1% cutoff and p = 0.53 for the 10% cutoff). Furthermore, a previous study reported that there was no difference in immunohistochemical staining between 10% unbuffered formalin and 10% neutral buffered formalin. 21 We considered that fixative conditions did not influence the standardization of CD30 immunohistochemistry staining in this research.

In conclusion, we used the Ber‐H2 clone in this study to achieve standardized IHC staining for CD30 with three automated immunostaining systems. B III 400x and DO 100x were the conditions showing the greatest concordance with VBMU. We expect that adjustment of staining protocols in individual institutions based on these conditions as standard will improve the loss of treatment opportunities with BV for patients with PTCL.
