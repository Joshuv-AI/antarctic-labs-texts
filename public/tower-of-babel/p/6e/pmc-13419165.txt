Managing Silicosis in the United States
Authors: Michael R Kahn, Chanidapa Klinhom, William D Wallace, Sarah Edminster, Toby M Maher, Luis E Huerta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOnce considered a disease of the past in developed countries, silicosis is making a worrying comeback as an irreversible and potentially fatal pulmonary disease in the United States. Silicosis has been an unfortunate mainstay of respiratory disease in the developing world, but modern industries, such as stone countertop fabrication, are causing a public health crisis in the United States. This recent uptick in silicosis cases, led by the first recent case in the United States in a stone countertop worker in 2015, may be just the beginning. Because patients seem to present at a younger age with more severe disease than prior cohorts with silicosis, we have found an absence of clear guidelines and standards of care for patients with severe silicosis, particularly for those with end-stage disease. Drawing on experiences from patients with silicosis at the Los Angeles General Medical Center/University of Southern California, we have compiled a clinical review of the topic and provide our institutional approach to screening, examination, diagnosis, management, and, if necessary, referral for lung transplantation. In this “How I Do It,” we share our experience from California to equip clinicians with tools to better diagnose silicosis, recognize end-stage disease, and support patients to lower the morbidity and mortality of this entirely preventable pneumoconiosis.Key Words: interstitial lung disease, occupational lung diseases, public health, silicosis

With origins dating back to the age of Hippocrates, silicosis has been a cause of death and disease for millennia, mostly in developing countries. In the United States, governmental regulation and reduced employment in dust-generating industries, such as manufacturing and mining, led to a decline in silicosis morbidity and mortality.1,2 However, recent data suggest that the prevalence of silicosis has been increasing in the United States since 2015, driven by modern industries, notably stone countertop fabrication.3 Our experience at the Los Angeles General Medical Center (LAGMC), the largest public hospital in southern California, matches these data as the pulmonary service has diagnosed dozens of patients in recent years, 12 of whom presented in 2023 alone. In this edition of “How I Do It,” we share our diagnostic and treatment strategy with the silicosis outbreak as it affects younger patients with more severe, rapidly progressive disease compared with previous silicosis cohorts.

Case 1: It’s All in the History

A healthy 51-year-old Mexican-American male construction worker presented to the hospital with right-sided chest pain and was found to have a pneumothorax. A percutaneous chest tube was placed, which resulted in resolution of his pneumothorax. Two months later, the patient presented with similar symptoms and a left-sided pneumothorax. He was admitted to the hospital for treatment and further diagnostic workup. On high-resolution CT (HRCT) imaging, there was diffuse interstitial reticulation with coalescing plaques, tree-in-bud nodules, and lymphadenopathy. A more careful occupational history was obtained, and it emerged that his construction work actually involved cutting and polishing granite countertops with inconsistent respirator use. He underwent bronchoscopy with BAL and endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial fine needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA); biopsy results revealed silica particles under polarized light.

The Ws of Silicosis: Who, What, Where, When, and Why

Silicosis is caused by inhalation of respirable crystalline silica (RCS), meaning crystalline silica associated with particles in the respirable size range. Crystalline silica is found in materials such as quartz, sand, and granite.4,5 Although the incidence in the United States is not measured nationally, models estimate an incidence of at least 7,300 cases based on 2.3 million Americans exposed to RCS annually.6,7 Although a wide range of industries have been associated with silica exposure in the United States (Table 1), stone countertop fabrication has caused a recent outbreak of silicosis disproportionately affecting young, Latinx men without a college education in California.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8

Inhaled RCS bypasses mucociliary defenses and reaches the alveoli; macrophages then bind to silica through the scavenger receptors SR-AI and SR-AII, increasing type II pneumocyte surfactant production.9 Over time, surfactant is enzymatically degraded, resulting in silicoproteinosis (accumulation of proteinaceous debris in alveoli).10 Macrophages up-regulate IL-1, tumor necrosis factors, and capase-1 production, causing acute inflammation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, collagen secretion, and chronic interstitial fibrosis. Crystalline silica also directly damages macrophage cell membranes, releasing pro-fibrotic factors and silica, leading to a cycle of progressive inflammation, fibrosis, and silicotic nodule formation via lymphocyte aggregation.11 Silica-containing particles and byproducts of these processes are transported to the intrathoracic lymph nodes, resulting in calcified lymphadenopathy in the mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes.

Does My Patient Have Silicosis?

Definitions

Acute silicosis occurs following high-intensity exposure to RCS, with time between exposure to first symptoms ranging from a few weeks to 2 years (occasionally up to 5 years) after inhalation.4 Patients present with rapid-onset dyspnea, fatigue, weight loss, and hypoxia. Accelerated silicosis develops 5 to 10 years after RCS exposure with cough and dyspnea, or even respiratory failure.

Chronic silicosis is characterized by symptom onset occurring over 10 years following RCS exposure.4 These patients typically report progression of respiratory symptoms and occasionally can present with pneumothorax, especially following severe coughing bouts or respiratory infections. Distinguishing between the more common chronic simple silicosis and chronic complex disease is discussed in the following section.

Initial Workup

A detailed occupational and exposure history remains the best screening tool for silicosis in patients with respiratory symptoms.12,13 For all patients with suspected silicosis, we start with pulmonary function tests (PFTs) and chest radiograph (CXR), noting these tests have low sensitivity. Results of PFTs (spirometry, lung volumes, and diffusion capacity) can be normal in early or mild disease. Spirometry results classically reveal a restrictive defect; in late-stage disease, spirometry findings can also indicate obstructive or mixed defects with loss of lung volumes and gas transfer deficits.

Upper lobe nodularity and volume loss are the characteristic CXR findings in acute and accelerated silicosis, although CXR can be unremarkable in mild disease.9,10,12 Acute silicoproteinosis is typically associated with an alveolar filling pattern resembling pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (Fig 1). In addition, CXR findings in accelerated silicosis include more prominent parenchymal nodules, Kerley B lines, and pleural effusions (Fig 2).6,12,14 CXR findings in chronic silicosis include upper lobe-predominant sub-centimeter nodules, hilar and mediastinal fullness (enlarged lymph nodes), and masses.12,14

Accelerated silicosis with pleural effusion and pleural nodule on chest radiograph (left) and chest CT imaging (right).

If there are abnormal findings on CXR or if a high index of suspicion for silicosis remains despite normal PFT and CXR results, we perform HRCT imaging. HRCT in acute silicosis shows alveolar filling, ground-glass opacities with interlobular septal thickening (“crazy paving”), and consolidations representing early fibrosis.12 Classic findings in accelerated and chronic disease include innumerable well-circumscribed micronodules in the upper lobes, progressive massive fibrosis (PMF) with an apical/hilar predominance, emphysema and bronchiectasis adjacent to PMF lesions, and mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

Chronic simple silicosis presents with upper lobe-predominant sub-centimeter nodules with or without lymphadenopathy (Fig 3). Chronic complex silicosis features progressive massive fibrosis defined as conglomerations of nodules into masses measuring > 10 mm in diameter, sometimes with cavitation, and hilar/mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Fig 4). Lung parenchyma and peribronchial vessels surrounding the nodules may also be distorted, causing bronchiectasis. PMF can cause a mixed obstructive/restrictive pattern on spirometry. Calcification of nodules and lymphadenopathy suggest more chronic or distant exposure. Less common findings include pleural effusion, pneumothorax, or an usual interstitial pneumonia pattern rather than the upper lobe-predominant lesions.

Chronic complicated silicosis with right-sided tension pneumothorax and progressive massive fibrosis on chest radiograph (left) and chest CT imaging (right).

With characteristic PFTs and imaging findings as noted here, and a history of silica exposure, we make the diagnosis of silicosis. We then order a 6-min walk test to assess function, followed by additional studies to consider alternative diagnoses and to identify any comorbid or exacerbating conditions (Table 2).

When Should Additional Workup Be Pursued?

If there is diagnostic ambiguity following the aforementioned studies, only then do we consider bronchoscopy with BAL and/or biopsy. BAL can reveal foamy macrophages with periodic acid-Schiff staining, which contain birefringent silica particles (observable under polarized light), particularly in cases of silicoproteinosis.18,19 Although BAL alone is often nondiagnostic, it can help exclude concurrent infection and other interstitial lungs with similar imaging findings such as hypersensitivity pneumonitis or eosinophilic pneumonia.

EBUS-TBNA can be performed if there is lymphadenopathy and the diagnosis remains unclear. Histopathology of lymph nodes can reveal dust-laden macrophages and small birefringent silica particles in early disease; silicotic nodules from chronic exposure are characterized by concentric arrangement of dense, acellular collagen fibers with peripheral macrophages and lymphocytes (Fig 5).19

Results of endobronchial or transbronchial biopsies, including cryobiopsy, can be used to identify silicotic nodules (Fig 6), but due to the increased risk of pneumothorax, we only pursue biopsy if definitive diagnosis is necessary.20 On gross examination, silicotic nodules appear as well-circumscribed, firm, tan-gray masses ranging in size from millimeters to > 1 cm and can coalesce into larger, conglomerate lesions (PMFs).19 Calcification is often present within or surrounding these nodules. Cavitation, if present, suggests a superimposed infection, such as TB, or an infarction. Microscopically, silicotic nodules display a characteristic eosinophilic, whorled appearance consisting of well-demarcated, hyalinized collagenous whorls with central density. Early lesions may exhibit peripheral fibroblastic and histiocytic inflammation and, eventually, calcification. Notably, discrete epithelioid (sarcoid-like) granulomas are not a feature of silicosis. Silica particles identified within silicotic nodules and alveolar walls exhibit weak birefringence under polarized light. They are typically polyhedral in shape and approximately 2 μm in size; larger crystals, if present, are characteristically needle-shaped.21

Case 1, Continued: A Rapidly Progressive Disease

Following discharge from the hospital requiring supplemental oxygen, the patient followed up in the pulmonary clinic. Results of his PFTs revealed a severe restrictive ventilatory defect, a repeat CT chest showed enlarging masses, and his BAL grew Mycobacterium kansasii. After cessation of stonecutting and regular follow-up with pulmonology, he began treatment for his nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) infection and was referred for lung transplant evaluation. Despite several months of treatment and close follow-up, he experienced his third pneumothorax. Following 1 week in the hospital without resolution of the pneumothorax, the patient received an offer for lung transplantation.

My Patient Has Silicosis—Now What?

At Diagnosis

For patients with obstructive disease or bronchiectasis, we provide airway clearance therapies, including nebulized hypertonic saline, cough assist devices, vest physiotherapy, and inhaled long-acting β2 agonists and long-acting muscarinic antagonists.

We collect a serum interferon-gamma release assay and three sputum samples (or one BAL) for AFB smear with culture and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) polymerase chain reaction for all patients at diagnosis. This is both because silicosis has a known association with MTB and NTM infections and because our patient population at LAGMC has a high prevalence of MTB.22,23 In patients with severe features, we prefer BAL over expectorated sputum due to the radiographic and clinical overlap between silicosis and these infections, and the importance of distinguishing latent TB infection from active TB and diagnosing NTM infection.24, 25, 26, 27 If EBUS-TBNA is performed, lymph node samples are sent for AFB studies and MTB-polymerase chain reaction. Recommendations should be considered on local MTB and NTM prevalence.

Patients with latent TB infection and active pulmonary TB should receive guideline-based treatment.24, 25, 26 Pulmonary NTM infections are challenging because patients with silicosis and positive NTM cultures meet Infectious Diseases Society of America clinical/radiographic requirements by definition.27 Although deciding to treat NTM is usually made with a patient-centered approach, we generally treat NTM in our silicosis cohort, especially if the patient is a transplant candidate. In patients without evidence of mycobacterial disease, we do not use prophylactic antibiotics.23

We also order a transthoracic echocardiogram for patients who require supplemental oxygen, have signs of volume overload, or have reduced diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide; this is because shunts, pulmonary hypertension, or cardiomyopathy affects treatment and prognosis.

Health Maintenance

Management of silicosis involves counseling patients on avoiding further RCS exposure, treatment of concurrent pulmonary or comorbid conditions, administration of lung-protective vaccinations (annual influenza/COVID-19 and pneumococcal series), counseling on pulmonary health, smoking cessation, and referral for psychosocial support.28,29

We order annual basic metabolic panels and PFTs to monitor for kidney disease and declining lung function. Worsening PFT results should prompt re-evaluation with imaging and microbiology studies.

The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies crystalline silica as a carcinogen, prompting us to consider annual low-dose CT scans in our silicosis cohort, especially if the patient has any tobacco use history or other cancer risk factors.30, 31, 32, 33 Because there are no current screening guidelines for silicosis, we engage in shared decision-making with patients regarding annual cancer screening.

There is an absence of effective treatments for severe silicosis. Whole lung lavage has been studied: a recent article reported radiographic improvement in six patients following whole lung lavage.34 Although this may play a role in acute silicoproteinosis, it has significant risks and unclear clinical benefit, and thus we do not consider whole lung lavage in our patients. Antiinflammatory, immunomodulatory, and antioxidant agents such as steroids, anakinra, and N-acetylcysteine reportedly reduce fibrotic changes in animal models, but their efficacy in human silicosis has been disappointing.11

What About Worsening Symptoms?

In patients with symptomatic worsening without hypoxemia, we pursue outpatient respiratory viral panels, sputum bacterial and AFB cultures, spirometry, and two-view CXR (Table 2). We initiate or reinforce airway clearance therapy while awaiting infectious study results and avoid empiric treatment with antibiotics.

We pursue inpatient admission for new or worsened hypoxemia. In addition to the aforementioned protocol, we initiate empiric antibiotics targeting previous pathogens (or community-acquired pneumonia if no prior data exist). We also pursue CT imaging to look for pneumothorax, disease progression (particularly in PMF), and ground-glass opacities. Although lacking evidence, we have found success with corticosteroids in patients who have RCS exposure within 12 months, refractory hypoxemia, and ground-glass opacities, in the absence of infection. The LAGMC experience and other outbreaks worldwide suggest that silicosis from artificial stone can continue to have active inflammation and disease progression for months to years following exposure cessation.35 Steroid dosing is individualized based on comorbidities and symptom severity. We repeat PFTs once symptoms improve, and worsening disease may prompt transplant evaluation.

What About End-Stage Disease?

Although avoidance of RCS and use of oxygen and supportive care are sufficient for patients without severe or progressive disease, the only known disease-modifying treatment for end-stage silicosis is lung transplantation. To our knowledge, there are no specific guidelines for referring patients with silicosis for transplant. We use criteria for nonidiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis as starting points (Table 2).36

We also routinely refer patients for transplant evaluation if they have PMF, pneumothorax at diagnosis, hemoptysis, or worsening imaging over 12 months (suggesting ongoing inflammation). We pursue early referral for lung transplant evaluation, particularly if the patient is hypoxic at diagnosis and within 12 months of last RCS exposure as these patients often have disease progression.35, 36, 37 For severe exacerbations, we initiate expedited inpatient referral for lung transplantation.

Patients with occupational lung disease were previously thought to have poor outcomes following transplantation, perhaps due to analysis of only small cohorts or surgical difficulty due to pleural involvement.38 However, some studies now show that patients with silicosis are not at increased risk for posttransplant mortality compared with those with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or any indication.39,40 For patients who are potential transplant candidates, pleurodesis should be avoided, and persistent pneumothorax should prompt urgent inpatient lung transplant referral.

Antifibrotics are not routinely used in silicosis, although nintedanib and pirfenidone both show promise for reducing fibrosis from silicosis in animal models.41 In patients with PMF or worsening fibrosis, we consider initiating nintedanib based on its expanded indications for progressive pulmonary fibrosis.42,43 In patients with silicosis and group 3 pulmonary hypertension, it is reasonable to start inhaled treprostinil.44

Case 2: Prevention

A 42-year-old man was referred to the pulmonary clinic for a chronic cough and mildly reduced FVC seen on spirometry ordered by his primary care doctor. He owns a countertop fabrication company. His CXR revealed apical nodules, and a diagnosis of chronic simple silicosis was made. He was counseled on the risk of silica exposure, and he transitioned into a management role in his business, avoiding further exposure. He informed his employees of what he learned from his own experience and requested an Occupational Safety and Health Administration evaluation. After several follow-up appointments, the patient’s symptoms, spirometry findings, and imaging all remain stable.

Conclusions

Our diagnostic approach to silicosis (Fig 7) begins with an occupational history consistent with silicosis, prompting us to order PFTs and CXR. With characteristic findings on these studies, we diagnose silicosis and perform additional serologic, microbiologic, and radiologic studies to better characterize alternative diagnoses, comorbid conditions, and complexity of disease. Ultimately, the severity and progression of disease will dictate treatment and transplant referral, and we hope the LAGMC experience helps illustrate the nuances of the disease course.

As silicosis becomes increasingly prevalent in the United States, it is important to remember that this is a preventable disease. When patients present to pulmonologists, perhaps morbidity has already occurred; however, physicians still play a key role in educating patients and advocating for primary prevention.45 We recommend an educational initiative, aimed at high-risk workplaces and pulmonologists, to raise awareness of modern professions with high RCS exposure, improve early detection, and teach physicians and patients about workplace safety. Pulmonologists can educate patients on the availability of workplace consultation (www.osha.gov/consultation) and can feel empowered to share Occupational Safety and Health Administration requirements for ventilation, wet-cutting techniques (for stonecutting), and personal protective equipment (Table 4).2 We also advocate for adoption of electronic case reporting for silicosis in all states, similar to what has been implemented in California, and a national workplace reporting system.46 Lastly, we suggest development of guidelines for silicosis treatment/monitoring and an update to lung transplant referral criteria for this population.

Although the global incidence of silicosis is increasing, there is still significant underdiagnosis, likely related to the often decades-long lead time between exposure and symptom onset in addition to psychosocial or financial limitations to seeking medical care, including access to transplantation, which contributes to the severity of illness we are seeing on presentation. Furthermore, our most vulnerable patients work in these high-risk occupations, and there are significant personal dangers that can come with reporting unsafe working conditions. It is along these lines that we urge governmental involvement, perhaps in a fashion similar to the creation of the Coal Workers’ Health Surveillance Program. This effort must include assistance in providing access to disease screening and treatment, minimizing risks for reporting unsafe working conditions, advocating for workplace reform, and funding for research. Given the climbing rates of disease, pulmonologists must also be on the lookout for silicosis and, with each new case, we must advocate for our patients, knowing this is a preventable disease.
