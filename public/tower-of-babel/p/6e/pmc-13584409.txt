Risk of antidepressant-induced hyponatremia in geriatric patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yuan Zhang, Jin Zhang, Rui Jing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHyponatremia is a frequent electrolyte disorder in clinical practice and is particularly prevalent among elderly populations. Antidepressants have been identified as an important pharmacological contributor. However, the event rate, overall risk, and differences across antidepressant classes remain incompletely characterized. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantify the event rate and comparative risk of antidepressant-induced hyponatremia in geriatric populations.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, and EMBASE were systematically searched from inception to February 5, 2026. A random-effects model was applied to estimate pooled event rates and risk ratios (RRs) among antidepressant users. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to antidepressant classes and individual compounds. Head-to-head comparisons were performed to evaluate differences in hyponatremia risk across antidepressant classes.ResultsA total of 21 cohort studies, comprising 2,115,187 geriatric antidepressant users, were included. The pooled event rates of any hyponatremia and clinically relevant hyponatremia were 0.0825 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.0566–0.1125) and 0.0287 (95% CI: 0.0117–0.0526), respectively. Serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) demonstrated the highest event rates (0.1040 and 0.0503, respectively), followed by selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs: 0.0981 and 0.0275, respectively).Antidepressant use was associated with a significantly increased risk of any hyponatremia (RR = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.42–4.14, P = 0.001) and clinically relevant hyponatremia (RR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.57–5.57, P < 0.001), resulting in an absolute increase of 0.0037 (95% CI: 0.0011–0.0080) and 0.0046 (95% CI: 0.0013–0.0108) in event rate compared with non-users, respectively. This increased risk remained significant in subgroups of SSRIs (RR = 2.40 and 3.22, respectively) and SNRIs (RR = 2.90 and 2.91, respectively). Head-to-head comparisons indicated that SNRIs were associated with higher risks of both any and clinically relevant hyponatremia compared with SSRIs. In contrast, mirtazapine was associated with significantly lower risks of any hyponatremia (RR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.39–0.61, P = 0.031) and clinically relevant hyponatremia (RR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.39–0.61, P < 0.001) compared with SSRIs.ConclusionAntidepressant use in geriatric patients is associated with an increased risk of hyponatremia, with clinically relevant differences observed among antidepressant classes. SNRIs appear to confer a relatively higher risk, whereas mirtazapine may represent a comparatively safer option. However, these findings should be interpreted considering substantial heterogeneity and potential residual confounding among observational studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07911-y.

Background

Hyponatremia, defined as a serum sodium concentration below 135 mmol/L, is one of the most common electrolyte disturbances encountered in clinical practice [1, 2]. It is independently associated with more than a 50% increase in the risk of hospital mortality, a 14% or 1-day increase in the length of hospital stay, and substantial hospital resource utilization [3, 4]. The clinical manifestations of hyponatremia range from mild and nonspecific symptoms, such as fatigue and nausea [5], to severe neurological complications, including seizures, coma, and death, depending on the severity and the rate of sodium decline [6, 7]. The prevalence of hyponatremia has been reported to be 20–35% in hospitalized elderly [8], 50% in patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage [9], and 20% in hospitalized patients with heart failure [10]. Older adults are particularly susceptible to hyponatremia due to age-related physiological changes, including impaired renal function, altered water homeostasis, and increased sensitivity to hormonal regulation [11–13].

Among the diverse etiologies of hyponatremia, drug-induced mechanisms have received increasing attention in recent years. Antidepressants, which are widely prescribed for mood disorders in elderly populations, have been consistently implicated as a significant contributor to hyponatremia [14, 15]. This association is especially relevant in geriatric patients, who exhibit both a higher prevalence of depression and an increased vulnerability to adverse drug reactions [16]. Previous studies have suggested that selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are associated with a higher risk of hyponatremia compared with other antidepressant classes [17, 18].

The underlying mechanism is most commonly attributed to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) [19, 20]. Antidepressants may affect central neurotransmitter pathways, particularly serotonergic and noradrenergic systems, leading to increased secretion of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) or enhanced renal responsiveness to its effects [21, 22]. This results in increased water reabsorption and dilutional hyponatremia. The overall event rate of antidepressant-induced hyponatremia ranges from 0.87% in the first 30 days to 10.5% in the first 3 years after drug initiation [23]. Furthermore, comorbid conditions, polypharmacy, and concomitant use of medication such as diuretics further amplify the risk in elderly individuals [24]. It has been reported that SIADH may occur in 12% to 33% of elderly populations receiving SSRIs [25].

Despite growing awareness of antidepressant-induced hyponatremia, several important gaps remain. Reported event rates vary considerably across studies, reflecting heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, and outcome definitions [26–28]. Moreover, although SSRIs and SNRIs are often regarded as high-risk agents, quantitative comparisons across different antidepressant classes, including tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) and atypical agents such as mirtazapine, remain limited. In addition, clinically relevant hyponatremia, which carries greater clinical significance, has not been consistently distinguished from milder forms in previous analyses.

Therefore, the present study aimed to conduct a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis focusing specifically on elderly populations. The objectives were to estimate the pooled event rates of both any and clinically relevant hyponatremia associated with antidepressant use, to quantify the relative risk compared with non-use, and to compare the risk profiles among different antidepressant classes.

Methods

Comprehensive literature search

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [29]. A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science, and EMBASE from database inception to February 5, 2026. No language restrictions were applied. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text terms related to antidepressants and hyponatremia. The detailed search strategy is provided in Additional file 1: Table S1. In addition, the reference lists of relevant studies were manually screened to identify additional eligible publications.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Study eligibility was determined according to the PECOS framework as follows:Participant (P): Elderly patients aged ≥ 60 years;Exposure (E): Antidepressants use;Comparator (C): Non-users of antidepressants, users of alternative antidepressant classes or compounds, or no comparator group for studies reporting event rates only. Comparisons with non-users were considered separately from head-to-head comparisons among antidepressant treatments;Outcome (O): Hyponatremia;Study design (S): Observational cohort studies or randomized controlled trials

Hyponatremia outcomes were categorized into two predefined groups. Any hyponatremia was defined as either a reported diagnosis or a serum sodium concentration < 135 mmol/L. Clinically relevant hyponatremia was defined as symptomatic hyponatremia, hyponatremia requiring treatment or hospitalization, or a serum sodium concentration < 130 mmol/L.

Studies were excluded if they were case reports, conference abstracts, reviews, or meta-analyses, or if sufficient data for extraction were unavailable. For overlapping populations, the study with the largest sample size was included. Two reviewers (JZ, RJ) independently conducted literature selection. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (YZ).

Data extraction

Two investigators (JZ, RJ) independently extracted data using a standardized form. Extracted information included study characteristics (first author, publication year, country/region, study design, healthcare settings), patient demographics (sample size, clinical indications, mean age, sex distribution, baseline sodium status, comorbidities, concomitant medications), antidepressant exposure characteristics (classes, specific compounds, treatment initiation status), diagnostic criteria for hyponatremia, adjusted confounding factors, follow-up duration, and outcome data (event counts for overall antidepressants, antidepressant classes, and individual compounds). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (YZ).

Quality assessment

Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies, which evaluates selection, comparability, and outcome domains [30]. Each study could receive up to nine scores, with scores of 7–9, 5–6, and ≤ 4 indicating high, moderate, and low methodological quality, respectively. Two reviewers (JZ, RJ) independently conducted quality assessment. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (YZ).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 18.0 (StataCorp, TX, US). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with values > 50%, 25%-50%, and < 25% indicating substantial, moderate, and low heterogeneity, respectively. However, a random-effects model was applied, regardless the level of between-study heterogeneity, for all meta-analyses to provide more conservative estimates. Event rates with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CIs) were pooled using the Freeman-Tukey transformation. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) and 95% CIs were calculated for comparisons between antidepressant users and non-users, as well as for head-to-head comparison among antidepressant classes. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses regarding study design, study quality, healthcare settings, region, psychiatric conditions, and treatment initiation. Meta-regression analyses were conducted when at least ten studies were available to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, including mean age, percentage of females, and outcome definitions. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots. Egger’s test was performed only when at least ten studies were available. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of eligible studies

The initial literature search identified 1721 records. After removal of duplicates, 999 studies remained for title and abstract screening, leading to the exclusion of 933 studies. The remaining 66 articles underwent full-text assessment, of which 45 were excluded for various reasons (Additional file 2: Table S2). Ultimately, 21 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1) [26–28, 31–48].

Fig. 1Flowchart of literature search and selection

Flowchart of literature search and selection

All included studies were cohort studies, comprising 18 retrospective and 3 prospective studies [35, 36, 45]. Seventeen studies were conducted exclusively in elderly patients, while four studies provided subgroup analyses for elderly participants [28, 40–42]. Sample sizes varied substantially, ranging from 15 to 1,238,300 participants, with a total of 2,115,187 elderly patients receiving antidepressants. Definitions of hyponatremia varied across studies. Eleven studies used a threshold of serum sodium concentration < 135 mmol/L, two studies applied < 130 mmol/L [45, 48], and one study employed < 125 mmol/L [40]. The remaining studies documented hyponatremia based on clinical outcomes such as treatment requirement or hospitalization [27, 28, 31, 34, 37, 43, 44]. Fifteen studies exclusively enrolled patients who newly initiated antidepressants. The methodological quality of included studies varied, with NOS scores ranging from 2 to 9 (Additional file 1: Table S3). Seven studies were classified as high quality [27, 34, 35, 37, 40, 41, 43], six as moderate quality [26, 31, 36, 38, 44, 45], and the remaining studies as low quality. Event rate of hyponatremia

Table 2 summarizes the event rates of antidepressant-induced hyponatremia overall, by antidepressant classes, and by individual compounds in geriatric patients. There were substantial between-study heterogeneity of meta-analysis of the event rate of any hyponatremia (I2 = 99.96%). The pooled event rate of any hyponatremia among elderly patients receiving antidepressants was 0.0825 (95% CI: 0.0566–0.1125; Fig. 2). When stratified by antidepressant classes, SNRIs demonstrated the highest event rate at 0.1040 (95% CI: 0.0476–0.1769; Additional file 2: Fig. S1), followed by SSRIs at 0.0981 (95% CI: 0.0604–0.1431; Additional file 2: Fig. S2). In contrast, TCAs and mirtazapine showed lower event rates of 0.0350 (95% CI: 0.0032–0.0876; Additional file 2: Fig. S3) and 0.0309 (95% CI: 0.0036–0.0790; Additional file 2: Fig. S4), respectively. The forest plots of any hyponatremia induced by different antidepressant compounds are presented in Additional file 2: Fig. S5 and Fig. S6.

Hyponatremia risk of antidepressant users

Six studies were included in the meta-analysis comparing the risk of hyponatremia between antidepressant users and non-users (Table 3) [26, 27, 33, 34, 37, 38]. The pooled analysis demonstrated that antidepressant use was associated with a significantly increased risk of any hyponatremia (RR = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.42–4.14, P = 0.001; Fig. 4), resulting in an absolute increase of 0.0037 (95% CI: 0.0011–0.0080) in event rate compared with non-users. Subgroup analyses showed that both SSRIs (RR = 2.40, 95% CI: 1.35–4.27, P = 0.003) and SNRIs (RR = 2.90, 95% CI: 1.34–6.29, P = 0.007) were significantly associated with an increased risk. Meta-analysis of four studies revealed that antidepressant use were associated with significantly increased risk of clinically relevant hyponatremia in geriatric patients (RR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.57–5.57, P < 0.001; Fig. 5), indicating an absolute increase of 0.0046 (95% CI: 0.0013–0.0108) in event rate compared with non-users. A significantly increased risk of clinically relevant hyponatremia was also observed in users of SSRIs (RR = 3.22, 95% CI: 1.68–6.16, P < 0.001) and SNRIs (RR = 2.91, 95% CI: 1.23–7.53, P = 0.028).

Head-to-head comparison by antidepressant classes

We directly compared the risk of hyponatremia between different antidepressant classes (Table 4). Head-to-head comparisons using SSRIs as the reference demonstrated that SNRIs were associated with a significantly higher risk of both any hyponatremia (RR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.04–1.51, P = 0.018; Fig. 6) and clinically relevant hyponatremia (RR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.00-1.44; P = 0.045; Fig. 7). In contrast, mirtazapine was associated with significantly lower risks, with an RR of 0.61 (95% CI: 0.39–0.96, P = 0.031; Fig. 6) for any hyponatremia and 0.49 (95% CI: 0.39–0.61, P < 0.001; Fig. 7) for clinically relevant hyponatremia compared with SSRIs. No significant differences were observed between TCAs and SSRIs (P > 0.05).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Further subgroup analyses were performed regarding study design (prospective cohort vs. retrospective cohort), study quality (high quality vs. moderate to low quality), healthcare settings (inpatients vs. outpatients), region (North America, Europe, Oceania, East Asia), and sensitivity analyses were conducted in patients newly initiating antidepressants and in depressed patients (Additional file 1: Table S5). There were significant between-subgroup differences for study design (P = 0.025) and study quality (P < 0.001). In patients newly initiating antidepressants, the pooled event rates of any hyponatremia and clinically relevant hyponatremia were 0.0661 (95% CI: 0.0401–0.0975) and 0.0211 (95% CI: 0.0061–0.0441), respectively. In depressed patients, the pooled event rates were 0.1199 (95% CI: 0.0777–0.1690) and 0.0069 (95% CI: 0.0019–0.0141), respectively.

Meta-regression analysis and publication bias

Meta-regression analysis demonstrated that the definition of hyponatremia may partially explain the source of heterogeneity (P < 0.001; Additional file 1: Table S6). Studies defining clinically relevant hyponatremia showed significantly lower event rates than those using serum sodium concentration < 135 mmol/L as the cutoff. Funnel plots were largely symmetric upon visual inspection (Additional file 2: Fig. S13 and Fig. S14), indicating no obvious publication bias. Egger’s test was not conducted because fewer than ten studies were available.

Discussion

This meta-analysis, integrating 21 cohort studies involving over 2.1 million participants, demonstrated a significant association between antidepressant use and the risk of hyponatremia in geriatric patients. It also provides clinically relevant information on both the magnitude of the risk and differences across antidepressant classes.

The present study revealed that the pooled event rate of any hyponatremia after antidepressants use in geriatric patients was 8.25%. When stratified by drug classes, the event rates were 10.40%, 9.81%, 3.50%, and 3.09% for SNRIs, SSRIs, TCAs, and mirtazapine, respectively. These estimates are notably higher than those reported in general adult populations in recent meta-analyses [17, 18]. In one meta-analysis comprising more than eight million adults, the event rates of any hyponatremia were 4.65% for overall antidepressants, 7.44% for SNRIs, 5.59% for SSRIs, 2.66% for TCAs, and 1.02% for mirtazapine [17]. In another meta-analysis with nearly four million adults, the corresponding rates were 6.04%, 6.13%, and 5.98% for overall antidepressants, SNRIs, and SSRIs, respectively [18]. These rates in general populations were clearly lower than those observed in the present study, which focused exclusively on elderly participants.

Importantly, the event rate of clinically relevant hyponatremia after antidepressant use was 2.87% in geriatric patients, as shown in the present meta-analysis. The event rates in geriatric patients (2.83%, 5.03%, 2.75% for overall antidepressants, SNRIs, and SSRIs, respectively) were also higher than those reported in general adult populations reported by Gheyses T et al. (1.10%, 2.57%, 1.26%) and Li Y et al. (1.26%, 1.33%, 0.82%) [17, 18]. These findings are in line with age-stratified subgroup analyses from several large-scale studies [40, 41]. A multi-institutional cohort study reported that the event rates of hyponatremia ranged from 2.7% to 3.2% in patients aged ≥ 60 years, compared with 1.1% to 1.8% in those aged 40–59 years and 0.9% to 1.3% in those aged 18–39 years [41]. Another study showed a 3% event rate of profound hyponatremia in individuals 65–79 years and 4% in those ≥ 80 years, whereas the rates were ≤ 1% in younger groups [40].

The higher pooled event rate in elderly populations compared with younger individuals may be related to increased physiological vulnerability. This includes reduced renal function, impaired water excretion, altered hormonal regulation, and a higher likelihood of comorbid conditions that predispose to hyponatremia [49]. Although the event rate of clinically relevant hyponatremia is numerically lower (2.87%), it still represents a clinically meaningful burden, especially given its association with serious neurological complications.

The risk estimates in our meta-analysis further emphasize the clinical relevance of this association. Antidepressant use was associated with a 2.43-fold increase in the risk of any hyponatremia and a 2.96-fold increase in the risk of clinically relevant hyponatremia. As a results, there was an absolute increase of an additional 4 geriatrics (95% CI: 1–8) suffering any hyponatremia and 5 (95% CI: 1–11) geriatrics suffering clinically relevant hyponatremia for every 1000 geriatrics treated with antidepressants. Although the absolute risk increases are small, antidepressants may cause a clinically meaningful burden in geriatric populations given the high prevalence of antidepressant use and the potential consequences of sodium disturbances. These associations remained significant in subgroup analyses of both SNRIs and SSRIs. Notably, the risk was higher for clinically relevant hyponatremia. This suggests that antidepressant exposure may not only increase the occurrence of hyponatremia but also predispose patients to more severe forms. The magnitude of these risk estimates is consistent with previous meta-analyses that included case-control studies in adult populations, which reported a two- to three-fold increased risk of hyponatremia with antidepressant use [17, 18]. Take together, these findings support the robustness of the association across different study designs and populations.

The interpretation of risk comparisons between antidepressant users and non-users warrants careful consideration. In the present meta-analysis, non-users of antidepressants were used as the comparator group in studies evaluating the association between antidepressant exposure and hyponatremia. This comparison is clinically meaningful because it estimates the overall excess risk associated with antidepressant exposure in routine practice. However, antidepressant users may differ from non-users in several important aspects, including psychiatric disease burden, comorbid conditions, healthcare utilization, and concomitant medication exposure. Although several included studies applied multivariable adjustment or propensity score matching for potential confounders [26, 27, 34, 37], adjustment strategies varied considerably across studies, and residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the pooled risk estimates should be interpreted as associations rather than definitive causal effects.

The observed association between antidepressant use and hyponatremia risk in elderly populations may be explained by several pathophysiological mechanisms. SSRIs enhance serotonergic neurotransmission, which stimulates hypothalamic 5-HT2 receptors and promotes the release of ADH [50]. SNRIs, in addition to serotonergic effects, increase noradrenergic activity, which may further modulate ADH secretion through both central and peripheral pathways [51]. The combined serotonergic and noradrenergic effects of SNRIs may therefore amplify the process, which could explain the higher event rates and relative risk observed with this class. Age-related physiological changes further contribute to this effect. Elderly individuals often have a reduced glomerular filtration rate, impaired urinary dilution capacity, and increased renal sensitivity to ADH [11, 13]. These factors limit the ability to excrete free water, increasing susceptibility to dilutional hyponatremia. Beyond pharmacological mechanisms, patient-specific vulnerability factors likely contribute substantially to the observed risk. Older adults frequently have multiple comorbidities, including chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and endocrine disorders, which may impair water and sodium regulation. Furthermore, polypharmacy is common in geriatric populations, and concomitant medications such as diuretics, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, antipsychotics, and mood stabilizers may independently increase susceptibility to hyponatremia or interact with antidepressant exposure [44]. However, detailed information regarding baseline sodium concentrations, renal function, comorbidity profiles, and concomitant medications was inconsistently reported in the included studies, preventing comprehensive evaluation of these patient-level risk modifiers.

The comparative analysis of antidepressant classes represents a key strength of this study and provides clinically useful information. SNRIs showed the highest event rates, with 10.40% for any hyponatremia and 5.03% for clinically relevant hyponatremia. SSRIs followed closely, with rates of 9.81% and 2.75%, respectively. Direct comparisons indicated that SNRIs were associated with a higher risk than SSRIs, with relative risks of 1.25 for any hyponatremia and 1.20 for clinically relevant hyponatremia. Although these differences may appear modest, they are clinically meaningful in the context of a high baseline risk, particularly in geriatric populations.

In contrast, mirtazapine was associated with a lower risk. The event rates of any and clinically relevant hyponatremia were 3.09% and 1.36%, respectively. In addition, the risks of both outcomes were significantly lower than those observed with SSRIs (both RRs < 1). This finding is consistent with differences in pharmacological mechanisms. Pharmacodynamic analyses have shown a correlation between decrease in serum sodium and binding affinity for the serotonin transporter (SERT), suggesting that the relatively low affinity of mirtazapine for SERT may partly explain its lower risk [27]. Mirtazapine acts as an antagonist at presynaptic α2-adrenergic receptors and selectively blocks 5-HT2 and 5-HT3 receptors, rather than inhibiting serotonin reuptake [52]. As a result, it is less likely to stimulate ADH release, which may explain its more favorable safety profile [53]. This observation is in line with previous studies suggesting that mirtazapine may be a safer alternative in elderly patients at high risk of hyponatremia or in those who develop SSRI-induced SIADH [54, 55]. More recent studies have also explored other alternatives, such as agomelatine, particularly in patients with prior SSRI-induced hyponatremia, further highlighting the importance of individualized treatment selection [56].

These findings may help guide drug selection and monitoring strategies in clinical practice. Given the relatively high event rate of hyponatremia, routine monitoring of serum sodium should be considered in elderly patients starting antidepressant therapy, as recommended in several guidelines [57, 58]. Monitoring appears especially important during the early phase of treatment (2–4 weeks), when the risk is highest [59]. In addition, the choice of antidepressant should be individualized. For patients at high risk of hyponatremia, mirtazapine may be a reasonable option, whereas SNRIs should be used with caution.

Similar to our study, several previous meta-analyses have evaluated the risk of antidepressant-induced hyponatremia [17, 18, 60]. However, these studies included adult populations across broad age ranges, without specifically addressing the substantially higher vulnerability of older adults. Our study specifically focused on elderly patients and provided age-specific estimates of absolute event rates, comparative risks, and clinically relevant hyponatremia associated with different antidepressant classes. Furthermore, our study performed detailed subgroup regarding study design, study quality, healthcare settings, region to explore the potential source of extremely high heterogeneity. In addition, we evaluated the event rates in geriatric patients who newly initiated ADs and who were depressed, providing evidence to decision-making in specific populations.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, most included studies were observational, which introduces the possibility of residual confounding and bias. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed across several analyses, particularly for pooled event rates. Although subgroup, sensitivity, and meta-regression analyses were conducted, other potential sources of heterogeneity, including patient-level vulnerability factors and treatment-related characteristics, could not be fully explored because of limited reporting. Factors that may influence prescribing patterns, monitoring frequency, and the probability of detecting hyponatremia, including illness severity, hospitalization status, acute versus chronic illness, and functional impairment, were rarely reported. Therefore, pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously, particularly when substantial between-study variability exists. Third, data for some antidepressant classes and individual compounds were limited, which restricted more detailed subgroup analyses. Another important limitation is that treatment-related factors, including antidepressant dosage, duration of exposure, and changes in therapy over time, could not be systematically evaluated. The available studies did not consistently provide sufficient dose and temporal information for quantitative synthesis. Future studies should focus on prospective designs with standardized definitions of hyponatremia and more detailed characterization of patient populations. Studies examining dose-dependent effects and temporal patterns of risk would also be valuable for clinical decision-making.

Conclusions

In summary, this systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that antidepressant use in elderly populations is associated with an increased risk of hyponatremia, with clinically meaningful differences observed among antidepressant classes. SNRIs showed relatively higher risks, whereas mirtazapine demonstrated a comparatively favorable risk profile. However, these findings should be interpreted considering substantial between-study heterogeneity, potential residual confounding, and limited availability of detailed patient- and treatment-level characteristics. Future prospective studies with standardized outcome definitions and comprehensive assessment of individual risk factors are needed to further guide antidepressant selection and monitoring strategies in older adults.
