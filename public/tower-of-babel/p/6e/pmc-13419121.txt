Methods for Uncertainty Quantification in Dictionary Matching to Advance Reliability of Quantitative MRI
Authors: Brian Toner, Ute Goerke, Eze Ahanonu, Kevin Johnson, Vibhas Deshpande, Holden H. Wu, Maria I. Altbach, Ali Bilgin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims Purpose: Dictionary matching is a standard tool in quantitative MRI (qMRI), but typically lacks uncertainty quantification (UQ). This is critical when advanced reconstructions (e.g., compressed sensing, deep learning) introduce complex‐valued, spatially varying, and temporally correlated noise that violates standard assumptions of independent and identically distributed (iid) noise.MethodsTwo voxel‐wise uncertainty methods: a frequentist Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT) and a Bayesian marginal posterior approach, are introduced. Noise is modeled as spatially varying and temporally correlated using the covariance estimated from background regions. Methods were validated via simulations and phantom experiments using radial turbo spin‐echo (T2 mapping) and radial Look‐Locker (T1 mapping). In vivo experiments characterized uncertainty under varying acceleration factors.ResultsSimulations confirmed both methods achieve nominal coverage rates (e.g., 95% intervals containing the true value 95% of the time) where standard iid assumptions fail. Phantom results showed excellent agreement with gold‐standard spin‐echo references. In vivo experiments highlighted that higher acceleration factors widen uncertainty intervals for T1 and T2. The LRT method proved more computationally efficient than the Bayesian approach while providing comparable interval estimates.ConclusionA robust framework is presented for UQ in dictionary‐matched qMRI. By modeling the non‐iid noise inherent in modern reconstructions, these methods provide statistically interpretable UQ that assesses the reliability of parameter maps in clinical settings.

Introduction

Quantitative MRI (qMRI) is becoming a staple of modern MRI due to its ability to provide reliable, reproducible, and quantifiable measurements of important biomarkers within the body [1, 2, 3]. With quantitative biomarkers, it is imperative to understand the statistical properties of the measurements being made to characterize their accuracy and precision, defined by mean error and measurement standard deviation, respectively. Measurement noise in MRI originates as thermal noise in k‐space measurements, which is independent and identically distributed (iid) across spatial frequencies and timepoints [4, 5]. When reconstruction methods are unitary and linear (e.g., single‐coil, fully‐sampled inverse Fourier transform), noise remains standard imaging and reconstruction techniques like parallel imaging, non‐Cartesian trajectories, subspace constraints, compressed sensing (CS), and deep learning (DL) are often non‐linear and exploit the inherent temporal and spatial correlations of the underlying signal to reconstruct images from undersampled datasets. Images reconstructed with these methods display temporal correlations in their noise distributions, and therefore necessitate robust uncertainty quantification (UQ) [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

Relaxometry, the process of estimating T1 and T2, provides unique opportunities for increasing the reliability and reproducibility of diagnoses using MRI. T1 measurements are valuable in diagnosing heart [13, 14] and liver disease [15, 16, 17]. T2 measurements are used for musculoskeletal MRI applications [18, 19, 20] and are effective in characterizing focal liver lesions [21]. The small classification margin based on relaxometry parameters necessitates accurate and precise measurements.

Many methods have been proposed for T1 and T2 mapping, including the variable flip angle [22] and Look‐Locker techniques [23, 24, 25] for T1, radial turbo spin‐echo [26, 27, 28, 29, 30] and T2 prepared balanced steady‐state‐free precession [31] methods for T2, and magnetic resonance fingerprinting (MRF) [32] for both. These methods involve fitting reconstructed MRI signals to a simulated signal model. While fitting methods vary, dictionary matching is often chosen because the computationally expensive dictionary generation occurs only once, making matching efficient compared to other techniques [27, 28, 32].

Previous studies have proposed UQ methods for qMRI, but all have limitations. Polders et al. [33] proposed a Monte Carlo (MC) bootstrap method to quantify uncertainty in parameter estimation. Lin et al. [34] suggested a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approach to draw many samples of the posterior distribution. The MC bootstrap and MCMC methods are computationally expensive, making them infeasible for clinical settings. Metzner et al. [35] proposed a Bayesian framework for calculating uncertainty maps from MRF parameter maps through marginalization of the posterior distribution. A key limitation of all these methods is the assumption that the noise distribution is iid across timepoints for a given voxel. Because modern reconstruction techniques are often non‐linear, we show that this assumption is statistically invalid.

Our framework defines confidence intervals (CIs) and/or credible intervals (CrIs) and uses the interval size as a measure of uncertainty, which provides robust statistical guarantees with well‐defined interpretations [36]. We extend the Bayesian framework to accommodate more realistic noise distributions and propose an additional method utilizing the likelihood ratio test (LRT). A simulation study tests these methods' statistical guarantees, a phantom study verifies their accuracy when reference values are known, and an in vivo study demonstrates their practical utility.

Theory

Dictionary matching compares the measured signal evolution against a large dictionary of simulated signals, selecting the single best match. We expand this concept using frequentist and Bayesian statistical frameworks. Beyond point estimates, we evaluate the statistical likelihood of all dictionary atoms to build CIs/CrIs. Because advanced MRI reconstructions alter measurement noise structure, our theory explicitly models complex spatial and temporal noise correlations to ensure accurate uncertainty metrics.

We refer to the series of Nt coil‐combined images used for parameter mapping as contrast images, where Nt is the number of contrasts in the temporal dimension. The signal model for a single voxel r is given by (1) X(r)=s(r)μ(θ(r))+ϵ(r), where X(r)∈ℂNt×1 represents the reconstructed contrast image series at voxel r, s(r)∈ℂ is a spatially varying unknown scaling factor (absorbing physical constants such as proton density, receiver coil sensitivities, and arbitrary background phase), μ is a function μ:Θ→ℂNt×1 that maps parameters within the parameter space θ(r)∈Θ (such as T1, T2, etc.) to the unit sphere with the condition ||μ(θ(r))||2=1∀θ(r)∈Θ, and ϵ∼𝒞𝒩(0,∑(r)) is zero‐mean additive complex Gaussian noise. The model allows for a spatially varying scaling factor s(r) and noise variance ∑(r). We drop r for brevity, with the understanding that the model remains spatially varying. Subspace constrained reconstructions are common in modern MRI, where the temporal dimension is truncated from Nt contrasts to P coefficient maps, with P<Nt. This reconstruction has the relationship X′=UHX for some compression operator U∈ℂNt×P, where denotes the Hermitian transpose. The coefficient maps can be used in place of the contrast images, which we test later, but for simpler notation within this section, we use the contrast images X.

To fit X to a parameter θ, we first construct a dictionary of atoms μ(θi) that describe possible signal evolutions of a voxel across contrasts. We assume μ is specific to an experiment with imaging parameters known a priori, but is parameterized by unknown tissue parameters θi. Each atom is normalized to have ||μ(θi)||2=1, and the dictionary matching procedure selects the optimal atom based on the maximum magnitude of the inner products (2) θ^=argmaxθiXHμ(θi). The normalization ||μ(θi)||2=1∀i prevents bias towards atoms with larger l2 norms. While mathematically equivalent to minimizing the l2 distance for magnitude data, Equation (2) discards arbitrary background receiver phase, ensuring the matching process is robust to complex‐valued data. We utilize complex‐valued reconstructions X and a real‐valued signal model μ(θi).

The value X‖X‖2Hμ(θ^), which is bounded above by 1 by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, could be used as a goodness‐of‐fit metric, but lacks statistical rigor and interpretability. We propose alternative methods to characterize and visualize uncertainty in parameter estimation via dictionary matching. The approach defines a 1−α level CI or CrI, where α represents the pre‐specified error rate.

Estimation of Noise Covariance

Both methods require knowledge of the noise covariance. The primary source of measurement noise in MRI is thermal noise, which is modeled as iid complex Gaussian noise in k‐space [5]. Given a fully sampled Cartesian acquisition, the reconstructed image via an inverse discrete Fourier transform (iDFT) reconstruction for a single coil would lead to complex Gaussian noise in image space that is spatially and temporally iid. However, this transformation relies on the linear and unitary nature of the iDFT. Non‐Cartesian trajectories, undersampling, multi‐array coils, and advanced reconstruction techniques all contribute to invalidating the linear and/or unitary properties of the reconstruction operator, which in turn leads to noise in image space that is not spatially and temporally iid.

We model the noise in image space as a spatially varying, temporally correlated complex Gaussian distribution. To ensure tractability while being more realistic, we assume a separable covariance structure where, although the covariance matrix varies in magnitude spatially, both the heteroscedasticity along the diagonal and the temporal correlations on the off‐diagonal are proportional throughout the slice. That is, we assume (3) ∑(r)=σ2(r)∑0∀r, where σ2(r)∈(0,∞) and the unscaled covariance ∑0∈ℂNt×Nt is estimated empirically using a background region of interest (ROI) as (4) ∑0=1J−1∑j=1J(Xj−X‾B)(Xj−X‾B)H, where X‾B is the sample mean of the background ROI B with J voxels Xj∈B. ∑(r) varies spatially only through the scaling term σ2(r).

We note that the assumption of a separable covariance structure, where the temporal correlation shape (∑0) is fixed, and only the scale (σ2(r)) varies spatially, is a simplification. While the aforementioned advanced reconstruction techniques introduce spatial variations, non‐linear methods like CS and DL are also inherently signal‐dependent. Consequently, the effective noise propagation in tissue voxels differs from the spatially varying noise‐only behavior captured in the background ROI. Fully characterizing these complex, signal‐dependent covariance structures remains a highly challenging area of active research. Nevertheless, our proposed separable model serves as a tractable and highly practical compromise. As demonstrated later by our repeated measurement experiments (Sections 3.6 and 4.3), this model provides a significant improvement over the standard temporally iid assumptions used in previous literature, yielding reliable and specific uncertainty estimates in practice.

Likelihood Ratio Test with Profile Likelihoods

For a dictionary atom μ(θ), the Gaussian likelihood function conditioned on a single voxel is (5) L(θ,s,σ2|X)=exp−(X−sμ(θ))H(σ2∑0)−1(X−sμ(θ))πNt|σ2∑0|. This is the foundation for the proposed UQ methods, which we will explain concisely here and more rigorously in Supporting Information. Let (6) Q(θ,s|X)=(XH∑0−1X)−2ℜs∗μ(θ)H∑0−1X+|s|2(μ(θ)H∑0−1μ(θ)). Using the determinant property |σ2∑0|=(σ2)Nt|∑0|, the log likelihood is (7) log(L(θ,s,σ2|X))=−Ntlog(π)−Ntlog(σ2)−log(|∑0|)−1σ2Q(θ,s|X). θ represents the parameters of interest, and s and σ2 are considered nuisance parameters that make it complicated to use the likelihood function efficiently. One option is to optimize over nuisance parameters for a fixed set of parameters of interest. This formulation is called a profile likelihood [37, 38] and can be used in place of the true likelihood in many applications.

By optimizing over s and σ2, we get the final profile likelihood Lp, defined as (8) log(Lp(θ|X))=−NtlogXH∑0−1X−|μ(θ)H∑0−1X|2μ(θ)H∑0−1μ(θ)−Ntlog(π)+Ntlog(Nt)−log(|∑0|)−Nt, derived in Supporting Information Section S1. The LRT [36] can be used to obtain a 1−α level confidence region for θ using profile likelihood functions [37, 38]. Given the null and alternative hypotheses H0:θ=θ0, H1:θ≠θ0, the desired confidence region is defined Ω={θ0:H0not rejected at level1−α}. The likelihood ratio statistic is defined: (9) Λ(θ0)=Lp(θ0|X)Lp(θ^MLE|X), where θ^MLE is the maximum likelihood estimate. The LRT states that under regularity conditions, −2log(Λ(θ0)) asymptotically follows a χ2 distribution with degrees of freedom (df) equal to the dimension of θ [39]. In general, we have (10) Ω1−α=θ0:−2log(Λ(θ0))≤χdf,1−α2. To use the LRT to compute a 1−α level CI, we simply calculate Lp(θ|X) for each voxel and each atom in the dictionary. For a multi‐parametric signal model (θ∈ℝM, M>1), the LRT defines a multi‐dimensional confidence region. We can define a scalar interval for a single parameter of interest (e.g., T2) by projecting this multi‐dimensional region onto the specific parameter axis. In practice, this simply means the 95% confidence interval for T2 is defined by the minimum and maximum T2 values contained within the multi‐dimensional boundary.

Credible Intervals via Integration of the Marginal Posterior Distribution

Bayesian methods have previously been proposed to quantify uncertainty in parameter estimation via dictionary matching [35, 40]. These methods, applied to MRF, place non‐informative priors on the parameters of interest and use numerical integration to obtain marginal posterior distributions for each parameter individually. As previously discussed, these previous works used the noise model ∑(r)=σ2(r)I, where I is the identity matrix. We modify the formulation to generalize to our more realistic noise model.

First, we start with the same likelihood L(θ|X) from Equation (5). We use common non‐informative priors [41] for the nuisance parameters P(s)∝1, P(σ2)∝1σ2 and flat priors for the parameters of interest θ. By integrating out s and σ2, we obtain the marginal posterior (11) logP(θ|X)=−(Nt−1)logXH∑0−1X−|μ(θ)H∑0−1X|2μ(θ)H∑0−1μ(θ)−logμ(θ)H∑0−1μ(θ)+K, where K is constant. A detailed derivation can be found in the Supporting Information Section S2. This defines a marginal posterior for every atom within the dictionary, which allows the use of numerical integration to marginalize for just one of the parameters in the dictionary in the case of a multi‐parametric signal model.

Methods

Application to T1 and T2 Mapping

The radial turbo spin‐echo (radTSE) [42] and radial Look‐Locker (radLL) [24] sequences, which have implementations developed by our group and provided by Siemens as work‐in‐progress (WIP) T2‐mapping and T1‐mapping research sequences, are ideal sequences to test these methods, as they require advanced reconstruction techniques due to the inherent undersampling of contrast images, and utilize dictionary matching for T2 and T1 mapping [24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. In radTSE, k‐space data is grouped based on TE during acquisition, enabling reconstruction of contrast TE images, with the number of contrasts equal to the acquisition's echo train length (ETL). In radLL, radial data is acquired with varying inversion time (TI), enabling the grouping of temporally adjacent radial views during reconstruction to obtain contrast TI images. The number of radial views per TI and corresponding temporal resolution is a tunable reconstruction parameter. radTSE acquisitions are accelerated by acquiring fewer views per TE, and radLL acquisitions are accelerated by truncating the long TIs from the T1 recovery curve. TE or TI images can be reconstructed with similar reconstruction techniques, and both allow compression to a low‐rank temporal subspace.

For radTSE, θ=(B1+,T2), and we use the slice‐resolved extended phase graph (SEPG) [43] model for μ. For radLL, θ=(B1+,T1), and we use a Bloch simulation for our signal model μ. In both cases, B1+ refers to a factor that scales the nominal flip angle to achieve the true flip angle experienced by a given voxel due to a heterogeneous transmit field. B1+ is a nuisance parameter, so we are only interested in the T1 or T2 CIs/CrIs. When using the LRT on radTSE data, after calculating the 2D confidence region Ω1−α for each voxel and each θ, we can calculate ΩT2,1−α=[min{T2:(B1+,T2)∈Ω1−α},max{T2:(B1+,T2)∈Ω1−α}]. For the Bayesian method, we calculate a (1−α) level CrI ΩT2,α for T2 through numerical integration (12) 1−α=∫ΩT2,1−α∫ℝP(B1+,T2|X)dB1+dT2. This does not uniquely define a CrI ΩT2,1−α, so we choose to center the interval symmetrically around the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate. The same was done for T1 using radLL data.

Dictionary Generation

The SEPG [43] model for radTSE simulation has a combination of acquisition and tissue parameters as input. The acquisition parameters consist of flip angle, echo spacing, echo train length, and the simulated slice profiles of the excitation and refocusing pulses. The tissue parameters consist of T1 and T2 relaxation times and the B1+calibration factor. Previous studies [29, 44] have shown that the T2‐weighted signal in radTSE is not sensitive to T1 due to a relatively long repetition time (TR), and that using a T1 of 1000ms for all dictionary atoms does not significantly affect T2 quantification accuracy. Previous work demonstrates that refocusing pulse trains in multi‐echo spin‐echo sequences (like radTSE) can induce incidental magnetization transfer (MT) effects, which can lead to the underestimation of T2 values [45]. While acknowledging this potential bias, we omit explicit MT modeling, keeping dictionary matching constrained to a single‐pool model. The radTSE dictionary used in this study has T1=1000ms for all atoms. The dictionary has T2∈[5,300]ms, following a concatenated grid of NT2={231,44,25} points linearly spaced over the intervals [5,120], [121,200], and [201,300], respectively. This offers finer resolution for shorter T2 values, as these are more often observed in tissues of interest. The radLL dictionary was generated using a Bloch simulation, which utilizes acquisition parameters, T1, and B1+ as parameters. The dictionary uses T1∈{1,2,…,3000}ms. The precision of the T1 and T2 measurements, as well as their corresponding uncertainty estimates, is fundamentally constrained by the discretization of the dictionary. Because the parameter estimates and the bounds of their uncertainty intervals are limited to dictionary values, the resolution directly impacts the granularity of these metrics. Consequently, the finer grid resolution at shorter T2 values enables tighter, more precise uncertainty bounds, whereas the coarser resolution at longer T2 values results in wider, more coarsely quantized uncertainty estimates. Thus, the dictionary grid should be tailored to the specific tissue relaxation times of interest. For the numerical simulation only, we used a modified, expanded dictionary consisting of T2∈{5,5.125,5.25,…,300} to provide more precise estimates throughout the T2 range. The total number of atoms also affects computational efficiency, which is discussed in greater detail in Section 4.5.

Both dictionaries use B1+∈[0.4,1.2], which was selected based on observations from a previous study [46] that reported B1+ in the abdomen at different field strengths. Both sequences used linearly spaced B1+ samples within that range, with 40 samples in the T2 dictionary and 9 samples in the T1 dictionary. However, B1+ can be measured by a separate sequence [47], and we evaluated using these measurements to constrain the dictionary to atoms with B1+ values close to the measured value.

Singular value decomposition (SVD) on either signal dictionary defines a low‐dimensional subspace by truncating to just the first few principal components (PCs). For the dictionaries used in this study, the first four PCs account for greater than 95% of explained variance. This allows subspace‐constrained reconstructions, which are very effective in both image reconstruction and parameter mapping [26, 27, 44, 48].

Truncation of Contrasts

The signal from a radTSE sequence decays with increased TE. After a certain point, signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) is too low to reliably contribute to parameter estimation. This can be especially problematic in short T2 voxels where signal decays rapidly. When operating in PC space, each PC encodes information from all TEs, which mitigates this problem. In TE space, we employ a two‐step regime: an initial fit determines T2, after which TEs beyond 3×T2 are truncated for final point and uncertainty estimation. The signal from a radLL sequence recovers with increased TI, so we do not have an issue with a lack of SNR for later timepoints. However, acquisitions can be accelerated by sampling a shorter curve [25]. To simulate this acceleration retrospectively, we truncate the contrasts in k‐space before reconstruction.

Numerical Simulation

The uncertainty methods were first tested in simulation using radTSE. This provides ground truth parameter values that cannot be obtained in phantom or in vivo imaging. Noise‐free curves were generated following the SEPG model, and then different realizations of complex Gaussian noise were added to the signal. 95% CIs/CrIs (α=0.05) were calculated for each noisy signal, and we calculated the CI/CrI size and the frequency with which the CI/CrI covered the true T2 value. The added noise followed the noise distribution estimated from a phantom scan in Section 3.5 and scaled to produce a noisy curve with a SNR of 15 dB.

We tested the methods in both the full TE space with 32 timepoints and the compressed PC space with just 4 principal components. Each noise‐free signal was generated with a B1+ that was selected randomly following a uniform distribution in the range [0.9,1.1]. Distinct intervals were generated using the full dictionary with B1+ values defined on [0.4,1.2] and a constrained dictionary only with the atoms with B1+ values defined on [0.9,1.1]. This evaluated how restricting B1+ uncertainty via a separate map impacts T2 estimation.

Phantom Experiment

Ten phantoms were prepared with different concentrations of agar and NiCl2 to create a range of T1 and T2 values. All phantom data were acquired on a 3T scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens‐Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) with a 32‐channel head coil. These phantoms were first imaged with a series of 2D single‐echo spin‐echo (SESE) and 2D spin‐echo inversion recovery (SEIR) sequences to determine reference T2 and T1. They were also imaged with a B1+‐mapping sequence, four radTSE scans, and two radLL scans.

All sequences were acquired with a 200×200mm2 field of view (FoV) and a voxel size of 1.5625×1.5625×6mm3. Uniform parameters facilitate voxel‐wise registration across reconstructions. 17 SESE scans were acquired with a TR=5000ms and a variable TE∈{10,20,…,90}∪{110,130,…,250}ms to create a series of T2‐weighted contrast images. T2 values were obtained by fitting each voxel to the signal model |S(TE)|=ρexp−TET2, where ρ represents proton density, using a non‐linear least squares fit. 11 SEIR scans were acquired with TR=5000ms, TE=10ms, and a variable TI∈{40,90,150,300,500,750,1000,1500,2000,3000,4000}ms to create a series of T1‐weighted contrast images. Reference T1 values were obtained by fitting each voxel to the signal model |S(TI)|=ρ−βexp−TIT1, where β accounts for inversion efficiency.

A B1+ map of the phantoms was also acquired using a preconditioning radio‐frequency pulse with a turboFLASH readout [47] (TE=1.88ms, TR=10s, and flip angle=8∘). The radTSE scans were acquired with ETL=32, echo spacing=7.16ms, refocusing flip angle=180∘, receiver bandwidth (RBW)=494Hz/Px, and 256 readout points per radial view. The scans were acquired with 8192, 384, 192, and 96 radial views, which corresponded to acquisition times (TAs) of 21:27 min, 1:07 min, 37 s, and 20 s, respectively.

The radLL scans were acquired with 800 radial views (to sample the T1 recovery curve), TE=1.75ms, TR=3.3ms, flip angle=10∘, RBW=700Hz/Px, and 256 readout points per radial view. The first was a standard single‐shot acquisition per slice (TA=5s), while the second was a modified multi‐shot sequence (TA=3:23min) that used 16 sequential shots with rotated radial views to provide 16× radial measurements for a single slice. In reconstruction, we group the radial views from the standard acquisition to give 16 views per TI, resulting in 50 contrasts. In the repeated measurement acquisition, this same temporal resolution gives 256 views for each of the 50 TI contrasts.

The 8192‐view radTSE and multi‐measurement radLL data were reconstructed with a non‐uniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT) [49, 50]. To reduce undersampling artifacts, we utilize subspace filtering (SF) reconstruction, where we project the contrast images to the temporal subspace described in Section 3.2. These PC images can be fit directly to a T1/T2 map or can be projected back to TI/TE space. The accelerated scans were reconstructed with both SF and a locally low rank (LLR) CS reconstruction [26].

A 95% CI/CrI (α=0.05) was calculated for each voxel of each radTSE and radLL reconstruction with both the LRT and Bayesian methods. In the case of radTSE, separate CIs/CrIs were generated both with and without the use of the separately acquired B1+ map. During fitting, we constrain the dictionary to all atoms corresponding to B1+ values within 10% of the measured value. In the case of radLL, separate CIs/CrIs were also generated with truncation of the original 50 TIs to 30, 10, or 5 TIs to simulate acceleration.

Fixed volume ROIs were placed in each phantom, and the mean T1 or T2 value and average CI limits across voxels within the ROI were recorded. These point estimates and CIs/CrIs were then compared to the reference values from the SEIR T1 map or the SESE T2 map. The CIs/CrIs were used to produce uncertainty maps by displaying the size of each 95% CI/CrI within the image.

Noise Distribution

Our underlying assumption is that it is possible to estimate the structure of the covariance matrix from a background region of a single slice, while allowing it to vary throughout the slice only through a spatially varying unknown scaling factor. This is a significant increase in model complexity to more accurately characterize noise compared to previous studies that assume iid noise across timepoints for each voxel [33, 34, 35]. To better understand the issues of the iid noise assumption, the numerical simulation and radTSE phantom experiments described before were repeated using the simplified noise model employed by previous studies.

To empirically validate the proposed noise model, we conducted a prospective repeated measurement experiment. Using the phantom protocol described in Section 3.5, a 192‐view radTSE dataset was acquired 100 times sequentially. Each scan was reconstructed with LLR and fit to a T2 map. An empirical T2 uncertainty map was generated by calculating the range between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles across the 100 measurements to isolate the variance driven by thermal noise. Additionally, we conducted a retrospective measurement experiment using the 8192‐view dataset, retrospectively undersampled using 41 unique trajectories. This allowed us to capture the effective noise, which combines thermal processes with pseudo‐random undersampling artifacts. Finally, these datasets were used to extract and compare the empirical covariance matrices from both background and signal regions.

In Vivo Experiment

Nine healthy volunteers (7 M, 2 F; aged 21–66 years), who consented according to the University of Arizona's IRB, were imaged to evaluate our method in vivo. Of the nine volunteers, six were imaged with a brain protocol, and six were imaged with an abdominal protocol. All data were acquired on the same 3T scanner from the phantom experiment. The brain protocol and results can be found in Supporting Information: Section S4. The abdominal radTSE scans were acquired with 5 slices, ETL=32, refocusing flip angles=150∘, FoV=400×400mm2, slice thickness=6mm, RBW=501Hz/Px, 512 readout points per radial view, and echo spacing=8.0ms. These scans were respiratory‐triggered, with a TR of one respiratory cycle, so the TA varied by subject. An additional 96‐view scan was acquired for one subject to demonstrate the failure mode when there is insufficient data for reliable T2 mapping. Before image reconstruction, data preprocessing consisted of SVD coil compression to 6 virtual coils, and abdominal data underwent streak removal [51].

The single‐shot (1‐measurement) radLL scan acquired five slices, and the abdominal data were acquired as a breath‐hold (TA=24s). The multi‐shot (16‐measurement) scan acquired just the center slice of the 1‐measurement scan and was respiratory triggered (TA varied by subject). The radLL scans were acquired with flip angle =10∘ and RBW=700Hz/Px, FoV=400×400mm2, slice thickness=6mm, 384 readout points per radial view, TR=3.1ms and TE=1.63ms.

The 8192‐view radTSE data and 16‐measurement radLL data were both reconstructed with an NUFFT reconstruction for each contrast image. The 1‐measurement radLL scan and the accelerated radTSE scans were reconstructed with the LLR method [26]. The accelerated radTSE data were also reconstructed with a DL method designed for abdominal T2 mapping [30]. After all contrast images were reconstructed, T1 and T2 fitting was performed via dictionary matching, and 95% CIs/CrIs were calculated with the LRT and Bayesian methods. For radTSE, this was done both with and without the B1+ map restricting the dictionary during fitting. For radLL, this was done with all 50 contrasts as well as retrospectively truncated to 30, 10, and 5 contrasts.

Fixed volume ROIs were placed on anatomical images from each acquisition. ROIs were placed in the liver, each kidney, spleen, and paraspinal muscle for each slice where the organ appeared. The mean T2 value and CI/CrI limits were recorded for each ROI. Due to long scan times, the SEIR and SESE references could not be acquired for in vivo subjects, so the 8192‐view radTSE served as a reference T2 to which the accelerated radTSE scans could be compared, and the 16‐measurement radLL served as a reference T1.

Results

Numerical Simulation

Figure 1A shows noise‐free and noisy signal examples from the simulation experiment. The generated 95% CIs/CrIs for each curve are shown in Figure 1B. Nearly all methods cover the ground truth 95% of the time. The only exception is the LRT method when calculated on data within the temporal subspace. This is because the LRT states that the test statistic asymptotically approaches a χ2 distribution. The full TE space utilizes 32 timepoints, which appears sufficient for this assumption to hold. However, the PC subspace utilizes only 4 timepoints, which appears to be insufficient, although it still produces coverage frequencies above 90%.

(A) Examples of a noise‐free and noisy signal in TE and PC space for T2=50ms, B1+=1.0. (B) Coverage percentage and (C) CI/CrI size for curves with simulated 15 dB added noise. All curves were simulated with B1+∈[0.9,1.1] and varying T2 values (horizontal axis). For each value of T2, 1000 unique noisy signal curves were generated. CI/CrI size increases with T2 until it approaches the upper bound of T2 values included in the dictionary, showing the importance of including all feasible values of interest in the dictionary.

Figure 1C plots the mean interval size from the simulation experiment for each T2 value. The general trend is that the interval size increases with increased T2 until it approaches the limit of the finite dictionary, showing the importance of designing a dictionary with all feasible T2 values. Other trends include the fact that both methods provide smaller intervals in TE space than in PC space, which is due to the increased number of temporal samples. In addition, constraining B1+ with prior knowledge unsurprisingly reduces interval size, providing evidence of the benefits of acquiring the B1+ map.

Noise Distribution

Figure 4 displays the results of the simulation and phantom experiments when applying the simplified noise model (∑0=I) used by previous studies. As expected, assuming temporally iid noise on data with correlated noise degrades performance. In simulations, coverage frequency drops well below 95% (Figure 4A), and in the radTSE phantom data, the resulting CIs/CrIs either become excessively wide (lacking specificity) or fail to contain the reference value (lacking accuracy). This highlights the necessity of our proposed, more realistic noise model.

The prospective repeated measurement experiment (Figure 5A) reveals that the empirical uncertainty driven purely by thermal measurement noise is substantially smaller than the uncertainty estimated by our models. However, the retrospective measurement experiment, which captures the variance caused by pseudo‐random undersampling artifacts across varying trajectories, shows that these artifacts dominate the overall variance. When capturing this effective noise (thermal noise plus aliasing artifacts), our estimated uncertainty maps are consistent with the empirical maps across all phantoms (Figure 5B). This agreement is also observed in vivo using highly non‐linear LLR and DL reconstruction techniques (Supporting Information: Figure S1). Furthermore, these empirical datasets allowed us to validate the separable noise model. Supporting Information: Figure S2 shows that the correlation structure (∑0) estimated in the background region shares a similar underlying shape to that of the true signal region. While they differ in absolute scale due to the signal‐dependence of the reconstructions, this difference is successfully absorbed by our model's spatially varying scalar, σ(r). Ultimately, both matrices are starkly different from an identity matrix, highlighting the invalidity of the temporally iid assumption.

In Vivo Experiment

Our methods translate effectively to both T1 and T2 in vivo estimation (Figures 6 and 7). The point estimates of the accelerated reconstructions correlate well with their well‐sampled counterparts, and the reference and test 95% CIs/CrIs consistently intersect. The asymmetry around the point estimate reflects the difference between ordinary and generalized least squares solutions. The abdominal uncertainty maps (Figures 8 and 9) corroborate our phantom findings: parameter uncertainty scales predictably with acceleration and, for T2, is mitigated by incorporating the B1+ map. Viewing these uncertainty maps side‐by‐side with parameter maps informs spatial confidence. Regions with high SNR and minimal motion (e.g., liver, kidneys) exhibit low uncertainty, whereas long T2 regions, edges, and motion‐corrupted areas (e.g., the gastrointestinal tract) display distinctly higher uncertainty for both T1 and T2 mapping. Corresponding results for brain data can be found in Supporting Information: Section S4.

Computational Efficiency

Clinical utility requires efficiency, and runtime depends on the condition number of the covariance matrix, number of voxels (Nv), number of timepoints (Nt), and number of dictionary atoms (Na). We achieved efficiency by vectorizing operations across voxels and atoms. The core computations for obtaining point estimates, LRT CIs, and computing the marginal posteriors for the Bayesian method are all O(Nv·Nt·Na). Because the marginal posteriors do not uniquely define a CrI, we have an additional step of defining the CrI symmetrically around the MAP estimate, for which our algorithm has complexity O(Nv·Nq2), where Nq is the number of T1 or T2 values in the dictionary for each value of B1+.

We evaluated the efficiency of the LRT and Bayesian methods on the same radTSE data and dictionary to control for these factors. Computations were performed using a serial implementation (see data availability statement for code) on an Intel Xeon E5‐2699A v4 CPU with 512 GB of RAM but could be significantly accelerated using parallel computing and/or GPU processing. On a 256×256 single slice with 32 timepoints and a dictionary with 300 T2 values and 40 B1+ values, a simple point estimate takes ≈0.84 s and ≈0.12 s without and with the B1+ map, respectively. The LRT method takes ≈10.04 s and ≈0.37 s, respectively. The Bayesian method takes ≈40.45 s and ≈26.13 s, respectively. These experiments verify that the LRT method is more efficient than the Bayesian method.

Discussion

We have presented two methods for providing uncertainty estimates in parameter mapping through dictionary matching. The methods utilize a novel noise model with a covariance that is spatially consistent in correlations but varies spatially in scale. Both methods have the potential to provide important insights when relying on qMRI to make clinical decisions.

We evaluated these methods in both the full temporal TE space and the compressed PC subspace. Ideally, the full TE space is preferred because larger sample sizes support robust estimates and the LRT method's asymptotic assumptions. However, the PC subspace yields smaller, better‐conditioned covariance matrices for inversion. Projecting PCs back to the full TE space is discouraged, as the data remains in a lower‐dimensional subspace, rendering the full covariance matrix singular. Consequently, the number of timepoints is limited to the subspace dimension used in reconstruction.

Future work will utilize these methods to improve downstream analysis tasks. Uncertainty estimates can provide insights into the selection of reconstruction parameters. For example, when reconstructing radLL data, we typically group TIs with 16 views/TI. This grouping parameter balances the sparsity of the individual TI datasets and the overall temporal resolution, both of which contribute to uncertainty of parameter estimation. Varying this parameter and calculating the corresponding uncertainty maps provides a probe to determine the ideal balance between the two sources of uncertainty.

These methods enhance downstream classification by quantifying decision confidence. For example, Supporting Information: Figure S4 shows distinct white and gray matter T1 values with full recovery curves (non‐intersecting 95% LRT CIs), whereas 10‐TI truncation yields intersecting CIs/CrIs, complicating differentiation. This capability is crucial for distinguishing classes with fine margins, such as malignant versus benign liver lesions [21], directly translating parameter uncertainty into classification confidence.

Reliable noise characterization is essential. While our model captures temporal correlations and heteroscedasticity better than iid assumptions, it restricts spatial covariance variation to a scalar. This is a current limitation of the approach, and relaxing the uniform correlation structure assumption could further enhance robustness.

This study focused on T1 and T2 estimation via the radLL and radTSE sequences. These methods can be generalized to various dictionary matching applications that are ubiquitous in qMRI, including MRF [32, 35], other T1 or T2 signal models [28, 52], proton density fat fraction quantification [53], and multicomponent fitting [54, 55]. Larger dictionaries for more complex signal models will lead to longer processing times, but these methods could be accelerated significantly through parallelization and GPU processing, which was not necessary for radTSE and radLL data.

Conclusion

This study presents two methods for producing robust and statistically interpretable uncertainty estimates for parameter estimation by dictionary matching. The LRT is a frequentist tool that can be used to calculate CIs, while the Bayesian method provides the ability to calculate CrIs. Both methods were validated in numerical simulation and phantom experiments. They were then evaluated using in vivo data in both brain and abdomen images for both T1 and T2 quantification. These methods robustly quantify uncertainty in dictionary matching, offering a pathway to clinically interpretable quality control for advanced qMRI reconstructions.
