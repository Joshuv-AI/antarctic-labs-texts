Comparison of growth, time series, and hybrid models for HIV infectious disease incidence in adults in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Muhammad Daniyal, Nadia Shabnam, Hanan Alyami, Ali Jaber Alqahtani, Madiha Shakeel, Mohammed Sharaf Alabdullah, Batool Mohammed Alhassan, Abbas Al Mutair, Hibah Abdulrahim Bahri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimHIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) is a virus that causes the immune system to be damaged, which results in reducing the body’s ability to defend against infections and illnesses. This study aims to model new HIV cases among adults in KSA using selected growth, time series, and hybrid models.MethodsThe study utilized official data on new HIV/AIDS cases among adults (15–49) from the World Bank website for the period 1990–2024. Various growth, conventional time series and hybrid models were proposed and used to model the series. The accuracy measures like RMSE, MAE, MAPE, MASE, and SMAPE were used to compare models, and the DM test was applied to assess the consistency between model predictions. Results were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05 and all the analyses and visualizations were performed in R-Studio.ResultsThe time-series data of newly infected HIV cases among adults (ages 15–49) from 1990 to 2024 showed the mean number of cases was approximately 502 ± 323. Among the hybrid frameworks evaluated, Exponential-ETS emerged as the superior model, attaining the lowest RMSE value of 91.500, outperforming all competing specifications. The forecast for 2025 using the hybrid model projects new cases at 1,553.91 (95% CI: 1,272.23–1,828.94), rising to 2,157.74 (95% CI: 1,497.40–2,866.45) by 2029. However, the projected annual increment is lower than the surge observed in 2019–2024.ConclusionThis study concluded that the hybrid model provides the most robust forecasting framework for HIV incidence in KSA. The 5-year projections indicated a continued upward trend, but with a decelerated rate of growth compared to the sharp surge observed in 2019–2024. This deceleration suggests progress towards Vision 2030 goals of reducing the burden of infectious diseases through strengthened health infrastructure, expanded screening programs, and improved public health awareness.

Introduction

HIV remains one of the most serious public health challenges today. Since the first case was reported, it has spread to almost every country in the world and is a severe infectious disease [1, 2]. Both HIV carriers and AIDS patients can transmit the virus to others through bodily fluids [3]. The first case of AIDS in KSA was reported in 1984 [4]. By the end of 2009, 10,217 new HIV cases had been reported to the Ministry of Health (MoH), of which 2,956 were Saudi nationals and 7,261 were non-Saudis [5]. Although these numbers are below the threshold according to international standards, they almost certainly underestimate the true prevalence [4, 6]. Currently, HIV in the Middle East and North Africa has been growing, with new infections rising every year at a rate that makes this region one of only two worldwide where the epidemic is still accelerating [7]. Over the past decades, a rich methodological framework has been developed, spanning classical time series frameworks such as Box-Jenkins methodology, growth models, and ML and hybrid architectures [9–11]. Over the past decade, annual HIV infections have been increasing across different regions. More recently, hybrid combinations and ML models of time series, have been applied and have sometimes performed better than other models in prediction and forecasting [12, 13]. Sato [16] provided a detailed demonstration of disease management using ARIMA in a time series context, highlighting the model’s suitability for health incidence data characterized by non-stationarity and autocorrelation. A study by [17] subsequently applied ARIMA to influenza positivity rates among children in Wuhan, China. For HIV and AIDS specifically, a study [18] conducted a landmark epidemiological and time series analysis of AIDS and HIV incidence and mortality in China. They used optimal ARIMA models identified through AIC/BIC minimization, specifically for AIDS incidence. Another study [19] deployed the ARIMA model to forecast HIV incidence in the Kumba Health District of Cameroon, demonstrating the transferability of Box-Jenkins methods to sub-Saharan Africa [19]. The ARIMA methodology has similarly been applied to HIV case analysis and short-term forecasting of incident HIV infections [20–25]. Some studies compared ARIMA, Exponential Smoothing (ES), and other advanced models while modeling infectious diseases [26–29]. The most comprehensive recent comparison of individual and combined models in the HIV/AIDS context was conducted using 170 months of Chinese national surveillance data [30]. A systematic review of advanced models applications confirmed that deep learning approaches performed better than classical statistical methods in terms of predictive accuracy when sample size is large [31]. Another study applied deep learning framework comparing RNN, LSTM, and GRU for time series HIV infectious forecasting purposes [32]. Growth models represents different methodological approach to epidemic forecasting compared to the above. Historical outbreaks spanning diverse transmission routes the GGM revealed that sub-exponential growth is the norm rather than the exception [14]. Chowell et al. [15] provided a comprehensive review of mathematical models used to characterize early epidemic growth and documented the theoretical foundations and empirical performance of exponential, generalized growth, logistic, generalized logistic Richards and Gompertz specifications. Li and Li [33] compared different advanced and ARIMA model for predicting AIDS incidence in China and found notable differences in performance. Wu et al. [34] applied the BP neural network to STD/AIDS prevalence data as early as 2007 while another study [35] deployed a modelling approach to predict HIV/AIDS prevalence which provided one of the earliest ANN applications in this context. Another study of HIV forecasting in the Philippines used a Multilayer Perceptron Neural Network to forecast monthly incidence during the COVID period [36]. A comparative study in China evaluated ARIMA and hybrid specifications against monthly AIDS incidence [37]. Hybrid models have also been tested specifically in the HIV context by [29]. Regarding growth models, a study by [42] systematically analyzed six growth models, which were GGM, GLM, the Richards model, GRM the Gompertz model, and a modified SEIR framework. Another study then combined these developments and provided accessible implementations of GGM, GLM, Richards and Gompertz models alongside bootstrap-based uncertainty quantification [40]. A study by [41] applied the GGM specifically to HIV epidemics in Brazil, examining the influence of socio-demographic factors. The GGM has been applied to the HIV/AIDS epidemic and various Ebola outbreaks in West Africa [14], consistently demonstrating its versatility as a cross-pathogen analytical tool. Epidemiological research specific to HIV/AIDS in KSA and the broader MENA region remained sparse relative to the volume of work produced for sub-Saharan African and Asian settings.

A study published the first comprehensive characterization of the HIV/AIDS epidemic in Saudi Arabia [4]. Mazroa et al. [5] updated this record with a ten-year retrospective review of annual HIV case notification returns to the MoH from 2000 to 2009. While the study by [6] provided a contemporary review of HIV in KSA framed around co-infections, published in Infection and Drug Resistance in 2021. They further reviewed current and future challenges facing the HIV epidemic in KSA [43]. While the literature on HIV modelling is extensive globally, there is still a lack of quantitative modelling work specific to KSA. The available studies from the region are predominantly descriptive epidemiological analyses, and few studies have applied time series or hybrid methods to the KSA HIV surveillance record. The existing literature on KSA is almost exclusively descriptive and retrospective, focusing on prevalence rates, case notifications, and demographic profiles of expatriates versus Saudis. This study will apply a rigorous, multi-family modelling framework to the KSA surveillance record. Another major scientific contribution lies in demonstrating that phenomenological growth models combined with other time series models for modelling and forecasting purposes.

The present study addresses twofold gaps by applying conventional growth time series and their hybrid models using complete available KSA HIV data for adults. The aim is to identify the best model, projecting future trends and align the outcomes with healthcare initiative regarding reducing HIV cases.

Materials and methods

Data

The study utilized annual data on adults (ages 15–49) newly infected with HIV during 1990–2024, and it was obtained from the World Bank website (https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SH.HIV.INCD?locations=SA), which is freely available, and there was no missing data observed.

Ethical approval

This study used publicly available, anonymized secondary data obtained from the World Bank database and did not involve human participants, human biological materials, or identifiable personal data. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethical approval committee, Institutional Review Board, Almoosa Health Group.

Modelling techniques

This section briefly explains growth, time series, hybrid models that combine linear and non-linear algorithms. The choice of both traditional along with their hybrid combinations, is motivated by their dual-nature characteristics.

Growth models: equations & parameters

Growth models characterize the epidemic trajectory using closed-form equations fitted by Nonlinear Least Squares (NLS). Each model differs in how it handles saturation and asymmetry. Due to the non-monotonic nature of the HIV incidence series, the growth models struggled to achieve optimal convergence. The parameters reported represent the best-fitting solutions obtained under the NLS framework.

Hybrid time series models

The hybrid models in which traditional models are combined by using the approach proposed by Zhang [48]. These models are developed through a two-step process that effectively captures both linear and nonlinear patterns. First, the linear part is fitted and modeled, and the corresponding forecast is obtained. The residuals from the linear model contain only the nonlinear relationship. By modeling the residuals using the non-linear models, the non-linear relationship can be explored. The same approach was applied to combine the linear with other nonlinear time series approaches.

The proposed hybrid model integrates parametric nonlinear growth functions (e.g., exponential, logistic, Gompertz, and Richards) with ETS-based error correction. The growth component captures the underlying deterministic trend, while the ETS component models stochastic deviations through error-trend smoothing.

Model validation and robustness

The validation and robustness of forecasting ability were evaluated by rolling-origin cross-validation. Rolling origin method maintains the sequential framework by training a model on past data and testing it on future data across all validation folds to evaluate robustness. In this study, an initial training window was set, and one-step-ahead forecasts were made on the remaining series. It can be applied using the steps below; Let the time series be {y1, y2, …., yn}.

In cross-validation procedure, an initial subset of k observations is used as the training set:

1. Traint = {y1, y2, …, yk}.

The next observation is treated as the test point:

2. Testt = {yk+1}.

The forecasting method is trained on Trainₜ to predict the next value ŷₖ₊₁. The squared error for this step is then computed:

3. SEk+1 = (yk+1− ŷk+1)2.

The training window is then expanded by one observation, and the procedure will be repeated sequentially until forecasts have been generated for all remaining points in the series. Finally, the RMSE is calculated across all iterations to provide an overall measure of predictive accuracy.

Results

The time-series data of newly infected HIV cases among adults (ages 15–49) from 1990 to 2024 showed the mean number of cases was approximately 502 ± 323, with major variability in annual infections. The highest number of cases was observed in 2024 (1,400 cases), while a sharp rise beginning around 2019 and accelerating through 2023 (1,300 cases).

The lowest number of cases (100 cases) occurred repeatedly during the period from 2005 to 2010, representing a sustained low phase in the trend. Earlier years, particularly between 1994 and 1997, also showed relatively high values (around 700–750 cases), before declining toward 2000. After a prolonged low plateau in the mid-2000s, the trend began to increase again from 2014 onward, eventually reaching its peak in recent years (Fig. 2).

To formally assess structural instability, we applied the Bai-Perron test for multiple unknown breakpoints using the strucchange() package in R. The supF test strongly rejected the null hypothesis of no structural break (supF = 133.66, p < 2.2e-16). The optimal number of breaks, selected by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), was five, occurring in the years 1995, 2002, 2007, 2013, and 2018. These breakpoints correspond to the major turning points in the series: a decline in the late 1990s, a prolonged trough in the 2000s, and the sharp resurgence from 2014 onward (Fig. 3). To estimate the parameters, time was defined as t=Year − 1990, setting the first observed year as the baseline. All parameters were estimated directly from the observed HIV incidence series through Nonlinear Least Squares (NLS) using the robust Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm (minpack.lm()). The initial starting values were logically derived from the empirical data characteristics: the initial value (a∼500), the long-term growth scaling (derived from ln(1400/500)/34∼0.03), and the steepest recent growth rate of approximately 168 cases per year observed between 2019 and 2024, which specifically informed the S-curve parameters ( K, r, and t0) to ensure the inflection point aligned with the actual resurgence period. Additionally, wide but biologically sensible bounds were imposed on parameters (e.g., bounding K between 500 and 5000) to prevent numerical instability while allowing the data to determine the optimal fit (Fig. 4).

Residual analyses revealed consistent patterns: exponential and power residuals displayed a U-shaped trend, indicating overestimation of troughs and underestimation of peaks. (Fig. 5). The stationarity of the HIV incidence series was evaluated using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) and Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin (KPSS) tests. The ADF test on the original series indicated non-stationarity (test statistic = -0.3250, p = 0.9838), with the KPSS test (under the trend-stationarity assumption) also rejecting trend-stationarity (test statistic = 0.2198, p = 0.01). After first differencing, the ADF test continued to indicate non-stationarity (test statistic = -2.4077, p = 0.4150), whereas the KPSS test approached the significance threshold (test statistic = 0.4454, p = 0.0576). Notably, second differencing yielded an ADF test statistic of -3.7488 with a p-value of 0.0369, thereby rejecting the null hypothesis of non-stationarity and confirming stationarity at the 5% significance level; the KPSS test on the second difference also supported this conclusion (test statistic = 0.0663, p = 0.1). Despite the second differencing achieving stationarity, the series exhibits evident structural breaks, and the first-difference results remained contradictory between the two tests. Therefore, to ensure a robust and conservative model selection, we evaluated candidate time-series models with both d = 1 and d = 2 differencing orders (Fig. 6).

Cross-validation

The robustness and out-of-sample predictive performance of all competing models, was evaluated by a rolling-origin cross-validation procedure. An initial training window of k = 30 years (corresponding to data from 1990 to 2009) was established. For each subsequent fold, the training window was expanded by one year, and a one-step-ahead forecast was generated for the following year, continuing until the end of the series in 2024. The Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) was calculated across all 15 validation folds to compare the predictive accuracy. Rolling-origin cross-validation confirmed Exponential-ETS as the best-performing model with the lowest RMSE (101.219). A second-best group comprised the ARIMA-based hybrids (Logistic, Gompertz, Exponential, and Power), with RMSEs ranging narrowly from 105.600 to 106.800. Conversely, the NPAR-based hybrids displayed the poorest predictive capacity (RMSE > 135) (Table 6).

Discussion

This study compared growth models, conventional time series and hybridization modelling of the HIV/AIDS series for adults aged 15–49 in KSA. The findings provided important methodological and epidemiological understandings that are relevant for HIV forecasting in KSA. The study concluded that hybrid models performed better than individual time series and growth models while handling the HIV time series data.

This study noted that none individual growth models did not perform well in capturing the non-monotonic behavior of the HIV time series which are consistent with the findings of [15], which documented that models relying solely on exponential or sigmoid growth assumptions have systematically overestimated or underestimated epidemic trajectories in past outbreaks, including HIV/AIDS in the U.S. Our findings regarding growth models also align with the study [39] which highlighted that the reliability of phenomenological growth models depends on model structure and parameter identifiability. The limitations of alone growth models observed in our study were also reported by [14, 15], who applied the GGM to HIV epidemics and concluded that sub-exponential growth is more common than exponential growth. By using the growth models, it was noted in the study that the trend in HIV infections over the study period was notably non-monotonic. National surveillance data spanning 18 years showed that non-Saudi expatriates made up the majority of reported cases [45], while earlier cohort analyses reported 6,046 cumulative cases between 1984 and 2001, with non-Saudis accounting for 78.7% of infections [46]. A longitudinal cohort study at KFSHRC (1989–2010) further documented an increasing trend in HIV infections as compared to developing countries [44].

The KSA HIV series, marked by structural breaks and non-monotonic variation, exhibited a more complex trajectory than what these models were initially designed to handle. While growth models are still useful for characterizing the early phase of an epidemic and for initial uncertainty assessment [40], their usefulness for long-term prediction in diverse transmission settings seems quite limited. The cumulative case notification record indicated 10,217 newly reported HIV cases between 2000 and 2009 [4]. Study [43] reviewed the evolving burden and challenges of HIV in Saudi Arabia. The sharp rise in cases after 2019 may partly reflects improved surveillance and the progressive expansion of screening programs since 2008 to include HIV, HBV, and HCV testing [47].

Our findings related to the ARIMA were consistent with findings of [18], which reported that improperly specified ARIMA models produce systematically biased forecasts. The optimal model of ARIMA aligned with the ARIMA optimization approach reported by [19] for HIV forecasting in [16]. Based on the weakness of individual growth and conventional ARIMA models, the hybrid models are recommended to capture the complex pattern of the series together with growth models.

In our study, the performance of the growth and their hybrid models showed clear superiority over other models. The hybrid model, especially Exponential-ETS, achieved the best overall performance and showed superior predictive accuracy and strong capability in capturing complex patterns in the data with the lowest accuracy measures. This finding aligns with the literature on hybrid model superiority for HIV forecasting. A study by [12] showed that hybrid models outperformed ARIMA and ES in forecasting HIV incidence, particularly when the series exhibited complex non-linearity. The superiority of combined models over individual frameworks is well established across diverse settings, including studies in China [37], Pakistan [8], and the Philippines [36]. The superiority of the Exponential-ETS hybrid model over other frameworks highlighted the importance of decomposing the HIV incidence series into a macro-level growth component and a micro-level linear residual component. This aligns with the findings of [37, 38] and [8], who demonstrated that hybrid models combining a deterministic trend capture with stochastic residual modeling are particularly effective for epidemiological data featuring structural breaks and non-linearity. Furthermore, while the n = 35 annual observations represent a significant constraint, the consistent outperformance of hybrids across all evaluated metrics suggests that hybridizing flexible growth curves (like exponential and logistic) with exponential models specification provides an optimal balance between model complexity and predictive accuracy for this data-limited setting.

It is possible that underreporting and surveillance limitations historically shaped the declining trend. As noted in our study and supporting literature [4, 6], HIV in KSA has been severely underestimated due to social stigma, legal barriers, and the disproportionate burden among the non-Saudi expatriate population, who often faced barriers to formal healthcare reporting. However, the expansion of systematic screening programs reversed this underreporting dynamic. The progressive inclusion of HIV, HBV, and HCV in the mandatory premarital screening program starting in 2008 [47], coupled with the expansion of facility-based testing, may partially reflect the sharp resurgence in detected cases from 2019 onward.

Conclusion

This study concluded that the Exponential-ETS hybrid model provides the better forecasting framework for HIV incidence in KSA as compared to conventional individual models. The 5-year projections indicated a continued upward trend, but with a decelerated rate of growth compared to the sharp surge observed in 2019–2024. This relative deceleration showed that current public health efforts, including pre-marital screening programs, ART expansion, and national AIDS awareness campaigns, are beginning to moderate the rate of new infections. Nevertheless, continued surveillance and resource allocation remain critical to managing the absolute numerical burden of the disease.

Limitations

Although this study provided good insights, it has limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the annual surveillance record of limited available data represents a constraint inherent to the availability of KSA HIV data since 1990. The World Bank data represent one of the most systematically compiled sources of national HIV surveillance estimates, providing consistent annual trends for HIV incidence in Saudi Arabia. However, a key limitation is the potential underestimation of the true burden of disease. This is largely attributable to social stigma, cultural and legal sensitivities surrounding HIV, and barriers to testing and disclosure. As longer surveillance records become available, future studies should revisit deeper architectures. Third, the growth and time series models applied here do not incorporate epidemiologically informative covariates; therefore, future studies should explore compartmental models that incorporate covariates alongside machine learning frameworks.
