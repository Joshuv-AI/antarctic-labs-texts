Association between osteoporosis and severe depressive symptoms among postmenopausal women attending tertiary outpatient clinics in Nigeria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Uchenna Cosmas Ugwu, Osmond Chukwuemeka Ene
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/ObjectivesOsteoporosis and depression are significant, frequently co-occurring conditions in postmenopausal women, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to integrated care is limited. This study aimed to examine the association between osteoporosis and severe depressive symptoms among postmenopausal women attending tertiary outpatient clinics in Nigeria and to identify related demographic, clinical, and lifestyle correlates.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional survey was conducted from July 2024 to May 2025 across 12 purposively selected tertiary outpatient clinics representing all six geopolitical zones of Nigeria. A total of 712 postmenopausal women aged ≥ 45 years (mean age: 60.3 years) were recruited using multistage and convenience sampling. Data were collected via a validated, structured questionnaire; the Osteoporosis and Depression Assessment Questionnaire (ODAQ), which included sociodemographic data, menopausal and clinical history, osteoporosis risk factors, PHQ-9 depression screening, and lifestyle variables. Descriptive statistics and chi-square (χ²) tests were used to analyze the association between osteoporosis and depressive symptoms, with significance set at p ≤ 0.05.ResultsSevere depressive symptoms were identified in 75.6% of participants. Depression was significantly more common among those with osteoporosis (79.1% vs. 66.5%; χ² = 2.190, p = 0.010) and a history of fractures (87.1% vs. 68.2%; χ² = 5.513, p = 0.012). Other factors associated with higher depressive symptoms included older age (≥ 60 years), late menopause (after age 50), low parity (≤ 5 pregnancies), comorbid chronic conditions (80.4%; p = 0.031), and abnormal anthropometry. Adequate calcium intake was associated with lower depression rates (61.1% vs. 80.9%; p = 0.010). Notably, non-smokers and alcohol users exhibited higher depression rates compared to their counterparts, suggesting possible coping behaviors or reporting biases.ConclusionThe findings underscore a strong association between osteoporosis and severe depressive symptoms in Nigerian postmenopausal women, highlighting the urgent need for integrated mental and bone health interventions in tertiary and primary healthcare settings. Screening for depression should be routinely included in osteoporosis management to improve outcomes in aging women. This study supports WHO’s call for integrated non-communicable disease care and offers evidence to inform policy and clinical practice in similar low-resource settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-03951-5.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Depression, Postmenopausal women, Nigeria, Cross-Sectional study

Background

Osteoporosis and depressive symptoms are two interrelated and significant public health challenges that disproportionately affect postmenopausal women globally. Osteoporosis, defined by reduced bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, increases fracture risk and is a leading contributor to morbidity and reduced quality of life among aging women [1, 2]. Concurrently, depressive symptoms are common during the postmenopausal period and have been linked to hormonal changes, particularly estrogen withdrawal, as well as to social and psychological transitions such as loss of fertility and changing family roles [3, 4]. A growing body of international literature highlights a consistent intersection between osteoporosis and depressive symptoms. Women diagnosed with osteoporosis often experience higher rates of depression, attributed to factors such as chronic musculoskeletal pain, limited physical mobility, increased risk of falls, and subsequent social withdrawal [5–7]. Studies from the Middle East and South Asia have similarly reported impaired mental well-being among postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, reflecting broader psychosocial impacts of the condition [8, 9]. Furthermore, comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension have been independently associated with depression in this population, compounding health burdens and complicating clinical care [10, 11].

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including those in Sub-Saharan Africa, the convergence of osteoporosis and mental health issues poses a distinct public health challenge. These regions often face significant gaps in geriatric healthcare infrastructure, limited access to mental health services, and low prioritization of age-related conditions within national health agendas [12]. The lack of integrated care approaches that address both physical and psychological health in aging women further exacerbates the burden. As such, understanding the interaction between osteoporosis and depressive symptoms in LMIC contexts is crucial for designing responsive health systems and targeted interventions. Despite global recognition of the importance of non-communicable disease (NCD) integration, there remains a paucity of region-specific data on the co-occurrence of osteoporosis and depression in Sub-Saharan Africa. This study addresses a critical gap in the literature by exploring these associations among postmenopausal women attending tertiary outpatient facilities in Nigeria.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a hospital-based cross-sectional design conducted between July 2024 and May 2025, involving twelve tertiary outpatient clinics strategically selected across Nigeria. To ensure national representation, two tertiary hospitals were purposely selected from each of the six geopolitical zones of the country. Selection criteria were based on the availability of specialized services in geriatrics, orthopedics, psychiatry, obstetrics, gynecology, and nutrition, without regard to the sociodemographic characteristics of their clientele. Each participating facility contributed a maximum of 60 participants, with data collected from their respective outpatient departments. In the North Central zone, data were collected from Jos University Teaching Hospital (JUTH), Jos, Plateau State, and Benue State University Teaching Hospital (BSUTH), Makurdi, Benue State. From the North East, the participating centers were University of Maiduguri Teaching Hospital (UMTH), Maiduguri, Borno State, and Federal Teaching Hospital, Gombe (FTHG), Gombe State. In the North West, the selected facilities included Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital (AKTH), Kano State, and Usmanu Danfodiyo University Teaching Hospital (UDUTH), Sokoto State. For the South West zone, data were obtained from Lagos State University Teaching Hospital (LASUTH), Ikeja, Lagos State University, and University College Hospital (UCH), Ibadan, Oyo State, affiliated with the University of Ibadan. The South South region was represented by University of Calabar Teaching Hospital (UCTH), Calabar, Cross River State, and University of Uyo Teaching Hospital (UUTH), Uyo, Akwa Ibom State. Lastly, the South East zone contributed participants through Abia State University Teaching Hospital (ABSUTH), Aba, Abia State, and University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital (UNTH), Ituku-Ozalla, Enugu State. This geographically diverse selection of tertiary health centers ensured broad demographic and clinical variability, providing a robust platform for generating representative data on postmenopausal women’s health across Nigeria.

Participants and eligibility criteria

The target population comprised postmenopausal women aged 45 years and above, with a mean age of 60.3 years. Eligibility criteria included: being present at the outpatient clinic during the study period, confirmed postmenopausal status, ability to provide informed consent, and being mentally stable to complete the questionnaire. Participants were also required to be seeking consultation for age-related health issues such as osteoporosis or depression. The postmenopausal women who did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded from the study.

Sampling procedure

A multistage sampling technique was utilized. In the first stage, the six geopolitical zones of Nigeria formed six clusters using cluster sampling. In the second stage, two tertiary hospitals (one federal and one state-owned) were purposively selected from each zone based on availability of relevant care services. In the third stage, a convenience sampling method was used to recruit 60 eligible participants from each of the 12 facilities, yielding a total sample of 720 women. Sample size determination

Sample size was calculated using the formula: n = 39 × D × P/T², where D is the estimated prevalence of osteoporosis (73.8%), P is 100% – D (26.2%), and T is 15% of D (11.07). The estimated minimum sample size was n = 615. To account for a 10% non-response rate, the target was increased to 720 participants. This approach follows best practices in social science and public health research [13].

Materials and data collection instrument

Data were collected using the Osteoporosis and Depression Assessment Questionnaire (ODAQ), a structured instrument developed specifically for this study. The ODAQ comprised five sections: (A) Sociodemographic Information; (B) Menopausal and Medical History; (C) Osteoporosis Risk Factors; (D) Depressive Symptoms Assessment using the PHQ-9; and (E) Lifestyle and Physical Activity. The development of the ODAQ was informed by items adapted from established instruments and prior studies related to osteoporosis and depression in middle-aged women [1, 2, 6, 7, 10, 11, 14, 15]. To ensure cultural relevance and content validity for Nigeria’s multi-ethnic population, the instrument underwent content validation by a panel of 11 multidisciplinary experts in public health, geriatrics, gynecology, orthopedics, and psychiatry. The experts evaluated each item for clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness. To enhance accessibility, the questionnaire was worded in simple, clear English, which is widely used in Nigeria’s healthcare and research settings. Based on expert feedback and pilot testing, no translation was required, as all study participants were literate in English. The finalized ODAQ demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85, exceeding the standard reliability threshold (≥ 0.70) commonly accepted in social science research [16]. Data collection was conducted by 24 trained research assistants (two per participating hospital), all of whom were experienced, hospital-based nurses familiar with the outpatient care environment and the cultural context of their respective locations. The assistants received a 30-minute standardized training session led by the principal investigator to ensure consistency in administration. Surveys were conducted under supervision, and clarification was provided to participants when necessary. Completed questionnaires were retrieved immediately to prevent data loss and ensure data quality.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 [17]. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to summarize categorical variables. Associations between categorical variables (e.g., osteoporosis status and depressive symptoms) were assessed using Chi-square (χ²) tests. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05, and all tests were two-tailed. Effect sizes were interpreted following conventional thresholds. A post hoc power analysis confirmed that the final sample size (n = 712) provided 80% power to detect moderate effect sizes, consistent with established methodological standards for cross-sectional studies [13]. The study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure transparency and methodological rigor [18].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Nigeria, Nsukka (UNN), in accordance with the guidelines of the National Health Research Ethics Committee (NHREC). The ethical clearance reference number is REC/FE/2024/00049. The study adhered strictly to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [19] and the American Psychological Association’s Ethical Guidelines [20]. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the data collection and analysis processes. No financial or material incentives were offered to participants, in order to minimize potential bias. In addition to institutional approval, further authorization was obtained from the Chief Medical Directors of all participating healthcare facilities, ensuring local institutional support and oversight. A copy of the ethical approval letter from the University of Nigeria Nsukka Research Ethics Committee is attached as supplementary material, in compliance with the reviewer’s request.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

A total of 712 postmenopausal women participated in the study. The majority were aged 60 years and above (60.8%), with 39.2% aged 45–59 years. Most participants (63.5%) reported experiencing menopause after the age of 50. Educational attainment was high, with 78.8% having completed tertiary education. A significant proportion (62.4%) had five or fewer pregnancies, and only 34.7% were currently employed. Regarding marital status, 58.7% were not in a union (widowed, divorced, or single), while 41.3% were married or cohabiting. Tobacco use was reported by 31.3%, and a large majority (83.3%) reported current alcohol consumption. Notably, 75.6% of participants screened positive for severe depressive symptoms, indicating a substantial mental health burden in this population. (Refer to Table 1).

Association Between osteoporosis-related factors and severe depressive symptoms

Severe depressive symptoms were significantly more prevalent among women with a diagnosis of osteoporosis (79.1%) compared to those without (66.5%) (χ² = 2.190, p = 0.010). Similarly, a history of fractures was strongly associated with depression, with 87.1% of those reporting previous fractures experiencing severe depressive symptoms (χ² = 5.513, p = 0.012). Although family history of osteoporosis and use of osteoporosis medications did not show statistically significant associations, higher depressive symptoms were noted among those affected. Comorbid chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes, hypertension) were significantly associated with depression (80.4% vs. 30.4%; χ² = 3.815, p = 0.031). Anthropometric measures were also relevant: women with abnormal height and weight had significantly higher rates of depressive symptoms (83.2% and 82.0%, respectively; p < 0.05). In terms of nutrition, those with adequate calcium intake reported significantly lower levels of depression (80.9% vs. 61.1%; χ² = 3.246, p = 0.010). Sunlight exposure and use of nutritional supplements were not significantly associated with depressive symptoms. (Refer to Table 2).

Association between sociodemographic variables and severe depressive symptoms

Age was significantly associated with severe depressive symptoms. Among women aged ≥ 60 years, 85.0% reported severe depression compared to 60.9% among those aged 45–59 (χ² = 0.880, p = 0.011), corresponding to an odds ratio (OR) of 3.66 [95% CI: 2.44–5.48], indicating that women aged ≥ 60 were over three times more likely to report severe depression. Late menopause (after age 50) was also significantly associated with depressive symptomatology: 86.5% of women with late menopause reported severe symptoms compared to 56.5% among those with earlier menopause (χ² = 1.494, p = 0.020), with an OR of 5.28 [95% CI: 3.59–7.78]. Parity status showed a significant association as well. Women with five or fewer pregnancies had a higher prevalence of severe depression (82.2%) than those with more than five (64.6%; χ² = 1.055, p = 0.013), corresponding to an OR of 2.54 [95% CI: 1.74–3.70]. Although education level did not reach statistical significance (χ² = 3.311, p = 0.102), women with tertiary education had a much higher rate of depression (85.9%) compared to those with lower education (37.1%), yielding an OR of 9.99 [95% CI: 6.52–15.29], suggesting a potentially important trend warranting further investigation. Employment and marital status were not significantly associated with depressive symptoms. However, depressive symptoms were more prevalent among unemployed/retired individuals (80.0%) and those not in a marital union (86.8%). Interestingly, tobacco use showed an inverse association. Non-smokers reported higher depressive symptoms (82.4%) compared to smokers (60.5%; χ² = 2.812, p = 0.001), with an OR of 3.07 [95% CI: 2.10–4.50], which may reflect underreporting or alternative coping mechanisms among smokers. Alcohol use was also significantly associated with depression. Women who consumed alcohol had a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms (79.3%) than non-users (57.1%; χ² = 3.541, p = 0.021), with an OR of 2.85 [95% CI: 1.89–4.30]. (Refer to Table 3).

Discussion

The findings of this cross-sectional study from Nigeria significantly contribute to the global understanding of the interplay between osteoporosis and severe depressive symptoms in postmenopausal women. The findings reveal a strikingly high prevalence of severe depressive symptoms (75.6%), even among women with high educational attainment (78.8% tertiary-educated), suggesting that health literacy alone may be insufficient to mitigate vulnerability to comorbid conditions [14]. Moreover, the study found a higher incidence of depression among women with osteoporosis (79.1%) and those with a history of fractures (87.1%), aligning with earlier reports from India, Lebanon, and Qatar [8, 9, 21]. The study further highlights that late menopause was significantly associated with higher depressive symptom burden; a finding supported by hormonal theories implicating prolonged estrogen fluctuation in mood dysregulation [22]. Nutritional inadequacies, including low calcium intake and abnormal body composition, were also linked to depressive symptoms, consistent with previous research from Iran, India, and Saudi Arabia [23–25].

An unexpected and noteworthy pattern emerged in which non-smokers reported significantly higher rates of severe depression than smokers (82.4% vs. 60.5%) [15]. While at first glance this may seem counterintuitive and contrary to well-established literature linking tobacco use with poor mental health outcomes, several possible explanations warrant consideration. First, sociocultural norms in Nigeria may influence the accuracy of self-reported smoking status. Given that smoking, especially among older women, is culturally stigmatized, some participants may have underreported smoking habits, resulting in misclassification bias. Second, the self-medicating hypothesis offers a plausible explanation: nicotine has short-term psychoactive effects, including dopamine release, which may temporarily alleviate symptoms of depression and anxiety. Consequently, individuals who smoke may experience transient mood regulation, leading to lower reported depressive symptoms despite long-term health risks.

Furthermore, it is important to consider that non-smokers in this cohort may have been exposed to other unmeasured psychosocial stressors. For example, caregiving burdens, financial strain, social isolation, or untreated comorbid conditions might disproportionately affect non-smokers in this population, overshadowing the protective effect typically associated with abstinence from tobacco. Additionally, smokers may represent a distinct subgroup who engage in other coping mechanisms (e.g., community engagement, social interactions in smoking settings) that indirectly buffer against depression. These culturally and contextually specific nuances suggest that the observed association is complex and may not reflect a direct protective effect of smoking, but rather highlight the need for more nuanced assessments of behavioral and psychosocial risk factors in mental health research. It is also important to note that this finding contrasts with prevailing global literature, where smoking is consistently associated with higher rates of depression and anxiety. Therefore, further research, preferably longitudinal and mixed-methods in design, is needed to investigate this unexpected association and explore the underlying mechanisms within the Nigerian and broader sub-Saharan African context.

The high prevalence of severe depressive symptoms (75.6%) and their strong association with osteoporosis and related factors such as prior fractures, comorbidities, and poor nutrition reflects a pressing but under-recognized public health challenge in aging populations, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Globally, the intersection between osteoporosis and mental health among postmenopausal women is well-documented. Numerous studies across different regions have established that women with osteoporosis often report higher rates of depressive symptoms due to chronic pain, reduced mobility, and social withdrawal resulting from fractures and physical frailty [5–7]. The current study corroborates this, showing a significantly higher rate of severe depressive symptoms in women with osteoporosis (79.1%) and previous fractures (87.1%). These results align with data from Middle Eastern and South Asian studies [8, 9], which reported similar patterns of psychosocial impairment and reduced quality of life among women diagnosed with osteoporosis. Also consistent is the noted link between chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes, hypertension) and depression [10, 11], suggesting that multimorbidity exacerbates psychological vulnerability.

From a geriatric health standpoint, these findings underscore the need for integrated care models that simultaneously address mental health and musculoskeletal health in aging women. Mental health screening should be embedded within routine osteoporosis management programs, particularly in tertiary outpatient settings. In terms of global public health, the study offers critical insight into how non-communicable diseases (NCDs) intersect with mental health in women’s health trajectories. This supports WHO’s call for greater integration of mental and physical health services within primary care, especially as women age and become more susceptible to hormonal and structural body changes [12].

The high rates of depressive symptoms among older women (85% in those ≥ 60 years) also echo findings from studies in Ethiopia [26], India [27], and Middle Eastern countries [28], emphasizing that this is not a localized problem but a widespread issue that necessitates coordinated global action. The observed association between late menopause and higher depression rates (86.5% vs. 56.5%) is biologically plausible, as prolonged estrogen fluctuation may affect mood regulation [22, 23]. Similarly, the higher depressive burden among women with fewer pregnancies mirrors earlier findings suggesting that multiparity may confer some psychological or hormonal resilience [29]. Calcium intake was a notable protective factor, with better-nourished participants reporting significantly fewer depressive symptoms (61.1% vs. 80.9%). This aligns with evidence from studies in Iran, India, and Saudi Arabia, showing that poor dietary habits contribute both to low bone mineral density and mood disorders [23–25]. These findings advocate for bone health management protocols that integrate mental health evaluation, especially in settings where fracture prevention and fall risk reduction are key priorities. Despite high educational attainment (78.8% tertiary educated), depression and osteoporosis remained prevalent, suggesting that knowledge alone does not protect against health deterioration in the absence of effective service delivery and behavioral interventions.

Furthermore, healthcare systems and policy makers in LMICs must prioritize screening and dual-management programs targeting both osteoporosis and depression. The Nigerian context, as this study illustrates, presents unique challenges; social isolation, low employment among older women (only 34.7% employed), and cultural stigmas around mental illness likely compound health disparities. This supports calls from researchers like Ugwu and Ene [14] for comprehensive aging policies in Sub-Saharan Africa that encompass both physical frailty and psychosocial dimensions of elder care. For healthcare providers, these results emphasize the importance of interdisciplinary teams involving endocrinologists, psychiatrists, and geriatricians. For public health officials, it provides justification for community-based outreach programs, awareness campaigns, and nutritional support schemes tailored for older women. International stakeholders and donors, including WHO and IOF, should note this data as evidence to advocate for inclusion of osteoporosis and mental health services within NCD frameworks for women’s health in developing nations.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the first large-scale, multi-centre investigations to explore the association between osteoporosis and severe depressive symptoms among postmenopausal women in Nigeria. Its nationally representative sampling across all six geopolitical zones enhances the generalizability of findings within the country. The use of a rigorously developed and validated instrument—the Osteoporosis and Depression Assessment Questionnaire (ODAQ), ensured high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) and content validity, strengthening measurement reliability. Trained hospital-based research assistants conducted data collection in a supervised setting, minimizing interviewer bias and data loss. Ethical rigor was maintained throughout, with adherence to international research standards, including the Declaration of Helsinki and APA guidelines. Moreover, the large sample size (n = 712) provided adequate statistical power (80%) to detect moderate effect sizes, aligning with established epidemiological standards. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference between osteoporosis and depressive symptoms, as temporal relationships cannot be established. The reliance on self-reported data, including PHQ-9-based depression assessment and lifestyle behaviors such as alcohol and tobacco use, may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. The use of convenience sampling within facilities, though practical, may introduce selection bias, potentially limiting external validity beyond clinical outpatient populations. Additionally, while the study considered a wide range of demographic and clinical variables, potential confounders such as medication side effects, pain severity, and hormonal profiles were not assessed. Lastly, exclusion of women not mentally stable enough to respond may have led to an underestimation of depression prevalence in the broader postmenopausal population.

Recommendations and conclusion

This study provides robust empirical evidence linking osteoporosis and severe depression among postmenopausal women in Nigeria, echoing trends observed globally. The high prevalence and significant associations suggest a critical need for integrated interventions spanning mental health, geriatric care, nutritional education, and public health policy. Future research should focus on longitudinal designs to determine causality and explore the effectiveness of integrated care interventions. Gender-specific and culturally appropriate mental health services, tailored for postmenopausal populations, are urgently needed to mitigate the dual burden of osteoporosis and depression in aging societies. This study contributes to the limited but growing body of evidence on the intersection of physical and mental health in postmenopausal women in LMICs. By documenting the high co-prevalence of osteoporosis and depressive symptoms in Nigerian outpatient settings, it underscores the need for integrated service delivery models that combine mental health screening with routine musculoskeletal care. These findings reinforce the World Health Organization’s call for the integration of mental health services within primary care and NCD frameworks. For clinicians, the results highlight the importance of interdisciplinary care teams; comprising endocrinologists, psychiatrists, nutritionists, and geriatricians; to manage complex cases involving comorbidity. For policymakers, the findings offer compelling justification for implementing community-based outreach programs and culturally sensitive health promotion strategies targeting older women. Moreover, the study advocates for enhanced investment in geriatric health systems in LMICs, particularly in addressing the psychosocial dimensions of aging.
