Multi-source machine learning for short-term influenza forecasting in Hunan Province, China: a spatiotemporal analysis using surveillance, search, meteorological and air-quality data
Authors: Qianlai Sun, Jiangshaya Bahati, Hongfeng Zhao, Zixin Hu, Yanhua Su, Xiaolei Wang, Zhihong Deng, Zhifei Zhan, Jia Rui, Zeyu Zhao, Tianmu Chen, Kaiwei Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate short-term influenza forecasting is important for early warning and public health preparedness, but routine sentinel surveillance may be delayed and may not fully capture behavioural and environmental signals related to influenza activity. This study aimed to develop and evaluate multi-source machine-learning models for short-term influenza forecasting in Hunan Province, China.MethodsWeekly influenza-like illness plus (ILI+) data from 54 sentinel hospitals across 14 prefecture-level cities in Hunan Province were used to characterise influenza activity from 2015 to 2024. For forecasting during 2020–2024, ILI+ data were integrated with Baidu Search Index indicators, meteorological variables and air quality index data. ILI + was calculated as the product of the weekly ILI proportion and weekly influenza virus test positivity rate. Five models, including LSTM, GRU, stacked autoencoder, random forest and LightGBM, were compared for 1–3-week-ahead forecasting under four feature settings. Performance was evaluated using RMSE, MAE, MAPE, SMAPE and R², with SeasonalNaive52 as the baseline comparator.ResultsInfluenza activity showed pronounced winter–spring seasonality with substantial interannual and city-level heterogeneity. At the provincial level, the all-features RF model achieved the lowest absolute errors among the machine-learning models (RMSE = 0.00882, MAE = 0.00389) and an out-of-sample R² of 0.723. Compared with the SeasonalNaive52 baseline, the machine-learning models showed lower RMSE, MAE and SMAPE and higher out-of-sample R². In city-level analyses, RF ranked highest in 11 of 14 cities based on the descriptive summary score, whereas GRU ranked highest in Yueyang, Zhangjiajie and Hengyang, indicating that model suitability may vary across local epidemic settings.ConclusionsWe developed a city-level multi-source framework for short-term influenza forecasting in Hunan Province by integrating surveillance, Baidu Search Index, meteorological and air-quality data. Multi-source predictors improved forecasting beyond a seasonal naive baseline, with RF providing a robust option for reducing absolute weekly errors. City-level performance differences highlight the need for local validation before operational deployment. With prospective validation, this framework could support 1–2-week-ahead early warning and targeted influenza preparedness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13811-8.

Introduction

Influenza is a major acute respiratory infectious disease worldwide, causing an estimated 3–5 million severe cases and 290 000–650 000 deaths each year [1]. In recent years, influenza activity in China has intensified, with increasingly pronounced seasonal fluctuations; during the 2023–2024 winter–spring season, many regions reported 30–50% higher numbers of influenza-like illness cases than in the corresponding periods of previous years [2, 3]. This upward trend poses a persistent challenge to the public health system and underscores the critical importance of accurate disease forecasting.

Hunan Province is located in the middle reaches of the Yangtze River in central-southern China and has a distinctive geographic setting, characterised by a subtropical monsoon climate with a mean annual temperature of 16–18 °C and relatively high humidity. With a permanent population of more than 66 million and its role as a major transportation hub, Hunan is a key junction in the influenza transmission patterns of northern and southern China [4]. These distinctive geographic and demographic characteristics create complex epidemiological conditions that require region-specific forecasting approaches. Therefore, developing accurate and timely forecasting models for this region is crucial for optimising healthcare resource allocation, strengthening prevention strategies and reducing the influenza-associated disease burden.

Traditional influenza surveillance relies primarily on sentinel hospital–based reporting systems, which track disease activity by collecting influenza-like illness (ILI) data and conducting virological testing [5]. However, this approach is inherently subject to temporal delays, typically requiring 1–2 weeks to complete data collection, laboratory testing and reporting processes [6], thereby constraining its early warning capacity. With advances in machine learning and deep learning, a wide range of predictive models—including traditional statistical approaches such as ARIMA that capture seasonal patterns, machine learning methods such as random forest and support vector machines that are well suited to modelling nonlinear relationships, and deep learning architectures such as LSTM that perform well for time-series data—have been increasingly applied to influenza forecasting [7–9].

However, existing studies have three key limitations. First, most work has focused on national or global scales, with relatively few studies providing fine-grained forecasts at the provincial level, making it difficult to capture region-specific transmission patterns. Second, with respect to predictor variables, many studies rely on a single or limited data source (e.g. surveillance data alone or surveillance combined with meteorological data), with insufficient integration of multidimensional factors such as web search behaviour and air quality. Third, in terms of model comparison and optimisation, previous research has often concentrated on applying a single model or comparing only a small number of models, lacking a systematic evaluation and optimisation framework that considers traditional statistical models, machine learning methods and deep learning architectures in parallel, thereby limiting the identification of truly optimal forecasting strategies.

Multi-source data integration offers a promising avenue for enhancing influenza forecasting capacity. Surveillance data provide an authoritative benchmark for epidemic activity but are subject to reporting delays; web search data can capture real-time changes in public health attention and thus offer leading warning signals [10, 11]; meteorological factors (e.g. temperature, humidity and diurnal temperature range) directly influence virus survival and population susceptibility [12]; and air quality indicators (such as PM2.5 and PM10) are closely associated with susceptibility to respiratory diseases [13]. Together, these heterogeneous data streams characterise the complex dynamics of influenza transmission from virological, behavioural and environmental perspectives, and their synergistic integration can uncover nonlinear relationships and interaction effects that are not apparent from any single data source, thereby substantially improving the accuracy and timeliness of influenza forecasts.

Therefore, in this study we integrated four types of data for Hunan Province between 2020 and 2024: influenza surveillance data, Baidu Search Index data, meteorological data and air quality data. We systematically compared the predictive performance of representative machine-learning and deep-learning models, including tree-based ensemble models (random forest and LightGBM), a representation-learning model (stacked autoencoder, SAE), and two recurrent neural network architectures with different gating mechanisms (LSTM and GRU).

By applying feature engineering, hyperparameter optimisation and model ensemble strategies, we identified the optimal forecasting model and used it to generate prospective predictions of future influenza trends. This study aims to provide a methodological framework and decision-support tool for influenza forecasting in Hunan Province and other central–southern provinces with similar geographical and climatic characteristics, thereby enhancing the scientific basis and precision of regional influenza prevention and control.

Methods

Influenza surveillance data were obtained from the China Influenza Surveillance Information System. The Hunan provincial surveillance network comprises 54 sentinel hospitals distributed across 14 prefecture-level cities (Fig. 1). The primary outcome was weekly influenza-like illness plus (ILI+), a composite indicator of laboratory-supported influenza activity calculated from sentinel ILI surveillance and virological testing data. Data sources

Influenza surveillance data

Influenza surveillance data were obtained from the China Influenza Surveillance Information System. The Hunan provincial surveillance network comprises 54 sentinel hospitals across 14 prefecture-level cities (Fig. 1), selected based on healthcare accessibility, facility level, and laboratory testing/reporting capacity. The primary outcome was weekly influenza-like illness plus (ILI+), a composite indicator used to represent laboratory-supported influenza activity. For each prefecture-level city c and epidemiological week t, ILI + was calculated as:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ILI+(c,t)\:=\:ILI\%(c,t)\:\times\:\:Positivity(c,t)$$\end{document}

where ILI% (c, t) denotes the proportion of outpatient and emergency visits that met the influenza-like illness case definition among sentinel hospitals in city c during week t, and Positivity (c, t) denotes the proportion of tested respiratory specimens that were positive for influenza virus during the same city-week. When multiple sentinel hospitals were available within a city, ILI% and test positivity were first summarized at the city-week level and then used to calculate weekly ILI+. Therefore, ILI+ should be interpreted as a relative indicator of laboratory-supported influenza activity, rather than as the number of laboratory-confirmed influenza cases or the incidence rate in the general population.

Baidu search index

Baidu Search Index data for Hunan Province were obtained from Baidu Index [14]. Six influenza-related terms were queried, including disease names (“influenza”, “influenza A”, “influenza B”) and symptoms (“fever”, “nasal congestion”, “runny nose”). Daily search volumes were aggregated by epidemiological week to weekly mean values. A composite Baidu feature was constructed as a weighted average of the six weekly indices (weighting scheme and rationale in Supplementary Methods). Data covered January 2020 to December 2024.

Because online search behaviour may not be synchronous with observed influenza activity, we assessed the potential lag between the composite Baidu feature and ILI+ across prefecture-level cities. The lag-sensitivity analysis showed that the preferred lag varied across regions; therefore, we did not impose a single fixed lag for all cities. Instead, the composite Baidu feature was used within historical input windows in the forecasting models, allowing the models to learn city-specific temporal dependencies and potential delayed associations between Baidu search activity and subsequent ILI+ trends.

Meteorological data

Meteorological data were obtained from the China Meteorological Data Service Center (http://data.cma.cn/). Daily city-level meteorological records for the 14 prefecture-level cities in Hunan Province were extracted, including daily minimum temperature and daily maximum temperature. For each city-day record, daily diurnal temperature range (DTR) was calculated as the daily maximum temperature minus the daily minimum temperature. To match the weekly resolution of influenza surveillance, daily meteorological variables were aggregated within each city and ISO epidemiological week using arithmetic means. Specifically, weekly DTR was calculated as the mean of daily DTR values within the corresponding city-week. Therefore, the weekly DTR used in the models represents the average daily within-day temperature fluctuation during that week, rather than the difference between the highest and lowest temperatures observed over the whole week. The weekly meteorological variables were then merged with city-level weekly ILI+ data using city and ISO epidemiological week codes [15].

Air quality data

Air quality data were obtained from the Tianqihoubao historical air quality archive (https://www.tianqihoubao.com/aqi/). Daily mean AQI values were extracted for monitoring stations in Hunan Province, averaged across stations within each prefecture-level city, and aggregated to weekly means to align with the influenza surveillance data. AQI is a composite indicator incorporating PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, O3, and CO, and has been linked to influenza susceptibility and transmission [16].

Data preprocessing and feature engineering

Data quality control

The data cleaning process included outlier handling, missing-value imputation, and data integrity checks. Outliers were detected using Tukey’s boxplot method (IQR-based) and were manually reviewed to distinguish true epidemic extremes from data errors; erroneous values were corrected using interpolation/smoothing, while plausible extremes were retained. Missing values were imputed using interpolation or moving-average approaches depending on missingness patterns and proportions (details in Supplementary Methods). All data sources were aligned and merged by ISO epidemiological week to construct a unified multi-source weekly time-series dataset.

Feature standardisation

Because ILI + is non-negative, we truncated any negative values that might arise after back-transformation to ensure that all predicted values remained ≥ 0. Standardisation parameters were estimated exclusively from the training set and then applied to the validation and test sets, in order to avoid data leakage.

Feature engineering

Temporal features were created to capture seasonality, including ISO week-of-year (1–52/53) and calendar month (1–12), which were one-hot encoded. To model autoregressive dynamics, lagged ILI+ values from the previous 1–4 weeks were included as additional predictors.

To avoid information leakage in the multi-step forecasting setting, all external predictors were aligned according to the forecast origin. For a forecast issued at the end of week t, only information available up to week t was used. Therefore, when predicting ILI + for week t + h, external predictors from week t + h or any future week were not included. Instead, Baidu Index, AQI and meteorological variables were entered as lagged predictors that were observable at the forecast origin or earlier. For example, for a 1-week-ahead forecast of ILI + at week t + 1, predictors from week t or earlier were eligible, whereas predictors measured during week t + 1 were excluded. For a 2-week-ahead forecast, predictors from weeks t + 1 and t + 2 were excluded.

Exploratory data analysis and feature combinations

Construction of the time-series dataset

We constructed a unified weekly time-series dataset by temporally harmonizing influenza surveillance, Baidu Index, meteorological, and air-quality variables using ISO epidemiological week codes for downstream integrative modelling. The modelling dataset covered weeks 1/2020 to 52/2024, during which all multi-source predictors were consistently available. The full 2015–2024 surveillance series was used to characterise long-term seasonality and interannual epidemic patterns, whereas forecasting models were restricted to 2020–2024. Forecasting models used aggregate ILI + as the outcome rather than type- or subtype-specific influenza activity. Predictors were scaled to [0,1] using min–max normalization (Sect. 3.2).

Lagged time-series association analysis

To evaluate the temporal associations between weekly ILI + and candidate external predictors, we replaced the simple Spearman correlation analysis with an autocorrelation-aware lagged time-series analysis. First, we estimated prewhitened cross-correlation functions (CCFs) between ILI + and each candidate predictor, including Baidu Index, AQI, minimum temperature and diurnal temperature range. Prewhitening was used to reduce the influence of serial autocorrelation before estimating lagged associations. Lags from 0 to 12 weeks were examined. A positive lag was defined as the predictor observed at week t-lag being associated with ILI + at week t.

Second, we conducted a lag-sensitivity analysis using expanding-window time-series cross-validation. An autoregressive baseline model including lagged ILI+ values from the previous 1–4 weeks and seasonal week-of-year terms was first fitted. Each candidate external predictor was then added one at a time at lags 0–12 weeks, and the corresponding cross-validated RMSE was compared with the autoregressive baseline. This analysis was used to assess whether a specific lagged predictor improved forecast performance while respecting the temporal ordering of the data.

Prediction models

To benchmark forecasting performance across representative modelling approaches, we compared five machine-learning/deep-learning models: two recurrent neural network architectures with different gating mechanisms (LSTM and GRU), a representation-learning model (SAE), and two tree-based ensemble models (RF and LightGBM). In addition, a SeasonalNaive52 model was included as a conventional baseline comparator. Detailed model architectures and hyperparameter settings are provided in Supplementary Material 1 (Tables S1–S5; Figs. S1–S5).

Long short-term memory network (LSTM)

We implemented a stacked LSTM model to capture temporal dependencies in the weekly time series for multi-step forecasting. Hyperparameter settings are reported in Supplementary Material 1 (Table S1). Predictive uncertainty was estimated using Monte Carlo dropout, from which 95% prediction intervals were derived.

Gated recurrent unit (GRU)

A stacked GRU model was implemented as an alternative recurrent architecture for time-series forecasting. Hyperparameter settings are provided in Supplementary Material 1 (Table S2). Uncertainty estimation followed the same Monte Carlo dropout procedure as for LSTM.

Stacked autoencoder (SAE)

We used a stacked autoencoder to learn latent representations from the input predictors, followed by supervised fine-tuning for forecasting. Model configuration and hyperparameters are summarized in Supplementary Material 1 (Table S3).

Random forest (RF)

Random forest was implemented using a multi-output regression setup to generate multi-step forecasts simultaneously. Hyperparameters are reported in Supplementary Material 1 (Table S4). Forecast uncertainty was quantified using bootstrap resampling to obtain 95% prediction intervals.

LightGBM-based quantile regression

We implemented LightGBM with quantile regression to produce probabilistic forecasts. Models were trained to estimate multiple conditional quantiles (e.g., lower, median, and upper quantiles), which were combined to form 95% prediction intervals. Hyperparameter settings are provided in Supplementary Material 1 (Table S5).

Baseline comparator: seasonal naive model

A SeasonalNaive52 model was used as the baseline comparator. For each forecast target, this model used the observed ILI+ value from the same epidemiological week in the previous year as the predicted value. The seasonal naive baseline was evaluated using the same test period, forecast horizons and performance metrics as the machine-learning models. The detailed definition and formula of the SeasonalNaive52 baseline are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Model training and validation

Forecast-origin design and leakage prevention

To avoid information leakage, all models were trained and evaluated using a fixed forecast-origin design. For each prefecture-level city and each forecast origin defined as the end of epidemiological week t, the input sequence contained only information observed up to and including week t. Specifically, the models used the historical 7-week sequence of ILI+, Baidu Search Index, AQI, minimum temperature and diurnal temperature range from weeks t-6 to t, and generated direct multi-output forecasts of ILI + for weeks t + 1, t + 2 and t + 3. Thus, Baidu, meteorological and air-quality values from the target forecast weeks were not used as predictors. The same temporal rule was applied to all feature combinations and all five model classes.

Data splitting strategy

We adopted a time-ordered evaluation protocol. The first 70% of observations were used for training and the remaining 30% for testing. Random splitting was avoided to prevent information leakage in the time series setting [17].

Hyperparameter optimisation

For deep learning models, hyperparameters were tuned using a validation subset from the training period with early stopping (patience = 20). For machine-learning models (RF and LightGBM), hyperparameters were tuned using time-series cross-validation within the training set (rolling/expanding window). Gradient clipping (max_norm = 1.0) was applied during deep learning training to stabilise optimisation [18].Hyperparameter ranges and selected values are reported in the Supplementary Methods (Tables S1–S5).

Performance evaluation

Forecast accuracy was primarily evaluated using RMSE and MAE, which quantify absolute prediction error, and SMAPE, which provides a more stable percentage-based error measure when ILI+ values are low. R² was reported as a secondary measure of out-of-sample goodness-of-fit rather than as a primary measure of forecast accuracy. MAPE was retained for completeness but was not used for primary model ranking because ILI+ values near zero can inflate percentage errors. Metric definitions are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

For visual comparison across models, each metric was also rescaled to a 0–100 standardised score (error-type metrics: lower is better; R²: higher is better). These standardised multi-metric scores were used only as descriptive visual summaries and were not treated as a formal weighted objective function or as the sole basis for model selection. (Supplementary Methods).

Results

Spatiotemporal patterns of influenza-like illness

We summarised spatiotemporal patterns of weekly ILI+, a composite indicator combining ILI consultation proportion and influenza test positivity, in Hunan Province using surveillance data from 2015 to 2024. Descriptive analyses used the full 2015–2024 series, whereas forecasting models were developed for 2020–2024 because multi-source predictors (Baidu Search Index, meteorological variables, and AQI) were only consistently available from 2020 onwards.

Influenza activity showed a consistent winter–spring pattern (Fig. 2). Epidemic activity generally increased from epidemiological weeks 40–45, peaked between weeks 50 and week 4 of the subsequent year, and returned to baseline by weeks 8–12. The mean epidemic duration was 14 ± 3 weeks, with occasional bimodal seasons (e.g., 2017–2018).

Across prefecture-level cities, epidemic timing was highly synchronised, with differences in peak timing of < 2 weeks. In contrast, epidemic intensity varied spatially: peak ILI + in major cities (e.g., Changsha, Yueyang and Changde) was on average 30–50% higher than in more mountainous areas.

Marked interannual variation was observed. The 2017–2018 season had the highest intensity and a prolonged epidemic period (> 20 weeks). In 2020–2021, influenza activity was minimal during COVID-19 control measures. After 2022, influenza activity increased again, approaching pre-pandemic levels.

Selection of predictor variables and lagged time-series association

Based on data availability and prior evidence, we considered four candidate predictors for influenza forecasting: Baidu Search Index, AQI, minimum temperature (Tmin), and diurnal temperature range (DTR). In the revised autocorrelation-aware analysis, the prewhitened cross-correlation function (CCF) indicated that Baidu Index had the strongest positive association with weekly ILI + at lag 0, supporting its interpretation as a near-real-time behavioural signal. AQI showed a weaker positive association, with the largest adjusted CCF at lag 11. Minimum temperature showed a negative association with ILI+, with the strongest adjusted CCF at lag 2, whereas DTR showed weaker and less stable lagged associations (Fig. 3A).

The expanding-window time-series cross-validation analysis provided a complementary assessment of the predictive usefulness of these lagged predictors. At the provincial level, AQI, minimum temperature and DTR showed their lowest RMSE at short lags, particularly around 1 week in the one-step no-leak forecasting setting. For Baidu Index, the province-level CV curve showed the lowest RMSE at lag 4, although the difference between lag 0 and lag 4 was small and city-level analyses supported lag 0 in 10 of 14 prefecture-level cities. Taken together, these findings support treating Baidu Index as a near-real-time behavioural signal while encoding AQI and meteorological variables as forecast-origin-valid lagged predictors (Fig. 3B-C).

To quantify the incremental value of each data source, we evaluated four nested feature sets: (i) ili_only (ILI+ history only), (ii) ili_baidu (ILI + + Baidu Index), (iii) ili_baidu_aqi (ILI + + Baidu Index + AQI), and (iv) all_features (ILI + + Baidu Index + AQI + Tmin + DTR).

Comprehensive evaluation of model performance

Overall performance comparison

We trained five machine-learning models (Supplementary Figures S1–S5) and compared their predictive performance using metric-specific criteria. Across the evaluated models, the random forest (RF) model showed the most consistent performance for absolute-error metrics. Under the all-features configuration, RF achieved the lowest RMSE (0.00882) and MAE (0.00389), with SMAPE = 116.6 and out-of-sample R² = 0.723 (Table 1). Therefore, RF was interpreted as the most robust default model for minimising absolute weekly forecasting errors, while proportional error and goodness-of-fit were interpreted separately using SMAPE and R², respectively (Fig. 4; Table 1).

Feature-combination analysis suggested that additional data sources provided incremental predictive value, although the magnitude differed by metric and model. For RF, RMSE decreased from 0.009316 in the ILI-only model to 0.008925 after adding Baidu Index, 0.008869 after further adding AQI, and 0.008820 under the all-features setting. Out-of-sample R² also increased from 0.700 to 0.723, but this metric was interpreted as supplementary goodness-of-fit rather than as the primary evidence of forecast accuracy. Similar improvements in absolute-error metrics were observed for GRU and LSTM when external predictors were incorporated. These results suggest that the Baidu Index contributed the largest incremental gain for absolute-error reduction, whereas the additional contributions of AQI and meteorological variables were comparatively smaller. For LightGBM, however, the all-features configuration showed a slightly lower test-set R² than the ILI+Baidu and ILI+Baidu + AQI configurations, indicating that additional environmental predictors did not improve out-of-sample generalisation for this model.

Model fit and error characteristics

Quantitative evaluation on the test set (Table 1) showed that prediction errors generally decreased as additional predictors were incorporated, particularly for RF and the deep learning models. Notably, MAPE values were extremely large for several models, whereas SMAPE remained within a more interpretable range (Table 1). This pattern is expected when true ILI+ values approach zero, because percentage errors that use the observed value alone as the denominator can become numerically unstable. SMAPE uses the average magnitude of the observed and predicted values as the denominator and therefore provides a more stable complementary percentage-based metric for low-incidence periods.

Compared with the SeasonalNaive52 baseline, all machine-learning models achieved substantially better predictive performance in terms of RMSE, MAE, SMAPE and R². At the provincial level, the SeasonalNaive52 baseline had RMSE = 0.03136, MAE = 0.02188, SMAPE = 154.3 and R² = -1.659. In contrast, all machine-learning models across the four feature configurations showed lower RMSE, MAE and SMAPE and higher R². RF under the all-features setting achieved the best overall machine-learning performance, with RMSE = 0.00882, MAE = 0.00389, SMAPE = 116.6 and R² = 0.723.

Discussion

Using influenza surveillance data from 2015 to 2024, we identified a consistent winter–spring epidemic pattern in Hunan, with a main peak from late December to February and a mean epidemic duration of 14 ± 3 weeks. This seasonal profile aligns with reports from other Chinese provinces and temperate settings worldwide [19–23], while the slightly longer duration compared with northern regions [24] may reflect subtropical climatic conditions. Occasional bimodal seasons are consistent with alternating circulation of viral subtypes [25, 26], which adds complexity to short-term forecasting.

The observed seasonal patterns may also have been influenced by changes in circulating influenza types and subtypes across years. Such subtype replacement could partly contribute to occasional bimodal activity and interannual differences in epidemic magnitude. Therefore, our forecasting models should be interpreted as models of short-term seasonal dynamics in aggregate ILI+, rather than subtype-specific transmission.

Spatially, influenza activity showed “temporal synchrony but heterogeneous intensity”: peak timing differed by < 2 weeks across prefecture-level cities, yet peak ILI+ levels were higher in densely populated cities than in mountainous areas. Similar spatiotemporal patterns have been reported across China and in other countries [27–29], potentially driven by mobility networks and seasonal population movements such as Spring Festival travel. The unusually weak 2020–2021 season, followed by rebound after 2022, is consistent with global observations during and after COVID-19 non-pharmaceutical interventions [30–32], providing a natural context to assess forecasting robustness under disrupted transmission.

Our results support the complementary value of integrating digital and environmental data sources. Web search behaviour (Baidu Search Index) showed a positive association with ILI + and can serve as a timely proxy for population-level information-seeking, consistent with digital surveillance literature [14, 33, 34]. Meteorological conditions and air quality also showed associations with influenza activity in directions supported by prior evidence, including enhanced viral persistence at lower temperatures and potential effects of air pollution on respiratory infection risk [15, 35–38]. Intercorrelations among predictors suggest that environmental conditions may influence both transmission and public concern, highlighting the value of feature-level integration of multi-source predictors rather than relying on any single predictor.

In model comparison, RF showed the most consistent performance for absolute-error metrics under the full feature set. This should not be interpreted as universal superiority of tree-based models over recurrent neural networks for influenza forecasting. Rather, it is consistent with the modelling context of this study, namely a moderate-sized weekly surveillance dataset with heterogeneous tabular predictors, where tree-based ensembles often provide robust generalisation. From a public health perspective, RF may therefore serve as a practical default model for routine short-term forecasting because it is comparatively stable, less data-hungry than neural architectures, and directly useful for minimising typical weekly forecasting errors. The standardised summary scores shown in Fig. 4 are therefore descriptive only, whereas our primary interpretation relies on RMSE, MAE and SMAPE.

Performance was comparable to prior influenza forecasting studies using traditional models and digital indicators [39–42]. The strong performance of RF is also consistent with previous evidence that tree-based ensemble models can perform well in moderate-sized, heterogeneous tabular datasets [43–46].In our setting, this may reflect the combination of a limited number of weekly observations, multiple predictor types and nonlinear but relatively short-lag relationships between surveillance, search behaviour and environmental variables [47, 48], Therefore, RF should be interpreted as a robust and practical model in this specific forecasting setting, rather than as evidence that one model class is universally superior across all regional influenza forecasting tasks.

Stepwise feature-set analysis quantified the marginal value of each data source, but these findings should be interpreted primarily through forecast-error metrics rather than R² alone. Adding the Baidu Index produced the largest reduction in RF prediction error, with RMSE decreasing from 0.009316 to 0.008925 and MAE decreasing from 0.004112 to 0.003955. Further inclusion of AQI and meteorological variables produced smaller incremental reductions, with RF reaching RMSE = 0.008820 and MAE = 0.003885 under the all-features setting. Out-of-sample R² showed a similar pattern, increasing from 0.700 to 0.723, but was interpreted as supplementary goodness-of-fit rather than as direct evidence of forecast accuracy. This pattern supports the utility of digital behaviour data for early epidemic signals [13, 14], while also recognising the biological relevance of meteorological drivers of seasonal transmission [35, 49]. The modest marginal gain from meteorology may partly reflect omitted factors such as humidity, which has been reported as important in other contexts [50].

City-level evaluation revealed substantial heterogeneity in forecasting performance, with generally better predictability in the Changsha–Zhuzhou–Xiangtan urban agglomeration and lower performance in mountainous western areas. Similar urban–rural gradients in predictive accuracy have been reported elsewhere [50, 51] and may reflect differences in transmission stability, surveillance completeness and representativeness of digital indicators [52, 53]. In addition, GRU was competitive in selected cities, particularly Yueyang, Zhangjiajie and Hengyang, suggesting that local epidemic dynamics may influence model suitability. Cities with more regular temporal dependence or smoother epidemic trajectories may benefit from sequence models, whereas RF may be more robust in cities with noisier surveillance signals or more heterogeneous relationships among surveillance, search and environmental predictors. These findings underscore the need for location-specific model validation and support “precision public health” approaches to influenza control [54]. Because this city-level comparison was exploratory, the city-specific model differences should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence that a given architecture is optimal for a specific city type.

From a practical perspective, short-horizon (1–2-week-ahead) forecasts can provide actionable lead time for vaccination planning, resource allocation and school-based prevention [55]. Integrating multi-source predictors within routine surveillance systems may therefore improve situational awareness during seasonal upswings and help calibrate responses during low-activity periods.

Limitation

Several limitations should be noted. First, the modelling period overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic; changes in non-pharmaceutical interventions and care-seeking behaviour during this period may influence the stability and generalisability of models to routine influenza seasons. Second, the Baidu Index captures information-seeking behaviour among internet users and may not fully represent the whole population, and its temporal relationship with influenza activity may vary across regions. In addition, important covariates such as vaccination coverage and absolute humidity were not available. Because influenza type/subtype composition was not included as a model predictor, changes in viral dominance across seasons may limit model generalisability, particularly during seasons with unusual subtype replacement, bimodal activity, or post-pandemic rebound. Future studies should incorporate virological subtype proportions and evaluate subtype-specific forecasting models when longer and consistently linked virological time series become available. Third, we focused on short-horizon forecasting, and model performance for longer prediction horizons and in other provinces or climatic settings warrants further evaluation. Although the machine-learning models outperformed the SeasonalNaive52 baseline, further prospective validation is needed to assess whether these gains remain stable in routine post-pandemic influenza seasons and in other provinces.

Conclusion

We developed a full multi-source feature model for influenza forecasting in Hunan Province and systematically compared five algorithms across feature configurations. Random forest achieved the best overall performance at the provincial level (R² = 0.723) and performed well across most cities, supporting the use of tree-based ensembles for moderate-scale epidemiological forecasting. Stepwise integration of digital behaviour (Baidu Index) with environmental predictors improved model performance, highlighting the complementary value of heterogeneous data streams. With further integration into real-time surveillance workflows and multi-region external validation, this framework could support short-term early warning and inform vaccination planning and healthcare preparedness.
