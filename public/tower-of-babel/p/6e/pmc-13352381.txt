Blood acid/base balance in poultry: Dietary, environmental, and physiological determinants and emerging diagnostic opportunities
Authors: AJ Cowieson, N Reisinger, A Milanese, UM McCormack, CA Phillips
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcid/base balance is a central integrative process in poultry physiology, linking respiratory gas exchange, renal ion handling, skeletal mineral buffering, and cellular metabolism, with direct consequences for growth performance, skeletal integrity, eggshell quality, cardiopulmonary function, and survival. Although the mechanistic basis of acid/base regulation in birds has been well described for decades, direct assessment of blood acid/base status remains uncommon in commercial poultry production, where diet formulation targets and performance outcomes are often used as lagging and indirect proxies for physiological disruption. This review synthesizes classical and contemporary evidence describing the unique features of avian respiratory and renal physiology that govern blood pH, bicarbonate availability, electrolyte distribution, and calcium bioavailability. These physiological features explain why poultry are particularly sensitive to disturbances arising from dietary electrolyte composition, heat stress, ventilation, stocking density, and renal compromise. We highlight limitations of the dietary electrolyte balance concept as a surrogate for physiological status and demonstrate that both metabolic acidosis and alkalosis exert predictable but often under‑recognized effects on mineral metabolism, oxygen transport, and cardiovascular health. Central to this synthesis is the argument that blood acid/base biomarkers including pH, bicarbonate, base excess, electrolytes, strong ion difference, the anion gap, and others, provide a highly informative, integrative readout of nutritional adequacy, environmental stress, and renal function. The emergence of portable point‑of‑care blood analysers now enables rapid, on‑farm measurement of these variables, offering a practical pathway to move from anticipatory and formulation‑driven, to data-led physiology‑driven, decision‑making. Routine use of blood biomarker testing therefore represents a significant opportunity to improve diagnostic precision, nutritional management, and health and welfare outcomes in modern poultry systems.Keywords: Biomarkers, Acid base balance, Poultry, Blood, Diagnostics

Introduction

Acid/base balance is a fundamental homeostatic process in poultry, integrating respiratory gas exchange, renal ion handling, skeletal mineral metabolism, and cellular biochemistry. In broiler chickens, blood pH is maintained within a narrow physiological range (∼7.4-7.5 depending on bird age), and even modest deviations from this range can have disproportionate effects on growth performance, eggshell quality, skeletal integrity, cardiopulmonary function, and survival. The importance of acid/base regulation in poultry has been recognized for more than five decades, yet direct assessment of blood acid/base status remains uncommon in commercial practice, where performance metrics are often used as indirect and delayed indicators of physiological disturbance.

Early physiological studies established that avian blood possesses a well‑defined buffering system dominated by bicarbonate (HCO3) and haemoglobin (Hb), and that this system responds rapidly to changes in ventilation and dietary ion supply (Nightingale and Fedde, 1972; Cohen and Hurwitz, 1974). Subsequent work demonstrated that dietary salts, electrolyte ratios, and environmental conditions such as heat stress can reliably shift blood pH, HCO3 concentration, and blood gas variables (Hamilton and Thompson, 1980; Bottje and Harrison, 1985; Borges et al., 2007). More recent applied studies have extended these findings to modern commercial conditions, showing consistent associations between diet, environment, blood biochemistry, and performance outcomes in broilers and layers (Popoola et al., 2020; Livingston et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2024).

Despite the extensive literature, acid/base balance is still rarely measured directly in poultry production. Instead, nutritional and management decisions are frequently made based on feed formulation targets, environmental guidelines, or performance outcomes that lag physiological disruption. This review synthesizes classic and contemporary research to argue that blood acid/base biomarkers represent an underutilized but highly informative diagnostic interface between diet, environment, and bird health.

Physiological foundations of acid/base regulation in poultry

Blood buffering systems and respiratory control

The foundational work of Nightingale and Fedde (1972) provided the first comprehensive characterization of the buffering properties of chicken blood. Using carbon dioxide (CO₂) equilibration curves, these authors demonstrated linear relationships between blood pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO₂), and HCO3 concentration within the physiological range, confirming that HCO3 and Hb are the dominant buffers in avian blood. Importantly, they showed that in vivo buffering differs from in vitro measurements, underscoring the dynamic influence of respiration, circulation, and tissue metabolism on acid/base status.

Birds rely heavily on respiratory regulation of acid/base balance due to their high metabolic rate and limited renal concentrating capacity. Changes in ventilation therefore translate rapidly into changes in blood pCO₂ and pH. Experimental studies manipulating ventilation or exercise consistently show that increased ventilation reduces pCO₂ and raises blood pH, whereas hypoventilation has the opposite effect (Gleeson and Brackenbury, 1984). This reliance on respiratory regulation makes poultry particularly vulnerable to environmental conditions that alter breathing patterns e.g. ambient temperature, altitude, air quality in the shed, ventilation rates, stocking density, respiratory tract infections and so on.

Unique features of avian renal anatomy and function

The avian kidney plays a central, though often under‑appreciated, role in systemic acid/base regulation. Anatomically, the avian kidney is trilobed and located immediately behind the lungs on each side of the vertebral column, with each kidney composed of a heterogeneous population of nephrons that differ fundamentally from those of mammals (Lierz, 2003). Approximately 70–80% of avian nephrons are reptilian‑type nephrons lacking loops of Henle, while the remaining mammalian‑type nephrons possess short loops that extend into the medullary cones (Braun and Dantzler, 1972; Dantzler, 2003). This nephron heterogeneity constrains maximal urinary concentrating ability but does not preclude effective regulation of hydrogen ion (H⁺), HCO₃, and electrolyte balance.

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) in birds is relatively high on a mass‑specific basis, reflecting their elevated metabolic rate, but is tightly regulated by renal perfusion pressure and hydration status. Under normal conditions, large quantities of HCO₃ are filtered daily, necessitating highly efficient tubular reclamation to maintain systemic acid/base balance. Consequently, avian renal physiology is strongly oriented toward HCO₃ conservation and proton excretion, rather than urinary acidification through high titratable acidity, which is limited by the absence of concentrated urine formation (Craan et al., 1982; Dantzler, 2003). Most bird species, including domestic fowl, do not have a urinary bladder. Urine enters the cloaca and moves by reverse peristalsis into the large intestine which allows reabsorption of water and electrolytes. Urine is voided from the body as a thick paste that is low in water content but contains a high concentration of uric acid.

Tubular handling of bicarbonate and the role of carbonic anhydrase

Reabsorption of filtered HCO₃ occurs primarily in the proximal tubule and is mechanistically similar to that described in mammals, although quantitatively more critical due to limited renal reserve. Luminal H⁺ secretion via sodium (Na)/H⁺ exchangers allows filtered HCO₃ to combine with H⁺ to form carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), which is rapidly converted to CO₂ and H₂O by carbonic anhydrase (CA) expressed on the brush border membrane (Dantzler, 2003; Lierz, 2003). CO₂ diffuses into tubular epithelial cells, where intracellular CA catalyzes the reformation of H₂CO₃, dissociating into H⁺ and HCO₃. The newly generated HCO₃ is transported across the basolateral membrane into the circulation, effectively reclaiming filtered base (Fig. 1).

Carbonic anhydrase activity is therefore essential for both HCO₃ reabsorption and net acid excretion in birds. Pharmacological inhibition of CA in avian models leads to rapid HCO₃ wasting, metabolic acidosis, and impaired growth performance, underscoring the limited buffering capacity outside the renal–respiratory axis (Bernstein and Schraer, 1972). Given the reliance of poultry on dietary electrolyte manipulation, renal CA activity represents a key physiological bottleneck determining the effectiveness of alkalinizing or acidogenic dietary strategies.

Ammoniagenesis: glutamine, glutamate, and ammonia shuttling

Net acid excretion in poultry depends heavily on renal ammoniagenesis, a process that is quantitatively important despite the absence of urine acidification to mammalian levels. In the proximal tubule, glutamine is metabolized to glutamate and subsequently to α‑ketoglutarate, yielding two ammonium (NH₄⁺) ions and two HCO₃ ions per molecule of glutamine (Dantzler, 2003). The newly generated HCO₃ enters systemic circulation, contributing directly to base repletion, while NH₄⁺ is secreted into the tubular lumen, either directly via substitution for H⁺ on Na/H⁺ exchangers, or transported as NH₃, which subsequently combines with luminal H⁺. In birds, a substantial proportion of excreted nitrogen is eliminated as uric acid; however, ammoniagenesis remains essential for acid/base balance rather than nitrogen disposal per se. During metabolic acidosis, renal glutamine uptake and ammonium production increase markedly, whereas alkalosis suppresses these pathways, reducing endogenous HCO₃ generation.

Renal injury, whether due to dehydration, heat stress, mycotoxins, heavy metals, or infectious agents, can impair ammoniagenesis and thereby compromise the bird’s ability to replace consumed or lost HCO₃. This mechanism provides a physiological explanation for the frequent association between renal pathology, deranged plasma uric acid concentrations, elevated anion gap metabolic acidosis, and poor performance observed in commercial poultry. For example, Bideshki et al. (2023) explored the relationship between blood biochemistry and kidney lesions in broiler chickens. In a total of 80 birds, kidney necrosis severity was separated into either mild, moderate or severe categories. A total of 36 out of 80 birds had acute tubular necrosis, with 14 birds classified as ‘mild’, 12 as ‘moderate’ and 10 as ‘severe’. Plasma uric acid was increased in birds with moderate (from 5.04 mg/dL to 7.77 mg/dL) or severe lesions (5.04 mg/dL to 9.65 mg/dL) while blood urea nitrogen and creatinine were increased only in birds with severe tubular necrosis. Unfortunately, the authors did not measure plasma pH or electrolytes and concede in the paper that uric acid alone is inadequate to diagnose acute tubular necrosis in broilers (as e.g. dietary protein concentration can also influence plasma uric acid). Nevertheless, the paper shows a significant relationship between renal health and plasma biomarkers, including uric acid. Additional biomarkers such as those mentioned herein would provide differential information.

Proton secretion and titratable acidity

In addition to ammonia buffering, birds excrete H⁺ bound to titratable buffers, primarily phosphate. However, the capacity for titratable acid excretion is limited by dietary phosphate availability and by the relatively dilute nature of avian urine (Dantzler, 2003). Consequently, shifts in systemic acid/base balance are reflected more rapidly in blood HCO₃ and pH than in urinary pH, reinforcing the diagnostic value of blood‑based biomarkers in poultry, in this case especially blood phosphorus, pH, anion gap, uric acid, Na, chloride (Cl) and potassium (K).

The reliance on ammonia and HCO₃ handling rather than urine acidification means that poultry are particularly sensitive to disruptions in proximal tubular function. Conditions that impair tubular secretion or reabsorption e.g. infectious bronchitis, heavy metal toxicity, mycotoxins, vitamin deficiency, excess protein, salt or calcium (Ca) supply etc (Yousaf et al., 2025) can disproportionately affect acid/base balance compared with mammals, even in the absence of overt renal failure.

Renal handling of sodium, potassium, and chloride

Renal regulation of acid/base balance in poultry is inseparable from electrolyte handling. Sodium reabsorption provides the driving force for H⁺ secretion and HCO₃ reclamation, linking dietary Na supply directly to acid/base status (Cohen and Hurwitz, 1974; Ruiz‑Lopez and Austic, 1993). Chloride plays a pivotal role in maintaining electroneutrality; elevations in plasma Cl displace HCO₃, producing hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis when renal compensation is exceeded.

Potassium exerts both direct and indirect effects. Hypokalemia promotes intracellular acidosis and enhances renal H⁺ secretion, whereas hyperkalemia suppresses ammoniagenesis and H⁺ excretion, predisposing birds to metabolic acidosis (Kim et al., 1989; Nursoy et al., 2011). During heat stress, increased urinary and faecal K losses further complicate renal acid/base regulation, particularly when dietary electrolyte supply is marginal (Popoola et al., 2020).

Integration with respiratory and skeletal buffers

Renal compensation for acid/base disturbances in poultry is inherently slower than respiratory regulation. During respiratory alkalosis induced by panting, reduced pCO₂ elevates blood pH and suppresses renal H⁺ secretion and ammoniagenesis, leading to progressive depletion of circulating HCO₃ (Bottje and Harrison, 1985; Toyomizu et al., 2005; Comito et al., 2007). The limited ability of the avian kidney to rapidly regenerate HCO₃ under sustained respiratory alkalosis explains the characteristic decline in base excess observed during prolonged heat stress.

When renal compensation is insufficient, skeletal buffering through mobilization of alkaline Ca salts becomes increasingly important, linking renal acid/base physiology directly to bone integrity and eggshell quality (Ruiz‑Lopez and Austic, 1993; Nursoy et al., 2011). Thus, renal, respiratory, and skeletal systems operate as an integrated triad in maintaining systemic pH in poultry. Indeed, bone plays an active role in systemic acid/base regulation by serving as a reservoir of alkaline salts. During acidogenic states, Ca salts are mobilized from bone to buffer excess H+, whereas during alkalosis, Ca solubility decreases and less Ca is available in ionized form (Kohne and Jones, 1975; Ruiz‑Lopez and Austic, 1993; Nursoy et al., 2011). This skeletal buffering mechanism is especially relevant in poultry due to the high Ca flux associated with growth and egg production. Crucially, blood pH influences the proportion of Ca present as ionized Ca, the biologically active fraction. Alkalosis reduces ionized Ca without necessarily altering total Ca concentration, with an axiomatic effect in acidaemia, a phenomenon demonstrated in both broilers and laying hens (Kohne and Jones, 1975; Hughes, 1988; Nursoy et al., 2011). The substantial influence of blood acid/base status on ionizable Ca complicates Ca homeostasis and interferes with the regulatory role of parathyroid hormone and vitamin D3 in plasma mineral equilibria. This provides a mechanistic link between acid/base balance and disorders of eggshell quality, bone strength, and neuromuscular function, and highlights the inadequacy of evaluating Ca nutrition without considering acid/base status.

Dietary electrolytes and salt sources as drivers of acid/base balance

Sodium, potassium, chloride, and the DEB concept

A recurring theme across decades of research is that diets with identical dietary electrolyte balance (DEB) values can produce markedly different blood acid/base responses depending on the salt sources used. Studies comparing chloride (Cl) or HCO3‑based salts demonstrate that Cl exerts a disproportionately acidogenic effect relative to the alkalizing effect of HCO3, even at similar DEB values (Hamilton and Thompson, 1980; Khajali and Saedi, 2011). This observation has been repeatedly confirmed in broilers and layers (Kurtoglu et al., 2007; Squires and Julian, 2001).

Sodium bicarbonate supplementation increases circulating HCO3 and elevates blood pH, shifting birds toward metabolic alkalosis (Ruiz‑Lopez and Austic, 1993; Davison and Wideman, 1992; Martínez et al., 2021) and, in fact, is a common therapeutic intervention in human medicine for patients with metabolic acidaemia associated with chronic kidney disease. This is especially significant during proximal tubule acidosis which is characterised by a failure to reclaim filtered HCO3 (Raphael, 2024). In contrast, NaCl or NH4Cl loading reduces HCO3 concentration and promotes metabolic acidosis (Cohen and Hurwitz, 1974; Branton et al., 1986; Ruiz‑Lopez and Austic, 1993; Shlosberg et al., 1998). These shifts have important downstream consequences for calcium metabolism, bone buffering, and cardiovascular physiology.

Dietary manipulation of Na, K, and Cl has long been recognized as a powerful determinant of blood acid/base status in poultry (Wideman et al., 2003). Early feeding studies demonstrated that these ions exert predictable effects on blood pH and HCO3 concentration, independent of energy or protein intake (Cohen and Hurwitz, 1974; Hamilton and Thompson, 1980). Chloride consistently acts as an acidogenic ion, reducing plasma HCO3 and lowering blood pH, whereas Na and K salts paired with metabolizable anions such as HCO3 tend to promote alkalosis (Squires and Julien, 2001).

Squires and Julien (2001) fed broiler chickens a commercial ration that was adjusted to vary the Cl to HCO3 ratio. Specifically, dietary manipulation of NaCl and NaHCO3 resulted in three experimental treatments that were based on NaCl, a mix of NaCl and NaHCO3 or NaHCO3 alone. Compared with the control diet (NaCl with no NaHCO3), birds that received the diet based on NaHCO3 (4.2 g/kg NaHCO3) had a significantly higher blood pH (7.36 vs. 7.28), pCO₂ (64.7 vs. 60.1 mmHg) and HCO3 concentration (36.6 vs. 28.4 mmol/L) and significantly lower Cl (105.2 vs. 114.3 mmol/L). Interestingly, blood ionized Ca was also reduced in birds that received the diet based on NaHCO3 (1.31 vs. 1.42 mmol/L). These effects contributed to a significantly lower weight gain in birds fed the diet based on NaHCO3 of around 7-8% relative to the NaCl control group and a non-significant increase in FCR of 8 points. Importantly, an adjacent experiment reported in the same paper, whilst resulting in similar changes in haematological parameters, resulted in more modest, though directionally consistent, consequences for live performance outcomes.

Nursoy et al. (2011) fed Ross 308 male broiler chickens a series of diets varying in dietary Na and Cl concentrations (from ∼0.1-0.5% Na and from ∼0.11-0.22% Cl) and measured the influence of these dietary Na:Cl ratios on blood biochemistry. Increasing dietary Na from 0.1 to 0.5% in the grower phase generated a significant increase in plasma pH i.e. classical hypernatremia-associated alkalosis (Fig. 2), whereas diets with a lower Na to Cl ratio resulted in lower plasma pH. Plasma ionized Ca dropped from ∼1.42 mmol/L in the diet that created the lowest plasma pH, to ∼1.28 mmol/L in the diet with the highest plasma pH. Importantly, the diets that created the highest plasma ionized Ca often had the lowest total dietary Ca concentrations, further illustrating the importance of acid/base status in establishing mineral homeostasis (Nursoy et al., 2011). It should be noted that the diets fed by Nursoy et al. (2011) were extreme and created shifts in plasma Na and Cl of up to 5 mmol/L, with commensurate displacement of plasma HCO3 of up to 6-7 mmol/L. Furthermore, Nursoy et al. (2011) used NH4Cl in some of the dietary treatments, and this salt is known to be a more robust modulator of plasma pH than HCO3.

Effect of altering plasma pH using dietary salts on plasma ionized calcium in broiler chickens (Nursoy et al., 2011).

Livingston et al. (2022) adjusted dietary Na and Cl ratios (using sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) or sodium chloride (NaCl) without the use of NH4Cl and kept diet Na concentrations relatively constant at ∼0.18-0.20% whilst Cl was adjusted from ∼0.18-0.29%. These more subtle changes in dietary Na to Cl ratio did result in changes to plasma biochemistry but, for example, plasma Cl only rose by around 1.5 mmol/L when dietary Cl was increased from 0.18 to 0.24% in the finisher phase, and this was insufficient to generate a significant influence on plasma pH or ionized Ca concentration. Nevertheless, studies by Cohen and Hurwitz (1974), Ruiz-Lopez and Austic (1993), Squires and Julien (2001), Khajali and Saedi (2011), and Nursoy et al. (2011) have all shown substantial influences of dietary Na, Cl and HCO3 on plasma pH and other cation/anion related biomarkers.

One important dietary feature that plays a role in the strength of the influence of Cl on plasma pH is the per se concentration of cations (especially Na and K) in the feed. Increasing dietary Cl at low dietary cation concentrations will have a more profound influence on plasma pH than would otherwise be the case (Cohen and Hurwitz, 1974). This may be related to the strong ion difference in blood (Na – Cl, mmol/L) and the capacity of plasma to accommodate a rising Cl concentration before the renal system is required to offload HCO3 to maintain electroneutrality. In short, blood with a low net strong cation excess has limited ionic ‘headroom’ such that an increase Cl or unmeasured anions would be more likely to narrow the strong ion difference and create acidaemia. Furthermore, the ability of the bird to buffer the acidifying or alkalising effects of dietary salts depends on other blood buffering systems such as plasma proteins (albumin in particular), phosphate and Hb, and/or the per se HCO3 concentration in the feed or drinking water. For example, anaemia may predispose birds to acid/base derangement whereas polycythaemia may be protective, though both Hb-associated derangements generate complications in other domains such as blood gas management and cardiovascular health. Thus, a holistic view is crucial in order to generate reliable diagnoses.

As mentioned above, K occupies a distinct role as the dominant intracellular cation. Manipulation of dietary K influences intracellular buffering capacity, renal hydrogen ion secretion, and HCO3 conservation. Studies altering the dietary Na:K ratio demonstrate corresponding changes in blood gas variables and acid/base status, with implications for growth and mineral metabolism (Kim et al., 1989; Nursoy et al., 2011; Joardar et al., 2020). Under heat stress, K losses via urine and feces further complicate acid/base regulation, reinforcing the need for integrated electrolyte assessment (Popoola et al., 2020).

Some of these cation/anion derangements in commercial broiler chickens are illustrated in Fig. 2. Healthy broiler chickens have plasma Na, K, Cl, HCO3 and anion gap of 143, 5, 110, 24 and 16 mmol/L, respectively. However, these can become deranged due to inappropriate diet or drinking water ion concentrations, kidney or respiratory insults, or dysbiosis in the enteric microbiome. For example, excess Cl intake in either drinking water or feed can be the proximate cause of hyperchloremic metabolic acidaemia (Fig. 3). High anion gap metabolic acidaemia is also relatively common in commercial broilers (Fig. 3) and can be caused by uremia, viruses, excess dietary salt, protein or Ca, vitamin deficiency, heavy metal or mycotoxin toxicity, ketoacidosis or lactate acidosis. In short, renal health is crucial in acid/base physiology. As discussed above, damage to the renal tubules can impair the conversion of glutamine to two ammonium ions and two HCO3 ions, compromising the ability of the bird to replace HCO3 in systemic circulation (Dantzler, 2003; Raphael, 2024). Alternatively, renal damage may interfere with secretion of protons which can associate with either ammonia or another titratable buffer such as monohydrogen phosphate. Thus, measurement of plasma biomarkers related to cation/anion balance can be instructive in identification of clinical or sub-clinical renal function which is a prerequisite for nutrition, management or veterinary intervention.

The DEB; (Na + K − Cl) concept emerged as a practical tool to capture some of these effects in diet formulation. Numerous studies have shown that DEB influences growth, feed efficiency, and acid/base balance, particularly under heat stress conditions (Kim et al., 1989; Borges et al., 2007; Mushtaq and Pasha, 2013; Fu et al., 2021). However, whilst DEB is a useful and practical concept for feed formulation, it is based on generic contributions from the various dietary ingredients included in the feed. Given the importance of respiratory, renal and enteric health in blood acid/base balance, direct measurement of plasma pH and electrolytes provides more personalised insight into flock status and would allow DEB to be adjusted accordingly.

Physiological consequences of excess alkalinization and acidification

Excessive alkalinization through high NaHCO3 intake has been associated with increased blood volume, reduced erythrocyte deformability, hypocalcaemia, pulmonary hypertension, and ascites in broilers (Mirsalimi and Julian, 1993; Davison and Wideman, 1992; Khajali and Saedi, 2011; Nursoy et al. 2011). In particular, high blood pH (and HCO3) increases the affinity of Hb for oxygen, improving oxygen loading at low partial pressure of oxygen, in the lungs but making it potentially more difficult to offload oxygen in tissues. These findings illustrate that attempts to correct acid/base disturbances through dietary intervention can have unintended consequences if not guided by direct physiological measurement. Indeed, Ravindran et al. (2008) noted that increasing NaHCO3 concentration in a broiler diet from 2.4 to 16.2 g/kg resulted in a ∼10% drop in weight gain, a ∼7 point rise in FCR, a ∼40% rise in excreta moisture and significant reductions in the ileal digestibility of nitrogen and amino acids of 4-7%. Furthermore, the efficacy of exogenous phytase was significantly compromised by diets with high concentrations of NaHCO3 (Ravindran et al., 2008). These effects that may relate to the influence of HCO3 and/or Na, on acid/base status and the association of these with the solubility of phosphate in both the gut lumen and in plasma and/or the important role Na, K and Cl play in nutrient absorption.

Conversely, excessive Cl intake exacerbates metabolic acidosis, impairs growth, and increases the incidence of skeletal abnormalities such as tibial dyschondroplasia, likely due to increased skeletal buffering and Ca mobilization (Ruiz‑Lopez et al., 1993a; Ruiz‑Lopez et al., 1993b; Nursoy et al., 2011). Additionally, drinking water chemistry is often ignored in commercial poultry production systems e.g. salinity, pH, hardness, heavy metals etc, but can make a crucial contribution to overall acid/base status in the flock. The aforementioned studies underscore the central role of acid/base balance in mediating the effects of dietary minerals on skeletal health.

Environmental and management stressors

Heat stress is among the most powerful disruptors of acid/base balance in poultry. Elevated ambient temperature induces panting, increasing respiratory CO₂ loss and rapidly reduces pCO₂, leading to respiratory alkalosis (Bottje and Harrison, 1985; Toyomizu et al., 2005). Acute heat exposure elevates blood pH and progressively depletes HCO3, with limited capacity for renal compensation during sustained stress (Toyomizu et al., 2005; Beckford et al., 2020).

The downstream effects of heat‑induced alkalosis are multifaceted. Reduced HCO3 availability limits calcium carbonate formation in the shell gland, contributing to poor eggshell quality (Hughes, 1988; Kim et al., 2024). Heat stress also alters circulating Na, K, and Cl concentrations, reflecting dehydration, renal compensation, and altered electrolyte balance (Popoola et al., 2020; Livingston et al., 2022).

Ventilation strategy and stocking density modulate the severity of heat stress and respiratory alkalosis. High stocking density exacerbates thermal load and intensifies disturbances in blood pH, blood gases, and electrolyte status (Park et al., 2018; Jhetam et al., 2024). Early‑life environmental conditions, including brooding temperature and ventilation, influence blood gas and electrolyte status in young birds and may have lasting effects on skeletal development and resilience to stress (Crespo and Grimes, 2024).

Physiological stress independent of temperature also alters acid/base balance. Experimental infusion of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) produces consistent changes in blood pH, HCO3, electrolytes, hematocrit, and plasma volume, reflecting the integrated effects of endocrine stress on respiration, renal function, and fluid balance (Olanrewaju et al., 2006; Olanrewaju et al., 2007). More recent work suggests that amino acid nutrition interacts with acid/base balance, indicating that endogenous acid production from protein metabolism contributes meaningfully to systemic pH regulation (Ibrahim et al., 2024).

Blood biomarkers as integrative indicators of health and nutrition

Blood acid/base biomarkers provide a direct window into the integrative physiological status of the bird. Measurement of blood pH, pCO₂, base excess, strong ion difference and HCO3 allows differentiation between respiratory and metabolic disturbances, while concurrent assessment of Na, K, the anion gap, and Cl reveals dietary and renal contributions to acid/base balance (Cohen and Hurwitz, 1974; Lierz, 2003; Borges et al., 2007). The anion gap, though rarely used in poultry diagnostics, integrates these measurements to identify unmeasured acids and mixed disorders and has recently been highlighted as a valuable interpretive tool across biological systems (Alter and Zha, 2025).

Point‑of‑care diagnostics

Historically, the application of blood acid/base assessment in poultry has been limited by logistical challenges associated with laboratory‑based diagnostics. However, the emergence of portable point‑of‑care platforms has transformed this landscape. Devices such as the i-STAT Alinity (600-9008-25, Abaxis, Union City, CA, United States) and VetScan VS2 (500-0041, Abaxis, Union City, CA, United States) enable rapid, on‑farm measurement of blood gases, electrolytes, hematocrit, and metabolites using small blood volumes.

Reference ranges for common haematological parameters in commercial poultry, using these portable analysers, have been published in recent years (Livingston et al., 2020; Adams et al., 2022; Ripplinger et al., 2022; Ruiz-Jimenez et al., 2022; Zaelsakova et al., 2025) and generally compare favourably to more conventional laboratory-based analysis (Ruiz-Jimenez et al., 2021). These reference ranges are a critical prerequisite for the detection of derangement and clinical interpretation. Recent applied studies demonstrate that these tools can detect physiological responses to heat stress, dietary electrolyte, amino acid, energy, Ca and P manipulation, vitamin supplementation, and management interventions in real time (Livingston et al., 2022; Cowieson et al., 2024; Crespo and Grimes, 2024). By providing immediate physiological feedback, point‑of‑care diagnostics allow veterinarians and nutritionists to move beyond formulation targets and performance outcomes toward physiology‑driven decision‑making. Whole blood was analyzed using point-of-care diagnostic platforms, including i-Stat (Abbot Point of Care Inc., Princeton, NJ, USA) and the VetScan2 Chemistry Analyzer (Abaxis Inc., Union City, CA, USA)). For i-Stat analysis, lithium-heparinized whole blood was loaded into Chem8+ cartridges within 10 min of collection. Cartridges were stored refrigerated at 3–8°C and allowed to warm to room temperature before use while remaining unopened. Approximately 100 µL of whole blood was used to fill each cartridge to the minimum fill line, after which the cartridge closure was secured and the sample was analyzed. Individual i-Stat analyses required approximately 2–3 min per sample.

For VetScan2 analysis, lithium-heparinized whole blood was analyzed within 60 min of collection. Avian/reptile rotors were stored refrigerated at 3–8°C and used the same day after removal from refrigeration. The rotors were used within 20 min of pouch opening and loaded into the analyzer within 2 min of sample introduction. Approximately 100–150 µL of whole blood was added to each rotor, with care taken to avoid air bubbles. Each VetScan2 analysis required approximately 12–15 min per sample.

Sampling sessions were organized to ensure that device-specific processing windows were met, particularly the 10-min limit for i-Stat analysis and the 60-min limit for VetScan2 analysis. Blood analysis was performed by trained technicians, while necropsy observations and sample metadata were recorded concurrently to maintain linkage between bird level clinical observations, body weight, and blood biomarker results.

Future directions

Measurement of blood biomarkers in commercial poultry only becomes useful when reliable reference intervals are available to allow the detection of derangement. Since 2020, there have been several papers published (cited herein) that provide normal ranges for broilers, turkeys and ducks. Haematology reference intervals for laying hens, pullets and breeders require further examination. As reference ranges become available, more research is needed to explore the diagnostic opportunities offered by this novel information. Whilst there are some published papers showing blood acid/base complications arising from e.g. heat stress or dietary electrolyte imbalances, there are surprisingly few showing effects of viral or bacterial diseases (enteric, renal or respiratory) on such blood parameters. Randomised controlled trials to validate the influence of various common poultry diseases on blood biomarkers would help validate such approaches for field veterinarians. A similar approach in nutrition would be logical to more comprehensively establish the plasticity of blood biomarkers to dietary inadequacy e.g. vitamin deficiency, toxin presence or macronutrient imbalance. Finally, whilst measurement of blood biomarkers under field, or experimental, settings, is undoubtedly of value, full data integration would logarithmically increase the opportunity space. Integration of live production data, processing plant data, hatchery settings, feed formulation details and market economics, with on-farm portable metabolomics, will yield insights that will significantly enhance the decision-making strategy of commercial poultry operations. Once such integrated data lakes are available, overlaying supervised machine-learning and artificial intelligence is then an obvious next step.

Conclusions

Acid/base balance is a central physiological nexus linking diet, environment, and health in poultry. Decades of research demonstrate that dietary salts, electrolyte balance, heat stress, ventilation, stocking density, and physiological stress profoundly influence blood pH, electrolyte distribution, and calcium availability. Despite this, direct assessment of blood acid/base status remains underutilized in commercial poultry production. Integrating blood biomarker measurement, particularly through point‑of‑care diagnostics, offers a powerful opportunity to translate extensive physiological knowledge into improved health, welfare, and productivity in modern poultry systems.
