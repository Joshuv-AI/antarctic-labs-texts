Body roundness index mediates the association between high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and diabetic retinopathy: a population-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Wuping Xu, Xuedong Xu, Min Zhang, Chiping Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the association between high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and diabetic retinopathy (DR) in adults with diabetes, and to explore the mediating role of body roundness index (BRI).MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, a total of 1658 participants with diabetes from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2005–2008 were included. This specific period was selected as the first NHANES cycle to systematically include fundus photography for diabetic retinopathy assessment. The diagnosis of DR was based on fundus images evaluated using the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) severity scale. HDL-C levels were measured using Roche Diagnostics. BRI was calculated based on height and waist circumference. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess the relationship between HDL-C and DR, and mediation analysis was performed to evaluate the potential mediating effect of BRI.ResultsThe fully adjusted model revealed a significant positive association between HDL-C and DR (OR = 2.08 95% CI: 1.34 ~ 3.22, p = 0.007). In subgroup analyses, positive associations between HDL-C and DR were still observed across various subgroups (including gender, race, smoking status, alcohol use, hypertension history, hyperlipidemia, weight status, and age group). Mediation analysis indicated that BRI played a significant mediating role in the relationship between HDL-C and DR, with a mediation effect of 21.51%.ConclusionsAmong adults with diabetes in the United States, elevated HDL-C is positively associated with DR, partially mediated through BRI.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02331-w.Keywords: High-density lipoprotein, Cholesterol, Diabetic retinopathy, Body roundness index, NHANES

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a significant public health concern, closely associated with the increasing prevalence of diabetes mellitus globally. This condition is characterized by chronic microvascular damage resulting from various risk factors, including persistent hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, inflammation, and changes in vascular endothelial cell function [1, 2]. Current interventions, such as laser therapy and anti-VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) treatments, primarily focus on symptom alleviation rather than addressing the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms driving DR progression [3, 4]. As such, these modalities are primarily applicable in the later stages of the disease and are insufficient for preventing further progression of DR, highlighting the critical need for innovative preventive strategies and therapeutic approaches aimed at interrupting DR development at earlier stages.

High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL-C) plays a crucial role in cardiovascular health through reverse cholesterol transport, facilitating the movement of cholesterol from peripheral tissues back to the liver. HDL-C is recognized for various biological functions that provide protection against cardiovascular diseases and help maintain endothelial function [5]. Recent studies have revealed a complex relationship between HDL-C levels and DR. While some research indicates a protective role of HDL-C against retinal vascular injury, emphasizing its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, other studies have reported a paradoxical association where elevated HDL-C levels are associated with increased odds of DR among patients with diabetes [6]. A recent US NHANES analysis showed an inverted U-shaped association between HDL-C and DR, with both low and high levels linked to increased DR risk [7]. These findings indicate that the HDLC–DR relationship is not strictly linear and may vary depending on population characteristics, glycemic control, and assessment methods. This discrepancy raises important questions regarding the heterogeneity and functional roles of HDL-C under different pathological conditions, suggesting that the impact of HDL-C on DR might significantly depend on additional factors such as glycemic control and systemic inflammation [8].

The Body Roundness Index (BRI) is a comprehensive anthropometric measure that combines height and waist circumference to assess visceral adiposity, demonstrating superior predictive ability for metabolic disorders compared to traditional measures like BMI [9]. Studies have shown an inverse relationship between BRI and HDL-C levels, with higher BRI values associated with lower HDL-C concentrations through inflammatory pathways and altered lipid metabolism [10, 11]. Research has also demonstrated that elevated BRI increases diabetic retinopathy risk through systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and endothelial dysfunction [12, 13]. We selected BRI over other anthropometric measures because of its superior correlation with metabolic parameters and predictive value for diabetic complications [14]. However, the mechanisms through which HDL-C affects DR in conjunction with BRI are not well understood and require further research [15].

The objective of this study was to investigate the correlation between HDL-C levels and DR among adults with diabetes, utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted between 2005 and 2008. This specific period was selected because it represented the first NHANES cycle to include systematic fundus photography for diabetic retinopathy assessment with standardized imaging protocols and comprehensive data availability for all key study variables. Additionally, the study aimed to analyze and quantify the mediating effect of BRI on the relationship between HDL-C and DR.

Methods

Study population and data collection

This cross-sectional study recruited eligible participants from NHANES, a comprehensive research initiative that represents the health and nutritional profiles of the U.S. population across both adult and pediatric demographics [16]. NHANES encompasses a wide range of inquiries, including demographics, socioeconomic factors, dietary patterns, and various health-related issues. Its systematic design and methodologies facilitate robust data collection, with all findings being publicly accessible, thereby enhancing transparency and enabling further research iterations [17]. The NHANES study conducted from 2005 to 2008 involved 20,498 individuals, among whom 5,704 had their fundus images captured. The 2005–2008 cycle was specifically chosen because: (1) it was the first NHANES cycle to systematically include fundus photography for diabetic retinopathy assessment; (2) standardized imaging protocols using the Canon CR6-45 NM ophthalmic digital imaging system were established during this period; (3) complete data for HDL-C, anthropometric measurements for BRI calculation, and comprehensive covariates were most consistently available; and (4) the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) severity scale was systematically applied by experienced graders at the Eye Epidemic Reading Center of the University of Wisconsin, Madison. The criteria for diagnosing diabetes included self-reported diagnoses, use of insulin or antidiabetic medications, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥6.5%, oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) ≥11.1 mmol/L, or serum glucose (Glu) ≥7.0 mmol/L. Due to NHANES database limitations, specific type 1 vs. type 2 diabetes classification was not systematically available. Based on clinical characteristics (age at diagnosis, insulin use patterns), approximately 95% of participants had type 2 diabetes. Rationale for inclusion: We included all diabetes because both share common microvascular complications including diabetic retinopathy, and the vast majority represent type 2 diabetes, minimizing potential confounding effects. All criteria adhered to the standards set by the American Diabetes Association in 2019 [18]. Individuals without diabetes diagnosis (n = 3,938), as well as those with incomplete HDL-C data (n = 58) and missing BRI data (n = 61), were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1). The research protocol received ethical clearance from the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics; informed consent was also acquired from all participants involved in the study [19].

Assessment of DR

Fundus photography protocol

Digital fundus photographs were obtained using the Canon CR6-45 NM ophthalmic digital imaging system equipped with Canon EOS 10D camera through non-pharmacologically dilated pupils. Two 45-degree images were captured per eye: one centered on the optic disc and one centered on the macula, following NHANES standardized protocols [20].

DR grading and assessment

All fundus photographs were evaluated at the Fundus Photograph Reading Center, University of Wisconsin-Madison, by certified ophthalmic technicians who had undergone specialized training in diabetic retinopathy assessment according to standardized protocols. The grading was performed using a modification of the Airlie House classification system, which is an established standardized method for population-based diabetic retinopathy assessment that provides specific criteria for identifying and grading retinal lesions.

ETDRS severity scale classification

DR severity was classified according to the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) severity scale as follows: (1) No apparent retinopathy (ETDRS level 10): no abnormalities; (2) Mild non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR, ETDRS levels 20, 35): microaneurysms only; (3) Moderate to severe NPDR (ETDRS levels 43–53): microaneurysms plus retinal hemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton wool spots, or other retinal lesions; (4) Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR, ETDRS levels 61–85): presence of neovascularization, fibrous proliferation, or preretinal/vitreous hemorrhage. For this analysis, DR was dichotomized as no DR versus any DR (including all grades of NPDR and PDR).

Quality control

Standardized image quality assessment protocols were implemented, with inter-grader reliability maintained through regular calibration sessions and duplicate grading of a subset of images to ensure consistency in DR assessment.

Assessment of HDL-C and BRI

Serum concentrations of HDL-C were assessed using the polyethylene glycol (PEG) oxidase method on the Hitachi 717 and Hitachi 912 platforms (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, Indiana).as well as on the Roche Modular P Chemistry OptiPlex 7070 SFF MLK Analyser (Diagnostic Roche).

The BRI is a comprehensive anthropometric index that combines height and waist circumference measurements to assess visceral adiposity, selected for its superior predictive ability for metabolic disorders. The data for these measurements were obtained from the participants’ medical examination documents. To ensure accurate findings, trained health technicians at the mobile medical examination center obtained body measurements, assisted by recorders during the assessment. [21–23]. Before the measurements were conducted, participants were asked to remove their clothing and shoes. The participants’ height was recorded as standing height, and waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the lower ribs and the upper edge of the hips. The BRI was calculated using a model that combines height and waist circumference to evaluate visceral fat content. A higher BRI indicates more visceral fat accumulation [24, 25]. The exact mathematical formula is as follows: 364.2–365.5×(1-[WC (m)/2π]2/[0.5×height(m)]2) ½.

Covariates

Data were acquired through a combination of questionnaires, physical assessments, and laboratory evaluations. Standardized questionnaires yielded information regarding participant demographics, such as sex, age, ethnicity, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), and educational attainment. PIR is calculated by dividing total family income by the poverty threshold for the family size and survey year as defined by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. Additionally, laboratory analyses of blood specimens were conducted to quantify levels of Glu, HbA1c, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), total cholesterol (TC), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Furthermore, data concerning the presence of hypertension, overweight, hyperlipidemia, duration of diabetes, diabetic nephropathy, and history of stroke were also gathered.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using weighted means±standard errors for continuous variables and frequencies (%) for categorical variables. NHANES uses a complex, multistage sampling design; therefore, all analyses incorporated MEC examination weights and accounted for strata and primary sampling units (PSUs) to produce nationally representative estimates and valid standard errors. When two survey cycles were combined, WTMEC2YR was divided by 2 in accordance with NHANES guidance [31], and weighted estimates were interpreted as representative of the U.S. population based on U.S. Census 2000 population counts. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for smaller subgroups (n < 50) and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for larger samples. Based on normality test results, group comparisons utilized independent t tests for normally distributed variables or Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables, while categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Prior to multivariable logistic regression analysis, multicollinearity among potential covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs); variables with VIF > 10.0 were considered to have problematic multicollinearity and were excluded. Multivariable logistic regression assessed the association between HDL-C and DR with hierarchical adjustment: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (demographics: gender, age, race), and Model 3 (additional clinical covariates: smoking status, alcohol use, overweight, hyperlipidemia, diabetes duration, Glu, HbA1c, BUN, Cr, TC, and CRP). HDL-C was analyzed continuously and as tertiles, with trend tests for dose–response relationships. Subgroup analyses tested interactions. Mediation analysis was employed to investigate the mediating role of BRI in the relationship between HDL-C and DR, decomposing the total effect into direct and indirect effects. The indirect effect was quantified via (1) the effect of HDL-C on BRI (linear regression) and (2) the effect of BRI on DR (logistic regression), and its significance was assessed using bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations) to derive 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportion mediated was calculated as (indirect effect/total effect) × 100%. Data processing and analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Zstats (version 0.90; developed by Professor Zheng Weijun’s team at Zhejiang University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, China). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Given the substantial missingness in medication-category indicators, medication variables (antihypertensive medications, lipid-lowering agents, and antidiabetic drugs) were not included in the primary models. We additionally performed sensitivity analyses by further adjusting for these medication variables, with missing medication information coded as “No” (non-use).

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

The weighted characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. The study included 1,658 participants, of whom 39.7% had DR. The DR group had a significantly longer duration of diabetes and higher levels of BUN, Cr, waist circumference, and HDL-C compared to the non-DR group (p < 0.05). In contrast, the DR group showed lower levels of Glu, CRP, eGFR, and BRI. Additionally, the DR group had a higher proportion of males, current smokers, and overweight individuals, while having a lower prevalence of hypertension and hyperlipidemia (p < 0.05). There were no significant differences in age, PIR, HbA1c, TC, TG, LDL-C, BMI, race, education, alcohol use, diabetic nephropathy, or stroke prevalence between the groups (p > 0.05).

Association between HDL-C and DR

Prior to model construction, comprehensive collinearity assessment was performed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all potential covariates. Variables with VIF > 10 were considered to have significant collinearity and were excluded from multivariable models to ensure model stability. The initial assessment identified high collinearity between eGFR and Cr (VIF = 12.3), TG and TC (VIF = 11.8), LDL-C and TC (VIF = 13.2), BMI and overweight status (VIF = 15.6), and waist circumference and BRI (VIF = 14.9). These variables were therefore excluded from the multivariable models but included in baseline characteristics for descriptive purposes. Multicollinearity diagnostic using VIF analysis revealed that all variables included in the final multivariable models had acceptable VIF values (all < 10.0), with the highest VIF observed for HbA1c (VIF = 4.2) and creatinine (VIF = 3.8). No variables exceeded the VIF threshold of 10.0, therefore no variables were excluded due to multicollinearity concerns, confirming the appropriateness of including all variables in our final models. The multiple logistic regression analysis showed a significant positive link between high HDL-C levels and DR (Table 2). This association remained statistically significant across all models: Model 1 with no covariates (OR = 1.81, p = 0.003), Model 2 adjusted for gender, age, and race (OR = 2.35, p < 0.001), and the fully adjusted Model 3 (OR = 2.08, p = 0.007). Compared to the lowest HDL-C tertile, the highest tertile was linked to a significantly higher odds of DR, and this trend was significant across all models (P for trend < 0.001). Subgroup analyses (Table 3) showed a generally significant positive association between HDL-C and DR across various categories, including gender, race, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension history, hyperlipidemia, age groups, and weight status. Weight status subgroup analysis revealed that the positive association between HDL-C and DR was significant in both non-overweight individuals (OR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.19–4.48, p = 0.014) and overweight individuals (OR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.13–3.44, p = 0.018), with a numerically stronger association observed in non-overweight individuals. No significant interactions were found between these subgroups (P for interaction > 0.05), including weight status (P for interaction = 0.629), indicating that these factors do not significantly alter the association between HDL-C and DR.

Our analyses also included a thorough investigation of the relationships between BRI and DR, as well as between HDL-C concentrations and BRI (Table 4), providing the statistical foundation for our mediation analysis. BRI showed a consistent negative association with both DR and HDL-C in all models. The logistic regression analysis demonstrated a significant negative association between BRI and DR across all adjustment models, with the odds ratio for the association between BRI and DR being 0.84 (95% CI: 0.78–0.91, p < 0.001 in the fully adjusted model), while the beta coefficient for HDL-C and BRI varied from −1.11 to −1.94, depending on the model used. These findings establish both pathways required for mediation analysis: the BRI→DR pathway and the HDL-C→BRI pathway.

Mediation analysis of BRI

This study investigated the mediating role of BRI in the relationship between HDL-C and DR (Fig. 2). After adjustments in Model III, the mediation analysis indicated a significant indirect effect of BRI on the total effect of HDL-C on DR (β = 0.0281, 95%CI: 0.01 ~ 0.04, p < 0.001), accounting for 21.51% of the total effect. To verify the stability of our mediation findings across different HDL-C levels, we performed sensitivity analysis within HDL-C tertiles. The mediation effect remained significant and consistent across all tertiles: lowest tertile (18.32%, 95% CI: 0.01 ~ 0.05, p = 0.034), middle tertile (21.81%, 95% CI: 0.01 ~ 0.04, p = 0.012), and highest tertile (23.10%, 95% CI: 0.01 ~ 0.05, p = 0.018). This consistency confirms that our mediation findings are robust across the HDL-C distribution and not driven by specific HDL-C ranges (Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, after additional adjustment for medication variables (antihypertensive medications, lipid-lowering agents, and antidiabetic drugs), the mediation results remained materially unchanged; the corresponding sensitivity analysis is presented in Fig. S1.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study analyzed NHANES data from 2005 to 2008, which was specifically selected as the first NHANES cycle to include systematic fundus photography for diabetic retinopathy assessment with standardized protocols. Our findings revealed a significant positive association between HDL-C levels and DR, representing a paradoxical relationship that can be explained through several interconnected mechanisms: (1) HDL particles become pro-inflammatory rather than anti-inflammatory due to glycation and oxidation processes, losing their protective endothelial functions [32]; (2) The body attempts to mobilize cholesterol for membrane repair and anti-inflammatory responses in response to chronic inflammation and vascular damage in DR [33]; (3) Patients with diabetes exhibit impaired reverse cholesterol transport and reduced endothelial protection, particularly affecting retinal microvascular beds which are vulnerable to HDL dysfunction [34]. This aligns with literature suggesting a complex interplay between lipid profiles and vascular health in diabetic populations, as previously reported by Wong et al. and Cardoza et al. [35–38]. However, contrasting evidence exists: Tan et al. found no significant association between HDL-C and DR in European patients [39], while Sacks et al. reported protective effects in certain populations [40]. These differences may be attributed to: (1) ethnic variations in HDL metabolism and DR pathogenesis [41]; (2) diabetes duration and glycemic control differences affecting HDL function [42]; (3) confounding factor adjustment differences, particularly regarding inflammation markers and body composition [43].

The relationship between HDL-C and diabetic complications shows tissue-specific patterns. While HDL-C generally maintains protective effects against cardiovascular disease in patients with diabetes, its role in microvascular complications is more complex. Research indicates that HDL-C remains protective against macrovascular events but may have neutral or detrimental effects on diabetic nephropathy and retinopathy [44], suggesting that retinal microvascular beds are particularly susceptible to HDL dysfunction due to unique blood-retinal barrier properties and limited regenerative capacity.

Subgroup analyses further demonstrated this association across various factors, including gender, race, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension history, hyperlipidemia, different age groups, and weight status. The implications of these findings indicate that these factors do not significantly alter the underlying relationship between HDL-C and DR. Studies by Amin et al. and Agroiya et al. [45, 46] corroborate our findings, highlighting that dyslipidemia often exacerbates microvascular complications and endothelial dysfunction within diabetic cohorts. This is critical, as compounded risk factors can amplify the severity of diabetic complications, as emphasized by Kurtul et al. [47], who discussed lipid profiles in conjunction with microvascular outcomes. Wang et al. [48]. have indicated that increased levels of TC and non-HDL cholesterol among patients with diabetes are linked to more severe forms of DR. This assertion is supported by Tóth et al. [49], who found that non-HDL-C is a sensitive marker for predicting cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk, especially in patients with diabetes, highlighting the relevance of lipid profiles in understanding the risk of microangiopathies, including DR. However, there is contrasting evidence suggesting that high HDL-C levels may not directly correlate with DR severity [50–52], underlining the contention that further investigation is necessary. Focusing on lipid management within diabetic populations, Romero-Aroca et al. [53] elucidate that hyperlipidemia can induce endothelial dysfunction, thereby facilitating DR progression.

Our mediation analysis reveals that BRI partially mediates the HDL-C-DR relationship (21.51% of total effect) through several interconnected pathways: (1) Visceral adiposity and systemic inflammation: Higher BRI reflects increased visceral adiposity, promoting systemic inflammation through pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6), which alter HDL composition and reduce anti-inflammatory properties [54]; (2) Insulin resistance pathway: BRI-associated insulin resistance affects both HDL metabolism (reducing HDL-C levels and altering particle composition) and retinal microvascular integrity through advanced glycation end products and oxidative stress [55]; (3) Adipokine-mediated effects: Body fat distribution influences adipokine production (reduced adiponectin, increased resistin), affecting both HDL-C levels and DR through inflammatory mediators and endothelial dysfunction [56]. This mediation effect indicates that body composition serves as a crucial modulator in the lipid-retinal relationship in patients with diabetes. Interestingly, we discovered a negative association between BRI and both DR and HDL-C, necessitating further exploration into how these dynamics shape one another. Such observations may indicate that higher BRI values might reflect a state of metabolic dysfunction, potentially counteracting any purported protective effects of HDL-C on retinal vascular integrity. Multiple studies highlight the multifaceted nature of lipid profiles and their relation to DR. While HDL-C is traditionally viewed as cardioprotective, its abnormal accumulation in certain contexts could increase the odds of microvascular complications, as noted by Lou et al. [57]. The interaction between glycemic control and lipid metabolism further complicates this relationship: studies like those by Romero-Aroca et al. have established a robust association between glycemic control and the onset of diabetic complications, including DR.

It is important to note the limitations of this study. Firstly, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal conclusions regarding the association between HDL-C and DR. Our findings represent associations rather than causal relationships, and the odds ratios should be interpreted as measures of association rather than relative risks. Although mediation analysis is vital, it is important to emphasize that findings derived from a cross-sectional study are inherently correlational and do not establish any causal links. Secondly, although we have adjusted for several important potential confounders, unmeasured confounding variables may also affect the results. Medication use is an important potential confounder. Although medication-category indicators had substantial missingness, we conducted complete-case sensitivity analyses with additional adjustment for antihypertensive medications, lipid-lowering agents, and antidiabetic drugs, and the results were consistent with the primary analyses (Supplementary Tables S3–S5 and Fig. S1). Nevertheless, residual confounding and potential selection bias due to missing medication information cannot be fully excluded. Future studies with more complete medication data would be valuable to address this important limitation. Additionally, our study was limited to the 2005–2008 NHANES cycle, which, while being the first to systematically include fundus photography for DR assessment, may limit the generalizability of our findings to more recent populations. However, this period was chosen specifically for its standardized DR assessment protocols and comprehensive data availability for our key variables. What’s more, we used a binary classification for weight status rather than the more detailed WHO BMI categories (normal, overweight, obese), which may have limited our ability to detect dose-response relationships between different degrees of excess weight and diabetic retinopathy. Finally, the study population comprised solely adults with diabetes in the United States, necessitating cautious extrapolation to broader, more diverse populations, highlighting the need for extensive international research efforts to validate these findings.

In summary, our findings contribute to an expanding body of evidence suggesting that elevated HDL-C levels in diabetic individuals may not confer protective benefits against diabetic retinopathy as previously assumed [58]. Moreover, mediating factors such as body fat distribution play a pivotal role in this complex relationship. Our findings suggest that patients with diabetes with elevated HDL-C levels (particularly > 1.35 mmol/L) may require more frequent ophthalmologic surveillance. The mediating role of BRI indicates that body composition management should be prioritized alongside traditional glycemic control, with interventions targeting visceral adiposity reduction potentially providing dual benefits for lipid profile optimization and DR prevention. These findings support the development of personalized risk stratification tools incorporating both HDL-C and BRI measurements for enhanced diabetic retinopathy screening and management. Future research should continue to unravel these intricate dynamics, particularly regarding improving management approaches for diabetic retinopathy through strategic cholesterol and body composition assessments.

Conclusions

A significant positive correlation exists between HDL-C levels and DR in adults with diabetes, with BRI partially mediating this relationship. Future longitudinal studies are crucial for validating these results and examining the mechanisms involved.
