Pharmacogenomics in everyday practice: realities after Bupa’s DNA-based medication screening
Authors: Shahbaz Shakil, Mahira Ijaz, Aman Kumar Shah
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

Bupa’s recent decision to offer DNA-based medicine screening has made pharmacogenomics (PGx) a topic that more people are interested in. This step is a big step forward for personalized medicine, which is now becoming a reality in the clinic. However, as we enter this new era, it is important to think carefully about the moral and practical problems that need to be solved before PGx can be safely and fairly used in everyday life. This article is compliant with the TITAN Guidelines 2025 governing the declaration and use of artificial intelligence tools in research and publication[1].

The most exciting thing about PGx is that it could change how prescriptions are written. By looking at a person’s genes, doctors can better guess how they will react to many common drugs, from heart drugs to other medicines. This can make recommending drugs less of a trial-and-error process, lower the chance of bad drug effects, and make treatment more effective, all of which can make the person safer and get better care[2].

Even though this is possible, there are still big problems that need to be solved before it can be widely used. A lot of medical practitioners do not have the specific education needed to comfortably read complicated PGx test observations and use individuals to make therapeutic choices. Additionally, incorporating that information smoothly into medical records as well as clinical procedures presents a significant administrative and technical personnel challenge that remains unresolved in the majority of medical facilities[3].

Some people think it is wrong to do PGx testing a lot. It is important to have clear rules about who owns data, how private it is, and whether it is okay to discriminate based on genetics. As soon as their insurance starts to pay for these tests, people should be able to get them. If PGx does not have a clear plan and only gives personalized treatment to people who can pay for it[4], some people may get sicker.

In conclusion, Bupa’s DNA screening program is an important move because it forces healthcare professionals to deal with the practical implications of pharmaceutical genome sequencing. To get the most out of this equipment, it is essential to do more than just work on distinct tasks. We need to put our heads together in order to fill in any deficiencies in doctors’ knowledge, technological facilities, and legal tracking so that every person is able to utilize personalized medical care.
