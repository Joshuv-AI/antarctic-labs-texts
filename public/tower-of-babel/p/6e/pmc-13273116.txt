LySeqST: a targeted sequencing assay for robust genomic classification of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma

ABSTRACT
Key Points•The LySeqST targeted sequencing panel can be used for accurate genomic classification of DLBCL tumors.•Application to a population-based cohort reveals subtype proportions and outcomes that will support clinical trial design.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is a heterogeneous disease with respect to both molecular features and clinical outcomes. The biological basis underlying this heterogeneity has been evaluated through gene expression profiling (GEP), which identifies cell-of-origin (COO) and dark zone signature (DZsig) subgroups associated with distinct biology and variable response to standard-of-care R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone)–based chemoimmunotherapy.1, 2, 3, 4 More recently, genomic profiling through targeted capture or whole-exome sequencing (WES) has demonstrated additional biological heterogeneity not fully captured through GEP.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Despite the variation in classification systems, major subgroups have consistent core features and relationships to GEP subgroups, outcomes, and potential biological vulnerabilities.11,12 Genomic profiling of DLBCLs has been explored as a means to stratify patients for subtype-guided therapy to target vulnerabilities unique to each subtype, and preliminary prospective and retrospective data have demonstrated promise.13,14

Two tools, LymphGen7 and DLBclass,8 have been designed for sample-by-sample classification compatible with clinical workflows. Although WES remains the gold standard approach for both classifiers, smaller targeted sequencing panels can reduce the costs associated with sequencing and data storage. However, few existing panels cover all genes required for high-fidelity classification. We designed the LySeqST targeted sequencing panel specifically to capture all genes relevant to LymphGen classification and to include biomarkers relevant to other mature B-cell lymphomas.15 Here, we present validation of LySeqST for LymphGen and DLBclass in an unselected population-based DLBCL cohort,3 demonstrating the potential robustness of well-designed targeted panel sequencing for DLBCL classification.

Methods

Validation approach

Three approaches were used to evaluate the performance of the LySeqST assay for LymphGen and DLBclass. First, for in silico evaluation, the published WES variant calls from the National Cancer Institute (NCI) cohort5,7 were subset to the target regions for LySeqST or other targeted capture panels. Classification was performed together with published array comparative genome hybridization (CGH) copy number variant (CNV) and structural variant (SV) calls. To benchmark different approaches to CNV calling, NCI cohort exomes were processed with the PureCN and CNVkit CNV callers, and LymphGen calls generated with each set of CNVs were compared with those generated with the array CGH CNVs.

Second, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) evaluation tested the impact of including noncoding aberrant somatic hypermutation (aSHM) regions in LymphGen classification. To identify aSHM regions that influence LymphGen classification, the list of LymphGen features was extracted from the outputs of LymphGen run on the WGS cohort (supplemental Table 1),16, 17, 18 without inputting CNVs or calling the A53 class. Feature frequency was compared between complete WGS data and WGS subset to the LySeqST coding regions only. Features that occurred in at least 10% of genomes and whose frequency decreased by at least 10% in the coding-only subset were considered likely to be important aSHM features. The boundaries of each region affected by aSHM were defined according to results from Arthur et al.16 Variant calls from the WGS cohort were then subset to LySeqST capture space with or without these noncoding aSHM regions, and LymphGen results from each subset were compared. CNV calls were derived from Battenberg or ControlFREEC and SVs from break-apart fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH).

Third, real-world validation was performed by sequencing a population-based cohort of newly diagnosed tumors of DLBCL morphology with the LySeqST panel.3 A subset of these was also subjected to WES. CNV calls were derived with CNVkit and SVs from break-apart FISH. Library construction, sequencing, and analysis

Libraries were constructed, captured, and sequenced as previously described.17 The sequencing panel coordinates are described in supplemental Table 3. After alignment, quality control was performed with GATK,19 and exomes with <50× or LySeqST with <150× coverage were excluded. In the WGS and real-world validation cohorts, somatic variants were called with the SLMS-3 ensemble variant calling approach (supplemental Methods).20 Refined COO subtypes were generated from the nanoString DLBCL90 assay2, 21 and used to subdivide the EZB LymphGen class into EZB-DZsig-POS or EZB-DZsig-NEG, which were previously labeled EZB-MYC+ and EZB-MYC−.2,3 All statistical analyses were performed in R, version 4.1.3, and statistical tests were corrected for multiple testing as necessary. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by The University of British Columbia/BC Cancer Research Ethics Board (H21-01197).

Results

In silico evaluation of the LySeqST feature space

The in silico evaluation measured the theoretical performance of the LySeqST panel by subsetting the NCI cohort WES variant calls to the covered regions. This analysis used the published array CGH CNV calls to specifically measure feature space sufficiency for mutations, which make up most features used by LymphGen. As expected, the LySeqST subset reproduced the complete WES classifications with 100% accuracy (Figure 1A; supplemental Table 4), demonstrating that the LySeqST feature space is complete with respect to mutational features. The 128-gene LySeqST panel outperformed both the 523-gene Illumina TrueSight Oncology (TSO-500) and the 417-gene MSK-IMPACT Heme22 panels (supplemental Figure 1). Although not designed specifically for DLBclass, most top features used by DLBclass are represented in the panel (supplemental Table 5). We next compared the impact of 2 different CNV calling approaches, PureCN and CNVkit, compatible with exome or targeted sequencing on LymphGen classification (Figure 1B). Each approach resulted in few changes to LymphGen assignments, with both leading to a slight increase in the rate of A53 classification, but the neither tool was significantly more accurate at calling the A53 class (CNVkit, 92.6%; PureCN, 91.1%; McNemar test, P = .7). A comparison of the CNV features used by LymphGen reveals that both methods identify several key events with relatively high accuracy (supplemental Figure 3). However, exome-based methods had poor sensitivity for homozygous deletions (including CDKN2A) and each identified several potential artifacts at a higher frequency than in the array CGH data.

WGS evaluation of LySeqST aSHM regions

Although the LymphGen algorithm includes synonymous variants associated with aSHM as important features, LymphGen was originally trained and tested only on variants occurring in coding space, and the NCI cohort mutation data exclude all variants occurring outside of coding exons. However, this approach and typical exome panels inadequately capture the variants that are introduced by subtype-specific aSHM, which may contribute to accurate subtype assignment. We explored the effect of extending the LySeqST panel to include noncoding regions affected by aSHM of genes that are existing LymphGen features. First, to identify key aSHM features, LymphGen classification was performed on the complete WGS or coding-only variant calls from 278 DLBCL tumors, and feature use was tallied for each run. In total, 12 features occurred in ≥10% of DLBCL genomes and had a ≥10% decrease in frequency in the coding-only analysis (supplemental Figure 4A). The LySeqST target space was then extended using the boundaries of these aSHM regions defined based on the analyses presented in Arthur et al.16 The effect of including these regions was evaluated by subsetting the WGS variant calls to this modified LySeqST capture space with coding and noncoding regions. With only LySeqST-captured coding variants, LymphGen left 30.9% of tumors unclassified (“Other”) vs 28.4% with the extended capture space or 27.7% with complete WGS data (Figure 1C; supplemental Table 7). The MCD, BN2, and ST2 groups, which all have several key features associated with aSHM (eg, PIM1, BTG1, BTG2, CD83), are classified at a higher rate when noncoding variants are retained. A tally of mutation counts across aSHM regions confirms the enrichment of these features within specific LymphGen subtypes (supplemental Figure 4B-C). These results demonstrate the effect of including noncoding aSHM regions in targeted sequencing panels designed for LymphGen classification.

Real-world validation of LySeqST vs WES

To validate the real-world performance of the LySeqST panel, we applied it to 445 samples from our previously published population-based DLBCL cohort (Table 1; supplemental Table 2),3 of which 120 samples were also subjected to WES to emulate the sequencing strategy used for the LymphGen training cohort. The median-corrected coverage across target regions was 118× for exomes and 416× for LySeqST (supplemental Figure 5). Comparing LymphGen calls from WES to coding-only LySeqST variants yielded an accuracy of 77.5%, with >80% balanced accuracy for all major classes except A53 (supplemental Figure 6A). Consistent with the in silico validation, the complete LySeqST panel including noncoding regions results in a lower rate of samples classified as Other (30%) relative to WES (32.5%; Figure 2A; supplemental Table 8). The frequency of tumors classified as Other was increased when restricting LySeqST to coding variants and decreased when noncoding aSHM variants were added to WES from matched LySeqST data (supplemental Figure 6). Consistent with the WGS data, aSHM of each region was strongly associated with specific LymphGen subtypes (supplemental Figure 7A), and tumors that were only classified by the complete LySeqST panel were also enriched for coding mutations associated with that class, for example MYD88L265P in tumors reclassified from Other to MCD (supplemental Figure 7B). By DLBclass, LySeqST was overall 67.5% accurate relative to WES, with balanced accuracy of >80% for all classes except C4, for which LySeqST is missing several key features (supplemental Table 5), and the CNV-driven C2 class, highlighting the challenges associated with relying on CNV calling for robust genomic classification (supplemental Figure 8A; supplemental Table 9).Figure 2.Real validation of the LySeqST panel. Population estimate of LymphGen subgroup distribution and outcomes

Within our unselected population-based cohort, BN2 and EZB occur less frequently and ST2 more frequently than previously estimated, whereas MCD, N1, and A53 occur at similar frequencies as projected in Wright et al7 (Figure 2B). The expected associations with refined COO classification are preserved, with MCD and A53 enriched in ABC, ST2 and EZB enriched in GCB, and most DZsig-POS tumors classified as EZB (Figure 2C). DLBclass calls generated with LySeqST have an increased rate of classification of C3 and the CNV-driven C2 class and a decrease in C1 and C5 compared with Chapuy et al8 (supplemental Figure 8A-B; supplemental Table 8). Given the high accuracy of LySeqST for DLBclass demonstrated through the in silico evaluation (supplemental Figure 2) and the broad consistency of classifications assigned through WES vs LySeqST in the validation cohort (supplemental Figure 8A), these frequencies are only partly accounted for by technical differences between LySeqST and WES.

Among the 445 tumors with LySeqST sequencing, 314 were from R-CHOP–treated patients and included in outcomes analyses and, as in previous publications,7 the EZB subgroup was subdivided into DZsig-POS and DZsig-NEG (also known as MYC+ and MYC−) based on GEP. Two-year time to progression and overall survival were significantly inferior in patients with EZB-DZsig-POS tumors relative to several other subgroups; however, no other pairwise comparisons of LymphGen classifications showed a significant difference in outcomes (Figure 2D-E; supplemental Figure 9A-B). Multivariable Cox models confirm that the poor outcomes in the EZB-DZsig-POS group are independent of the International Prognostic Index components (supplemental Table 10). Although these outcomes differ from some previously published, they closely mirror those found in the application of LymphGen to R-CHOP–treated patients in a population-based cohort from the United Kingdom.23 There was also no association with outcomes in any group except EZB-DZsig-POS when considering all patients regardless of treatment (supplemental Figure 9C-D). Similarly, there was no association with any DLBclass subgroups and outcomes, although the C3 group, which includes most DZsig-POS tumors (supplemental Figure 8C), has the lowest 2-year survival (supplemental Figure 8D-E). Within the subset of patients with tumors classified as GCB or Unclassified by COO, expression of DZsig was associated with inferior outcomes among both EZB and non-EZB classified tumors (supplemental Figure 10A-B). Furthermore, although limited by small numbers, EZB-DZsig-POS tumors were associated with inferior outcomes regardless of the presence of concurrent MYC and BCL2 rearrangements (supplemental Figure 10C-D).

Discussion

This study demonstrates the robust performance of the LySeqST targeted sequencing panel for genomic classification of DLBCL. Through both theoretical benchmarking and real validation of the panel, we have demonstrated that LySeqST is complete with respect to mutational features required for accurate LymphGen classification and performs very consistently with WES. Although not specifically designed for DLBclass, LySeqST covers most required features and is comparable to WES for this classification.

The application of LySeqST to an unselected population-based cohort reveals the population frequencies of each LymphGen and DLBclass subtype and the outcomes after R-CHOP therapy. Recent studies have established different distributions in other populations, such as in South Korea and China,10,24 which should be considered when designing global trials. Furthermore, the consistent association of DZsig expression with inferior outcomes demonstrates the importance of including GEP in classification to identify high-risk patients within the EZB subgroup.

Although the validation cohort was population based, the requirement for sufficient TNA for sequencing and the minimum coverage thresholds imposed may have introduced some bias among the subset of tumors with LySeqST sequencing. However, there were no significant differences between the selected patients and the complete cohort with respect to any clinical variables, such as extranodal biopsies, which are typically associated with smaller biopsies and inferior outcomes, suggesting that the selection criteria did not introduce significant bias.

Our study highlights CNVs as a key challenge associated with genomic classification in DLBCL. Because CNVs are difficult to identify consistently and vary dramatically depending on the CNV calling approach, there is significant uncertainty surrounding the A53 LymphGen and C2 DLBclass subtypes, which are primarily defined by TP53 mutations and broad arm- and chromosome-level CNV features. Given the challenges associated with CNV calling and the lack of biological uniformity of the CNV-driven classes,25 it may be advantageous to emphasize genomic classes and classification approaches that are agnostic to CNVs.

Overall, this study confirms the robustness of targeted panel approaches to genomic classification. Population-based frequencies and outcomes associated with each subgroup will be valuable for the design of future clinical trials of subtype-guided therapy.
