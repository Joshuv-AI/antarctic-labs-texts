Arthroscopic Anatomical Glenoid Reconstruction Using Iliac Crest Autograft With Double End-Button Fixation Technique
Authors: Kazuha Kizaki, Masahiro Ikezu, Shintarou Kudo, Takanori Kubo, Masazumi Hirata, Soshi Uchida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArthroscopic anatomic glenoid reconstruction is increasingly recognized as an effective surgical technique for addressing significant glenoid bone loss associated with shoulder instability. This technical note provides a step-by-step guide for performing arthroscopic anatomic glenoid reconstruction using an iliac crest autograft with double TightRope end-button fixation (Arthrex, Naples, FL). The procedure is conducted through 3 arthroscopic portals: anteroinferior, anterosuperior, and posterior. Bone tunnels on the glenoid undersurface are created using a Transglenoid Drill Guide (Arthrex). A tricortical autograft measuring 20 mm in length and 10 mm in width is harvested from the ipsilateral iliac crest. Two bone tunnels are prepared in the graft to accommodate 2 TightRope and DogBone constructs (Arthrex). The graft is introduced via the extended anteroinferior portal, passing through a partially dissected rotator interval while the integrity of the subscapularis tendon is preserved. It is then securely fixed to the anterior glenoid rim using double TightRope end-button fixation, with 2 DogBones positioned on the posterior glenoid rim. The procedure is completed with a Bankart repair, fully covering the graft. This all-arthroscopic technique offers several advantages, including minimal invasiveness, avoidance of hardware-related complications, preservation of subscapularis tendon integrity, and reliable bone graft union with the iliac crest autograft.

Anterior shoulder dislocation with significant glenoid bone loss exceeding 13.5% presents treatment challenges, particularly for high-risk competitive athletes.1 Although arthroscopic Bankart repair yields satisfactory results, its ability to ensure shoulder stability in contact sports athletes remains debatable.2 Glenoid augmentation techniques, such as Latarjet and bone block procedures, provide rigid fixation and increased glenoid surface area, thereby providing effective shoulder stabilization.3

Arthroscopic anatomic glenoid reconstruction (AAGR) is gaining popularity because of its streamlined surgical steps compared with arthroscopic Latarjet procedures. Wong et al.4 have been instrumental in advancing the adoption of AAGR globally. Among graft fixation methods, end-button fixation has comparable strength to screws, reducing the risk of screw-related complications.5 Iliac crest autografts, recognized for their superior union rates, have the potential to reduce future resorption and expedite the return to sports after AAGR.

AAGR is primarily indicated in cases with recurrent anterior shoulder instability associated with significant glenoid bone loss, typically exceeding 13.5%, measured in reference to the best-fit circle method using 3-dimensional (3D) computed tomography (CT) imaging. Biomechanical studies have shown that glenoid defects exceeding 13.5% compromise joint stability, particularly during abduction and external rotation. Other indications include failure of previous soft-tissue stabilization procedures, such as Bankart repair, and active patients engaged in high-risk activities or contact sports (e.g., judo, rugby, or football) in which the risk of recurrent dislocation is elevated. Overhead athletes with shoulder dislocation are suitable candidates because AAGR provides a satisfactory range of motion compared with the Latarjet procedure.6

AAGR is contraindicated in patients with advanced glenohumeral osteoarthritis, active infection in the shoulder, poor bone quality (e.g., severe osteoporosis), or neuromuscular disorders that predispose to shoulder instability (e.g., brachial plexus palsy or uncontrolled seizure disorders). In addition, a thorough evaluation of patients’ adherence to postoperative rehabilitation protocols, together with the avoidance of high-risk activities during the early phases of healing, is essential.

Surgical Technique

Patient Positioning

The patient is placed in the lateral decubitus position under general anesthesia, with a 30° posterior tilt to align the glenoid parallel to the ground. A pneumatic limb positioner is used to maintain 30° of arm abduction (Fig 1, Video 1).

Portal positioning (outside view) for arthroscopic anatomic glenoid reconstruction using an iliac crest autograft with a double TightRope end-button fixation technique for right shoulder instability. The patient is placed in the lateral decubitus position with the right shoulder superior, tilted posteriorly by approximately 30°, such that the glenoid surface is nearly parallel to the ground. (AIP, anteroinferior portal; ASP, anterosuperior portal; PP, posterior portal.)

Portal Placement

Bony landmarks (clavicle, scapular spine, acromion, acromioclavicular joint, and coracoid process) are marked preoperatively. Three portals are established: posterior portal (PP), anterosuperior portal (ASP), and anteroinferior portal (AIP). The portal positions are determined based on bony landmarks and preoperative 3D CT imaging. The AIP should be set onto the coracoacromial ligament, coracoid, and conjoint tendon to avoid neuromuscular risks (e.g., lateral cord of the brachial plexus and musculocutaneous nerves). The AIP becomes a portal for graft insertion (Fig 2). The PP is aligned parallel to the glenoid, avoiding interference with the scapular spine. The AIP is located in the rotator interval above the coracoid, facilitating graft visualization. The ASP is positioned anterior to the biceps tendon on the extension line of the anterior glenoid, as referenced from 3D CT imaging.

Landmarks for anteroinferior portal in right shoulder. Viewing from the posterior portal with the patient in the lateral decubitus position, the humeral head is positioned superiorly and the glenoid is positioned inferiorly. The rotator interval is opened to ensure that the anteroinferior portal is established lateral to the coracoacromial ligament (CA lig) and the conjoint tendon. The opened rotator interval then serves as the pathway for graft insertion. Yellow arrow, future graft insertion pathway. (C, coracoid; Sub, subscapularis tendon.)

Glenoid Preparation

Working from the ASP, a traction suture is placed on the medial glenohumeral ligament at the 3-o’clock position. With the use of a radiofrequency ablator, the anterior glenoid labrum and capsule are released from the 2- to 6-o’clock position. Soft tissue is dissected from the coracoid base and anterior glenoid rim, including the coracohumeral ligament. A barrel burr smoothes the anterior rim to achieve a flat surface perpendicular to the glenoid surface.

A half-pipe guide is inserted through the PP, followed by a Transglenoid Drill Guide (Arthrex, Naples, FL). With this guide, two 3-mm cannulated drills (Arthrex) are inserted parallel to the glenoid surface 7 mm underneath the glenoid surface (Fig 3). The 2 drills remain underneath the glenoid surface while two 1.8-mm FiberTak knotless all-suture anchors (Arthrex) are placed at the 5:30 clock-face position inferior to the inferior drill and 4:30 clock-face position at the space between the 2 drills for subsequent Bankart repair.

Bone tunnels underneath glenoid surface in right shoulder. Viewing from the anterosuperior portal with the patient in the lateral decubitus position, a Transglenoid Drill Guide is introduced through the posterior portal (PP) and positioned on the glenoid surface. With this guide, two 3-mm cannulated drills (Arthrex) are advanced parallel to the glenoid surface, 7 mm inferior to the articular margin.

Graft Preparation

A 5-cm incision is made lateral to the iliac crest, posterior to the anterior superior iliac spine, avoiding the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. A tricortical bone graft measuring 20 to 30 mm in length and 10 to 15 mm in width is harvested, depending on the severity of glenoid bone loss. Drill holes are created in the graft to match the glenoid drilling pattern, ensuring a precise fit and fixation. Two TightRope sutures (Arthrex) are passed through the holes with 2 DogBone end-buttons (Arthrex), as shown in Figure 4.

Graft preparation with TightRopes and end-buttons. This procedure is performed at the back table. A tricortical bone graft measuring 20 to 30 mm in length and 10 to 15 mm in width is harvested from the ipsilateral iliac crest, depending on the severity of glenoid bone loss. In this case, a graft measuring 20 mm in length and 10 mm in width is obtained. Drill holes are created in the graft corresponding to the glenoid drill hole positions to ensure a precise fit and fixation (arrow, step 1-1). A parallel TransGlenoid Drill Guide (T) is used to create 2 holes on the bone graft for double end-button fixation (steps 1-2 and 1-3). Two TightRope (TR) sutures are passed through the holes using a suture passer (SP) (steps 2 and 3), each loaded with a DogBone end-button (E). (D, 3-mm drill; ICBG, iliac crest bone graft sized 20 × 10 mm.)

Donor Site Grafting

After graft harvest, the donor site defect in the iliac crest is filled with Osferion (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), a bioresorbable synthetic bone substitute. This step aims to minimize postoperative pain, which has been shown to be less pronounced compared with leaving the defect unfilled. To prevent injury to the iliohypogastric nerve along with the iliac crest, the use of electrocautery is minimized during the dissection and exposure of the iliac crest. Instead, meticulous sharp dissection is performed to reduce the risk of nerve damage and to preserve soft-tissue integrity. This approach enhances patient recovery and minimizes potential complications related to nerve irritation or neuroma formation.

Graft Insertion

The AIP is extended distally to allow graft passage, up to 30 mm. TightRope sutures are passed through the glenoid rim and tensioned from the PP to stabilize the graft on the anterior rim. The graft is secured with additional 2 DogBone end-buttons onto the posterior glenoid rim over the posterior capsule, providing rigid fixation (Fig 5, Video 1).

Graft fixation onto anterior glenoid in right shoulder. Viewing from the anterosuperior portal with the patient in the lateral decubitus position, the bone block (graft) is fixed onto the anterior glenoid rim, with the cortical surface of the graft precisely aligned with the subchondral bone of the glenoid. A computed tomography scan obtained at 3 months postoperatively shows that the graft surface is flush with the glenoid subchondral bone.

Bankart Repair

Two 1.8-mm FiberTak knotless all-suture anchors (Arthrex) placed at the 4:30 and 5:30 clock-face positions are used to perform Bankart repair, shifting the anteroinferior labrum and capsule superiorly (inferior-to-superior capsule shift). An additional 1.8-mm FiberTak knotless all-suture anchor at the 3-o’clock position ensures complete graft coverage and anterior shoulder stabilization (Fig 6, Video 1). The luggage tag on the medial glenohumeral ligament is secured at the 2-o’clock position using a 2.9-mm PushLock knotless suture anchor (Arthrex). A total of 4 anchors are used to complete the Bankart repair. The comprehensive surgical steps are described in Table 1 and Video 1.

An additional 1.8-mm FiberTak knotless all-suture anchor placed at the 3-o’clock position ensures complete graft coverage and anterior shoulder stabilization. Remplissage (Optional)

Remplissage is considered only when the Hill-Sachs lesion is off-track on 3D CT imaging; however, it is generally avoided in overhead athletes.

Postoperative Management

The patient is immobilized using a shoulder brace in neutral rotation for 3 weeks. Passive mobilization is initiated during the first 2 weeks, followed by a gradual progression to active-assisted and active range-of-motion exercises. Isometric exercises for the subscapularis muscle commence at 3 weeks postoperatively under ultrasound imaging to stimulate the subscapularis muscle and to verify that the graft position remains stable (Fig 7). Full active range of motion is achieved within 12 weeks.

At 12 weeks postoperatively, a CT scan is performed to assess the union of the iliac crest autograft. Once graft union is confirmed, strengthening exercises are introduced. Return to sports is determined on an individual basis and is considered after sports-specific athletic training, typically at 3 to 4 months postoperatively. Handstand performance is allowed at 3 to 4 months postoperatively for overhead athletes (Fig 8, Video 1).

(C) After confirmation of union, approximately 2 weeks of athletic training typically enables handstand performance by 3 months postoperatively (in this case, the right shoulder underwent arthroscopic anatomic glenoid reconstruction using iliac crest autograft with the double TightRope end-button fixation technique). This early functional recovery is attributed to 3 factors: (1) robust union facilitated by graft selection (iliac crest autograft) and fixation method (double TightRope and DogBone end-buttons), (2) ultrasound-guided isometric stimulation of the subscapularis tendon, and (3) the absence of range-of-motion restrictions owing to the avoidance of coracoid transfer. (A, acromion; C, coracoid; GL, glenoid; ICBG, iliac crest bone graft.)

Discussion

This article outlines AAGR using an iliac crest autograft with double TightRope end-button fixation via 3 portals (AIP, ASP, and PP). Although the arthroscopic Latarjet technique is the gold-standard technique, it demands significant surgical experience. In contrast, AAGR is more straightforward, with fewer steps for training surgeons.7

The most significant advantage of this procedure is its safe and concise execution, which is performed entirely arthroscopically. The number of portals required is identical to that of standard Bankart repair, with only minor extensions to the skin incision at the AIP for graft insertion (approximately 20-25 mm) and at the PP for the drill guide (approximately 15-20 mm).

A key limitation is the requirement for bone autograft harvesting. Although the lateral decubitus position facilitates iliac crest bone graft harvesting, this step may pose challenges when performed in the beach-chair position. Nevertheless, compared with soft-tissue repair, this technique significantly accelerates the timeline for return to sports, primarily owing to the reliable bone graft union on the anterior glenoid.

The end-button fixation technique does not require screws; therefore, hardware-related complications do not occur. The double TightRope and DogBone end-button technique offers reliable fixation rigidity owing to the broad surface on the cortical surface of the graft, minimizing the risk of graft fracture. Unlike the Latarjet procedure, this technique does not involve coracoid dissection, which may help preserve shoulder proprioception. Additionally, it avoids splitting the subscapularis tendon, allowing for early initiation of isometric and kinematic subscapularis muscle training. This facilitates a faster return to sports, particularly for activities requiring internal arm rotation, such as judo.

The use of iliac crest autograft ensures high rates of bone union. Although graft harvesting remains a drawback, Oldfield et al.8 have shown that the use of iliac crest autograft is both safe and effective.
