Community level trait patterns are detectable even along short stress gradients but not readily predictable under very limited soil resources
Authors: Dan Gafta, Silvia Oroian, Mihaela Sămărghițan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo date, the stress-dominance hypothesis has not been tested along short gradients of very limited soil resources, like those encountered on rocky cliffs and walls. Under such conditions, the plant trait structure may be primarily driven by either environmental filtering or limiting similarity, depending (among others) on the net outcome of interspecific competition. Therefore, we aimed to reveal community-wide trait patterns induced by the two opposing processes that were hypothesized to govern the assembly of 39 seral chasmophytic communities, while controlling for their regional grouping. Eleven qualitative traits were employed to test their dependency on abiotic variables and variation in their dispersion along the combined (soil nutrient/water) stress gradient. There was strong evidence of single trait dominance – environment relationships, including a significant effect of ‘Region’. The traits implying resource investment (i.e., ‘vegetative propagation’, ‘woodiness’, and ‘tuft formation’) displayed decreasing dispersion, whereas the conservative traits (i.e., ‘polyploidy’, ‘succulence’, ‘ground-lying leaf rosette’ and ‘short-life span’) showed a steady increase in dispersion along the productivity gradient. Unexpectedly, the ‘springtime blooming’ showed a declining dispersion along the mentioned gradient, while the single trait diversity associated with ‘N-fixing’, ‘underground storage organs’ or ‘reproduction via spores’ displayed no monotonic variation. Also, mixed patterns of convergence and divergence were observed for ‘woodiness’ and ‘springtime blooming’. Despite some inconsistent patterns in trait dispersion, our results demonstrate that the predictions of the stress-dominance hypothesis are applicable even along short environmental gradients, but are also highly dependent on the regional species pool and on the net outcome of interspecific competition for limited resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20321-1.Keywords: Environmental filtering, Limiting similarity, Regional species pool, Soil nutrients, Stress-dominance hypothesis, Trait dominance/DispersionSubject terms: Biodiversity, Community ecology

Introduction

The processes involved in plant community structuring can be organized into a series of hierarchical filters (i.e., dispersal limitation, environmental suitability and biotic interactions) eventually selecting the component species from the regional species pool1,2. Following the reconciliation between two opposing classical theories3,4, a relatively stable community is conceptually viewed as a set of functionally complementary and redundant species, whose relative abundances are determined by the balance between deterministic, niche-based processes (e.g., limiting similarity, facilitation, niche differentiation) and probabilistic, neutral processes (e.g., dispersal limitation, stochastic immigration or exclusion)5. Generally, the relative importance of stochastic processes and environmental constraints in community structuring decreases, while that of biotic interactions increases once soil conditions improve along the primary succession6. However, under strong abiotic filtering in harsh environments, the neutral processes – apart from dispersal limitation - may play a relatively minor role even in pioneer communities7,8.

The trait-based approach is a powerful tool for exploring community assembly rules, as selection acts on traits rather than species9. As various functional traits may respond differently to the two main filtering processes (i.e., abiotic filtering and limiting similarity), both patterns, trait convergence and divergence, can be observed within a community. Generally, environmental filtering due to strong abiotic constraints should lead to trait convergence, whereas in benign habitats, limiting similarity due to large niche overlap should induce trait divergence10,11. However, according to the revised stress-gradient hypothesis12, if the abiotic stress is strong enough and determined by a limiting resource (e.g., water or nutrients), there is a switch from facilitation to competition that could eventually induce either trait convergence or trait divergence. This is because the output of strong interspecific competition may have opposing effects on trait distribution, depending on whether the competition is symmetric or asymmetric. In the former case, niche differentiation usually leads to trait divergence (most likely in productive habitats), whereas in the latter case, the exclusion of the loser species may lead to trait convergence (most likely in harsh habitats). Contrary to the mentioned hypothesis, a transition from trait divergence to trait convergence with increasing productivity was found in some studies13–15. Based on such contradictory results as well as their low replicability, Pakgohar et al.16 pointed out the weakness of the second prediction of the stress–dominance hypothesis i.e., competitive interactions are more important in benign environments. Last but not least, stochastic events, like disturbance (e.g., stone/snow sliding off, late spring frosts) or dispersal limitation, may blur, mask or override the trait patterns induced by deterministic ecological processes17,18. In addition, the co-occurring, opposite effects of environmental filtering and limiting similarity may apparently break the trait – environment relationships and lead to observing random distribution in some traits9. This situation may occur, for instance, when the effect of facilitation on certain traits is cancelled out by the strong abiotic filtering19.

To our knowledge, the stress-dominance hypothesis has never been tested before in chasmophytic communities, which develop in very harsh edaphic conditions associated with cliffs, crests or rock outcrops. Such rocky habitats are extremely inhospitable given a series of particular features, among which very steep slopes, low water and nutrient availability, either intense or scarce direct solar radiation, high susceptibility to wind and rain erosion, lack or presence of very shallow soil20. In fact, even inconspicuous, feeble geomorphic processes (e.g., grain flow, needle ice creep or runoff) can limit plant colonization and development21–23. Therefore, the chasmophytic vegetation is usually sparse, fragmented, species-poor and usually represents early successional stages of lithoseres. The floristic composition of such communities is directly related to the quantity of soil accumulated in crevices24,25.

Plants growing in such extreme habitats must cope with multiple physiological constraints and therefore, have developed specific adaptations such as powerful roots for greater anchorage and higher efficiency in water and nutrient uptake, multiple shoots or tussocks for debris trapping and water flow interception, underground metamorphic storage organs like rhizomes, bulbs and tubers26–28, development of symbiotic relationships with nitrogen-fixing bacteria29. Succulent stems and leaves, ground-lying leaf rosettes and cushion/tuft forms represent also adaptations for water preservation30,31 and interception from fog, dew and pluvial/snowmelt runoff32. In particular, highly specialized ferns have developed desiccation tolerance through the development of mechanisms for the control of water content33,34. Many chasmophytes are polyploids and often more resilient to high stress levels, which positively impacts their survival and persistence rates35,36. Finally, a common strategy among these species is to enable early flowering/sporulation in spring and quick seed/spore maturation31,37, to shorten the full life cycle and complete it before the usual summer drought38,39.

Based on the presumptions of the mentioned theories, we hypothesized that the assembly of chasmophytic communities is mainly governed by the interplay between environmental filtering and limiting similarity, depending on the level of the abiotic stress and the trait considered, and to a lesser extent by stochasticity. To test such a hypothesis, we aimed at answering the following questions: (i) how does the dominance of species traits within chasmophytic communities respond to variation in natural site conditions and soil fertility? and, (ii) how does the strength of trait under-/over-dispersion change along the latent stress gradient of soil resource (water/nutrient) availability? For this purpose, we analysed a series of chasmophytic vegetation relevés ascribed to two distinct syntaxa (seral stages), while controlling for the regional grouping of communities in the Eastern and Southern Carpathians. Depending on the nature of the species traits (acquisitive or conservative), we expected to reveal positive or negative monotonic responses in trait dominance (and conversely, in trait dispersion) along the (short) successional - productivity gradient determined by soil water and nutrient availability.

Materials and methods

Study areas

The disjoined study areas consist of two low mountain valleys (Sadu and Mureș) that are spatially separated about 155 km from one another (Fig. 1). The Sadu Valley is located in the central part of the Southern Carpathians, on the north-eastern sector of Parâng Mountains, lying between Cibin and Lotru Mountains. Sadu Valley is about 45 km long and runs from southwest (upstream) to northeast (downstream). The Mureș Gorge is situated in the Eastern Carpathians and is flanked by the Călimani Mountains in the north and the Gurghiu Mountains in the south. The gorge stretches 42 km from east (upstream) to west (downstream). The elevation range is wider in Sadu Valley (450–940 m) compared to Mureș Valley (500–650 m).

Geographic location of the two study areas (Mureș valley and Sadu valley) within the Eastern and respectively, Southern Carpathians (background satellite imagery retrieved from Google Earth Pro v7.3.6.10201 for Windows, https://www.google.com/earth/about/versions/#download-pro).

The geological formation of Sadu Valley is composed mostly of crystalline metamorphic rocks, mainly represented by mica-schists and paragneisses40. The Mureș Gorge cuts through various types of vulcanite (andesites, pyroclastic breccias and tuffs), along with intrusions of conglomerates and sandstones41.

Both study areas experience a moderate continental climate, with peak rainfall and temperatures in June and July, respectively. At similar elevations (630–640 m), the monthly means of maximum and minimum temperatures are very similar in the two valleys (Supplementary Fig. S1a online). Similarly, the monthly mean precipitation levels are alike, although Mureș Valley receives slightly more summer rainfall than Sadu Valley (Supplementary Fig. S1b online). Especially in winter, thermal inversions are rather frequent and persistent on the bottom of both valleys, as evidenced by the altitudinal distribution of the forest vegetation42,43.

The chasmophytic vegetation in the study areas has developed on the steep, rocky slopes of the two valleys and is currently represented by plant communities of Sempervivetum heuffelii Schneider-Binder 1969 (hereinafter Sh) and Asplenio trichomanis-Poëtum nemoralis Boşcaiu 1971 (hereinafter AP). The latter represents a slightly more advanced seral stage on siliceous rocky cliffs, which is reflected in the higher vegetation cover and is mainly determined by the higher amount of soil and humus accumulated in/on the underlying crevices/ledges.

Data collection

A total of 39 relevés of chasmophytic vegetation were gathered in order to reach – as far as possible – a balanced representation by region (Mureș and Sadu valleys) and by plant community type (14 of Sh and 25 of AP). Except for 20 original relevés performed recently in the Mureș valley, the rest of 19 relevés from the Sadu valley were retrieved from Schneider-Binder44,45 and Drăgulescu42 (see Supplementary Table S1 online). Within each valley, the sampled sites were not spread more than 2–3 km one from another, so that the assumption of no dispersal limitation may be presumably accomplished, given the long-distance dissemination of the spores/light seeds of the pioneer fern/herbaceous vascular species colonizing low-mountain rocky cliffs and walls. The values of topographic variables (elevation, slope, and aspect) at each site were also recorded/retrieved along with the abundance scores of all vascular species. In addition, the indicator values for nutrients were collected from Chytrý et al.46, for most of the 145 plant species, and from Sârbu et al.47 for the few subendemic taxa.

A series of morphologic, karyologic, physiologic, and life-history traits, which were retained relevant in the context of plant species composing chasmophytic communities, were selected for inferring the effects of abiotic filtering or limiting similarity along the mixed, water – nutrient stress gradient underlying the coenocline developed from the more xerophilous-oligotrophilous Sh coenoses to the successionally, slightly more advanced AP communities (Table 1). In particular, we employed only categorial (relatively stable), response traits that are more sensitive to environmental changes48 and display low intraspecific variation compared with their interspecific variation49, at the expense of estimating a limited resolution of the trait dispersion. Given the microenvironmental differences between the two community types under study, we expected to observe signatures of increase or decrease in single trait dispersion depending on the dominant ecological processes that either filter out (divergence trend) or, respectively, promote (convergence trend) that plant attribute (Table 1). Instead, similar monotonic variations due to regional factors were presumed to be less important, given the little climatic differences between the Mureș and Sadu valleys (Supplementary Fig. S1 online).

The data concerning the species biological traits were retrieved from different published and online sources i.e., Săvulescu et al.50, Sârbu et al.47, and the databases Biolflor51, Pladias46 and FloraVeg.EU52. Only two plant taxa had missing data, specifically regarding their ploidy type. The mean temperature and precipitation data concerning two topographically similar sites (Stânceni – 640 m a.s.l. in Mureș Valley and Râu Sadului – 630 m a.s.l. in Sadu Valley) were retrieved from Weather Spark database (https://weatherspark.com).

Data conversion and summarisation

Species cover scores (estimated on Braun-Blanquet’s scale) were converted to percentages by referring to the corresponding cover class midpoints. The compositional dissimilarity between relevés was assessed by employing the percentage difference, also known as Bray-Curtis coefficient (function vegdist() in ‘vegan’ library). For complying with the Euclidean axioms, the resulting distances were squared-rooted prior to their input in various analyses.

The potential annual direct incident radiation and heat load were estimated by using the latitude, slope and aspect at each site as input variables in appropriate equations53. As an independent predictor, the terrain aspect was converted into a linear variable ranging from 0 (the coolest) to 2 (the warmest) by employing Beers et al.54 equation: A′ = cos (45° − A) + 1, in which A represents the measured aspect in degrees and 45° corresponds to the coolest aspect in continental Europe i.e., north-east. The level of soil fertility at each site was inferred by computing the mean of species indicator values for nutrients corresponding to each relevé, but excluding the generalist taxa.

Each selected trait was expressed through a binary variable corresponding to either presence or absence of the target feature in each plant taxon. To fully describe the single trait response along environmental gradients, Ricotta and Moretti55 advocated the estimation of both the mean and dispersion of the trait values. As a surrogate of the community weighted mean to be applicable for binary traits, we defined a community-wide importance value (hereinafter called trait dominance) that was calculated as the product between trait relative frequency and the relative cover sum of species featuring that trait. The dominance index can vary between 0 (no species features the target trait) and 1 (all species share the target trait). Instead, the dispersion of trait states was estimated at community level by computing the Rao’s quadratic entropy (function dbFD() in ‘FD’ library) based on pairwise, square-rooted Gower distances; the latter are appropriate for categorical data and can be represented in a Euclidean space. The standardized Rao’s Q index (weighted by species relative cover) was estimated separately for each single trait, as the multiple assembly processes, which operate simultaneously to structure plant communities, could be masked by a multivariate trait index56.

Numerical analyses

The constrained ordination of the relevés was conducted through distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) by using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index (function dbrda() in ‘vegan’ library). A forward selection of predictors (soil nutrient level, elevation, aspect, slope, and region) of the pairwise distances was performed in advance by multivariate linear testing (based on 9999 free permutations of species in the community matrix), to achieve the best model in terms of AIC (function ordistep() in ‘vegan’ library). Multicollinearity was low in the retained model since the variance inflation factors ranged between 1.13 and 1.62, which is far below the maximum acceptable threshold of 10 (function vif.cca() in ‘vegan’ library). The significance of the first two ordination axes was estimated through ANOVA permutational test (function anova.cca() in ‘vegan’ library). The pure and shared, relative contributions of predictors were assessed by partitioning the variation in compositional dissimilarities via db-RDA (function varpart() in ‘vegan’ library).

Unconstrained, distance-based redundancy analysis was employed to explore the trait correlation structure and to estimate the amount of functional redundancy in the species space defined on the basis of the complement of Sørensen similarity (function dbrda() in ‘vegan’ library).

Generalized linear models (GLMs) were applied to estimate the effects of region and abiotic variables on the dominance of each trait separately at the community level (see Supplementary Table S2 online). In order to achieve the most parsimonious models, a manual backward selection procedure of explanatory variables along with Lasso estimation and AICc validation was employed. Whenever useful to improve model diagnostics, square-root, log or exponential transformation of explanatory environmental variables were applied on a case-by-case basis (see Supplementary Table S2 online). Subsequently, the final models were retained only after additionally testing for significance all paired, interaction terms and quadratic terms involving the previously selected predictors. Their relative importance was assessed by dependent resampled inputs and reported as singular (pure) effects.

In order to detect signatures of single trait convergence/divergence, a large number (9999) of Rao’s Q values were simulated for each community under the null hypothesis of no species-trait relationship. The simulations consisted in randomising the trait states across species, while the species matrix was kept unchanged (T1 algorithm in Götzenberger et al.57). All traits were shuffled together so that the trait correlations were preserved. In addition, the null model was run independently within each region so that the differences in species pool were still reflected in the null distribution of trait states. The (left-sided) probability associated with the real Q value in each community was estimated based on the absolute frequency (n) of simulated values being equal to or lower than the observed one, as follows: p=(n + 1)/(9999 + 1). Since the null distributions of Rao’s quadratic entropy was strongly skewed in most cases, we followed the procedure proposed by Lhotsky et al.58 to use probit-transformed p-values as estimates of effect size (ES) i.e., the strength of trait dispersion (function probitlink() in ‘VGAM’ library). An envelope of non-significant ES values around null was built by reference to the probit-transformed values of 0.05 and 0.95 probability. Consequently, negative ES values smaller than − 1.645 point to trait under-dispersion or convergence (functional diversity lower than that expected under null assembly), whereas positive values larger than + 1.645 indicate over-dispersion or divergence (greater functional diversity than that expected under null assembly). Conversely, small ES values within the mentioned envelope denote random distributions of trait states, possibly due to neutral processes. The whole procedure outlined above was repeated for each trait and region separately.

Finally, to assess the importance of local and regional environment in driving the presumed trends in trait dispersion, generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) was employed to fit the ES corresponding to each single trait against the relevé scores on the db-RDA axis 1, while accounting for the inherent correlation determined by the compositional resemblance within region (function glmmTMB() in ‘glmmTMB’ library). All these GLMMs were conducted assuming a Beta response distribution and applying the probit link function, as the dependent variables were composed of untransformed p-values associated with the observed Rao’s quadratic entropies in different communities. The quadratic term of the main predictor (i.e., the ordination scores) was also tested in each model, but it always resulted non-significant.

A critical level of 5% of the alpha-probability was employed in all the standard statistical tests. Apart from the normality tests and GLM analyses that were conducted in JMP Pro v18.0.1 (https://www.jmp.com/en/software/predictive-analytics-software), all the others were performed in base R v4.4.2 environment (https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/) with additional support from the packages ’vegan’59, ’FD’60, ’glmmTMB’61 and ’VGAM’62.

Results

Floristic composition variation along environmental gradients

The two local species pools shared only 22.7% of the total plant taxa, while more than a half (i.e., 57.9%) were exclusively recorded in the Mureș valley.

The compositional variation was significant along both ordination axes (F = 12.246, p < 0.0001 for axis 1; F = 2.960, p = 0.0082 for axis 2), which translated in sharp clustering of the relevés by community type along the first axis and, by region along the second axis (Fig. 2). Except for slope, all other environmental variables were significantly or at least marginally related with the plant species composition of the study communities (Table 2). While the terrain aspect and soil nutrients were mostly responsible for the floristic variation between community types i.e., along the first axis, the region and elevation explained most of the compositional variation along the second axis (Fig. 2). Overall, the level of soil nutrients explained most of the compositional variation (13%), but shared almost half of its contribution with the slope aspect (Fig. 3). The region was the second most important explanatory variable (5%), which also shared almost half of its contribution with site elevation (Fig. 3).

Trait correlation structure

The topological disposal in species ordination space did not reveal a clear differentiation between the traits expected to increase or decrease their dispersion between the two seral communities (Fig. 4). Nevertheless, there were conspicuous positive correlations between ‘vegetative propagation’ and ‘underground storage organs’ as well as between ‘N-fixing’ and ‘woodiness’ attributes. Overall, the set of traits considered herein displayed low redundancy, as the proportion of variance explained by the first two db-RDA axes was only 30% (Fig. 4).

Trait dominance – environment relationship

Except for polyploidy, the dominance of all traits was conditioned on the level of soil nutrients, but in different ways. The dominance of ‘vegetative propagation’, ‘tuft forming’ and ‘woodiness’ increased with soil fertility, while the opposite was true for ‘N-fixing’, ‘ground-lying leaf rosette’, ‘reproduction via spores’, ‘springtime blooming’, ‘storage organs’, ‘succulence’ and ‘therophytic life-form’ (Table 3). The dominance of no less than six traits i.e., ‘vegetative propagation’, ‘N-fixing’, ‘polyploidy’, ‘ground-lying leaf rosette’, ‘reproduction via spores’ and ‘storage organs’, declined significantly toward higher elevations (Table 3). Increasing heat load determined a significant dominance increment of ‘N-fixing’, ‘polyploidy’, ‘ground-lying leaf rosette’ and ‘springtime blooming’ (Table 3). The dominance of ‘reproduction via spores’, ‘storage organs’ and ‘succulence’ increased, while that of ‘woodiness’ declined steadily, from lower to higher aspect-transformed scalar. The dominance of the ‘tuft forming’ attribute decreased once the potential solar radiation became weaker, and vice-versa. The dominance of ‘underground storage organs’, ‘succulence’ and ‘therophytic life-form’ increased with slope steepness. Apart from ‘springtime blooming’ and ‘woodiness’ that were not significantly affected, the dominance of all other traits was strongly influenced by region and precisely, higher values were observed in Mureș valley than in Sadu valley (Table 3). Overall, the strongest effect size on trait dominance was exerted by soil nutrient level and region.

Trends in trait dispersion along the main stress gradient

Thirty-four (i.e., 87%) out of the 39 studied communities displayed non-random dispersions (either convergence or divergence) with respect to at least one of the eleven traits considered. Nonetheless, the percentage of communities showing significant over- or under-dispersion in single traits was low, varying between 0% (for N-fixing) and 35.8% (for vegetative propagation) (Fig. 5).

Four traits (‘woodiness’, ‘vegetative propagation’, ‘tuft forming’ and ‘springtime blooming’) showed a significant decrease in dispersion effect size along the db-RDA axis 1, representing a convergence trend (Fig. 5d, g, i, k). Conversely, other four traits (‘succulence’, ‘polyploidy’, ‘therophytic life-form’ and ‘ground-lying leaf rosette’) displayed a significant increase along the main successional/productivity gradient, suggesting a divergence trend (Fig. 5a, c, e, h). The rest of the traits (‘N-fixing’, ‘storage organs’, and ‘reproduction via spores’) did not vary monotonically along the main RDA axis (Fig. 5b, f, j). Few instances of mixed patterns of convergence and divergence were observed for ‘woodiness’ and ‘springtime blooming’ toward the more favourable end of the combined stress gradient, that is in a few AP communities (Fig. 5d, k).

Discussion

Trait dominance and the regional species pool

The compositional distinction between the two studied plant assemblages was clearly determined by differences in the availability of nutrients, water and light, since the Sh communities appeared more oligotrophilous, xerophilous, and heliophilous than their AP counterparts. Within each community type, the floristic differentiation between the two valleys was induced by the higher elevations of slopes in Sadu Valley and, probably, by the dissimilar geological substrate (volcanic versus metamorphic). The latter supposition is based on the acknowledged differentiation that the edaphism can induce in flora and vegetation63. Unexpectedly, the identity of geographic region (expressed through the two Carpathian valleys) also had a major contribution to explaining the dominance of traits. Despite the higher taxonomic distinctiveness and diversity of its chasmophytic species pool, the Mureș valley hosts communities displaying higher dominance of conservative/stress-adaptive traits, as compared to Sadu valley. This is probably determined by the poorer (volcanic) substrate but higher geodiversity in the Mureș valley.

Trait dominance and topographic conditions

The topoclimatic differentiation between the lower, middle, and upper sections of slopes may explain the relative importance of elevation in controlling the dominance of all conservative traits, except for ‘succulence’. The biological features related to resource acquisition (i.e., ‘N-fixing’, ‘ground-lying leaf rosette’), nutrient storage, asexual reproduction (via sprouting or spores) and resistance to cold/drought stress (i.e., ‘ploidy’) were preferentially selected at lower elevations, because the base of rocky cliffs is usually the steepest, devoid of soil and frequently affected by thermal inversion (including early/late frosts) and persistent fog. In such inhospitable habitats, the basal leaf rosette may play an important role in intercepting the organic debris carried by rainwater flow22 and capturing water drops from fog32, whereas N-fixing is an important adaptation to low moisture conditions in that nitrogen investments in photosynthesis can buffer the water deficit stress29,64.

Slope inclination per se had a positive effect on the dominance of traits concerning the development of both storage organs (succulent stems/leaves and specialised underground organs) and short but fast ontogenetic cycle. The co-occurrence of annuals and succulent plant species indicates contrasting strategies that allow drought avoidance and tolerance, respectively65. The non-negligible relative effect of the slope aspect on the dominance of several traits was very likely due to the topoclimatic asymmetry between the main slopes of the two valleys, given their geographical orientation. As reported for alpine meadows lying on south-facing slopes66, higher potential solar radiation was positively associated with the dominance of conservative traits regarding resource preservation (‘succulence’ and ‘underground storage organs’), nutrient capture (‘tuft forming’) and low-cost reproduction (via spores), very likely because stronger insolation leads indirectly to increased evapotranspiration. In this regard, the caespitose plants have the advantage of being able to capture a small amount of the pluvial/snowmelt runoff. Also, the spores of many chasmophytic ferns have developed desiccation tolerance through the thick spore walls that prevent dehydration of cells33,34. On the contrary, the increasing dominance of ‘woodiness’ with decreasing insolation on more shaded slopes is linked to slower carbon gain/growth but longer life-span and persistence under low light availability67.

The physiological stress, related to increasing heat load on sunny cliffs, promoted traits that induce springtime flowering and enhance resource acquisition (e.g., N-fixing), water capture (via the ground-lying leaf rosette), and resistance to drought (e.g., polyploidy). The early flowering is part of some plants’ strategy to benefit from the wetter spring season and grow rapidly68, and to set their seeds before July when the summer water deficit becomes limitative. Although the heat load and nutrient indicator value were not significantly correlated, their negative confounding effect on the dominance of the ‘springtime flowering’ trait suggests that litter decomposition and humus accumulation are slowed down on warmer and drier slopes. In other words, flowering in spring is limited to some extent by nutrient availability, as it requires an energetic investment that is not always sustainable.

Trait dominance and nutrient availability

Except for ‘polyploidy’, the dominance of all traits herein considered was more or less controlled by the level of soil nutrients along a natural, seral-related fertility gradient. The dominance of traits that are high resource consumers (‘vegetative propagation’, ‘tuft forming’ and ‘woodiness’) increased with soil fertility, whereas the opposite was observed in the case of the stress-adaptive (conservative) traits. Lhotsky et al.58 also observed that with increasing productivity of grasslands along a moisture gradient, the role of seeds/spores in regeneration becomes smaller, while the role of vegetative spread is growing.

Trait convergence/divergence and determinant ecological processes

Apart from ‘N-fixing’, all traits showed instances of significant under- or over-dispersion in at least one of the two seral plant assemblages, which means that the traits selected were appropriate for tracking non-neutral processes involved in community assembly. However, few communities displayed significant convergence or divergence in single trait dispersion, such patterns being more exceptions than the norm. Moreover, the idiosyncratic dispersion of ‘woodiness’ and ‘springtime blooming’ (from convergence to divergence) at the more favourable end of the environmental gradient i.e., exclusively in AP communities, is surprising, given the relative homogeneity in both species composition and habitat conditions. All these may be due to neutral processes (e.g., disturbance events), overriding effects of abiotic filtering and ecological niche differentiation, and/or the noise effect of microenvironmental heterogeneity and landscape context17. Interestingly, the dominant species (Sempervivum heuffelii and Poa nemoralis) in the communities assigned to Sh and, respectively, AP bear conspicuous traits (‘succulence’ and, respectively, ‘tuft forming’) that are promoted by habitat filtering. On the contrary, several subordinate species in terms of abundance are characterised by traits (i.e., ‘underground storage organs’ and ‘woodiness’) that are filtered through niche differentiation.

Trait dispersion variation along the stress gradient

As predicted, the traits implying resource investment (i.e., ‘vegetative propagation’, ‘woodiness’ and ‘tuft formation’) displayed decreasing dispersion with gradual mitigation of the environmental conditions, especially in terms of organic layer development and belowground moisture retention. This outcome is in accordance with the positive relationship between functional diversity and soil moisture/nitrogen in early successional, herbaceous communities developed in cold-dry climate conditions69. The convergence tendency in the trait ‘vegetative propagation’ within AP communities may have been also promoted by the presumably more intense disturbance caused by grain flow, resulting from the enhanced rock weathering by plants with a more developed root system (e.g., shrubs and cespitose graminoids). However, contrary to our predictions, the dispersion of the trait ‘underground storage organs’ did not show significant variation (i.e., decline) along the successional gradient, probably because very few species are able to develop similar organs in such extremely shallow soils.

In line with our expectations, the traits associated with the stress-tolerant - ruderal strategy adopted by species colonising rocky, water-limited and disturbance-prone habitats (i.e., ‘polyploidy’, ‘succulence’, ‘ground-lying leaf rosette’ and ‘short-life span’) showed a steady increase in dispersion along the successional gradient. In fact, as productivity increased, environmental constraints on the traits related to resource use become weaker and the coexistence of various water and nutrient use strategies become possible58. An exception to this plausible theory was the declining dispersion of the trait ‘springtime blooming’ along the positive nutrient-moisture gradient, suggesting a niche-based, not environmental filtering. Although polyploidy is usually strongly associated with vegetative reproduction70, the two traits showed opposite dispersion trends, which actually agree with our initial predictions.

Concluding remarks and limitations

Overall, there was strong evidence of single trait dominance – environment relationships and, most traits (8 out of 11) displayed a monotonic trend in dispersion, as expected along such a short, mixed stress gradient. The direction of these monotonic trends complied with the theoretical predictions in seven traits but, there were few or, sometimes no chasmophytic communities displaying convergence/divergence patterns in single traits.

The dispersion trends revealed in ‘vegetative propagation’ and ‘springtime blooming’ versus ‘succulence’ are in accordance with the concept according to which traits related to reproduction are usually subject to limiting similarity, whereas conservative traits related to the adaptation of species to abiotic stress are generally constrained by environmental filtering10,71. Although these outcomes are congruent with the predictions of the stress-dominance hypothesis, inconsistent patterns in single trait location and/or dispersion along the productivity gradient were not uncommon, despite the compositional and synecological similarity of the studied communities. In addition, the functional diversity of single traits was much influenced by the regional species pool, which is in line with the site-specific trends detected by Pakgohar et al.16. One possible explanation for such inconsistencies would be that the environmental and biotic filters act synergically on multiple traits simultaneously and therefore, trait interactions and trade-offs may also play an important role in community assembly56,72,73. Still, another source of inconsistent trends could be the uncertainty in the net outcome of interspecific competition under the strong stress determined by a limiting resource12. That adds to the low replicability of trends in trait dispersion16. Therefore, the generality of the stress–dominance hypothesis is much limited by third-party effects that make the patterns of trait dispersion dependent on the particularities of the factor inducing the stress (resource or non-resource), of sites being sampled (e.g., disturbance history, habitat heterogeneity, herbivory pressure), of the sampling design (grain size and extent), of the traits being considered (response or effect traits, regeneration or vegetative traits, acquisition or conservative traits) and of the local species pool (low or high functional redundancy)8,58,74–76. Such striking multifactorial dependence in trait dispersion renders the validation of the stress–dominance hypothesis very problematic, even on harsh environments hosting species-poor communities.

The present study has some inherent limitations related to both data availability and numerical modelling. One limitation is our reliance on a limited set of categorial, presumably invariant (response) traits at species level. Another is that the null model approach, although powerful, was not able to distinguish between pure neutral processes and null effects resulting from opposing, balanced forces (environmental filtering and limiting similarity). Nevertheless, our study has the merit of revealing signatures of trait-based assembly rules in chasmophytic communities even along short environmental gradients.
