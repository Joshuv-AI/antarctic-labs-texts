Precision Medicine for Atrial Fibrillation Recurrence in East Asia
Authors: Takeshi Aiba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Recent advancements in catheter ablation (CA) technologies and techniques have markedly improved the clinical outcomes of atrial fibrillation (AF) treatment. However, a considerable proportion of patients still experience AF recurrence following CA. Multiple clinical risk factors—including type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, alcohol consumption, chronic kidney disease, low physical activity, and heart failure—have been reported to contribute to post-ablation AF recurrence. Conversely, a subset of patients with AF, particularly younger individuals or those with a familial history, may lack these conventional risk factors and instead exhibit a primary genetic predisposition. Rare familial AF cases have been attributed to pathogenic variants in genes such as KCNA5, KCNQ1, KCNH2, SCN5A, and LMNA, which are associated with ion channelopathies or cardiomyopathies.1, 2, 3 Nonetheless, such cases remain exceedingly rare.

Most cases of AF are considered complex common diseases. Even when a genetic background is present, it typically involves genetic polymorphisms that, by themselves, exert only modest effects on AF development. Genome-wide association studies in individuals of European ancestry have identified 3 major genomic loci associated with AF: 4q25 (PITX2), 16q22 (ZFHX3), and 1q21 (KCNN3).4 Moreover, the concept of a polygenic risk score—where the cumulative effect of multiple genetic variants contributes to AF susceptibility—has been increasingly recognized.5 Thus, the genetic architecture of AF is generally polygenic or oligogenic, rather than monogenic, and reflects a multifactorial mode of inheritance. Genetic vs Acquired Factors in Occurrence of AF

In early-onset (familial) atrial fibrillation (AF), rare mutations in genes related to ion channel disorders and cardiomyopathies play a significant role. In contrast, AF that develops in middle-aged and older individuals is associated with multiple common variants (single nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs]). The ALDH2 polymorphism, which is prevalent among East Asian individuals, is considered one of the factors that increases the risk of AF recurrence after catheter ablation, particularly in patients with concurrent alcohol consumption or diabetes mellitus (DM).

As genetic polymorphisms vary across ethnic groups, the variants associated with AF risk also differ between Asian and Western populations. Several AF susceptibility gene polymorphisms have been reported in Japanese cohorts.5 Similarly, a recent genome-wide association study involving 600 Japanese patients who underwent CA for AF identified a variant in ABCC8 that enabled stratification of recurrence risk following CA.6 These findings underscore the importance of population-specific genetic data—particularly from Asian populations—for accurately assessing AF susceptibility and informing personalized risk stratification strategies.

Alcohol consumption is a well-established risk factor for the development of AF.7,8 A key enzyme involved in alcohol metabolism is the mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2), which catalyzes the oxidation of acetaldehyde to acetate. The ALDH2 gene harbors a well-characterized polymorphism, c.1510G>A, p.Glu504Lys (rs671), resulting in 3 genotypes: wild type (∗1/∗1), heterozygous (∗1/∗2), and homozygous mutant (∗2/∗2). This polymorphism is highly prevalent in East Asian populations, with approximately 40% to 50% of Japanese individuals carrying either the heterozygous or homozygous variant.9,10 In contrast, the ∗2 allele is virtually absent in Western populations, accounting for the higher prevalence of alcohol intolerance among East Asian individuals. These genetic variants significantly affect the efficiency of alcohol metabolism and may modulate individual susceptibility to alcohol-induced AF development.11

The risk of AF development associated with alcohol consumption depends on both drinking habits and the quantity consumed. However, individual differences in the ALDH2 genotype may confer varying susceptibility to AF, even among individuals with comparable levels of alcohol intake. Based on this hypothesis, a recent study investigated the relationship between AF recurrence after CA, alcohol consumption, and ALDH2 genotype in a Japanese cohort.12 The study demonstrated that, among patients with similar drinking patterns, those carrying heterozygous (∗1/∗2) or homozygous (∗2/∗2) ALDH2 variants exhibited a significantly higher rate of AF recurrence compared with individuals with the wild-type (∗1/∗1) genotype.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Jin et al13 also investigated the association between ALDH2 genotype and AF recurrence after CA in a Chinese population. According to the authors, type 2 DM and ALDH2 polymorphism remained significant modifier factors associated with AF recurrence after CA, as determined by Cox regression analysis. This study included 324 patients who underwent CA for AF, of whom 88 had type 2 DM. Given the relatively small sample size, the possibility of selection bias cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, the findings are noteworthy: among non-DM patients, no significant difference in AF recurrence was observed between those with and without ALDH2 polymorphisms, whereas DM patients carrying heterozygous (∗1/∗2) or homozygous (∗2/∗2) ALDH2 variants exhibited a markedly increased recurrence rate.

In contrast to the previous Japanese study,12 which focused on the impact of alcohol consumption, this study found that a history of alcohol intake was not a significant predictor of AF recurrence after CA. Instead, type 2 DM, ALDH2 genetic variants, and persistent AF were identified as independent risk factors. Moreover, AF recurrence risk increased in a genotype-dependent manner, with wild-type (∗1/∗1), heterozygous (∗1/∗2), and homozygous (∗2/∗2) individuals showing progressively higher recurrence rates. These results support the hypothesis that ALDH2 polymorphism may be closely linked to the prevalence of DM and diabetic cardiomyopathy.14,15

The mechanisms linking ALDH2 polymorphism to AF recurrence in patients with type 2 DM remain poorly understood. ALDH2 is involved not only in alcohol metabolism but also in the detoxification of endogenous aldehydes (eg, 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal [4-HNE]),16,17 playing a critical role in mitigating oxidative stress. Reduced ALDH2 activity may lead to the accumulation of these toxic aldehydes, potentially resulting in chronic inflammation and insulin resistance. Therefore, in the context of DM, hyperglycemia is thought to increase susceptibility to AF through mechanisms such as myocardial fibrosis, left atrial enlargement, increased atrial stiffness, impaired cardiac function, and progression of heart failure—processes likely mediated by oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. The ALDH2 polymorphism may exacerbate AF development in patients with type 2 DM by amplifying these pathological pathways and, additionally, by modulating cardiac ion channel function.18

Despite differences in study populations (eg, Japanese vs Chinese) and clinical factors such as alcohol consumption and type 2 DM, 2 recent studies12,13 consistently demonstrate that the risk of AF recurrence can be stratified according to the ALDH2 genotype. The validation of ALDH2 genotype–based risk stratification for AF recurrence across ethnically and clinically diverse cohorts represents a highly significant finding, highlighting its potential generalizability and clinical relevance.

In summary, the ALDH2 genotype has emerged as a shared genetic determinant influencing the risk of both AF and type 2 DM. Given its high prevalence in East Asian populations, ALDH2 polymorphism represents a promising biomarker for precision medicine. Incorporating genetic information into clinical decision making may enhance risk prediction, guide preventive strategies, and optimize treatment outcomes for patients with AF and type 2 DM.
