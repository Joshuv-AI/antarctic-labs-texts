Alfalfa Rotation Enhances Soil Microbial Diversity and Reduces Tilletia foetida DNA Abundance in Common Bunt-Infected Wheat Fields
Authors: Delai Chen, Guy Smagghe, Jingjing Su, Ruirui Liu, Tian Tian, Shujuan Xu, Yongqiang Yang, Shirong Ma, Wenjie Zhang, Shijuan Li, Fuhan Zhong, Ghulam Muhae-Ud-Din
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The pathogenic fungus Tilletia foetida causes common bunt in wheat, replacing grains with black spores and consequently reducing yield and quality. To assess the impact of crop rotation on soil health and pathogen dynamics, we investigated the effects of Medicago sativa L. (alfalfa) rotation on common bunt-infected wheat fields, compared with Avena sativa L. (oat) rotation. Soil eukaryotic communities were analyzed using Illumina MiSeq 18S rDNA sequencing. Additionally, soil physicochemical properties, enzyme activities (urease, phosphatase, peroxidase, and sucrase), and the abundance of T. foetida DNA were measured. Results showed that alfalfa/wheat rotation increased soil eukaryotic communities in the soil rhizosphere. The most abundant phyla were Mucoromycota, Mollusca, and Basidiomycota. While total phosphorus (TP), total nitrogen (TN), and pH remained largely unchanged, NO3−-N, NH4+-N, and available potassium (AK) levels varied, correlating with changes in enzyme activities and soil eukaryotic communities composition. Compared with diseased wheat monoculture, alfalfa rotation was associated with significantly lower soil T. foetida DNA abundance and enhanced soil enzyme activity. These findings indicate that alfalfa rotation substantially improves the soil eukaryotic community, and plays a crucial role in reducing the T. foetida DNA abundance in soil.

Wheat is an economically important staple crop cultivated worldwide, ranking third in global crop production and serving as a major source of vitamins, minerals, proteins, fibers, and carbohydrates (Jabran et al., 2023; Ibe et al., 2025; Muhae-Ud-Din et al., 2020). In China, one of the largest wheat producers, crop rotation systems have been employed to improve wheat productivity and grain quality by more than 10% (Wang et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2021). Rotations involving corn and soybeans have been reported to enhance wheat yield and water-use efficiency compared to monocropping systems (Al-Musawi et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Leguminous crops, such as alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), are widely used in rotations to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, support beneficial eukaryotic soil communities, reduce pesticide use, and improve water quality, thereby promoting plant health and environmental sustainability (Koropeckyj-Cox et al., 2021). Specifically, alfalfa-wheat rotations have been shown to enhance wheat growth and yield by reducing N2O emissions, increasing nitrogen uptake, and improving soil nutrient content (Guseynov et al., 2022; Trozzo et al., 2020). Moreover, alfalfa cultivation affects the rhizosphere microbial diversity, inducing structural changes in bacterial communities and enhancing wheat survival in soils previously cultivated with alfalfa (Wang et al., 2022).

Soil microbial communities in the rhizosphere are central to plant health, contributing to nutrient acquisition, maintaining biogeochemical cycles, and detoxifying harmful compounds (Singh et al., 2025). These microbial populations are influenced by multiple factors, including soil pH, temperature, organic matter content, chemical composition, and enzyme activities (Chernov and Zhelezova, 2020; Dincă et al., 2022). Interactions between plants and beneficial microbes can improve plant physiological development by enhancing resistance to biotic and abiotic stress, facilitating nutrient exchange, and optimizing metabolic and nutrient cycles (Muhae-Ud-Din et al., 2025). Conversely, phytopathogens can disrupt these processes, leading to reduced plant growth and grain quality (Jabran et al., 2022).

One such pathogen, Tilletia foetida, is the causal agent of common bunt in wheat, which can cause substantial economic losses worldwide by contaminating grains and reducing grain quality (Delai et al., 2024; He et al., 2022a; Poyraz et al., 2018). The disease is transmitted via both seed- and soil-borne inoculum, with teliospores from infected plants contaminating emerging seedlings (He at al., 2022b; Xu et al., 2020). Infected grains are replaced by dark-colored spores that emit a strong, unpleasant odor, severely diminishing crop quality and market value (Din et al., 2021; Goates, 2012). T. foetida teliospores can remain viable in soil for 2–3 years, posing a long-term risk to subsequent wheat crops. Disease management strategies include fungicide seed treatments and biological control using microbial agents to reduce infection and spore penetration (Aboukhaddour et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022). While chemical control can suppress the disease, it may also negatively affect plant attributes and beneficial rhizosphere microbial communities essential for wheat growth.

Infection by T. foetida alters soil properties, creating conditions favorable for pathogen germination. Soil moisture, pH, and temperature are critical factors for fungal development, with infection predominantly occurring during cooler and wetter winter and spring wheat seasons (Maurya et al., 2022). Pathogen proliferation further disrupts soil fertility and rhizosphere enzyme activities (Lee et al., 2020). Healthy soil, characterized by high organic matter, nitrogen, and phosphorus content, as well as elevated enzyme activities, is crucial for mitigating disease and supporting beneficial microbial dynamics. Studies have shown that soils under double-crop rotations exhibit higher enzyme activities and nutrient contents, which correlate with improved wheat productivity and grain quality (Wang et al., 2020). Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the role of crop rotation in shaping rhizosphere microbial communities and functional profiles, which are critical for soil health and plant disease dynamics (Dou et al., 2025). For example, rotation practices have been shown to enrich beneficial microbial taxa while suppressing pathogen prevalence by modifying soil metabolite profiles and microbial assembly processes, demonstrating rotation’s potential to influence disease-related soil functions beyond nutrient cycling (Wang et al., 2023). Although numerous studies have examined the benefits of crop rotation on wheat yield and quality, there is limited research on the effects of rotation in fields affected by common bunt. In this study, we investigated the impact of a two-year rotation with M. sativa on T. foetida-infected wheat fields. We hypothesize that alfalfa rotation may influence soil eukaryotic communities in a manner that is associated with reduced soil T. foetida DNA abundance. To test this, we analyzed microbial diversity, including alpha- and beta-diversity, using Illumina 18S rDNA sequencing. Soil physical and chemical characteristics, as well as key enzyme activities, which are urease, catalase, phosphatase, and sucrase, were also assessed to elucidate the effects of rotation on soil health and its association with lower T. foetida DNA load in soil.

This study addresses key biological questions concerning the interactions among crop rotation, soil microbial dynamics, and disease management in wheat, with implications for sustainable agricultural practices and plant pathology. By linking soil eukaryotic communities and soil functionality to reduced soil T. foetida DNA abundance, this research contributes to a mechanistic understanding of how rotational crops, such as alfalfa, may help shape soil conditions associated with reduced T. foetida DNA abundance while enhancing wheat productivity.

Materials and Methods

Site overview and sample collection

The study was conducted in the Loess Plateau of eastern Gansu Province. The soil type is black loessial soil, and the main winter wheat variety cultivated is Long Yu No. 8. Common bunt disease has been observed in this region for over 8 years, causing substantial reductions in wheat yield and grain quality. Rhizosphere soil samples were collected at a depth of 0–6 cm from wheat fields exhibiting symptoms of common bunt, which had been cultivated for one and two years in rotation with alfalfa and oat. The experimental design included five replicate soil samples per treatment, along with five corresponding replicates from the rotated plots; equal amounts of residual soil from each replicate were pooled for storage at −80°C and for downstream microbial community analyses, including high-throughput sequencing. For microbial community analysis, 10 g of rhizosphere soil was suspended in 90 mL of sterile distilled water, shaken for 10 min in a shaker incubator, and subsequently incubated for an additional 5 min. Aliquots from each treatment were then collected for high-throughput sequencing.

This sampling depth, 0–6 cm, was selected because it corresponds to the primary root distribution zone of wheat and alfalfa during the active growing stage, where root density and rhizosphere interactions are most pronounced. Previous studies have shown that the upper soil layer (0–10 cm) typically contains the highest concentration of fine roots, root exudates, and soil-borne pathogens, and exhibits elevated microbial and enzymatic activity.

Eight rhizosphere-soil treatments were evaluated: T_CK (healthy wheat rhizosphere soil; Triticum aestivum), T_T (wheat rhizosphere soil from common bunt affected field), M1R_CK (healthy 1-year-old alfalfa rhizosphere soil; Medicago sativa), M1R_T (1-year alfalfa rhizosphere soil in a field with common bunt history from the previous wheat season), M2R_CK (healthy 2-year-old alfalfa rhizosphere soil), M2R_T (2-year alfalfa rhizosphere soil in a field with common bunt history from two years prior), A_CK (healthy oat rhizosphere soil; Avena sativa), and A_T (oat rhizosphere soil in a field with common bunt history from the previous wheat season) (Table 1).

All experimental plots were managed under consistent agronomic practices throughout the study period. Fertilization type and application rates followed local standard field management guidelines and were applied uniformly across treatments. Irrigation frequency and timing were consistent across plots and were determined by regional agricultural practices and crop growth requirements. Although alfalfa and oats have different nutrient demands, management practices were standardized to minimize confounding from differential fertilization or irrigation inputs. No synthetic fungicides were applied during the experimental period.

18S rDNA sequencing of soil eukaryotic communities

Soil eukaryotic community DNA was extracted from 2 g of soil suspension using the HiPure Soil DNA Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA was purified using the Agencourt AMPure XP Nucleic Acid Purification Kit. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer based on the 260/280 nm absorbance ratio. The 18S rDNA region was amplified using primers EF4 (5′-GGAAGGGR[G/A] TGTATTTATTAG-3′) and EF3 (5′-TCCTCTAAATGACCAAGTTTG-3′) to capture a broad range of fungal taxa (Anderson et al., 2003; Smit et al., 1999). PCR amplification conditions were initial denaturation at 94°C for 3 min (1 cycle), followed by 30 cycles of 94°C for 1 min, 48°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 3 min, with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min.

Illumina Miseq PE300 sequencing

PCR products were further amplified with Illumina overhang adapters. Amplicons were pooled, run on 1% agarose gel, and purified using the Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The prepared DNA libraries were subjected to paired-end 300 bp sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform.

Quantitative analysis of T. foetida in soils

Absolute quantification of T. foetida was performed using real-time PCR. Primer specificity was confirmed in silico against NCBI databases and experimentally using positive and non-target fungal controls. DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Soil DNA Kit. Primers for qPCR were F: 5′-TCACTTCAAGGTCGTTCCCG-3′ and R: 5′-CGGGTCGAGGGGCGTAAACTTGA-3′. PCR conditions were: 94°C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. Amplification products were verified on 1% agarose gel, and target fragments were recovered. Real-time PCR was performed in triplicate on all DNA samples. Gene copy numbers per μL of DNA were converted to copies per gram of dry soil, taking into account DNA extraction volume and soil dry weight. Pathogen abundance is reported as gene copies per gram of dry soil (copies g−1). Copy numbers were calculated from the standard curve and normalized for DNA extraction yield and the dry weight of the soil used for extraction. A melt curve analysis was performed after amplification to confirm the specificity of the PCR products, and a single peak was observed for all reactions. A standard curve was generated using 10-fold serial dilutions of plasmid DNA containing the target Tilletia foetida fragment, ranging from 108 to 101 copies μL−1. The standard curve exhibited a strong linear relationship between Ct values and log-transformed copy number (R2 = 0.998). Mean values of technical replicates were used for statistical analysis.

Soil physical and chemical properties and enzyme activities

Soil samples were sieved through a 2-mm mesh to remove debris and roots. Physical and chemical properties measured included pH, soil organic carbon (SOC), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), total potassium (TK), available phosphorus (AP), available potassium (AK), nitrate (NO3−-N), and ammonium (NH4+-N). SOC was determined using the K2Cr2O7-H2SO4 method (Walkley and Black, 1934), and pH was measured in a 1:2.5 soil-to-water suspension (Lu, 1999). TN was measured using an Elemental Analyzer (Elementer, Langenselbold, Germany), AP by the HCl-NH4F extraction method, and AK by flame spectroscopy after ammonium acetate extraction (Pang et al., 2021). TP and TK were determined colorimetrically using the NaOH-molybdenum method. NO3−-N and NH4+-N were analyzed colorimetrically after 18 h of soil suspension in 2 M KCl (Gong et al., 2019).

Soil enzyme activities were assessed using standard protocols. Soil urease activity was determined using the indophenol blue colorimetric method. After incubation, the released NH4+ was quantified colorimetrically at 578 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Urease activity was expressed as mg NH4+–N g−1 soil h−1.

Catalase activity was determined using the KMnO4 method (Li et al., 2020). Five grams of soil was reacted with 25 mL of 3% H2O2 at 25 °C for 20 min. The remaining H2O2 was titrated with 0.02 M KMnO4. Catalase activity was expressed as mL KMnO4 consumed g−1 soil. Soil sucrase (invertase) activity was measured using sucrose as substrate. Five grams of g soil was incubated with 15 mL of 8% sucrose solution and 5 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 5.5) at 37°C for 24 h. The released glucose was determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method at 508 nm (Gong et al., 2019). Phosphatase activity was measured using a fluorometric technique (Peng and Wang, 2016). Phosphatase activity was determined using 4-methylumbelliferyl phosphate (MUF-P) as a fluorescent substrate. Soil suspensions were incubated with 5 mM MUF-P at 30°C for 1 h. Fluorescence was measured at an excitation wavelength of 365 nm and an emission wavelength of 450 nm. Enzyme activity was expressed as nmol MUF released g−1 soil h−1.

Data processing and analysis

Raw sequencing reads were processed using the QIIME 2 pipeline (Caporaso et al., 2010). Reads with lengths <200 bp, Phred score <25, or containing ambiguous sequences were discarded (Xiao et al., 2016). High-quality sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity using UPARSE version 7.1 (Edgar, 2013).

Alpha diversity was assessed using Shannon, Inverse Simpson, Pielou evenness, observed OTUs, and Chao1 indices. Beta diversity was evaluated using principal component analysis (PCA), non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS), and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) (Ramette, 2007). A significant threshold of P < 0.05 was applied.

Correlations between microbial communities and soil physicochemical properties were evaluated using the Mantel test. Shared OTUs were visualized as Venn diagrams in MOTHUR (Schloss et al., 2009). Statistical analyses were performed using Excel 2016 and SPSS Statistics 22, and figures were generated using R.

Taxonomic assignment of representative OTU sequences was performed in QIIME 2 (version Feature Data). A pre-trained naïve Bayes classifier was used against the reference database (e.g., SILVA vs. 138/PR2 vs. 4.14), which was trimmed to the amplified 18S rDNA region defined by the EF3/EF4 primers prior to classifier training. Taxonomy was assigned using the feature-classifier classify-sklearn plugin with a confidence threshold of 0.7 (default setting).

Soil physicochemical properties (pH, available nitrogen, available phosphorus, available potassium, etc.) and soil enzyme activities (urease, catalase/peroxidase, sucrase, and alkaline phosphatase) were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When data did not meet normality or homogeneity assumptions, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. Significant differences among treatments were determined using Tukey’s HSD post hoc test at P < 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates (n = 3).

Results

Diversity and analysis of OTUs

Soil samples from healthy and T. foetida-infected wheat fields were collected, and 18S rDNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (paired-end 300 bp, PE300). Raw reads were generated and processed to obtain clean tags, which were further trimmed to effective tags for downstream analysis. Rarefaction curves (Supplementary Fig. 1A) indicated that sequencing was sufficient across all samples, with each generating more than 30,000 reads, demonstrating adequate sequencing depth to capture diverse OTUs in each treatment. Shannon-Wiener curves (Supplementary Fig. 1B) confirmed that sequencing depth adequately reflected species diversity, with more than 90% of species recovered across all treatments.

Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 1A) illustrated shared and unique OTUs across the six treatments, with a shared/core 115 OTUs identified. Each treatment also contained OTUs unique to its respective soil. Flower plot analysis (Fig. 1B) indicated that 21 OTUs were shared among all five replicates within each treatment, with each replicate containing both shared and unique OTUs. The unique OTUs for each replicate were as follows: T-T (1–5) contained 89, 99, 97, 109, and 103; T_CK (1–5) contained 74, 111, 98, 95, and 86. The M1R_CK (1–5) samples included 131, 125, 137, 133, and 125 unique OTUs, while M2R_CK (1–5) had 150, 157, 153, 165, and 150. The M1R_T (1–5) treatment had 119, 93, 95, 139, and 143 unique OTUs, and the M2R_T (1–5) treatment consisted of 171, 153, 148, 159, and 157 unique OTUs. Notably, the M2R_T treatment exhibited the highest number of unique OTUs, suggesting enhanced microbial diversification after two years of alfalfa rotation.

Alpha- and beta-diversity analyses of soil eukaryotic communities

Alpha diversity indices, including Chao1, Shannon, PD whole tree, and observed species, were calculated to assess microbial richness and diversity (Fig. 2A). Significant differences were observed among treatments. The M2R_T treatment exhibited the highest Chao1, Shannon, and observed species indices and clustered closely with M2R_CK, indicating that two years of alfalfa rotation restored microbial diversity to levels comparable to healthy soil. PD whole tree values varied slightly, with M1R_T and M2R_CK showing the highest values.

Beta diversity analyses were performed to evaluate differences among treatments. Weighted and unweighted UniFrac distance analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2) revealed that replicates of T_T exhibited larger distances in unweighted UniFrac, indicating greater variability, whereas weighted UniFrac heatmaps showed smaller distances, reflecting similarity in overall microbial abundance. Other treatments displayed smaller distances, indicating greater community similarity. NMDS analysis showed clear separation among treatments, indicating significant differences in soil eukaryotic community structure. NMDS analysis (Fig. 2B) showed that M1R_T, M2R_T, M2R_CK, and M1R_CK samples were tightly clustered, whereas T_T and T_CK replicates were more dispersed, indicating higher heterogeneity. Similarly, PCA revealed distinct distribution patterns among treatments; however, PCA is a descriptive multivariate visualization and does not, by itself, demonstrate statistical significance. PCA analysis (Fig. 2C) confirmed differences among treatments, with PC1 accounting for 34.62% and PC2 for 18.55% of the variance.

Abundance and composition of soil eukaryotic communities in soil rhizospheres at taxonomic levels

Soil eukaryotic community composition was analyzed at multiple taxonomic levels, including phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. Across all treatments, 12 phyla, 16 classes, 19 orders, 19 families, 20 genera, and 20 species were identified. The dominant phylum across all treatments was Mucoromycota, accounting for more than 75% of the abundance (Fig. 3A, 3B). Notably, Mucoromycota remained dominant regardless of alfalfa rotation. Other abundant phyla included Mollusca and Basidiomycota, whose proportions varied between infected wheat and alfalfa-rotated treatments. Less abundant phyla included Chytridiomycota, Ascomycota, Arthropoda, and Cercozoa.

At the class level (Supplementary Fig. 3A), Glomeromycetes were highly abundant across most treatments, except in M1R_T, which was enriched with Bivalvia and unidentified taxa. At the order level, Glomerales was prevalent across all treatments, while Paraglomerales and Mytiloida varied, except in T_CK samples, which were enriched with these taxa. Diversisporaceae and Boletales were also observed in varying proportions across treatments (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Family-level analysis showed Glomeraceae as dominant, with Clariodeoglomeraceae, Diversisporaceae, and Mytilidae present in varying proportions (Supplementary Fig. 4A).

Genus-level analysis (Fig. 4A, 4B) revealed that unidentified organisms predominated across all samples. In healthy wheat rhizosphere soils (T_CK), Funneliformis, Geukensia, and Rhizopogon was dominant. Wheat rhizosphere soil from common bunt affected field (T_T) showed higher proportions of Glomus and Entrophospora. The M1R_T increased the relative abundance of Entrophospora, Claroideoglomus, Diversispora, and Geukensia, whereas two-year rotation (M2R_T) restored soil eukaryotic community closer to healthy wheat and alfalfa soils, with increased Rhizophagus, Rhizopogon, and Funneliformis, and decreased Glomus abundance.

Species-level analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4B) revealed clear shifts in community structure. Claroideoglomus, Geukensia demissa, Paraglomeraceae, and Glomus species were abundant in healthy wheat rhizosphere soil and healthy 1-year-old alfalfa rhizosphere soil; Medicago sativa (T_CK and M1R_CK). Wheat rhizosphere soil from common bunt affected field (T_T) showed increased Glomus abundance and decreased Geukensia demissa. Alfalfa rotation, particularly in the second year, introduced species absent from infected soils, including Diversispora sp., Septoglomus, Rhizophagus iranicus, Rhizopogon sp., Geukensia demissa, and Claroideoglomus lamellosum, indicating a restoration of a more balanced eukaryotic soil community.

Quantitative analysis of T. foetida in different soils

Alfalfa rotation was associated with reduction in soil T. foetida DNA abundance (copies g−1 dry soil), as determined by qPCR. DNA extracted from each treatment had concentrations >20 μg (±2), with 260/280 ratios >1.8 and 260/230 ratios >1.6, confirming high purity for downstream qPCR analysis (Supplementary Table 1). qPCR amplification detected T. foetida DNA in all treatments (Fig. 5). Quantitative PCR revealed that alfalfa rotation reduced pathogen abundance: one-year rotation decreased copy numbers from 546,666 to 441,396 copies g−1 dry soil, while two-year rotation further reduced them to 396,212 copies g−1 dry soil. In contrast, one-year oat rotation had minimal impact, with 509,345 copies g−1 dry soil detected, indicating that alfalfa rotation effectively associated with lower T. foetida DNA load in soil.

Soil physical and chemical characteristics and enzyme activities

Soil chemical properties varied among treatments (Fig. 6A). Total phosphorus (TP), total potassium (TK), and pH were relatively unchanged, whereas TN, SOC, NH4+-N, and AP differed among treatments. AK and NO3−-N contents also varied; AK decreased from 23.07 to 17.43, and NO3−-N decreased from 30.87 to 28.48 in healthy versus infected soils. Alfalfa rotation further altered AK (325.20 → 110.20) and NO3− N (54.48 → 19.18) over two years, indicating significant effects on soil nutrient dynamics.

Soil enzyme activities (sucrase [Invertase], phosphatase, urease, and peroxidase) also differed among treatments (Fig. 6B). Wheat rhizosphere soil from common bunt affected field (T_T) exhibited higher peroxidase and invertase activities, but reduced phosphatase and urease compared to healthy wheat rhizosphere soil (T_CK). The M1R_T and M2R_T enhanced overall enzyme activities, except for phosphatase, suggesting that alfalfa positively influences rhizosphere enzymatic functions. Oat rotation had a smaller effect. Overall, diseased soils increase this, whereas they promote enzymatic balance and mitigate pathogen-induced shifts.

Discussion

Previous studies have reported that crop rotation significantly alters soil eukaryotic community, leading to increased crop yield and quality, enhanced soil attributes, and the promotion of healthy rhizosphere microbial populations (Gong et al., 2019). Soil physical, chemical, and enzymatic characteristics also play critical roles in shaping microbial dynamics and community composition (Curtright and Tiemann, 2021). In this study, we investigated the influence of long-term rotation with M. sativa L. (alfalfa) on T. foetida-infected wheat and composition, using 18S rDNA sequencing. Additionally, soil physicochemical properties and enzyme activities were analyzed to evaluate nutrient content and soil fertility, and to assess their relationships with soil eukaryotic communities.

Soil eukaryotic community and richness, assessed by alpha diversity (Fig. 2A), were highest in the M2R_T treatment, indicating that extended alfalfa rotation reshaped the soil eukaryotic community. Beta diversity analyses (Fig. 2B, 2C, Supplementary Fig. 2) revealed that alfalfa-treated samples were more similar to each other, suggesting stabilization of microbial communities under rotation. Previous studies have shown that M. sativa rotation enhances soil eukaryotic diversity and community richness compared with monoculture or non-rotated systems (Li et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2019). Our results confirm that alfalfa rotation exerts a pronounced influence on the microbial communities of smut-infected wheat rhizospheres, potentially contributing to improved soil health and plant resilience.

At the taxonomic level, relative abundance analysis revealed notable shifts in microbial populations across treatments. Mucoromycota was the dominant phylum across all soils, while Basidiomycota and Mollusca were also abundant but varied between treatments (Fig. 3A). The predominance of Mucoromycota is consistent with previous reports highlighting its role in nutrient cycling in wheat rhizospheres (Simonin et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019). As the largest fungal phylum, Mucoromycota includes arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), which form symbiotic networks within plant roots, enhancing nutrient and water uptake (Khaliq et al., 2022). Interestingly, while other studies report Ascomycota as a major soil fungal group (Din et al., 2021; Simonin et al., 2020), our study observed lower Ascomycota abundance, highlighting the unique microbial composition of smut-infected soils. Mollusca, representing soil invertebrates, were also abundant and contributed to decomposition, nutrient cycling, and soil biodiversity, serving as soil quality indicators (Lavelle et al., 2006). Notably, changes were observed in the relative abundance of dominant AMF, including Rhizophagus and Funneliformis. Alfalfa, a perennial legume with an extensive root system and strong symbiotic associations, promotes AMF colonization and persistence in soil. Previous studies have reported enrichment of AMF taxa following legume-based rotations, which has contributed to improved biological soil functions and plant performance. The observed shifts in Rhizophagus and Funneliformis abundance in the present study are therefore consistent with the ecological influence of alfalfa rotation on rhizosphere fungal community assembly. These findings support the reliability of our community structure analysis and suggest that crop rotation history plays a key role in shaping functionally important eukaryotic taxa in the soil.

As shown in Fig. 4, genus-level analysis demonstrated that Diversispora, Funneliformis, Glomus, Rhizophagus, Claroideoglomus, Entrophospora, Geukensia, and Rhizopogon was predominant. Intercropping with alfalfa notably altered the microbial composition of infected wheat soils, increasing the relative abundance of Entrophosporaceae and Claroideoglomus, along with the emergence of Septoglomus and Diversispora after rotation. The genera Glomus and Geukensia, which belong to Mucoromycota and Mollusca, were present in both healthy and infected soils but decreased following alfalfa rotation. These observations agree with Horsch et al. (2023), who reported that Glomeraceae (Septoglomus, Funneliformis, Glomus, Rhizophagus) and Entrophosporaceae (Entrophospora) enhance phosphorus availability for plant growth. Additionally, Claroideoglomeraceae has been shown to contribute to phosphorus and nitrogen acquisition (Ma et al., 2021).

Our results demonstrated positive correlations between Glomeraceae abundance and changes in available phosphorus (AP) in the soil, while total phosphorus (TP) remained unchanged. Soil properties, including organic matter, pH, total nitrogen (TN), nitrate (NO3-N), ammonium (NH4+-N), available potassium (AK), and total potassium (TK), also influenced AMF abundance. Consistent with previous studies, nitrogen and nitrate levels were reduced in infected wheat but improved following alfalfa rotation (Entz et al., 2001; Masri and Ryan, 2006). Notably, AK showed an inverse trend, being higher in infected soils than in alfalfa-rotated soils, while TK and pH remained stable (Fig. 6A). These findings highlight the importance of soil physicochemical properties in maintaining soil eukaryotic community and soil health (Liang et al., 2020).

Soil enzymes are key indicators of nutrient cycling and fertility (Curtright and Tiemann, 2021). In this study, urease, invertase, peroxidase, and phosphatase activities were measured (Fig. 6B). Our results align with previous reports showing that crop rotation enhances soil enzyme activities (Borase et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2018). Both first- and second-year alfalfa rotations increased urease, invertase, and peroxidase activities compared to smut-infected wheat soils, whereas phosphatase activity decreased, consistent with Gong et al. (2019). Oat rotation also influenced enzyme activities, particularly phosphatase and invertase, albeit to a lesser extent. Correlations between enzyme activities and soil nutrients were observed: higher nitrogen in M2R_CK was associated with elevated urease activity, whereas phosphatase activity was not directly associated with phosphorus availability. Organic matter levels slightly affected invertase and peroxidase activities, highlighting the complex interplay between soil chemistry and microbial functions.

Quantitative analysis of T. foetida revealed that teliospore abundance decreased following rotation with alfalfa and oats (Fig. 5B). While various control measures, including seed treatments, biological agents, and fumigation, have been employed, the persistence and dispersal of teliospores remain major challenges (Matanguihan et al., 2011). Biological control approaches have reduced spore abundance in previous studies (Yolageldi and Turhan, 2005), and fungicide seed treatments have also been effective (Din et al., 2021). However, these studies did not examine long-term effects on teliospore viability in the soil rhizosphere. Our results demonstrate that alfalfa rotation effectively reduces T. foetida spores, particularly after two years, outperforming both first-year alfalfa and oat rotations. However, it is important to note that qPCR quantifies pathogen DNA abundance and does not directly assess teliospore viability, infectivity, or field disease incidence. Therefore, while reduced soil T. foetida DNA abundance suggests a potential decrease in inoculum load, further studies incorporating field disease assessments or infectivity assays are required to confirm direct effects on common bunt incidence and severity.

Despite these promising results, we believe that this study also has certain limitations. First, the experiments were conducted at a single site in Gansu Province; soil microbial responses may vary across environments and soil types. Second, although 18S rDNA sequencing captured fungal community dynamics, it excluded bacteria and other microbial groups, limiting a comprehensive understanding of rhizosphere microbiomes. Third, the study focused on short-term measurements of soil physicochemical properties and enzyme activities; seasonal and long-term dynamics were not assessed. Future research should explore multi-year, multi-site trials, include metagenomic analyses of bacterial and archaeal communities, and investigate plant performance and yield outcomes under rotation. Additionally, exploring the mechanisms by which alfalfa influences T. foetida DNA abundance and microbial interactions will provide deeper insights into sustainable disease management strategies.

Our results align with recent findings that crop rotation alters rhizosphere community structure and function, potentially influencing host-pathogen interactions and soil health (Chen et al., 2025). For instance, rotations have been demonstrated to promote the recruitment of taxa associated with nutrient cycling and metabolite production while reducing the relative abundances of pathogenic lineages in rhizosphere soils, reinforcing the role of rotation in biotic stress regulation. Furthermore, legume-based rotations have been recognized for their capacity to modulate rhizosphere microbiomes and enhance ecosystem resilience through symbiotic associations and functional shifts in microbial communities (Liu et al., 2025), highlighting the ecological mechanisms that may associated with reduced soil T. foetida DNA abundance dynamics.

Overall, the present study provides novel evidence that long-term alfalfa rotation positively influences soil microbial diversity, restores beneficial fungal communities, enhances nutrient cycling, and reduces T. foetida abundance in smut-infected wheat fields. These findings highlight the potential of crop rotation strategies to modulate soil pathogen abundance and reshape rhizosphere microbial communities, thereby contributing to sustainable management of soil-borne diseases in wheat production systems.

This study demonstrates that legume rotation, particularly with M. sativa, significantly enhances soil health and soil eukaryotic community in smut-infected wheat fields. Nutrient content and soil enzyme activities were higher in M1R_T and M2R_T, and microbial communities were more abundant and diverse, restoring beneficial taxa disrupted by infection. Long-term alfalfa rotation effectively reduced T. foetida teliospore abundance, with the second-year rotation (M2R_T) showing the greatest impact, underscoring its potential as a sustainable disease management strategy. These findings underscore the practical significance of legume rotations in managing common bunt disease, promoting soil fertility, and enhancing microbial community structure. Changes in dominant fungal taxa and soil biochemical properties suggest potential ecological mechanisms through which crop rotation may influence soil-borne pathogen dynamics. However, because this study did not directly assess pathogen infectivity, teliospore viability, or field disease incidence, the findings should be interpreted as indicative of associative patterns rather than confirmed biocontrol effects. Further studies that integrate viability assays and field disease evaluations are needed to elucidate the causal mechanisms underlying pathogen regulation in rotation systems.
