Knowledge, attitudes and practices on malaria and mosquitoes in the Far-North region of Cameroon
Authors: Simon Pierre Yinyang Danga, Bouladji Yonki, David Ngadvou, Amarachi Keziah Ezeike, Alain Djonga, Elias Nchiwan Nukenine, Charles Okechukwu Esimone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a significant public health burden in Cameroon, with the Far-North region experiencing high transmission rates. Despite the widespread distribution of Long-Lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLINs) and other control measures, the disease persists. Understanding the community's knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) is crucial for developing effective and targeted interventions. This study aimed to assess the KAP regarding malaria and its mosquito vectors among households in the Far-North region of Cameroon.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted in 50 randomly selected villages across the region from August to September 2020. A total of 500 households were interviewed using a structured questionnaire translated into local languages. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation to identify factors associated with key knowledge and practice outcomes.ResultsGeneral knowledge about malaria was high: all respondents had heard of malaria, 88.2% correctly identified mosquito bites as the primary cause and 98.8% were aware it could be fatal. However, a critical lack of knowledge was identified, with 67.2% of respondents unaware that mosquito larvae develop in stagnant water. Ownership and reported use of LLINs were near-universal (98.8% and 96%, respectively), yet malaria prevalence in households was 91.6% (self-reported malaria incidence within the last year). Sub-optimal net maintenance practices were common, including washing with detergent and drying in the sunlight. A heavy reliance on self-medication (39.0%) and traditional plant-based remedies (72.4%) for treatment was reported, primarily driven by the perceived high cost of hospital care. Correlation analysis revealed that higher education levels were significantly associated with better knowledge of malaria cause (r = 0.101, p = 0.024) and mosquito larval habitats (r = 0.124, p = 0.006).ConclusionThe population of the Far-North region exhibits high awareness of malaria but critical gaps in understanding mosquito breeding sites. The paradox of high LLIN coverage coupled with high malaria prevalence suggests issues with net maintenance and proper use. Background

In 2019, there were an estimated 229 million malaria cases and 409,000 deaths worldwide. The WHO African Region accounted for approximately 215 million cases and 384,000 deaths, representing a disproportionate 94% of the global malaria burden for both metrics. Cameroon accounted for approximately 2.9% of global malaria cases and 2.4% of global deaths, representing roughly 6.6 million cases and 9,800 fatalities [1]. An estimated 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 malaria deaths were recorded worldwide in 2023 [2]. The WHO African region accounted for 94% of global cases (approximately 247 million cases) and 95% of global deaths (approximately 567,000 deaths) [2]. In Cameroon, there were an estimated 7,343,057 malaria cases and 11,602 malaria deaths [2]. Several Anopheles species such as An. gambiae, An. arabiensis, An. coluzzii, An. funestus, An. nili and An. moucheti known for their high anthropophilic behaviour, are considered major vectors of malaria in the country [3]. Four Plasmodium species infecting humans have been reported in Cameroon, namely P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale and P. malariae. An. gambiae is the predominant species of mosquito responsible for transmission of malaria and P. falciparum being the principal parasite species [4].

In the Far-North region of Cameroon, the prevalence of malaria was 29.9% [5] with 29,596 cases fully treated and death rate of 37% in the different health centres in 2019 [6]. The transmission of malaria is the highest from July to October in the region [7]. However, not all cases are recorded, and it has been reported that 48% of malaria cases were underestimated from January 2019 to December 2021 [8].

Vector control has been a vital component of malaria prevention and control, relying mainly on the use of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs). Since 2000, Cameroon has benefited from the support of various international partners to implement malaria control strategies. Over 20 million LLINs have so far been freely distributed to the population through several campaigns, with the support of the Global Fund [9]. Primary measures adopted by the government of Cameroon are the intermittent preventive treatment of malaria in pregnancy, the seasonal malaria chemoprevention and the free of charge systematic treatment of uncomplicated malaria in under-five children. Unfortunately, with all those malaria control strategies put in place, malaria still remains a burden on the population [10].

The risk factors for malaria transmission are the presence of water-retaining containers and ground pools in and around households, inappropriate disposal of waste, poor management of toilets, lack or poor water draining systems, lack of knowledge of mosquito biology, as well as non-use of LLINs [10]. To reverse the situation, it has become increasingly important to develop strategies to improve the use of malaria prevention measures among the population. Appropriation of any control programme by the population and their willingness to act is largely influenced by their level of understanding, attitude and perception towards the disease and existing control measures [11]. Hence, to promote the use of malaria control strategies among households living in the Far-North region of Cameroon, it is necessary to understand their knowledge, attitudes and perceptions of the disease and its mosquito vectors. Knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) surveys are common tools that can capture critical information to guide the design of control strategies, ensuring community involvement, acceptance and adherence [11–13]. Malaria KAP surveys have been conducted in some regions of Cameroon [12, 13] although the impact of those research findings has not been assessed yet. However, to the best of our knowledge, no systematic study has investigated the KAP regarding malaria among households in the Far-North region of Cameroon, thus the choice of this region as study site.

Thus, the main goal of the present study was to generate evidence-based insights that guide the National Malaria Control Program or other health organisation in developing targeted strategies to reduce malaria morbidity and mortality. The specific objective was to assess the community's KAP regarding malaria and its mosquito vectors in the Far-North region of Cameroon.

Methods

Study area, period and interview panel

The Knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) survey was conducted among population of the Far-North region of Cameroon (Fig. 1). The climate is of the Sudano-Sahelian type with a short rainy season from May to October and the rain becomes rarer when going towards Lake Chad. With an area of 34,263 km2, it has 4,432,529 people according to the projections of the Central Bureau of Censuses and Population Studies (BUCREP). It has mostly sedentary populations and some nomads who move through the region according to the seasons [14]. The cross-border insecurity with Nigeria and Chad by the Boko Haram jihadists has led to internal displacement of populations and an influx of Nigerian refugees in the MINAWAO camp which has nearly 58,600 people [15]. Administratively, it is subdivided into six departments (Diamaré, Mayo Tsanaga, Mayo Kani, Mayo Sava, Mayo Danay and Logone et Chari), 47 sub-divisions and more than 3000 villages/quarters [14]. In terms of health system, the region has 31 health districts and 301 health centres and 372 integrated health centres. Ten of the thirty districts border with Nigeria and face cross-border insecurity; these are the health districts of Kousseri, Mora, Mokolo, Goulfey, Mada, Makary, Koza, Bourha, Tokombere and Kolofata [6]. The KAP survey was carried out from August to September 2020. The interview panel of six members (2 women and 4 men, all holders of MSc degree except 1 who had PhD degree) completed a two-day training program, during which trial sampling was conducted to ensure mastery of the procedures.

Study population and eligibility criteria

A community-based cross-sectional descriptive study was carried out to identify knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding malaria and mosquitoes among population of Far-North region, Cameroon. The study population was made up of households in the region with the exclusion of villages confronted with cross-border insecurity caused by the Boko Haram jihadists. These villages were excluded for the safety of the interviewers.

Any person aged at least 18 years of both male and female at the time of survey, a family member at the time of survey, living continually in the selected villages for at least a year and voluntarily agreed to participate in the interview was included in the study. Any member of the selected household, who was deaf, dumb, non-resident, had a mental health problem and incomplete questionnaire was excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using the standard formula for estimating a single population proportion [16]: n = z2pq/d.2

z = Standard normal deviation set at 1.96 normal interval; P = prevalence of malaria in the Far-North region (29.9%) [6]; q = proportions that does not have the characteristics being investigated (q = 1-p), q = 1–0.299 = 0.701; d = Level of significance set at 0.05 (precision set at 5%); n = (1.96)2 × 0.299 × 0.701/(0.05)2; n = 322.

This calculation yielded a sample of 322. To account for the cluster sampling design, this was multiplied by a design effect of 1.5 [17], giving an adjusted sample size of 483.

The sample was further increased by 10% to account for potential non-response, resulting in a final target sample size of 531. We surveyed 500 households for operational feasibility (this remains representative given that the 1.5 design effect was applied).

Sampling

To carry out the cluster sampling, five elements were considered. The total cumulative number of the population (N = 1,882,605) was calculated based on the last census of Cameroon by summing the respective numbers of the villages [14]. The sampling step was calculated: this was the number representing the total number of the population divided by the number of villages (50 chosen in the present study; 10 households were surveyed in each village), i.e. Sampling step = N/50. In this study, the calculated interval was: 1,882,605/50 = 37,652. A random number whose number of digits was equal to the number of digits of the sampling step, i.e. 5 in the present study (37,652) was selected. This number was obtained from an online random number generator. The first cluster (village) (C1) was the one whose cumulative number of population was equal or greater than the random number; then C2 = Random number + sampling step; C3 = C2 + sampling step (Cx = Cx-1 + sampling step) until C50. It was in these villages that the survey was carried out.

Random sampling was set up to select participants. Two distant households were chosen from the centre, north, south, east and west of each village in order to minimise bias.

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was developed based on a review of existing KAP surveys [18, 19] and adapted to the local context. It was pre-tested and refined (pilot test). The final tool contained four sections: (i) socio-demographics, (ii) knowledge, (iii) attitudes and (iv) practices. The questionnaire is provided as a supplementary file. The questionnaire was administered to 500 randomly selected households in August and September 2020. It was originally developed in French and then translated into the primary local languages (Fulfulde, Tupuri, Mundang, Mafa, Guiziga and Massa) using a forward–backward translation process by independent, professional translators to ensure conceptual equivalence. Interviewers were trained to use the standardized local language versions.

Ethical clearance

The study was approved by the North Regional Review Board and was given ethical approval with the reference number of No056020L/RN/DRSPN/GRA prior to the beginning of the study. A supportive letter was obtained from the Faculty of Science, University of Ngaoundere. Full verbal explanation of the study was given to members of selected households, consent and witness signatures were obtained before inclusion as participants. Respondents were given the right to refuse to take part in the study as well as to withdraw at any time during the interview. Privacy and confidentiality were maintained throughout the study.

Statistical analyses

Data were set in Microsoft Excel and transferred to SPSS software (SPSS version 25.0) for the statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages and means) were calculated for all variables [20]. To identify factors (sex, age and education) associated with key knowledge and practice outcomes, Pearson correlation coefficients (r) test was also performed on the data to describe the strength and direction of relationships between continuous variables at p < 0.05 probability level of significance. The strength of correlations was interpreted as 0 = no relationship, 0.10–0.29 = small correlation, 0.30–0.49 = medium correlation and 0.50–1.00 large correlation [21].

Results

Socio-demographic and environmental characteristics

A total of 500 respondents participated in the study. The majority were male (61.8%) and the mean age was 43.2 years (SD = 12.8), with the largest age group being 26–40 years (40.4%) (Table 1). Educational attainment was low, with 36.0% having never attended school and only 2.8% having attained higher education. The primary occupation for household heads was farming (69.4%). Housing conditions were predominantly traditional, with 87.8% of households having clay floors and clayey brick walls. The majority of households (58.8%) had no electronic appliances.

Environmental risk factors for mosquito breeding were highly prevalent. Nearly all households (97.8%) reported the presence of water containers and 78.4% reported the presence of stagnant water (non-moving water) around their homes (Fig. 2). Dense vegetation was also common, with 77.6% of respondents reporting "average" or "much" vegetation. Malaria was the most frequently reported recurrent disease, affecting 96% of households, far surpassing other diseases like typhoid (53.8%) (Table 1).

Knowledge about malaria

General knowledge about malaria was high among respondents. All participants (100%) had heard about malaria, primarily through health centres (63.4%) (Table 2). Recognition of the primary symptoms was excellent, with headache (96.2%), fever (95.6%) and cold/chills (84.6%) being the most commonly identified. A significant majority correctly identified the cause of malaria as a mosquito bite (88.2%) and understood that the disease is most predominant during the rainy season (98% combined for middle and end of the season). Most respondents (98.8%) were aware that malaria can be fatal if not treated. Children under five years old were correctly identified as the most vulnerable group by 63% of respondents (Table 2).

Knowledge about mosquitoes

Knowledge specifically about mosquitoes was also substantial. Nearly all respondents (97%) knew that mosquitoes can cause malaria and the vast majority (92.2%) correctly identified night-time as when mosquitoes bite the most (Table 3). However, a critical knowledge gap was identified regarding mosquito breeding sites, with 67.2% of respondents not knowing that larvae live in stagnant water. When asked what is bad about mosquitoes, 65.4% cited bites and malaria, while 20.4% focused on the nuisance of mosquito noise.

Attitudes towards mosquitoes and malaria

Attitudes towards mosquitoes and malaria were serious. A large proportion of households (67%) were perceived as having "much" mosquito density. The vast majority of respondents viewed malaria as a real problem in their locality (93%) and affirmed the importance of early treatment (99.6%) (Table 4). A notable finding was that only 30.4% believed that malaria can be prevented, while 61% believed it cannot.

Preventive practices

Almost all respondents (98.4%) reported using at least one personal protection method. The use of Long-Lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLINs) was nearly universal. Ownership was reported in 98.8% of households and 99.2% of respondents stated they use them (Tables 5 and 6). Almost all household members (96%) were reported to sleep under them and 90.8% of respondents claimed to have slept under a net the night before the survey. The primary reasons for non-use were forgetting (3.8%) and heat (3.0%). The burning of plants to repel mosquitoes was more common, practiced by 45.6% of respondents. Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS) was largely unavailable, reported by 81.2% of respondents (Table 5).

However, net maintenance practices were sub-optimal. Almost half of the users (47.2%) washed their nets only once a year and 35.8% did not use detergent for washing (Table 6). While 34.2% dried their nets in the shade, a significant portion (58.8%) dried them in the sunlight. Other preventive methods were less common. The use of mosquito repellent was low (14.2%), primarily due to unavailability (64.4%). Spiral mosquito coils were used by 39.2% of respondents, but mostly only sometimes (35.8%).

Treatment practices for malaria

Treatment-seeking behaviour revealed a preference for informal care. Self-medication was the most common first action (39.0%), slightly higher than seeking hospital care (34.8%) (Table 7). A significant majority (83.4%) sought treatment within 24 h of symptom onset. The use of plants to cure malaria was widespread, reported by 72.4% of respondents. The primary reasons for self-medication were the perceived high cost of drugs in hospitals (9.4%) and a general lack of money (7.6%) (Table 8).

Factors associated with key knowledge and practices

Pearson correlation analysis revealed several significant associations (Table 9). Higher education level was positively correlated with correct knowledge about the cause of malaria (r = 0.101, p = 0.024) and mosquito larval habitats (r = 0.124, p = 0.006). Sex was a factor, with males more likely to know the cause of malaria (r = 0.095, p = 0.033), to have late bedtime (r = 0.246, p < 0.001) and to dry nets in sunlight (r = 0.127, p = 0.004), but they also reported washing nets less frequently (r = −0.095, p = 0.034). Higher education was also associated with wrong practices such as drying nets in the sun (r = 0.135, p = 0.002). Conversely, higher education was negatively associated with washing nets with detergent (r = −0.115, p = 0.01), sleeping outside (r = −0.106, p = 0.018) and seeking treatment promptly (r = −0.111, p = 0.013). A weak but statistically significant positive correlation was observed between sex and the observed presence of stagnant water (non-moving water) around households (r = 0.097, p = 0.015). Similarly, sex showed a significant positive association with the place where treatment for malaria is sought (r = 0.094, p = 0.018), suggesting modest differences in treatment-seeking behaviour by gender. There was a significant positive correlation between age and the presence of stagnant water (r = 0.136, p = 0.001). Age was not significantly associated with the place to seek treatment (r = 0.031, p = 0.242). Education level exhibited a significant positive correlation with the place where malaria treatment is sought (r = 0.092, p = 0.020), implying that respondents with higher education were more likely to report seeking care at appropriate facilities (e.g., health centres rather than self-medication). No significant association was found between education and the presence of stagnant water (r = −0.009, p = 0.420). None of the three socio-demographic variables (sex, age, education) showed statistically significant correlations with the observed presence of water containers/ground pools (all p > 0.2), presence of vegetation as a potential mosquito habitat factor (all p > 0.2), or the use of plants to cure malaria (all p > 0.1). All non-significant correlations were very weak (r ≤ 0.039).

Discussion

This study provides a detailed assessment of the knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) regarding malaria and mosquitoes among households in the Far-North region of Cameroon. The findings reveal a population with high general awareness of malaria but significant gaps in specific knowledge and preventive practices, alongside a strong reliance on self-medication and traditional plant-based remedies.

The universal awareness of malaria (100%) and the high recognition of its primary symptoms (fever, headache, chills) and its transmission via mosquito bites (88.2%) are consistent with findings from other malaria-endemic regions in Sub-Saharan Africa [22]. This high level of knowledge is likely a result of sustained public health campaigns [23] and the pervasive nature of the disease, with 96% of households reporting malaria as a recurrent illness. Furthermore, the accurate identification of the rainy season as the peak period for malaria and the recognition of children under five and pregnant women as the most vulnerable groups align with epidemiological data, indicating that core public health messages are being received [2].

However, critical lack of knowledge persists. A substantial majority (67.2%) did not know that mosquito larvae develop in stagnant water and 40.8% were unaware of the provenance of mosquitoes. This gap is particularly concerning given that 35.2% and 16.4% of households reported "much" or "average" presence of stagnant water around their homes, respectively, and a variety of water-holding containers were identified as potential breeding sites. This disconnect between recognizing adult mosquitoes as a nuisance and understanding their aquatic larval stages represents a major barrier to effective source reduction and larval control strategies [24]. Without this knowledge, community-based interventions to eliminate breeding sites are likely to be less effective.

The attitude towards malaria was predominantly serious, with 98.8% acknowledging that untreated malaria can be fatal and 99.6% affirming the importance of early treatment. Despite this, a paradoxical finding was that 61% of respondents believed malaria could not be prevented. This reveals a profound sense of fatalism that likely stems from the lived reality of intervention failure within the community. This belief should not be dismissed as a simple lack of knowledge; rather, it reflects a rational response to seeing neighbours who consistently use Long-Lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLINs) yet still fall ill. When current tools fail to provide the promised protection, whether due to physical net degradation, improper maintenance, or the biological reality of insecticide resistance in Anopheles, it reinforces the perception that the disease is an unavoidable part of life. This cycle of "learned helplessness" creates a significant barrier to the adoption of new strategies, as communities may remain sceptical of any intervention if they perceive existing "gold standard" tools to be ineffective. [22, 25]. It implies that while people understand the severity of the disease, they may not feel empowered to stop its transmission, focusing instead on treatment.

Preventive practices were dominated by the use of mosquito nets, with near-universal ownership (98.8%) and a high proportion of households where everyone slept under one the previous night (90.8%). This high coverage is a public health success, likely driven by mass distribution campaigns [26]. However, sub-optimal practices threaten the efficacy of this intervention. A significant proportion washed their nets only once (47.2%) or twice (31.8%) per year and over a third (34.2%) dried them in the shade. Washing nets infrequently and drying them in the shade can preserve insecticide, but excessively long intervals may compromise hygiene, while sun-drying can degrade the pyrethroid insecticide, reducing its protective effect [27]. Furthermore, the misuse of nets for purposes like fishing (3.2%) and catching termites (12.6%), though reported by a minority, indicates that the perceived utility of nets extends beyond malaria prevention, which could affect their longevity and availability for their intended purpose. The high prevalence of malaria, despite a substantial net ownership rate of 98.8%, presents a complex public health paradox that extends beyond suboptimal net maintenance. Approximately 70% of respondents retired to bed after 22:00, a period during which primary malaria vectors are highly active; consequently, this behaviour increases the risk of exposure to infective bites prior to the protection afforded by bed nets. This maybe a partial justification of the paradox. While behavioural factors such as washing nets with detergents or sun-drying significantly reduce the physical and chemical integrity of LLINs, the role of biological factors, specifically insecticide resistance, cannot be overlooked. In Cameroon, documented resistance in Anopheles gambiae and Anopheles coluzzii to pyrethroids, organochlorines, and carbamates has reached critical levels, driven by both target-site mutations (kdr) and metabolic detoxification mechanisms. This widespread resistance means that even when nets are used correctly, they may fail to provide the intended lethal effect or "mass killing" benefit, allowing resistant vectors to survive contact and sustain transmission. Consequently, the high prevalence of malaria in the Far-North Region is likely the result of a synergistic failure: the degradation of net efficacy through poor maintenance practices coupled with the diminishing susceptibility of local Anopheles populations to the insecticides currently deployed [28]. In addition, the use of LLINs is sometimes insufficient; even when nets are present, mosquitoes may adapt by biting earlier in the evening or resting outdoors, thereby avoiding contact with the insecticide [29].

The use of other preventive methods was limited. The low utilization of insecticide sprays (14.2%) and coils (38.6%) was primarily attributed to their cost and unavailability, highlighting access and affordability issues. The most common alternative method was the burning of plants (45.6%) as mosquito repellents. This demonstrates the utilization of local botanical knowledge. A similar richness of plant species utilized as alternative mosquito repellents has been documented globally [30]. Treatment-seeking behavior revealed a heavy reliance on self-medication (39%) and the use of plants (72.4%). The primary reasons for self-medication were the perceived high cost of drugs in hospitals and a general lack of money. This aligns with findings across low-resource settings where financial barriers drive individuals to seek cheaper, unregulated alternatives [31]. The correlation analysis offers insights for targeted interventions. Higher education was significantly associated with better knowledge of malaria causation, mosquito larval habitats and treatment-seeking behaviour. This underscores the fundamental role of education in health literacy. Sex-based differences were also notable; being male was correlated with better knowledge of malaria cause and better net drying practices, while also being associated with a later bedtime, which could increase exposure to nocturnal biting Anopheles mosquitoes.

Limitations of the study

This study has limitations. The KAP design relies on self-reported data, which is subject to social desirability bias; for instance, the reported 90.8% net use the previous night might be an overestimate. The cross-sectional nature of the study allows for the identification of associations but not causal relationships. Furthermore, the survey was limited to specific locations within the Far-North region, and thus the findings may not be fully generalisable to the entire region or other ecological zones in Cameroon.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the population of the Far-North region of Cameroon demonstrates high baseline knowledge of malaria but critical gaps in specific areas, particularly regarding mosquito breeding. Positive attitudes are overshadowed by a perceived inability to prevent the disease. While mosquito net coverage is excellent, practices related to their maintenance and use can be optimized. The reliance on self-medication and a wide array of medicinal plants, driven by economic constraints, point to the need for integrated interventions. Since 78.4% of households have stagnant water but 67.2% don't know larvae live there, the National Malaria Control Program should prioritize community-led larval source management alongside net distribution in future public health strategies. Further research could also be carried out to scientifically validate the efficacy and safety of the most commonly used traditional plants.
