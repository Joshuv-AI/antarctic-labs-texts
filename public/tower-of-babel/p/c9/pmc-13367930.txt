Severe Craniofacial Trauma From Defective Airbag Deployment
Authors: Marc Aoude, Elia Kassouf, Georges Gharghour, Samer Abou Zeid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Airbags are designed to reduce morbidity and mortality during motor vehicle collisions; however, rare manufacturing defects may result in catastrophic injuries. We report a unique case of a 45-year-old woman involved in a low-speed collision, who sustained projectile-induced airbag trauma. Deployment of a defective airbag expelled a metallic projectile, causing complex panfacial fractures, extensive maxillary bone loss, and a full-thickness palatal defect. The patient initially received suboptimal management, necessitating secondary reconstruction at a tertiary referral center. Revision surgery included panfacial fracture realignment, orbital floor reconstruction with bone grafting, and closure of the palatal defect using a temporalis musculofascial flap, achieving satisfactory functional and aesthetic outcomes. This case underscores the distinct and potentially lethal mechanism of airbag failure associated with recalled inflators; highlights the importance of early recognition, thorough reporting, and multidisciplinary management; and provides critical insights for improving patient safety and guiding clinicians encountering similar injuries.

Airbags are safety devices that deploy a fabric bag through a rapid-inflation gas generator, reducing injuries during motor vehicle collisions. When properly designed, they significantly reduce mortality; however, severe injuries have been reported in cases of malfunction.1,2

In rare circumstances, manufacturing defects may result in the expulsion of a high-velocity projectile, causing devastating craniofacial trauma. Such injuries may involve panfacial fractures with bone and soft-tissue loss, posing major reconstructive challenges.3 We report a unique case of projectile-induced airbag injury with documented expulsion of a metallic object, resulting in complex panfacial fractures and a full-thickness palatal defect requiring secondary reconstruction. No previous reports describe such an extensive pattern of maxillofacial injury following airbag projectile expulsion, underscoring a lethal failure mechanism that warrants clinical vigilance.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 45-year-old woman was driving in a 2008-model sedan on October 30, 2025, at low speed, as confirmed by footage from a home surveillance camera. She was involved in a low-impact collision with another vehicle, resulting in minor damage to the front bumper. Following airbag deployment, a metallic projectile from the passenger-side airbag shattered the windshield, and a similar propelled object from the driver-side airbag entered the patient’s mouth directly. The patient was transferred to the nearest external hospital with profuse oral bleeding. An emergency tracheostomy was performed to secure the airway, along with repair of minor lacerations and hemostatic control. No intracranial injuries were identified. Computed tomography revealed bilateral zygomatic tripod fractures, bilateral orbital floor fractures, a comminuted maxillary fracture with loss of teeth and bone on the right side associated with a full-thickness palatal defect, a displaced mandibular parasymphyseal fracture, and a complex right mandibular fracture involving the angle and ascending ramus, with an associated coronoid process fracture, creating 3 separate mandibular segments (Figs. 1–3).

Initial surgical management by local maxillofacial surgeons resulted in inadequate fracture reduction, leading to malocclusion, persistent oronasal communication, facial asymmetry, and diplopia (Fig. 4). The patient remained dependent on nasogastric tube feeding. After a 28-day stay in the intensive care unit, she was referred to Hôtel-Dieu de France University Hospital for specialized reconstruction.

Three-dimensional computed tomography reconstruction following primary surgery at an external hospital showing inadequate reduction of maxillary and mandibular fractures, resulting in malocclusion, persistent displacement, and diplopia due to insufficient fixation of the bilateral zygomatic tripod and orbital floor fractures.

Revision surgery included reopening the inferior vestibular incision and manipulating existing hardware to achieve proper anatomical reduction of the mandibular parasymphysis. An intraoral approach combined with a right Risdon incision was used to revise fixation and restore alignment of the right mandibular ramus with the coronoid process. A superior vestibular incision allowed correction of the comminuted maxillary fracture, with intermaxillary fixation to obtain the best possible occlusion. Bilaterally, subciliary and infra-suprabrow incisions were reopened to achieve accurate reduction of the zygomatic tripod fractures, followed by rigid fixation. Inadequate orbital floor metallic mesh was removed and replaced with iliac crest bone grafts for orbital support. A right musculofascial temporalis muscle flap was performed to close the palatal defect. The postoperative course was uneventful, with gradual return to oral feeding, tracheostomy decannulation, resolution of diplopia, closure of the oronasal communication, improved symmetry, and restoration of acceptable occlusion given the extent of maxillary bone loss.

DISCUSSION

At our institution, secondary reconstruction was performed, including revision of fracture fixation, orbital floor reconstruction, and closure of the palatal defect. We restored airway safety, oral feeding, occlusion, diplopia, and symmetry. The strength of this article lies in the uniqueness of the trauma mechanism, the projectile-related injury pattern, and the complexity of secondary surgery. Future stages may include maxillary bone grafting for implant-supported dental rehabilitation and temporal lipofilling to optimize aesthetic outcomes. Microsurgical reconstruction with a free bone and soft-tissue flap was considered but not pursued due to patient-related economic constraints. Limitations include loss of the patient to follow-up, preventing acquisition of additional postoperative imaging; therefore, emphasis was placed on a detailed surgical description.

Reconstructive options for severe panfacial trauma prioritize restoration of skeletal buttresses, occlusion, and facial projection through anatomic reduction, rigid fixation, and selective bone grafting. Attention to orbital volume, nasal patency, and soft-tissue resuspension or augmentation is essential to optimize both functional and aesthetic outcomes.4

Although airbags are intended to enhance safety, rare but severe maxillofacial injuries can occur through different mechanisms, including direct impact. Francis et al5 reported that 7% of orbital blowout fractures in motor vehicle collisions were caused solely by airbag impact. Similarly, Roccia et al3 found that 3% of car crash–related maxillofacial fractures resulted from direct airbag contact, most commonly involving the zygomatico-orbital complex. Aksam et al6 described a high-energy airbag impact causing a comminuted mandibular fracture requiring staged microsurgical reconstruction.

In contrast, projectile injuries from malfunctioning airbags represent a more destructive mechanism. Khoschnau et al7 reported a case series describing various mandibular fracture patterns caused by the same cylindrical metal projectile in similar vehicle models, frequently associated with head and neck injuries. Terenzi et al8 described a penetrating airbag-related facial injury with nerve damage, tissue loss, and comminuted maxillozygomatic fractures requiring surgery.

Notably, the same defect has been implicated in fatal injuries. Albalooshi et al1 reported a lethal cervical injury caused by ejection of an identical metallic cylinder from a defective airbag in a 2008 sedan—the same vehicle model involved in our case. Similarly, Jothee et al9 and Vadysinghe et al2 documented fatal carotid and neck vessel injuries caused by the same projectile, with Jothee et al9 illustrating the identical car model. This underscores the potentially lethal nature of this failure mechanism and highlights the importance of awareness, early recognition, and reporting to improve patient safety. Importantly, the vehicle model involved in our case is among those affected by the Takata airbag recall—the largest automotive safety recall worldwide—initiated due to a known inflator defect predisposing to high-velocity metallic projectile ejection.10

CONCLUSIONS

Projectile expulsion from airbags represents a potentially fatal mechanism of maxillofacial trauma. Compared with conventional blunt injuries, these traumas produce combined penetrating and blast damage, resulting in severe comminution and bone and soft-tissue defects that complicate anatomical reduction and often necessitate revision surgery, particularly after suboptimal primary management. Early recognition, multidisciplinary management, and timely referral to specialized centers are essential to optimize outcomes.
