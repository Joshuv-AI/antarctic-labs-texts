The Sore Throat and Antibiotic Resistance (STAR) Study in Malaysia: Nationwide Survey of Antibiotic Use for Upper Respiratory Tract Infection and Sore Throat
Authors: Baharudin Abdullah, So Fie Tan, Shinee Tan, Zahiruddin Wan Mohammad, Andy Watson, Adrian Shephard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is exacerbated by the ongoing misuse of antibiotics for upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs), and it is vital to try and determine factors associated with antibiotic misuse. The Sore Throat and Antibiotic Resistance (STAR) study aims to evaluate public knowledge, perceptions, and behaviors to antibiotic use for acute URTIs and sore throat in Malaysia.MethodsA nationwide online survey was carried out in June 2024 among Malaysian adults who reported having sore throats with other URTI symptoms in the past six months using a random sampling method. A 26-item (plus 5 for screening) self-administered questionnaire, adapted from the Eurobarometer survey on AMR, was shared through the Toluna online panel. The questionnaire included four sections that covered demographic details, URTI symptom patterns, antibiotic use, and self-medication practices for symptomatic relief, as well as knowledge, perceptions, and behaviors about these practices.ResultsAmong 1031 respondents, sore throat was the most common symptom (62%). It often occurred significantly (p < 0.001) with cough (51.4%) and flu-like symptoms (53.8%). Antibiotic use was high, with 72% using them for respiratory illnesses and 33.4% for sore throat. Self-medication for symptomatic relief occurred mostly for sore throat (62%). While 78% had seen AMR messages and 70% knew about antibiotic risks, many held misconceptions: 66.1% thought antibiotics relieve pain and 72.5% believed they speed recovery. Confidence in non-antibiotic care was low at 42%, and 80% expressed anxiety about not using antibiotics. Unsafe behaviors included keeping leftover antibiotics (34%) and stopping treatment early (45%).ConclusionsThis study found a significant gap between perceived knowledge and practice as well as behaviors that contribute to inappropriate antibiotic use. These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions such as public education, improved communication between healthcare providers and patients, including the benefits of a pro-symptomatic approach to first line management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03515-5.Keywords: Upper respiratory tract infection, Sore throat, Antimicrobial resistance, Symptomatic relief, Primary care

Introduction

Upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) and sore throats are mainly viral in origin and typically resolve without medical intervention and the use of antibiotics [1]. Current medical guidelines advocate for supportive care which includes adequate rest, maintaining hydration, and using throat lozenges or mild analgesics to ease symptoms [2]. Despite the growing concerns, antibiotics are still routinely given for viral URTIs worldwide. This ongoing practice directly contributes to the rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a major problem that the World Health Organization warns is one of the ten most serious threats to our health globally [3]. The global burden of AMR is significant, having been directly implicated in 1.27 million deaths in 2019 [4]. Furthermore, when considering cases where AMR was an associated factor, the mortality figure rises substantially to 4.95 million individuals worldwide [5]. This notably exceeds the combined mortality rates attributed to HIV/AIDS and malaria during the same period [6]. Without urgent action, AMR could kill 10 million people globally every year by 2050, while driving healthcare costs up by USD 1 trillion and cutting worldwide economic output by as much as USD 3.4 trillion annually by 2030 [7].

Despite explicit international and national recommendations, including those issued by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and Malaysia’s National Antimicrobial Guidelines, inappropriate antibiotic prescribing for uncomplicated URTIs remains prevalent [1, 8, 9]. Antibiotics in Malaysia are legally prescription-only and are typically obtained via healthcare practitioners in both public and private settings. Nevertheless, the sources of antibiotics may differ, and they may occasionally be obtained through other means. Antibiotics are taken inappropriately due to patient misunderstanding in Malaysia [10–13]. It was found that up to 68.4% of antibiotic prescriptions for URTIs are unnecessary, with the general public having inadequate awareness of how antibiotics work or that AMR is a potential consequence of their use [13, 14]. There are several misconceptions, especially in rural areas, where many think that antibiotics are needed to treat viral illnesses or to ease pain [15]. While existing studies demonstrate the extent of inappropriate antibiotic use, they offer limited understanding of how patients perceive and manage antibiotics during URTI episodes, contributing to a persistent gap between clinical guidelines and real-world practice [8, 16, 17]. To better understand these issues, Malaysia was included in the second phase of the global ‘Sore Throat and Antibiotic Resistance (STAR)’ study, focusing on public perceptions, behaviors, and antibiotic use to treat URTIs. The findings are intended to support the development of effective, context-specific antibiotic stewardship initiatives, particularly in primary care. It was hypothesized that widespread awareness of AMR coexists with persistent misconceptions about antibiotics, which in turn contribute to inappropriate antibiotic use for URTIs and sore throats.

Methods

Study Design

The study protocol was approved by the Reading Independent Ethics Committee Review Board (now designated as the South Central-Berkshire B Research Ethics Committee) and adhered to all relevant local and regional regulatory requirements. This nationwide, cross-sectional, web-based survey was conducted in June 2024 and is reported in accordance with the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) (Supplementary Table 1) [18]. The survey constituted the second phase of the global STAR study [19–21]. The aforementioned study is a multinational survey that examines public knowledge, perceptions, and behaviors regarding antibiotic use for sore throat and URTIs. Earlier phases investigated self-management practices, healthcare-seeking behavior, and factors driving antibiotic use across multiple countries, using surveys to identify misconceptions and contributors to inappropriate antibiotic use.

Setting and Participants

The target population comprised adults aged 18–64 years, residing in Peninsular Malaysia (West Malaysia) and the states of Sabah and Sarawak (East Malaysia), who reported experiencing a sore throat or other URTI symptoms within the preceding six months. The six-month recall period was selected to align with the global STAR study protocol and to allow for sufficient sample size, given that shorter recall windows might have limited the number of eligible participants. Peninsular Malaysia, located on the Malay Peninsula, contains the capital city, Kuala Lumpur, and is the most densely populated region of the country. East Malaysia, comprising the states of Sabah and Sarawak on the island of Borneo, is less densely populated and geographically separated from the peninsula by the South China Sea. Participants self-reported their symptoms and did not need to have consulted a doctor. Participants could have experienced any number of the URTI symptoms listed in the questionnaire and did not have to have a sore throat as one of their symptoms.

Respondents were recruited from an online consumer database maintained by Incite Marketing Planning (London, UK) [22] which included individuals who voluntarily registered to participate in surveys. This database compiles individual-level information from publicly available records, commercial data partnerships, and consumer-provided data collected through surveys, registrations, and other opt-in interactions. Data acquisition and processing were conducted in accordance with applicable data protection and privacy regulations. The survey was administered using the Toluna online panel platform [23]. Stratified random sampling was applied to achieve proportional representation across age group, gender, and region. Participants were randomly selected within each stratum according to their proportion in the Malaysian adult population, ensuring that the final sample captured a representative cross-section of adults, even if some chose not to respond. The survey was a closed survey, accessible only to invited participants who met the predefined eligibility criteria.

Recruitment Process and Survey Administration

Eligible participants received an invitation to participate via the online panel system. The survey was voluntary, and no information was collected that could directly identify participants. The survey was designed to take no more than 20 min, as this reflects the typical time participants are willing to commit to accurate completion. Pilot testing was undertaken prior to questionnaire finalization to verify that the number of questions could be completed within this timeframe. No specific time limit was applied to participants to complete. The survey was conducted using a single-session design. Respondents were able to review and change their answers before final submission. No randomization of items was applied, and all the questions were presented in a fixed order. Before accessing the questionnaire, participants were presented with an online information page describing the study objectives, expected duration, voluntary nature of participation, anonymity of responses, and data protection measures. Electronic informed consent was obtained prior to survey initiation, and only participants who provided consent were able to proceed.

Survey Instrument

The survey instrument consisted of 26 items, preceded by five screening questions, and was adapted from the Eurobarometer survey on AMR [24]. The instrument was organized into four sections. The first collected basic demographic details, including age, gender, and region. The second explored symptom patterns, noting how often URTI symptoms occurred, how severe they were, and which symptoms tended to appear together. The third examined participants’ use of antibiotics and their self-treatment practices (defined as self-administering medications or remedies for symptomatic relief without consultation from a healthcare professional), the conditions being treated, and the reasons for choosing to self-treat. The final section assessed participants’ knowledge, perceptions, and behaviors through a 5-point Likert scale, capturing their awareness of antibiotic risks, understanding of AMR, and usual antibiotic-use habits. The full questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Data Quality Control and Response Rates

A range of data quality checks was conducted, including red herring questions, assessment of speeders and straight-liners, and automated in-field monitoring to detect unusual or inconsistent response patterns requiring further review or exclusion. Participation was restricted to individual accounts to avoid multiple entries from a single respondent. Responses were screened for completeness and duplication by the panel provider. Entries were considered incomplete and removed if participants did not answer all mandatory questions (e.g., key demographic items, URTI symptom reporting, or antibiotic use questions). Incomplete questionnaires and duplicated entries were excluded from the final dataset, and only fully completed surveys were analyzed.

Statistical Analysis

The data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 30 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized demographic characteristics, symptom prevalence, healthcare seeking behaviors, and antibiotic use. The Chi-square test was used for categorical data and Spearman’s rank correlation evaluated relationships between variables. Correlation coefficient (rs) values less than 0.3 were considered very weak, 0.3–0.5 low, 0.5–0.7 moderate, 0.7–0.9 high and more than 0.9 very high [25]. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1031 completed responses were analyzed. Respondents were evenly distributed by gender, comprising 51% men and 49% women. The largest age group was 25–34 years (32%), followed by 35–44 years (27%), 18–24 years (18%), 45–54 years (18%), and 55–64 years (6%). Most participants were from Selangor (28%), followed by Kuala Lumpur (15%) and Johor (12%). Smaller proportions were from Penang and Sabah (7% each), Perak and Sarawak (5% each), Kedah and Kelantan (4% each), and Malacca, Negeri Sembilan, Pahang, and Terengganu (3% each), with Perlis contributing the smallest proportion (1%).

Symptom Clusters

Sore throat was the symptom most often reported (62%), frequently occurring alongside nasal symptoms and cough. The average duration of symptoms was 3.5 days, with most episodes lasting 2–5 days (Fig. 1). 51.4% of respondents who reported a cough also had a sore throat (p < 0.001) (Table 1). Similarly, 53.8% of those with flu-like symptoms also reported a sore throat (p < 0.001).

Antibiotic Use and Treatment Practices

In the total cohort of 1031 respondents, antibiotic use for respiratory symptoms including cough and nasal symptoms, was reported by 72%, whereas antibiotic use for sore throat was reported by 33.4%. Most antibiotics (83%) were obtained from medical staff, with the rest from other sources (Fig. 2). Doctors were the leading providers of antibiotics, contributing 52%, followed by pharmacists at 15%. Antibiotics were commonly prescribed in response to symptoms such as flu-like symptoms and sore throats. Chest conditions such as bronchitis and pneumonia received the fewest prescriptions. Common over the counter (OTC) treatments included throat lozenges, painkillers, and other home remedies. Self-medication with OTC medications or home remedies was common for sore throats (62%), followed by sneezing (51.3%), nasal obstruction (51.2%), fever (46.7%), and cough (46.3%). Respondents quoted convenience, cost-effectiveness, and mildness of symptoms as reasons for this practice.

Method of acquiring antibiotics to treat upper respiratory tract illnesses

Healthcare Seeking Behavior

When last unwell with an URTI and seeking advice, 53% consulted a pharmacist, 37% consulted a general practitioner, 29% consulted a specialist doctor, and 14% consulted a nurse (Fig. 3). The top three reasons for consulting healthcare practitioners were to obtain explanation on the severity (48%), to discuss possible treatments (45%), and to receive something for the pain (31%). Most respondents, 81.7%, reported getting diagnostic tests before receiving antibiotics, while 15.9% received antibiotics without any testing. An additional 2.4% were unsure or unaware if testing had been done. Physical examination was the most common diagnostic method (55%), followed by blood tests (31%), throat swabs (26%), and chest x-rays (13%).

Knowledge, Perceptions, and Behaviors

The majority (79%) of respondents had seen messages about the unnecessary use of antibiotics, suggesting widespread delivery of information regarding AMR. However, despite 70% showing awareness of antibiotic resistance and 68% acknowledging the side effects of antibiotics (Table 2), misconceptions were still common, with 53.6% of respondents believing that antibiotics effectively treat viral respiratory illnesses (15.7% strongly, 37.9% somewhat), while 30.4% remained neutral and 12.9% disagreed. A considerable number still believed that antibiotics relieve pain (66.1%) and quickly alleviate symptoms (72.5%) for viral respiratory illnesses, showing a continued misunderstanding of their proper use. Many respondents lacked confidence in managing illnesses without antibiotics. Only 42% felt confident in non-antibiotic treatment strategies, while 25.2% lacked confidence and 32.9% remained neutral. Nearly 80% of respondents reported anxiety when not using antibiotics, indicating a strong emotional dependence on antibiotics. Unsafe behaviors were common, with 34% admitting to keeping leftover antibiotics and 45.4% admitting to stopping their treatment early when feeling better.

Discussion

This national survey as part of the global STAR study provides important information about antibiotic use for URTIs among the Malaysian public. The most common symptoms reported were typical of URTIs, often including sore throat alongside cough or nasal symptoms. Most symptoms resolved within 3 to 4 days, emphasizing that most URTIs tend to be short-lived and self-limiting. Although viral infections are prevalent, many visits to healthcare professionals still result in antibiotics being prescribed. A significant number of respondents reported using antibiotics for viral respiratory illnesses. This reflects a common global trend of inappropriate antibiotic use as seen within the STAR study for usually self-limiting illnesses. Although this study was conducted in Malaysia, the patterns of antibiotic misuse, misconceptions, and self-medication behaviors observed are consistent with reports from other settings. Therefore, the findings may be relevant to other countries, particularly those with similar healthcare access, antibiotic availability, and antimicrobial stewardship challenges.

The frequent occurrence of sore throats with cough and flu-like symptoms highlights the diagnostic challenges in URTIs. This symptom overlap can lead to uncertainty in identifying viral from bacterial causes, especially in primary care. Without reliable diagnostic tools, this uncertainty often results in unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions, even when guidelines advocate for symptomatic management in uncomplicated cases. The pattern of antibiotic prescriptions observed for various symptoms in this study suggests that decisions might be more heavily influenced by the perceived severity of symptoms and what patients expect, rather than by precise diagnoses. Nearly half of the respondents mentioned that they knew people who used antibiotics for URTIs, showing the social norms that support this behavior. The lack of clarity about how to differentiate bacterial and viral causes, especially with sore throats, adds to the problem. The low prevalence of Group A Streptococcus (GAS) in Malaysian residents with sore throats increases the urgency for better antibiotic stewardship. GAS occurs in only 2–5% of Malaysian adults with sore throats, compared with 5–15% globally in adults and 15–30% in children [26, 27], emphasizing that the low prevalence makes antibiotic treatment for most sore throat cases inappropriate.

Most respondents reported being aware of the unnecessary use of antibiotics, showing that ongoing awareness campaigns have reached a wide audience. However, misconceptions persist. Although many recognized the side effects of antibiotics and were aware of AMR, this knowledge has not consistently translated into safe use. Many respondents still thought that antibiotics reduce pain and speed up recovery from viral infections. This shows a gap between awareness and actual behavior regarding antibiotic use. This gap may come from a lack of trust in non-antibiotic treatments and deep-rooted cultural beliefs that see antibiotics as a ‘quick fix’. This indicates that information shared about AMR raises general awareness but does not sufficiently change people's perceptions or expectations. Stronger or more effective communications and stewardship from healthcare professionals are needed to help address this issue. Behavioral factors were significant in driving inappropriate antibiotic use. A deeply rooted issue is the fear of not using antibiotics and uncertainty about managing illnesses without them. This highlights that just understanding the issues is not sufficient to change behavior, especially when emotional factors like fear of complications and recovery uncertainty are not addressed. The emotional attachment to antibiotics may overshadow logical decision-making, even in those familiar with AMR. Misconceptions like thinking antibiotics ease pain or hasten recovery from viral infections worsen this problem. Unsafe practices, such as stopping antibiotics early and storing leftover ones, demonstrate ongoing nonconformity despite reasonable knowledge levels [28, 29].

There is an increasing trend toward self-medication for mild URTI symptoms, such as sore throat, nasal congestion, and cough. Several studies have outlined various factors influencing self-medication [30–35]. Convenience, affordability, and the notion that symptoms are mild drive this behavior, suggesting that many see URTIs as conditions that do not require professional advice. While OTC therapies can provide effective short-term symptomatic relief, prolonged or unresolved symptoms may increase patient anxiety and delay appropriate clinical assessment, which can subsequently drive inappropriate antibiotic requests when symptoms persist or worsen. As inflammation underlies many URTI symptoms, non-antibiotic symptomatic treatments, including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents such as low-dose topical flurbiprofen, as well as decongestants and antitussives, represent appropriate and effective alternatives for symptom control [9, 36]. While OTC treatments can relieve most symptoms, patients with prolonged or worsening illness often seek medical evaluation. In these cases, healthcare professionals play a critical role in determining whether antibiotics are medically indicated, based on clinical assessment and evidence-based guidelines. When antibiotics are not necessary, clinicians provide education to correct misconceptions about their effectiveness, advise on appropriate symptom management, and offer clear return precautions so that patients know when to seek further care if their condition does not improve or worsens. This approach helps ensure safe and rational use of antibiotics while addressing patient concerns.

This study demonstrates the inconsistency of high awareness but ongoing misconceptions about antibiotic use and AMR among the Malaysian public. These misunderstandings, combined with emotional reliance and unsafe practices, call for a comprehensive public health response. First, educational efforts should directly address specific misconceptions. Public campaigns must move beyond general awareness and focus on correcting myths, using culturally relevant messaging and accessible formats like infographics, short videos, and community storytelling [37]. Integrating AMR education into school programs can also encourage early behavior change [38]. Second, behavioral interventions are crucial to promote proper antibiotic use [39]. Community involvement, through peer-led discussions and behavioral nudges like text reminders and visual cues in healthcare environments, can encourage adherence to prescribed treatments and discourage keeping leftover antibiotics [40]. Sharing success stories of recovery for viral URTIs without antibiotics may help normalize alternative treatment methods. Improving communication between healthcare providers and patients is essential for addressing the anxiety-driven demand for antibiotics. Patients often seek antibiotics for reassurance, especially when symptoms are persistent or severe. Providers, in turn, may feel compelled to prescribe to keep patients satisfied. Since physical examinations are more often performed than laboratory tests, using clinical scoring systems like the Modified Centor or McIsaac criteria [8, 9] can help guide antibiotic prescribing more judiciously, reducing the risk of overprescription. The low confidence of the public in using non-antibiotic medications offers significant opportunity for healthcare professionals to encourage the first-line use of evidence-based supportive care treatments to antibiotics. Counseling the appropriate expectations regarding the course of URTIs is equally important. Providing guidance that a sore throat typically resolves within a week, flu-like symptoms may persist for up to two weeks, and cough can extend for three to four weeks can help reassure patients and reduce anxiety. Third, promoting diagnostic testing before prescribing antibiotics could effectively minimize unnecessary treatments [41]. Point of care tests, such as rapid antigen detection tests for streptococcal pharyngitis or C reactive protein tests, can help distinguish between bacterial and viral infections. Incorporating these tools into routine clinical workflows supports evidence-based prescribing and lowers diagnostic uncertainty. Additionally, institutional support is needed in terms of training, infrastructure, and reimbursement options to enable widespread adoption of these practices. Fourth, system level stewardship measures should be enhanced. These include enforcing policies that restrict antibiotic prescriptions, promoting unit-dose dispensing to avoid leftover medications, and setting up follow-up systems to support treatment adherence [42–44]. Surveillance of prescription patterns and giving feedback to healthcare providers can also strengthen accountability. Finally, collaborations across sectors such as media, religious leaders, educators, and community influencers can enhance the reach and credibility of AMR messaging. Regular monitoring and evaluating public knowledge and practices will be crucial to understanding the effectiveness of these antibiotic and supportive care interventions and guiding improvements over time. By addressing issues at both the patient and provider levels, these measures support global stewardship frameworks that encourage a more responsible and sustainable use of antibiotics.

Strengths and Limitations

Even though this study does not rely on electronic health records, it offers real-world behavioral understanding from the patient’s perspective, complementing clinical data and informing antimicrobial stewardship measures. The main strength lies in its focus on a common clinical presentation and its reflection on real healthcare practices. However, several limitations should be noted. Relying on self-reported data can introduce potential biases related to recall and social desirability, and the study’s design limits the ability to establish cause-and-effect relationships. The findings may not be generalizable outside Malaysia or to children, and the lack of a clinically verified diagnosis means that URTIs were not definitively diagnosed. Future research should consider longitudinal designs to evaluate how targeted antibiotic and supportive care interventions, including Centor-based decision support and stewardship efforts, affect antibiotic use. Rigorous comparative studies conducted to evaluate interventions will be essential for developing workable strategies to address AMR. This study is limited by the lack of analysis of respondents’ income and educational attainment in relation to antibiotic perceptions and usage. While these data were collected, they were beyond the scope of the current analysis and will be addressed in a subsequent publication.

Conclusions

Our findings highlight a major challenge. Despite widespread awareness of AMR, antibiotic use for viral sore throats and upper respiratory tract infections remains common. Misunderstandings, emotional dependence, and unsafe practices are key factors driving inappropriate use. To close the gap between guidelines and actual practice, various interventions are needed. These include clinical decision support, behavioral initiatives, and targeted public education. These strategies are vital to preventing misuse of antibiotics, preserving their effectiveness, and combating the rising issue of AMR.
