Comprehensive analysis of RNA-seq data reveals novel insight in the formation of bamboo embryogenic callus in Bambusa changningensis
Authors: Xiaoyan Gu, Zhou Fang, Jiarui Tan, Sen Chen, Jiahui Peng, Wei Feng, Yiqin Huang, Ying Cao, Shanglian Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bamboo usually undergoes a prolonged vegetative growth period for several decades. Additionally, not all bamboo species produce seeds, and the regulatory mechanisms governing embryogenic callus formation remain unclear, which constrains molecular breeding progress in bamboo. Here, we used buds of Bambusa changningensis Yi et B. X. Li as explants for callus induction. The results revealed that bamboo embryogenic callus (EC) on media added coconut powder, 2,4-D and proline increased faster than that on other media. The content of EC increased with the increase of 2,4-D concentration on different media. The concentrations of auxin and gibberellin (GA) in non-embryogenic callus (NE) were significantly higher than those in EC. Furthermore, RNA-seq analysis revealed that 795 DEGs were mainly enriched in pathways of starch synthase and amyloplast metabolism. Gene set analysis revealed that genes associated with amylopectin synthesis and metabolism pathways were upregulated in EC, and several starch synthesis-related genes were significantly enriched in EC. We then observed that EC exhibited more clearly amyloplast, higher starch content, and three starch metabolism related genes (SS1, SBE1, STP-1) showed higher expression levels compared to NE. Collectively, these results identified that the transition from NE to EC was associated with amyloplast synthesis and metabolism, and it providing new insights into the establishment of callus regeneration and genetic transformation systems in bamboo.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07267-2.

Introduction

As one of the most fast-growing forest plants in the world, bamboo can complete growing season within 2–4 months [1]. Bamboo usually going through a prolonged vegetative growth period for several decades, not all bamboo species produce seeds [2], which leads to the difficulty in callus induction by using seed embryos as explants and genetic improvement of bamboo through traditional cross breeding [1]. So it’s important to choose the appropriate explant for bamboo callus induction and improve genetic breeding in bamboo by using asexual propagation methods. However, the regulating mechanism of bamboo callus regeneration remain unclear, and there is no any universal and stable asexual propagation methods used in bamboo, which greatly restricts molecular breeding of bamboo [3]. B. changningensis is a new bamboo species and natural hybrid of Bambusa rigida and Dendrocalamus farinosus, which mainly distribute in Changning County and Wenxin County, Sichuan Province [4]. As the important paper-pulp bamboo species and valuable economic plant, B. changningensis is characterized by high hardness, high fiber contents, and especially remarkably high regeneration ability [5].

Based on tissue culture, agrobacterium-mediated transformation is widely used in dicotyledons [6]. But in gramineous plants, the explants for callus induction are limited to the lack of cambium, so seed become the most important organ for inducing callus [7]. However, the unique flowering characteristics of bamboo greatly limits the establishment of bamboo tissue culture system [8]. So the appropriate explant is essential for successful transformation in bamboo. Callus induction has been used to establish genetic transformation and gene editing systems in several bamboo species. For example, young shoot of Dendrocalamus latiforus Munro and seed embryo of Phyllostachys edulis were used as explants to establish agrobacterium mediated genetic transformation methods, respectively [3, 9, 10]. Meanwhile, the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing systems were established in D. latiforus Munro and P. edulis [3, 10]. Recently, the method of in-planta, agrobacterium-mediated transient gene expression for exogenous genes was developed in P. edulis [1]. An efficient transient transformation of protoplasts from different tissues was developed in P. edulis and D. latiforus Munro [11]. The expression system with large cargocapacity for function research of bamboo gene was constructed using bamboo mosaic virus [12]. But the existing genetic transformation and gene editing systems in bamboo were restricted by the genotypes and in vitro regeneration capability of bamboo [1]. So bamboo gene function verification was usually conducted in other species instead of bamboo, such as Arabidopsis and rice [13, 14].

The transform process of NE to EC is important for callus regeneration, which is mediated by transcription factors and specifically expressed genes. The analysis of transcriptomes were usually carried out to identify embryogenesis related genes in plant embryonic callus formation [15]– [16]. Many gene families that respond to embryogenesis and regeneration in vitro have been identified in plants, such as LEAFY COTYLEDON (LEC1/LEC2) [17], BABY BOOM (BBM) [18], WUSCHEL (WUS) [19], wound-induced (WIND1) [20], WUSCHEL RELATED HOMEOBOX (WOX5/WOX7/WOX11/WOX12) [21–23], LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARIES-DOMAIN 16 (LBD16) [24], PLETHORA (PLT1/PLT2) [25], SCARECROW (SCR) and SHORT ROOT (SHR) [26]. Besides important genes, the transform process of NE to EC was involved in other factors including nutritional additives and growth regulators [15]. For instance, supplementation of the medium with proline, glycine and hydrolyzed casein enhanced the frequency of somatic embryogenesis [15]. More EC were obtained by supplementing the date palm-containing medium with glutamine and polyamine [27]. Exogenous proline could facilitate the conversion of rice callus into EC [28].

Utilizing regeneration-related genes during in vitro culture enhanced callus regeneration, thereby improving plant transformation efficiency [29]. For example, miR156 and WOX2a have been identified to regulate SE in citrus [30] and maize [31], respectively. LEC2 has been shown to promote the formation of somatic embryos by activating the early embryonic patterning genes WOX2 and WOX3 [32]. WOX5 has been demonstrated to overcome genotype dependency in wheat genetic transformation [29]. Cellular polarity and embryogenic totipotency were initiated by SELTP-assembled amyloplasts within a single embryogenic cell [33]. But only a limited number of regeneration-related genes have undergone functional analysis in bamboo. For instance, D. hamiltonii NiR may contribute to enhancing the regeneration capacity and transformation efficiency in bamboo [34]. Additionally, DlamiR156 has been shown to improve regeneration efficiency in transgenic D. latiflorus [35]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying callus regeneration in bamboo remain poorly understood. There is an urgent need to characterize the genes associated with the transformation from NE to EC and the regulation of regeneration capacity in bamboo.

Here, we used buds of B. changningensis as explants for callus induction. The bamboo callus increment on CMS2 medium was much more than that on MS2 medium, whereas the XMS medium (supplemented with 2,4-D, proline, glutamine and casein hydrolysate) was more conducive to the growth of EC. The content of EC increased with the increase of 2,4-D concentration on different media. The levels of auxin and GA in NE were significantly higher than those in EC. RNA-seq analysis identified 795 DEGs during the callus development of B. changningensis. Further analysis revealed that these DEGs were primarily associated with amyloplast and starch grain-related processes. Gene set analysis illustrated that key genes including MES17, CYP71C4, ATPase, APG, SS1, Starch phosphorylase H, STP-1 and SBE1 that involved in starch synthesis were upregulated during the transition from NE to EC. Furthermore, starch content was significantly higher in EC compared to NE. Additionally, the amyloplast synthesis and metabolism related gene SBE1, SS1 and STP1 exhibited higher expression levels in EC compared to NE. Thus, we suggested that the synthesis and degradation of amyloplast were crucial for the transition of NE to EC, and our results providing new insights into the establishment of callus induction and genetic transformation systems in bamboo.

Results

The formation and growth of callus in B. changningensis

B. changningensis used in this study that planted at Southwest University of Science and Technology. Healthy stem segments of bamboo were sterilized, rinsed and then removed buds (Fig. 1A). These stem segments and separate buds of B. changningensis were both used as explants, which were cultivated for callus induction on MS media for 15 days (Fig. 1B and C). The frequency of callus induction from buds and stem segments were 50%~80% and 30%~50%, respectively. The results indicated that young buds were more appropriate explants for callus induction than stem segments. So we then used sterile bamboo buds to perform induction, differentiation and proliferation of callus in B. changningensis. To analyze the effect of different media on the growth of B. changningensis callus, we inoculated the callus to MS2, CMS2 (containing 2 g/L coconut powder) and PMS2 (containing 2 g/L proline) media for 30 days, respectively (Fig. 1B). The results showed that bamboo callus cultivated on MS2 media were white-transparent and loose texture, and the bamboo callus cultivated on CMS2 and PMS2 media showed obvious yellow and loose phenotype (Fig. 1B).

Furthermore, we found that on MS2 medium, the day increment of callus reached to the peak was 0.267 g per unit weight during 15–20 days, and callus increment was 3.14 g per unit weight after 30 days. However, on CMS2 medium, the day increment of callus reached to the peak during 20–25 days that was 0.548 g per unit weight, and callus increment was 6.86 g per unit weight after 30 days (Fig. 1D). These results showed that the proliferation and differentiation of NE and the growth rate of bamboo callus growing on CMS2 medium supplemented with coconut powder were much higher than those cultured on MS2 medium. The differentiated and proliferated callus was used to the induction of EC.

Morphology of callus during the transformation from NE to EC in B. changningensis

The callus induced from buds of B. changningensis was cultivated on CMS5 medium (containing 5 g/L coconut powder) for 60 days, two types of callus including NE and EC were produced. Meanwhile, the morphology and cell configuration of bamboo callus were observed using scanning electron microscope and paraffin section method. There were obvious differences in the morphology of NE and EC. NE showed a phenotype of white color, soft, uneven surface and loose texture (Fig. 2A), and the cells of NE were fewer, relatively large and disorganized (Fig. 2C). The morphology of NE started to change after two weeks of culture, and EC emerged following a further four weeks of culture under dark conditions. The cell fate had changed from normal callus cells to embryonic cells during this time. And the EC showed yellow, smooth and tight texture (Fig. 2B), the cells were more closely and highly organized, globular embryos began to appear (Fig. 2D). The paraffin section revealed that EC cells were more numerous and smaller in size compared to those of NE (Fig. 2E and F).

Effects of different medium on the growth of EC of B. changningensis

To promote the large-scale generation of EC B. changningensis, we cultivated EC on four different media including XMS (containing 2 mg/L 2,4-D, 1 g/L proline, 1 g/L glutamine, 0.6 g/L casein hydrolysate), MSA (containing 0.1 mg/L 2,4-D), MSB (containing 0.5 mg/L 2,4-D) and MSC (containing 1 mg/L 2,4-D) for 30 days, respectively. The results showed that the increment of EC on XMS medium were higher than that on other three media (Fig. 3A), and the increment of EC reached to the peak that was 1.67 g per unit weight on XMS medium after 30 days (Fig. 3A), which might be associated with the medium supplemented with proline, glutamine and casein hydrolysate. The increase in EC was positively correlated with the rising concentration of 2,4-D on MSA, MSB and MSC media. Such as the EC increment was 0.45 g per unit weight after 30 days on MSA medium that supplemented with the lowest concentration of 2,4-D. Morphology of B. changningensis EC also showed significant different on these four media after 30 days (Fig. 3B). EC cultivated on XMS medium was dark yellow and tight texture. EC cultivated on MSA medium showed light yellow and loose texture (Fig. 3B). These results indicated that the XMS medium was more conducive to EC growth. Therefore, EC was continuously cultured on XMS medium, then green buds and roots began to emerge after 60 and 90 days, respectively (Fig. 3C).

Furthermore, we determined the level of endogenous hormone in NE and EC. The results showed that the levels of auxin and GA in NE were much higher than those in EC. Compared to other media, higher levels of auxin and GA were determined in EC on CMS5 and MSA media. The levels of 6-BA in NE on CMS5 medium and in EC on MSA medium were both highest. The 6-BA levels in EC on XMS medium were the lowest, measuring 3.88 µg/g. Additionally, the levels of auxin, GA and 6-BA in EC decreased with increasing 2,4-D concentration on MSA, MSB and MSC media (Fig. 3D and E). The levels of ETH in NE were lower than those in EC, and ETH levels in EC on MSB medium were the lowest. No significant differences were observed in the ETH concentration of EC on CMS5, XMS, MSA and MSC media (Fig. 3E). The contents of soluble sugar and protein in NE were both the lowest, measuring 6.7% and 0.128 mg/g, respectively. Moreover, both the contents of soluble sugar and protein were highest in EC cultured on XMS media, reaching 57.3% and 0.73 mg/g, respectively, which may be associated with the high concentrations of proline, glutamine and casein hydrolysate added to the XMS medium. Compared to CMS5 medium, EC cultured on XMS, MSA, MSB, and MSC media exhibited higher soluble sugar levels, whereas EC on MSA, MSB, and MSC media showed lower soluble protein contents. (Fig. 3F). The peroxidase activity in callus exhibited no significant difference between CMS5 and XMS media, but both were higher than those on MSA, MSB and MSC media. Additionally, peroxidase activity in EC increased with the increasing 2,4-D concentration on different media (Fig. 3F).

Analysis of gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across different developmental stages in B. changningensis callus

To analyze the gene expression levels that regulating induction of callus at different developmental stages, we extracted mRNAs from EC vs. NE, ECX vs. NEX and ECP vs. NEP to perform Illumina sequencing, a total of 62.08 Gb clean data was obtained. Each sample contained 6.16 Gb clean data, and the percentage of quality score above 30 (Q30) was more than 92.92%. Compared to the specified reference genome, the alignment efficiency ranged from 78.25 to 81.36% by sequencing the clean reads of each sample (Table S1). A total of 4332 DEGs were obtained in ECX vs. NEX, of which 2846 genes were up-regulated and 1486 genes were down-regulated. The 4264 DEGs were found in EC vs. NE with 2408 up-regulated and 1856 down-regulated DEGs. Whereas 7679 DEGs were obtained in ECP vs. NEP, which had 4658 up-regulated and 3021 down-regulated genes (Fig. 4A and Tables S2-3). These results showed that there were significant differences in gene expression levels between different developmental stages in NE and EC. Furthermore, we obtained 795 DEGs as EC induction genes in ECX vs. NEX, EC vs. NE and ECP vs. NEP, which suggested that these genes were particularly associated with the transition from NE to EC (Fig. 4B).

To study the functions of these unigenes, we carried out the analysis of GO and KEGG (Table S4). GO analysis showed that these 795 DEGs were classified into three major categories: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC) and molecular function (MF). The 30 subcategories were divided into three major categories (Fig. 4C). The biological processes including the metabolic process, catabolic process and biosynthetic process of glucan, starch, carbohydrate and polysaccharide. The five major components of CC were plant-type vacuole, amyloplast, extracellular matrix, stromule and starch grain. The molecular function categories included transmembrane transportation, starch synthase activity and NADPH binding (Fig. 4C). KEGG analysis revealed that these 795 DEGs in ECX vs. NEX, EC vs. NE and ECP vs. NEP were enriched 30 pathways, the most representative pathways were starch and sucrose metabolism, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, glutathione metabolism and drug resistance (Fig. 4D).

Identification of gene regulatory sets related to the transition of NE to EC

To identify the specific genes or molecular responses driving the morphological differences between NE and EC, we constructed the functional gene sets for all transcriptome datasets of callus across different developmental stages using gene set enrichment analysis (GSVA) (Table S5). The result illustrated that gene sets involving starch grain synthetic pathways and amyloplast metabolic pathways were upregulated in all groups (Fig. 5A and Table S6). SS4, GBSSII_R1, SSII-3, WAXY, SS1_R1, WSSI-2, SSII-2_R1, SS1_R2, GBSSII_R2 were down-regulated in NE and remarkedly enriched in nearly all three groups of EC, suggesting these genes might be involved in the transition from NE to EC (Fig. 5B).

Analysis of specific molecular functions for gene sets and co-expression network in callus development

To further analyze the specific molecular functions of gene sets during callus development. 30 pathways were enriched using GO classification method, and these cluster were roughly categorized into three patterns (Fig. 6A and Table S7). The biological processes included the plastid and chloroplast organization, photosynthesis light reaction and porphyrin-containing compound metabolic process. The major components of CC were plastid and chloroplast inner membrane. The group of molecular function were isomerase activity, oxidoreductase activity and intramolecular oxidoreductase activity. Consistent with the GO results, KEGG analysis revealed that all genes were enriched across 30 pathways, with the most representative ones been the pentose phosphate pathway, antigen processing, porphyrin metabolism, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms (Fig. 6B and Table S7). These results indicated that the plastid/chloroplast organization and pentose phosphate pathways exist in the transition of NE to EC.

Analysis of amyloplast morphology and expression of starch synthesis related genes during EC formation

To further understand the molecular mechanism during the transition of NE to EC. We examined cell morphology and measured the starch content in both NE and EC at different stages. The results demonstrated that EC exhibited more distinct amyloplasts in EC compared to NE (Fig. 8A and B), and the starch content in EC (86.31 mg/g) was significantly higher than that in NE (49.11 mg/g) (Fig. 8C). We also documented a sequential transition in EC, where callus cells without amyloplasts (stage 1) developed into those with amyloplasts (stage 2, 3 and 4), and then reverted to callus cells without amyloplasts (stage 5) (Fig. 8B). Meanwhile, we validated the expression levels of four key crucial embryonic development-related genes (SS1, STP-1, SBE1 and CYP71C4) by qRT-PCR analysis (Table S9). Consistent with WGCNA analysis, SS1, SBE1, CYP71C4 and particularly STP-1, exhibited significantly higher expression levels in EC compared to NE (Fig. 8D). Based on the analysis of amyloplast morphology and expression of key genes associated with EC formation, we proposed that the process of amyloplast synthesis and metabolism was critical for the transition from NE to EC.

Discussion

Bamboo is one of the fastest-growing forest plants in the world, typically undergoing a prolonged vegetative growth period for several decades [2]. So it’s difficulty to induce callus by using seed embryos and flowers as explants [1]. It is essential to select appropriate explants for bamboo callus induction and establish an efficient regeneration system through callus induction [2]. Establishing a regeneration system via callus inducing serves as the foundation for constructing bamboo’s genetic transformation system. However, the precise molecular mechanisms governing callus regeneration remain unclear, which greatly restricts the molecular breeding in bamboo [3].

Compared with dicotyledons, the obtain of tender plant tissue from monocotyledons is difficult, so seed embryo is generally used to inducing callus in monocotyledons, such as wheat and bamboo [36, 37]. The regeneration capacity of shoot-derived callus is significantly lower than that of seed-derived callus in plants [38]. Seed embryos of P. edulis were used as explants to establish an agrobacterium mediated genetic transformation methods [3]. However, not all bamboo species produce seeds, which make it’s extremely difficult to obtain bamboo seeds in nature [2]. So young shoots have been widely used as explants to establish agrobacterium mediated genetic transformation protocols in D. latiforus Munro [9]. Similarly, we induced callus from sterile shoots derived from culture of bamboo nodes in B. changningensis (Fig. 1). The regenerative capacity was very low and depended heavily on bamboo genotypes, which restricted the existing genetic transformation and gene editing systems in bamboo [1]. We found that the young and delicate B. changningensis tissue was the most desirable explant for induction of plant callus because its strong regeneration ability. Here, we established an effective protocol for the transition from NE to EC in bamboo by using the buds of B. changningensis as explants. Based on this efficient EC induction system, we investigated the molecular dynamics during the transition from NE to EC. Key genes and putative regulatory pathways governing both the transition of NE to EC and shoot regeneration were identified in B. changningensis.

The transition from NE to EC is influenced by multiple factors, including nutritional supplements, growth regulators, particularly transcription factors and functional genes [15]. For instance, supplementing the medium with proline, glycine and hydrolyzed casein has been shown to enhance the frequency of somatic embryogenesis [15]. Additionally, a higher yield of EC was achieved by adding glutamine and polyamine to the liquid or solid media supplemented with date palm [27]. Exogenous proline has been shown to enhance the transition of rice callus to EC [28]. In accordance with these results, we found that coconut powder effectively promoted bamboo callus growth, and the XMS medium (containing 2,4-D, proline, glutamine and casein hydrolysate) was more favorable for EC growth (Fig. 3A). Accordingly, 2,4-D increased the amount of EC by using different media containing different concentration of 2,4-D. The levels of auxin and GA in NE were much higher than those in EC, and EC contained higher levels of auxin and GA on CMS5 medium supplemented coconut powder compared to other media (Fig. 3D-E). The levels of 6-BA in NE on CMS5 medium and in EC on MSA medium were both highest (Fig. 3E). The contents of soluble sugar and protein in NE were much lower than those in EC (Fig. 3F). The highest levels of soluble sugar and protein were detected in EC on XMS media, which might be related to the high concentration of proline, glutamine and casein hydrolysate supplemented. The lower levels of 6-BA and soluble sugar were detected from EC on media supplemented high concentration of 2,4-D, which suggesting that 2,4-D inhibited the generation of 6-BA and soluble sugar. But the activity of peroxidase in EC increased with the increase of 2,4-D concentration on MSA, MSB and MSC media.

Moreover, the transition from NE to EC was analyzed by transcriptome sequencing. GO and KEGG analyses revealed that the DEGs were primarily enriched in biosynthetic and metabolic processes of glucan, starch and carbohydrates. These DEGs were annotated to molecular functions such as transmembrane transport, NADPH binding activity, and especially starch synthase activity, starch and amyloplast-specific genes, which were consistent with previous research findings (Fig. 4 and Table S4). For example, starch grain deposition and amylase accumulation were observed during the maturation of somatic embryos [30]. Cellular polarities and embryogenic totipotency were initiated by amyloplasts within individual embryogenic cell [33].

Meanwhile, consistent with the results from GO/KEGG analysis of DEGs and GSVA analysis, the WGCNA analysis identified specific genes or signaling pathways activated during the transition from NE to EC, including MES17, CYP71C4, Plasma membrane ATPase, APG, SS1, Starch phosphorylase H, STP-1 and SBE1 (Fig. 7 and Table S8). The primary function of SS and SBE has been demonstrated to be the synthesis of branched starch [39]. And starch biosynthesis is crucial for embryo development during seed germination [40]. Moreover, amyloplasts assembled and polarized by SELTP are essential for initiating the embryogenic pathway [33]. Consistent with WGCNA analysis and previous studies, we observed that starch content was higher in EC than that in NE (Fig. 8C). Within EC, callus cells underwent a sequential transition: started from amyloplast-free callus cells (stage 1), changed to callus cells with amyloplasts (stage 2, 3 and 4), and finally reverted to the callus cells without amyloplasts (stage 5) (Fig. 8B). Notably, SS1, SBE1, CYP71C4 and particularly STP-1 exhibited higher expression levels during the transition from NE to EC (Fig. 8D). As an α-1,4 glucan starch phosphorylase, STP1 may mediate amyloplast degradation during the transition from stage 4 to stage 5 in EC. Collectively, these observations lead us to hypothesize that the process of amyloplast synthesis and degradation play a critical role in EC formation, thereby facilitating bamboo callus regeneration. Our findings provide novel insights into promoting bamboo EC formation, which is conducive to the establishment of efficient regeneration systems.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the medium containing 2,4-D, proline, glutamine and casein hydrolysate was conducive to EC growth in bamboo. RNA-seq analysis revealed that 795 DEGs were primarily associated with amyloplast and starch synthesis during B. changningensis callus development. Key genes including MES17, CYP71C4, ATPase, APG, SS1, Starch phosphorylase H, STP-1 and SBE1 that involving the starch synthesis exhibited upregulation during the transition from NE to EC. Additionally, EC displayed higher starch contents, and amyloplasts along with three starch metabolism related genes (SS1, SBE1, STP-1) were remarkedly enriched during the transition from NE to EC in bamboo. Collectively, these findings indicate that amyloplast synthesis and metabolism are involved in the transition from NE to EC, thereby providing novel insights into the establishment of systems for callus induction and genetic transformation in bamboo.

Materials and methods

Plant tissue and callus induction

B. changningensis planted at Southwest University of Science and Technology that was introduced from Changning County, Yibin City, Sichuan Province, China (28°29′N, 104°58′E) as reported previously [5]. Healthy bamboo nodes were sterilized with 70% ethanol and 0.1% HgCl for 30 s and 30 min, respectively, and then washed with sterile distilled water for six times. The sterilized bamboo nodes were inserted in MS medium and shoots were gained for callus induction after 15 d. NE was incubated on MS2 medium supplemented with 2 mg/L 2,4-D, 0.4 mg/L IBA and 0.2 mg/L KT for 4 weeks at 28 °C under continuous dark condition. NE was then transferred on CMS5 medium containing 2 mg/L 2,4-D, 0.4 mg/L IBA, 0.2 mg/L KT and 5 g/L coconut powder for embryogenic inducing, and EC was transited from NE that were cultured for six weeks in dark condition.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) of callus

The callus of non-embryogenic and embryogenic were fixed with glutaric dialdehyde solution for 48 h and vacuum freeze-dried for 48 h. The dried callus samples were used for morphological observation by SEM.

Paraffin section

The callus of non-embryogenic and embryogenic were soaked in 50%, 70%, 90% ethanol and absolute ethanol for 2 h, and fixed with FAA solution for 2 d, then soaked in 50%, 70%, 90% and 100% xylene mixing with ethanol for 2 h, soaked in the mixture of 50% xylene and paraffin at 60 °C for eight hours, subsequently. The mixture solution of 50% xylene and paraffin were replaced every 2 h for 3 times, and finally embedded and solidified with pure paraffin liquid to prepare for slicing.

Illumina sequencing and data analysis

The total RNA were extracted from EC vs. NE, ECX vs. NEX and ECP vs. NEP for Illumina sequencing using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China), and the amount of each sample was 1 µg. The sequencing library was generated by using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit. Poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads were applied on mRNA purification from total RNA. First strand cDNA was synthesized and second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA-seq analysis included three biological replicates. A total of 62.08 Gb clean data was obtained, and the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) value were calculated using HISAT2 and StringTie (Table S1). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between different tissues were filtered using DESeq2 with logFC > 2 and FDR < 0.01 (Tables S2-3).

Gene functional annotation

Gene function was annotated based on the following databases: Nr (NCBI non-redundant protein sequences); Nt (NCBI non-redundant nucleotide sequences); Pfam (Protein family); KOG/COG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups of proteins); Swiss-Prot (A manually annotated and reviewed protein sequence database); KO (KEGG Ortholog database); GO (Gene Ontology).

KEGG and GO pathway enrichment analysis, GSVA and WGCNA analysis

Gene expression matrix between different samples were transformed to enrichment score matrix of GO gene matrix using clusterProfiler. The GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis and result visualization by using clusterProfiler (Table S4). The variance analysis for gene set was performed by using edgeR (log2FC > 0.5, p value < 0.05). The variance analysis results of pathways were visualized using ggplot2 (Table S5). The heat map of variance pathways analysis for gene set was created using pheatmap. The correlation network analysis was performed by using WGCNA, and hub genes were obtained from differently expression genes and the network of hub genes was visualized by using cytoscape (Tables S6-8). All software package including clusterProfiler, edgeR, ggplot2, pheatmap, clusterProfiler, WGCNA and cytoscape were all obtained from R package.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Callus of B. changningensis including NE and EC were used to extract total RNA by using RNA extract kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). We then checked the quality of total RNA by using NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Beijing, China) and agarose gel eletrophoresis. cDNA was reverse transcribed by the FastKing Quantscript RT Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). All primers were designed by Primer Premier 5.0, and the internal reference genes were Tubulin and Actin [41] (Table S9). The expression level of starch synthesis related genes was performed through qRT-PCR analysis that by using SYBR qPCR Master MIX kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and Real-Time System thermal cycler (CFX96 Touch, BIO-RAD, California, USA). The qRT-PCR program condition was 98 °C for 4 min, 40 cycles of 98 °C for 20 s and 60 °C for 30 s, 55–90 °C for 30 s and 2-ΔΔCt method was used to calculate relative transcriptional levels of starch synthesis related genes. The experiment included three biological replicates, and each with three technical replicates.

The starch staining and detection of starch contents in both NE and EC

2% (w/v) starch staining solution was prepared by dissolution of 2 g KI powder and 0.2 g I2 powder in 100 mL ddH2O. Starch content was measured by using starch content assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China).
