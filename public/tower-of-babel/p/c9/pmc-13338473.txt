A Longitudinal Study on Predicting Treatment Response in Depression Using Self-Reported Symptoms
Authors: Yoojin Song, Sang Jin Rhee, Daun Shin, Eun-Jeong Joo, Hyeyoung Kim, Hee Yeon Jung, Sungwon Roh, Kyu Young Lee, Myoung-nam Lim, Yong Min Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression causes serious disabilities, and response to treatment is difficult to predict. This study aimed to examine whether self-reported initial symptoms can predict treatment response in depression.MethodsOne hundred fifty-seven patients who were diagnosed with mood disorders (major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder) and in a state of moderate or severe depression were recruited from six psychiatric hospitals between 2018 and 2022. Participants were classified into treatment responders (n = 89) and non-responders (n = 68) based on the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale, and changes in a wide range of symptoms, including anxiety and depression, were assessed using both clinician-administered scales and self-reports. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with treatment response, and the performance of several prediction models using demographic information and symptom scores was compared.ResultsThere were no significant baseline differences between responders and non-responders in diagnoses, medications, clinician-rated scales, medication adherence, or objective depressive and anxiety scores. However, non-responders initially reported higher levels of obsessive-compulsive symptoms, interpersonal sensitivity, phobic anxiety, and paranoid ideation, along with lower satisfaction in social relationships. The comparison of predictive models showed that those incorporating subjective symptoms outperformed models based solely on demographic covariates (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC] = 0.725, 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.643–0.807 vs. 0.666, 95% CI, 0.578–0.754). Notably, phobic anxiety was identified as a strong independent predictor of treatment response (odds ratio [OR], 1.93, 95% CI, 1.30–2.85).ConclusionSubjective symptoms other than depression or anxiety are important factors for predicting treatment response in depression. Therefore, incorporating patients' self-reported symptoms is crucial for predicting treatment response in depression.Keywords: Mood Disorders, Depression, Treatment Outcome, Self Reports, Anxiety

INTRODUCTION

Depression is a significant personal and societal burden that leads to severe disability.1 The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that by 2030, depressive disorders will result in more serious disability than any other condition.2 Depression includes unipolar and bipolar forms, which are classified as distinct diagnoses in the DSM-5. However, Akiskal and colleagues have continuously proposed various hypotheses regarding the bipolar spectrum, as reviewed by Terao,3 and there remains debate over whether these conditions can be clearly divided in a strictly dichotomous manner.4 Recent evidence suggests that, although several clinical and biological differences exist between unipolar and bipolar depression, a shared approach appears warranted when managing the clinical status of ‘depression.’4 Depression is also closely associated with suicide.5 Korean data shows that 39.8% of suicides are related to mood disorders.6 Furthermore, in 2022, South Korea had the highest suicide mortality level among countries of the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), with a rate of 25.2 per 100 000 individuals.6

Depression with a poor treatment response—defined as less than a 50% reduction in symptoms—is generally referred to as treatment-resistant depression (TRD). However, the precise definition of TRD varies somewhat in clinical practice, and recently, attention has shifted beyond pharmacological failure to emphasize the need for managing depression that does not achieve full remission.7,8,9 From this perspective, depression should be approached within a chronic disease management model, and for difficult-to-treat depression (DTD), even a symptom reduction of ≥ 25% has been argued to represent a clinically meaningful improvement.8,9 DTD underscores the importance of comprehensive assessment, encompassing not only depressive symptomatology but also subjective domains such as quality of life and functional impairment.

Even with prolonged treatment, 30% of patients with depression experience an insufficient response to therapy, and 50% face relapse or chronicity.10,11,12,13 The lack of response or remission to treatment has various psychosocial and environmental predictors, including sex, age, age of onset (early-onset), severity of depressive symptoms, presence of comorbid conditions, high stress levels, stressful life events, or childhood abuse.14,15,16 Furthermore, numerous studies have shown that a poor response to treatment is closely associated with the severity of depressive symptoms.16,17,18

According to most studies, it is difficult to accurately predict individual prognosis based solely on baseline symptom scores.19 Moreover, no single factor among the various variables associated with treatment outcome has been shown to independently provide reliable predictive power.19 Even prediction models that combine multiple variables have demonstrated limited performance.19 For example, a machine learning model that included pre-treatment depression scores measured by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) achieved an area under the curve of only approximately 0.57, suggesting predictive accuracy close to chance level.20 A limitation of this study was that the predictive model was constructed using only the PHQ-9 to assess depression severity, without incorporating other objective measures.

Symptom evaluation tools include both clinician-administered and self-report measures, and these two approaches are not interchangeable.21 In fact, discrepancies between clinician and patient ratings have been associated with poorer treatment outcomes.22 However, recent studies have tended to use only a single type of symptom severity measure, and few have investigated differences in treatment response and contributing factors among groups with similar levels of depressive symptoms.

In this study, we aimed to examine the role of self-reported initial symptoms in predicting treatment response in depression.

METHODS

Participants

This study included 157 patients who were diagnosed with a mood disorder (major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder) and in a moderate or severe state of depression according to the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS; score ≥ 20).23 Individuals were recruited between August 2018 and June 2022 from Seoul National University Hospital; Nowon Eulji Medical Center and Uijeongbu Eulji Medical Center, Eulji University; Inha University Hospital; Seoul National University Boramae Medical Center; and Hanyang University Hospital. Diagnosis was based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition. Patients were classified as responders or non-responders based on a ≥ 25% reduction in MADRS scores after 2 months.24,25,26 As all participants were recruited from university hospitals, we considered the practical likelihood of a higher proportion of patients with poor treatment response.

As this study serves as a secondary analysis from proteomic research using blood samples, the following exclusion criteria were applied: use of anti-inflammatory analgesics (including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and steroids, with the exception of acetaminophen) in the 2 weeks prior to the study; a history of neuromodulation or neurosurgery; central nervous system diseases (including epilepsy, meningitis, Parkinson’s disease, and stroke); cancer; tuberculosis; current lactation or pregnancy; a history of substance abuse other than alcohol, caffeine, or nicotine; intensive psychotherapy in the 2 months before participation; predicted intellectual disability; or difficulty interpreting Korean. To exclude individuals in manic or mixed states, only those with a Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) score < 13 at baseline were included in the study.27

Baseline evaluation items

A comprehensive baseline evaluation was conducted, comprising sociodemographic factors such as age, sex, marital status, socioeconomic status, and occupation; body mass index; smoking habits; physical activity levels; alcohol consumption; and non-psychiatric medical treatments. Further, psychiatric data was collected, including age at onset, age at first treatment, family history of psychiatric conditions, and current psychiatric medications. Additionally, participants were asked to complete self-report measures, which included the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, Wender Utah Rating Scale, Composite Scale of Morningness, and Seasonal Pattern Assessment Questionnaire.28,29,30,31

Symptom evaluation at baseline and 2 months

The study employed the MADRS to evaluate depression symptoms both at baseline and after 2 months, while the YMRS was used at baseline to rule out manic or mixed states.32,33 The Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) was used to assess objective anxiety, and the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) was used to evaluate general psychiatric symptoms.34,35 The 24-item BRPS was divided into four categories (affect, positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and activation) based on previous research.36 Furthermore, participants completed the Symptom Checklist-90-Revised (SCL-90-R) and the brief form of the WHO Quality of Life Assessment Instrument (WHOQOL-BREF) as self-reported measures to evaluate a range of symptoms and quality of life.37,38 Finally, medication compliance was monitored during the 2-month study period and assessed by asking patients in interviews to recall the percentage of prescribed doses they had taken over the preceding 3 months.

Statistical analyses

To compare responders and non-responders, continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test, while categorical variables were assessed using the χ2 test. Repeated measures were tested using the paired t-test. Although this study was exploratory, the scales used in the analysis were additionally adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed on subjective self-reports and clinician-administered symptom scales that were significant in the univariate analysis. Demographic and clinical variables with P values < 0.1 were included as covariates. The performance of various logistic regression models using demographics and significant symptoms in predicting treatment response was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v21 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. 1806-1065-951) and all other participating hospitals and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant after the study procedures were fully explained.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Supplementary Table 1, no significant differences in hospitals attended, diagnosis, or psychiatric medication were observed between responders (n = 89) and non-responders (n = 68). The results of the analyses stratified by treatment response within the unipolar and bipolar groups are presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. Among non-responders with bipolar depression, the proportion of males is relatively high; this pattern contributes to the higher male proportion observed among non-responders in the overall participants. As shown in Table 1, there were no significant baseline differences in the average scores of the clinician-rated scales (MADRS, YMRS, HAMA, and BPRS) between the groups. Furthermore, no differences were found in the individual MADRS items (Supplementary Table 4). In contrast, significant differences were observed in several self-reported items at baseline, with non-responders having reported higher average scores in the obsessive-compulsive, interpersonal sensitivity, phobic anxiety, and paranoid ideation domains of the SCL-90-R, and lower scores in the WHOQOL-BREF social relationships domain (Table 1). By diagnosis, non-responders in the unipolar depression group showed higher CTQ scores as well as higher interpersonal sensitivity and phobic anxiety (Supplementary Table 5). In contrast, non-responders in the bipolar depression group reported lower satisfaction with social relationships (Supplementary Table 6). Among all participants, when classified by treatment response, CTQ scores did not differ between groups. After correction for multiple comparisons, only phobic anxiety was still significant (P = 0.042). After 2 months, treatment compliance was 92.3% (standard deviation [SD], 17.9%) for responders and 91.0% (SD, 21.1%) for non-responders, showing no statistical difference (P = 0.672).

Clinician-rated symptom changes

While responders showed significant symptom reductions across all 10 items of the MADRS after 2 months, non-responders only exhibited a mild reduction in suicidal thoughts (10th item of the MADRS, Table 2). Similarly, non-responders showed no changes in the total score or any subscales of the HAMA, or the negative symptoms and activation domains of the BPRS. The results of the analyses stratified by treatment response within the unipolar and bipolar groups are presented in Supplementary Tables 7 and 8. When stratified by diagnosis, the pattern mirrors that in Table 2: aside from a few domains, non-responders show minimal change in scores over 2 months. However, the BPRS total and its affect subscale displayed significant shifts even among non-responders. In the bipolar group, non-responders exhibited relatively large changes in suicidal ideation scores (Supplementary Table 8), a pattern that differed from that observed among unipolar non-responders (Supplementary Table 7).

Non-responders had significant but mild improvements in four domains of the SCL-90-R, while the remaining six (somatization, anxiety, anger-hostility, paranoid ideation, psychoticism, and additional items) demonstrated no changes. Logistic regression analysis

Logistic regression analysis was conducted on the six symptom domains from the SCL-90-R and WHOQOL-BREF that showed group differences in multivariate analyses, as summarized in Table 4. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, alcohol use, and age at onset. Models 2–6 present the results of separate logistic regression analyses for each of the five self-reported symptom domains: obsessive-compulsive, interpersonal sensitivity, phobic anxiety, paranoid ideation, and social relationships. Of the remaining covariates, sex was the only significant predictor (P < 0.05) in Models 2–5. However, this effect was not significant in Model 6, which included social relationship as an independent variable. All models demonstrated higher predictive performance compared to Model 1, with statistically significant improvements in the AUROC. Among the symptom domains, the model including phobic anxiety exhibited the strongest predictive performance. Model 7 included all five domains simultaneously and achieved an AUROC of 0.725 (95% confidence interval, 0.643–0.807), indicating superior predictive performance relative to models incorporating only a single symptom domain, though there was only a slight improvement when phobic anxiety was the included symptom (Model 4).

DISCUSSION

There were no significant differences between the two patient groups in terms of diagnosis, psychiatric medications, medication adherence, or baseline scores on the MADRS, YMRS, HAMA, or BPRS. The depression domain of the SCL-90-R was also comparable between the groups. Several studies have suggested that the severity of baseline symptoms is the most important factor in predicting treatment response.16,17 However, there is ongoing debate about whether greater initial symptom severity actually predicts poorer treatment outcomes. Some research has shown that patients with severe depression, particularly those with comorbid pain, are more likely to experience poor treatment response.17,39 In contrast, other studies have reported that individuals with more severe depressive symptoms tend to show greater absolute symptom improvement, and therefore may respond better to treatment than those with milder symptoms.18 Restricting the sample to individuals with moderate or greater depression severity, as determined by MADRS scores, allowed our study to account for the conflicting and inconclusive results reported in previous research.

Compared to responders, non-responders were more likely to be male and initially reported higher levels of obsessive-compulsive symptoms, interpersonal sensitivity, phobic anxiety, and paranoid ideation on the SCL-90-R, as well as lower perceived quality of social relationships on the WHOQOL-BREF. These findings suggest that even when there are no differences in clinician-rated or self-reported depression severity at baseline, treatment outcomes may still vary depending on sex and other subjective symptoms. While previous studies have reported that male sex is associated with poorer treatment response, some have also argued that women tend to overreport their symptoms, which has also been linked to poorer outcomes.13,22 In the context of our findings, the higher proportion of male patients in the non-responder group may be partially explained by prior evidence.

The elevated scores for various non-depressive subjective symptoms in the non-responder group remain unexplained. More than half of patients with depression experience anxiety.40 An individual may meet the clinical criteria for an anxiety disorder, may have met them in the past but now the symptoms are in remission, or may experience a subthreshold level of anxiety.41,42 Comorbid anxiety increases the severity of depressive symptoms and is a risk factor for poor treatment response.43,44 Early identification of initial co-occurring anxiety could significantly aid in patient care. However, our results indicated no differences in baseline anxiety between responders and non-responders based on objective assessments, including the initial HAMA scores. Nonetheless, non-responders recorded higher levels of phobic anxiety, obsessive-compulsiveness, and paranoid ideation through initial subjective reporting than responders. Therefore, to fully understand patients with “difficult-to-treat” depression in real-world settings, subjective assessments of a variety of symptoms should be included along with the evaluation of depressive symptoms.26

In addition to anxiety-related symptoms, dissatisfaction with social relationships, high interpersonal sensitivity, and phobic-anxiety were exploratory predictors of poor treatment response in the present study. Although there were interpretation challenges due to a lack of information on patients’ personality traits, high neuroticism is known to be negatively associated with satisfaction in social relationships.45 Further, personality traits such as shyness, neuroticism, and alexithymia are associated with increased interpersonal problems, higher levels of subjective symptoms and distress, and a diminished quality of life.46,47,48,49 In our study, this distress might have manifested as various subjective complaints and a decrease in WHOQOL-BREF social relationships domain scores.

Furthermore, our study demonstrated that prediction models incorporating self-reported symptoms outperformed those adjusted only for basic demographic and clinical variables. In addition, a model that included all subjective symptom domains showed better predictive performance than models based on individual symptoms alone. These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that predicting treatment response using a single self-report measure is highly limited.19 However, our findings showed that phobic anxiety alone demonstrated predictive performance comparable to that of the full model incorporating all subjective symptom domains. Even after additional multiple-comparison corrections, the statistical robustness of phobic anxiety remained preserved. This suggests that psychological states involving physiological arousal—such as phobia, fear—may be closely linked to treatment response. Furthermore, it implies that phobic anxiety may serve as a strong predictor of treatment outcomes even in the absence of clinician-rated assessments.

Self-reporting has several advantages. First, it allows for an understanding of the subjective experience of symptoms. Patients can convey their feelings, emotions, thoughts, and behavioral changes related to depression, thus helping clinicians to understand their internal experiences. Second, self-reporting can be used as an auxiliary tool in the diagnostic process.16 Third, patient self-reports are useful for monitoring treatment response and predicting prognosis during the treatment process, which is consistent with the results of this study.50 Fourth, self-reporting contributes to the establishment of personalized treatment plans. Patients’ subjective accounts provide essential information about their unique concerns, quality of life, and functional status, allowing clinicians to create treatment plans tailored to each patient's needs.51,52,53 Finally, self-reporting enhances patients’ engagement in the treatment process. Patient participation is not only a crucial element of value-based healthcare but also improves treatment satisfaction and outcomes.54

This study determined that non-responders exhibited greater severity of certain subjective symptoms than responders both initially and during treatment for depression. However, this study had several limitations. First, the sample size was small, which might have reduced statistical power. Second, using only the MADRS to assess objective depressive symptoms may be considered a constraint, although it can be argued that a single measure such as the MADRS is sufficient because of its sensitivity to changes over time.26 Third, although medications were considered as dichotomous variables according to four categories, the analysis lacked sufficient consideration of the specific types and dosages of the drugs. Fourth, although additional analyses were performed for unipolar and bipolar patients to examine diagnostic characteristics within the overall mood disorder group, these evaluations may not have captured all subtleties inherent to diagnostic differentiation. Fifth, a between-group imbalance in sex cannot be ruled out as influencing the overall findings. The proportion of male patients was higher among non-responders than responders, and this imbalance consistently affected both group comparisons and the logistic regression results. Accordingly, future studies should be designed to adequately account for sex differences to enhance the reliability of the findings. Finally, various other factors known to be related to treatment response were not evaluated, including high stress levels and stressful life events.13,14,15,16

The strength of this study lies in its novel approach utilizing common yet clinically underused self-report measures such as the SCL-90-R and WHOQOL-BREF to describe the characteristics of non-responders, emphasizing the need for multifaceted assessments.7,26 The findings are particularly significant considering that the simplicity and clinical utility of the SCL-90-R and WHOQOL-BREF minimize patient burden and personnel needs. Furthermore, this study introduced the possibility of predicting treatment response using self-reporting, which is a vital step that requires further research.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that subjective phobic anxiety symptoms and interpersonal sensitivity are important factors for predicting treatment response in depression. Careful consideration of these aspects when planning treatment and managing those with depression may improve outcomes.
