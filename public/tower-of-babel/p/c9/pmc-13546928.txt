Varivax (Aesculus hippocastanum L.): A dietary supplement with antioxidant and antidepressant potential in adult zebrafish
Authors: Lorena S Lima, Maria Rayane C de Oliveira, Gabriela A do Nascimento, João Gabriel L da Silva, Daniela Braga de Sousa, Paulo AT Coelho, Jonas Araújo de Sousa, Keciany A de Oliveira, Francisco Lucas A Batista, Hamilton M Ishiki, Erlândia A Magalhães Queiroz, Vanessa Lavor Sobreira, Cassia Maria M da Silva, Lucas Soares Frota, Sara Ingrid CG Barbosa, Selene Maia de Morais, Wildson Max B da Silva, Solange de Oliveira Pinheiro, Renalison Farias-Pereira, Sacha Aubrey AR Santos, Ana Lívia O de Sousa Rodrigues, Raul Ricardino Gois, José Rodolfo R Assis, Antonio Eufrásio Vieira-Neto, Luiz FWG Moura, Ramon da S Raposo, Adriana R Campos, Francisco Ernani A Magalhães
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVarivax (Natulab®) is a dietary supplement composed of dry extract of horse chestnut (Aesculus hippocastanum L.), standardized to 20% anhydrous escin and indicated for chronic venous insufficiency, pain, and leg swelling. In this context, the present study aimed to investigate the antioxidant and antidepressant effects of Varivax (VRX) and its possible neuromodulatory mechanisms. Initially, VRX was subjected to in vitro antioxidant activity assessment using the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) and ABTS [2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)] assays. For the in vivo assays, to evaluate non-clinical safety, adult zebrafish (aZF; n = 6/group) were treated (20 μL; oral administration) with VRX (0.1, 1.0, or 10 mg/mL), and sedative effects (open field test) and toxicity (96 h) were assessed. To investigate the antidepressant effect, aZF (n = 6/group) were subjected to the Tail Immobilization Test (TIT), and antidepressant-like activity was characterized by an increase in mobility time (s). Subsequently, the possible in vivo modulation of the serotonergic system by VRX was investigated, and in silico molecular docking was performed to evaluate the potential modulation of serotonergic receptors (5-HT2A, 5-HT1, 5-HT2A/2C, and 5-HT3A/3B) by escin. Fluoxetine was used as the reference drug for validating the results. As a result, VRX did not exhibit toxic or sedative effects in adult zebrafish and demonstrated antidepressant-like activity involving serotonergic receptors 5-HT2A, 5-HT1, 5-HT2A/2C, and 5-HT3A/3B, which were supported by the interaction potential observed in the in silico assays. The results of in vivo and in silico analyses demonstrated that VRX exhibited an antidepressant effect at the highest dose tested, with a response comparable to fluoxetine. The present study highlights the medicinal potential of this supplement in the prospecting of phytopharmaceuticals for depression.Keywords: Depression. Antioxidant. Introduction

Depression is among the most prevalent psychiatric disorders worldwide, with an estimated lifetime prevalence of approximately 12% (Johannsen et al., 2022). This diagnosis has become increasingly common, particularly after the COVID−19 pandemic, which led to a significant rise in reported cases. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the global prevalence of anxiety and depression increased by more than 25% during this period. Symptoms such as depressed mood, insomnia, fatigue, and anxiety crises occur recurrently, severely impairing the quality of life of affected individuals (Santomauro, 2021, Da Silva, 2024).

Regarding pharmacological treatment, a wide range of medications is available for the management of depression, including Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs), such as fluoxetine, escitalopram, and sertraline, which are generally considered first-line therapies (Silva et al., 2024). Other drug classes include Serotonin and Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs), tricyclic antidepressants, benzodiazepines, and anticonvulsants, which may also be used to treat this condition (Balon and Starcevic, 2020, Karrouri, 2021) However, these medications are frequently associated with adverse effects, such as weight gain, sexual dysfunction, dizziness, drowsiness, and sedation, which may negatively affect treatment adherence and patients’ quality of life (Garakani et al., 2020).

In this context, plant-derived products have historically been used both in the treatment and prevention of diseases (Veloso et al., 2023). Interest in the study of herbal medicines stems from their effects on the central nervous system, with evidence of efficacy in the management of neurological disorders such as anxiety, depression, and seizures, offering a viable alternative to conventional therapies, often with fewer side effects, as described in the literature (Bum, 2011, Nobrega, 2022).

Aesculus hippocastanum L., native to the Balkan Peninsula, is the main component of the dietary supplement VARIVAX. Commonly known as horse chestnut, it belongs to the subfamily Hippocastanaceae within the family Sapindaceae (Owczarek et al., 2021). This species is a valuable source of bioactive compounds, such as flavonoids and organic acids (Kučera et al., 2021), with pharmacological potential mainly attributed to escin, a saponin and its principal bioactive constituent. Escin has been reported to exhibit several pharmacological activities, including anti-edematous, antisecretory, and vasoprotective effects in chronic venous insufficiency (Idris et al., 2020), as well as broad-spectrum antiviral activity, including against SARS-CoV−2.

The pharmacological activity of a plant species can be assessed by analyzing its antioxidant activity, which plays a crucial role in counteracting the effects of free radicals, byproducts of physiological reactions in the body. These free radicals are involved in the pathophysiology of several diseases, including depression, a condition that has been associated with oxidative stress, characterized by an imbalance between free radical production and the body’s antioxidant capacity. Excessive production of these radicals in the brain may lead to cellular damage, inflammation, and neuronal dysfunction, thereby contributing to depressive symptoms. Thus, the consumption of antioxidant sources may neutralize or reduce free radical-induced damage, aiding in the prevention and management of depression, as well as other non-communicable chronic diseases (NCDs) that share oxidative stress-related pathophysiological mechanisms (Abshirini et al., 2019, De Oliveira et al., 2021, Prevedello and Comachio, 2021).

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) have emerged as an efficient model for the study of mental disorders and the development of potential therapeutic strategies, as they share similarities with mammals regarding key brain regions involved in emotional and cognitive processing (Wang et al., 2023). In addition, zebrafish represent a low-cost experimental model with a short life cycle, high reproductive rates, and reduced compound requirements for experimentation, resulting in greater experimental efficiency (Chahardehi et al., 2023).

Importantly, zebrafish possess a conserved serotonergic system with neurotransmission pathways and serotonin receptor subtypes analogous to those described in mammals, including receptors involved in mood regulation and antidepressant responses (Sourbron et al., 2016, Näslund et al., 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that serotonergic modulation in zebrafish is associated with anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, supporting the translational relevance of this model for neuropharmacological and antidepressant screening studies (Faria et al., 2021, Näslund et al., 2024).

In this context, the present study reports the standardization of a depression model in adult zebrafish (aZF), the evaluation of the antioxidant and antidepressant effects of the dietary supplement Varivax (Aesculus hippocastanum), and the possible involvement of the serotonergic system.

Materials and methods

Drugs and reagents

The following drugs and reagents were used in this study: dimethyl sulfoxide, P.A.–A.C.S. grade (DMSO; Synth; Code. P.10.0402.000.00.81 CAS 67–68–5); diazepam (DZP; Neo Química®;Code. LB 1650925; CAS 439–14–5); fluoxetine (Flx; EMS®; Code. L2W6500; CAS 54910–89–3); cyproheptadine (Cipro; Sigma-Aldrich; Code. 279072; CAS 129–03–3); pizotifen (Piz; Sigma-Aldrich; Code. B9688; CAS 15574–96–6); and granisetron (Gran; Sigma-Aldrich; Code.Y0000396; CAS 109889–09–0). 2,2-Diphenyl−1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH; Sigma-Aldrich; Code. D9132 CAS 1898–66–4), (2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline−6-sulfonic acid; ABTS⁺; Sigma-Aldrich; Code.A1888; CAS 30931–67–0), quercetin (Sigma-Aldrich; Code. Q4951; CAS 117–39–5), and gallic acid (Sigma-Aldrich; Code. G7384; CAS 149–91–7) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Corp. (USA).

Test sample

Varivax (VRX; Natulab®; batch no. 0023190; manufacturing date: October 2023; expiration date: October 2025) was used in this study and purchased from a Drogasil pharmacy in Iguatu, Ceará, Brazil. VRX solutions (0.1, 1.0, and 10 mg/mL) were prepared by maceration followed by dilution in dimethyl sulfoxide (3% DMSO; vehicle), P.A.–A.C.S. grade. VRX consists of a dry extract of Aesculus hippocastanum L. seeds (horse chestnut), standardized to 20% triterpene glycosides, calculated as anhydrous escin (Fig. 1).

Commercial label of the herbal medicinal product containing Aesculus hippocastanum L. (horse chestnut). The product contains 100 mg of dry extract per coated tablet and is intended for oral administration in adults. According to the manufacturer, it is indicated for the treatment of symptoms associated with varicose veins, including pain and swelling of the legs, and for chronic venous insufficiency. The package contains 30 coated tablets.

Antioxidant activity

Antioxidant activity was evaluated using the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl−1-picrylhydrazyl) assay, following the methodology described by Becker et al. (2019), with modifications, and the ABTS [2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline−6-sulfonic acid)] assay described by Re et al. (1999). Both assays were performed in flat-bottom 96-well microplates using a BioTek ELX 800 ELISA reader. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm for the DPPH radical after 60 min of incubation and at 630 nm for the ABTS⁺ radical after 10 min of incubation. Values corresponding to the natural coloration of the extracts were excluded from the analysis. Gallic acid and quercetin were used as reference antioxidant compounds.

Adult zebrafish (Danio rerio)

Adult wild-type zebrafish (Danio rerio; aZF) of both sexes, aged 90 days, with body length of 3.5 ± 0.5 cm and body weight of 0.4 ± 0.1 g, were obtained from Agroquímica: Comércio de Produtos Veterinários LTDA, a supplier located in Fortaleza, Ceará, Brazil. Groups of 50 fish were acclimated for 24 h in glass aquaria (40 × 20 × 25 cm) containing dechlorinated water (ProtecPlus® anti-chlorine) and aerated with air pumps equipped with submerged filters, maintained at 26 °C and pH 7.0, under a 14:10 h light/dark cycle. Fish were fed ad libitum (1–5% of body weight; 20 mg/aZF) with commercial feed (Spirulina®) for 24 h before the experiments. Animals were randomly distributed among experimental groups. Sex was not included as an experimental factor in the present study; previous reports have described mixed findings regarding sex-related influences on zebrafish behavior, with some studies showing no significant effects in locomotor and anxiety-related endpoints, whereas others have reported sex-dependent differences in activity and affective-like behaviors. A total of 246 adult zebrafish were used throughout all experimental procedures. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Use Ethics Committee of the State University of Ceará (CEUA-UECE), under protocol no. 04009489/2023.

General protocol

Zebrafish experiments were conducted based on methodologies proposed by Magalhães et al., 2017, Magalhães et al., 2018. On the day of the experiments, fish were randomly selected, transferred onto a moist sponge, and treated orally (p.o.) with the test samples or control solutions (Collymore et al., 2013). Subsequently, the animals were individually placed in glass beakers (250 mL) containing 150 mL of aquarium water for recovery. For oral treatments, a variable-volume automatic pipette (20 µL) with sterile tips was used. Animal behavior was recorded by calibrated and blinded observers (Magalhães et al., 2017, Magalhães et al., 2018).

Non-clinical safety assessment

96-h Acute toxicity

The acute toxicity study was conducted in adult zebrafish (D. rerio) according to methodologies proposed by the OECD (2019) and Batista et al. (2018). Animals (n = 6/group) were treated orally with 20 µL of VRX (0.1, 1.0, or 10 mg/mL), fluoxetine (Flx; 5.0, 10, or 15 mg/mL), or vehicle (control; 3% DMSO; 20 µL; p.o.). After 96 h of treatment, the number of dead fish in each group was recorded, and the lethal concentration required to kill 50% of the animals (LC₅₀) was determined using the Trimmed Spearman–Karber mathematical method with a 95% confidence interval (Arellano-Aguiar et al., 2015).

Locomotor activity (Open field test)

The open field test was performed as described by Magalhães et al. (2017) to evaluate potential alterations in motor coordination, including sedation and/or muscle relaxation. Animals (n = 6/group) were treated orally with 20 µL of VRX (0.1, 1.0, or 10 mg/mL), diazepam (DZP; 10 mg/mL), or vehicle (3% DMSO; 20 µL; p.o.). An untreated group (naive; n = 6/group) was also included. One hour after oral treatments, animals were individually placed in glass Petri dishes (100 × 15 mm) containing aquarium water and divided into four quadrants. Locomotor activity (LA) was assessed by counting the number of line crossings (LC) during a 0–5 min observation period.

Antidepressant-like effect

The antidepressant-like effect of VRX was evaluated using the Tail Immobilization Test (TIT), according to the methodology described by Demin et al. (2020), with adaptations. Adult zebrafish (aZF; n = 6/group) were treated orally with 20 µL of VRX (0.1, 1.0, or 10 mg/mL) or fluoxetine (Flx; antidepressant control; 5.0 mg/mL). An untreated group (naive) was also included. One hour after treatment, except for the naive group, animals were individually immersed in ethanol (EtOH; 1%), a central nervous system (CNS) depressant, for 30 min. Subsequently, aZF were individually subjected to the Tail Immobilization Test, and antidepressant-like activity was characterized by an increase in mobility time (s) during a 5.0 min analysis period (Moura et al., 2025).

Neuromodulation via the serotonergic system (5-HT)

The involvement of the serotonergic system in the antidepressant effect of the lowest effective concentration of VRX was investigated using the Tail Immobilization Test, based on methodologies proposed by Kordjazy et al. (2016), de Melo et al. (2019), and Demin et al. (2020), employing serotonergic antagonists as described by Benneh et al. (2017). Initially, animals (n = 6/group) were treated orally with 20 µL of VRX (5.0 mg/mL) or fluoxetine (Flx; antidepressant control; 5.0 mg/mL) or cyproheptadine (Cipro; 5-HT2A antagonist; 0.8 mg/mL) or pizotifen (Piz; 5-HT1 and 5-HT2A/2C antagonist; 0.8 mg/mL) or granisetron (Gran; 5-HT3A/3B antagonist; 0.5 mg/mL).

Using the same experimental protocol, animals (n = 6/group) were orally pretreated with cyproheptadine (Cypro; 5-HT2A antagonist; 0.8 mg/mL; p.o.) or pizotifen (Piz; 5-HT1 and 5-HT2A/2C antagonist; 0.8 mg/mL; p.o.) or granisetron (Gran; 5-HT3A/3B antagonist; 0.5 mg/mL; p.o.), 15 min before treatment with 20 µL of VRX (10 mg/mL; p.o.) or fluoxetine (Flx; 5.0 mg/mL; p.o.) or vehicle (3% DMSO; 20 µL; p.o.). A group of animals without treatments was included (naive). 1 h after oral treatment, except for the naive group, animals were individually immersed in ethanol (1%) for 30 min. Subsequently, aZF were subjected to the Tail Immobilization Test, and antidepressant-like activity was characterized by an increase in mobility time (s) during a 5.0 min analysis period.

Molecular docking

The interaction between serotonergic receptors (5-HT1B, 5-HT2A, and 5-HT3A) and the ligands escin, diazepam, and fluoxetine was evaluated through molecular docking simulations. The three-dimensional structure of escin was constructed using ChemSketch and subsequently subjected to energy minimization to obtain a stable 3D conformation. The optimized structures of diazepam and fluoxetine were obtained from the PubChem database (CID: 3016 and 3386, respectively).

The crystallographic structures of the receptors 5-HT1B, 5-HT2A, and 5-HT3A were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) under the accession codes 5V54, 6A94, and 6W1Y, respectively. All receptor structures were determined by X-ray crystallography, with resolutions ranging from 2.30 to 2.97 Å. Prior to docking simulations, receptor preparation involved the removal of co-crystallized ligands, non-structural water molecules, ions, and other heteroatoms not directly involved in receptor stability or ligand recognition. Polar hydrogens were added to the receptor structures, and structural optimization was performed before docking analysis.

Ligand preparation was conducted using the LigPrep protocol coupled with the Epik module (Shelley et al., 2007), allowing the generation of energetically favorable conformations and appropriate protonation states under physiological conditions. All ligands were subjected to energy minimization prior to docking calculations. Molecular docking simulations were performed using HEX software version 8.0.0 (Macindoe et al., 2010). Although HEX is widely employed for protein–protein docking analyses, it has also been successfully applied in protein–ligand interaction studies due to its efficient spherical polar Fourier correlation approach, which enables rapid assessment of shape and electrostatic complementarities between molecular structures. In the present study, HEX was selected as an exploratory and comparative tool to evaluate the potential interactions between escin and serotonergic receptors. Validation of the docking protocol was performed by redocking the co-crystallized ligands into the respective receptor binding sites, allowing comparison between predicted and experimental binding conformations. The protocol successfully reproduced the experimentally observed binding poses, supporting the reliability of the adopted docking methodology. The resulting docking conformations and interaction clusters were analyzed using PyMOL version 1.4.7 (DeLano, 2015), enabling visualization and characterization of ligand–receptor interactions.

Statistical analysis

In in vitro assays, all samples were analyzed in triplicate. Values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation of three aliquots per group. In in vivo experiments, results were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) for each group of six animals. After confirming data normality and homogeneity of variance, differences among groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 8.4.3, with the level of statistical significance set at 5% (p < 0.05).

Results

The results of the antioxidant assays indicate that the mean inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values of VRX ranged from 10.13 ± 0.23–15.86 ± 0.44 μg/mL for DPPH radical inhibition [Fig. 2A (F₄,₁₀ = 724.3)], and from 21.01 ± 0.41–26.28 ± 0.15 μg/mL for ABTS⁺ radical inhibition [Fig. 2B (F₄,₁₀ = 724.3)]. Regarding fluoxetine (Flx), IC₅₀ values ranged from 12.02 ± 0.49–17.36 ± 0.23 μg/mL for DPPH radical inhibition [Fig. 2C (F₄,₁₀ = 1769)] and from 7.26 ± 0.57–19.33 ± 0.21 μg/mL for ABTS⁺ radical inhibition [Fig. 2D (F₄,₁₀ = 1125)]. It is noteworthy that gallic acid, used as a reference compound, exhibited an IC₅₀ of 1.94 ± 0.27 μg/mL against the DPPH radical and 13.01 ± 0.03 μg/mL against the ABTS⁺ radical. Quercetin presented IC₅₀ values of 2.74 ± 0.08 μg/mL for DPPH radical inhibition and 3.98 ± 0.13 μg/mL for ABTS⁺ radical inhibition (Fig. 2A–D).

For all tested concentrations of VRX (0.1 or 1.0, or 10 mg/mL) and fluoxetine (Flx; 5.0 or 10, or 15 mg/mL), no toxicity was observed in adult zebrafish (aZF) up to 96 h, as determined using the Spearman–Karber mathematical method. These findings suggest that the LC₅₀ for VRX is higher than 10 mg/mL and that for fluoxetine is higher than 15 mg/mL (Table 1).

Antidepressant-like effect

VRX exhibited an antidepressant-like effect in adult zebrafish. Animals treated with VRX (10 mg/mL; 20 µL; p.o.) showed a significant increase in mobility time (MT) in the Tail Immobilization Test (TIT; 5 min) (q = 8.585, p < 0.0001 vs. vehicle), differing from the MT observed in vehicle-treated animals (3% DMSO) and showing no significant difference compared to untreated animals (naive) (q = 4.027, p > 0.05 vs. naive). This antidepressant-like effect of VRX was comparable to that of fluoxetine (Flx; 5.0 mg/mL; 20 µL; p.o.), used as a positive antidepressant control (q = 4.027, p > 0.05 vs. Flx). Fluoxetine significantly increased MT compared to the vehicle group (q = 12.70, p < 0.0001) and showed no significant difference compared to the naive group (q = 0.0839, p > 0.05), as shown in Fig. 4 (F₅,₃₀ = 34.97).

Molecular docking of escin with the 5-HT1B receptor

To validate the involvement of the serotonergic system in escin's effects, molecular docking simulations were performed with 5-HT receptors. Regarding the 5-HT1B receptor, the most energetically favorable cluster showed an energy loss of −400.94 kcal/mol upon ligand complexation, suggesting a stronger interaction than the binding energy of the classical agonist fluoxetine (−313.84 kcal/mol). In this receptor, escin exhibited affinity and specificity for a binding site distinct from the canonical fluoxetine binding site, a ligand known to modulate binding sites of the 5-HT1B receptor (Borroto-Escuela et al., 2018), suggesting the existence of a primary and a secondary binding site for this receptor (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Varivax (Natulab®), a dry extract obtained from horse chestnut seeds (Aesculus hippocastanum L.), contains triterpenic glycosides, mainly anhydrous escin. In Brazil, it is a dietary supplement used commercially to manage symptoms associated with venous insufficiency, such as pain and heaviness in the legs, cramps, pruritus, edema, and capillary fragility. In the present study, the in vitro antioxidant potential and the in vivo antidepressant-like effects of this supplement were evaluated in adult zebrafish (Danio rerio), suggesting a possible expansion of its use beyond venous insufficiency, considering its effects in the context of depressive disorder.

In this regard, the measurement of DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging is widely recognized as a reliable model for evaluating a substance's ability to neutralize free radicals (Frota et al., 2023), which are associated with the development of chronic non-communicable diseases (Prevedello and Comachio, 2021). Oxidative stress contributes to depression through several mechanisms, including neuroinflammation and neurotransmitter dysfunction, particularly involving serotonergic pathways (Bhatt, Nagappa & Patil, 2020). Therefore, activating antioxidant pathways and using compounds that reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) represent promising targets for the prevention and treatment of depression (Ji et al., 2023).

In the in vitro assays, all VRX concentrations showed higher antioxidant activity than the reference compounds, gallic acid and quercetin. According to Kuete and Efferth (2010), IC₅₀ values below 50 µg/mL indicate strong antioxidant activity. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that extracts obtained from the bark, seeds, flowers, and leaves of Aesculus hippocastanum L. display significant antioxidant potential, as evidenced by their ability to scavenge free radicals such as DPPH, ABTS, superoxide, and peroxynitrite (Braga et al., 2012, Celep et al., 2012, Vašková et al., 2015, Küp et al., 2020; Owczarek et al., 2021a, Owczarek et al., 2021b; Owczarek et al., 2021b).

Moreover, escin, one of the main active compounds present in VRX, has demonstrated in vivo antioxidant potential in mice by preventing lipid peroxidation induced by high-fat diets (Küçükkurt et al., 2010). The literature has also described its anti-inflammatory, vasoconstrictive, vasoprotective, and gastrointestinal protective activities (Busia, 2016, Yang et al., 2020, Idris et al., 2023), as well as its antitumor potential, making it a promising agent for the treatment of acute and chronic inflammatory conditions and for cancer prevention (Rasheed et al., 2020, Vejselova Sezer, 2024).

Adult zebrafish represent a reliable translational model for neurobehavioral and toxicological investigations, allowing the evaluation of compounds with potential antidepressant activity and serotonergic modulation (Wang et al., 2023).

In the present study, VRX was found to be safe in adult zebrafish, with no toxicity or mortality observed at the tested concentrations (Table 1), in agreement with the literature describing the traditional and safe use of horse chestnut in humans (Idris et al., 2020). Treatment of zebrafish with anxiolytic drugs, such as benzodiazepines, may induce sedative effects and reduce locomotor activity (Gupta et al., 2014, Benneh et al., 2017, Chen et al., 2021). However, none of the tested VRX concentrations reduced zebrafish locomotor activity, suggesting a non-sedative profile.

In rodents, the tail suspension test is widely used to assess the antidepressant effects of substances and natural products (Kordjazy, 2016). In the present study, using the tail-immobilization test (TIT), VRX showed a significant antidepressant effect only at the highest dose tested, promoting an increase in zebrafish mobility time, with a response comparable to that observed for fluoxetine. In contrast, the lower doses did not produce significant changes in animal behavior, showing results similar to the control groups. These findings corroborate previous studies demonstrating the antidepressant-like potential of natural compounds in adult zebrafish and reinforce the applicability of 1% ethanol as an inducer of depressive-like behavior (de Melo et al., 2019, Demin et al., 2020). Although this is the first study evaluating horse chestnut in adult zebrafish, the observed behavioral response was consistent and progressive, reinforcing the suitability of this experimental model for screening compounds with antidepressant potential (Wang et al., 2023, Verma et al., 2022).

Depression has been strongly associated with dysfunctions in serotonergic neurotransmission, including alterations in 5-HT receptor signaling pathways involved in mood regulation and emotional processing (Pannu & Goyal, 2023). In the present study, the attenuation of the antidepressant-like effects of VRX following administration of serotonergic antagonists suggests the involvement of serotonergic pathways, particularly 5-HT1B, 5-HT2A, and 5-HT3A receptors, in the observed behavioral responses.

Previous studies have demonstrated that serotonergic receptors, particularly 5-HT1, 5-HT2A/2C, and 5-HT3 subtypes, play important roles in emotional regulation and in the pathophysiology of depressive disorders. The 5-HT2A receptor is highly expressed in brain regions associated with cognition and emotion, such as the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, and is involved in glutamatergic neurotransmission (Zieba et al., 2021). Likewise, alterations in 5-HT1 receptor signaling and reduced receptor binding in regions including the hippocampus, raphe nuclei, and cingulate cortex have been reported in patients with depression (Wang et al., 2016, Savitz et al., 2009, Albert et al., 2014). In addition, 5-HT2A/2C and 5-HT3 receptors modulate glutamatergic and GABAergic neurotransmission, influencing neural circuits related to mood and anxiety regulation (Bhatt et al., 2020, Popova et al., 2022, Shi et al., 2024). Dysregulation of these pathways may contribute to alterations in the excitation–inhibition balance in limbic and cortical regions associated with depressive-like behaviors.

In the present study, in vivo experiments demonstrated that the antidepressant-like effect of VRX is likely associated with serotonergic pathways (5-HT2A, 5-HT1, 5-HT2A/2C, and 5-HT3A/3B), as the administration of serotonergic receptor antagonists significantly attenuated its antidepressant-like effects, as evidenced by a reduction in the animals’ mobility time. This effect may be associated with modulation of serotonergic neurotransmission, leading to downstream activation of 5-HT receptors involved in mood regulation (Benneh et al., 2017).

Regarding the in silico molecular docking analysis, the results demonstrated favorable binding affinities between the ligands and the 5-HT1B, 5-HT2A, and 5-HT3A receptors. Structural and spatial analyses corroborated the energetic data obtained from the ten most favorable clusters for each complex, revealing interaction energies ranging from −475.48 to −209.66 kcal/mol.

Although horse chestnut has been traditionally used to treat chronic venous insufficiency, its mechanisms of action are not fully understood. However, studies have reported its involvement with serotonergic receptors (Felixsson et al., 2010, Dudek-Makuch and Studzińska-Sroka, 2015). Therefore, the present study highlights the antioxidant and antidepressant potential of VRX and suggests that its activity is associated with escin, the main bioactive compound in this dietary supplement, and emphasizes its relationship with the serotonergic system, which plays a central role in the pathophysiology of depression.

Conclusion

Our results support the use of 1% EtOH as a depression-inducing agent in the Tail Immobilization Test in adult zebrafish (Danio rerio) as an alternative behavioral model of depression. Using this method, fluoxetine was standardized as an antidepressant control, and Varivax (Aesculus hippocastanum L.) exhibited a putative antidepressant effect, possibly mediated by serotonergic systems involving 5-HT2A, 5-HT1, 5-HT2A/2C, and 5-HT3A/3B receptors. In addition, Varivax demonstrated high in vitro antioxidant activity against DPPH and ABTS⁺ free radicals and did not exhibit toxic effects in the adult zebrafish (aZF). Notably, only fluoxetine showed sedative effects. From this perspective, these findings encourage further investigations into Aesculus hippocastanum and its bioactive constituents for the development of novel therapeutic strategies for the treatment of depression.
