Migraine and auditory dysfunction: beyond comorbidity
Authors: Wandi Xu, Ni Zhai, Jingyu Chen, Shun Zhou, E Tian, Zhaoqi Guo, Ziyu Zhai, Zhanghong Zhou, Xixi Yu, Xin Zhang, Xin Ma, Yisheng Lu, Sulin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMigraine operates as a systemic disorder of multisensory sensitization. Within this spectrum, auditory manifestations, including sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, and hyperacusis, represent prevalent yet frequently overlooked otologic phenotypes. Epidemiological data show that 15%–49% of migraine patients present with comorbid auditory symptoms, and nearly two-thirds exhibit auditory electrophysiological abnormalities. However, optimal clinical management remains constrained by an incomplete understanding of pathogenic mechanisms, variable audiological assessment protocols, and dispersed therapeutic data. This review synthesizes epidemiological, audiological, and pathophysiological data linking migraine to auditory dysfunction. The underlying pathology involves a peripheral-to-central continuum across three interacting axes. Anatomical and hydrodynamic vulnerabilities drive localized microvascular ischemia and aberrant brain–ear fluid exchange. Localized neurochemical imbalances, involving glutamate excitotoxicity, neuropeptide signaling, and innate immune activation, disrupt inner ear homeostasis. Concurrently, central sensitization facilitates neural network plasticity, characterized by impaired efferent gating and thalamocortical dysrhythmia. Drawing upon these underlying pathways, current and emerging therapeutic strategies are categorized by specific clinical objectives: blocking upstream migraine pathways, protecting the cochlear microenvironment, and correcting central hypersensitivity. Introduction

Migraine affects over one billion people globally [1, 2], and is increasingly recognized as a complex central sensitization syndrome marked by widespread sensory processing dysregulation [3]. Its hallmark is reduced tolerance to multimodal environmental stimuli during both the ictal and interictal phases [4–6]. While vestibular and visual symptoms of migraine are well-established, the relationship between migraine and auditory abnormalities, such as hearing loss, tinnitus, and hyperacusis, remains clinically underdefined. Epidemiological studies indicate that 15% to 49% of migraine patients present with comorbid auditory symptoms [7–9], with nearly two-thirds exhibiting auditory electrophysiological abnormalities [10]. While the concept of a bidirectional brain–cochlea axis has been introduced to explore neurological disease-associated hearing loss [11, 12], optimal management of migraine-associated auditory dysfunction remains hindered by three specific knowledge uncertainties: an incomplete pathophysiological cascade linking peripheral cochlear injury to central sensitization, the insensitivity of routine pure-tone audiometry (PTA) to detect subclinical deficits, and the lack of mechanism-driven therapeutic classifications. Although the concept of “cochlear migraine” has been proposed to categorize these phenotypes [13, 14], it remains a hypothetical clinical entity not currently recognized by the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3), and its validation requires a deeper mechanistic understanding.

It remains unclear whether the inner ear is merely a downstream target of migraine or an independently involved node in a shared pathological network. To address these gaps, this review synthesizes current epidemiological and audiological data to outline a multifactorial peripheral-to-central pathophysiological continuum. By integrating the inner ear’s anatomical-hydrodynamic vulnerabilities, localized neurochemical imbalances, and subsequent central neural network remodeling, we synthesize basic evidence to propose this multifactorial hypothetical continuum. This mechanistic framework aims to facilitate early identification, precise phenotyping, and cross-disciplinary neuro-otologic care for patients with migraine-associated auditory dysfunction, while deducing directions for future translational research.

Methods

A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases for peer-reviewed articles published from database inception up to May 2026 to minimize selection bias and ensure an objective synthesis of the current evidence. The search strategy utilized Boolean operators to combine clinical and mechanistic keywords: (“migraine” OR “vestibular migraine” OR “headache”) AND (“hearing loss” OR “phonophobia” OR “tinnitus” OR “hyperacusis” OR “auditory dysfunction” OR “aural fullness” OR “cochlea” OR “cochlear” OR “hair cell” OR “auditory pathway” OR “auditory transmission”). The search was strictly limited to articles published in English. Clinical studies were prioritized for inclusion if they utilized rigorous diagnostic boundaries. Specifically, migraine and vestibular migraine (VM) diagnoses had to strictly adhere to the ICHD-3 criteria. For clinical evidence, prospective cohorts, case–control studies, and cross-sectional epidemiological studies were included. For mechanistic insights, basic translational science investigations were incorporated, prioritizing in vivo animal studies and in vitro molecular assays.

Epidemiology

Clinical manifestations

Phonophobia and hyperacusis

Sensory aversion represents a core manifestation of the altered neural processing inherent to the migraine spectrum. The environmental intolerance characteristic of migraine encompasses distinct auditory phenomena, notably phonophobia and hyperacusis. Phonophobia, an established diagnostic criterion according to the ICHD-3, is defined as sound-triggered fear, aversion, or anticipatory anxiety, affecting 70% to 81% of patients during ictal episodes [15–17]. In contrast, hyperacusis represents an altered perceptual state rather than a primary emotional reaction. It manifests as a reduced physiological tolerance to ordinary sounds, causing patients to perceive routine ambient noise as excessively loud or uncomfortable [18]. This hypersensitivity is supported by both subjective complaints and objective findings, such as reduced loudness discomfort levels [19, 20]. Notably, hyperacusis often persists into the interictal phase, indicating sustained central sensory dysregulation rather than transient attack‑related changes [21]. Clinical differentiation between phonophobia and hyperacusis is critical for localizing dysfunction: phonophobia is primarily driven by central sensitization and affective network dysregulation, whereas hyperacusis suggests concomitant peripheral auditory pathway impairment, such as cochlear hypersensitivity or efferent feedback deficits. Consequently, identifying persistent hyperacusis warrants targeted audiological evaluation, rather than dismissal as a benign transient symptom.

Tinnitus

Tinnitus represents a spontaneous manifestation of aberrant neural firing within the auditory pathway [22]. Migraine and tinnitus share a bidirectional comorbidity [23, 24], reflecting common pathophysiological mechanisms in sensory network dysregulation [25, 26]. Clinical cohorts demonstrate that up to 45% of patients with primary tinnitus experience concurrent headaches [23, 27–29]. Furthermore, a history of migraine, particularly migraine with aura [30], serves as an independent predictor for incident tinnitus, increasing the risk by 1.4- to 3.3-fold [9, 31]. Paradoxically, while migraine predominantly increases the risk of mild subjective rather than severe tinnitus [32], these patients report a significantly higher overall symptom burden (e.g., depression, vertigo) compared to those with isolated tinnitus [33–35]. This discrepancy suggests that migraine-related central sensitization severely amplifies the affective distress of tinnitus, despite minimal peripheral lesions. Clinicians must meticulously differentiate this intrinsic progression from iatrogenic tinnitus caused by acute anti-migraine pharmacotherapies, such as triptans [36].

Hearing loss

Hearing impairment in migraine ranges from transient functional deficits during acute attacks (e.g., fluctuating or sudden sensorineural hearing loss, SSNHL) to persistent dysfunction [37–39]. Epidemiological data show a 24.5% migraine prevalence among individuals with primary hearing complaints [23]. A defining hallmark of migraine-associated hearing impairment is its frequent “subjective–objective dissociation”: patients report functionally disabling deficits that contrast with normal or minimal threshold shifts on conventional PTA [16]. This suggests that dysfunction may arise from occult cochlear synaptopathy or impaired central processing, supporting expanded diagnostic protocols (e.g., extended high-frequency audiometry, speech-in-noise testing) to capture subclinical deficits.

Abnormal otic sensations

Abnormal otic sensations constitute a prevalent but frequently misinterpreted clinical domain within the migraine spectrum. These symptoms include both nociceptive and non-nociceptive manifestations, reflecting a broader dysfunction in the trigeminal sensory processing network rather than isolated structural defects. For nociceptive presentations, idiopathic otalgia affects a substantial portion of migraine cohorts, with prevalence reaching approximately 42% [40]. Beyond frank pain, migraine is closely associated with aural fullness. The Bárány Society defines this non-nociceptive somatic abnormality as a distinct sensation of intra-aural pressure or blockage [41]. Epidemiological analyses suggest an elevated prevalence of aural fullness in migraine populations relative to non-migraine controls [42]. Conversely, up to 54% of patients presenting with ostensibly “idiopathic” aural fullness harbor comorbid migraine [43, 44]. Mechanistically, these symptoms likely arise from trigeminovascular activation and local neurogenic inflammation, inducing inner ear microvascular fluid shifts or amplified central somatic mechanosensory processing. Beyond neurovascular pathways, temporomandibular disorders (TMD) frequently co-occur with migraine [45, 46]. Clinical evidence suggests that aural fullness is the most prevalent otologic symptom in TMD patients [47]. Sustained temporal and masticatory muscle tension may mechanically impede Eustachian tube dynamics, causing middle ear pressure imbalances perceived as aural fullness, while concurrently stressing the joint capsule to induce referred otalgia. Recurrent otalgia or aural fullness with normal otoscopic and tympanometric findings should trigger prompt evaluation for comorbid migraine and underlying TMD.

Current epidemiological associations should be interpreted cautiously. The majority of published studies on migraine-auditory comorbidity present only prevalence data, without providing effect estimates adjusted for potential confounding factors, which may preclude the establishment of definitive causality or directionality. Furthermore, even among the higher-quality studies included in Table 1 that provide adjusted effect estimates, inherent methodological limitations may persist. In addition, auditory symptoms in migraine cohorts might be susceptible to clustering confounding variables. Factors such as noise exposure, age, vascular risks, and somatic disorders independently contribute to cochlear vulnerability, while concurrent psychiatric comorbidities (e.g., anxiety and sleep disturbances) significantly amplify subjective symptoms like tinnitus and hyperacusis. Future prospective longitudinal studies controlling for these confounders are necessary.

Audiological assessments

To facilitate the early detection of migraine-related auditory dysfunction, audiological evaluations can be conceptualized through a proposed model that mirrors the peripheral-to-central pathophysiological continuum:

Tier 1 (PTA and extended high-frequency audiometry) for occult vulnerability; Tier 2 (otoacoustic emissions, OAEs) for peripheral injury and efferent gating; Tier 3 (auditory brainstem response, ABR) for brainstem conduction; and Tier 4 (AERPs) for central processing.

PTA: methodological limitations

In migraine and VM cohorts, routine audiometric profiles often yield inconsistent results regarding threshold shifts and frequency distribution patterns (Table 2). While certain studies report intact auditory thresholds within standard frequency bands [19], targeted subgroup analyses reveal underlying deficits. For instance, among patients presenting with tinnitus, the presence of comorbid migraine or VM is associated with poorer pure-tone thresholds [34]. Furthermore, the affected frequency spectrum reported across the literature varies widely, spanning from 0.5 kHz to 16 kHz [48–51]. This audiometric variability manifests clinically as a prominent subjective–objective dissociation. Researchers quantified this discrepancy, demonstrating that while 34% of patients with migraine complained of subjective hearing loss, standard protocols confirmed deficits in only 14% [16]. This mismatch likely occurs because standard protocols (≤ 8 kHz) fail to detect early episodic ischemic damage preferentially affecting the cochlear basal turn. This limitation inevitably leads to a systematic underestimation of early occult auditory damage. Therefore, expanding assessments to extended high-frequency audiometry is crucial for identifying this subclinical occult vulnerability.

Otoacoustic emissions: subclinical lesions and efferent pathway dysfunction

Unlike PTA, which assesses global auditory perception and is susceptible to subjective–objective dissociation, OAEs can detect subcellular outer hair cell (OHC) lesions prior to overt threshold shifts [52]. Meta-analyses reveal an elevated risk of altered DPOAEs in migraineurs relative to healthy controls [12], which correlate positively with disease duration and attack frequency [10, 48, 49]. Conversely, some analyses report no significant differences in TEOAE profiles between female migraineurs and matched controls [53]. This discrepancy likely arises from both the pathophysiological dynamics of the bidirectional brain–cochlea axis [12], and methodological heterogeneity across studies, including variations in disease stage and the specific emission metrics utilized (Table 2).

Beyond peripheral mechanics, auditory processing is tightly regulated by the central efferent pathway, primarily the medial olivocochlear (MOC) system. OHCs receive dense efferent innervation from MOC fibers [54], which modulate their mechanical activity and provide a protective mechanism against noise-induced cochlear damage [55, 56]. Clinically, the contralateral suppression of OAEs serves as a reliable, non-invasive metric for assessing MOC functional integrity [57]. Across several cohorts, patients with migraine consistently exhibit attenuated or entirely absent contralateral suppression of TEOAEs [49, 53, 58] (Table 2). These findings indicate that auditory pathology in migraine extends beyond isolated peripheral OHC lesions to include compromised brainstem efferent regulation. Integrating standard OAEs with contralateral suppression actively differentiates localized cochlear stress from brainstem efferent dysregulation.

Auditory brainstem response: central sensitization and progressive conduction deficits

ABR provides an objective, non-invasive measure of functional integrity along the ascending auditory pathway, from the cochlear nerve to the brainstem [59]. While early investigations reported no overt abnormalities [60], subsequent research has identified distinct electrophysiological alterations in migraine cohorts. These alterations primarily manifest in two domains: altered neural excitability and impaired neural conduction. Regarding excitability, studies demonstrate reduced ABR thresholds and elevated wave amplitudes during acute migraine attacks [61]. This specific electrophysiological profile of low thresholds and hyper-responsivity reflects a hyper-irritable central auditory network. Pathophysiologically, this hyper-excitability provides the direct neurophysiological substrate for both clinical hyperacusis and broader central sensitization. In terms of neural conduction, the most consistent finding is latency prolongation. Multiple cohorts exhibit significant delays in the absolute latencies of waves I, III, and V [10, 48], alongside elongated I–V interpeak intervals [48].

A central pathophysiological debate is whether these conduction abnormalities represent transient functional disturbances or permanent structural injuries. Current data indicate that this progression is modulated by patient age and disease duration. In pediatric populations, ABR deficits appear fully reversible. Prolongations of wave V and the I–V interpeak latency occur strictly during ictal episodes and normalize completely during interictal periods [62]. This reversibility suggests that early-stage alterations are driven by transient vascular or metabolic fluctuations associated with acute attacks. Adult cohorts present a more persistent pathological profile. While some adults maintain attack-dependent ABR changes, approximately 35% demonstrate persistent prolongations of absolute and interpeak latencies throughout pain-free interictal phases [60]. These sustained abnormalities imply that recurrent migraine attacks can propel a pathological transition from reversible functional disruption to cumulative neurostructural damage over a prolonged disease course. For clinical practice, this age- and duration-dependent transition carries significant diagnostic value. Consequently, ABR serves not only as a baseline diagnostic measure but also as a longitudinal monitoring tool. Detecting persistent I–V interpeak prolongations during interictal phases provides an objective biomarker for chronic central neuro-otologic impairment, supporting the clinical rationale for early neuromodulatory interventions to prevent progressive auditory network damage.

Auditory event-related potentials: cognitive load and compensatory central processing

AERPs provide objective neurophysiological metrics of higher-order central auditory processing. Functionally, the P1–N1 complex indexes early auditory cortical sensory gating and primary attentional allocation, mapping onto the basal perceptual stage of acoustic processing [63]. Mismatch negativity (MMN) captures pre-attentive acoustic change detection and short-term auditory memory, reflecting higher-order auditory discrimination and error correction [64]. The AERPs profile in migraine cohorts exhibits substantial heterogeneity, largely contingent on the specific processing stage and cognitive state evaluated. Regarding early sensory processing, multiple investigations report normal P1–N1 latencies [65–67], indicating preserved peripheral-to-cortical auditory conduction and intact basal perception. However, this preservation is not universally observed, as some literature documents divergent early-latency alterations [68].

For higher-order discrimination indexed by MMN, conflicting outcomes appear heavily dependent on the specific experimental condition. Under passive listening conditions, migraine patients exhibit significantly prolonged latencies, pointing to a deceleration in preconscious and automatic auditory processing [65]. In active tasks, however, these latencies often normalize, while subsequent late-latency components, such as P3a and reorienting negativity, show marked alterations [69]. This specific divergence highlights a compensatory mechanism. Robust top-down attentional engagement may mask early automatic processing deficits, shifting the detectable impairment to later stages of attentional regulation rather than basic acoustic detection.

Central auditory processing dysfunction in migraine represents a dynamic network phenomenon modulated significantly by cognitive load. For clinical practice, these electrophysiological nuances carry direct diagnostic implications. They explain why migraine patients frequently report auditory fatigue or difficulty processing speech in complex environments despite presenting with intact standard audiograms. Because robust attentional effort during standard testing can temporarily mask underlying central deficits, evaluating the cognitive exhaustion associated with these compensatory mechanisms prevents the premature dismissal of symptoms based solely on normal baseline evaluations.

Pathophysiology

The clinical overlap between migraine and auditory dysfunction is well documented, yet the precise biological mechanisms connecting these conditions require systematic classification. To deduce these underlying mechanistic links and inspire future targeted translational explorations, we integrated current clinical observations with basic translational evidence to construct a theoretical peripheral-to-central framework. We hypothesize that the pathogenesis of migraine-associated auditory dysfunction follows a cascade comprising three interactive axes: anatomical‑hydrodynamic vulnerability, local neurochemical imbalance, and central network remodeling (Fig. 1).

Pathophysiological conceptual continuum of migraine-associated auditory dysfunction. This schematic shows the interconnected pathological cascades linking the sensitive central nervous system (left panel) and the vulnerable peripheral auditory system (right panel). In migraine, activation of meningeal trigeminal nerve endings releases pro-inflammatory and neuroactive mediators. These mediators may reach the inner ear via the cochlear aqueduct and IAC, and also be accumulated by the disrupted BBB and BLB. In the inner ear, trigeminal activation further amplifies microvascular ischemia, leading to the release of additional inflammatory mediators and ROS. This inflammatory milieu causes cochlear hair cell injury, overactivation of NMDA and AMPA receptors, and impairment of afferent auditory transmission. Abnormal auditory afferent signals then converge with nociceptive information in the TNC and CN. These integrated signals are relayed to the thalamus and auditory cortex, inducing TCD and engaging reciprocal interactions with the limbic system. Collectively, this peripheral-central reciprocal signaling axis may underlie the structural and functional abnormalities that drive migraine-associated auditory dysfunction. Trigemino-vascular reflex: ischemic vulnerability and microvascular infarction

The trigeminal sensory fibers directly innervate the cochlear spiral modiolar artery [70]. Trigeminal ganglion stimulation induces localized vascular hyperpermeability and plasma extravasation within the cochlea [71, 72]. Conversely, the presence of endolymphatic hydrops significantly reduces retrograde neuronal labeling in the ipsilateral trigeminal ganglion [73]. This bidirectional coupling demonstrates the trigeminal network’s direct influence on inner ear homeostasis. As a systemic vasomotor disorder, migraine with aura is an independent risk factor for ischemic events [74]. Within this hyperreactive environment, the inner ear is highly susceptible. Perfused exclusively by the labyrinthine artery [75], a terminal vessel lacking collateral circulation, the cochlear microenvironment remains extremely vulnerable to minor perfusion fluctuations [76, 77]. Migraine-associated vasospasm subjects the cochlea to episodic ischemia–reperfusion cycles. During the reperfusion phase, the abrupt reintroduction of oxygen triggers a localized burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [78]. Recurrent episodes of oxidative stress uncouple mitochondrial respiration within hair cells and spiral ganglion neurons. Concurrent migraine-induced hemorheological factors, such as elevated plasma fibrinogen and increased blood viscosity [79], further exacerbate this localized hypoxia, driving continuous lipid peroxidation and structural damage. Clinical studies link migraine with SSNHL and vasomotor symptoms, supporting microvascular ischemia as a key mechanism [80, 81]. Post-mortem histopathological analyses of such patients reveal severe cochlear fibrosis consistent with chronic infarction [82]. Accordingly, scholars have proposed that fluctuating hearing loss might be the characteristic otologic manifestation of migraine-related vasculopathy [38]. For clinical practice, these findings indicate that migraine-induced otologic deficits are not strictly transient or functional. Clinicians evaluating SSNHL in migraine cohorts should consider acute microvascular ischemia as a primary etiology, recognizing that prompt, vascular-targeted interventions may be required to prevent permanent microstructural infarction.

Dysregulation of “brain–ear” fluid homeostasis: central inflammatory infiltration

The traditional concept of the inner ear as an immune-privileged compartment insulated by the blood–labyrinth barrier (BLB) is evolving. Contemporary neuroanatomical studies reveal a dynamic, direct fluid crosstalk between the central nervous system (CNS) and the peripheral auditory apparatus. Specifically, physical continuity between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and inner ear fluids is maintained through parallel anatomical conduits, including the cochlear aqueduct [83–85], and the internal auditory canal [11, 12]. Emerging microanatomical tracing also delineates extensive perineural fluid networks along which CSF tracks to peripheral nerve terminals [86]. This supplementary anatomical route allows CNS-derived inflammatory mediators to bypass traditional neurovascular barriers and directly infiltrate the cochlear microenvironment during migraine attacks. Consequently, established CNS pathologies are increasingly linked to the development of delayed endolymphatic hydrops [87].

This fluid communication provides a direct anatomical route for central biochemical disturbances to destabilize inner ear homeostasis. During acute migraine episodes, the CSF accumulates high concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines and neuropeptides. Instead of remaining confined to the cranial vault, these biochemical mediators utilize the cochlear aqueduct and perineural networks to infiltrate the inner ear. Upon reaching the cochlear microenvironment, they catalyze a local oxidative burst and directly activate peripheral sensory ganglia [88]. Simultaneously, this inflammatory influx increases the endothelial permeability of the BLB [89]. At the cellular level, this permeability is driven by ROS-mediated oxidative degradation of tight junction proteins between endothelial cells [90]. Clinical imaging confirms this abnormal BLB permeability in migraine patients presenting with auditory symptoms [91]. For diagnostic and therapeutic protocols, this aberrant fluid exchange holds specific clinical relevance. It suggests that peripheral auditory dysfunction can be a direct downstream consequence of central neuroinflammation. Therefore, integrating concurrent systemic management to address the continuous influx of central inflammatory mediators may provide additional therapeutic benefits, effectively complementing localized inner ear treatments such as intratympanic injections.

Axis 2: neurochemical and neuroinflammatory cascades

This axis may contribute to cellular injury and inflammation within the cochlea (Fig. 2).

Neurotransmitter imbalance

Glutamate serves as the principal excitatory neurotransmitter within the auditory system. Under physiological conditions, its release and reuptake at cochlear ribbon synapses are strictly regulated to maintain synaptic homeostasis [92]. During acute migraine attacks, glutamate concentrations are elevated in both the cerebrospinal fluid and systemic circulation [93]. This excess glutamate accesses the inner ear through a compromised blood–labyrinth barrier or direct brain–ear fluid conduits. At the synaptic level, glutamate accumulation overstimulates postsynaptic AMPA and NMDA receptors, initiating massive calcium influx [94, 95]. This sustained calcium overload compromises mitochondrial membrane integrity within spiral ganglion neurons, precipitating the uncoupling of the electron transport chain and generating excessive mitochondrial ROS. The ensuing oxidative stress rapidly depletes local antioxidant reserves, initiating lipid peroxidation and downstream apoptotic cascades [96]. Consequently, this ROS-mediated excitotoxicity can induce spiral ganglion neuron degeneration and occult cochlear synaptic damage [97]. For clinical practice, this specific microstructural injury provides a direct biological explanation for patients with migraine who present with normal PTA thresholds but complain of persistent tinnitus and hyperacusis.

In contrast to the neurotoxic effects of excess glutamate, serotonin (5-HT) exerts multifaceted effects within the inner ear depending on receptor subtype and local concentration. Physiologically, endogenous 5-HT released from auditory efferents binds to inhibitory receptors on hair cells and spiral ganglion neurons, protecting against excitotoxic damage [98, 99]. During migraine episodes, altered 5-HT metabolism disrupts this local transmitter homeostasis [100]. Elevated, non-specific 5-HT binding can induce plasma extravasation within the inner ear microvasculature [101]. Concurrently, structures such as the spiral ganglion, vestibular ganglion, and stria vascularis express 5-HT1B/1D receptors, which are the established pharmacological targets of triptans in the trigeminovascular system [102]. Activation of these specific receptors by triptans induces microvascular constriction and inhibits the release of pro-inflammatory neuropeptides [103, 104]. Clinically, this distinct receptor distribution suggests that specific 5-HT agonists offer a dual therapeutic mechanism. They possess the potential to mitigate both the central migraine cascade and local cochlear microenvironmental disruption, provided that dosing is carefully managed to avoid ischemic side effects.

Neuropeptide dysregulation

Converging evidence establishes calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) as a pivotal driver of migraine pathogenesis [4, 105, 106]. It promotes the release of pro-inflammatory mediators [107, 108], and modulates glutamatergic neurotransmission at both presynaptic and postsynaptic levels [109–111], contributing to the sensitization of nociceptive circuits [106]. Pharmacokinetically, CGRP readily crosses the dura mater, entering the CSF and systemic circulation [112]. During attacks, this elevated CGRP accesses the inner ear through the compromised blood–labyrinth barrier and perineural channels. Previous investigations from our group demonstrate upregulated CGRP signaling within the inner ear of migraine animal models [113]. Functionally, peripheral CGRP elevation increases the spontaneous firing rate of the cochlear nerve [114], potentially mediating elevated ABR wave amplitudes, and amplifies the acoustic startle reflex [115]. These neurophysiological changes offer a structural and chemical basis for auditory hypersensitivity, such as hyperacusis and phonophobia, reported in clinical cohorts. Beyond functional sensitization, prolonged CGRP receptor activation initiates neurogenic inflammation within the cochlear microenvironment. Our laboratory has further shown that this inflammatory cascade, combined with mitochondrial DNA leakage and local cellular stress, induces programmed cell death in OHCs [113]. This structural degradation likely underlies the persistent hearing loss documented in patients with a prolonged migraine history.

Substance P (SP) is frequently co-released with CGRP from trigeminal afferents. The primary SP receptor, neurokinin-1, is expressed in the cochlear spiral ganglion [116], establishing an anatomical substrate for SP-mediated auditory modulation. Physiologically, trigeminal sensory fiber stimulation induces neurogenic plasma extravasation in the inner ear via SP release [71]. Furthermore, exogenous SP administration elevates cochlear blood flow [117], whereas its receptor antagonists inhibit auditory nerve action potentials [118, 119]. Together, the co-release and subsequent signaling of CGRP and SP disrupt inner ear homeostasis through direct modulation of auditory neural transmission and the induction of local neurogenic inflammation. From a translational perspective, this neuropeptide network highlights a critical intervention window. The targeted blockade of CGRP pathways, currently standard in neurology, warrants rigorous clinical evaluation for its secondary efficacy in rescuing migraine-associated auditory decline.

Immune cell activation

Following neuropeptide release, immune cell activation represents a subsequent component in migraine-related inner ear pathology. Tissue-resident mast cells mediate responses to these neuropeptide signals, contributing to localized neurogenic inflammation [120]. During acute migraine episodes, neuropeptides such as pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP) enter the systemic circulation and cerebrospinal fluid. These peptides bind to MRGPRX2 receptors on tissue-resident mast cells [121, 122], inducing degranulation and the release of mediators including chymase and histamine [123]. This degranulation affects the inner ear through two distinct pathways. Systemically, circulating pro-inflammatory mediators can compromise the blood–labyrinth barrier. Locally, because mast cells naturally reside within the mammalian cochlear parenchyma and endolymphatic sac [124], they mount a direct local inflammatory response. The concomitant release of ROS establishes a pro-oxidant microenvironment exhibiting cytotoxicity toward spiral ganglion neurons and hair cells [125]. Concurrently, mast cell activation in the endolymphatic sac impairs fluid reabsorption, potentially inducing secondary endolymphatic hydrops [126]. Pharmacologically, administering mast cell stabilizers such as sodium cromoglycate mitigates sensorineural hearing damage [127], and reduces the frequency of specific migraine attacks [128].

In addition to mast cells, tissue-resident macrophages participate in the inflammatory phase of migraine. Anatomically, macrophages are positioned within meningeal perivascular spaces near trigeminal nerve terminals [129], and along the cochlear aqueduct [130]. During the delayed phase of an attack, meningeal macrophages upregulate inducible nitric oxide synthase, releasing nitric oxide alongside cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 to maintain sensory sensitization [122]. Within the central nervous system, these neuroinflammatory cascades, mediated by inflammasome activation and cytokine release, likely lower the activation thresholds of neural pathways [131, 132]. Such central hyperexcitability offers a plausible mechanistic explanation for the altered ABR profiles. This inflammatory cascade may extend to the peripheral compartment, where macrophages constitute the primary immune cell population in the inner ear [133, 134]. In animal models of stress and migraine, cochlear macrophages exhibit marked activation and migration toward the OHC and ribbon synapse regions [113, 135]. This activation triggers the NLRP3 inflammasome and subsequent IL-1β release, exerting local cytotoxicity and leading to apoptosis [136].

The overlapping neuro-immune signatures of mast cells and macrophages provide a clear molecular rationale for migraine-associated auditory dysfunction. For clinical and translational practice, these findings suggest that the inner ear is highly vulnerable to systemic neuroinflammation. Rather than viewing inner ear damage as a strictly parallel phenomenon, clinicians and researchers should explore whether repurposing existing immunomodulatory agents, such as specific mast cell stabilizers or targeted inflammasome inhibitors, can provide a viable adjunct therapy to protect auditory function in refractory migraine phenotypes.

Axis 3: maladaptive central plasticity

Following peripheral pathogenesis, central nervous system abnormalities constitute the essential basis for the chronification of migraine-associated auditory dysfunction. Prolonged nociceptive input from the trigeminal system drives central sensitization, facilitating functional and structural remodeling within ascending auditory pathways and associated sensory networks. Symptoms such as tinnitus, hyperacusis, and phonophobia emerge from this combination of aberrant peripheral afferent signals and altered central sensory modulation. Notably, subclinical neurofunctional alterations often persist into the pain-free interictal period. These changes include auditory habituation deficits, thalamocortical dysrhythmia, impaired sensory gating, and altered limbic modulation (Fig. 3).

Nociceptive-auditory crosstalk: the brainstem relay

The cochlear nucleus (CN) functions as the initial relay of the central auditory pathway and a primary site for integrating auditory and somatosensory nociceptive information [137]. In migraine cohorts, prolonged latency and elevated amplitude of ABR wave III indicate aberrant primary auditory processing in this region [138]. Anatomically, VGLUT2-positive axons project directly from the trigeminal complex to the dorsal cochlear nucleus (DCN) [137, 139]. Chronic nociceptive input from the trigeminal system upregulates these excitatory projections, shifting the excitation-inhibition balance within the DCN [140]. This sustained input induces synaptic plasticity in principal neurons, characterized by increased spontaneous firing rates and long-term potentiation [141]. Consequently, the central nervous system erroneously encodes somatosensory signals as acoustic stimuli. Moreover, hyperactivity in DCN neurons reciprocally enhances sensitivity to trigeminal input [142]. This brainstem-level nociceptive-auditory feedback loop provides a distinct neural substrate for somatosensory tinnitus. Clinically, identifying this specific crosstalk mechanism suggests that peripheral trigeminal neuromodulation or targeted physical therapy for the cervical-trigeminal complex may offer secondary benefits in managing refractory tinnitus among migraine patients.

Decompensation of auditory efferent inhibition: MOC system dysfunction

Alongside alterations in the ascending auditory pathway, deficits in the descending inhibitory pathway participate directly in migraine-related auditory dysfunction. Under physiological conditions, the MOC system releases acetylcholine to inhibit OHCs electromotility, actively filtering background noise [143, 144]. In migraine cohorts, the suppressive effect of this system on OAEs is attenuated, reflecting compromised cochlear efferent regulation and a state of central auditory hypersensitivity [49, 53, 145]. Mechanistically, efferent terminals co-release CGRP, which inhibits local acetylcholine signaling [146]. Elevated CGRP levels around OHC occur in migraine animal models [113]. This local accumulation disrupts the negative feedback loop of the MOC system, impairing its descending regulatory function. The resulting efferent dysfunction compromises auditory signal filtering and lowers acoustic tolerance thresholds. For diagnostic protocols, this mechanism indicates that contralateral suppression testing of otoacoustic emissions can serve as an objective metric to quantify the loss of efferent inhibition. Integrating this assessment provides a physiological marker for hyperacusis that complements standard behavioral audiometry.

Impaired auditory perception and sensory gating: thalamocortical and limbic networks

Deficits in higher-order central auditory processing and sensory gating contribute significantly to the chronification of these symptoms. Sustained nociceptive input from the trigeminovascular system disrupts thalamic homeostasis [147]. Aberrant peripheral sensory input activates the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN), which subsequently issues inhibitory signals to thalamic relay nuclei, promoting thalamic neuronal hyperpolarization [148]. This hyperpolarized state relieves the inactivation of T-type calcium channels, shifting thalamic neuronal firing patterns from normal high-fidelity tonic firing to low-frequency (theta band) burst firing [149, 150]. These low-frequency oscillations propagate rostrally and are entrained by the cerebral cortex, eliciting high-frequency cortical gamma oscillations [151], and driving global thalamocortical dysrhythmia (TCD) [152]. Electrophysiologically, this TCD manifests as a habituation deficit of the P50 middle-latency auditory evoked potential, indicating impaired sensory gating within the primary auditory cortex [6, 153, 154]. In addition, the intensity dependence of auditory potentials, which serves as a metric of auditory pathway response gain, is frequently elevated in migraine, amplifying cortical acoustic responses [155–157]. Because the thalamus functions as an obligatory auditory relay [149], parallel TCD networks appear in patients with tinnitus [158], where auditory cortical gamma synchronization correlates with symptom severity [159]. Under this hypersensitive state, impaired sensory gating permits increased neural gain in response to ambient noise, generating clinical hyperacusis, while spontaneous cortical firing may be perceived as phantom tinnitus.

Deficits in sensory processing among migraine patients extend beyond primary signal perception to involve the limbic system. Auditory event–related potential studies suggest impaired habituation of cognitive components, including the contingent negative variation and P300 [160–163]. Aberrant limbic system activation provides a neural substrate for integrating acoustic stimuli with negative emotional valence [164]. Acoustic stimulation elicits heightened responses in the amygdala and nucleus accumbens (NAc) during both ictal and interictal phases [165, 166], modifying the emotional perception of sound [167]. The NAc regulates the dopaminergic reward and pain modulatory system implicated in migraine chronification [168, 169], operating within the top-down auditory cognitive gating network. Dysregulation here impairs the emotional gating of ambient noise [170, 171]. Disruption of these dopaminergic projections impairs both sensory and emotional habituation to auditory inputs. For comprehensive clinical management, this limbic involvement demonstrates that phonophobia and distressing tinnitus are not purely otologic defects, but involve severe affective network dysregulation. Consequently, multidisciplinary approaches incorporating cognitive behavioral therapy or centrally acting neuromodulators may be required to break the cycle of emotional hyper-responsiveness and sensory aversion in chronic patients.

Therapy

While inner ear symptoms are typically managed as primary otologic conditions, emerging evidence suggests that refractory cases, including idiopathic SSNHL and Ménière’s disease, may represent otologic manifestations of migraine pathophysiology. Due to this phenotypic overlap, clinical studies suggest that preventive migraine treatments may benefit patients presenting with inner ear disorders and concurrent migrainous features, even when ICHD diagnostic criteria are not strictly met [172]. For patients with SSNHL unresponsive to standard interventions, migraine-targeted pharmacological regimens offer a potential alternative. Available data indicate that these interventions can facilitate auditory recovery, including in individuals lacking classical headache presentations [173]. This perspective emphasizes addressing shared pathophysiological mechanisms rather than relying solely on isolated symptom management. Reviewing these targeted therapies serves a dual purpose: it not only outlines a multidisciplinary clinical management strategy but also provides robust reverse-translational validation for our proposed theoretical framework. By demonstrating that specific anti-migraine agents effectively resolve otologic symptoms, we substantiate the clinical plausibility of the peripheral-to-central continuum. Consequently, therapeutic interventions can be stratified based on specific biological targets. The following sections categorize current and emerging treatments according to three primary objectives: blocking upstream migraine pathways, protecting the cochlear microenvironment, and correcting central sensory hypersensitivity.

Blocking upstream migraine pathways: targeted neurogenic modulation

One therapeutic approach involves targeting the systemic neurochemical pathways implicated in migraine pathogenesis to potentially reduce the inflammatory burden on the inner ear. Anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies (e.g., erenumab) and small-molecule antagonists (e.g., rimegepant) attenuate headache and vestibular symptoms [174, 175]. In preclinical models, rimegepant administration attenuates OHC injury, an effect attributed to the inhibition of local neurogenic inflammation [113]. Preliminary clinical observations indicate that rimegepant use is associated with improved hearing thresholds [176]. Furthermore, recent clinical evidence demonstrates that both acute and preventive administration of rimegepant completely resolves refractory Ménière’s disease symptoms in patients with comorbid migraine [177], reinforcing the central role of CGRP in otologic pathogenesis. Beyond CGRP, the pharmacological modulation of other neuropeptides driving this localized inflammatory cascade, including SP, might present additional therapeutic targets. Future experimental and clinical studies are necessary to determine whether antagonizing these specific pathways provides comparable otoprotective effects.

Protecting the cochlear microenvironment: metabolic and vascular interventions

Stabilizing inner ear hemodynamics and mitigating localized oxidative stress represent additional therapeutic objectives. Conservative lifestyle and nutritional interventions contribute to this stabilization. Migraine and auditory symptoms frequently share precipitating factors, including specific dietary triggers, sleep disturbances, and psychological stress [178]. Dietary components such as tyramine, monosodium glutamate, and caffeine precipitate both attacks and auditory symptom fluctuations [179]. Implementing preventive dietary modifications reduces these symptoms [180, 181]. Furthermore, nutritional supplements utilized in migraine prophylaxis offer localized cochlear protection. Riboflavin supports mitochondrial function and mitigates inner ear oxidative stress [182, 183]. Magnesium supplementation reduces excitotoxicity [184], and exhibits protective effects on cochlear neural structures [185, 186]. Integrating these conservative modifications serves as an initial management strategy for patients with comorbid Ménière’s disease and migraine [187].

Pharmacological modulation of the local microcirculation provides another intervention pathway. Calcium channel blockers, which alter vasomotor reactivity [188], exhibit specific cytoprotective properties. In preclinical models, flunarizine attenuates cochlear hair cell apoptosis by limiting intracellular calcium influx, preventing mitochondrial calcium overload and subsequent ROS production [189]. Clinically, flunarizine reduces migraine-associated tinnitus severity [190], and serves as an adjunctive intervention in steroid-refractory SSNHL [191]. The non-dihydropyridine agent verapamil also increases sound tolerance thresholds [192], and decreases Tinnitus Functional Index scores [193, 194].

Conversely, the administration of β-adrenoceptor blockers, such as propranolol, presents mixed auditory outcomes. Prophylactic administration can stabilize pure-tone hearing thresholds [38], attenuate hyperacusis [195], and alleviate pulsatile tinnitus [196]. However, new-onset tinnitus and hearing decline are documented adverse events in specific treated subsets [197, 198]. Thus, the prophylactic use of β-blockers requires careful monitoring, as drug-induced systemic hypotension may inadvertently compromise cochlear perfusion.

Correcting central sensory hypersensitivity: neuromodulation and limbic regulation

Interventions targeting central sensory hypersensitivity address the maladaptive central network remodeling associated with interictal tinnitus, hyperacusis, and phonophobia. Anticonvulsant medications modulate central nervous system excitability. Topiramate enhances GABAergic transmission and antagonizes glutamatergic receptors to correct thalamocortical dysrhythmia [199]. Clinical data associate topiramate-inclusive regimens with reduced tinnitus severity [194], increased hyperacusis tolerance [192], and improved pure-tone thresholds [200]. Similarly, sodium valproate promotes spiral ganglion neuronal differentiation [201], and correlates with improved speech recognition scores [202]. However, the clinical application of valproate requires consideration of its dose-dependent neurotoxicity [203], and potential to induce de novo tinnitus [204]. The application of gabapentin for isolated otologic indications currently lacks sufficient high-quality evidence due to significant population heterogeneity [205–207].

Antidepressant medications, including tricyclic antidepressants and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, modulate monoamine neurotransmission [188]. This pharmacological profile is hypothesized to attenuate central auditory gain and influence nociceptive integration within the limbic system. Amitriptyline reduces subjective tinnitus severity [208], and improves thresholds in SSNHL [200]. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors like paroxetine decrease the tinnitus burden in patients with comorbid mood disorders [209], and elevate uncomfortable loudness levels in hyperacusis [210]. Clinical studies indicate both classes decrease episode frequency in Ménière’s disease [211].

Non-pharmacological interventions serve as adjunctive strategies. Cognitive behavioral therapy modulates emotional gating within auditory-limbic networks, while transcranial magnetic stimulation attenuates hyperexcitability within the auditory cortex [138, 212–214]. Clinical recognition of centralized hypersensitivity guides treatment selection. For patients exhibiting persistent auditory aversion or phantom sounds during pain-free intervals, peripherally acting otologic agents may demonstrate limited efficacy. In such cohorts, evaluating central neuromodulators, cognitive behavioral therapy, or targeted brain stimulation provides alternative approaches to address the underlying network dysregulation.

Conclusion and future directions

Migraine represents a systemic disorder of multisensory sensitization extending beyond the trigeminovascular system. Within this pathological network, migraine-associated auditory dysfunction might emerge as a distinct clinical entity rather than an incidental comorbidity. The inner ear and central auditory pathways are vulnerable, with sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, and hyperacusis linked to a continuum of microvascular ischemia, neurochemical imbalance, and central neural network remodeling.

Currently, the precise causal vector, whether the inner ear acts strictly as a downstream target of central neuroinflammation or functions as an independent pathological node, remains undetermined. Rather than establishing a definitive causal sequence, this review highlights a critical clinical reality: the necessity of auditory protection in migraine management. Tiered audiological assessments serve as the cornerstone for the early identification of subclinical auditory injury, enabling mechanism-driven, cross-disciplinary therapeutic strategies.

Future longitudinal cohorts and targeted mechanistic studies are essential to elucidate these causal directions and establish unified diagnostic criteria for “cochlear migraine.” Concurrently, objective biomarkers, such as CGRP, should be validated to improve early and precise phenotyping. Large-scale multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are needed to validate the efficacy of mechanism-targeted interventions using objective audiological endpoints. Resolving these knowledge gaps and implementing integrated neurology-otology clinical pathways will ultimately facilitate evidence-based, full-chain neuro-otologic management for this patient population.
