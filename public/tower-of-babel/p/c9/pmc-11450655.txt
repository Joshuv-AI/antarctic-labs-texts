Switching carbamazepine to lacosamide improves gamma‐glutamyl transpeptidase levels
Authors: Yuichi Kubota, Mario Prado
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbamazepine (CBZ) use has been limited by multiple adverse reactions. Lacosamide (LCM) is a functional amino acid anti‐seizure medication (ASM), approved for focal seizure patients more than 4 years old. It is non‐inferior in terms of efficacy to controlled release CBZ and was proven to have better tolerability. This study examines the effect of abruptly changing CBZ to LCM in epilepsy patients with elevated gamma‐glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT). Consenting adult patients aged 18 years old and above, with controlled focal seizure disorder for more than 2 years, who were consistently taking CBZ and who had elevated GGT were included in this study. Out of 1526 patients screened, only 12 satisfied the inclusion criteria. After abruptly changing CBZ to LCM, the GGT level significantly dropped from a median of 141.5 to 63.5 IU/L (z = 3.06, p = 0.0005). Moreover, there was significantly lower proportion of patients with abnormal GGT levels after the switch in medications was done (100% vs. 66.7%, McNemar χ2 = 8, p = 0.008). Moderate to high levels of GGT in patients with focal epilepsy can be decreased by changing CBZ to LCM. Moreover, abruptly changing CBZ to LCM without cross‐titration may be safe and effective in preventing seizure incidence within a 1‐month period.Plain Language SummaryAlthough carbamazepine (CBZ) is the standard drug for focal seizures, its numerous side effects, especially in the liver, limits its use in a lot of patients with epilepsy. Gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), which is elevated in liver disease of whatever cause including intake of CBZ, is associated with increased mortality. In this study, we found that abruptly changing CBZ to Lacosamide (LCM) can significantly decrease the GGT level in 1 month without apparent increase in seizure recurrence and side effects. Therefore, we conclude that high levels of GGT may be decreased by abruptly changing CBZ to LCM.

INTRODUCTION

Carbamazepine (CBZ) is a first‐generation anti‐seizure medication (ASM) developed in 1950s, still used primarily in developing countries as first‐line medication for focal seizures. 1 It is a cheap, effective, and safe inhibitor of fast sodium (Na) channels which serves as a standard for comparison of newer ASMs for focal epilepsy. Moreover, due to its mechanism of action and ability to increase the threshold for excitation, it is also commonly prescribed in patients with hyperexcitability disorders such as peripheral nerve hyperexcitability (PNH) syndromes, hemifacial spasm, and some neuropathic disorders. 1 , 2 Nevertheless, as it strongly induces the cytP450 enzyme, its use has been limited by multiple adverse reactions. 1 , 3

CBZ is known to auto‐induce itself, decreasing its steady‐state concentration by 50% in 3 weeks from initiation. 1 , 3 , 4 Moreover, due to its strong protein binding, drug–drug interaction is common causing low correlation between oral and serum CBZ concentration, thus necessitating the need for frequent serum dose monitoring. Further, among patients with known genetic susceptibility such as Asians with HLA‐B*1502, HLA‐B*1511, and HLA‐A*3101 alleles, the risk for severe cutaneous reactions like Steven‐Johnson Syndrome (SJS) or Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis Syndrome (TENS) is increased to 10 times when CBZ is taken. 1 Specifically those with HLA‐A*3101, CBZ‐induced skin reactions are increased from 5% to 26%. Secondary to its enzyme‐inducing potential, CBZ has also been known to decrease WBC, folate, and thyroid hormones among others resulting in different metabolic derangements such as hypercholesterolemia and metabolic syndrome. 1 , 4 , 5 , 6 As induction happens in the liver, elevation of markers for hepatic disease such as alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), and gamma‐glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) is frequently encountered. 3 , 4 , 5 , 7 , 8 , 9

GGT is an enzyme responsible for extracellular catabolism of glutathione (GSH). As such, it is a marker for oxidative stress and increased production of reactive oxygen species. 3 , 7 , 10 , 11 Although it is most abundant in the kidneys, its elevation in the serum often marks a possible liver damage. As it is commonly found elevated in almost all conditions that involve liver problem, its specificity is limited, and in patients on CBZ, it is often believed that its elevation is secondary to induction and not on any hepatic pathology. 3 , 4 , 7 Nevertheless, in a recent study, elevated GGT regardless of cause is linked to dose‐dependent increased risk of mortality. In the same study, patients with severely elevated GGT were also found to have around seven times risk of dying secondary to liver disease. 12 CBZ is known to increase GGT more than the normal level in up to 33% of patients. 7

When complications from CBZ are present, it is advised to switch to oxcarbazepine (OXC), its structurally related but less toxic form. Due to its higher hyponatremic potential however, other epilepsy specialists often prefer to switch to safer third‐generation ASMs than OXC. 1 Lacosamide (LCM) is a third‐generation, functional amino acid ASM, approved for focal seizure disorder patients more than 4 years of age. 13 , 14 It is non‐inferior in terms of efficacy to controlled release CBZ (CBZ CR) and was proven to have better tolerability and less drug–drug interactions as it has low protein binding and very minimal to none inducing and inhibition capability of cytP450 enzymes. 8 , 9 Similar to Levetiracetam, it has high first‐pass effect in the GI tract, ensuring almost similar oral and serum dose concentrations. Moreover, as it is metabolized in the kidney, it does not usually affect the blood chemistries unlike CBZ. 13 , 14 This study examines the effect of abruptly changing CBZ to LCM in epilepsy patients with elevated GGT.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Consenting adult patients aged 18 years old and above, with controlled focal seizure disorder for more than 2 years, who are consistently taking CBZ, whether monotherapy or in combination with other ASMs, and who had elevated GGT were included in this study. Those with identified other possible causes of liver damage or elevated GGT such as hepatitis and/or hepatoma as well as those who cannot switch medications due to cost or other reasons were excluded.

Operational definition of variables

The abrupt change from CBZ to LCM served as the independent variable while the change in GGT; incidence of seizures within a month after the change; and safety of LCM were considered dependent variables in this study. Abnormal GGT level was defined as more than 40 IU/L.

Data collection

Charts of OPD patients with focal epilepsy who were taking CBZ were screened. General data, dose of CBZ prior to change, dose of started LCM, use of other ASMs, individual GGT prior and 1 month after the change, incidence of seizure after the change, and occurrence of side effects probably related to LCM were collected in an abstraction form. Data regarding alcohol intake among patients were also obtained. This paper followed the Declaration of Helsinki. The author ensures that all procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines, and this paper has been approved by the appropriate institutional committee (TMG Asaka Medical Center Ethics Board).

Statistical methods

Comparison between before and after the switch GGT level was done using signed rank test, while comparison of proportion with abnormal GGT level before and after the change was conducted using McNemar test. Moreover, comparisons of mean change in GGT between monotherapy and polytherapy and between alcoholic and non‐alcoholic drinkers were done using rank sum test. All data were inputted in Microsoft Excel, while analysis was done using STATA SE 18.0. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was used as a cut‐off to determine statistical significance.

RESULTS

Of the 1526 focal epilepsy patients screened, 528 (34.6%) were taking carbamazepine. Of these, only 12 (2.3%) patients satisfied the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Baseline characteristics of the included patients

Most patients included were male (91.7%) with a median age of 48.5 years (range: 23–82 years). The median CBZ dose per day was 200 mg (range: 200–600), while the median LCM dose was 100 mg (range: 100–200). Half are using other ASMs aside from CBZ, with most taking Levetiracetam (3/6). Other ASMs taken include clozapine and perampanel. Only two (16.7%) were alcohol drinkers (see Table 1).

Change in GGT levels

After abruptly changing CBZ to LCM, the GGT level significantly dropped from a median of 141.5 to 63.5 IU/L (z = 3.06, p = 0.0005). Even when patients who were on alcohol were removed, the decrease was still significant (133.5 IU/L vs. 57.5 IU/L, z = 2.803, p = 0.002). There was no difference in the mean change in GGT levels before and after the change between mono and polytherapy with ASMs (110 IU/L vs. 98 IU/L, z = 0.480, p = 0.7), while there was significantly higher change in the alcoholic than non‐alcoholic epilepsy patients (210 IU/L vs. 83.1 IU/L, z = −2.15, p = 0.03). After the change, there was significantly lower proportion of patients with abnormal GGT levels (100% vs. 66.7%, McNemar χ2 = 8, p = 0.008) (see Tables 1 and 2; Figure 1).

Safety of LCM

Only one patient had seizure after abruptly changing CBZ to LCM. Beyond 1 month, no seizure was noted in all 12 patients. Although one excluded patient developed rash immediately after changing CBZ to LCM, none of the 12 patients included in the study developed similar or other complications.

DISCUSSION

This study established that GGT levels may be increased after initiation of CBZ and may be decreased once CBZ is discontinued or shifted to other ASMs like LCM. Moreover, this study also noted that abruptly shifting CBZ to LCM is probably safe and effective as only one patient had seizure within a month. Further, this study also found the possible interaction between alcohol and CBZ in increasing GGT as alcoholic patients had higher baseline GGT levels than non‐alcoholic patients.

The decrease in GGT after discontinuation or shifting CBZ to other ASMs has been consistent with other studies. While most studies found elevation of GGT after initiation of CBZ, the study by Isojarvi also noted that shifting CBZ to OXC can decrease GGT in 2 months onward as well as improve other hematologic markers such as white blood cells. 5 , 8 Nevertheless, in studies involving GGT and CBZ, it is most often assumed that GGT elevation is probably caused by the induction of cytP450 by CBZ and not really secondary to any hepatic pathology. 3 , 5 , 7 However, in a recent paper by Cho, any elevation of GGT was found to increase the risk of dying from any cause, and specific to liver disease by 1.4 and seven times, respectively. 12 Nevertheless, a prediction tool to determine which among the patients with drug‐induced elevation of GGT will have unfavorable outcomes has not yet been developed hence it is recommended that in patients with moderate to severe elevation of GGT, either decreasing the dose or shifting CBZ to safer ASMs with similar efficacy is recommended, just like what we did in our cohort.

Abruptly changing the ASM of an epilepsy patient with controlled seizure is also controversial. Accordingly, cross‐titration within a month is sometimes suggested to decrease the risk of breakthrough seizure especially when the reason for shifting is not life threatening. 15 However, in our paper, we have changed CBZ to LCM safely and effectively, with only one of our patients having a seizure within a month. This is similar to the study conducted by Isojarvi when they abruptly changed CBZ to OXC. 5 Either the long elimination half‐life of CBZ or the high bioavailability of LCM, or both, are hypothesized to be the reasons.

Within a month of observation, none of our 12 patients had adverse events secondary to LCM. In recent clinical trials comparing LCM against CBZ CR, LCM was found to be non‐inferior, more tolerated, and with less potential for drug–drug interactions. 8 , 9 Specifically, in that study, only 4.3% of patients discontinued LCM compared to 7.8% who stopped intake of CBZ CR. 8 Moreover, only nasopharyngitis, headache, and dizziness were found to be the most common treatment‐emergent adverse events in the LCM cohort with slight prolongation of PR interval as possibly the worst complication. 8 , 14 Psychiatric, metabolic, weight changes, rashes, and cognitive disorders were also recognized but rare complications of LCM. The inert inhibiting and inducing capability of LCM can be the reason for the low incidence of these complications. 13 , 14 Although safe and effective, the price of LCM may limit its use especially in low‐ to middle‐income countries. In these situations, oxcarbazepine may be a cheaper alternative.

The low sample size and the short observation time decrease the power to observe significant change. For the change in GGT level from baseline to 1 month however, this was not observed. This can be attributed to large difference in GGT between prior to and after CBZ was changed to LCM. This was observed even when non‐parametric statistics were used. We expect that the same finding will still be persistent even when larger samples were to be used in future studies. In contrast, as the incidence of seizure recurrence and adverse drug events increases with the number of subjects and observation time, we cannot concretely conclude that abruptly changing CBZ to LCS will not lead to higher occurrence of these complications (a type 2 error). This is one of the limitations of the study as will be described later.

The higher baseline pre‐change and mean change in CGT in alcoholic patients is interesting and might suggest possible interaction between alcohol and CBZ. While CBZ is a treatment medication in patients suffering from alcohol dependence, it is usually taken only for a period of time unlike in alcoholic patients with epilepsy. 16 A larger study may be needed to clear the connection between long‐term intake of CBZ and alcohol. Other important effect modifiers or confounders such as presence of co‐morbidities and intake of other medications among others should also be obtained and analyzed in future studies.

Limitations

The retrospective nature of the study limits the number of variables to be included. Moreover, the absence of control (CBZ shifted to other ASMs like OXC) and short observation time (1 month) limit the validity of our results. Further, the low number of samples (despite screening more than 1500 patients) makes the study underpowered and prone to type 2 errors, especially for the safety and seizure outcomes. A large randomized controlled trial using the same population, with LCM as the intervention and other ASMs like OXC as control, to be observed for longer period is recommended if studies with similar objective are to be done in the future.

CONCLUSION

Moderate to high levels of GGT in patients with focal epilepsy can be decreased by changing CBZ to LCM. Moreover, abruptly changing CBZ to LCM without cross‐titration may be safe and effective in preventing seizure incidence within a 1‐month period.
