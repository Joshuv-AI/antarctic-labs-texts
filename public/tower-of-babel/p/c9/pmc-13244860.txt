Are right-sided diaphragmatic parameters sufficient to accurately evaluate global diaphragmatic physiological function? Commentary on “diaphragm physiological similarity index (DPSI): a de novo speckle-tracking ultrasound metric for HFNC adjustment in acute respiratory failure”
Authors: Feier Song, Ying Chen, Jie Ma, Daozheng Huang
Published: 2026

He et al. introduced the diaphragm physiological similarity index (DPSI), a speckle-tracking ultrasound metric quantifying how closely diaphragmatic motion resembles a healthy reference pattern during HFNC therapy in ARF [1]. DPSI represents a shift from amplitude-based assessment toward pattern recognition and was shown to be feasible and reproducible in clinical practice. DPSI correlated moderately with peak diaphragm excursion (r² = 0.60), the ROX index (r² = 0.62), and diaphragmatic thickening fraction (r² = 0.68). Several issues, however, warrant further discussion.

A key strength of DPSI is its separation of waveform morphology from absolute diaphragmatic displacement through signal normalization. The healthy reference template, derived from 45 volunteers across different age groups, showed similar normalized displacement–time curves despite age-related differences in excursion, resulting in comparable DPSI values (Fig. 3). This suggests that DPSI primarily reflects temporal coordination and motion pattern rather than contraction magnitude, distinguishing it from conventional indices such as diaphragmatic excursion or thickening fraction. In ARF patients with reduced diaphragmatic displacement, DPSI therefore emphasizes waveform features including smoothness, peak timing, and synchrony, potentially detecting discoordination patterns—such as delayed activation, multiphasic waveforms, or segmental asynchrony—not captured by amplitude-based measurements alone.

More importantly, DPSI was proposed to guide HFNC titration toward diaphragm-protective respiratory support in non-intubated ARF patients. The concept of diaphragm protection originates from invasive mechanical ventilation, where both over-assistance and insufficient support may be harmful: excessive unloading can promote diaphragm disuse atrophy, whereas inadequate support under high respiratory drive may cause diaphragm injury [2]. Extending this physiological concept to HFNC is innovative, as the optimal HFNC flow setting remains poorly defined. DPSI therefore represents an attempt to identify flow conditions associated with more coordinated diaphragmatic motion and potentially more balanced respiratory muscle loading.

A major limitation is that only the right hemidiaphragm was evaluated. Unilateral diaphragmatic dysfunction is common in critically ill patients, and compensatory mechanisms may obscure impairment [3]. For example, left-sided dysfunction may be masked by increased right-sided activity, while early right diaphragmatic dysfunction may be compensated for by preserved left-sided function. Restricting analysis to the right hemidiaphragm risks overlooking asymmetric dysfunction and compensatory patterns that may be clinically relevant [3]. Although left hemidiaphragm speckle tracking is technically more difficult, advances in ultrasound imaging have improved left-sided visualization and may make bilateral assessment increasingly achievable [4].

Another methodological concern relates to the acquisition. Since DPSI is derived from displacement–time waveform analysis, accurate alignment between the ultrasound tracking axis and the true direction of diaphragmatic motion is essential. The manuscript provides limited technical detail regarding excursion measurement and whether anatomical motion-mode (AMM) was performed during speckle-tracking analysis [5]. Moreover, Although cohort 2 was primarily designed to evaluate inter-operator reproducibility, DPSI values were compared across ICU patients receiving different respiratory support modalities. Under pressure-assisted conditions, diaphragmatic excursion may not accurately reflect true inspiratory muscle effort because displacement can be substantially influenced by ventilator assistance, lung inflation, and patient–ventilator interaction [6, 7], potentially affecting the physiological interpretation of DPSI.

The authors examined the relationship between DPSI and the ROX index ([SpO₂/FiO₂]/respiratory rate), a commonly used predictor of HFNC failure [1]. However, the VOX index, defined as (SpO₂/FiO₂)/tidal volume, may better reflect early increases in respiratory drive and has shown promising predictive performance for intubation [8, 9]. Future studies evaluating the relationship between DPSI and VOX may therefore provide additional clinical insight.

DPSI was developed using a healthy volunteer-derived reference pattern. However, ARF patients often exhibit altered respiratory drive, increased inspiratory load, respiratory muscle weakness, and systemic inflammation. Under these conditions, reproducing a “healthy” diaphragmatic motion pattern may not necessarily represent the optimal physiological target. Diaphragm-protective respiratory support aims not simply to normalize diaphragm motion, but to avoid both insufficient effort and excessive inspiratory load that may contribute to diaphragm injury [10, 11]. Therefore, maximizing DPSI may not always indicate optimal diaphragm protection.

Although waveform morphology appeared similar across healthy age groups, the pooled reference template inevitably oversimplifies the heterogeneity of diaphragmatic dysfunction in ARF. In the third ARF cohort, DPSI values were highest at HFNC flow rates around 30–40 L/min, suggesting this range may be physiologically favorable. However, whether these “optimal” DPSI conditions truly reflect diaphragm-protective breathing effort remains uncertain. As shown in Fig. 4, DPSI alone cannot reliably determine the optimal HFNC flow, and serial measurements may be required to identify individualized responses. The substantial interindividual variability observed further suggests that disease severity remains the primary determinant of HFNC titration, with DPSI serving mainly as an adjunctive physiological marker.

The authors appropriately acknowledge that a high DPSI does not necessarily indicate diaphragm protection, since coordinated motion may coexist with excessive inspiratory effort. Future studies should therefore determine whether diaphragmatic thickening fraction at these flow settings falls within the proposed diaphragm-protective range (15–30%), and whether inspiratory effort indices such as ΔPes, diaphragmatic electrical activity (EAdi), or P0.1 are simultaneously reduced to physiologically appropriate levels [2]. Without concurrent assessment of inspiratory effort and diaphragm-protective parameters, waveform similarity alone may be insufficient to identify truly protective HFNC settings.

Accordingly, whether DPSI-guided HFNC titration improves clinically meaningful outcomes remains uncertain and should be validated against respiratory drive, VOX index, diaphragm-protective parameters, and patient-centered outcomes rather than waveform similarity alone [12].

There are also minor typographical errors in the manuscript: the legends for Figs. 3 and 4 appear to have been interchanged, and the correlation coefficient was incorrectly reported as r instead of r².

In summary, DPSI is an innovative and physiologically interesting metric that assesses diaphragmatic coordination independently of absolute displacement. However, important limitations remain, including unilateral assessment, reliance on a pooled healthy reference template, and uncertainty regarding whether similarity to a healthy waveform represents an appropriate target in critically ill patients. Future studies should focus on refining the methodological framework of DPSI and determining its clinical value in ARF management through prospective outcome-based validation.
