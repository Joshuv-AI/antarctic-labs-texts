A choline-sensing regulator coordinates metabolic adaptation and pathogenesis in Pseudomonas aeruginosa pulmonary infections
Authors: Yingjie Song, Xiyu Wu, Bo Song, Ziqi Zhu, Derong Dai, Qinqin Ma, Rui Bao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pseudomonas aeruginosa exploits host-derived phosphatidylcholine (PC) to establish persistent lung infections, yet the mechanistic link between metabolic adaptation and pathogenesis remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that choline (Cho)–induced regulator (CodR), a GcvA-type transcriptional regulator, serves as a master regulator integrating virulence, antibiotic resistance, and PC catabolism during pulmonary infection. CodR directly binds Cho, the key metabolite of PC degradation, to activate pchP and norA, facilitating exogenous PC/Cho utilization. Genome-wide profiling reveals that CodR targets conserved motifs in promoters of mexA, pslA, and amrZ, synchronizing virulence and tolerance pathways. codR deletion attenuated biofilm formation, type III secretion system activity, siderophore production, and PC catabolism, reducing bacterial pathogenicity in a murine pneumonia model. Notably, Cho/PC pretreatment potentiates CodR-dependent transcriptional activation of antibiotic resistance genes, elevating tolerance to ciprofloxacin and meropenem. Our findings elucidate a paradigm wherein P. aeruginosa co-opts host-derived Cho via CodR to simultaneously potentiate virulence and antibiotic resilience, exposing CodR as a druggable node to break infection-resistance synergies.

INTRODUCTION

The evolutionary refinement of metabolic adaptation strategies in pathogenic microorganisms represents a ‌central paradigm‌ for understanding ‌host-microbe interplay (1–3). During initial infection phases‌, diverse bacterial species ‌activate sophisticated nutrient acquisition systems‌ to overcome host nutritional immunity, ‌establishing critical footholds‌ for subsequent colonization and systemic spread (4–6). Emerging evidence reveals‌ that these assimilated molecules ‌function beyond conventional metabolic roles‌, ‌orchestrating‌ virulence regulation and bacterial persistence through ‌dynamic metabolic cross-talk (7, 8). A notable illustration‌ is Staphylococcus aureus’s ‌bimodal adaptation strategy‌: While ‌exploiting exogenous proline for enhanced planktonic proliferation‌, it ‌concurrently activates biofilm formation‌ in response to immunometabolites such as itaconate, ‌demonstrating remarkable plasticity‌ in mucosal microenvironment navigation (1, 8). ‌Among clinically relevant pathogenic microorganisms‌, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ‌stands out for its devastating clinical impact‌, driving ‌infection-related mortality‌ in chronic respiratory disorders including cystic fibrosis (CF), non-CF bronchiectasis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (9–11). During pulmonary infections, this highly adaptable pathogen co-opts host-derived phosphatidylcholine (PC) as a ‌dual-function carbon/nitrogen source to maintain high–cell density growth (12, 13). This metabolic dependency positions cognition and acquisition of exogenous PC as a critical determinant of respiratory tract colonization success (14).

Bacterial PC metabolism is initially triggered via the released lipase C and hemolytic phospholipase C (PlcH) from P. aeruginosa, which degrade host cell membrane components, including PC and sphingomyelin (SM), to phosphorylcholine (ChoP), glycerol, and long-chain fatty acids (14, 15). ChoP is thought to access to the periplasm via outer membrane porins and hydrolyzed to choline (Cho) by bacterial ChoP phosphatase (PchP) (14). At least four active transporters have been reported to be involved in Cho transport, including BetT1, BetT2, BetT3, and CbcXWV (16–18). The cytoplasmic Cho is further oxidized to glycine betaine (GB), which usually serves as an osmoprotectant and general stress protectant in bacteria (18). The CF lung environment is especially osmotically stressful because of the accumulation of dehydrated mucus and electrolytes (19, 20). The conversion of Cho promotes P. aeruginosa to adapt to high osmolality and other environmental stresses such as antibiotic treatments (18, 21). Evidences also confirmed that deletion of the plcH or genes involved in Cho oxidase system impaired bacterial survival in the mouse lung (21, 22). Despite its scarcity in external environments, Cho’s dual role in both bacterial pathogenicity and antibiotic tolerance underscores the necessity for P. aeruginosa to prioritize its acquisition (18, 21).

Similar to other well-studied soil and water-dwelling pathogens, P. aeruginosa uses versatile strategies to balance Cho metabolism via sophisticated signal–sensing mechanisms (14, 17, 18, 22). The regulator Glycine betaine- and dimethylglycine-responsive regulator (GbdR) was previously reported to be necessary for Cho catabolism through sensing exogenous GB and dimethylglycine (DMG) (16). Data from genetics and chemical biology experiments supported that GbdR bound to the promoters of genes encoding GB catabolic enzymes and Cho transport proteins (17). Deletion of gbdR leaded to notably down-regulation of downstream target genes in the presence of Cho; nevertheless, there is no direct evidence to show that GbdR could recognize GB or its intermediates (17, 18). Another AraC family regulator SouR (Sarcosine oxidation and utilization regulator) was found to be required for full growth when sarcosine as a carbon and nitrogen source by inducing the expression of genes involved in the catabolism of sarcosine (23). Furthermore, the transcriptional regulator CdhR (Community development and haemin regulator) could enhance its own expression and levels of Cho catabolism operon in response to carnitine, one of quaternary amine compounds (24). Collectively, these findings preliminarily revealed the intricate regulatory networks of Cho metabolism in P. aeruginosa, while the molecular mechanisms linking metabolic adaptation to Cho-mediated pathogenesis and tolerance are still not fully understood.

In this work, we demonstrate that CodR (Cho-induced regulator), a putative GcvA-like regulator located adjacent to pchP and betT2, serves as a master regulator that directly links extracellular Cho availability to virulence deployment and antibiotic resilience. Specifically, we identify that Cho functions as a ligand of CodR to induce the expression of codR and downstream target genes, including mexA, pslA, and amrZ. The codR knockout attenuates the expression levels of proteins related to Cho metabolism, type III secretion system (T3SS), siderophore biosynthesis, biofilm formation, and antibiotic resistance, leading to impaired colonization in a mouse lung infection model. Furthermore, CodR enhances bacterial resistance to ciprofloxacin and meropenem in a Cho-dependent manner, suggesting a potential target for therapeutic interventions. Together, this study unveils a pivotal metabolic adaptation strategy by which CodR integrates host-derived Cho signaling to modulate virulence and antibiotic resistance in P. aeruginosa, shedding light on the sophisticated mechanisms of host-pathogen interaction.

RESULTS

Identified an autoregulated GcvA family regulator involved in PC metabolism

The transcriptional regulation of gene clusters involved in bacterial Cho catabolism is predominantly mediated by neighboring regulatory proteins, exemplified by GbdR from P. aeruginosa (17), and glycine betaine-responsive regulator/osmoprotectant uptake system regulator (GbsR/OpcR) from Bacillus subtilis (25). Comparative genomic analysis of P. aeruginosa reference strains UCBPP-PA14 and PAO1 revealed at least seven distinct Cho-associated gene clusters (fig. S1A). To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms governing Cho metabolism in P. aeruginosa PAO1, we systematically screened these clusters and identified a putative LysR-type transcriptional regulator (LTTR), PA5293 (designated as codR), positioned immediately upstream of betT2 and pchP (Fig. 1A). Notably, the adjacent norA gene on the opposing strand encodes a multidrug efflux pump (26), and homology searching demonstrated the widespread conservation of the codR-norA locus across Pseudomonas species (Fig. 1A). Moreover, CodR and its homologs exhibited strong phylogenetic affinity with the GcvA subfamily, known for its dual regulatory role in glycine cleavage system activation (Fig. 1B). Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) confirmed the direct interaction between CodR and its own promoter region (Fig. 1C), prompting the hypothesis that CodR might autoregulate the codR-pchP operon. Functional validation using a β-galactosidase reporter system demonstrated a 2.79-fold transcriptional reduction in ΔcodR compared to wild-type (WT) strain (***P < 0.001; Fig. 1D), whereas no significant alteration was observed in ΔnorA or ΔpchP mutant (P > 0.05; Fig. 1E). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) corroborated these findings, revealing a substantial decrease in mRNA levels of pchP and norA in ΔcodR relative to WT strain (**P < 0.01; Fig. 1E and fig. S1B). These data collectively establish CodR as a positive regulator of pchP and norA transcription.

Given that PchP hydrolyzes host-derived ChoP to Cho, sustaining P. aeruginosa growth (27), we next hypothesized that CodR might be associated with the regulation of PC catabolism. The results from bacterial growth curves showed that deletion of codR notably impaired bacterial growth in M9-PC medium containing PC as a sole source of carbon/nitrogen, whereas no growth defect was observed in M9–palmitic acid medium (Fig. 1, F and G). In vivo reporter assays revealed a 2.97-fold increase in codR promoter activity in PC-containing medium (**P < 0.01; Fig. 1H), which was abolished in ΔcodR (Fig. 1H). Consistent with the results mentioned above, transcriptional assay confirmed that presence of PC notably enhanced the expression level of codR by 3.44-fold (**P < 0.01; Fig. 1I). Crucially, pchP deletion disrupted the activation of codR in M9-PC (Fig. 1I), establishing an intracellular link between PC catabolism and CodR-mediated regulation.

CodR functions as a specific response regulator of host-derived Cho

To elucidate CodR’s role in PC metabolism, we used DeepFRI for functional prediction based on sequence features (28). The results indicated that CodR functions as a hypothetical organic compound binding regulator responsible for cellular metabolic process regulation with a high score of 0.97. Moreover, CodR shares 23.71% sequence similarity with CO2-concentrating mechanism regulator (CcmR) from Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803, which is essential for bacterial adaption to nutritional variations by sensing external 2-oxoglutarate (29). Considering that CodR is located upstream of Cho-associated cluster and responsible for PC metabolism in P. aeruginosa, we hypothesized its potential recognition of PC-derived molecules (Fig. 2A). Thus, the microscale thermophoresis (MST) assay was performed, and the results demonstrated that CodR bound to the Cho and GB with estimated dissociation constant (KD) values of 1.27 ± 0.19 and 161.17 ± 18.65 μM, respectively (Fig. 2B), while showing no affinity for ChoP, DMG, or glycine (fig. S2, A to C). In vivo promoter-lacZ fusion reporter assay indicated that additive Cho could remarkably increase the transcriptional activity of codR promoter (**P < 0.01; Fig. 2C), and this induction was diminished in ΔcodR strain, establishing CodR as a Cho-responsive regulator.

EMSA revealed dose-dependent enhancement of CodR-DNA binding by Cho, achieving complete probe shift at 1:8 DNA/protein ratio (Fig. 2, D and E). Meanwhile, both PC and GB did not affect the binding of CodR to target gene promoter (Fig. 2F). MST analysis further confirmed that the addition of Cho rather than GB approximately halved the KD value of the CodR-DNA interaction (fig. S2D). Consistent with this, the expression level of codR in WT P. aeruginosa was remarkably up-regulated by 3.27- and 4.57-fold in the M9 medium supplied with PC and Cho, respectively (***P < 0.001; Fig. 2G). The adjacent norA gene exhibited similar expression pattern, with a 4.71- to 9.34-fold increase as the amount of Cho increased (***P < 0.001; Fig. 2G). A modest 1.52- to 1.83-fold codR induction was also observed in the presence of GB (*P < 0.05; Fig. 2G). Given that Cho predominantly derives from eukaryotic hosts, while GB is more environmentally ubiquitous, the heightened responsiveness to Cho may optimize codR-pchP operon activation and subsequent PC catabolism in P. aeruginosa. This strategy likely enhances metabolic flexibility during early infection, facilitating bacterial colonization.

The common mechanism of LTTR-dependent synergistic transcriptional activation is proposed as two LTTR dimers simultaneously bind with their cognate binding sites (30, 31). The LTTR would tetramerize upon inducer binding, leading to the formation of a competent transcription activation complex with RNA polymerase (30). In line with this, CodR formed a homodimer in aqueous solution as depicted by gel filtration (fig. S3A). The native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis showed that CodR interacts with DNA in a ratio of 4:1, as the overall molecular weight of complex was close to 220 kDa, and Cho binding did not affect the assemble of CodR-DNA complex (fig. S3B). These findings support a model where Cho acts as an inducer to stabilize CodR-DNA interactions, facilitating transcription complex assembly through LTTR-typical tetramerization.

Numerous studies have identified a conserved structural motif among LTTRs, characterized by a large C-terminal regulatory domain (RD) composed of two Rossmann fold–like subdomains (RD-I and RD-II) (31). Our structural prediction using AlphaFold2 revealed that CodR adopted a canonical LTTR architecture, comprising an N-terminal DNA binding domain (DBD) and two C-terminal RDs (Fig. 2H). Moreover, the RD-I and RD-II interface of CodR constituted a potential ligand binding pocket, which is usually responsible for the allosteric regulation of LTTR-dependent transcription activation (Fig. 2H) (30, 31). To elucidate the molecular basis of CodR regulation, we used AlphaFold3 and complementary protein-ligand docking tools to investigate CodR-Cho interactions (32). Docking analysis demonstrated stable binding of Cho within the ligand-binding pocket of CodR (estimated binding energy of −3.71 kcal/mol), surrounding by residues P99, S100, T101, W129, R198, T223, and L224 (Fig. 2H and fig. S4A). In addition, the RDs of CodR superimposed well with previously reported crystal structures, including BenM in complex with its inducer molecule cis,cis-muconate (33), and Itaconic acid-inducible system regulator (ItcR)-itaconate complex (34) (fig. S4B). The trimethylammonium group of Cho inserts into a hydrophobic cavity formed by nonpolar residues P99, W129, T223, and L224, stabilizing its binding to CodR. Structural analysis further revealed that T101 forms two critical hydrogen bonds with the ethanolamine moiety of Cho, which likely accounts for the observed lower binding affinity of CodR for GB (Fig. 2H and fig. S4C). Functional validation through in vitro reporter assays demonstrated that mutations in key residues (T101, R198, and T223) resulted in a 2.33- to 4.35-fold reduction in CodR-dependent transcriptional activity (Fig. 2I). Notably, substitution of W129 with alanine nearly abolished transcriptional activation, underscoring its indispensable role in ligand recognition (***P < 0.001; Fig. 2I). Collectively, these results indicate that Cho serves as an inducer for CodR-mediated transcriptional regulation.

The loss of Cho-CodR signaling attenuates the expression of virulence-associated genes

To comprehensively elucidate the regulatory function of CodR in response to Cho, we conducted global proteomic profiling comparing P. aeruginosa ΔcodR and WT strains grown in M9-Cho medium. As shown in Fig. 3A, the codR deletion profoundly altered the bacterial proteome, with 71 proteins significantly down-regulated and 27 up-regulated [fold change > 2, adjusted P (Padj) < 0.05]. These differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were functionally annotated via Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses (Fig. 3B). Notably, 29% of the down-regulated proteins were virulence-associated factors, including T3SS components (ExsE, ExsC, ExoU, Pcr3, PopB, PscB, HcpA, and PscL) (35), quorum sensing (QS) regulators (RhlA and LasA) (36), and siderophore biosynthesis (PchD, PchE, PchF, PchG, and PchI) (37) (Fig. 3, A to C, and table S1). Moreover, proteins critical for biofilm formation (AlgU and PslH) and antibiotic efflux, such as MexG, MexH, MexI, and OpmD, were markedly reduced (Fig. 3, B and C, and table S1) (38, 39). Notably, PchP exhibited decreased expression in ΔcodR, reinforcing CodR’s role as an activator of the codR-pchP operon (Fig. 3, A and C).

Given the attenuated response of CodR to GB (Fig. 2, C and G), we extended our analysis to GB-supplemented M9 medium. While GB primarily affected antibiotic resistance pathways, most of the proteins with pronounced down-regulation are involved in the biosynthesis of phenazine, a crucial metabolite contributing to antibiotic tolerance and metabolic heterogeneity in P. aeruginosa biofilms (40). These included PhzC1, PhzH, PhzE1, PhzF1, and PhzM (Fig. 3D and table S2). In addition, proteins related to amino acid biosynthesis (AroQ1, CdrB, AcoB, and HutU) and carbon utilization [Aliphatic amidase (AmiE) and Cation and acetate symporter (YjcG)] were also repressed (table S2). In contrast, virulence-associated proteins (T3SS, QS, and siderophores) remained largely unaffected (Fig. 3D), highlighting the differential induction of Cho and GB via CodR. The proteomic profiling under nutrient-rich condition was also carried out. We observed that in comparison to parental strain, ΔcodR grown in LB medium exhibited reduced expression level of proteins implicated in antibiotic resistance and synthesis of secondary metabolites (Fig. 3, E and F, and table S3). These observations are in agreement with previous studies, which report that Cho might play accessory roles in shaping bacterial metabolic and pathogenesis due to its limited availability (18). These data collectively underscore the specific role of the Cho-CodR axis in virulence regulation.

CodR serves as a global transcriptional regulator that contributes the expression of virulence and antibiotic tolerance genes

Previous literature reported a series of DNA binding motifs of regulators from P. aeruginosa via a high-throughput systematic evolution assay, in which the recognition motif of CodR was predicted as two distinct 5-bp motifs separated by a 7-bp spacer (Fig. 4A) (41). A comprehensive whole-genome scanning against P. aeruginosa PAO1 genome was performed to search the potential target genes of CodR, resulting in four binding loci including amrZ, pslA, mexA, and codR (Fig. 4B and detailed promoter information in fig. S5). In addition, the direct binding of CodR to their promoters was further confirmed by EMSAs (Fig. 4, C to E). Consistent with the down-regulated expression of virulence genes observed in Fig. 3, AmrZ has been demonstrated to be a critical regulator in modulating the expression of genes related to virulence factors, alginate biosynthesis, biofilms formation, and type IV pili localization (42). Both plsA and plsH locate in psl locus, which is responsible for extracellular polysaccharide biosynthesis in P. aeruginosa, while mexA encodes a membrane bridge subunit of MexAB-OprM efflux pump and plays a central role in multidrug resistance (43).

To delineate the codR promoter region essential for CodR-mediated activation, we cloned the full-length promoter fragment (P1) and two truncated fragments (P2 and P3) into lacZ reporter vector and then transformed them into both WT and ΔcodR strains (Fig. 4F). In Cho-supplemented medium, P1 and P2 (both containing intact CodR-binding motif) exhibited robust transcriptional activation in WT strain (***P < 0.001; 5.79- and 5.21-fold, respectively), whereas no induction was observed in ΔcodR mutant (Fig. 4F). Notably, P3, lacking critical regulatory elements, failed to demonstrate CodR-dependent activation (Fig. 4F). In vivo reporter system and transcription analysis further confirmed CodR’s regulatory function, as target genes showed pronounced induction in the WT strain upon Cho stimulation (Fig. 4, G and H), while this response was abolished in ΔcodR mutant (Fig. 4, G and H). Moreover, genetic complementation with a codR-expressing plasmid (ΔcodR-comp) restored the transcriptional activation (Fig. 4H). These findings unequivocally establish CodR as a pivotal transcriptional activator governing virulence and antibiotic tolerance in P. aeruginosa.

CodR mediates Cho-driven intrinsic antibiotic resistance in P. aeruginosa

Previous studies have demonstrated that P. aeruginosa could exploit polyamines present in the CF lung microenvironment to enhance antimicrobial tolerance (44). Although Cho and other low–molecular weight osmoprotectants primarily function in counteracting hyperosmotic stress, emerging evidence suggests their potential involvement in bacterial adaptation to antibiotic-induced stress (18). Building on these findings and our experimental data (Figs. 1D, 3, and 4), we postulated that CodR might regulate the intrinsic antibiotic tolerance of P. aeruginosa, thereby promoting bacterial survival under diverse hostile conditions. To validate this hypothesis, we first used qRT-PCR to assess whether Cho or its metabolic intermediates modulate the expression of antibiotic resistance genes identified in Figs. 3 and 4. Notably, both Cho and PC exhibited dose-dependent (0.01 to 0.5 M) up-regulation of mexA, mexG, and norA expression in WT P. aeruginosa, whereas no obvious transcriptional changes were detected in ΔcodR mutant (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, we investigated the functional relevance of CodR-mediated gene regulation by comparing the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of four clinically relevant antibiotics between WT and ΔcodR strains. The ΔcodR mutant displayed increased susceptibility to ciprofloxacin and meropenem, while genetic complementation of codR restored its antibiotic resistance to WT levels (fig. S6, A and B).

To further investigate the role of CodR in bacterial adaptation to antibiotic stress, we conducted growth inhibition assays by exposing WT and ΔcodR strains to subinhibitory concentrations of ciprofloxacin or meropenem. Notably, supplementation with 0.1 mM Cho or PC efficiently rescued growth inhibition in WT strain rather than ΔcodR strain (Fig. 5, B to G). Consistently, antibiotic killing assays revealed that pretreatment with Cho or PC markedly enhanced the survival rate of the WT strain, whereas GB and glycine showed minimal protective effects (Fig. 5, H and I). The ΔcodR mutant displayed severely compromised survival even in the presence of Cho or PC, with survival rates significantly lower than those of the WT strain (**P < 0.01; Fig. 5, H and I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that host-derived Cho potentiates antibiotic resistance in P. aeruginosa by up-regulating CodR-dependent resistance gene expression, thereby highlighting the potential strategies to improve bacterial clearance via disruption of CodR signaling.

CodR integrates Cho catabolism with virulence to drive P. aeruginosa adaptation during lung infection

Emerging evidences highlight that opportunistic pathogens exhibit dynamic metabolic reprogramming to modulate biofilm architecture and colonization efficiency in response to host-derived nutrients, thereby underscoring metabolic plasticity as a critical virulence determinant in pulmonary niches (4, 8, 21). Given the established role of host-derived Cho as a pivotal nutrient for respiratory tract colonization by P. aeruginosa, we postulated that CodR functions as a central regulatory hub integrating Cho catabolism with biofilm formation and virulence factor expression during lung infection. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that PC or Cho exposure induced 2.01- to 3.26-fold up-regulation of amrZ, pslA, and algD mRNA levels in WT PAO1 (**P < 0.01; Fig. 6A), whereas this induction was markedly attenuated in ΔcodR mutant (Fig. 6A). Consistent with transcriptional data, the ΔcodR mutant displayed impaired biofilm formation after 24- to 48-hour incubation compared to the WT (**P < 0.01; Fig. 6B). Notably, supplementation with PC or Cho enhanced biofilm biomass of WT strain by 1.51- to 1.84-fold relative to glucose controls, indicating CodR-mediated metabolic sensing facilitates biofilm maturation (**P < 0.01; Fig. 6B). We further investigated the effects of other lung metabolites on P. aeruginosa biofilm dynamics. Consistent with previous reports, succinate exerted potent inhibitory effects on biofilm formation in both WT and mutant strains (**P < 0.01; fig. S7A), while itaconate stimulated a 2.91-fold increase in biofilm synthesis in WT strain (**P < 0.01; fig. S7A) (45). Critically, itaconate treatment effectively rescued the biofilm deficiency observed in the ΔcodR mutant, restoring synthesis levels comparable to the WT (fig. S7A). Meanwhile, growth inhibition assays also revealed that itaconate enhanced bacterial tolerance to ciprofloxacin and meropenem (**P < 0.01; fig. S7B). These findings demonstrate the dual regulatory roles of lung metabolites in shaping P. aeruginosa biofilm formation.

In addition, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that T3SS-associated genes, including pcrG, exoU, and exsA, were notably up-regulated in WT strain cultured with PC or Cho compared to glucose-based medium (Fig. 6C). This nutrient-responsive transcriptional activation was remarkably impaired in the ΔcodR mutant (Fig. 6C), highlighting the indispensable role of CodR in bridging Cho metabolism with virulence activation in P. aeruginosa. Notably, this transcriptional reprogramming translated to functional virulence phenotypes, as PC/Cho-treated WT strain exhibiting enhanced cytotoxicity toward human A549 lung epithelial cells (**P < 0.01; Fig. 6D), while ΔcodR strains showed no obvious cytotoxicity elevation regardless of nutrient conditions (Fig. 6D). Neither itaconate nor succinate supplementation could recapitulate this cytotoxic enhancement, emphasizing the distinct virulence modulation capacity of Cho (fig. S7C). Consistent with these findings, ΔcodR mutant exhibited attenuated invasiveness compared to WT strain in gentamicin protection assays using A549 cells (**P < 0.01; Fig. 6E), whereas this defect was restored in the complemented strain.

The impact of CodR on bacterial pathogenesis was further elucidate in a murine pneumonia infection model (Fig. 6F). Infection with WT P. aeruginosa caused ~42% mortality rate at 40 hours postinfection and 75% mortality rate at 72 hours postinfection. In contrast, deletion of codR delayed the death and resulted in nearly 52% survival rate within 72 hours (***P < 0.001; Fig. 6F). At 24 hours after infection, the bacterial burdens in lungs of mice infected with the parental and ΔcodR strains were log10 2.9 and log10 2.2, respectively (*P < 0.05; Fig. 6G). Notably, at 48 hours postinfection, the lung bacillary burden in ΔcodR-infected mice was decreased by 12.5-fold in comparison to WT (***P < 0.001; Fig. 6G). Histopathological evaluation further demonstrated that ΔcodR-infected lungs retained alveolar architecture integrity with diminished neutrophil infiltration compared to WT-challenged tissues (Fig. 6H and fig. S8). Quantitative analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) revealed a substantial attenuation of albumin extravasation in ΔcodR-infected mice compared to WT (*P < 0.05; Fig. 6I), paralleled by reduced proinflammatory cytokine levels that correlated with bacterial load (Fig. 6J). These results suggest that CodR plays a vital role in the activation of T3SS, which triggers inflammasome activation and alveolar destruction (35). ΔcodR mutants displayed impaired PC acquisition capacity, evidenced by the higher PC accumulation in BALF from WT-infected mice (*P < 0.05; Fig. 6K). No remarkable difference in phosphatidylinositol (PI) or phosphatidylserine (PS) levels was observed (Fig. 6K), confirming specific utilization of host-derived PC as a critical infection driver. Overall, these results indicate that the metabolic adaption of Cho in P. aeruginosa via CodR signaling contributes to bacterial colonization and pathogenesis during lung infection.

Conservation of Cho-CodR axis among P. aeruginosa clinical isolates

Chronic airway infections in patients with COPD and CF drive P. aeruginosa to undergo genetic modifications, which facilitate bacterial metabolic adaptation and long-term persistence (1, 3, 8, 10). One common change is the mutation inactivation of key regulators, such as lasR, rccR, and ampR (10, 46). To gain a deeper understanding the role of CodR in shaping adaptions of clinical isolates, the DIAMOND blast tool of Pseudomonas Genome Database was performed across P. aeruginosa clinical isolates for the searching of CodR homologs (90% similarity and 90% gene length coverage). After filtering out incomplete or unannotated sequences, we obtained 478 homologs from CF-derived isolates and 681 from non-CF isolates (table S4). Sequence alignment revealed 13 nonsynonymous substitutions across 15 codons (table S4). Notably, 7.3% CF isolates harbored frequent substitutions in the RD-II domain (e.g., D172E, G210V, and E211D), whereas 3.2% exhibited substitutions in the DBD domain (table S4). Similarly, 4.4 and 2.4% of non-CF isolates carried nonsynonymous substitutions in the RD-II domain and DBD domain, respectively (table S4). Phylogenetic analysis also revealed close evolutionary relationships between CF and non-CF groups (fig. S9A). To elucidate their potential effects, the AlphaFold2 was used to map the location of the variants on the structure of CodR (fig. S9B). Results revealed that the residues D172, G210, and E211 are peripheral to the core α/β Rossmann-like fold in RD-II domain, indicating that they are not directly involved in inducer interaction (fig. S9B). In vivo reporter assays also revealed that no obvious alteration was observed for the transcriptional activities of these mutants compared to WT CodR (fig. S9C). To assess functional conservation, we isolated 18 P. aeruginosa strains from chronic and acute infections (table S5). All strains retained intact, highly conserved codR genes (fig. S10A), and qRT-PCR demonstrated notable induction of codR expression upon Cho exposure (fig. S10B). Together, these findings underscore the evolutionary stability of the Cho-CodR regulatory axis.

DISCUSSION

P. aeruginosa, a versatile Gram-negative pathogen, exhibits remarkable adaptability in diverse environments by effectively using available nutrients (9, 47). Evidence suggests that this bacterium exploits host-derived PC as a dual carbon/nitrogen source to facilitate bacterial colonization and systemic dissemination during lung infections (12, 14). However, the intricate molecular mechanisms linking metabolic adaptation to pathogenesis and antibiotic tolerance remain poorly understood. In this study, we identified CodR as a pivotal self-regulating transcriptional activator that induces the expression of genes such as amrZ, plsA, and mexA in response to extracellular Cho (Fig. 7). Notably, the absence of CodR disrupts the Cho-mediated induction of antibiotic resistance genes, rendering P. aeruginosa more susceptible to a variety of antibiotics. Furthermore, host-derived PC and Cho stimulated the production of virulence-associated proteins, a process attenuated in the codR mutant, resulting in defective bacterial burden in a murine model. These findings collectively unveil a distinct Cho-mediated signaling pathway that is central to the virulence and metabolic adaptation of P. aeruginosa.

Proposed model of CodR mediated metabolic adaption and pathogenesis in P. aeruginosa during lung infection.

During respiratory infections, P. aeruginosa secretes lipase C and PlcH, which hydrolyze host PC, generating ChoP, glycerol, and long-chain fatty acids. Extracellular ChoP is subsequently internalized and metabolized to Cho, which activates the transcriptional regulator CodR. This, in turn, up-regulates the expression of virulence-associated genes, including pchP, norA, amrZ, pslA, and mexA. The coordinated activation of these genes enhances bacterial virulence and promotes metabolic adaptation within the host environment. The elements were provided by Servier Medical Art (https://smart.servier.com/), licensed under CC BY 4.0 (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/).

Opportunistic pathogens such as S. aureus use intricate metabolic adaptation mechanisms to thrive in the dynamic metabolic landscape of the respiratory mucosa, where exogenous proline and itaconate differentially modulate bacterial growth and biofilm formation (1, 8). Previous studies have shown that pulmonary surfactant, primarily composed of PC, can be cleaved into ChoP and long-chain fatty acids, serving as major nutrient sources for the initial colonization of P. aeruginosa (13). Pan et al. (15) screened the global gene expression profile of P. aeruginosa grown in bronchioalveolar lavage fluid of mice and identified Pseudomonas virulence regulator A (PvrA), a regulator that controls the utilization of extracellular long-chain fatty acids and production of virulence factors in response to the metabolic intermediate palmitoyl coenzyme A. Deletion of pvrA attenuated bacterial virulence in murine pneumonia models, underscoring the dual role of PC catabolites during infection (12, 15). Our study further extends these findings by revealing that PC-derived Cho exhibits potential virulence and tolerance induction in P. aeruginosa through interaction with CodR. This unusual GcvA-like regulator directly binds Cho to transcriptionally activate the genes involved in PC metabolism, antibiotic resistance, biofilm formation, and T3SS. These observations provide a plausible explanation about how CodR links bacterial metabolic reprogramming and virulence regulation in P. aeruginosa upon entering into host lung environments. We also systematically evaluated the roles of key immunometabolites, including succinate and itaconate, in modulating P. aeruginosa biofilm dynamics and virulence (fig. S7). Succinate demonstrated a remarkably inhibitory effect on biofilm formation, correlating with increased antibiotic susceptibility. Conversely, itaconate augmented biofilm biomass in WT strain and fully restored the biofilm-deficient phenotype in the ΔcodR mutant. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assays confirmed that neither metabolite affected bacterial cytotoxicity (fig. S7). These observations imply a complex host-pathogen interaction wherein host-derived metabolites play a vital role in shaping pathogen metabolic plasticity. Meanwhile, comparative transcriptomics of P. aeruginosa from CF lung isolates revealed iron acquisition gene up-regulation (48). Proteomics in this work also demonstrate that codR deletion down-regulated a substantial number of proteins involved in siderophore biosynthesis in the presence of Cho, suggesting that the mechanism linking Cho catabolism to bacterial virulence may be more sophisticated. Thus, a deep analysis of these data and exploration of mechanisms underlying PC metabolism would aid in understanding the pathogenesis of P. aeruginosa.

The regulatory landscape of Cho metabolism in P. aeruginosa extends beyond CodR, encompassing multiple transcriptional regulators with distinct functional outputs (17, 21, 24). Notably, GbdR has been demonstrated to activate genes such as plcH and pchP in response to both GB and Cho (14). Genome-wide mapping of GbdR-binding sites revealed 26 loci, including those encoding enzymes involved in GB/Cho catabolism (e.g., betX and cbcXWV), highlighting its central role in metabolic adaptation (17). While regulators such as SouR (governing the sox operon) and CdhR (regulating the carnitine catabolism operon) contribute to Cho metabolite utilization, functional divergence is evident (23, 24). Our findings indicate that CodR predominantly modulates virulence by regulating T3SS activity and biofilm formation (Fig. 3), despite its partial regulation of pchP. This suggests a specialized role for CodR in virulence coordination under host-derived Cho conditions, whereas GbdR and SouR primarily support metabolic colonization. MST assays also revealed that CodR exhibits a weaker binding affinity to GB, and qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated a modest up-regulation of codR upon GB exposure (Fig. 2, B and C). However, proteomic profiling showed no obvious induction of virulence-associated proteins in GB-enriched cultures (Fig. 3D), indicating that while GB acts as one of ligands for CodR, it fails to activate downstream virulence pathways. This selectivity may reflect evolutionary adaptation, as Cho predominantly derives from eukaryotic hosts, while GB is more environmentally ubiquitous. The heightened responsiveness to Cho may optimize codR-pchP operon activation and subsequent PC catabolism in P. aeruginosa, emphasizing the specialized role of PC-derived metabolites in regulating P. aeruginosa pathogenicity without triggering broad metabolism reprogramming. In addition, the dual role of Cho as both a metabolite and signaling molecule is not specific to P. aeruginosa. For instance, in Streptococcus pneumoniae, CiaR and LicR respectively activate/depress the lic locus for adhesion and colonization in response to Cho (49). These observations underscore the complexity of Cho-mediated signaling across bacterial pathogens, emphasizing its dual function in metabolic adaptation and virulence modulation.

The ability to convert Cho into GB enables microbes to withstand harsh environmental stresses (18, 50). For P. aeruginosa, supplementation with GB or precursors such as PC and Cho mitigates growth arrest under high-salinity conditions (18). While not an obligate human pathogen, P. aeruginosa preferentially infects CF individuals, where the osmotically stressful lung environment is rich in Cho (9). Transcriptomic studies of P. aeruginosa grown in CF sputum also highlighted the up-regulation of genes involved in Cho transport and catabolism, suggesting the interactions between nutrient availability and osmotic stress adaptation (21). Meanwhile, evidences have implicated that P. aeruginosa undergoes substantial variability under nutrient conditions during infections within patients with CF, such as lactate and glucose, resulting in potential changes in antibiotic sensitivity (51, 52). Our study further demonstrates that Cho increased antibiotic tolerance in WT strain, but this effect was lost in the codR mutant (Fig. 5). Proteomic analyses revealed that CodR up-regulated proteins involved in antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation in the presence of Cho (Fig. 3, A to C). These data suggest that Cho acquisition within host environments may enhance bacterial antibiotic resistance, a critical concern in clinical settings. Furthermore, genetic variations and transcriptional analysis revealed that Cho-CodR axis functions conservatively among P. aeruginosa clinical isolates, indicating its fundamental role in bacterial adaptation and pathogenicity (figs. S9 and S10). Given that chronic respiratory tract infections in patients with CF present critical challenges for antibiotic treatment due to biofilm formation and persistence (19, 52), these findings provide critical insights into developing effective therapeutic strategies. Further research into the molecular mechanisms underlying this axis may facilitate the development of targeted interventions to combat chronic respiratory infections more effectively.

In conclusion, our findings provide a comprehensive analysis of how P. aeruginosa integrates host-derived Cho signaling to coordinate bacterial virulence and antibiotic resistance. The identification of CodR as a master regulator of these processes offers profound insights into host-bacterial interactions and metabolic-based virulence regulation. Given the widespread presence of Cho uptake systems in bacteria, future studies should investigate whether other pathogens exploit Cho to modulate virulence during infection.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

All animal protocols and experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics and Safety Review Committee of Sichuan Normal University (permit number: 2025LS021) and performed in compliance with the National and Sichuan Normal University guidelines regarding the use of animals in research.

Bacterial strains, plasmid, and growth conditions

The detailed information about bacteria strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in table S6. The Escherichia coli DH5α, E. coli BL21 (DE3), and WT P. aeruginosa and mutant strains were grown in LB broth (Oxoid) at 37°C with shaking (220 rpm) or on LB agar plates. As for 1× M9 medium with sole carbon source, the basial medium was prepared as 50 mM ‌Na2HPO4, ‌25 mM KH2PO4, 25 mM NaCl, 20 mM ‌NH4Cl, 2 mM ‌MgSO4, and 5 mM ‌CaCl2 and supplied with 0.4% (w/v) glucose or 0.5% (w/v) PC, Cho, GB, and palmitic acid. As for P. aeruginosa clinical isolates, bacteria were growth on Mueller-Hinton broth to optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.5 at 37°C, and then the cells were collected and incubated with fresh M9 medium supplied with 0.5% (w/v) glucose or 0.5% (w/v) Cho for 1 hour at 37°C. The bacteria were collected and further applied for RNA extraction or cytotoxicity assays.

Bioinformation analysis

To explore the distribution of codR-norA like organization, the DIAMOND blast tool of Pseudomonas Genome Database (53) was performed across Pseudomonas species using protein sequences of CodR and NorA from P. aeruginosa PAO1 as templates (coverage > 50% and identify > 50%). As for phylogenetic analysis, the UniProt Blast (www.uniprot.org/) was used to search CodR homologs (coverage > 50% and identify > 30%), and the top 50 hits were selected. Multiple sequence alignment was done using Clustal Omega algorithm (www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo) online server and further applied to PhyML 3.1/3.0 aLRT (www.phylogeny.fr/) for the building of phylogenetic trees via the maximum likelihood method. The obtained data were refined graphically using iTOL v6 (https://itol.embl.de/).

The DIAMOND blast tool of Pseudomonas Genome Database was performed across P. aeruginosa clinical isolates for the searching of CodR homologs (90% similarity and 90% gene length coverage). After filtering out incomplete or unannotated sequences as previously reported (54), 478 homologs from CF-derived isolates and 681 from non-CF isolates were obtained. To investigate the evolutionary relationships between protein sequences derived from CF and non-CF isolates, a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis was conducted using R package. Identical protein sequences were consolidated into same operational taxonomic units, each annotated with the number of aggregated samples (n) to generate a nonredundant dataset. The Clustal Omega algorithm was used to perform progressive alignment of the processed sequences, with default gap opening and extension penalties. A pairwise distance matrix was computed under the maximum likelihood framework using the WAG amino acid substitution model. A neighbor-joining tree was constructed from the distance matrix to provide an initial topology. Model parameters, including branch lengths and substitution rates, were refined via the optim.pml in the R package, using the WAG model with γ-distributed rate heterogeneity. Topological robustness was assessed through 1000 bootstrap replications, with consensus support values calculated from the resultant majority-rule tree.

As for mutation frequency calculation, all protein sequences from both CF and non-CF groups were aligned against the reference sequence from PAO1 using Clustal Omega implemented in R package. The alignment process ensured accurate positional correspondence across all sequences for subsequent comparative analysis. Positions containing missing data (−) or double-gap alignments were excluded from analysis. The mutation frequency per site was calculated using the following formula: Mutation frequency = (Number of mutated sequences / Total sequences in group). Statistical significance of observed mutation frequencies was determined using binomial tests (*P < 0.05)

Construction of P. aeruginosa ΔcodR, ΔnorA, and ΔpchP mutants

Briefly, the homologous recombination method was used to construct P. aeruginosa mutants, as previously reported (55). The upstream and downstream fragments (600 to 800 bp) of target genes from the P. aeruginosa PAO1 were subcloned into the vector pEX18Gm, respectively. Then, the recombinant pEX18Gm vectors were introduced into chemically competent E. coli S17-1λ pir cells, which served as a donor strain toward the WT P. aeruginosa PAO1 strain. The deletion of target gene was verified by PCR with the cognate primer pairs and further confirmed by sequencing. All the primers used in this work were listed in table S7. As for complementary strain, the pRK415-codR vector was also introduced into P. aeruginosa via E. coli S17-1λ pir cells and verified by PCR.

Expression and purification of CodR

E. coli BL21 carrying the pET22b-codR plasmid was grown in LB broth at 37°C until reaching an OD600 of 0.8, and then 0.4 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside was added and incubated with shaking (220 rpm) at 18°C for 14 hours. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (4000 rpm for 10 min) and resuspended in buffer containing 25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.8), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and 5% glycerol. The cells were lysed by sonication and further centrifuged at 12,500g for 25 min at 4°C. The supernatant was incubated with Ni–nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) resin at 4°C for 2 hours and eluted with gradient imidazole buffer (25 to 300 mM). The obtained CodR protein was further purified using an AKTA purifier (GE Healthcare) and concentrated to a final concentration of 7 mg/ml. Similar method was also applied for purification of mutant proteins.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

The promoter fragments (100 to 150 bp) of the targeted genes were cloned from the genome of P. aeruginosa PAO1 and purified using DNA purification Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing). DNA binding reaction was performed in 10-μl system containing 25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.8), 150 mM NaCl, 1 μM DNA fragments, and 0 to 16 μM CodR (37). The mixtures were incubated at 4°C for 1 hour, then mixed with 2 μl of loading buffer, and loaded onto an 8% polyacrylamide gel in 0.5× tris-borate EDTA buffer. After electrophoresis at 4°C under 130 V for 1 hour, the bands were visualized by staining with ethidium bromide and observed using a Bio-Rad imager.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

P. aeruginosa PAO1 and mutant strains were cultured at 37°C (220 rpm) in LB broth or M9 medium, and cells were harvested until reaching an OD600 of 0.8. The total mRNA was isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA), as previously reported (55). The PrimeScript RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, Beijing) was used to synthesize the cDNA, which served as templates for qPCR assays via 2× ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing). The oprL gene was used as a normalizer.

All P. aeruginosa clinical isolates were growth on Mueller-Hinton broth to OD600 of 0.5 at 37°C, and then the bacteria were collected and incubated with fresh M9 medium supplied with 0.5% (w/v) glucose or 0.5% (w/v) Cho for 1 hour at 37°C. The cells were collected for RNA extraction. Aliquots of RNA were reverse transcribed to make cDNA, as described above. Primer amplification efficiencies were determined using serial dilutions of cDNA templates from the reference strain PAO1 and the clinical isolates. The expression of codR in clinical isolates were calculated relative to the geometric mean of oprL.

Drug resistance assay

Overnight cultures of P. aeruginosa PAO1 and mutants were diluted into fresh M9 medium at a ratio of 1:100. Ninety-microliter aliquots of bacterial cultures were transferred to a 96-well plate supplied with 10 μl of antibiotic solutions or twofold-diluted antibiotic solutions and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Then, 5-μl bacterial cultures of each well were plated on LB agar plate to determine the values of MICs. The initial concentrations of meropenem, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, and polymyxin B solutions used in the study were 2.5, 0.4, 0.3, and 0.5 mg/ml, respectively. As for antibiotic tolerance assay (56), the WT and ΔcodR strains were pretreated with 0.1 mM PC, Cho, GB, or glycine in M9 minimal medium at 37°C for 4 hours and then incubated with meropenem (40 μg/ml) or ciprofloxacin (10 μg/ml) for 1 hour. The surviving cells were serially diluted and plated on LB agar plate to determine the viable colony-forming units (CFUs). The bacterial survival rate (%) = (number of surviving colonies under antibiotic treatment / number of surviving colonies without antibiotic treatment) × 100%.

MST for CodR binding assay

The purified CodR solution was diluted into 100 nM by PBST buffer [phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.05% (w/v) Tween 20] and labeled with the Monolith His-Tag Labeling Kit RED-tris-NTA 2nd Generation Kit (NanoTemper, Germany) (55). Meanwhile, Cho chloride or GB resolution (Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted with PBST buffer gradient from 0.2 M. Subsequently, the CodR and Cho dilutions were mixed at a volume ratio of 1:1, incubated at 4°C for 30 min, and further detected on a Monolith NT.115 instrument. The collected data were processed by NanoTemper Analysis 1.2.20 software.

Molecular docking

The predicted protein structure of CodR was obtained from AlphaFold2 (accession number: AF-Q9HTR1-F1). In addition, the Ideal coordinates of Cho and GB were obtained from Protein Data Bank (www.rcsb.org/). The AlphaFold3 algorithm (https://alphafoldserver.com/) used in this work was performing in the AlphaFold Server environment (32). CB-Dock2 server (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/) (57), AutoDock vina, and Chai Discovery (www.chaidiscovery.com/) were used to search the potential substrate binding pocket of CodR and dock Cho into CodR. Throughout all modeling and docking procedures, CodR was held rigid, while the conformation of each substrate molecule was optimized. The obtained structures of CodR-Cho complexes were applied for analyzing interaction between CodR and Cho via Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/) and visualized using PyMOL.

Total protein extraction and mass spectrometry analysis

P. aeruginosa WT and ΔcodR strains were cultured at 37°C (220 rpm) in M9-GB, M9-Cho, and LB media, and cells were harvested until reaching an OD600 of 0.8. The total protein was collected using the Bacterial Total Protein Extraction Kit (MeiLianKe, Jiangsu), and further reduced, and alkylated as previously reported (37, 55). After digested with sequence grade trypsin (Promega) at 37°C for 16 hours, about 3 μg of peptides of each sample were applied for reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific) under basic pH with a mobile phase [from 2% acetonitrile to 98% acetonitrile (pH 10.0)]. The collected peptides desalted and dried in the speed vacuum (Christ), then resuspended by buffer containing 0.1% formic acid, further applied on a homemade capillary column (75-μm inside diameter × 25 cm; ReproSil-Pur C18-AQ; 1.9 μm), and tested via LC–tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) EASY-nLC 1200 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The obtained raw data of each sample were processed via Spectronaut against protein sequences of P. aeruginosa PAO1 obtained from UniProt Proteome database. In addition, these data were further processed by the Perseus software. As for statistical analysis, the Student’s t test was used to test the DEPs between untreated bacteria and bacteria exposed to As. Proteins within a statistical region of P < 0.05 and an expression fold change of ≥1.5 or ≤0.67 were considered as DEPs.

β-Galactosidase activity assay

The promoter regions (150 to 300 bp) of codR, mexA, pslA, and amrZ cloned from the P. aeruginosa PAO1 were cloned upstream of a promoter-less lacZ gene within the pRG970km vector, respectively (55). Then, the recombinant pRG970km vectors were introduced into chemically competent E. coli S17-1λ pir cells, which served as a donor strain toward the WT P. aeruginosa PAO1 or ΔcodR strain. The bacterial cultures were grown in LB broth or M9 medium at 37°C until reaching an OD600 of 0.6. Subsequently, 4 ml of culture was harvested and resuspended in 1 ml of buffer Z containing 0.06 M Na2HPO4, 0.04 M NaH2PO4, 0.01 M MgCl2, and 0.05 M β-mercaptoethanol. The cells were lysed with 50 μl of 0.01% SDS buffer. Two hundred microliters of supernatants were incubated with 40 μl of O-nitrophenyl-β-d-galactopyranoside buffer (4 mg/ml) at 25°C for 15 min, and the absorptions at 420 and 550 nm (A420 and A550) were detected. Final β-galactosidase activity Miller units (U) = 1000 × [(A420–1.75 × A550)]/(T × V × OD600), where T is the time of the reaction (in minutes) and V is the volume of culture used in the assay (in milliliters).

Cytotoxicity assays

The bacteria were grown in M9 medium containing PC, Cho, GB, or DMG until reaching an OD600 of 0.6. The cells were collected and washed with PBS and applied for cell infection. As previously reported (55), the murine Raw264.7 cells obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) were infected with different P. aeruginosa strains at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 in antibiotic-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) at 37°C. After incubation, the supernatant of each well was collected through centrifugation and applied for LDH assay using LDH cytotoxicity assay kit (55). The baseline level of LDH release (0% LDH release) was determined using DMEM. Cells treated with LDH release agent C0017-1 were used as a positive control for total release (100% LDH release). The percentage of cytotoxicity was calculated according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

Bacterial invasion assays

Briefly, the human alveolar type II epithelial cells (A549 cell, obtained from ATCC) were seeded at 2 × 105 density per well on a 24-well plate and infected with different P. aeruginosa strains at a MOI of 10 for 2 hours (55). Then, the cells were washed with sterile PBS and lysed for serial dilution and plating on LB agar plates.

Murine lung infection

Eight-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (Dossy, Chengdu) were used in this work. P. aeruginosa PAO1 and ΔcodR strains were cultured at 37°C (220 rpm) in LB medium, and cells were harvested until reaching an OD600 of 0.6 (37). The bacterial pellets were washed with sterile PBS and resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 5 × 108 CFU/ml. Forty microliters of solution was infused into the respiratory tract of C57BL/6 mice via nasal drip. At 48 hours postinfection, mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation, and the lungs were applied for histological examinations. In addition, equal weight lung tissues were homogenized in sterile PBS, diluted, and further counted on the LB agar plate.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

A cannula containing 1 ml of saline was inserted into each mouse’s trachea (including PBS-exposed or 24-hour-infected mice) and infused in and out three times to collect BALF. The supernatant of BALF was separated through centrifugation at 4°C for 10 min (1400 rpm). The anti-mouse albumin (#D721206, Sangon Biotech), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) (#D721017, Sangon Biotech), IL-6 (#D721022, Sangon Biotech), tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α) (BMS607-3, Invitrogen) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were used to assess the concentrations of albumin or different cytokines in BALF (58).

Determination of PC content in BALF

The obtained supernatants of BALF were mixed with equal volume of hexane/isopropanol solution (1:1, v/v) at 35°C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min. The upper solutions were evaporated using a rotary evaporator at 35°C and then dried with nitrogen gas. The extracts were dissolved with hexane/isopropanol solution (1:1, v/v) and applied with HPLC (59). The mobile phase was ethanol/acetonitrile/H2O (50:40:10, v/v/v) at a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min, and the detection wavelength was set as 205 nm. The PC, PI, or PS standard was used to make standard curves.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experimental data in this study were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 and are presented as means ± SEM. A minimum of three independent biological replicates were included for each experiment to ensure reproducibility. Statistical analyses were performed as follows: For pairwise comparisons in β-galactosidase activity assays and qRT-PCR experiments, a two-tailed Student’s t test was used. For multigroup comparisons in biofilm biomass quantification and cytotoxicity assays, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc correction was used. Survival data from mouse infection models were analyzed using the Mantel-Cox log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 for all analyses. Detailed methodological parameters (e.g., sample sizes, technical replicates, and variance homogeneity assessments) are provided in the corresponding figure legends and Materials and Methods to support transparency and experimental reproducibility.
