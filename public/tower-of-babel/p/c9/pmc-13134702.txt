How to efficiently establish animal models of cholangiocarcinoma: challenges and inspiration
Authors: Ruiqiang Gou, Ping Yue, Peng Liu, Jinyu Zhao, Chunfei Huang, Kiyohito Tanaka, Peng F Wong, Rungsun Rerknimitr, Jong H Moon, Tan T Cheung, Christian Waydhas, Azumi Suzuki, Yanyan Lin, Emmanuel Melloul, Hans Schlitt, John Fung, Joseph W Leung, Wenbo Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCholangiocarcinoma (CCA) consists of a class of malignant tumors of the biliary system, with a rising incidence and carries a poor prognosis. Developing reliable animal models of CCA is crucial to elucidate the pathogenesis, identify novel therapeutic targets, and assess treatment efficacy. Among these models, the in situ primary cholangiocarcinoma model which closely mimics clinical reality, serves as a robust tool for evaluating drug efficacy, simulating tumor progression characteristics, and facilitates immunotherapy strategies. Although in situ primary CCA models have been utilized in preclinical research, they have inherent limitations that warrant attention and further refinement. This review article highlights the status of the most relevant experimental animal models in preclinical CCA studies. It further delineates the differences in the development of intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts that influence CCA model construction. In addition, we review the advancements, strengths and weaknesses of CCA organoids, primary intrahepatic and extrahepatic CCA models, and examine the pivotal role of bile duct inflammation and biliary fibrosis in creating the invasive cancer models. We emphasize the innovative application and immense potential of single-cell sequencing technology in studying cancer models. Additionally, this article summarizes the progress of CCA-targeted therapies and associated clinical trials which offered relevant insights. With a comprehensive review of in situ primary CCA models, we aim to provide suggestions and recommendation for future development of more advanced and clinically relevant research in constructing CCA models.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) represents a class of malignant and aggressive tumor that originate from the bile duct epithelial cells [1]. CCA is often diagnosed late in an advanced stage [2], with a poor prognosis due to lack of effective early diagnosis and curative treatment for advanced cases [3]. Cholangiocarcinoma is primarily categorized into extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) [4], depending on the site of cancer location [5], 6]. eCCA arises in the bile ducts outside of the liver and is further subdivided into perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (pCCA) and distal cholangiocarcinoma (dCCA), in relation to the junction of the cystic duct and common bile duct [7], 8]. In contrast, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) originates within the liver, arising from the epithelial lining of the intrahepatic bile ducts and their branches, extending up to the interlobular bile ducts [9].

CCA are relatively uncommon malignant tumors [10], 11], but their overall incidence and associated mortality has increased worldwide in recent years [4], 12], 13], especially in Asian countries, where consumption of raw fish is a common practice including the northeastern part of Thailand [14], 15] and Cambodia [16], which can lead to infestation and multiplication of larvae of Opisthorchis viverrine [17], [18], [19] and propagation of the parasites within the bile ducts [20]. Clonorchis infestation is believed to be an important cause of the high incidence of CCA in these countries [21], 22], however in Western countries, sporadic CCA occurs more frequently. Reports have indicated a rising incidence and associated mortality of iCCA [23] whereas that of eCCA and gallbladder cancer (GBC) have remained relatively stable or even showed a slight decline [24], 25]. In recent years, the incidence of CCA has been on the rise, but its overall prognosis remains poor [26], 27]. Currently, radical resection is the only hope for cure for CCA [4], 28], 29]. However, it requires complex radical resection of the tumor and surrounding organs and tissues [30], [31], [32]. Therefore, accurate preoperative assessment of resectability is necessary before the operation [33], 34]. For unresectable CCA with recurrence or metastasis, systemic treatment should be considered [35], [36], [37]. The traditional non-operative treatment regimen included chemotherapy with combined gemcitabine and cisplatin. However, the median overall survival is less than one year in published reports [38], [39], [40]. Although recent studies have demonstrated promising results with the advent of immunotherapy, as demonstrated in the TOPAZ-1 and KEYNOTE-966 trials, combined with chemotherapy in the treatment of CCA [41], [42], [43]. In addition, it is important to develop effective targeted therapy for CCA. It is worth noting that in iCCA, IDH1/2 mutations (18 %) and FGFR2 alterations (9–20 %) are more frequently observed in the cases, while in pCCA, patients with HER2 mutations or amplifications (15 %) are more common, which provides opportunities for targeted therapy of CCA [44]. There is an urgent need for animal models for CCA to simulate tumor development and for assess the response to multimodality therapies, including immunotherapy. The results will benefit the clinical management of patients with CCA in real-world clinical setting.

Before exploring animal models for CCA, it is necessary to review established animal models of cholangitis. Currently, animal models for cholangitis can be created with three different approaches: surgical ligation, drug-induced modeling, and genetic modification [45], 46]. Commonly used drugs in this context include alpha-naphthylisothiocyanate (ANIT), lithocholic acid (LCA) and 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC). Specific knockout of the MDR2 gene represents a widely adopted gene-editing technique that has been extensively utilized in scientific research. Mechanistically, the key pathways involved include the NF-κB pathway, the Smad4 pathway, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) activation, all of which contribute to the generation of oxygen free radicals and inflammatory damage to bile duct epithelial cells [46], 47].

At present, animal CCA models commonly used in scientific research include carcinogen-induced models, implanted tumor models, surgical models and genetic models [48], 49]. Thus far, majority of the CCA models are created by exposing animals to diethylnitrosamine or other carcinogens [49], [50], [51], where its lack of specificity frequently, results in simultaneous development of liver cancer [52]. Alternatively, human tumor xenograft models can be established by transplanting human cancer tissues into immunoincompetent mice [53], 54], This approach is convenient for observation and the histological stability is maintained, however, it does not fully mimic the microenvironment of tumor growth [55], thereby limiting our ability to understand the molecular underpinnings of the disease. Surgical models are rarely used for CCA modeling in research due to the relative difficulty and complexity of the operating on animals and the long interval from injury to development of CCA [56]. Genetic models, which are constructed by editing selected genes of model animals, have been increasingly favored because it allows one to create a primary and in situ model with clear genetic background [57], [58], [59], [60], making it easier to investigate the molecular pathways that contribute to the development of cholangiocarcinoma [61]. However, it is limited by its high technical complexity and time consuming [62], 63]. In comparison, there are more studies using on animal models for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma than extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [64], 65]. The mechanism of biliary duct morphogenesis and tumor occurrence

The embryonic development of bile ducts

Bile ducts are anatomically located both inside and outside the liver, forming a network called the biliary tree, the left and right hepatic ducts, as well as other smaller bile ducts, which are anatomically located inside the liver, constitute the primary components of the intrahepatic bile duct [66], 67], while the extrahepatic bile duct is located outside the liver., comprised of the common hepatic duct, the gallbladder, cystic duct, and the common bile duct [68], 69]. The terminal opening at the papilla is located in the duodenum. Both types of ducts originated from the hepatic diverticulum [70], but they exhibited different characteristics during subsequent development [71] (Figure 2).

Development of intrahepatic bile ducts

The intrahepatic bile duct forms from the bigger cranial branch of the liver diverticulum [72], 73] during the human embryonic development. The main hepatic cord grows rapidly during development and develops into many branches that fuse with each other to form a reticular hepatic cord. The hepatic cord at the distal end differentiates into the hepatic plate and septum, and the hepatic duct and interlobular bile duct develops at the proximal segment [74], [75], [76]. At two months, bile canaliculi formed between hepatocytes [77], and by three months, bile begins to be secreted [78]. During this process, the development of intrahepatic bile duct network can be divided into four stages: 1) Bile duct plate stage: the process of bile duct epithelial cells forming bile duct plate; 2) Double-layer ductal plate: appearance of double-layer cells; 3) Migratory phase: the process of reabsorption and incorporation into the mesenchyma of the nascent portal space; 4) Bile duct stage: remodeling of the indivualized bile ducts and maturation of the bile duct network [79], 80]. Multiple genes have important regulatory roles in this process. For example, Hunter et al. [81] knocked out the HHEX gene in mice, resulting in disordered development of embryonic biliary ductal plate and thus altering the normal development process of intrahepatic bile duct in the late embryonic period. It is therefore important to emphasize that reviewing bile duct embryology is beneficial for studying CCA, for instance, Sox9 is a key factor in cholangiogenesis [82]. Induction of YAP activation and specific knockdown of Sox9 in a mouse model prevented bile duct epithelialization into iCCA in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [83], 84].

Development of extrahepatic bile ducts

In embryonic development, the formation of extrahepatic bile ducts occurs earlier than the development of intrahepatic ducts [85]. The caudal branch of the hepatic diverticulum develops into the gallbladder and cystic duct, and the root develops into the common bile duct. In the early stage of development, the gallbladder and cystic duct are tubular structures with solid inner walls, and there is no cavity in the gallbladder [86]. It is not until the eighth week of embryonic development that the lumen begins to appear. The cystic duct merges with the hepatic duct to create the common bile duct, which opens into the duodenum’s abdominal wall, while the pancreatic duct connects to it [87]. Because the right lateral wall of the duodenum develops faster than the left side, with the development and rotation of the duodenum itself, the opening of the common bile duct gradually shifts to the dorsally medial side of the duodenum [88], [89], [90]. Hes1 protein can be detected at the initiation of extrahepatic bile duct development [91]. It is widely considered to be an important factor controlling extrahepatic bile duct development, and its loss will lead to dysplasia of gallbladder and extrahepatic bile duct [92], 93]. In addition, studies in mice have shown that the development of extrahepatic bile duct and intrahepatic bile duct is an independent process [94], and the “secondary joining” occurs at the hepatic duct/hilum during their later development [94], 95], which also explains the expression of some transcription factors in extrahepatic bile duct, such as Pdx1 and HNF-6, that are not expressed in intrahepatic bile duct [96], [97], [98]. Additionally, this highlights how different the two types of bile ducts are.

The pathophysiology of CCA and its risk factors

Abnormalities of genes and epigenetics

Until recently, little was known about the pathogenesis of CCA and the related biomarkers for disease treatment and diagnosis [99], 100]. Over the past few years, studies on gene mutations and epigenetic modifications related to iCCA and eCCA have gradually clarified this issue [10], 11], [101], [102], [103]. Current research widely supports that genetic variations of CCA are influenced by a variety of pathogenic factors. For instance, Chan-on et al. [104] revealed that in CCA, not related to hepatic fluke infection, mutations in BAP1 and IDH1 genes were more frequent compared to those in CCA related to hepatic fluke infection. Variations in the genetic mechanisms of CCA are also noted by different anatomical sites and uniqueness of pathological subtypes [105]. In generally, mutations in TP53, KRAS, and SMAD4 genes occur more frequently in lager duct type iCCA, while for the small bile duct type, mutations in BAP1, IDH1/2, and FGFR2 fusion [106] have been reported. Notably, FGFR2 fusion is almost only observed in iCCA and rarely detected in eCCA [107]. Key post-translational modifications also appear to play a part during tumorigenesis. A recent study on the role of histone lactase suggested that the lactase of NCL proteins enhances tumor cell proliferation and invasion by affecting the glycolytic process [108]. CCA demonstrating high methylation of the CpG island of the promoter has different patterns in schistosome-related and non-schistosome-related CCA [109]. It should be noted that dysregulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling is most associated with these epigenetic alterations [110], 111]. In addition, the aforementioned microenvironmental signals converge on the epigenetic regulation of key transcription factors by altering chromatin accessibility. The necroptotic microenvironment upregulates Prdm5 while suppressing Tbx3 expression. Functionally, Prdm5 acts as a transcriptional hub that directly activates iCCA-associated gene programs, thereby locking in the cholangiocarcinoma cell fate [112].

Chronic inflammation and cholestasis

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a well-recognized risk factor for bile duct cancer [113], 114]. In PSC patients, the probability of CCA is markedly increased [115], the reported risk of CCA being 160–400 times greater than the general population. This long-term cholestatic condition can cause inflammation, fibrosis, and scarring of the bile duct tree. Beyond that, prolonged cholangitis, cholelithiasis, and chronic inflammation gradually release ROS, inflammatory factors, and accumulated bile acids, which can induce DNA damage and malignant cell transformation [116], 117]. This process evolves over multiple stages: normal bile duct epithelium gradually progresses in a stepwise manner to low-grade dysplasia, then to high-grade dysplasia, ultimately leading to invasive cancer. [118]. However, it has not been possible to reliably predict the progression of subsequent bile duct lesions.

Viral or parasitic infections and chemical damage to the liver

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) and Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection and liver cirrhosis, are known risk factors for the development of bile duct tumors, particularly for hepatocellular carcinoma and iCCA [119]. In contrast, patients with hepatitis B associated CCA are more likely to be younger male patients with a higher incidence of liver cirrhosis. Also, diabetes, obesity, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease can promote the occurrence of CCA through insulin resistance and chronic inflammation [120]. Studies of glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonists, such as liraglutide, have shown that inhibiting Akt and STAT3 signaling can slow the progression of iCCA [121].

Thorotrast, a contrast agent, has been widely recognized for its carcinogenic effect since the 1980s [122], 123]. Early studies have pointed out that in patients with CCA, there is deposition of thorotrast in the thoracic cavity as evidenced by imaging and pathological examinations [124]. A recent study reported that 1,2-dichloropropane (1,2-DCP), a substance that might be used in the printing industry, increases the risk of bile duct cancer for workers in this field. The patients’ bile ducts showed extensive chronic damage, precancerous lesions, and numerous mutations in cancer-related genes [125].

Clonorchiasis sinensis and Opisthorchiasis viverrini infections in the Southeast Asia are important risk factors for CCA, leading to a high incidence of the disease in Thailand, Vietnam, and Cambodia [126], 127]. The parasitic infestation causes hyperplasia and fibrosis of the bile duct epithelium, eventually evolving into invasive cancer [128]. In an animal model of Clonorchis sinensis infection [129], fatty acid synthase inhibitors in conjunction with immunotherapy have shown encouraging potential in the treatment of CCA by changing the immunosuppressive environment and increasing drug resistance.

Abnormalities in the microenvironment and signaling pathways

Specific forms of cell death within the liver microenvironment actively instruct tumor lineage fate. Molecular signals released during necroptosis activate immune cells and, through the TLR2/4 pathway, trigger a characteristic cytokine cascade. This unique cytokine milieu directly instructs malignantly transformed hepatocytes to override their intrinsic oncogenic drivers and instead initiate a cholangiocytic differentiation program, thereby promoting iCCA development. In contrast, an apoptosis-dominant microenvironment favors HCC [112]. Most of the research on the dysregulation of Notch and Wnt/β-catenin pathways has been done on CCA [130], [131], [132] which affect the malignant transformation of bile duct epithelial cells and de-differentiation. New research has found that the Notch pathway mediates the hepatocyte-driven iCCA process (hepatic cells – bile duct epithelial cells/iCCA) [133]. Historically, tumor angiogenesis is promoted by the high expression of VEGF in CCA, and tumor escape from the immunosuppressive milieu aids in the avoidance of immunological surveillance [134].

Cholangiocarcinoma organoid models

Apart from animal models, ex vivo miniature organ models that can be grown in the laboratory by three-dimensional culture technology using stem cells or cells from specific tissues are increasing favored by researchers [135], [136], [137], this technique was first described in intestinal epithelial organoids in 2009 [138]. Its value focuses on the ability to form collections of cells with specific organ-specific characteristics, to some extent mimicking the structure and function of real organs [139], [140], [141]. With this technique, patient derived organoids(PDOs)are cultured from tumor cells to produce organoids that carry the characteristics of tumors [142], [143], [144]. This may aid in the genetic and phenotypic characterization of tumor responsiveness to therapeutic agents. [145], making it especially significant in cancer research: allowing for better understanding of the tumor’s physiology and facilitates screening of therapeutic drugs [146], 147]. As a disease model, tumor organoids can simulate the heterogeneity and microenvironment of tumors [148]. Better understand of the mechanisms of tumor growth, metastasis and drug response in an organoid model can facilitate transition to treatment trials.

Despite progress in other solid tumor organoid models, research on CCA organoids remains preliminary [149]. At present, CCA organoids are mainly created through two methods: 1) extracting tumor tissues from CCA patients or CCA animal models for three-dimensional culture to construct organoids [150], 151]; and 2) transforming normal tissue organoids into tumor organoids using CRISPR/Cas9 technology based on normal human or mouse liver/bile duct organoids [152], [153], [154]. Nakagawa et al. [155], 162] developed a mouse model of eCCA (that is driven by KTC-K19CreERT) to obtain tumor tissue for culture to construct eCCA organoids. The results showed the organoids exhibited a high rate of tumor formation, in 67 % (8 out of 12) of immunodeficient mice, while no tumors were observed in the control group. This organoid also exhibited a similar response in another animal model, namely the loss of E-Cadherin expression, and may play a role in the study of E-Cadherin in the development of CCA.

Tissue obtained from clinical patients is the main source of most organoids when experimental animals are not available [163] Laura et al. [156] constructed eight organoids from patients with liver cancer, three of which were serendipitously CCA organoids, which retained their phenotypic and histopathological properties and could be used for drug screening assays. Saito et al. [157] used the constructed CCA organoid model to analyze the expression of telomere genes and screen drugs, among which nutlin-3a, an inhibitor of MDM2, showed good therapeutic efficacy with potential application. The study of primary cancer through organoids is useful for retrospective analysis. Lee et al. [158] conducted gene enrichment analysis using a CCA organoid model and established a prospective prediction model for the histological subtype of primary CCA. Kinoshita et al. [159] and Guo et al. [160] also focused on the role of organoids in precision medicine and drug screening. Guo et al. studied gemcitabine and cisplatin resistance and found that under organoid conditions, macrophages (TAMs) in the tumor microenvironment promote the development of resistance to both drugs. Gao et al. [161] constructed a hybrid hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma (cHCC-CCA) organoid model, this type of cHCC-CCA can be observed in clinical practice. This organoid model shared some characteristics of HCC and CCA, such as the rare high expression of AFP and HepPar1, which are uncommon in CCA [164], 165].

Organoid models that is closely mimic the in vivo environment have helped to advance our understanding of CCA mechanisms and new drug therapies [166]. However, these experimental models are far from perfect. For instance, organoids have limited retention of heterogeneity and are notoriously difficult to culture and/or maintain, and this is further hampered by the lack of established protocols to standardize culture procedures and analysis methods [167], [168], [169]. So how do we develop experimental models that fully recapitulate the genetic and phenotypic features of human tumors? We envisage that the establishment of orthotopic primary tumor animal models, especially using mice with well-defined genetic background, may hold the key to achieve this holy grail.

Primary iCCA animal model

The creation of animal models for CCA is no different to other tumor models i.e. employing drug/toxicant induction, surgery, orthotopic/ectopic tumor transplantation, and genetic engineering mice [170], 171]. The advantage of the orthotopic primary model is that the tumor originates directly from the orthotopic tissue [172], [173], [174], and the animal has an intact immune function, which can be used to study the genetic changes, inflammatory response and immune microenvironment of the tumor [175], [176], [177].

Carcinogen induction model

The key to the use of carcinogens to induce tumors in animals is the choice of the agent. The most common carcinogen is thioacetamide (TAA) [191], 192], which can induce CCA in animals within a short period of time. For example, Al-Bader et al. [180] used different TAA drug concentrations to induce tumorigenesis and found that high drug concentrations led to severe liver cirrhosis and early death of experimental animals. Yeh et al. [181] showed that the expression of c-met and c-erbB-2 was up-regulated in CCA. In a TAA-induced model, the process of cystic dilatation of the bile duct, dysplasia of the bile duct epithelium, fibrosis and progression to invasive carcinoma could be observed histopathologically. In humans, this multistep carcinogenesis is similar to the development of CCA in this animal model. Maronpot et al. [182] used furan to create an animal model with a high tumor formation rate, reaching 86–100 % at experimental concentrations. The animals showed biliary fibrosis and cholangitis in the early stage, which then progressed to invasive carcinoma. Yang et al. [56] used a combination of carcinogen and bile duct ligation to cause bile duct damage in a mouse model to induce tumor formation. Thamavit et al. [183] found that Clonorchis sinensis infection also increased the effect of carcinogen induction.

New carcinogens have been used to induce CCA in animal models in recent years. Shirakami et al. [184] injected azoxymethane (AOM) into mice with fatty liver and obesity. AOM is associated with colorectal cancer but was not previously considered a conventional CCA carcinogen [193], 194]. iCCA and liver inflammation developed in mice after 4 weeks of AOM administration, in addition to colorectal cancer. One possible explanation is that obesity and fatty liver are risk factors for iCCA [195], with carcinogens affecting the biliary system through the inflammatory pathway. Although carcinogen model of CCA is effective, it is not employed in recent years because the model is time-consuming [196], with a high cost and low tumor formation rate, and poor experimental controllability. An animal model that exposed mice to 1,2-DCP also explained the crucial role of the relevant signaling pathways in the occurrence of CCA. Kimura et al. administered 1,2-DCP to mice and found that γ-H2AX was significantly elevated in wild-type mice, and it revealed that 1,2-DCP might induce cholangial cell proliferation through a NRF2-dependent mechanism [197]. In addition, drug-induced CCA also resulted in severe liver damage [198]. In the above bile duct cancer model, liver cirrhosis and liver cancer will develop. Therefore, if liver cancer and liver cirrhosis are also present in the animal model of CCA, it will be unfavorable for the precise study of CCA.

Genetic engineering model

Transgenic animal model

Transgenic technology offers a novel approach to create animal tumor models, using the principle of inducing genetic alterations (either gene knock-in, gene knock-out or creating mutations) in oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes [199], 200], offers a novel approach for creating tumor animal models. Yamada D et al. [185] used the Sleeping Beauty (SB) transposon system to construct a mouse model of iCCA with fibrotic characteristics. The SB system consists of two key components: SB transposase and transposon vector. Unlike traditional viral vectors or plasmid transfection, the SB system can permanently insert genes into the genome, enabling long-term stable expression of the target gene [200]. They did not use the conventional high-pressure tail vein injection method because this route of administration leads to hepatocellular rather than bile duct epithelial carcinoma [201]. Instead, retrograde injection of Akt and YAP oncogene plasmids into the biliary tract was used with concomitant subcutaneous injection of interleukin-33 (IL-33), a bile duct proliferative factor, to increase the rate of CCA formation in 72 % of the animals. When interleukin-6 (IL-6) was specifically knocked out in mice, a mixture of HCC and iCCA developed, suggesting that the role of IL-6 may be more to promote the formation and growth of CCA.

Huang et al. [186] introduced the SB AKT/YAP1 plasmid into Mdr2−/−mice with a PSC background, and conducted transposon-mediated transgenic manipulation with this model, which replicated the progression of CCA in patients with PSC. They also noted the presence of TGF-β pathway inhibition, immune cell infiltration, and fibrotic glial adhesion in CCA, as opposed to the non-PSC model. Although the tumor formation rate of SB oncogene plasmid alone is not high [202], the rate is significantly increased by factors that promote bile duct injury, which induce a “multiple hit” on the normal bile duct epithelium. There is increasing evidence that intense biliary fibrosis is important in the progression from benign lesions to invasive carcinoma [203], [204], [205].

Genetic engineering model

Smad4 Co/Co Pten Co/Co Alb-Cre mice

SMAD4 is a tumor suppressor gene, and its inactivation can be seen in various cancers such as pancreatic cancer and colorectal cancer. [206], 207]. It is also a key link in TGF-β signaling, and its mutation or deletion led to dysregulation of the signaling pathway and promotes tumor development [208], 209]. Phosphatase and Tensin Homolog (PTEN) is a tumor suppressor gene, and its deletion or mutation leads to an increase in Phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-tris Phosphate (PIP3) level which activate Akt and inhibit apoptosis [210], 211].

Xu et al. [187] crossed SMAD4 knockout mice with PTEN gene knockout mice, and the resultant offsprings developed CCA within 4–7 months. This indicates that the inactivation of SMAD4 and PTEN has a synergistic effect on CCA development,by activating the Akt/mTOR pathway and inhibiting the expression of cyclin D1 in these CCA model mice. In addition, this animal model partially developed HCC after 12 months, which may be related to the inactivation of PTEN. This study suggested that SMAD4 and PTEN genes and their related pathways may form future targets for CCA treatment.

LSL-Kras G12D Pten fl/fl Alb-Cre mice

KRAS-G12D mutation is the substitution of glycine (G) for aspartic acid (D) at codon 12 in the KRAS gene, which results in KRAS protein in a state of continuous GTP binding, thus activates downstream pathways, and promotes cell proliferation and survival [212], 213]. This is a typical mutation in pancreatic cancer [214], colorectal cancer [215], and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [216]. Ikenoue et al. [188] used KRAS activation and homozygous PTEN deletion to synergistically induce iCCA, which developed invasive carcinoma at 7–8 weeks and the induced tumors were mainly iCCA. The mechanism of the model is the activation of PI3k and MAPK pathways. Pathological examination revealed a significant accumulation of fibrous collagen surrounding the tumor. This suggests that new strategies such as KRAS inhibitors and immunotherapy may provide new treatment options for patients in the future.

Alb–Cre, Idh2LSL−R172, KrasLSL−G12D

This is a composite genetic engineering mouse model that successfully simulates the common genetic coexistence events in human intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma by simultaneously activating the IDH2 R172K mutation and the Kras G12D oncogene in liver cells and precursor cells [189]. This model not only reproduces the multi-stage progression process from cholangiocyte intraepithelial neoplasia to invasive and metastatic cholangiocarcinoma, but also reveals the key mechanism by which IDH mutations block liver cell differentiation and synergize with Kras signals to drive the malignant transformation of the cholangiogenic lineage. It provides an important preclinical research platform for studying the biological characteristics and targeted therapy of IDH mutant cholangiocarcinoma.

Alb–Cre, Idh2LSL−R132C, KrasLSL−G12D

This study successfully established a gene-engineered mouse model of cholangiocarcinoma that can highly simulate human diseases [190]. The researchers used precise gene editing techniques to create mice carrying latent Idh1R132C or Idh1R132H mutant alleles, and crossed them with liver-specific Alb-Cre and oncogenic KrasG12D mice. The key finding of this model is that the Idh1R132C (which can produce extremely high levels of carcinogenic metabolite (R)-2HG) and KrasG12D work synergistically to efficiently drive the occurrence of invasive and metastatic intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and its histopathological features are highly consistent with human diseases. This model not only reveals the specific pathogenic role of IDH1 mutation subtypes in cholangiocarcinoma, but also provides an indispensable preclinical research platform for further in-depth exploration of the mechanism by which mIDH1 promotes immune escape and testing of combined therapies.

As discussed, intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts have different developmental pathways embryonic development, which may be one of the reasons why iCCA and eCCA have different genetic backgrounds and mechanisms of development. With better understanding of research on eCCA, the model of subcutaneous transplanted tumors cannot explain the pathogenesis [217]. There is lack of a good pathological model of primary eCCA. Hnf1b CreER ; Kras G12D ; Tp53 flox/flox (HKP) mice

Studies of IPNB and BillN, which are precancerous lesions of eCCA provide insight into the development of precancerous lesions in animal models and subsequent establishment of invasive cancer models. Nagao et al. [220] crossed KrasG12D mice with Tp53flox/flox mice, and crossed Hnf1bCreER mice to specifically derive the extrahepatic biliary system, after 4 weeks of tamoxifen administration, and finally in Hnf1b CreER ; Kras G12D Tp53 flox/flox (HKP) mice, IPNB and BillN were observed in extrahepatic bile ducts and gallbladder. Wnt pathway activation has been linked to gastric precancerous lesions [230], 231] and researchers attempted to cross HKP mice with Ctnnb1lox (ex3)/+ mice. The latter intervention resulted in the activation of the canonical Wnt pathway in mouse offspring [232]. However, this did not lead to invasive cancer, as the mice only carried precancerous lesions. KRAS G12D and TP53 were both highly mutated genes in eCCA, accounting for 31.5 and 36.9 % [233] of identified mutations, respectively. This indicates the targeted inactivation due to genetic modification drive the formation of extrahepatic bile duct cancer. It is therefore necessary to study critical factors that allow precancerous lesions to develop into invasive cancer.

LSL-KrasCreERT, Tgfβ2flox/flox, CDH1flox/flox-K19CreERT(KTC-K19) mice

Nakagawa et al. [155] used a more complex gene-knockout combination to create a mouse model of eCCA, and at 4 weeks, large gallbladder and extrahepatic bile ducts tumors were observed, histologically compatible with invasive cancers. They first crossed KRAS G12D mice with TGFβ2 knockout mice and used K19CreERT tool mice to target the knockout to the bile duct, forming KT-K19CreERT mice. However, no invasive carcinoma was found in these mice, and only mild hyperplasia of bile duct epithelium was observed. Given the pivotal role of specific loss of E-cadherin in inducing liver cancer, and they then conditionally knocked out the CDH1 gene which encodes E-cadherin, and combined CDH1flox/flox with KT-K19CreERT to generate KTC-K19 CreERT mice, ultimately successful in creating an eCCA mouse model. It is evident that the loss of E-cadherin enhances damage and cancer development in the bile duct epithelium. Thus creating the “second hit” for tumorigenesis. The results showed that a large number of IL-33 released by peribiliary gland (PBG) became an important factor in promoting cancer cell proliferation and invasion and could replace the effect of CDH1 conditional knockout. Targeting the inhibition of IL-33 may emerge as a novel approach for treating eCCA. Application of the model results is limited by the fact that all mice died after 4 weeks, probably from respiratory failure caused by lung metastasis.

Sox9CreERT2+; LSL-KrasG12D/+; PTENflox/flox(S+KPP) mice

The combination of gene mutations and deletions associated with eCCA is different between humans and mice [234], 235]. For example, Lin et al. [221] used the Sox9-CreERT tool mouse to target the Cre enzyme in order to cross the KRAS mutant and PTEN knockout mice, which were then used in this model (S+KPP) to show eCCA after 12 weeks. It should be noted that both iCCA and pancreatic cancer also developed in the S+KPP model. According to pathological findings, there were an abundance of inflammatory cells infiltrating the bile duct, with changes in cell polarity, and fibrosis. However, pancreatic cancer in this S+KPP model appeared to be more significant with rapid progression, resulting in a median survival time of only 93 days.

Of note, this Sox9-CreERT mouse model is only considered a transient requirement for bile duct biogenesis. However, the results pointed to the potential role of Sox9 in the genesis of biliopancreatic disease, which deserves further evaluation.

Pdx1-Cre; LSL-Pik3ca H1047R/+ mice

Can a single gene mutation or knockout induce the spontaneous development of extrahepatic bile duct tumors in animal models? Falcomata et al. [222] provided an answer by crossing Pik3caH1047R/+ mutant mice with Pdx1-Cre transgenic mice. Over the course of 800 days,90%of the animals developed eCCA and this observation was not random. BillN in the bile duct epithelium could be observed in the adult hybrid mice (3 months old), and the severity of this lesion would progress with time when high-grade dysplasia was observed at 6 months, and eCCA eventually developed in the aged mice., The experiment has a control group using a Pdx1-Cre, KRAS G12D model, and no BilIN or eCCA was found in follow up period. This indicates that Kras point mutations may not act independently to drive tumorigenesis of eCCA in mice.

PTEN L/L /Pdx1-Cre mice

In a recent study, Yang et al. [223] crossed PTEN knockout mice with Pdx1-CRE tool mice and observed the development of cholangitis in mice at 1 month, with cystic dilatation, fibrosis, and inflammatory infiltration of the bile ducts, possibly related to the NFκB signaling pathway activation. Highly heterogenous hyperplasia was observed in 15 % of the animals with 4 % developing eCCA. This was also confirmed in experimental animals that had been kept for more than 300 days: the cancer tissues were PTEN negative and Sox-9 and KI-67 positive, correlating with the origin of CCA and its invasiveness. Crossing PTEN l/l/Pdx1-Cre mice with Aurora kinase A (AURKA) gene knockout mice to generate PTEN l/l/Aurkal/l/Pdx1-Cre mice significantly inhibited the development of cholangitis and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma compared with PTEN l/l/Pdx1-Cre mice. The Aurka inhibitor MLN8237 [236], 237] may have therapeutic potential for eCCA in animal models suggesting the inhibition of Aurora kinase A may be a possible therapeutic target for eCCA.

Single-cell sequencing – a translational bridge from human tumor atlas to refined animal models

Animal models of CCA are indispensable for studying tumor biology and therapeutic efficacy, yet a critical limitation persists: their frequent inability to fully recapitulate the profound cellular heterogeneity and complex cellular cross-talk characteristic of human CCA [238]. The main reasons for are: a) the mouse model lacks specific cell subpopulations in human CCA [239], 240]; and b) some of the pathways that play a key role in the development of human CAA are not activated in mice [241]. Single-cell sequencing (SCS) techniques that reveal the dynamics of tumor evolution, drug responses, and immune interactions at the single-cell and subcellular levels can help researchers identify key malignant cell subpopulations and microenvironmental characteristics in human CCAs [242], 243], then select these subpopulations to build animal models, or design gene-editing mice based on the pathways which more accurately simulate human tumors for follow-up mechanisms and drug testing. SCS technologies, by providing high-resolution maps of human CCA at the single-cell level, have transitioned from a mere observational tool to a translational guide for animal modeling. SCS explicitly simulates human tumor characteristics

Identification of core cell subpopulations

Bouchard et al. [244] emphasize that interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME) and tumor cells can activate different physiological roles. Without targeted identification of non-cancerous cells in these tumor microenvironments, it is impossible to simulate the highly heterogeneous characteristics of human tumors. A recent SCS article on intrahepatic bile duct cancer confirms this: Li et al. [245] studied TME characteristics at the tumor-normal interface and pointed out that SPP1+ macrophages, endothelial cells, and POSTN+ cancer associated fibroblasts (CAFs) at the interface with the tumor can activate CD44 cancer stem cells within the tumor and induce angiogenesis to promote the progression of biliary cancer. Alvisi et al. [246] showed that iCCA tumor tissue is infiltrated by a large number of hyperfunctional CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), while CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) are functionally inhibited, this finding is directly related to the immune response in cancer. These studies directly affect the design of animal models should ensure that the model contains a representative repertoire of important cellular components in human tumors.

Lock the drive signal pathways

Current mouse models of CCA are not ideal to simulate signaling pathways in human CCA, so it is necessary to dissect the signaling interactions of human CCA in order to reproduce the signaling pathways in animal models. Ma et al. [247] by analyzing single-cell maps of human CCA, pointed out that transduction of VEGF signaling in TME is associated with immune modulation by the tumors, and that drugs targeting the VEGF signaling pathway may improve the effectiveness of immunotherapy, therefore, the animal model in this study needs to reproduce high expression of VEGF signaling in CCA. For example, VEGF+ tumor cells harvested from human CCA tissue and v-CAFs promoting microangiogenesis were transplanted into mice to mimic the specific situation of VEGF-enriched TME [248].

SCS guidance model types

Immune microenvironment driven models

Immune interaction is an important factor in tumor development and invasion, and also profoundly affects the prognosis in CCA [249], 250]. The sequencing results of Chen et al. [251]suggested that in patients with biliary cancer, PNOC+ B cell invasion indicates better prognosis, and it is possible to evaluate cancer proliferation as an independent indicator. Lin et al. [252] used the sequencing results to divide CCA specimens into three types: IG1, IG2, and IG3. The IG2 subgroup has strong immune escape and proliferation properties, and attenuation of interferon-γ signaling pathways has been examined. Analysis of cell composition and signaling pathways in the tumor immune environment provides a basis for building the type of animal model that best matches the study target.

Models of genetic characteristics guidance

CCA has a distinct genetic background, and different gallbladder cancers occur with different genetic mutations [107]. Amanda et al. [253] analyzed the role of transcription factors (TFs) in iCCA using single-cell epitomic sequencing and found that specific FOS/JUN sequences are highly expressed in iCCA, while CEBP-related sequences are expressed in HCC, providing a reliable idea to distinguish between iCCA and HCC. Another study suggested that inhibiting FOXA1 and FOXA2 expression could cause HCC to shift in the direction of iCCA [254]. Kitagawa et al. [255] performed sequencing and they found mutations in BCAT1 and BCAT2 in gallbladder cancer samples. How can these key pieces of information be applied in the modeling process? When SCS reveals that a certain signaling pathway (such as the VEGF signal [247]) is abnormally active in a specific cell subset and is associated with immunosuppression, it becomes the preferred target for the construction of GEMM. Based on this, researchers can design or select GEMM in which VEGF is conditionally overexpressed or its receptor is knocked out in bile duct cells, thereby directly testing the pathogenic function of this pathway in the in vivo model and converting the bioinformatics association into an in vivo model for mechanism research.

Model of cell interaction dependency

Cellular interaction is a major feature of CCA [256], Xu et al. [257] studied single-cell sequencing data from eCCA and confirmed that a large number of VEGF-related receptor-ligands were enriched within the mononuclear cell cluster in TME, which promoted the formation of tumor blood supply. Research by Li et al. [258] suggests that patients with iCCA infected with HBV tend to activate CD8+ T cells than non-virally infected patients, and that CD8+ T cells can promote tumor progression through signaling communication with ICAM1+ tumor-associated macrophages. Since cellular interaction is very common in the process of gallbladder cancer, transplantation models can consider co-transplanting specific cells with tumor cells, such as co-transplanting CAFs with tumor cells, or pre-modifying the transplant environment by injecting CAFs in advance, to simulate the microecology of human CCA after tumor cell transplantation [259].

Guiding cell engineering transplantation

The key components of the tumor microenvironment identified by SCS, such as the oncogenic SPP1+ macrophages or POSTN+ cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) [245], are valuable not only for discovery but also for providing clear cell engineering schemes for transplantation models. Based on this, researchers can isolate, enrich, or induce these specific cell subpopulations in vitro and co-transplant them with CCA cells into mice, thereby directly verifying the necessity of these cells in driving tumor stem cell characteristics or angiogenesis in the living environment. This marks a crucial step from “describing correlations” to “experimentally verifying causal relationships”.

SCS improves clinical relevance of models

Matching patient subgroup characteristics

Cell sequencing provides the gold standard for the clinical relevance of animal models by revealing the molecular and cellular characteristics of human tumors. Song et al. [260] performed single-cell sequencing of two different iCCA subtypes: perihilar large duct type (iCCAphl) and peripheral small duct type (iCCApps), identifying S100P and SPP1 as biomarkers that distinguish the two subtypes. The two subtypes cannot be distinguished histologically, but the prognosis is very different [261]. S100P+/SPP1-iCCAphl has a worse prognosis, and hence early sorting of cells is necessary to construct a PDX model of biliary cancer with a worse prognosis. In addition, a sequencing result for tumor stem also showed that biliary cancer cells had a high degree of tumor stem cells [262], and that the PDX model constructed by sorting the cells had a shorter incubation period and larger tumor clusters.

Predictive optimization of clinical treatment

Response of biliary cancer to treatment vary because of tumor heterogeneity, Wang et al. [263] based on the concentration of Zmiz1+ cells and Ybx1+ cells in single-cell sequencing results, separate biliary cancer into two subtypes of “inflammation” and “proliferation”. The two subtypes have a big difference in prognosis, and the proliferation type has a worse prognosis than “inflammation” types, the response to drug treatment is also inconsistent. Drug response when tested on animal models may be inconsistent with the clinical setting if the animal model does not specifically correlate with the clinical subtype. SCS provides a path to optimizing animal models.

Optimized model evaluation criteria

SCS guides model construction and provides a novel dimension for model evaluation. One single-cell sequencing study on dCCA noted that NEATI+ malignant cells are highly invasive in dCCA, which accounts for dCCA’s highly aggressive activity, and is also associated with perineural invasion (PNI) of dCCA. Another study focused on the role of the CD34+ CAFs subpopulation in the progression of iCCA, which, if knocked out CD34, promotes the progression of iCCA [264] SCS can evaluate animal models from the DNA, RNA, and protein levels, such as detecting the level of NEATI to determine the degree of PNI in the model, to provide dynamic evaluation of the model at different time points, and to establish a scoring system for the purpose of accurately simulating human CCA. The animal model can be evaluated and optimized through the patterns, as shown in Figure 5 (c).

Establish an iterative modeling closed loop of “discovery – construction – verification – optimization”

SCS should be integrated as the core of an iterative closed-loop mechanism that drives the continuous optimization of animal models: 1) Discovery: SCS analyzes the cellular and molecular characteristics of human CCA; 2) Transformation and Construction: Based on the above findings, rationally select or engineer the model (such as co-transplantation of specific cells, editing key genes); 3) High-resolution Verification: Conduct SCS analysis on the model to evaluate its fidelity at the single-cell level; 4) Feedback Optimization: Adjust the modeling strategy based on the deficiencies identified during the verification process. This framework elevates SCS from an auxiliary technology to a central pillar guiding the construction of CCA animal models with greater mechanistic insight and clinical predictability.

Reflections on SCS

Indeed, establishing human single-cell sequencing data as the “gold standard” for animal models and blueprint construction must take into account its inherent technical limitations and translational challenges. Firstly, there is a “capture bias” at the technical level [265], 266]. For instance, the tissue dissociation process may selectively lose delicate cell types (such as certain large tumor cells) or enrich specific immune cells, thereby causing distortion of the map [267]. Despite the differences in cell types and pathways among species, the single-cell map of human CCA reveals the core features of the disease (such as key intercellular communication axes, dominant malignant processes, and the basic principles of the tumor microenvironment). So that, when using SCS data to guide model design or as an evaluation benchmark, it is necessary to: 1) conduct cross-validation by combining multiple independent studies; 2) supplement the cell spatial localization information through techniques such as spatial transcriptomics; 3) clearly state the technical background and potential limitations of data generation.

The goal of animal models is not and cannot be to achieve a completely identical “replication”, but rather to reconstruct and experimentally manipulate these core features within a controllable system, thereby understanding their mechanisms. Therefore, acknowledging the differences among species is the prerequisite for research. The human SCS data does not provide a fixed and unchanging template, but rather a priority reference framework and an initial coordinate system for evaluation.

Targeted therapy for cholangiocarcinoma

CCA often present late in advanced stages, the presence of distant metastases preclude patients from undergoing radical resection, and in general, CCA is not very responsive to traditional chemotherapy, resulting in an overall poor prognosis. The recent use of next generation sequencing (NGS) to identify specific tumor mutations has led to development of targeted therapy targeting for specific gene mutations. These new drugs have been shown to be more effective in clinical treatment of CCA and improve survival. The latest FDA-approved drugs for targeted treatment of CCA are summarized in Table 4. Inhibitors targeting the FGFR gene

The FGFR genes are significant in CCA. The carcinogenic effect of FGFRs on cholangiocarcinoma is mainly achieved through gene fusions, mutations or amplifications [273], 274]. Among them, FGFR2 can fuse with a variety of partner genes (B1CC1 and TACC3) to promote the abnormal activation of downstream signaling pathways, such as MAPK pathway and PI3K/AKT [275].

Pemazyre, the first drug to target the FGFR gene for treating CCA, demonstrated effectiveness in patients with FGFR gene fusion/rearrangement in a randomized controlled trial [268], and this was subsequently approved by the FDA for the treatment of CCA and received the “Orphan Drug” designation. A recent case report indicates that Pemazyre can be used as a neoadjuvant agent for CCA to provide conditions and preparation for subsequent living donor liver transplantation (LDLT) for advanced CCA [276], indicating the potential application of this agent in future clinical treatment of CCA.

Infigratinib has shown efficacy in clinical trials targeting CCA [269], an FGFR kinase inhibitor approved in 2021 for treatment of advanced or metastatic CCA with an FGFR gene fusion. It can inhibit the activation of tyrosine kinases which, affect subsequent cell growth. However, in 2024, the FDA withdrew approval for its use in CCA. Futibatinib, an FGFR inhibitor, has been widely used in the treatment of cancer. In randomized controlled trials, Futibatinib induced remarkable tumor blockade or shrinkage in more than 80 % of patients [271], 277], improve patients’ quality of life and prolong survival.

Inhibitors targeting IDH1

Although uncommon in eCCA, IDH1 gene mutations are frequent in iCCA and IDH1 inhibitor has a beneficial effect on the progression of iCCA [278]. In experimental studies, Ivosidenib significantly prolonged survival in patients with advanced iCCA, without leading to the emergence of overt resistance. The pharmacokinetics study also reflected its significant effect on down-regulation of D-2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG) [270], 279], which is related to the metabolic pathway of cancer and the process of malignant transformation [280]. This survival benefit is of significant importance both statistically and clinically, and it has established Ivosidenib as the standard treatment for this refractory population.

In addition to the survival benefits, this study also highlighted the outstanding safety profile and the improvement in patient quality of life of Ivosidenib. The adverse event profile was mainly of low severity, and compared with the placebo group, it significantly delayed the deterioration of patient quality of life. More importantly, Ivosidenib treatment led to a rapid and sustained decrease in the level of the carcinogenic metabolite 2-HG in patients’ serum, which directly verified its targeted mechanism and made 2-HG a potential pharmacodynamic biomarker.

The success of the ClarIDHy trial not only provided the first effective targeted therapy for patients with IDH1-mutated CCA, but also conceptually confirmed that for specific molecular subgroups of CCA, even in second-line treatment, precise targeted intervention can translate into clear survival benefits. It strengthened the urgency of conducting routine genetic testing in advanced CCA to guide clinical decisions, and laid the foundation for exploring the application of IDH1 inhibitors in combination with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, etc. in more frontline or combined strategies.

Inhibitors that target HER2

Zanidatamab-hrii is a newly approved drug by the FDA for patients with HER2-positive advanced CCA and has shown good clinical efficacy and patient benefits in clinical trials [272]. HER2 mutation is relatively common, especially in eCCA, with reported prevalence of 18 %. The approval of Zanidatamab-hrii is considered a breakthrough in the treatment of refractory eCCA. The benefits of HER2 inhibitors in CCA has also been demonstrated in previous studies [281], 282].

Discussion

Having a suitable CCA animal model enables a proper understanding of the mechanisms of tumor initiation and progression and allows testing of novel strategies for early diagnosis and treatment of CCA. The tumor organoid model can replicate the growth patterns and development of solid tumors in vitro but its preparation is labor-intensive and technically challenging. Orthotopic primary animal tumor models can replicate the developmental process of human tumors. Since these models retain intact or minimally impaired immune function, they are suitable for studying tumor immunity. Carcinogen-induced methods are traditional approach aimed at induction of tumors in animals in response to inflammatory injuries and carcinogenic agents. However, most current carcinogen-based animal models require prolonged preparatory period and are associated with many complications, in particular hepatic dysfunction, which impede CCA research. Although the development of genetic engineering models is complex and time-consuming, it does offer the ability to accurately simulate human tumor progression. Thus, simplifying gene-editing techniques and identifying key genes that induce mutations may offer advancement in genetic engineering models. In both human and animal models, the development of CCA demonstrates a similar pattern, starting with bile duct inflammation and cystic edema, progressing to fibrosis and collagen deposition, subsequently advancing to precancerous lesions like BilIN and IPNB, which ultimately resulting in invasive carcinoma [283], 284]. Current research on precancerous lesions also suggests that appropriate “multiple hits” are key to the eventual development of cancer models [285], 286]. Thus, following initial genetic modification, observing the animal over time, allows one to study the evolution and impact of external manipulation of an initially benign lesion as it eventually degenerates into cancer. There is a need to develop an in situ CCA animal models to study different factors that drive bile duct injury in extrahepatic bile duct cancer.

SCS-guided modeling is a rational strategy of ‘understand first, then rebuild’: it uses a thorough preliminary analysis of human diseases (SCS) as a blueprint, ensuring that the constructed models directly target the core cellular and molecular features of the disease. This approach links the model closely to specific scientific questions (such as particular cell interactions or pathway functions) from the very beginning, greatly reducing the randomness of the research. The future development direction lies in the continuous guidance of SCS, through the complementary combination of modular and standardized genetic engineering strategies and rapid in vitro models (such as organoids), to further optimize the practical efficiency of this rational modeling process.

Conclusions

Cholangiocarcinoma remains a challenging disease – the relative rarity of disease, the advanced stage at presentation, the paucity of effective therapies and the failure to fully understand risk factors and the pathophysiology of the disease contributes to a generally poor prognosis, which has been compounded by limited resources including research interest and funding.

The purpose of this review is to provide investigators with an overview of a variety of animal models, which will aid future studies in CCA. Nevertheless, we also emphasize that animal tumor models may not ablet to completely replicate human tumors, as animals differ from humans in multiple aspects – physiologically, immunologically and pathologically. The tumor behavior of human eCCA is associated with early infiltration of adjacent surrounding tissues and distant metastasis, which is not seen in animal models [286], 287]. This difference may be potentially explained by insights from recent single-cell sequencing data, where individual or partial population-based data from CCA patients obtained from NGS approaches can be applied to the construction of novel animal models, may compensate for the difference.

The approval of new drugs offers a hope in the clinical treatment of CCA, as these can be tested in animal models for CCA. Development of animal models, or ex vivo organoid models is integral to the accelerated regulatory testing of these new CCA therapeutics, offering in-depth preclinical study of CCA, allowing for new drugs research and treatment methods.

In summary, current research on primary in situ CCA remains limited, and preclinical studies utilizing animal models are scarce. Insights gained from development of animal models for CCA offer new avenue for future investigations. Continued research into the mechanisms of CCA formation, and the interaction between oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, will ensure future development of research into more animal models studies.
