Spatial Accessibility to Emergency Departments and Surgical Treatment for Patients with Acute Appendicitis in South Korea
Authors: Kyungsik Kim, Ho Kyung Sung, Kyung-Shin Lee, Eunyoung Kim, Hansu Shin, Hye Sook Min
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRecent concerns regarding the delivery of surgical services in South Korea emphasize the need for close monitoring of spatial access to surgical care in patients with acute appendicitis at the regional level.Materials and MethodsThis study included emergency department (ED) visits with acute appendicitis and subsequent appendectomies from the National Emergency Department Information System database (2015–2021). Travel distances to the hospital were measured, and the proportion of patients admitted to regional hospitals was visualized on a geographic map. Factors related to complicated appendicitis were analyzed using logistic regression.ResultsAmong 207344 patients who visited the EDs, those from areas with low population density had higher proportions of patients aged ≥65 years (23.2%), Medical Aid recipients (4.8%), transfers from other hospitals (39.3%), and cases of complicated appendicitis (7.0%) compared to other regions. Additionally, patients in low-density regions had the highest proportion of traveling 50 km or more (27.7%), compared to those in mid-density (5.9%) and high-density (2.5%) regions. In the multivariate analysis, patients aged ≥65 years exhibited a stronger association with complicated appendicitis compared to other age groups.ConclusionThe current results suggested that regions with low population density experienced low access to surgical care, revealing significant regional variation linked to population density. Given the rapidly aging population and the concentration of residents in urban areas in South Korea, urgent policy action is needed to ensure the adequate delivery of emergency general surgery services in low-density regions.Keywords: Appendicitis, emergency general surgery, health services accessibility, healthcare variation

INTRODUCTION

Conditions requiring emergency general surgery (EGS) encompass a range of time-sensitive surgical conditions that demand immediate attention and substantial resources.1 However, lower-risk EGS conditions such as acute appendicitis have often been overlooked in terms of service accessibility compared to higher-risk conditions.2 Prior studies have indicated that patients with lower-risk but urgent surgical needs are often required to extend travel to high-volume centers.3,4 Factors contributing to this issue include a declining workforce in general surgery, the emergence of surgical subspecialties, and the closure of emergency departments (EDs) in remote areas.5,6

Acute appendicitis is a representative lower-risk EGS condition requiring timely surgical intervention to prevent serious complications. In South Korea, appendectomy ranks as one of the common surgical procedures, along with hemorrhoidectomy, cholecystectomy, and cesarean section.7 National data from 2021 showed that 85.3% of patients underwent appendectomy at local hospitals within the patients’ administrative district.7 Nevertheless, South Korea faces growing challenges in delivering surgical care, similar to other countries. Despite the presence of a National Health Insurance (NHI) system aimed at ensuring financial access to care for all citizens, regional inequality persists due to the unequal distribution of surgical staff and hospitals across regions, along with resource shortages.8,9 Accumulated evidence suggests that such resource imbalances lead to variation in the delivery of adequate healthcare services and treatable mortality rates.10,11,12,13 The recent trend of population concentration in metropolitan areas, along with the country’s rapidly aging population, further heightens concerns about healthcare accessibility in less densely populated regions.14

The concerns regarding the delivery of surgical services in South Korea emphasize the need for close monitoring of access to care in patients with acute appendicitis at the regional level. From a policy standpoint, it is crucial to understand regional variation in the availability of appropriate surgical care at nearby local hospitals and their impact on adverse clinical outcomes. To address this issue, our study used a nationwide ED database to examine patients with acute appendicitis who were admitted through EDs and underwent appendectomy. Our objective was to analyze regional variations in geospatial access to surgical care for acute appendicitis based on population density. Through this analysis, we aim to provide insights that will help ensure timely and equitable access to emergency surgical care for all citizens.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

This study used data from the National Emergency Department Information System (NEDIS) of South Korea (2015–2021), established in 2003, to assess the quality of emergency medical services. In 2015, 96% (403/420) of government-designated EDs transmitted information to the NEDIS database nationwide. This percentage steadily increased to 100% in 2021, with all 398 designated EDs contributing to the database. The design and detailed characteristics of the NEDIS database have been described in previous studies.15,16 Data masking is applied in the NEDIS; therefore, this study was reviewed and determined to be exempt from Institutional Review Board oversight by the National Medical Center (IRB no. NMC-2023-08-086).

Study population

As this study aimed to examine the accessibility of emergency surgery for patients with acute appendicitis, the study population included ED visits of patients with confirmed acute appendicitis and subsequent appendectomies between January 2015 and December 2021. The initial dataset included 59513242 ED visit records from the NEDIS database between 2015 and 2021 (Fig. 1). To capture as many ED visits for acute appendicitis as possible, the case definition encompassed diagnostic codes for acute appendicitis (K35, K35.2, K35.3, K35.8) and appendicitis (K36, K37) according to the 8th Korean Classification of Diseases; however, only cases in which the diagnosis was listed as either primary or secondary were included. Appendectomy procedures in South Korea are reimbursed through the diagnosis-related group (DRG) based prospective payment system. Considering the potential influences of the DRG payment system on the recording of the procedure codes, our study specifically defined complicated appendicitis as cases of appendicitis with generalized peritonitis (K35.2).17,18

Flowchart of the study population selection. ED, emergency department.

There were 224346 ED visits for acute appendicitis between 2015 and 2021; cases without sex and age information (n=2), without DRG procedure codes for appendectomy (Q2850, Q2861, Q2862, Q2863) (n=6835), and without address records at the second-level basic local administrative districts in South Korea (“Si,” “Gun,” and “Gu”) (n=10165) were sequentially excluded. Finally, 207344 ED visits were included in the study (Fig. 1). The number of ED visits was considered equivalent to the number of patients according to the case definition.

Variables

Demographic variables included age, sex, insurance status, and region of residence. The variables related to ED utilization comprised admission route, length of stay in the ED, admission, patient disposition after appendectomy, and the volume of the hospital visited. The variables of age (0–19, 20–39, 40–64, ≥65 years), insurance status (NHI, Medical Aid, uninsured), length of stay in the ED (<6 hours, ≥6 hours), admission type (general ward, intensive care unit), and patient disposition after appendectomy (discharged to home, transferred to another hospital, died) were categorized. The hospitals were categorized into four groups according to the number of beds (<300, 300–499, 500–699, ≥700 beds) provided by the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service in Korea. The patient’s region of residence at the second-level administrative district (“Si,” “Gun,” and “Gu”) was further categorized based on population density, which was used as an indicator of urbanization level.19 Population density was calculated by dividing the population of the district by its land area (km2) as of 2018,20 and the regions were divided into terciles according to the population density (low: <182.27 people/km2, mid: 182.27–2735.87 people/km2, high: ≥2735.88 people/km2). Accordingly, the 252 administrative districts registered as residential regions of patients were classified into low-density (n=88), mid-density (n=76), and high-density (n=88) regions.

Measurement of travel distance and geographical mapping

Travel distances to hospitals were measured using the address of all 393 EDs visited for acute appendicitis and the address of the central point of “Si,” “Gun,” and “Gu” in which the patient resides. Addresses were converted into longitude and latitude geocoordinates using Geocoder-Xr (Geoservice-Web v1.3.2.5, Seoul, Korea; http://www.geoservice.co.kr). To measure travel distance to the hospital, the 2021 transportation network database of the Geographic Information System (GIS) provided by the National Transportation DB (https://www.ktdb.go.kr) was used. The ArcMap 10.8.2 (ESRI) and its Network Analyst–OD Cost Matrix function were used for GIS analysis to generate a matrix through the Dijkstra algorithm, which finds the shortest route between multiple origins and destinations.21 The approximate distance traveled by the patient was divided into four groups (<15, 15–49.9, 50–99.9, ≥100 km). To visualize spatial accessibility in administrative districts, the proportion of ED utilization within 50 km was classified into four groups (0%–24%, 25%–49%, 50%–74%, 75%–100%) and mapped alongside the locations of visited EDs.

Statistical analysis

To describe general characteristics, chi-square tests were conducted for categorical variables according to the population density of regions. To explore factors related to the presentation of complicated appendicitis, sex, age, insurance status, travel distance, number of hospital beds, admission route, ED length of stay, and hospital admission were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. Bar graphs, scatter plots, and geographical mapping were used for the visualization analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R (version 4.2.1, R Foundation).

RESULTS

Demographic features and access to surgical care

Among all patients with acute appendicitis (n=207344), significant differences were shown between those residing in low-population-density regions and other regions (Table 1). Patients in low-density regions had higher proportions of those aged ≥65 years (23.2%), Medical Aid recipients (4.8%), transfers from other hospitals (39.3%), and patients who underwent appendectomies at hospitals with fewer than 300 beds (40.0%). Moreover, the proportion of complicated appendicitis (7.0%), admission to the intensive care unit (0.9%), and mortality following appendectomy (0.1%) were significantly higher in low-density regions compared to other regions.

Regarding travel distance to the hospital, a larger proportion of patients in high-density regions (89.1%) traveled less than 15 km, compared to 22.4% in low-density regions (Table 1 and Fig. 2A). Patients in low-density regions exhibited the highest proportion of traveling 50 km or more, at 27.7%, compared to those in mid-density (5.9%) and high-density regions (2.5%). Factors associated with the presentation of complicated appendicitis

In the multivariate analysis, patients aged ≥65 years exhibited a stronger association with the presentation of complicated appendicitis across all population density groups (Table 2). Medical Aid recipients in low-density regions were more likely to present with complicated appendicitis than those in other regions [adjusted odds ratio (aOR), 1.97; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.61–2.41]. In mid-density and high-density regions, the presentation of complicated appendicitis was more closely related to travel distances between 50–99.9 km than distances less than 15 km. In low-density regions, we observed a weak association between complicated appendicitis and long-distance travel (aOR 1.42; 95% CI 1.10–1.82 of travel distances ≥100 km, compared to travel distances <15 km) and between complicated appendicitis and transfers from other hospitals (aOR 1.25; 95% CI 1.10–1.41, compared to direct admission). Across all population density groups, the presentation of complicated appendicitis had lower aORs for receiving surgical care in larger hospitals with 700 beds or more (low-density, 0.52; mid-density, 0.60; high-density, 0.82) and higher aORs for admission to the intensive care unit (low-density, 4.00; mid-density, 4.68; high-density, 3.89) compared to the reference group.

DISCUSSION

In the current study, we found substantial variation in spatial access to hospitals for surgical care in patients with acute appendicitis across different regions in South Korea. Specifically, regions with low population density merit additional focus not only because residents in these areas traveled substantially longer distances to reach hospitals, but also due to significant variations in travel distances among these low-density regions. Similar variations have been documented in the United States, highlighting the uneven distribution of hospitals with surgical capabilities and the regional gaps at smaller area levels in geospatial access to EGS care.1,22,23,24 These studies indicated that the variations in access to EGS care could be attributed to various sociodemographic factors even within low-access areas, such as the proportions of uninsured, publicly insured, and racial and ethnic minority groups.1,22,25

In South Korea, despite decades of strong economic growth, regional variation persists among metropolitan cities, small and medium-sized cities, and rural areas. Given Korea’s highly concentrated and urbanized population, there are growing concerns that the existing regional gaps may worsen due to population decline and rapid aging.14 The present results revealed these issues in part by identifying the significant variation in population density and travel distances to the hospital across regions. Despite their sparse population, low-density regions harbor a relative concentration of socioeconomically and demographically vulnerable groups, including older adults, Medical Aid recipients, and young immigrants in South Korea.26,27,28 In addition, our findings showed that the proportion of postoperative transfers to other hospitals was relatively higher in these low-density areas. This may be because patients in such regions often travel considerable distances to receive surgery, and those requiring longer recovery periods may subsequently be transferred to hospitals closer to their place of residence. Overall, the current findings suggest that detailed analyses are necessary for both rural and urban regions, including the identification of vulnerable areas within metropolitan cities and distinguishing between the suburbs of metropolitan cities and more remote areas. Such analyses would offer insights into the specific needs and context of each community.

In the current healthcare context of South Korea, improving access to EGS in proportion to population density remains a significant challenge. If patients are consistently required to travel long distances for the treatment of relatively common and low-risk conditions such as appendicitis, this situation may negatively affect clinical outcomes in the future, particularly considering the rapidly aging population, even though the present study did not demonstrate significant differences.29,30 To address these challenges and improve healthcare access in medically vulnerable areas, several policy measures have been proposed. Improving access to surgical services involves more than simply increasing the number of local hospitals.31 The key is to implement feasible policy frameworks that allocate surgical resources based on local needs while ensuring system-wide efficiency, similar to regionalized care models established for specific conditions.32,33 Efforts to expand access must be grounded in the practical feasibility of service provision, with the consideration of the current surgical capacities. For instance, it may be unrealistic to expect hospitals with fewer than 300 beds in remote mountainous and island regions to maintain full surgical staffing and dedicated operative teams. Instead, policy priorities in such areas should focus on ensuring the availability of appropriately equipped EDs and building efficient referral and transfer systems to facilitate timely access to surgical care.22,24,34,35 Moreover, sustained governmental support would be necessary to incentivize and enable local hospitals currently providing EGS to maintain their surgical capacity. This may include introducing regional incentive schemes for general surgeons, fostering cooperative networks among regional surgical providers, and offering comprehensive support for interdisciplinary teams involved in emergency surgical care.36 Furthermore, continuous monitoring of local EGS demand, improvements in the transportation system, financial support for transferred patients, and the provision of caregiver services can also contribute to the effective provision of EGS.37,38

Prior studies have shown a trend among pediatric patients with acute appendicitis to prefer high-volume hospitals or specialized centers over smaller community hospitals, increasing travel distances for treatment.17,39,40 Fortunately, longer travel distances to the surgical facility did not increase the risk of complicated appendicitis.17 However, admission to high-volume hospitals might not be necessary in many instances, as most patients require hospitalization for less than 24 hours or no admission at all, with their final diagnoses mostly indicating uncomplicated conditions.39 Interestingly, our study revealed a negative association between visits to high-volume hospitals with more than 700 beds and the presentation of complicated appendicitis across all regional groups. These findings suggest that various factors beyond clinical severity may influence hospital selection for routine conditions like acute appendicitis. For example, the preference for laparoscopic surgery, which is more likely to be available at large-scale hospitals and was not included in the present study, may have contributed to the observed distribution. While the patient’s clinical needs should guide transfers to highly capable centers, routine transfers to such centers may not always be optimal for low-risk emergency situations. In addition, it is crucial to provide community hospitals with opportunities to enhance their capacity to treat EGS conditions.22 Ultimately, the goal is to strategically ensure community hospitals are equipped with surgical capabilities.39,41

The study’s findings should be interpreted with caution, given the limitations inherent in the study design. Firstly, this study was exclusively focused on patients who visited the designated EDs. Patients admitted through EDs not designated by the government, those who visited surgery centers during the day, or those who underwent percutaneous drainage and a delayed appendectomy were excluded. Secondly, the dataset did not include cases involving surgeries beyond appendectomies. As a result, severe cases of complicated appendicitis may have been excluded. In addition, the analysis did not separately evaluate cases of laparoscopic appendectomy. Third, the current study did not consider the impact of the DRG payment system or other policy changes on ED visits and hospital choice. The DRG payment system for appendectomies has been applied to all medical institutions since 2012 in South Korea, which may have substantially impacted the overall distribution of service. Moreover, the 2016 outbreak of the Middle East respiratory syndrome and the pandemic of coronavirus disease 2019 may have affected ED visit frequency. Fourth, this study used the geographic center of each administrative district based on ZIP code, rather than the addresses of individual patients, to measure travel distances. This privacy-related limitation only allowed the inference of general trends in travel distance. This study reflects the patterns of emergency medical center visits for most patients with appendicitis in South Korea, lending it significant validity. Additionally, the findings may be applicable to other studies assessing medical utilization in countries with low birthrates and super-aged populations. Fifth, as diverse socioeconomic and important clinical variables were not available in this study, a more detailed analysis of the relationship between accessibility and prognosis will require the inclusion of additional variables in future studies.

In summary, our findings indicated that low-population density regions experienced low geospatial access to surgical care, revealing significant regional variation according to population density in South Korea. Notably, low-density regions had higher proportions of patients aged ≥65 years and Medical Aid recipients, highlighting the need to reduce barriers to surgical care and improve EGS services in nearby community hospitals. While our findings suggest that travel distance is associated with differences in clinical outcomes, the interpretation of these results should be made with caution, as several important limitations must be considered. With population aging and the concentration of residents in urban areas, urgent action is required to implement detailed policy interventions aimed at improving the delivery of EGS services.
