Longitudinal analysis of stowaway microorganisms aboard modules and cargo spacecraft of the Chinese Space Station
Authors: Ying Zhang, Jinglin Ma, Lantao Zhang, Ran Wang, Xi Qu, Tao Wei, Jiangchuan Zhou, Weijie Liu, Xiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough microbial contamination in crewed spacecraft is well studied, quantitative assessment of microorganisms from spacecraft structures and cargo remains overlooked. Here, we investigate 165 microbial samples collected from five critical environments of China Space Station (CSS) missions undertaken in 2016–2024: ground assembly, cargo loading, crewed simulation cabin, test cabin, and on-orbit. The investigation aims to elucidate the impact of cargo- and cabin-derived microorganisms on the CSS microbial community while characterizing the station’s unique microbial profile.ResultsThe calculation results based on the microbial source tracking model revealed that the proportion of cabin-source microbes peaked during initial construction (14.02%) but declined after deployment, whereas cargo-source microbes reached 36.89%. The top three persistent microbial genera in orbit from both sources were Staphylococcus, Acinetobacter, and Corynebacterium. High-abundance CSS microorganisms in the orbital environment are commonly found in both humans and plants, and the few cabin- or cargo-derived microorganisms that entered the interior differed markedly from high-abundance microorganisms found in terrestrial environments. Cargo storage areas and controlled cleanrooms posed a high risk of microbial transfer into orbit, necessitating stricter sterilization measures.ConclusionsThese findings systematically characterize, for the first time, the dynamic patterns of structural/cargo-borne microbial contamination during CSS’s early phases, providing critical data for biosecurity protocols of long-duration missions. Introduction

Crewed space stations on-orbit provide an internal environment with temperature, humidity, nutrient, and atmospheric conditions suitable for microbial growth [1]. Multiple microorganisms have reportedly been identified in various space stations, including the retired Mir space station (MIR), and the operational International Space Station (ISS) and China Space Station (CSS) [2–5]. Some of these microorganisms (e.g., Staphylococcus, Enterobacter, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas and Bacillus) pose risks to astronaut health and space station longevity, highlighting the need for sustained attention to microbial ecological dynamics throughout space station operations.

Space stations function as a highly complex, artificial closed environment with three main sources of microorganisms: cabin, cargo, and humans [2, 6].

Cabin-source microorganisms remain in the cabin during the manufacturing and assembly of space station modules and are subsequently launched into space, representing the first microorganisms to enter the orbital space station. Microbial residues can be introduced at every stage of spacecraft equipment production, storage, use, and assembly, with air, machinery, personnel, and tools serving as potential vectors for the spread of microorganisms onto surfaces and internal structures [7]. Zhang et al. demonstrated that the assembly, integration, and test (AIT) hall, the transportation process, and the launch environment constituted the main sources of ground microbial contamination for the CSS [6]. Although CSS microbial control regulations mandate rigorous surface sterilization of all payloads prior to launch, design-related constraints allow a small number of residual microorganisms to persist, making complete decontamination unattainable.

Microorganisms inside the fully assembled space station primarily originate from cargo transport and astronaut activities. Cargo-source microorganisms enter the space station along with the cargo during transport, persisting on the outer packaging materials of cargo items or inside the cargo itself, which includes biological samples used for experiments [8] and non-sterilizable precision instruments. Additionally, during cargo loading phases, microorganisms on personnel responsible for loading cargo into the spacecraft may adhere to cargo surfaces.

Human-source microorganisms enter via astronauts and are spread throughout the station as the astronauts move around. The human body hosts over 10,000 types of microorganisms, with the microbial communities in the intestines, mouth, and skin being the most diverse. The intestines alone harbor trillions of bacteria [9–11]. Checinska Sielaff et al. tested the microbial composition on the surface of the ISS, finding that most were related to the human microbiome, with Enterobacteriaceae bacteria accounting for over 50%, followed by Bacillus (~ 13%) and Staphylococcus (~ 10%) [12]. Research by Avila-Herrera et al. and Voorhies et al. further showed that the astronauts’ own microbiota affected the microbial composition of ISS surfaces [13, 14].

Although previous studies have, to some extent, revealed the succession patterns of microorganisms and the characteristics of human-source contaminants during the later years of ISS operation, systematic research on these characteristics from cargo and cabin sources is lacking [12, 13, 15–17].

Quantifying the contributions of environmental sources to specific microbial communities (sinks), known as microbial source tracking (MST), is a longstanding challenge in microbiology. Developed in the early 1990 s, MST emerged as a tool to address contamination of aquatic environments by human and livestock fecal matter [18–20]. To further advance MST research, Professor Rob Knight’s research group developed the SourceTracker software in 2011 [21]. This tool employs Bayesian algorithms to track specific sources of microbial communities (e.g., soil, water) in environmental samples (referred to as the Sink) and estimate the proportional contributions of each source. There are two main approaches to MST: target-based (focused on detecting source-specific indicator species or chemical markers) and community-based (using community structure to evaluate similarities between sink samples and potential source environments) [22]. Two seminal research cases highlight the application of this tool in microbial source attribution. Lax et al. conducted a 6-week microbial monitoring study across seven households, employing the Source Tracker tool to analyze microbial origins and dynamics, microbial interactions between humans and their surroundings, and mechanisms of microbial transmission dynamics within indoor settings [23]. Fuhrmeister and colleagues investigated bacterial transmission pathways among soil, stored drinking water, and hands in 50 rural households in Bangladesh. Using SourceTracker, they quantified the shared microbial communities among these potential reservoirs, providing important evidence for understanding the transmission routes of pathogens at the household level [24].

This study marks the first application of the community-based MST method to trace the origins of cabin- and cargo-source microorganisms and investigate their impacts on the composition and structure of the microbial community within the CSS. From 2016 to 2024, we collected 165 microbial samples from the ground AIT center of the CSS, the ground-loading environment of the cargo spacecraft, the ground simulation cabin-crewed environment, and the on-orbit operation environment. Using molecular-biological and MST methods, this work focused on identifying and quantifying the contributions of cabin- and cargo-originating microbes to the microbial communities within the CSS. Our findings offer theoretical data that may inform microbial safety protocols enacted before the launch of crewed spacecraft modules and cargo missions in future human spaceflight programs.

Results

α-Diversity analysis

A total of 165 samples (detailed in Table 1) were collected, with the timeline of collection and corresponding space missions displayed in Fig. 1. These comprised 27 Tianjin AIT ground-source samples (Group A), 15 simulation-cabin samples (Group B), 28 Wenchang cargo ground-source samples (Group C), six test-cabin samples (Group D), and 89 on-orbit environmental samples collected by astronauts during five crewed Shenzhou (SZ) missions aboard the CSS (Groups SZ-12, SZ-13, SZ-14, SZ-15, and SZ-17). The shape of the rarefaction curve (Supplementary Fig. 1) indicated that our sequencing coverage was sufficient. Specifically, Group A samples exhibited a gradual increase and a consistently low counts of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) with increasing sequencing depth, reflecting their inherently low microbial diversity and abundance. In contrast, Group C samples, characterized by high microbial diversity and a large number of distinct ASVs, showed a continuous and rapid emergence of new variants as sequencing effort increased. To measure microbial diversity within each group, we applied four alpha diversity indices: Chao, Shannon evenness, Shannon, and Simpson: (Supplementary Fig. 2). Box plots of the Chao, Shannon, and Simpson indices showed that Groups B, C, D, SZ-12, and SZ-15 were generally more diverse than Groups A, SZ-13, SZ-14 and SZ-17. Based on the Shannon evenness indices, microbial communities in the SZ-13, SZ-14, and SZ-17 Groups exhibited relatively low evenness, with some species dominating.

SourceTracker analysis

SourceTracker modeling suggested that microorganisms in the on-orbit CSS could be traced to Groups A and C, but not to Groups B and D (Fig. 2a), indicating that only Groups A and C contributed to microbial introduction into the space station. Notably, the model output indicated that an estimated 36.89% of microorganisms in the on-orbit SZ-14 samples could be attributed to Group C, compared to an estimated ~ 14.02% from Group A for the SZ-12 samples.

To elucidate the precise ground origins and intra-station distribution patterns of these microorganisms, we implemented a refined regional categorization across all samples. This analysis quantified the microbial source attribution patterns from the four types of ground-source samples across the spatial compartments during multi-crew missions. Specifically, the model calculation indicated that the sanitation area in SZ-14 exhibited the highest microbial contribution rate (60.33%) from Group C, while the sanitation area of SZ-15 showed 35.72% microbial influx from the same source (Fig. 2b). In the condensate area of SZ-12 and the sanitary area of SZ-14, 25.81% and 24.81% of microbes, respectively, were traced to Group A.

Figure 2c presents a refined spatial partitioning analysis of microbes attributed to terrestrial Groups A–D, showing the following distributions. In SZ-12, 24.98% of the microbial community in the water condensate area was traced to the clean storage area of the Tianjin AIT hall. In SZ-14, 25.23% of the microbial influx in the sanitation area was estimated to originate from the pre-launch Tianhe Core Module (A_big_IIC). Microbial contributions from A_big_IIC were also estimated in the work areas of SZ-12, SZ-13, SZ-14, SZ-15, and SZ-17, accounting for 5.29%, 9.47%, 4.34%, 7.88%, and 5.69% of the communities, respectively. Further quantitative modeling suggested microbial migration from four localized areas (East [E], Entrance, West [W], and South [S]) of the Wenchang cargo (Group C) to the work zones in SZ-12, SZ-13, SZ-14, and SZ-15. Notably, 59.54% of microorganisms in the SZ-14 sanitary area were estimated to originate from the E area of Group C.

The analysis aimed to estimate the contributions of different ground-based sources using SourceTracker. Accordingly, all presented proportions are model-based calculations that provide reference values, not definitive evidence of microbial origin.

β-Diversity analysis

Because microbes from Groups B and D were absent from the on-orbit CSS, principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was performed on the remaining seven groups. As shown in Fig. 3a, Groups A and C formed distinct and well-separated clusters, indicating significant structural differences in the microbial communities. Group SZ-17 clustered independently, distant from both A and C, while SZ-12, SZ-13, SZ-14, and SZ-15 exhibited overlapping clustering, suggesting minimal microbial community differences among these missions. Five samples from Group A clustered closely with on-orbit samples from SZ-12, SZ-13, SZ-14, and SZ-15.

Analysis of shared ASVs between ground-source and on-orbit samples

Statistical analysis of shared ASVs was conducted across all nine groups, with overlaps illustrated in Fig. 4. The results revealed no common ASVs between ground-source Groups B and D with any of the on-orbit group samples. The highest ASV overlap occurred between Groups C and SZ-15 (196 ASVs), with the three most abundant shared variants representing Bacillus and Staphylococcus. Between Groups C and SZ-17, 111 ASVs overlapped, dominated by Streptococcus and Bacillus. Group A shared 63 ASVs with SZ-15, dominated by Bacillus, Streptococcus, and Methylobacterium, and 42 with SZ-17, where Bacillus and Stenotrophomonas were most abundant. Across all on-orbit and ground groups, the presence-absence-based shared-ASV pool was dominated by Acinetobacter (5.05%), Anaerococcus (4.73%), Bacteroides (3.79%), Streptococcus (3.79%), and Corynebacterium (2.84%) (Supplementary Table S1). These genera are commonly associated with skin, oral, or built environments, consistent with surface-linked transmission routes between terrestrial and orbital habitats. Building on these findings, a heatmap was generated to quantify both the absolute counts and relative percentages of shared ASVs between ground-source and on-orbit samples, relative to the total ASVs detected in on-orbit samples (Fig. 5). Notably, shared ASVs between Groups A and SZ-14 accounted for 44% of the total ASVs in SZ-14, while Group C shared 43% with SZ-14 and 39% with SZ-15. When viewed in terms of orbit-side abundance, normalized by total sum scaling (TSS; Fig. 5b), the genus-level contributions of the ground-orbit shared ASV pool were dominated by Bacillus (17.8%), Methylobacterium (17.3%), Staphylococcus (11.7%), Sphingomonas (7.0%), and Pantoea (6.7%) (Supplementary Table S2), highlighting the prevalence of stress-tolerant, surface-associated taxa in orbital habitats.

Discussion

As expected, statistical analysis based on SourceTracker model results demonstrated that microorganisms from ground simulation cabin samples (Group B) and test cabin samples (Group D) did not enter the interior of the on-orbit CSS (Fig. 2a, b). Figures 4 and 5 also support this conclusion. However, because these results are derived from SourceTracker model-based calculations, it is possible that the observed pattern reflects limitations of the model itself. Setting aside this possibility, there are three main explanations for the inability to trace microorganisms from these groups in the on-orbit CSS environment. First, the simulation module is a 1:1 replica of the CSS internal structure, but was assembled by personnel entirely distinct from those involved in the on-orbit CSS. Additionally, neither the simulation module nor the thermal-vacuum test cabin was ever launched into orbit. Previous studies demonstrated that microbial contamination on spacecraft surfaces predominantly originates from the hands of assembly personnel during construction [6]. Thus, microbial communities retained in the simulation/test modules during assembly likely differ considerably from those in the on-orbit CSS. Second, numerous studies have confirmed that astronauts are the primary source of microorganisms in orbital space stations [12–14]. Crew students participating in experiments in the ground simulation cabin (Group B samples) have not flown on-orbit, and their microbiomes differ from those of astronauts with on-orbit mission experience. Consequently, microorganisms carried by these students have never entered an operational space station. Similarly, the test cabin represented by Group D had never hosted astronauts, eliminating the possibility of astronaut-derived microbial contamination in that environment. These factors explain the differences between Groups B and D in terms of human-source microbial contamination of the on-orbit CSS. Third, the supply goods in the ground simulation cabin (Group B) are entirely distinct from on-orbit cargo, while the test cabin (Group D) has no cargo supplies, thus eliminating the possibility of supply-source microbial contamination. Consequently, substantial differences likely exist between Groups B and D in terms of supply-source contamination of the on-orbit CSS. Together, these factors underscore the substantial disparities between Groups B and D and the on-orbit CSS, likely explaining the absence of traceable microorganisms from these groups in the on-orbit CSS environment. Thus, samples from Groups B and D can be indirectly regarded as negative controls for the SourceTracker analysis.

SourceTracker model analysis revealed a gradually decreasing contribution of cabin-source (Group A) microbes to the on-orbit CSS microbial community across the SZ-12, SZ-13, SZ-14, and SZ-15 missions (Fig. 2a). This trend was likely driven by three interrelated factors. First, the on-orbit cabin environment, is a closed man-made environment, characterized by microgravity and sustained low-dose radiation exposure, differs markedly from ground conditions. These spatial environmental parameters likely impose selective pressures on the survival and proliferation of cabin-source microbial contaminants, preferentially filtering strains with specific tolerance traits to space environments. For instance, antibiotic resistance genes and multiple heavy metal resistance genes were identified in microorganisms from the ISS during antibiotic resistance screening [25]. Some resistance genes can integrate into microbial genomes via horizontal gene transfer, enhancing environmental adaptability [25]. Furthermore, weekly routine disinfection procedures within the cabin ensure that only microorganisms resistant to these treatments survive. These standard disinfection methods include wiping surfaces with disinfectant wipes containing quaternary ammonium compounds, benzalkonium chloride, or 95% medical alcohol, which have bactericidal and/or bacteriostatic effects. Consistently, numerous studies have demonstrated that space environments accelerate the formation of microbial biofilms [26]. Collectively, these findings suggest that only microorganisms adapted to space conditions persist in the cabin, with non-adapted strains gradually perishing. A second explanation for the observed trend involves the cessation of module launch operations, which halted the introduction of cabin-source microorganisms into the operational CSS. Third, the continuous input of human-source and cargo-source microbial contaminants may gradually displace cabin-source microorganisms. Astronauts periodically inhabit the cabin, continuously releasing microorganisms from their skin, oral cavities, and gastrointestinal tracts into the environment. A metagenomic analysis by Voorhies et al. confirmed that > 70% of microorganisms in the ISS environment originated from the skin surface microbiota of astronauts, demonstrating the significant and time-dependent impact of crew-associated microorganisms on cabin microbial communities [14]. Concurrently, ongoing cargo resupply missions ensure persistent contamination from supply-source microorganisms. Cabin-source microbial populations lacking competitive survival advantages in this environment are gradually replaced by human- and cargo-source contaminants. However, it is noteworthy that samples collected during the SZ-17 mission showed a slight rebound in the contribution from Group A. This implies that cabin-source microbial communities are unlikely to be completely replaced within the orbital environment.

By computational estimation of which specific Tianjin ground-source areas contributed microbial contaminants to corresponding sections of the on-orbit CSS (Fig. 2c, b), we found that > 20% of microorganisms in the condensed water systems, sanitary areas, and workspaces were traced to two areas: the clean storage area of Tianjin’s AIT hall and the pre-launch flight components of the Tianhe Core Module. High residual cabin-source microbial contamination in the SZ-12 water condensate area may be attributable to two factors. The first is the persistently high-humidity environment in this area, which likely facilitated biofilm formation by Group A microorganisms (e.g., Bacillus, Actinobacteria). Biofilm development and the confined condensate accumulation area reportedly reduced the efficacy of disinfection measures, enabling partial microbial survival in the on-orbit CSS [26–28]. Second, Group A microorganisms embedded within sealing materials during assembly may have gradually leached into the condensate, forming a long-term contamination reservoir. Persistent cabin-source microbial residues in the on-orbit CSS workspace likely stem from the pre-launch flight components of Tianhe Core Module, where dense electronic equipment and instruments create inaccessible niches (e.g., component gaps, internal cavities) resistant to thorough cleaning, allowing Group A microbial accumulation. Human- and cargo-associated microorganisms struggle to reach such inaccessible areas, allowing residual microbes in these regions to become dominant populations that are subsequently dispersed across cabin surfaces via air circulation during equipment operation. Previous studies reported that microbial biofilms tended to accumulate in electronic devices (e.g., radiators, air conditioning units) on the MIR and water recovery systems on the ISS, with microbial aerosols potentially disseminated via airflow during equipment operation [26, 29–31]. In our SourceTracker analyses of sanitary area samples from the on-orbit CSS, microbial contributions traceable to cabin-source microorganisms peaked during the SZ-14 mission, declining to near-zero levels in SZ-15 and SZ-17. Crevices in sanitary equipment and drainage conduits may act as “microbial refugia”, where cabin-source microorganisms evade cleaning protocols and spread to surfaces. Notably, following the SZ-14 mission, the frequency and intensity of sanitary area disinfection in the CSS were enhanced, effectively reducing residual cabin-source microbes.

SourceTracker computational estimation revealed that cargo-source microorganisms (Group C) contributed approximately 5% to the microbial communities during the early SZ-12 and SZ-13 missions, peaked at 36.89% in SZ-14, sharply declined to 17.01% in SZ-15, and further dropped to 1.52% in SZ-17 (Fig. 2a). Two primary factors may explain these findings. First, despite the stringent cleanliness controls (e.g., routine disinfection) for spacecraft assembly cleanrooms, their microbial communities remain stable over months to years. Over specific time intervals, the core composition of microbial communities within spacecraft assembly cleanrooms has shown minimal fluctuation [32, 33]. Thus, despite multiple resupplies of cargo spacecraft to the CSS, cargo and cargo compartments from different batches—stored in the Wenchang cleanroom prior to launch—carried surface-associated microorganisms with highly similar profiles, resulting in sustained homogeneous inputs. Second, during the SZ-14 mission, consecutive launches of cargo spacecraft (e.g., TZ-4 and TZ-5) likely introduced similar Group C microorganisms into the CSS, causing their cumulative contribution to peak at 36.89%. Over time, structural shifts in the CSS microbial community gradually reduced the proportion of these Wenchang-derived microorganisms within the on-orbit environment. This finding underscores the importance of strict temporal alignment in MST studies, highlighting the need to chronologically correlate pre-launch ground environments with corresponding on-orbit conditions. Future efforts should prioritize long-term, continuous sampling at the Wenchang cleanroom to establish a dynamic database of microbial communities at the launch site, enabling precise characterization of temporal patterns governing ground-derived microbial influx into the CSS microbiome.

A subsequent refined SourceTracker model estimation of on-orbit samples and Group C categorized by specific region, revealed the following. Microbial communities in the sanitation area of SZ-14 showed 59.54% traceability to Zone E of the Wenchang cleanroom. In the SZ-15 mission, microorganisms originating from various areas of the Wenchang cleanroom were detected in the workspace, sleeping, and sanitation areas, with 11.74% of microorganisms in the sanitation area traced to Zone S of the cleanroom (Fig. 2c). The predominance of Wenchang cargo-source microorganisms from Zones E and S may be attributable to the temporary use of these areas for storage during spacecraft preparation phases. Microorganisms in Zones E and S may enter the CSS by adhering to the surfaces of cargo packages. Zone S spans the hatch interfaces between the propelling and cargo modules, where environmental microbes are most readily transported into both modules during launch. Additionally, cargo loaded into the cargo module carries trace microorganisms that are dispersed across multiple cabin regions during on-orbit supply relocation by astronauts.

There are certain limitations to our SourceTracker-based analysis of microbes originating from cabins and cargo. For instance, incomplete spatial sampling (insufficient quantity and coverage of microbial samples from source regions) may lead to partial source attribution. Furthermore, future expansion of the CSS with additional modules (potentially forming cross-shaped or more extended configurations) will necessitate comprehensive sampling of cabin- and cargo-source microbial communities to validate and extend the current findings. Subsequent research should prioritize systematic microbial monitoring across all pre-launch assembly areas and expanded orbital modules to refine MST analyses for evolving station architectures.

The above discussions are all based on the calculation results of the SourceTracker model. They only provide reference values and cannot be used as absolute conclusions.

Microbial communities in the ISS exhibit unique ecological characteristics [34]. Our PCoA similarly revealed significant compositional differences between on-orbit CSS microbial communities and their ground counterparts (Fig. 3). This finding is consistent with the results reported by Rocha et al., who also observed a clear distinction between the ISS microbiome and that of cargo vehicles [35]. We hypothesize that this outcome is attributable to either limited transfer of microbes into the CSS or restricted survival of such microbes in the orbital environment. The former is less plausible, as microbial ingress into the CSS during assembly is non-selective, with all ground microbial taxa in cleanrooms having an equal likelihood of entering the station. Instead, the stark environmental contrast between space and Earth likely imposes ecological filtering, reshaping microbial abundance and fostering novel community structures [34]. Our statistical analysis of the abundance of dominant ground species in the CSS (Fig. 7) supports this conclusion, revealing a marked decline in their abundance and a loss of dominance within the station microbiome.

PCoA plots showed that samples from the SZ groups cluster by mission crew rather than functional area, further demonstrating temporal divergence but not spatial partitioning in CSS microbial communities across the missions (Fig. 3a–3c). This observation aligns with Urbaniak et al.’s 2022 findings on microbial succession patterns in the ISS [15]. The lack of microbial structural differences between functional areas likely stems from the highly enclosed environment of the CSS, where constant temperature and humidity result in essentially uniform environmental conditions. These standardized environmental parameters minimize physical variations in microbial growth across different areas. Frequent astronaut movement between work, sleeping, and sanitary areas further homogenizes these communities via airflows and surface contact. The temporal differences in microbial communities within the CSS mainly resulted from successive crew rotations and cargo resupplies that introduced diverse human- and cargo-source microorganisms. Our previous research revealed that 58.8% of in-flight microbial specimens were human-derived [36]. However, compared with inter-crew variations, the structural microbial community differences between ground and on-orbit samples exhibited more pronounced dissimilarities.

Of the ASVs identified, 274 ASVs were detected in Tianjin ground-source samples (Group A) and 2598 in Wenchang ground-source samples (Group C), with only 65 shared between the two sites (Figs. 4 and 5a). PCoA plots further revealed distinct microbial community compositions between the Tianjin and Wenchang cleanrooms (Fig. 3). This divergence primarily stemmed from differences in environmental conditions, human activities, and microbial sources at each location. Tianjin and Wenchang are respectively situated in northern and southern China, and experience distinct climatic conditions. Because microbial communities within cleanrooms may partially originate from local natural environments, these regional environmental differences contribute to divergent microbial compositions. Additionally, variations in personnel flow and operational protocols directly influence the microbiomes of cleanrooms. Owing to frequent cargo missions, the Wenchang cleanroom receives diverse materials from multiple regions, introducing richer microbial diversity, whereas microbial inputs to the Tianjin cleanroom are comparatively more homogeneous. Consequently, divergent microbial growth, dispersal, and community assembly resulted in unique microbiomes at each site. Although CSS modules assembled in Tianjin were ultimately transported to Wenchang for launch, the small number of microorganisms carried from Tianjin to Wenchang was insufficient to alter the microbial community structure of the Wenchang environment. Thus, Tianjin and Wenchang can be treated as independent cabin- and cargo-source microbial reservoirs.

Analysis of the distribution and relative abundances of ASVs shared between cabin-source microorganisms (Tianjin, Group A) and on-orbit samples revealed no discernible patterns, with both metrics exhibiting fluctuating trends (Figs. 4 and 5). One explanation for this observation is that cabin-source microorganisms in the CSS predominantly comprise extremotolerant species that persist through incomplete eradication during assembly phases and remain latent in hard-to-clean regions of the on-orbit CSS. Their abundance is dynamically regulated by subsequent disinfection protocols and environmental fluctuations. Another possible explanation involves fluctuations in cabin-source microbial abundance that may correlate with ongoing inputs of human- and cargo-source microorganisms, where newly introduced microbes compete with pre-existing cabin-source species, destabilizing their population dynamics.

A comparative analysis of shared ASVs between on-orbit samples across missions and cargo-source (Wenchang, Group C) microorganisms was carried out by evaluating their relative abundances in both environments (Figs. 7g and 8). Most surviving cargo-source microorganisms showed no significant differences in relative abundance between ground and on-orbit environments. Additionally, these shared microbial species were non-dominant in both ground and on-orbit settings. Dominant species from the ground-based cleanroom lost their ecological predominance following transfer to the on-orbit environment, likely attributable to altered selective pressures. Although some microorganisms from the Wenchang cleanroom were carried into the CSS, they did not substantially alter the microbial community structure within the station. Microbial communities in the CSS likely underwent niche-specific competition and selection, diminishing the competitive advantage of cargo-source species in the new environment. Our findings suggest that the original community structure of cargo-source microorganisms does not substantially impact the composition of established microbial consortia within the CSS.

In-depth investigation of on-orbit microbial ecology in the CSS revealed a notable phenomenon: consistent detection of Staphylococcus(ASV13) and Lactobacillus(ASV 103) in samples obtained from all five crew missions (Fig. 6). Staphylococcus is widely distributed on human skin and in the nasal cavity, and can survive on environmental surfaces for long periods. Lactobacillus is a Gram-positive bacillus that inhabits human and animal mucosal environments, including the intestines, oral cavity, and vagina. It is a key bacterium essential for maintaining microecological balance, with most Lactobacillus species recognized as harmless or beneficial to humans Their persistent survival in the extreme orbital environment implies robust adaptability to spaceflight stressors.

The shared ASV analysis (Figs. 4 and 5) revealed that three sources (Tianjin ground-source, Wenchang ground-source, and on-orbit CSS) collectively exhibited 40 shared ASVs. Among these, four bacterial species, which were detected in at least three missions and exhibited relative abundances ≥ 0.4% across all on-orbit samples, were selected for detailed investigation (Fig. 6).

The two most abundant (0.88% and 0.8%) species belonged to the genus Staphylococcus. These bacteria are ubiquitous in natural environments, particularly on human and animal skin and mucosal surfaces. Staphylococci also inhabit soil, water, air, and the surfaces of various daily necessities. Despite spaceflight stressors such as microgravity and radiation, Staphylococci exhibit enhanced environmental resilience through biofilm formation [37]. Research by Schiwon et al. on ISS-derived Staphylococci revealed their capacity to encode multiple antibiotic resistance genes, exhibit high horizontal gene transfer efficiency, and form robust biofilms [38]. Faced with ecological competition, this genus employs diverse survival strategies, including the production of multiple bacteriocins to suppress neighboring microbes [39, 40] and metabolic flexibility that confers an advantage in resource-limited settings. Its highly plastic genome enables rapid evolution via horizontal gene transfer and transposon-mediated integration of antibiotic-resistance genes [41–43]. Together, these traits have endowed Staphylococcus with strong competitive and survival abilities across diverse ecosystems.

Acinetobacter is a Gram-negative bacterium that is widely distributed in natural environments (e.g., soil, water, flora and fauna) and capable of colonizing healthy human skin and the oropharynx [44]. This bacterium shows remarkable adaptability, allowing it to persist even in strictly controlled cleanrooms. For example, Bashir et al. detected A. lwoffii in several NASA facilities [45]. A. lwoffii thrives via biofilm-mediated colonization on surfaces, enhancing its resistance to environmental stressors [46, 47]. Its competitive dominance in soil microbiomes [48].

Corynebacterium, a skin-commensal bacterium prevalent in ground environments, typically remains non-pathogenic, but may cause opportunistic infections in immunocompromised hosts. Corynebacterium has been detected in dust samples not only aboard the ISS [49] but also during the CSS SZ-12, SZ-15, and SZ-17 missions, confirming its ability to survive long term in the unique environment of orbital habitats.

The aforementioned bacteria, widely distributed across natural environments and human symbiotic systems, exhibit strong adaptability to the specialized conditions of the space-station environment. These survival advantages enable potential dissemination within the station via cargo resupply or crew activities. Notably, these microorganisms pose multifaceted risks in the confined orbital environment. Consequently, both ground-based and orbital microbial monitoring programs must be implemented, prioritizing these species for enhanced prevention and control measures. Future research should further investigate their survival mechanisms under microgravity and space radiation, while also characterizing their ecological roles and evaluating their potential applications within the space-station ecosystem.

The dominant microbial species and their relative abundances in the CSS underwent multiple successions across the SZ-12 to SZ-17 missions (Fig. 7). For example, during the SZ-12 mission, Pantoea (ASV49 and ASV40, ~ 28%) and Norcardiopsis (ASV67, ~ 10%) dominated, while SZ-13 was primarily dominated by Pseudomonas (ASV2, ~ 73%). The SZ-14 mission saw co-dominance of Methylobacterium (ASV7, ~ 28%) and Pseudomonas (ASV2, ~ 29%), with SZ-15 shifting to the genus Bacillus (ASV5 and ASV6, ~ 37%). Finally, the SZ-17 mission had Pseudomonas (ASV1, ~ 65%) as the dominant bacterial species. The quantitative differences in these high-abundance ASVs between ground-based and on-orbit environments were all highly significant (p < 0.05 as determined by Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by post hoc Dunn’s tests with Bonferroni correction). These shifts may be linked to changes in onboard environmental conditions, crew activities, and microbial interactions.

Pantoea, commonly found in ground environments (e.g., plant rhizospheres, soil, water, and humans [50]), exhibits survival advantages under nutrient stress and can act as an opportunistic human pathogen [50, 51]. Singh et al. reported that bacteria from the genus Pantoea were detected across all three ISS missions, exhibiting continuity and persistence while dominating the station microbiome [16]. Studies have shown that P. agglomerans can grow in nutrient-poor environments [52], form biofilms [51], and establish ecological dominance by producing antibiotics that eliminate competing microorganisms [51].

Norcardiopsis is widely found in soil and water bodies, and can also be isolated from various plants and animals, including humans. As a type of actinomycete, it carries an abundance of genes involved in secondary metabolite synthesis, enabling it to form biofilms on metal surfaces. This trait confers a competitive advantage within microbial communities and enhances its resistance to environmental pressures [53]. Methylobacterium, commonly found in soil, plants, water bodies, and the human body, utilizes energy sources including methanol, methane, organic acids, and carbohydrates [54–56]. Yang et al. found that Methylobacterium species can form biofilms with other microorganisms in recycled drinking water from the ISS [30]. Our preliminary research also identified Methylobacterium as a biomarker in the Wentian work area of SZ-14 [36]. Similarly, in 2019, Checinska Sielaff et al. tested microbial samples from three ISS missions over 14 months, finding wide distribution and dominance of Methylobacterium on the ISS [12].

Pseudomonas is among the most diverse and widespread bacterial genera, with species found in sediments, clinical samples, plants or their rhizosphere, sick animals, water, soil, oceans, and deserts. Some Pseudomonas species can cause diseases in various host organisms [57]. These bacteria possess rich metabolic pathways and adaptive genes that enable the utilization of diverse organic compounds for carbon and energy, while stress resistance genes [57] support their survival in extreme environments, such as microgravity and radiation aboard space stations. One species, P. veronii, can form biofilms [58], enhancing its adhesion and colonization capabilities on space station surfaces, contributing to its dominance within onboard microbial communities. In a previous study, we found wide distribution of Pseudomonas bacteria throughout the CSS [36]. Here, Pseudomonas once again dominated in the CSS during the SZ-17 mission. This genus has also been detected multiple times in the MIR and the ISS [2, 3, 59].

Bacteria of the genus Bacillus are distributed in soil, water, and air, and their highly resistant spores enable long-term survival under adverse conditions. In the MIR and multiple modules of the ISS, Bacillus bacteria have been frequently detected, occupying an important position within the onboard microbial community [2–5, 60]. This prevalence underscores their adaptability, highlighting the potential risk of Bacillus in the space station environment, such as forming biofilms that can lead to material corrosion or biofouling [61].

Competition among dominant bacteria arises from limitations in resources and space, encompassing both direct competition for nutrients and more complex mechanisms, such as niche competition, antimicrobial secretion, and biofilm formation. The inhibitory relationships and succession patterns among dominant bacteria are not yet fully understood. Future research could involve ground-based simulations of the orbital space-station environment, incorporating conditions such as microgravity and high radiation. Observation of the growth dynamics and interactions of dominant bacterial strains under these conditions could provide a scientific basis for managing microbial communities in orbit.

Research has shown that the limited microbial diversity on the ISS may negatively impact astronaut health [34]. This is because the human immune system requires periodic external stimulation, which may be suppressed in the space-station environment, thereby impairing immune function. However, maintaining a microbial community structure as diverse as that of Earth’s environment aboard a space station could increase the risk of biofilm formation, leading to equipment corrosion and system blockages. The presence of bacteria such as Pantoea, Pseudomonas, and Bacillus, which possess pathogenic and biofilm-forming capabilities, may threaten crew health and equipment (The common bacterial genera in each region are shown in Supplementary Tables 4–6). We recommend that targeted disinfection strategies be implemented based on the survival traits of these potentially harmful bacteria. Meanwhile, beneficial microbes such as Lactobacillus, which persist long term in the CSS and promote human health, should be preserved. Future microbial control strategies for spacecraft should prioritize minimizing microbial contamination in the propelling module areas of the Wenchang cargo spacecraft cleanrooms. In parallel, advancing selective sterilization or probiotic-based approaches could help establish a beneficial on-orbit microbial community that poses no threat to the station. Furthermore, it is critical to emphasize that space radiation and microgravity can induce genetic mutations in microorganisms, potentially converting beneficial strains into harmful variants under specific environmental conditions. Thus, continuous monitoring of the functional dynamics of beneficial microbes is imperative.

In this study, all samples collected on-orbit were stored at room temperature for 1 to 5 days before being returned to Earth. Immediately upon their return, they were transferred to a cold-chain environment for subsequent processing. It should be specifically noted that this difference could lead to discrepancies between the results of this study and those of International Space Station studies that employed on-orbit freezing protocols. Therefore, when comparing with the latter, the potential influence of this room-temperature storage condition must be carefully considered.

Methods

Sample collection methods and sampling sites

Of the 165 samples, the ground-sources samples were categorized into four major Groups (A–D) and the on-orbit samples into five major groups by mission (SZ-12/13/14/15/17). Group A comprised 27 Tianjin AIT ground-source samples from the assembly phase of the CSS in Tianjin. On January 14, 2016, 24 air and surface environmental samples were collected from the structural components of the CSS Core Module (a model for reliability testing) and the Tianjin AIT cleanroom during its assembly phase. Detailed sampling locations are shown in Fig. 9 (sample A11 was collected from the hands of assembly personnel and is not displayed in the figure). Sampling sites in the AIT hall included the logistics channel (LC), clean storage (CS) area, air shower room (AS), dressing room (DR), and encapsulation hall (EH). In March 2019, three surface environmental samples were collected from the flight components of Tianhe Core Module during its ground assembly phase, prior to launch. Detailed sampling locations are marked in Fig. 10 (triangular symbols). For both campaigns, sterile cotton swabs were used to perform surface wipe sampling: the swab heads were pre-moistened with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), wiped across the entire target area, and then immersed in sterile PBS for transport to the laboratory for further processing. Airborne microbial samples (2 m3 for each sample) were collected using a Sartorius Airport MD8 gelatin filter sampler (Sartorius AG, Göttingen, Germany). After air sampling, filters were removed from the support mesh and completely dissolved in sterile PBS for subsequent processing. Because these two sets of samples (27 in all) were collected from the Tianjin AIT cleanroom, and the structural and flight components of the Tianhe Core Module are identical in terms of material composition, dimensions, structural design, assembly processes, and personnel involved, they were categorized into the same group.

Group B comprised 15 simulation-cabin samples collected from a ground-simulation module. In September 2018, a 50-day ground-simulation crewed experiment for astronauts was carried out at the Beijing Astronaut Research and Training Center. Three male astronauts who had never participated in orbital missions completed the experiment in a simulation cabin matching the size and layout of the CSS Tianhe Core Module. The samples were collected utilizing “equivalent sampling plates” made of the same materials as the simulation-cabin surfaces, with a size of 11 cm × 11 cm (121 cm2). These plates were placed in hard-to-reach areas within the cabin, retrieved at scheduled intervals, and transported to the laboratory for processing.

Group C comprised 28 cargo ground-source samples from the cleanroom of a cargo spacecraft. On May 10, 2023, the Tianzhou-6 (TZ-6) cargo spacecraft was launched from the Wenchang Satellite Launch Center in China. Prior to the launch, on April 3, 2023, 28 environmental samples were collected from the cargo-spacecraft-handling cleanroom at Wenchang. Specific sampling locations are shown in Fig. 9. This sampling targeted environmental microorganisms in the cleanrooms storing the cargo compartment of the spacecraft and uplink cargo packages. The cleanroom was divided into the E, Entrance, W, and S functional areas based on area-specific operational requirements. For microbial sample collection, the walls and floors of the cleanroom were wiped using sterile pure-water wipes. After sampling, the wipes were placed into sterile sealed bags and transported to the laboratory for further processing.

Group D comprised six post-thermal-vacuum-test cabin samples from the test cabin of the Tianhe Core Module. In June 2017, a thermal vacuum test was conducted on the Tianhe Core Module test cabin within the Tianjin AIT Hall. The primary objective was to evaluate and validate the performance and reliability of onboard equipment and instruments under extreme space-like environmental conditions. Later, on June 21, 2017, six microbial samples were collected by wiping the cabin’s internal surfaces with sterile cotton swabs pre-moistened with sterile PBS. After sampling, the swabs were placed into sterile PBS solution and transported to the laboratory for further processing.

The 89 on-orbit environmental samples collected by astronauts aboard the CSS were grouped by mission: SZ-12/13/14/15/17. The period from the launch of the Tianhe Core Module on April 29, 2021 to the completion of the “T-shaped” basic configuration on November 3, 2022 constituted the construction phase of the CSS, after which the CSS entered its initial operational phase. During these two periods, five comprehensive microbial sampling campaigns were conducted at designated locations within the orbital CSS modules: Tianhe Core Module (work, sleeping, and sanitary areas), Wentian Laboratory Module (work and sleeping areas), and Mengtian Laboratory Module (work area). The sampling sites and corresponding timeline for these five sampling operations, which were executed by five independent crew teams (SZ-12/13/14/15/17) within 1 week prior to their scheduled Earth return, are provided in Fig. 10. The SZ-12 crew collected microbial samples from 12 sites in the Tianhe Core Module: 8 from work areas, 3 from sleeping areas, and 1 from a water-condensate area. The SZ-13 crew collected samples from 21 sites in the Tianhe Core Module: 14 from work areas, 6 from sleeping areas, and 1 from a sanitary area. During the SZ-14 mission, sampling was expanded to three modules: 14 samples from the Tianhe Core Module (5 from work areas, 5 from sanitary areas, 4 from sleeping areas), 5 samples from the Wentian Laboratory Module (3 from work areas, 2 from sleeping areas), and 2 samples from work areas in the Mengtian Laboratory Module. The SZ-15 crew collected samples from 4 sites in the Tianhe Core Module (2 from work areas, 1 from a sleeping area, 1 from a sanitary area), 4 sites in the Wentian Laboratory Module (2 from work areas, 2 from sleeping areas), and 3 work-area sites in the Mengtian Laboratory Module. Finally, during the SZ-17 mission, samples were collected from 20 sites in the Tianhe Core Module (7 from work areas, 6 from sleeping areas, 7 from sanitary areas), 2 work-area sites in the Wentian Laboratory Module, and 2 work-area sites in the Mengtian Laboratory Module.

For on-orbit microbial sample collection on the inner module surface, two methods were employed: (1) sterile pure water wipes used for surface wipe sampling at designated locations, after which the wipes were placed into sterile sealed bags for storage, for 1–5 days, and returned to ground via crewed spacecraft; and (2) equivalent material sheets comprising a membrane made of the same material as the wall of the module at the sampling site (size: 5 cm × 5 cm = 25 cm2), which astronauts affixed to the surfaces of specified module areas on the day of entry into the module, transferred into sterile sealed bags 1–2 days prior to their scheduled return to Earth, and transported back via crewed spacecraft.

Upon returning to Earth aboard the manned spacecraft, the orbital samples were transported to the laboratory at 4 °C within 24 h of landing, followed by sample processing and microbial analysis within the subsequent 12 h. Ground samples collected in Tianjin were transported to the laboratory, processed and analyzed within 3 h, while those from Wenchang were processed within 10 h of delivery. Throughout storage and transportation, all samples were maintained at room temperature and were not subjected to any sterilization procedures.

The processing timeline of our orbital samples strictly adheres to the core principle established in the studies by Singh et al. and Checinska Sielaff et al. [12, 16], which emphasizes that: to ensure the reliability of analytical results, we must strive to achieve rapid analysis to the greatest extent possible.‌ Specifically, all samples were first stored in an in-orbit, room-temperature environment for approximately 1–5 days. After landing, the samples were transported to the laboratory via cold chain within 24 h and processed within 12 h.

Sample processing

A flow chart illustrating the processing methods for the five types of samples employed in this study is provided in Fig. 11. Identical sampling tools were used for both on-orbit and ground sampling. These included commercial cotton swabs (Puritan Medical Products, USA) and laboratory-prepared sterilized cotton wipes moistened with purified water. Furthermore, the same protocol for sample pretreatment and DNA extraction was consistently applied across all samples. Samples collected using cotton swabs, equivalent material sheets, and wipes were processed as follows. The swab, equivalent material sheet, or wipe was placed into a tube containing sterile PBS solution, vortexed for 20 min, and spun dry using a mesh liner tube with low-speed centrifugation at 3500 rpm (2220 × g) for 10 min, and was ultimately removed after centrifugation to obtain the microbial-containing sample eluate. For the equivalent material plates, sterile cotton swabs pre-moistened with PBS were used to wipe the plate surface. After wiping the entire area, the swab was placed into a tube containing sterile PBS, vortexed to dislodge microorganisms from the swab into the solution, and then removed from the suspension, yielding the microbial-containing sample eluate. For air sampler-collected samples, the filter membrane was removed from the support mesh and fully dissolved in sterile PBS. The suspension was centrifuged, resuspended in 1 mL sterile PBS, and half of the suspension was used for molecular analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted from all five types of sample eluates using the E.Z.N.A. Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA) and used for high-throughput sequencing. The quality and concentration of DNA were detected using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., USA). DNA samples were stored at −20 ℃ for subsequent experiments. For microbial samples collected in orbit, the extracted DNA concentration typically ranges from 1 to 20 ng/μL, while for those collected in the ground assembly facility, the extracted DNA concentration is usually within the range of 50–100 ng/μL. All samples underwent sample processing and DNA extraction under ground ambient temperature conditions.

Sample processing flow chart

Negative controls, including no-template controls (three replicates) and templates derived from unused sample material (three replicates), were also subjected to PCR amplification. No bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequences were detected in these controls, indicating the absence of contamination in both PCR reagents and sample DNA.

Genomic sequencing

Microbial genomic sequencing was performed using region-specific amplification: the V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene for bacteria and the ITS region (spanning the small and large rRNA subunits) for fungi. Sequencing libraries were then constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Inc., USA). Paired-end sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene was performed on the Illumina MiSeq system (Illumina MiSeq, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols to generate raw sequencing data. Initial processing involved removing 3′-end sequencing primer adapters using Cutadapt (v1.18) with key parameters: -O 5 (overlap length) and -m 50 (minimum read length). The -m50 overlapping paired-end reads were then merged into single contiguous sequences using PEAR (v0.9.8) based on read overlap relationships. Based on the barcode sequence and primer sequence of each sample, reads were demultiplexed from the merged data, and corrected for sequence orientation. PRINSEQ (v0.20.4) was employed for stringent quality control, including trimming bases with Phred quality scores < 20 at read termini, applying a 10-bp sliding window to truncate sequences when average window quality fell below 20, and removing reads containing ambiguous bases (N), short lengths, or low-complexity regions to retain high-quality sequences. Denoising was performed using the dada2 package (v1.14.0) in R (v3.6.0), clustering sequences at 100% similarity to correct or remove low-quality reads and eliminate chimeras. Dereplication of denoised sequences produced an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table. Taxonomic classification of the ASVs was conducted using the RDP classifier (v2.12) with the RDP database.

Data analysis

Based on ASV clustering, rarefaction analysis was performed using Mothur (v1.43.0), with rarefaction curves generated in R (v3.6.0). Following the removal of singleton ASVs, all samples were rarefied to the minimum sequencing depth. Standard bioinformatic and statistical analyses were then performed on the resulting dataset. α-Diversity indices were calculated using Mothur, and all results and visualizations were generated in R (v3.6.0).

SourceTracker [21] uses a Bayesian classification model with Gibbs sampling to estimate the proportion of tag sequences from defined source environments that contribute to sink environments. It was employed to calculate the contribution of ground-source samples (sources) in Groups A–D to on-orbit samples (sinks) in Groups SZ-12/13/14/15/17 using default settings with a dilution depth of 1000. To evaluate source homogeneity and independence, leave-one-out cross-validation was performed to predict the sample type of each source sample when excluded from the model. This analysis was repeated three times, and the average was calculated as previously described [62, 63]. SourceTracker II (v2.0.1; http://github.com/biota/sourcetracker2) was then applied to different groupings, and resultant data were visualized as heatmaps via the CNSknowall platform (https://cnsknowall.com).

β-Diversity was visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on a Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix. The significance of predefined group structures in explaining the observed variance was assessed via permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations. Upset plots and Venn diagrams were generated based on ASV clustering results. To visualize shared ASVs between ground and on-orbit samples, taxonomic abundance heatmaps were plotted using R (v4.4.2). Heatmaps of the top 20 most abundant ASVs per group and bar plots of relative taxonomic composition were generated via the CNSknowall platform. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were applied to compare ASV abundance between Group C and on-orbit samples using R (v4.4.2), with results visualized as scatter plots. Each ASV is color-coded according to the statistical significance of its abundance difference between environments, as determined by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction.

Conclusions

In conclusion, cabin-source microorganisms predominated in the onboard microbial community during the initial construction phase of the CSS. Upon entering the early operational phase, their relative abundances gradually declined (but did not completely disappear), while cargo-source microorganisms became increasingly prevalent. Microorganisms prevalent in ground cleanroom environments were undetectable within the on-orbit CSS. The main cabin- and cargo-derived genera exhibiting long-term persistence and relatively high abundance in the CSS were Staphylococcus, Acinetobacter, and Corynebacterium. Microbial contaminants originating from cargo supplies in the CSS cabin did not significantly alter the community structure of the indigenous on-orbit microbes. Microorganisms found in the cargo storage area of the Wenchang cargo cleanroom and the clean storage area of the Tianjin AIT hall were most likely to enter the on-orbit CSS, highlighting the need for intensified sterilization protocols. The dominant strains in the on-orbit CSS module mainly originated from plants and the human body.
