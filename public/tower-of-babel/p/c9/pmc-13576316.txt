The impact of physical activity on health inequality among older adults: evidence from China
Authors: Xunuo Dang, Jiawen Pan, Xiangwei Xing, Xiao Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealth inequality among older adults is a persistent global challenge, yet the role of individual health behaviors in moderating such disparities remains underexplored. This study examines whether physical activity reduces health inequality among older adults in China and investigates the mechanisms through which this effect operates.MethodsThis study uses data from the 2023 China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS 2023). A relative health deprivation index based on self-rated health was constructed to measure health inequality. Tobit regression was used as the main estimation method, and instrumental variable estimation and propensity score matching were employed to address endogeneity concerns. Mediation and heterogeneity analyses were further conducted to examine potential pathways and subgroup differences.ResultsPhysical activity was significantly associated with lower relative health deprivation among older adults. Self-efficacy and social network size were identified as two significant mediating pathways, with the self-efficacy pathway showing a stronger indirect effect. The association was more pronounced among adults aged 80 and above, those with chronic diseases, pension insurance holders, rural residents, and those with lower educational attainment.ConclusionsPhysical activity is closely associated with lower health inequality among older adults in China, especially among physiologically vulnerable and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. Policies promoting accessible community-based physical activity may help improve health equity in aging populations.

Introduction

Population aging is a defining demographic trend of the twenty-first century. By 2050, the global population aged 60 years and older is projected to exceed 2.1 billion, with low- and middle-income countries accounting for the majority of this growth [1]. China, the country with the largest elderly population in the world, faces particularly acute aging pressures. By the end of 2024, the population aged 60 years and above in China reached 310 million, accounting for 22.0% of the total national population, of which those aged 65 years and above numbered 220 million, representing 15.6% of the total population [2]. Alongside this demographic shift, health inequality among older adults has emerged as a critical public health concern. Substantial evidence shows that older adults with lower socioeconomic status, as defined by income, education, or occupational history, experience markedly worse health outcomes, including higher chronic disease burden, greater functional decline, and lower self-rated health [3, 4] These disparities are not merely statistical artifacts; they reflect the cumulative disadvantage of differential access to health resources, material security, and social participation accumulated across the life course [5].

The existing research on health inequality in older populations has largely followed two streams. The first focuses on downstream healthcare redistribution, examining how insurance coverage and healthcare accessibility shape health outcome disparities among older adults [6, 7]. The second addresses upstream socioeconomic determinants, including pension income, wealth inequality, and social protection systems [8, 9]. While these studies offer important insights into the structural origins of health inequality, they share a common limitation: they predominantly emphasize external resource allocation and institutional mechanisms, giving insufficient attention to older adults’ own agency in health maintenance.

Physical activity has received increasing attention as an individual-level behavior with well-documented health benefits for older adults. A substantial body of evidence has demonstrated that regular exercise improves physical function, reduces the risk of chronic disease, alleviates psychological stress, and enhances subjective well-being [10–12]. From the perspective of preventive medicine, physical activity is widely regarded as a low-cost, high-return health behavior that individuals can pursue on their own initiative, and it has been recognized as a promising pathway to promote health equity [13, 14]. However, while existing studies have extensively examined the effects of physical activity on average health levels, its distributional consequences, namely, whether physical activity narrows or widens health gaps between socioeconomically advantaged and disadvantaged older adults, have received far less systematic attention.

The relationship between physical activity and health inequality is theoretically ambiguous. Some scholars, drawing on Bourdieu’s theory of social reproduction, have argued that health-promoting behaviors are more readily adopted by those with higher socioeconomic status due to greater awareness, available time, and access to facilities and may therefore reinforce rather than reduce health gaps [15, 16]. Others contend that physical activity’s health returns diminish with higher baseline health stock, implying that disadvantaged groups stand to gain proportionately more from participation [17, 18]. In the context of older adulthood specifically, retirement substantially compresses time-availability differences across socioeconomic groups, lowering a key structural barrier to exercise participation. This asymmetry in returns suggests that physical activity may exhibit stronger equalizing effects in older populations than in working-age adults [19]. Therefore, Hypothesis 1 is proposed: physical activity significantly reduces health inequality among older adults.

Beyond its direct effects, the mechanisms through which physical activity might reduce health inequality remain underspecified in the literature. The resource substitution theory offers a useful starting point: when economic capital is scarce, individuals can draw on noneconomic forms of capital to maintain health and buffer against adversity [20, 21]. Physical activity represents an accessible behavioral pathway through which such capital can accumulate at low cost, potentially operating through two distinct channels.

From the perspective of psychological capital, physical activity may reduce health inequality through self-efficacy. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in his or her capacity to organize and perform behaviors needed to achieve desired goals, and it is closely related to health behavior maintenance, psychological resilience, and subjective well-being among older adults [22]. For older adults, physical activity provides repeated experiences of bodily control, mastery, and accomplishment. These experiences may strengthen confidence in managing daily life and health-related challenges.

This mechanism is especially relevant for socioeconomically disadvantaged older adults. The equalizing effect does not arise simply because disadvantaged groups have lower initial levels of self-efficacy [23, 24]. Rather, it arises because physical activity can provide a low-cost and repeatable source of mastery that is less dependent on income, formal education, or access to market-based health services. Older adults with fewer socioeconomic resources often face weaker access to professional health services, lower health literacy, and fewer opportunities to obtain psychological and social support [3, 6, 7]. Under these conditions, the improvement in perceived control and health-related confidence generated through physical activity may have greater marginal value. In contrast, advantaged older adults may already possess more alternative resources for maintaining health, such as better medical access, stronger health knowledge, and broader social support, which may reduce the additional marginal benefit of physical activity [20, 21].

Therefore, physical activity may serve as a compensatory behavioral resource for disadvantaged older adults. By enhancing self-efficacy, it can improve their capacity to persist in health-promoting behaviors, cope with health decline, and manage daily health risks [22, 25]. This process may help narrow relative health deprivation between disadvantaged and advantaged older adults. Therefore, Hypothesis 2 is proposed: physical activity reduces health inequality among older adults through the mediating pathway of self-efficacy.

From a social capital perspective, physical activity may also reduce health inequality through the expansion of social networks. Social capital theory holds that embeddedness in social networks generates resources, including health information, emotional support, and informal care, that are consequential for health maintenance [26, 27]. Physical activity, particularly when conducted in community settings such as group exercise classes or public fitness areas, provides structured and recurring opportunities for social interaction [28]. Through participation, older adults can establish and sustain social connections, gain access to peer-sourced health information, and receive emotional support that buffers against stress and loneliness [29, 30]. For older adults with limited economic resources, social networks formed through physical activity can function as a form of resource substitution, providing informal support that partially compensates for the absence of market-based health services [31–33]. Given that socioeconomically disadvantaged older adults often face greater social isolation and have fewer opportunities to build social connections through other channels, the social capital generated by physical activity may be disproportionately valuable to this group, thereby contributing to the reduction in health inequality. Therefore, Hypothesis 3 is proposed: physical activity reduces health inequality among older adults through the mediating pathway of social network expansion. Compared with existing studies on physical activity and health among older adults, this study offers two potential contributions. The first contribution is the shift in research focus from average health outcomes to health inequality. Previous studies have widely documented the beneficial role of physical activity in improving older adults’ physical function, mental health, and overall well-being [10–12]. However, less attention has been paid to whether physical activity is associated with lower relative health disadvantage within the older population. By focusing on health inequality rather than only average health status, this study examines whether physical activity may generate an equalizing effect across the health distribution.

The second contribution is the explanation of how physical activity may function as a compensatory behavioral resource. Prior studies have shown that self-efficacy, social support, and behavioral resources are closely related to physical activity and healthy aging [22, 26, 27]. Building on this literature, this study examines self-efficacy and social networks as two non-economic pathways through which physical activity may reduce health inequality. We further investigate whether the association between physical activity and relative health deprivation is stronger among physiologically vulnerable and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. This provides a basis for understanding why physical activity may yield greater marginal health returns when institutional, market-based, and informational resources are limited.

Materials and methods

Participants and survey

The data used in this study were drawn from the 2023 wave of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS), a nationally representative survey conducted by the Population and Development Research Centre at Renmin University of China. We restrict our analysis to the 2023 wave to capture the most recent trends in aging and health inequality under current socioeconomic conditions. CLASS employs a stratified multistage probability sampling method and covers 28 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities in China, excluding Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan. The survey targets Chinese residents aged 60 years and above and collects information on health status, socioeconomic conditions, family characteristics, social support, psychological perceptions, community environment, and aging-related needs. CLASS is suitable for this study because its questionnaire contains the key variables required by our empirical design, including self-rated health, physical activity participation, pension insurance, household registration type, educational attainment, chronic disease status, family characteristics, and items related to self-efficacy and social networks.

The 2023 wave originally included 11,670 respondents. To ensure analytical consistency, observations with missing values in any core variable, including the dependent variable, independent variable, mediating variables, and all control variables, were excluded. Only 19 observations were dropped, corresponding to 0.16% of the original sample. The exclusion of these observations had a minimal impact on sample representativeness [34]. Consequently, a final analytic sample of 11,651 participants was retained for analysis.

Measures

Dependent variable

Health inequality was operationalized via the relative deprivation index constructed from self-rated health, following the measurement approach proposed by Kakwani [35] and subsequently applied in health inequality research [36]. Self-rated health was assessed via the survey question “How would you rate your current health status?”. Responses were recorded on a five-point scale with options of “very unhealthy”, “relatively unhealthy”, “average”, “relatively healthy”, and “very healthy”, which were reverse-coded and assigned values of 1 to 5, respectively, such that higher values indicate better self-rated health.

Composite aggregate measures such as the Gini coefficient or Theil index do not reveal individuals’ relative health positions within a distribution and therefore cannot identify who specifically bears the burden of health disadvantage [37]. The conceptual foundation of relative deprivation is social comparison: individuals assess their health not only in absolute terms but also relative to others in their reference group, which is particularly salient in older adulthood, where health assessments are known to be influenced by the status of age peers [38]. Health inequality measurement is therefore better served by shifting from the aggregate to the individual level [39]. The relative deprivation index achieves this by quantifying each individual’s health disadvantage relative to those with better health within the same reference group, yielding a continuous measure that captures the distributional consequences of health-promoting behaviors at the person level.

Independent variables

Physical activity was operationalized as a binary variable on the basis of participants’ self-reported exercise frequency. Participants reporting an average frequency of less than once per month were assigned a value of 0, whereas those reporting participation at least once per month on average were assigned a value of 1. This threshold was chosen for two reasons. First, the CLASS 2023 questionnaire measures physical activity frequency using an ordinal scale, with the lowest non-zero category being “at least once per month on average.” Since this study focuses on the distinction between participation and non-participation, this binary classification follows the survey design. This interpretation is also supported by evidence that low-frequency or short-duration physical activity may be associated with better health outcomes among middle-aged and older adults, including lower frailty risk and better functional health [40]. Second, monthly-level thresholds have been used in social survey research to distinguish participants from nonparticipants in health-related behaviors. For example, studies on cultural engagement and health outcomes have used “attendance once a month” as a meaningful threshold [41], and research on cognitive outcomes has found benefits from leisure-time physical activity even at light levels of one to four times per month [42].

Mechanism variables

Following Xia and Zhu [43] and drawing on items from the CLASS 2023 questionnaire based on respondents’ self-evaluations, four items were selected to construct the self-efficacy measure: (1) “I would be willing to participate in some work of the village or neighborhood committee if given the opportunity”; (2) “I often think about doing something more for society”; (3) “I enjoy learning new things”; and (4) “I feel that I am still a person useful to society”. Responses were assigned values on a five-point scale: not at all = 1, a little = 2, somewhat = 3, mostly = 4, and completely = 5. Self-efficacy was calculated by summing the scores across the four items, yielding a composite score ranging from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating stronger self-efficacy among older adults.

Social network size was assessed via the Lubben Social Network Scale [44]. The participants were asked separately about the number of relatives and friends they could meet in person, confided about personal matters, and relied on for practical assistance. The responses for each item were categorized into six ordered levels: “none”, “one”, “two”, “three to four”, “five to eight”, and “nine or more”, with scores ranging from 0 to 5. All the items were summed to produce a total score ranging from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating a larger social network.

Control variables

A set of individual-level and family-level covariates was included in all regression models on the basis of their established associations with both physical activity participation and health outcomes among older adults in prior literature [45–47]. Individual-level covariates included age, sex, marital status, educational attainment, household registration type, pension insurance status, and number of confirmed chronic disease diagnoses. The family-level covariates comprised the logarithms of total annual household income, household size, living arrangement, and number of living children. Statistical analysis

Given that the dependent variable is a continuous measure bounded at zero with a substantial proportion of observations taking the value of zero, a Tobit regression model was employed as the primary estimation approach, with provincial fixed effects included to control for unobserved regional heterogeneity. Three sequential models were estimated: Model 1 included only the physical activity variable and provincial fixed effects; Model 2 added individual-level control variables; and Model 3 further incorporated family-level control variables.

To examine the mediating roles of self-efficacy and social networks, a stepwise mediation analysis was conducted following the procedure outlined by Baron and Kenny [48]. The statistical significance of each indirect effect was assessed using Sobel tests and a bootstrap approach with 1,000 replications.

To explore whether the equalizing effect of physical activity varied across population subgroups, heterogeneity analyses were conducted by stratifying the sample along three dimensions: physiological characteristics (age group and sex), individual resources (chronic disease status and pension insurance), and socioeconomic background (household registration type and educational attainment).

The robustness of the baseline findings was assessed through four approaches: replacing the binary physical activity variable with physical activity duration as an alternative measure, excluding participants aged 80 and above, substituting OLS regression for the Tobit model, and clustering standard errors at the provincial level.

To address potential endogeneity arising from reverse causality between physical activity and health inequality, two additional estimation strategies were employed. First, an instrumental variable approach was used. The leave-one-out community-level average physical activity participation rate was used as the instrument for individual physical activity participation. This instrument captures the local exercise environment and peer participation norm while excluding the respondent’s own behavior from the community average. The validity of the instrument was assessed using the first-stage F statistic, with values exceeding the conventional threshold of 10 indicating that weak instrument concerns were unlikely. Second, propensity score matching was applied to reduce bias from observable confounding between participants and nonparticipants in physical activity. One-to-one nearest-neighbor matching was conducted on all control variables, and the standardized bias of covariates before and after matching was examined to verify balance. All analyses were conducted via Stata 18.0.

Replacing the core dependent variable

A potential concern is that our primary measure of health inequality, based on self-rated health (SRH), may be subjective. To address this, drawing upon the research of Liping Liao et al. [49, 50], we constructed the ADL limitations indicator based on the questions in the CLASS database: Do you currently require assistance with daily living activities (such as eating, bathing, dressing, using the toilet, moving, and walking)? If the respondent indicates that they need assistance, the value is set to 1; if they do not, the value is set to 0. As shown in column 1 of Table 4, the coefficient on physical activity remains significantly negative, indicating that our findings are robust to using an objective measure of health.

Replacing the core explanatory variable

Given that the binary indicator of physical activity may not fully capture the depth of older adults’ engagement in exercise, we replaced the binary participation variable with the duration of physical activity drawn from the CLASS questionnaire as an alternative measure of the core explanatory variable. As shown in Table 4, Column 2, the coefficient for physical activity duration remained significantly negative at the 1% level (β = −0.0156, p < 0.01), reinforcing the consistency of the baseline results.

Excluding the oldest-old subsample

Considering that adults aged 80 years and above may face irreversible health disadvantages due to natural physiological decline and that their participation in physical activity is often severely constrained by objective physical conditions, we re-estimated the baseline model after excluding this subgroup (n = 10,660). As shown in Table 4, Column 3, the coefficient for physical activity remained significantly negative (β = −0.0112, p < 0.01) after the exclusion of the oldest-old subsample, indicating that the baseline finding is not driven by or specific to this group and holds across the broader older adult population.

To verify that the conclusions do not depend on a specific model specification, we re-estimate the baseline equation via ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression as an alternative estimator. As shown in Table 4, in Column 4, after the estimation method was changed, the direction of the physical activity coefficient remained highly consistent with that of the baseline regression (β = −0.0187, p < 0.01, R² = 0.116), and the level of statistical significance remained high. The consistency of the coefficient across different estimation approaches further corroborates the robustness of the empirical results.

Instrumental variable estimation

Physical activity participation is self-reported by respondents, which may introduce endogeneity arising from reverse causality: older adults in better relative health may be more motivated and physically capable of engaging in exercise, potentially leading to upward bias in the baseline estimates. To address this concern, we employed instrumental variable (IV) estimation using the local average physical activity level as the instrument for individual physical activity participation. Following the approach of [51], this instrument was constructed by calculating the average physical activity level of all respondents within the same postal code area in 2023, excluding the respondent’s own activity level.

This instrument satisfies the relevance condition: individual behavior is often influenced by the social norms and peer effects of the surrounding community. A higher average level of physical activity in the local area creates a supportive environment and social pressure that significantly increases the likelihood of an individual engaging in physical activity. It also satisfies the plausible exogeneity condition: the aggregate physical activity level of a community is determined by the collective behavior of many individuals and is unlikely to be directly driven by the specific health status of a single respondent. Therefore, it is unlikely to directly affect individual-level relative health deprivation through channels other than the individual’s own physical activity participation.

The IV estimation results are presented in Table 5. The first-stage regression confirmed that the local average physical activity level was significantly and positively associated with individual physical activity, with a first-stage F statistic exceeding the conventional threshold of 10 and ruling out weak instrument concerns. In the second-stage regression, the instrumented physical activity variable remained significantly and negatively associated with health inequality. The IV estimate is consistent with the baseline Tobit estimate and provides additional support for the robustness of the main findings after addressing potential endogeneity concerns.

Propensity score matching

To further reduce confounding from observable differences between physically active and inactive participants, we conducted propensity score matching via one-to-one nearest-neighbor matching on the basis of the full set of control variables. Figure 2 presents the standardized bias of each covariate before and after matching. Prior to matching, substantial imbalances were observed across multiple covariates. After matching, the standardized biases of all covariates were substantially reduced, with absolute values falling within the commonly accepted threshold of 10%, indicating that adequate covariate balance was achieved between the matched active and inactive groups. The estimated average treatment effect of physical activity on health inequality in the matched sample was − 0.0144 (p < 0.01), which was closely consistent with the baseline Tobit estimate.

Models 1 and 2 in Table 6 present the results for the self-efficacy pathway. Model 1 examines the effect of physical activity on self-efficacy. Social network

Models 3 and 4 in Table 6 present the results for the social network pathway. Model 3 examines the effect of physical activity on social network size. The results show that physical activity was significantly and positively associated with social network size (β = 1.4156, p < 0.01), indicating that physically active older adults had substantially larger social networks than their inactive counterparts did. Heterogeneity analyses

The heterogeneity analysis was guided by two related perspectives: cumulative inequality theory and the resource substitution hypothesis. Cumulative inequality theory suggests that health disadvantages accumulate over the life course through unequal exposure to risks and resources [5]. From this perspective, physiological characteristics such as age and sex capture differences in accumulated biological vulnerability and health risks. Age group was included because health decline, mobility limitations, and chronic disease risks tend to increase with age, making physical activity particularly relevant for maintaining daily functioning among older adults [5, 17, 18]. Sex was included because older men and women may differ in baseline physical function, chronic disease profiles, and patterns of physical activity participation.

The resource substitution hypothesis suggests that non-economic or behavioral resources may play a more important compensatory role when other health-related resources are limited [20, 21]. Following this logic, we further examined heterogeneity by individual resources and socioeconomic background. Individual resources were measured by chronic disease status and pension insurance coverage, which reflect differences in health burden and institutional protection. Chronic disease status captures variation in baseline health needs, while pension insurance coverage reflects basic economic security in later life and may affect older adults’ ability to sustain health-promoting behaviors [17, 18, 45, 46]. Socioeconomic background was measured by household registration type and educational attainment, which reflect differences in access to healthcare, health information, exercise opportunities, and market-based health resources. Rural residents and less-educated older adults may have fewer alternative health-related resources, making physical activity a relatively accessible behavioral resource under conditions of institutional and economic constraint [20, 21].

With respect to physiological characteristics (Table 7), the association between physical activity and health inequality was not statistically significant among participants aged 60–69 years (β = −0.0044, SE = 0.0042, p > 0.05). In contrast, the association became significant and progressively stronger among those aged 70–79 years (β = −0.0171, SE = 0.0038, p < 0.01) and those aged 80 years and above (β = −0.0352, SE = 0.0093, p < 0.01). With respect to sex, physical activity was significantly associated with lower health inequality among both male (β = −0.0140, SE = 0.0038, p < 0.01) and female participants (β = −0.0123, SE = 0.0041, p < 0.01), with no substantively meaningful difference between the two groups.

Discussion

This study contributes to an ongoing and unresolved debate in the health inequality literature concerning whether health-promoting behaviors exacerbate or attenuate socioeconomic gradients in health. Using a relative deprivation index to capture individuals’ disadvantaged health position relative to their peer group, we find that physical activity functions as an equalizing rather than a stratifying force among older adults in China, a finding that holds across multiple estimation strategies and endogeneity corrections.

Some previous studies have argued that physical activity participation is socially stratified by class position, cultural resources, and embodied dispositions and may therefore widen rather than narrow health disparities between socioeconomic groups [15, 16]. Our findings do not fully align with this more pessimistic account in the context of older adulthood. One possible explanation is that retirement narrows time-related barriers to exercise across socioeconomic groups [17], whereas another is that older adults with poorer baseline health may derive greater marginal benefit from physical activity [18].

The mediation analyses revealed that self-efficacy and social network size each partially mediate the association between physical activity and health inequality. The self-efficacy pathway produced a stronger indirect effect, suggesting that the psychological mechanism is more proximate. For older adults whose sense of personal agency may be eroded by health decline, retirement, and material constraints, physical activity provides repeated experiences of mastery that strengthen self-efficacy and contribute to reduced health inequality [22, 24].

The social network pathway, while weaker in magnitude, confirms that physical activity expands older adults’ access to peer-sourced health information, emotional support, and informal care [26, 27]. For socioeconomically disadvantaged older adults with limited access to market-based health services, the social connections formed through exercise can partially compensate for material deficits, which is consistent with the resource substitution hypothesis [21].

Although self-efficacy and social networks were examined as parallel mediating pathways in the empirical analysis, they should not be understood as entirely independent mechanisms in practice. Social cognitive theory suggests that self-efficacy may be shaped not only by mastery experiences but also by social encouragement, modeling, and emotional reinforcement [22]. Meanwhile, social capital and social network perspectives emphasize that social ties provide health information, emotional support, and behavioral norms that may facilitate health-promoting behaviors [26, 27]. In real-world physical activity settings, especially community-based or group-based exercise among older adults, psychological and social resources may therefore reinforce each other. Physical activity may strengthen older adults’ sense of competence and personal agency, while expanded social networks may provide companionship, encouragement, and information that support continued participation [30, 32]. Thus, the psychological and social pathways identified in this study may operate not only separately but also interactively in shaping relative health deprivation.

The heterogeneity analyses further clarify the theoretical meaning of the baseline result. Rather than suggesting a uniform association across all older adults, the results indicate that the association between physical activity and lower relative health deprivation is more pronounced among groups with greater physiological vulnerability, heavier disease burden, or fewer alternative resources. This pattern is consistent with the resource-substitution perspective, which suggests that behavioral resources may have stronger health implications when other health-related resources are limited [20, 21]. In this study, physical activity can be understood as a relatively accessible behavioral resource that may help older adults maintain function, strengthen self-efficacy, and expand social networks when institutional, market-based, and informational resources are constrained.

The stronger association among the oldest-old suggests that physical activity may have greater health relevance when baseline health vulnerability is higher. Older adults aged 80 and above often face greater functional decline, mobility limitations, and chronic disease risks. In this context, regular physical activity may help maintain daily functioning and perceived health status. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that physical activity is important for maintaining mobility, preventing functional decline, and improving frailty-related outcomes among older adults [52, 53]. Similarly, the stronger association among older adults with chronic diseases may reflect the closer link between physical activity and symptom management, mobility maintenance, emotional regulation, and preservation of daily functioning [52]. For older adults without chronic diseases, baseline health deprivation is generally lower, so the short-term equalizing association of physical activity may be less visible in a relative deprivation framework.

A similar pattern was found for pension insurance, with a significant association observed among holders but not among the uninsured. Research has shown that financial insecurity is a major barrier to sustained health-promoting behavior among older adults [45, 46], and a basic level of institutional protection may therefore enable more consistent engagement in physical activity and its associated health benefits. However, it is worth noting that we did not find a statistically significant association between physical activity and health inequality reduction among participants without pension insurance. While this may suggest a differential effect, we urge caution in interpreting this null finding. First, this subgroup is relatively small, which limits the statistical power of our analysis. Second, this group may face severe multimorbidity constraints. It is plausible that individuals in this group suffer from a higher burden of chronic conditions that are not fully captured by our control variables. These underlying health issues could act as a “ceiling effect”, limiting their physical capacity to engage in exercise at an intensity or frequency sufficient to yield measurable health equalization benefits. Therefore, the non-significant result likely reflects a combination of limited statistical power and complex selection mechanisms related to health and socioeconomic status, rather than a lack of efficacy of physical activity per se.

The stronger association among rural residents and older adults with lower educational attainment can also be understood through the same logic. Compared with urban and better-educated older adults, these groups usually have more limited access to formal healthcare, paid exercise services, health information, and other market-based resources. Under these constraints, physical activity, especially walking, square dancing, group exercise, and other community-based forms of activity, may provide a comparatively accessible pathway for maintaining physical function, obtaining informal health information, and building supportive peer ties. Prior research has shown that social support is positively associated with physical activity among older adults [54]. Evidence from rural older adults also suggests that physical activity may affect health through education, income, and psychological resources [55]. For more advantaged groups, the same behavioral resource may be less decisive because formal care, health literacy, and private services already provide alternative channels for health maintenance.

Taken together, the heterogeneity results do not simply show subgroup differences. They suggest that physical activity may have stronger equalizing implications when older adults face greater health vulnerability or more limited institutional and market-based resources. This helps explain why the association between physical activity and relative health deprivation is stronger among the oldest-old, older adults with chronic diseases, rural residents, and those with lower educational attainment.

These findings have several implications for policy and practice. First, community sports infrastructure investment should be prioritized in rural and low-income areas, where access to formal healthcare is more limited. Expanding publicly funded fitness facilities, outdoor exercise spaces, and organized group activity programs in underserved communities would lower participation barriers for older adults with fewer economic resources. Second, program design should incorporate structured components such as instructor-led group exercise classes, regular skill progression assessments, and peer-based exercise partnerships, which simultaneously build participants’ sense of competence and foster ongoing social ties. Third, governments should establish subsidized or free community-based physical activity programs for older adults, ensuring that participation is not constrained by financial circumstances.

Despite these implications, this study still has several limitations. First, although instrumental variable estimation and propensity score matching were used to mitigate potential endogeneity concerns, the use of cross-sectional data limits our ability to fully account for unobserved individual heterogeneity. Future research could use multi-wave CLASS data or other panel datasets to further investigate how changes in physical activity are associated with changes in health inequality over time. Second, both self-rated health and self-efficacy were based on self-reported information, and subjective reporting may be affected by common method bias. Future studies could use objective health measures or multi-source data to further address this issue. Third, this study could not further distinguish between individual-based and group-based physical activities. Future research could use more detailed activity-context measures to examine whether different forms of physical activity are differently associated with health inequality and its underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

This study examined whether physical activity is associated with lower relative health deprivation among older adults in China and explored the mechanisms and heterogeneity underlying this relationship. The results show that physical activity was significantly associated with a lower relative health deprivation index, and this finding remained robust across multiple model specifications, robustness checks, and endogeneity analyses. The mediation analysis further showed that self-efficacy and social network expansion partially mediated this relationship, with the self-efficacy pathway showing a statistically larger indirect effect than the social network pathway. Heterogeneity analyses further revealed that the equalizing effects were more pronounced among the oldest-old, those with chronic diseases, rural residents, and those with lower educational attainment, which is consistent with the resource substitution hypothesis. These findings contribute to the understanding of how individual behavioral resources can reduce health inequality in aging populations and offer empirical grounding for policies aimed at promoting health equity among older adults in rapidly aging societies.
