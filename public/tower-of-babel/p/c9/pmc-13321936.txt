Rescue of Suprasellar Metastasis of EGFR-mutant NSCLC by Daily Osimertinib Re-escalation With Filgrastim Support
Authors: SHOICHIRO SAITO, SHUICHI MATSUDA, MASATO NAKAMURA, RINA TAKAHASHI, RYO YAMAMOTO, MASAAKI IBE, MIKIO TAKAMORI, MIYAKO KITAZONO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimManaging central nervous system (CNS) metastases of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated non-small-cell lung cancer can be challenging if dose-limiting toxicities prevent adequate drug exposure.Case ReportWe report the case of a 70-year-old woman with a suprasellar metastasis abutting the optic chiasm for whom radiotherapy was contraindicated. Although treatment with 80 mg osimertinib once daily was begun, recurrent grade 3 neutropenia required a dose reduction to 40 mg every other day. During alternate-day dosing, the suprasellar lesion progressed despite continued control of extracranial disease, threatening the patient’s vision. To enhance exposure of the CNS to osimertinib, its dosage was escalated to 40 mg once daily supported by granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (filgrastim). This strategy successfully led to tumor regression and maintained disease control without unmanageable toxicity.ConclusionThis case suggests that dose reduction can weaken the effect of osimertinib on the CNS and that maintaining dose intensity using granulocyte colony-stimulating factor support is a viable and effective strategy for controlling CNS lesions in eloquent areas when local therapy is contraindicated.

Introduction

Brain metastases of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated non-small-cell lung cancer are common. Osimertinib is the standard, first-line therapy owing to its central nervous system (CNS) activity (1). However, management can be difficult if localized, CNS-directed therapy is constrained by lesion location, and systemic treatment is limited by hematological toxicity. Suprasellar metastases abutting the optic chiasm have a high risk of causing irreversible visual impairment and may preclude stereotactic radiotherapy and surgical re-intervention. In such cases, increasing systemic exposure to a therapeutic agent may be considered, but dose escalation may not always be feasible.

While osimertinib is generally well-tolerated, clinically significant neutropenia can occur, particularly with concurrent cytotoxic chemotherapy (2). There is currently little evidence on modifying the osimertinib dosage and administering granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) to balance toxicity against control of CNS disease. We describe herein a case with multiple brain metastases of EGFR exon 19 deletion-positive lung adenocarcinoma, including a suprasellar lesion threatening the optic chiasm, in which recurrent grade 3 neutropenia necessitated repeated dosage adjustments. Because the suprasellar lesion progressed during the administration of a safety-driven, off-label, alternate-day regimen, the treatment was escalated to daily dosing and combined with G-CSF to regain disease control.

Case Report

A 70-year-old woman with a history of early-stage breast cancer treated with breast-conserving surgery 10 years earlier, without evidence of recurrence, presented with dizziness. Contrast-enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a 30-mm, right frontal mass with extensive edema (Figure 1A) and additional enhancing lesions in the right temporal lobe and the suprasellar region abutting the optic chiasm (Figure 1B and C). Three weeks later, the frontal lesion was resected for decompression. Radiotherapy was withheld because of the risk of visual impairment associated with the suprasellar lesion. Chest computed tomography revealed a 31-mm mass in the left upper lobe of the lung (Figure 2A).

Pathology of the resected brain tumor showed metastatic adenocarcinoma. Immunohistochemistry was positive for cytokeratin 7 and thyroid transcription factor-1, and negative for cytokeratin 20, GATA-binding protein 3, mammaglobin, and gross cystic disease fluid protein 15, supporting a lung primary. Bronchoscopy confirmed lung adenocarcinoma harboring an EGFR exon 19 deletion (programmed cell death ligand 1 expression <1%).

One month postoperatively, a regimen consisting of carboplatin at an area under the concentration-time curve of 5, 500 mg/m2 pemetrexed, and 80 mg osimertinib once daily was started on the basis of the patient’s baseline performance status of 0 (day 0). Levetiracetam had been initiated immediately after resection of the brain metastasis and was discontinued 3 days before the start of chemotherapy because of existing leukopenia. Although leukopenia, which was present before treatment, improved after levetiracetam was discontinued, neutropenia of grade 3 according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events developed 22 days after the initiation of osimertinib and chemotherapy (day 22: leukocytes 1,700/μl, neutrophils 750/μl) (3). Cytotoxic agents were discontinued, and osimertinib was temporarily withheld. Persistent neutropenia (day 29: leukocytes: 1,400/μl, neutrophils: 440/μl; day 43: leukocytes: 2,100/μl, neutrophils: 750/μl) required on-demand support with filgrastim (150 μg). Hematological evaluation found no evidence of a primary blood disorder such as dysplasia or blasts on a peripheral smear (day 47). After hematologic consultation confirmed count recovery (day 47: leukocytes: 3,960/μl, neutrophils: 2,680/μl), osimertinib was resumed at 40 mg once daily. Neutropenia recurred upon re-challenge, supporting osimertinib-associated dose-limiting neutropenia in this patient (day 52: leukocytes: 2,100/μl, neutrophils: 1,020/μl). A dose of 40 mg once daily was continued requiring intermittent filgrastim administration, with radiographically confirmed shrinkage of the intracranial lesions and regression of the lung mass, without the development of new metastases (Figure 2B and Figure 3A and B). Because blood counts had recovered by day 60 (leukocytes: 5,100/μl; neutrophils: 4,040/μl), re-escalation to 80 mg osimertinib once daily was attempted; however, neutropenia recurred by day 66 (leukocytes: 1,700/μl; neutrophils: 770/μl), indicating poor tolerability of 80 mg. Osimertinib was reduced to 40 mg once daily with filgrastim support, but neutropenia again worsened (day 73: leukocytes: 1,600/μl; neutrophils: 480/μl), prompting a 1-week interruption. After recovery (day 80: leukocytes: 1,700/μl; neutrophils: 880/μl), osimertinib was resumed at 40 mg every other day, after which the patient remained stable without the need for further filgrastim. This alternate-day regimen was maintained as a safety-driven, off-label bridging strategy (4).

Two months after initiating the alternate-day dosing, while chest computed tomography continued to show regression without new metastases, brain MRI demonstrated progression of the suprasellar lesion, threatening the optic chiasm (Figure 2C and Figure 3C). The imaging findings were also reviewed with a radiologist, who confirmed interval progression of the suprasellar lesion. Stereotactic radiotherapy was reconsidered but judged to have an unacceptably high risk of visual impairment. After obtaining informed consent regarding hematological toxicity, osimertinib was re-escalated to 40 mg once daily to enhance CNS exposure, supported by filgrastim. Although grade 3 neutropenia occurred 2 weeks later, it was managed with frequent monitoring and continued filgrastim administration once every 2 weeks. One month after re-escalation, MRI showed regression of the suprasellar lesion (Figure 3D), and to date, 3 months after re-escalation, disease control has been maintained without severe, infectious complications (Figure 2D and Figure 3E).

Written informed consent for publication of this case report and accompanying images was obtained from the patient.

Discussion

This case provides a practical cautionary message that non-standard dose de-escalation (alternate-day dosing) may compromise CNS control even when extracranial disease remains controlled. It also illustrates that maintaining dose intensity with carefully monitored G-CSF support can be considered when local CNS-directed therapy is contraindicated and disease progression threatens vital functions. While the CNS activity of osimertinib is superior to that of earlier-generation tyrosine-kinase inhibitors, its concentration in the cerebrospinal fluid remains lower than in plasma (1, 5). In addition, recent studies have shown that blood concentrations of osimertinib and its active metabolites are associated with both treatment efficacy and safety, supporting the clinical relevance of systemic exposure (6, 7). Therefore, a dose reduction intended to mitigate the drug’s hematological toxicity may lower its level in the CNS to a point below the efficacy threshold. Indeed, a recent retrospective study demonstrated that early dose reduction of osimertinib was an independent risk factor of the deterioration of brain metastases, particularly in patients with baseline CNS lesions (8).

In our patient, the suprasellar lesion progressed on alternate-day dosing and regressed after re-escalation to daily dosing, suggesting a dose–response relationship in the CNS. While such observations cannot establish causality, they are consistent with the concept that CNS lesions may be more vulnerable than extracranial lesions to reductions in dose intensity because CNS exposure is intrinsically lower and may fall below an effective threshold earlier (9). Therefore, a non-standard dose reduction, such as alternate-day dosing, may carry a high risk of inadequate CNS control even when the disease appears to be systemically controlled (8).

Standard management for grade 3 neutropenia typically involves dose interruption and reduction (10). However, in scenarios where tumor progression threatens the vital functions, such as vision in this case, strict adherence to dose reduction guidelines may lead to irreversible morbidity. Routine, prophylactic G-CSF is not a standard adjunct of monotherapy with EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitor; in the present case, G-CSF was used as secondary prophylaxis following a dose-limiting, neutropenic event to enable daily dosing and maintain control of the CNS lesion. This approach should not be generalized nor presented as a standard strategy without further evidence. If prolonged G-CSF support is considered, clinicians should acknowledge the uncertainties of the treatment and monitor for adverse effects, such as bone pain, leukocytosis, and rare, serious complications.

This report has important limitations. The plasma and cerebrospinal fluid concentrations of osimertinib were not measured; therefore, we were unable to confirm directly that modulating the dosage had produced a proportional change in systemic or CNS exposure to the drug.

Conclusion

Maintaining an adequate concentration of osimertinib may be indispensable for patients with CNS metastases of EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer in eloquent areas that are ineligible for radiotherapy. If neutropenia becomes dose-limiting, G-CSF administration as an individualized, adjunctive treatment may be considered as a means of maintaining effective CNS-directed therapy. In such a case, careful monitoring is required, and the treatment should be viewed as an exception for special cases rather than as a generally applicable method.
