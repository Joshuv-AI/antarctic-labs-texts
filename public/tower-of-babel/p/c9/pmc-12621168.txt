In Vitro Activity of Nitroxoline (5‐Nitro‐8‐Hydroxyquinoline) Against Aspergillus Species
Authors: Ada Hoffmann, Oliver A. Cornely, Jana Vonhoegen, Rosanne Sprute, Ilana Reinhold, Grit Walther, Frieder Fuchs, Michaela Simon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNitroxoline is an old antibiotic currently approved for the treatment of uncomplicated urinary tract infections. Its mode of action is based on the chelation of bivalent cations, such as zinc.ObjectivesAs zinc plays a central role in the metabolism of Aspergillus spp., we performed antifungal susceptibility testing against Nitroxoline using broth microdilution.MethodsOverall, 13 Aspergillus isolates were tested according to the EUCAST guidance document (Guinea et al. EUCAST, def. doc. 9.4, 2022) including two genetically proven Azole‐resistant strains.ResultsThe overall Minimal Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)50/90 was 0.5 mg/L.ConclusionsThese results correspond to excellent in vitro activity against Aspergillus spp.

Introduction

An increasing rate of resistance development, along with side effects and limited routes of administration, restricts the clinical utility of currently available antifungal agents [1]. This growing challenge necessitates innovative strategies to expand the arsenal of antifungal therapies. One promising approach is the repurposing of drugs already approved for other indications, as their toxicity profiles and optimal dosing have already been established, facilitating faster translation into clinical practice [2].

Nitroxoline (5‐nitro‐8‐hydroxyquinoline) is an antimicrobial agent that has recently been rediscovered for drug repurposing. Nitroxoline has been developed in the 1960s and is—particularly in the European region—still one of the first‐line treatment options for uncomplicated urinary tract infections (uUTI) caused by Enterobacterales such as Escherichia coli [3]. Its mode of action is based on the chelation of bivalent metal ions, such as zinc and magnesium, leading to starvation of the pathogen from essential nutrients necessary for physiological enzymatic functions [4, 5]. Through the removal of free cations, it acts bacteriostatic and inhibits indirectly the formation of bacterial biofilms [6, 7]. Pharmacokinetics support the use of Nitroxoline in urinary tract infection. After oral administration, Nitroxoline is rapidly absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract and conjugated in the liver. Unconjugated and conjugated substance accumulates in urine [8]; systemic substance levels are presumably low [9]. In adults, an oral dose of 250 mg three times daily is administered. Nitroxoline is usually very well tolerated, side effects include headache, nausea and stomach pain. EUCAST provides a clinical breakpoint at a minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 16 mg/L for E. coli in the setting of uUTI [10]. Breakpoints for other pathogens have not been defined yet. Despite its use for many years as one of the first line therapy options for uUTI, bacterial resistance against Nitroxoline is rare [11]. Deschner et al. reported in a recent study the emerge of low‐level Nitroxoline resistance in vitro only after long‐term exposure and in association with bacterial fitness loss and reduced virulence in E. coli [12].

Recent studies found further excellent in vitro activity against emerging multi‐drug resistant gram‐negative bacteria such as carbapenem‐resistant Enterobacterales [13, 14] and carbapenem‐resistant Acinetobacter baumanii [15]. Interestingly, in vitro activity against therapeutically challenging microorganisms has been demonstrated, e.g., drug‐resistant Mycobacteria [16] including Mycobacterium tuberculosis [17], Neisseria gonorrhoeae [18], Candida species, including Candida auris [19], and the free‐living amoeba Balamuthia mandrillaris [20].

Invasive disease by Aspergillus spp. is a major concern in immunocompromised and critically ill patients leading to high mortality rates [21]. The therapy of choice consists of Triazoles and liposomal Amphotericin B [22]. Azoles are associated with hepatic dysfunction, skin and neurological side effects and a high potential for drug interactions. Additionally, Azole‐resistance is rising [23, 24, 25], especially in Europe [26]. Liposomal Amphotericin B is known for considerable nephrotoxicity and electrolyte abnormalities, limiting clinical use.

As the metabolism of Aspergillus is highly dependent on zinc in several metabolic pathways [27, 28] and Nitroxoline has an antimicrobial effect through chelation of zinc and other bivalent cations, we hypothesised that antifungal activity of Nitroxoline might be present in Aspergillus. Therefore, we examined a collection of 11 clinically relevant Aspergillus isolates using Nitroxoline broth microdilution.

Methods and Materials

Fungal Strains

Eleven clinical Aspergillus spp. isolates (7 Aspergillus fumigatus , 2 A. flavus ) and two ATCC reference strains ( Aspergillus fumigatus ATCC 204305 and Aspergillus flavus ATCC 204304) were included. All available isolates that were regarded as clinically relevant, were included and derived from individual patients treated in Cologne, Germany. Isolates were cultured from bronchoalveolar lavages (N = 6), other respiratory secretions (N = 3) and two biopsies (lung and spine). Two clinical isolates held an Azole‐resistance (both cultured from bronchoalveolar lavage) with concordant genotypic mutations within the P450 sterol 14‐demethylase enzyme (cyp51A‐mutation).

Isolate 11 exhibited five point‐mutations within the cyp51A gene leading to amino acid exchange: phenylalanine to tyrosine at position 46 (F46Y) (1), methionine to valine at position 172 (M172V) (2), asparagine to threonine at position 248 (N248T) (3), aspartic acid to glutamic acid at position 255 (D255E) (4) and glutamic acid to lysine at position 427 (E427K). Isolate 10 demonstrated two point mutations within the cyp51A gene, tyrosine to phenylalanine at position 121 (Y121F) and threonine to alanine at position 289 (T289A). Additionally, a 46 base pair long tandem‐repeat was found in the promotor region of cyp51A (probably responsible for Azole non‐susceptibility of this strain).

All isolates were sent to the German National Reference Center for Invasive Fungal Infections (NRZMyk, Jena, Germany) for phenotypic antifungal susceptibility testing, detection of resistance‐associated genetic mutations and species confirmation using sequencing of the β‐tubulin gene for Aspergillus fumigatus and the calmodulin gene for Aspergillus flavus, respectively. β‐tubulin and calmodulin sequences of all clinical strains are deposited in GenBank under the accession numbers PX436691 to PX43670.

Antifungal Susceptibility Testing (AFST)

AFST was conducted and evaluated following the EUCAST guidance document (Guinea et al. EUCAST, def. doc. 9.4, 2022). In brief, Aspergillus isolates were cultured for 2–5 days on Sabouraud dextrose agar (BD, Franklin Lakes) at 35°C ± 1°C. One strain was incubated for 9 days because of slow sporulation (strain no. 3). Conidia suspensions were prepared in sterile water with 0.1% Polysorbate (Tween) 40 and filtered (pore diameter 11 μm), if necessary. The concentration was set at a McFarland turbidity standard of 0.5–0.6 (Nephelometer Densimat, Biomérieux). The Nitroxoline stock solution of 128 mg/L was prepared using powder with a potency of 99.99% (batch number 22321; MIP Pharma, Blieskastel, Germany) and Dimethylsulfoxid (DMSO, Merck (Supelco), Darmstadt, Germany). The dilution range from 0.125 to 32 mg/L of Nitroxoline was produced with RPMI 1640 MOPS 2% glucose broth (RPMI Sigma). Broth microdilution was performed in flat‐bottom 96‐well plates (Greiner‐CELLSTAR, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis) and incubated for 48 h at 35°C ± 1°C.

Measurements were performed in triplicates and results were reproducible within one MIC dilution. MICs of Voriconazole and Isavuconazole were determined at the National Reference Center for Invasive Fungal diseases (NRZMyk, Jena, Germany) and are demonstrated here for comparison (Table 1).

Quality Control

A quantitative culture was performed for each test run to check the concentration of the conidial inoculum. For quality control of Nitroxoline, reference strain E. coli ATCC 25922 was tested in parallel to each antifungal susceptibility test according to ISO 20776‐1 [29]. Reference strain ATCC E. coli yielded a mean MIC of 2 mg/L in every test run and was within the expected range (EUCAST breakpoint E. coli for uUTI < 16 mg/L). All examined quality requirements were fulfilled. The authors confirm that the ethical policies of the journal, as noted on the journal's author guidelines page, have been adhered to. No ethical approval was required as the research in this article related to micro‐organisms.

Results

The in vitro AFST was performed on 13 Aspergillus strains using Nitroxoline by broth microdilution. Nitroxoline demonstrated overall MICs of 0.125 to 1 (MIC50/90 = 0.5 mg/L, Table 1) in all Aspergillus isolates, including Azole‐resistant isolates. Azole resistance was confirmed and the CYP51A gene was sequenced at the NRZMyk, Jena, Germany.

Discussion

Over recent years, Nitroxoline has become a substance of increasing interest due to its broad activity against emerging bacterial and fungal pathogens. In this article, we report an excellent in vitro activity of Nitroxoline against Aspergillus spp. from clinically relevant samples.

Nitroxoline in vitro activity against pathogenic fungi has already been demonstrated for different Candida spp. including multidrug‐resistant isolates [19], as well as filamentous fungi strains of Fusarium spp., Trichophyton and Microsporum spp. [30]. The MIC data of Aspergillus spp. from this study are comparable to those previously reported against other filamentous fungi [30]. Compared to Candida spp. isolates, MIC values are similar or up to two dilution steps lower for Aspergillus isolates [19, 30]. Additionally, MICs of Nitroxoline against Aspergillus spp. demonstrate activity well below the EUCAST breakpoint of 16 mg/L for E. coli and uUTI [10].

Infections due to Aspergillus spp. usually occur in immunocompromised hosts in mainly lung or bronchial tissues or in patients with chronic pulmonary disease, such as COPD [31]. The mode of antifungal application in severe infections is mainly intravenous but new approaches for especially long‐term oral treatment and substance alternatives are favourable. Currently, one bottleneck of Nitroxoline seems to be its pharmacokinetic properties regarding tissue (especially lung) distribution and systemic concentrations. Published data refer to a small cohort from 1979 of healthy volunteers [32] which have been reviewed in 2018 [9], confirming high urinary concentrations but data regarding tissue and plasma distribution after oral administration is scarce.

A different approach to Nitroxoline administration could be nebulization. For pulmonary aspergillosis, different antifungal agents are currently evaluated for inhaled use, leading to delivery of the drug specifically to lung tissue in high concentrations and with minor systemic exposure or drug interaction [33]. Lipophilic compounds and micronised particles are ideal properties for inhaled delivery. Recently, three repurposed antifungals for inhalation have successfully reached clinical trials [34]. Moreover, several publications underline the use of nanoparticle formation for targeted drug delivery against pulmonary aspergillosis [35, 36, 37] as a promising pharmacological tool, especially necessary for non‐solubility in water. Additionally, to a therapeutic approach, Nitroxoline might hypothetically serve as a useful substance for prophylaxis. Prophylaxis by inhalation is currently used for patients having received a lung‐transplantation with Amphotericin B [22].

Although rare, manifestations with isolated Aspergillus infections in kidney tissue (fungal obstructive uropathy, Aspergillus fungal ball [38, 39, 40]) exist, suggesting a possible indication of Nitroxoline for urinary tract mould infections alongside standard therapy in these complex situations.

A promising clinical observation of a patient suffering from Balamuthia mandrillaris infection and subsequently severe amebic encephalitis reported successful treatment after Nitroxoline in usual dosage had been added to the existing (unsuccessful) therapy combination [41]. This leads to the assumption, that Nitroxoline could be active within the central nervous system and might even demonstrate antifungal effects against cerebral aspergillosis. Activity within the central nervous system has also previously been described in oncological research for the inhibition of glioma cell growth in vitro and in vivo [42]. For systemic levels, an increased dose of 240 mg/kg twice daily (instead of 30 mg/kg twice a day) did not increase toxicity in rodents in an experimental model [43]. Therefore, an increased dosage might lead to higher systemic concentrations and tissue accumulation of Nitroxoline, but more pharmacological studies are needed for confirmation.

Further studies verifying our findings in vivo are necessary. Our project has limitations: the sample size is rather small and larger sets of isolates with different Aspergillus species as well as Aspergillus fumigatus strains with different CYP51A mutations are necessary to confirm our findings. Moreover, pharmacokinetic issues regarding Nitroxoline distribution to different body compartments persist, so are in vivo results and the mode of action of Nitroxoline in Aspergillus spp.

In conclusion, Nitroxoline is a small molecule with broad activity against many clinically relevant bacteria and fungi. We demonstrate excellent in vitro activity against Aspergillus spp. Further studies should focus on a larger set of isolates, pharmacological aspects of Nitroxoline distribution and macrophage models.
