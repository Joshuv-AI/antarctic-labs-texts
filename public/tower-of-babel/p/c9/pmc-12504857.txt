Evolutionary Diversification and Functions of the Candidate Male Killing Gene wmk
Authors: Emilie Lefoulon, Sarah R Bordenstein, Leah R Carpenter, Jessica L Buchser, Caroline J Nowicki, Anastasiya A Yakhnina, Jeremy B Gutierrez, Rupinder Kaur, Madangchanok Imchen, Seth R Bordenstein, Daniel Sloan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSymbiont-mediated male killing (MK) is a mechanism that selectively eliminates male offspring, often by disrupting sex-specific developmental processes. In Drosophila melanogaster, the WO-mediated killing gene wmk from Wolbachia prophage WO transgenically reproduces the MK phenotype, yet how the gene evolves and functions across diverse Wolbachia has not been systematically investigated. We analyzed 32 Wolbachia genomes available in the NCBI database to study wmk homologs across different arthropod hosts, reproductive parasitism functions, and Wolbachia supergroups. First, we report at least five distinct wmk phylogenetic clusters (Types I to V), often organized in multigenic dyads or triads. Second, among MK Wolbachia, there is a significantly higher number of wmk genes and diversity in Lepidoptera strains than in Drosophila strains, which exclusively harbor wmk Types I and III. Third, there are three patterns of wmk sequence and genomic organizational changes in Drosophila MK strains that associate with different evolutionary trajectories underpinning the MK phenotype. Fourth, single and combinatory transgenic expression of Types I and III in D. melanogaster uncovers male-biased lethality associated with Type I; however, dual expression of the Types together elicits a major reduction in offspring number. Fifth, wmk genes have low expression level across D. melanogaster developmental stages relative to the cifA and cifB genes, which could explain why cytoplasmic incompatibility is expressed in this system. These findings establish a complex and phylogenetically informed genetic basis of wmk-induced lethality, highlighting the role of gene copy number and expression, wmk Types, and host background in shaping the phenotype.Keywords: Wolbachia, endosymbiont, male killing, symbiosis, bacteriophage WO

Endosymbiotic bacteria such as Wolbachia can manipulate host reproduction for their own benefit through a variety of mechanisms, including male killing (MK). While the genetic basis of MK has made significant progress recently, diversity in sequences and functions requires further characterization. Our study classified wmk (WO-mediated killing) genes into five phylogenetic clades across Wolbachia and revealed that Drosophila MK strains specifically contain Types I and III, arranged in different genomic patterns. We demonstrate that Type I underpins male lethality in the system, and dual expression of specific wmk variants enhances strong lethality in transgenic flies. These findings illuminate how wmk evolved across MK Wolbachia and insect orders, highlighting the variable evolutionary paths of reproductive manipulation in arthropod symbiosis.

Introduction

Maternal inheritance of microorganisms is widespread across animals and plants (Funkhouser and Bordenstein 2013; Corbin et al. 2017; Abdelfattah et al. 2023; Murphy et al. 2023). The most prevalent maternally-inherited bacteria tend to deploy parasitic modifications of sex ratios, gametogenesis, and sex determination (Weeks et al. 2003; Hornett et al. 2022), especially by the bacterial genus Wolbachia that occur widely in arthropods (Stouthamer et al. 1999; Werren et al. 2008; Kaur et al. 2021). Recent genetic studies specify that there are simple, independent genetic bases for the reproductive phenotypes by Wolbachia (Leclercq et al. 2016; Le Page et al. 2017; Fricke and Lindsey 2024). These genes predominantly localize to a specialized eukaryotic association module in prophage WO (Bordenstein and Bordenstein 2016, 2022), and different genes underlying each form of reproductive parasitism raise several questions about their evolutionary dynamics: What is the phylogenetic diversity of the reproductive parasitism genes? How does sequence diversity relate to function, and do homologs of the genes in the same genome evolve independent or synergistic functions? Does genetic conservation and/or divergence associate with host range and functional domains within the genes?

The most common reproductive manipulation by Wolbachia is cytoplasmic incompatibility (CI) (Shropshire et al. 2020), whereby two genes—cifA and cifB—modify developing sperm (Kaur et al. 2024, 2022). These modified sperm cause embryonic death when crossed with aposymbiotic females or females harboring an incompatible Wolbachia strain. Evolution of the cif genes involves rampant horizontal transfer and multiple phylogenetic groups or Types that induce CI (Le Page et al. 2017; Lindsey et al. 2018; Shropshire et al. 2020, 2021). The other manipulation strategies primarily result in a biased host sex ratio including parthenogenesis induction (PI), whereby symbiotic females produce all symbiotic daughters from unfertilized eggs (Ma and Schwander 2017). Putative parthenogenesis-inducing effector proteins, referred to as PifA and PifB (Fricke and Lindsey 2024) or Piff (Li et al. 2024), may alter the key host sex-determining factor Transformer to change sex ratios. Feminizing Wolbachia (FM) convert genetic males into functional females by either blocking transmission of Z chromosome from Z0 females, feminizing the Z0 progeny (Kageyama et al. 2017), or controlling the hormonal regulation pathway via a host genomic insert from Wolbachia (Leclercq et al. 2016).

Finally, among the four major phenotypes, male killing (MK) causes the selective lethality of male offspring at different stages of development, depending on the host species (Stouthamer et al. 1999). MK has evolved independently in multiple endosymbiotic bacteria taxa (Hurst and Jiggins 2000). In the case of Wolbachia, it occurs in a wide range of arthropods, including ladybugs, beetles, pseudoscorpions, flies, moths, and butterflies (Hurst et al. 1999; Fialho and Stevens 2000; Jiggins et al. 2000; Dyer and Jaenike 2004; Zeh et al. 2005; Sheeley and McAllister 2009). Several hypotheses explain the spread of MK Wolbachia in host populations, including sister fitness benefits via resource reallocation (Elnagdy et al. 2011; Zug and Hammerstein 2018) and enhanced spreading under sibling-structured larval competition (Jaenike et al. 2003). However, the effect of MK when female bias becomes extreme can also impose costs on both the host and symbiont by limiting female mating opportunities and reducing the overall population size (Hatcher et al. 1999; Jaenike et al. 2003). Severe MK can limit male mating capacity in some cases (Charlat et al. 2007), consistent with theoretical predictions that deterministic population extinction might occur when male mating capacity becomes severely limited (Hatcher et al. 1999).

The molecular mechanisms of selective male lethality by Wolbachia converge on the modification of sex-specific pathways. Identification of wmk (WO-mediated killing, WD0626 locus tag from Wolbachia wMel) (Perlmutter et al. 2019) was first achieved through transgenic expression in Drosophila melanogaster, which resulted in sex ratio distortion, embryonic death at the time of zygotic transcription, DNA damage, alteration of histone acetylation associated with Dosage Compensation Complex activity, and chromosome segregation defects resembling those observed in natural MK Wolbachia from D. bifasciata (Harumoto et al. 2018). The wmk gene is a putative transcriptional regulator predicted to encode two helix-turn-helix (HTH) XRE family domains that typically bind DNA (Aravind et al. 2005). Genetic variation in synonymous and non-synonymous sites of wmk can have wide-ranging functional effects spanning no male lethality, weak male lethality, and both male and female lethality (Perlmutter et al. 2021). Similarly, two wmk homologs from Wolbachia strain wHm-t in Homona moths cause strong male lethality and both male and female lethality when transgenically expressed in different combinations in D. melanogaster (Arai et al. 2023). Notably, wmk does not elicit MK in native moths (Arai et al. 2025a). In Lepidoptera, the oscar gene kills males (Katsuma et al. 2022), recapitulates MK in moths, and alters dosage compensation through the masculinizer gene (Sugimoto et al. 2015; Katsuma et al. 2022; Fukui et al. 2024). This gene was subsequently identified in prophage WO (Arai et al. 2023). oscar is conserved among MK and FM Wolbachia in Lepidoptera and absent from Wolbachia in Drosophila (Arai et al. 2024b). Moreover, transgenic expression of oscar in D. melanogaster does not induce MK (Arai et al. 2023), accentuating the independent evolutionary origins of MK genes in prophage WO that are sensitive to host background.

Here, we survey complete Wolbachia genomes responsible for MK (Hill et al. 2022; Arai et al. 2023, 2024a, 2024b; Muro et al. 2023) and report five key findings on the phylogeny, evolution, and phenotyping of wmk.

The phylogenomic analysis of 432 single-copy orthologs from 32 Wolbachia strains indicates four broad results. First, the tree discretely separates supergroups A and B of the arthropod Wolbachia (Fig. 1), and relationships between the MK strains are consistent with previous phylogenomic analyses (Perlmutter et al. 2019; Martinez et al. 2021; Hill et al. 2022; Muro et al. 2023; Richardson et al. 2023; Arai et al. 2024a, 2024b). Second, MK Wolbachia are distributed throughout both A and B supergroups and tend to cluster with strains that induce CI, though sample sizes are limited. Third and notably, the supergroup A MK strains wBiau1, wBiau2, wBor, wInn, wPse st. N101, wBif, and wRec strictly occur in Drosophila, while supergroup B strains wBol1-b, wHm-t, wFur, and wSca occur in Lepidoptera. These results suggest there is an association between natural MK, Wolbachia supergroup divergence, and arthropod host order. Fourth, the two strains known to phenotype switch from CI to MK in a new host genetic background (wCauA and wRec) belong to supergroup A (Sasaki et al. 2002). Relatedly, the supergroup A strain wMel from D. melanogaster naturally induces CI but not MK. However, the wmk gene derived from wMel is 99.7% identical to wRec and induces male lethality when overexpressed transgenically (Perlmutter et al. 2019), suggesting that its potential MK activity may depend upon host background and/or regulation of gene expression.

wmk Contains Five Phylogenetic Types (I to V)

To resolve the evolutionary diversity and phylogenetic relationships of wmk, we analyzed 179 wmk homologs from 32 genomes. Alignment and phylogenetic analyses identify five strongly supported sequence clades defined here as Types I to V (Fig. 2). Type I harbors the original reference wmk (WD0626 locus, wMel genome) with homologs from supergroups A and B. Divergence patterns in Type I subtly separate into subtypes Ia (wmk reference) and Ib (Fig. 2). Both Ia and Ib harbor variants that transgenically kill either males or both males and females (Perlmutter et al. 2019, 2020, 2021; Arai et al. 2023). Type I harbors an enriched number of genetic changes, including gene variants fragmenting into two smaller genes or truncations. Most of these genetic changes occur in MK Wolbachia (9 of 14 wmk sequences) (Fig. 2) spanning Drosophila lineages wPse N101, wBiau1, wBiau2, and Lepidopteran lineages wHm-t, wSca, wFur, and wBol1b. In contrast, wmk homologs displaying multiple premature stop codons tend to occur in Type II and III sequences from Wolbachia strains that induce MK (i.e. wSca and wFur) and non-MK Wolbachia (i.e. wWil and wPip), respectively (Fig. 2). Type III wmk wHm-t_12930 (also referred to as wmk-4 in Arai et al. 2023) exhibits no lethality when expressed alone in transgenic D. melanogaster, but when dual-expressed with the generally lethal Type Ib wmk gene wHm-t_12920 (wmk-3), the combination produces strong male lethality (Arai et al. 2023).

Type II, IV, and V homologs thus far do not cause MK and exclusively occur in supergroup B Wolbachia, with two exceptions from wHa and wCauA (Fig. 2). Importantly, these two are explained by horizontal phage WO transfers that frequently occur between supergroups A and B Wolbachia in coinfected hosts (Bordenstein and Wernegreen 2004). As noted above, Type I and III homologs occur widely in both supergroups A and B (Fig. 2), further supporting phage-mediated transfers of variants between the different Wolbachia supergroups. Finally, Type II and IV genes from the parthenogenesis strain wTpre in wasps (Stouthamer et al. 1990) are split into three coding sequences (CDSs), without the conserved HTH domains. This fragmented organization is consistent with relaxed selection and mutation accumulation in the PI strain that does not express MK. Taken together, these findings suggest functional diversity across wmk homologs, with only Type I exhibiting toxic effects (ranging from general to male-specific lethality) and Type III modulating the lethal effect during dual expression with Type I.

wmk Types and Abundance Link with Arthropod Host Range and MK Phenotype

We next set out to resolve if the abundance and/or copy number of wmk Types link to arthropod host range, genomic organization, and phenotypic function (Fig. 3). The most notable observation is a 2.88-fold higher number of wmk observed in the Wolbachia genomes from Lepidoptera (mean: 9.2+/−4.2 copies) than Drosophila (mean:3.2+/−1.9 copies) (Proportions Test: 92/146 vs. 54/146, P-value = 1.488e−05). For example, the Lepidopteran strain wCauA harbors a large number of variants (n = 17), while the Drosophila strains wBiau1, wBiau2, wBif, wRec, and wNo, symbionts of Drosophila spp., have the lowest number, with a single wmk gene.

The phylogenetic Types are frequently arranged either in a dyad (a pair of adjacent wmk Types, or more rarely, two to five genes away from each other) or a triad (when a dyad localizes two to five genes away from a third wmk Type) (Fig. 3c). For instance, the Type Ia reference gene wmk from wMel (WD0626) (Perlmutter et al. 2019) is organized as a triad with a Type III (WD0622) and an adjacent Type Ib (WD0623) just a few genes away (Fig. 4). Single transgenic expression of wmk Type III and Ib homologs in D. melanogaster does not cause MK (Perlmutter et al. 2020). Interestingly, all Drosophila strains that kill males have Type I and/or Type III, in contrast to strains that kill males in Lepidoptera, which harbor more diversity across all five wmk Types (Fig. 3d and e). While wmk does not kill males in Homona moth hosts (Arai et al. 2025a), oscar does (Arai et al. 2025a, 2025b). Since it is unclear whether all of the diverse wmk motifs observed in Lepidopteran strains associate with MK, the sequence diversity could be a signature of relaxed selection on genes no longer serving an MK function and/or selective pressures on genes in an evolutionary arms race with the host.

wmk Genetic Variation Associates with Structural Variation in MK Drosophila

We next evaluated whether wmk Types I and III in MK Drosophila link with genetic and structural variation in their adjacent genomic neighborhoods. Three wmk patterns emerge relative to the reference wmk in wMel prophage WO (Fig. 4). Interestingly, all three groups of sequence organization suggest that MK phenotypically emerges in relic prophage WO regions that are truncated by transposon insertions and subsequent deletions of WO genes over time (Figs. S1 to S3). We elaborate on this observation in the discussion.

The first pattern consists of wRec from the mushroom-feeding fly D. recens and wPse str. N101 from the fruit fly D. pseudotakahashii. They displayed a genomic pattern regionally comparable to that of wMel (Fig. 4a). They both contain Type Ia sequences a few genes away from cifA and cifB. Both Wmk proteins encode a single amino acid difference from the wMel Wmk reference protein. The wRec amino acid change occurs in the first HTH domain (L19S) region and kills both males and females when transgenically expressed in D. melanogaster (Perlmutter et al. 2021). Interestingly, wRec does not induce MK in the natural host D. recens but kills males when transferred to a sister species D. subquinaria (Jaenike 2007). Given the observed toxicity of wRec wmk in the transgenic model D. melanogaster, this amino acid change might not change wmk-induced MK in a symbiotic context, though direct validation in the natural system remains to be demonstrated.

The amino acid change in wPse strain N101 that naturally causes MK occurs in the region after the first HTH domain (P70 H). The protein is intact, while the cifA and cifB genes nearby are fragmented into multiple, small CDSs. A second contig contains intact cif genes, while the Type Ia wmk is split into two CDSs (Fig. 4a). Interestingly, this strain is suspected to coinfect with a CI strain (Richardson et al. 2023). Thus, the contigs of the wPse N101 draft assembly might derive from two different Wolbachia or a misassembled chimera, incorporating sequences from the CI coinfecting genome (Richardson et al. 2023). The complementarity of intact and fragmented CI and MK genes in these contigs is consistent with relaxed selection, with pseudogenization of cif genes possibly underpinning the emergence of MK. In summary, this first group of shared Type Ia variants may associate with Wolbachia strains that can cause CI and/or MK.

Second, the group containing wBiau1, wBiau2, and wBif in the fruit flies D. biauraria and D. bifasciata, respectively, each possesses a single truncated or fragmented wmk homolog (Fig. 4b). wBiau1 and wBiau2 carry the single truncated wmk Type Ia that encodes smaller proteins with only one HTH domain, similar to the first HTH domain present in wmk wMel (identical for wBiau1 and two amino acid differences for wBiau2) (Fig. 4b). In wBiau2, the Type Ia split into three potential CDSs (WBIAU2_11720, 11730, and 11740), with gene 11740 encoding a protein with an HTH domain similar to the second HTH domain in wmk from wMel (Fig. 4). Similar to the aforementioned group, wmk in these two strains is close to the cifA and cifB genes (Fig. 4b); however, multiple transposases are also present nearby (Fig. 4b). In particular, the presence of a DNA transposase with a DDE motif (DDE tnp) upstream could play a regulatory role or be involved in the rearrangement of the split or fragmented HTH-containing genes (Bourque et al. 2018). In contrast, the wBif strain harbors a Type III wmk fragmented into two CDSs, each encoding a protein with a single HTH domain (Fig. 4). These findings suggest, with caution, that some Wolbachia strains may induce the MK phenotype with Wmk proteins encoding a single HTH domain instead of the canonical two HTH domains in the wMel reference.

Third, the closely related wInn and wBor, respectively, symbionts of mushroom-feeding D. innubila and D. borealis, both retain a conserved triad motif with an intact Type III and Type Ib wmk that are two loci away from a Type Ia. The organization of this triad motif is structurally similar to that of wMel, but the wmk genes are more divergent (82.69% to 90% identity) (Fig. 4c). Interestingly, Type Ia in wInn and wBor encodes a protein with three coiled-coil motifs, whereas most of the Type Ia Wmk proteins contain two and the Type Ib and III only one (Fig. 4, gray color). Coiled-coil motifs are structural elements characterized by two or more alpha-helices that wind around each other and exhibit a broad range of functions, including protein–protein interactions involved in transcription factor binding to protein complexes (Burkhard et al. 2001). Transposase and group II intron genes flanking these regions (Fig. 4c) indicate that they may be prone to disruptions, genetic rearrangements, and/or mobilization. This particular triad is conserved in lepidopteran MK Wolbachia wSca and wFur (Fig. S1).

Finally, we identified more divergent genes annotated as transcriptional regulators with two HTH domains, resembling the wmk homologs (Fig. S2). These genes in wBif, wPse, wInn, and wBor tend to occur in prophage-WO relics and have more complex genomic neighborhoods with multiple insertion sequences (ISs) and ankyrin-domain containing genes near the 3′ end of prophage WO. Notably in the case of wBif, which lacks an intact wmk homolog similar to wMel, we identified another gene with two HTH domains positioned near a split cifA gene, suggesting that bioinformatically anchoring homology to only wMel may limit the identification of more diverse wmk-like genes across Wolbachia (Fig. S2 and Table S1).

Patterns of wmk Conservation and Divergence in MK Strains of Drosophila

We next assessed the conserved and divergent amino acid sites among intact Type I Wmk (N = 9, Fig. 5a) and/or Type III Wmk (N = 4, Fig. 5b) proteins based on the criteria that they are from Drosophila MK strains or wMel and harbor two HTH domains. There is a slight trend in which the DNA-binding motifs and HTH domains have higher amino acid identity than the full protein sequence (Fig. 5a-c). The second HTH domain is slightly more conserved than the first HTH domain in all comparisons (e.g. 88% vs. 79% identity for Type I and 86% vs. 81% for Type III). Within the HTH domains, the amino acid identity for the predicted DNA-binding motif is also slightly more conserved or equal to that of the first domain for all comparisons. While Type III Wmk displayed more amino acid identity than Type I (84.5% vs. 77%), this is likely attributable to the limited number of compared sequences that meet our criteria. When compared with the variability observed among intact Type I and Type III Wmk from MK Wolbachia in Lepidoptera (N = 29, Fig. 5d), the full protein is less conserved (57% average identity) but the HTH domains have similar conservation levels to that of the Type I and III sequences from Drosophila MK strains (Fig. 5d). Overall, the HTH domains and DNA binding regions within them exhibit slightly more conservation (Fig. 5). This variability may be due to selection to preserve the functional specificity of the residues and their possible interactions with Drosophila DNA.

Codivergence Between wmk and the Nearby cifA Genes

The genomic arrangement of wmk homologs relative to the cifA and cifB CI genes varies across Wolbachia strains (Figs. S3 and S4). In some genomes, wmk genes are located a few genes away from the cif genes, as in the reference wMel (Fig. 4) (Bordenstein and Bordenstein 2022). In these arrangements, we find that Type I wmk genes tend to associate closest in the genomic region to the phylogenetic Type I cif genes (Figs. S3 to S6, Table S2). For example, the triad motif observed in wMel (Type III + Ib; Type Ia) harbors a Type Ia wmk four genes away from the cifA and cifB genes (Fig. 4). We hypothesized that when wmk and cif genes are in close proximity (less than 5 kb and fewer than seven intervening genes), they may be physically linked to a degree such that they do not independently recombine, potentially leading to codivergent evolution. We assessed this codivergence hypothesis by testing the phylogenetic congruency and genetic divergence relationships between wmk and their neighboring cifA genes. Findings support significant but imperfect codivergence (Fig. 6) based on both the phylogenetically informed ParaFit test (ParaFitGlobal = 1.54, P-value = 0.006) and sequence-based similarity Mantel test (r = 0.82, P-value = 0.0002). This relationship holds when we additionally control sampling bias by removing four highly similar sequences (wMel, wYak, wHa, and wRec) from the analysis (Mantel test; r = 0.8074, P-value = 0.0019). Additionally, the phylogenies of wmk and cifA do not mirror Wolbachia phylogenomic history (Fig. 6), supporting that codivergence arises from reduced recombination in a genomic neighborhood rather than a genome-wide pattern.

Transgenic Expression of Single and Combinatory wmk Homologs in D. melanogaster

Since all MK strains in Drosophila harbor wmk Type I and/or III, we tested whether dual transgenic expression of these Types from wMel elicits an enhanced or different killing phenotype relative to Type I (wmk, WD0626) that typically kills ∼40% of the males (Perlmutter et al. 2019, 2020). We focused on the genes directionally organized in the triad motif of wMel (Fig. 4). Specifically, we examined the combinatory, transgenic effects of Type Ia wmk (WD0626), which is located two genes away from the adjacent dyad of Type Ib wmk (WD0623) and Type III wmk (WD0622) (Tables S3 to S5, Fig. 7 and Figs. Expression of the Type Ia gene (WD0626, wmk) under the armadillo driver causes a female-biased sex ratio, relative to the negative control gene WD0034, indicating approximately 50% male lethality (median = 0.50 male/female) (Fig. 7a). Expression of the Type Ib WD0623 and Type III WD0622 transgenes does not cause lethality. These results validate a previous study using Act (Act5C-gal4) and Arm (Arm-gal4) drivers (Perlmutter et al. 2019, 2020). We also observed a female-biased sex ratio for the dual expression of Types Ia (WD0626) and Ib (WD0623), with 36% male lethality (median = 0.64 male/female, Fig. 7a). A modest sex ratio bias is observed for the dual expression of Type Ib (WD0623) and Type III (WD0622), with 13% male lethality (median = 0.85 male/female, Fig. 7b) and for the dual expression of Type Ia (WD0626) and Type III (WD0623), with 12% male lethality (median = 0.88 male/female, Fig. 7b). Interestingly, all the tested dual expressions associate with a marked decline in total offspring number, indicating strong lethality (Fig. 7c and d).

All of the above experiments were set up with 20 females to 6 males per vial for mating. We performed an additional experiment with single pairwise matings in a vial (one female and one male) to examine individual pair variability. Across the dual expression treatments, all yielded a high proportion of crosses with no progeny (Fig. S8). The strongest lethality was observed for the combination of Types Ia and Ib (WD0626 to WD0623), which, as noted above, do not occur adjacently in a dyad in the Wolbachia genomes. This finding is consistent with potential negative selection against such a dyad occurring in nature. However, the dyad of the Types Ib and III is commonly found across studied Wolbachia genomes, including wMel, and dual expression of Type Ib WD0623 and Type III WD0622 results in strong lethality despite neither gene causing lethality when expressed individually (Fig. 7b and Fig. S8a). In contrast, Type Ib and III wmk homologs from wHm-t in transgenic D. melanogaster were lethal and had no effect when expressed individually, respectively; yet dual expression caused male-specific lethality (Arai et al. 2023). These results suggest that the interaction between wmk homologs is strain-specific, ranging from synergistic lethality to modulatory or antagonistic effects.

wmk and Its Homologs Exhibit Low Gene Expression Levels in wMel of D. melanogaster

Finally, we analyzed the natural expression levels of the wmk genes throughout the development of D. melanogaster. Using a previously published gene expression dataset (Gutzwiller et al. 2015), wmk homologs exhibited a consistently low level of expression across development using the normalized gene expression metric of transcripts per million (TPM, Fig. 8). Compared to the CI factor cifA required for paternal genome modification and embryonic rescue (Kaur et al. 2024, 2022), the different wmk homologs are markedly under-expressed 26- to 79-fold (−4.6 to −6.2 on the log2 scale) (Fig. 8). Low expression may contribute to why wMel in embryos naturally induces rescue of CI rather than MK, though additional factors can play a role. Moreover, the WD0508 homolog (Type II) exhibited higher expression relative to the other wmk homologs (2.5-fold average). The WD0622 homolog (Type III) displayed a slightly higher expression in embryo stages compared to the Type I wmk (WD0626) and WD0623, which are the least expressed among the wmk Types and cifA/B genes. Finally, we compared the expression of the wmk homologs with that of the other genes in the same genomic region to assess whether wmk expression reflects broader transcriptional patterns in its genomic neighborhood, and because neighboring genes are frequently involved in DNA repair and recombination activities (Fig. S9). We elaborate on the significance of these findings with regard to functional implications in the discussion.

Discussion

The selective killing of males by vertically inherited symbionts is, in theory, a simple phenotype subject to strong selective pressures by the symbiont and host (Hurst and Frost 2015), yet its wide distribution and suppression in arthropods (Charlat et al. 2005; Mitsuhashi et al. 2011) as well as its functional genetic underpinnings (Perlmutter et al. 2021; Arai et al. 2025b) reveal unexpected complexity. To address the complexity, we compiled and distilled a comprehensive set of wmk sequences and performed transgenic functional experiments for analysis of the phylogenetic diversity, evolutionary genetic patterns, and functional dynamics of wmk genes across diverse strains and hosts.

Initially, the candidate MK gene wmk (WO-mediated killing [WD0626]) was discovered upon comparative sequence analysis and heterologous expression in transgenic D. melanogaster (Perlmutter et al. 2019). Subsequent studies with wmk homologs from Lepidoptera demonstrated transgenic male lethality caused by the combined effects of two wmk sequences in D. melanogaster (Arai et al. 2023). Meanwhile, recent evidence uncovered that the gene oscar, rather than wmk, causes MK in Lepidoptera (Katsuma et al. 2022; Arai et al. 2025a). These findings motivated an investigation into the diversity, genomic, and functional patterns of wmk homologs across different host-symbiont systems.

We report five central findings: (i) there are at least five distinct phylogenetic Types of wmk homologs (I to V) organized frequently in multigenic clusters (dyads or triads); (ii) a significantly lower number and diversity of wmk Types occur in Drosophila MK strains (Types I and III) versus Lepidopteran MK strains (Types I to V); (iii) three distinct patterns of wmk sequence and genomic organization exist in association with relic prophage WO in various Drosophila MK strains; (iv) transgenic expression of single or combinatory Type I variants from wMel causes male lethality but not Type III; and (v) low gene expression of all wmk homologs in D. melanogaster is consistent with the suppression of MK relative to CI. We discuss these findings and their implications below. Our results provide a phylogenetic framework for understanding wmk genes, highlight the potential of mobile elements in driving the evolution of reproductive manipulation, and demonstrate complex interactions between wmk homologs with variable phenotypic outcomes.

Phylogenetic Framework for Understanding the Diversity and Functions of wmk Types

The wmk homologs are widespread in Wolbachia (Perlmutter et al. 2019; Arai et al. 2023). Our phylogenetic analysis reveals extensive wmk Type diversity across the evolutionary history of Wolbachia supergroups (Perlmutter et al. 2019). Notably, both MK and non-MK Wolbachia strains harbor multiple wmk Types regardless of their induced phenotypes, suggesting the MK functions of wmk gene variants might be suppressed in certain strains or host contexts. Classification of wmk into at least Types I to V offers a new framework for dissecting the genotype–phenotype relationship between wmk sequences, functions, and host-specific evolutionary trajectories.

One consistent result in the dataset is that the Drosophila MK strains exhibit a unique composition of Types compared to the total set of strains. Drosophila MK strains are strictly limited to wmk Types I and III, whereas Lepidoptera have all Types. The Drosophila non-MK strains can harbor Types II and IV, albeit at a lower abundance than in Lepidopteran strains. These results preliminarily suggest that selective pressures constrain the Type diversity in MK flies. Indeed, only transgenic expression of Type I and the combination of Types I and III induce MK in the D. melanogaster model (Perlmutter et al. 2019; Arai et al. 2023), suggesting there may be selection to maintain these Types in Drosophila. Moreover, wmk does not appear to cause MK in Homona moths (Arai et al. 2025a) despite their abundance, which suggests strong host-specific constraints on wmk function. Another explanation for the large diversity of Types is that wmk diversified in response to host suppression mechanisms, as part of an evolutionary arms race that may be specific to each host species. Further tests on the functional roles of wmk genes and their potential regulatory mechanisms are needed to help resolve the differences in genotype–phenotype relationships between Drosophila and Lepidoptera MK. wmk encodes HTH domains, which are commonly found in transcriptional regulators (Aravind et al. 2005). These DNA-binding domains may be functionally specific to fly host DNA targets. HTH domains frequently duplicate and diverge to play roles in the regulatory systems of both bacterial and phage genes (Aravind et al. 2005; Lei and Burton 2021).

Genomic Organization, Structural Variation, and the Role of Mobile Genetic Elements in the Evolution of wmk-Mediated Reproductive Manipulation

wmk Types are organized in triads, dyads, or singletons that were previously described and referred to as the WO protein cluster 2 (WO-PC2) (Bordenstein and Bordenstein 2022) and later as wmk gene cassettes (Arai et al. 2023). Among Drosophila MK strains, these gene arrangements and their genomic neighborhoods tentatively cluster into several categories of structural variation. First, when the Type I wmk is nearly identical to the wMel wmk gene (WD0626), it occurs in two configurations: a single complete wmk gene (wRec and wPse str. N101) or a truncated wmk gene (wBiau1 and wBiau2). In contrast, when wmk genes are more divergent in sequence than wMel wmk (wInn and wBor), they typically exhibit the same triad organization as wMel.

The single Type I wmk cases are noteworthy for their complex phenotypic transitions. wRec is a well-documented multipotent strain that switches from CI to MK upon introduction to a sister-species (Jaenike 2007). However, wRec did not induce MK in all tested D. subquinaria populations, indicating high sensitivity to host genotype (Jaenike 2007). wPse MK is an example of MK suppression by the host genotype (Richardson et al. 2023). Two types of Wolbachia strains have been identified in the natural population of Drosophila pseudotakahashii, a predominant strain-inducing CI and a rare MK-inducing strain that seems to only co-occur with the CI-strain. A nuclear suppressor exists in the host that is associated with MK suppression (Richardson et al. 2023). Altogether, the pattern of a single Type I wmk associated with a sensitive host background could enable the expression of MK. The multipotent potential of Wolbachia has been well studied (Hurst et al. 2002; Kraaijeveld et al. 2011), and cases of transition from CI to MK, including through host suppressor evolution, have been documented in different host contexts (Sasaki et al. 2002; Hornett et al. 2008; Duplouy and O’Neill 2010). Our results are consistent with the independent genetic bases of CI and MK and support the hypothesis that wMel retains the potential for MK, in addition to its known CI-induction ability. Overexpression of its Type I wmk transgene induces male lethality both in this study and in our previous work (Perlmutter et al. 2019).

A second pattern observed in Drosophila MK strains is that the wmk genes are found in a genomic region associated with intact or relic prophage WO, the latter of which are highly disrupted and often flanked by mobile genetic elements. Indeed, phage WO frequently transfers between coinfecting Wolbachia, and transposon insertions can be an ongoing process (Kent et al. 2011). In MK Wolbachia from Drosophila, wmk genes are associated with highly disrupted, smaller prophage WO modules and sequence changes of these genes (e.g. deletions, truncations, and splits of the genes). This suggests a new hypothesis in which the MK trait emerges from multipotent Wolbachia during/after prophage WO genome reductions and rearrangements, facilitated potentially by transposases and shifts in the transcriptional regulation of MK. Bacterial ISs are well known to constitute an important instrument in transition in lifestyles owing to recombination, genome reduction, and horizontal gene transfer (Siguier et al. 2014). Another hypothesis previously proposed is that wmk genes evolved from ancestral high-virulence genes that affected arthropods regardless of sex, but gene duplications and mutations eventually reduced their lethality to enable host survival, followed by specialization for male-killing and further wmk duplications that evolved to counteract host suppressor evolution (Arai et al. 2023). Additionally, a recurring pattern is the frequent presence of genes associated with DNA repair or recombination in the genomic region containing wmk. These include the RadC gene, which contains a JAB domain likely involved in DNA repair, the Recombination-promoting nuclease (Rpn) gene, and the DNA mismatch repair endonuclease MutL gene. Given the high genetic mobility of these genomic regions, these genes could be essential for maintaining DNA integrity (Benler and Koonin 2022). Taken together, these findings suggest that wmk gene organization may play a significant role in reproductive manipulation by Wolbachia, potentially associated with distinct host-symbiont interactions and evolutionary trajectories.

Transgenic Expression of wmk Types Induces Male-Specific Lethality in D. melanogaster

When the wMel wmk transgenes are expressed individually, only the Type Ia variant (wmk, WD0626) induces male lethality in D. melanogaster; Types Ib and III did not. These findings validate previous studies from Drosophila Wolbachia (Perlmutter et al. 2020). Moreover, the wHm-t Type Ib (wHm-t-12920, homolog of WD0623) was lethal for both sexes in D. melanogaster (Arai et al. 2023). The phenotypic differences between wMel Type Ia and Types Ib and III could be associated with sequence divergence or a genetic background effect. It has been previously demonstrated that more divergent wmk sequences have no effect on MK (Perlmutter et al. 2021). However, the difference between the Type Ib variants from wMel and wHm-t may be due to expression and localization differences, as the drivers differed in these experiments.

Since wmk Types tend to occur adjacently or nearby in dyad or triad patterns, we examined whether transgenic expression of the natural Type III–Ib dyad or unnatural Type Ia–Ib and Type Ia–III dyads from wMel induce a stronger phenotype. While all expressed dyads cause reduced offspring numbers, only Type Ia–Ib and Type Ib–III yield evidence of male-biased lethality. The absence of Type Ia–Ib dyads in Wolbachia genomes, coupled with the strongest offspring reduction when they are expressed together, suggests negative selection may prevent this arrangement in nature. The combined effect of wmk Types has been previously tested with sequences from wHm-t in D. melanogaster (Arai et al. 2023). Dual expression of Type Ib wHm-t-1920 and Type III wHm-t-12930 (respectively, homologs of WD0623 and WD0622) resulted in an antagonistic effect whereby Type III wHm-t-12930 appears to rescue the strong lethality caused by Type Ib wHm-t-1920. wHm-t Type Ia also does not kill males on its own, but this gene is split into two genes (wHm-t-12840 and 12850) relative to the intact wMel Type Ia gene. Similarly, wHm-t-12850 appears to rescue the strong lethality caused by wHm-t-12840. On the contrary, all tested combinations of wmk wMel resulted in a synergistic effect, increasing overall lethality. This suggests interactions between wmk homologs, although the nature and intensity can vary.

Low Gene Expression of wmk Correlates with Phenotypic Outcomes

While wMel naturally induces CI in D. melanogaster, it also harbors the wmk gene, the first discovered candidate MK gene from Wolbachia that can transgenically induce male lethality (Perlmutter et al. 2019). This finding raises the question of whether wMel could be a multipotent Wolbachia in which differential regulation of CI and MK gene expression may influence phenotypic outcomes. We examined this hypothesis using publicly available transcriptomic data (Gutzwiller et al. 2015) and found that there is a low level of expression of all wmk genes (except the Octomom gene WD0508) across the development of D. melanogaster relative to the cif genes or even the neighboring genes. Consequently, wmk Type expression may be tightly regulated. Combined with the lethal potential of dual expression of wmk homologs described above, we speculate that Wolbachia strains may have a regulatory system to optimize survival of the host, though this premise requires additional experimental support. Further investigation of wmk gene expression in single-phenotype or multipotent Wolbachia strains, such as wRec, would help validate this hypothesis.

Notably, among the different wmk homologs in wMel, the Type II WD0508 appears to be unique, as it is more highly expressed across D. melanogaster development. Transgenic expression of WD0508 does not induce MK; it increases Wolbachia densities and thus may putatively confer a fitness benefit to Wolbachia (Le Page et al. 2017), hence explaining its higher expression levels and the differential functions of wmk homologs. Interestingly, WD0508 occurs in the prophage WO-associated Octomom region based on sequence homology and synteny (Le Page et al. 2017). Octomom putatively originated by splitting from the eukaryotic association module of prophage WO into a WO-like island (Bordenstein and Bordenstein 2022). Its copy number is linked to Wolbachia growth rate in Drosophila (Chrostek and Teixeira 2015), suggesting that wmk homologs such as WD0508 may contribute to the regulation of Wolbachia proliferation.

Conclusion

The results suggest that wmk sequence diversity is substantial, with distinct phylogenetic Types. Moreover, male-specific lethality depends on factors spanning phylogenetic Type, gene regulation, changes in the prophage WO genomic neighborhood, and host genetic background influences. Future work focused on the functional specificity of wmk genes, the essentiality of conserved or rapidly evolving domains/amino acids, and the molecular mechanisms of both MK induction and host suppression are needed to unravel how wmk acutely kills males to assist the spread of Wolbachia.

Methods

Genome Completeness Estimates

We conducted a genomic analysis of 32 published Wolbachia complete or draft genomes; details of the dataset are summarized in Table 1. The selected genomes were chosen to represent the diversity of Wolbachia reproductive parasitism strategies, categorized into different phenotypic groups. Genome completeness was assessed using the BUSCO pipeline (Busco v5.7) based on the set of 432 orthologue genes common among alphaproteobacteria (lineage dataset: alphaproteobacteria_odb10; mode prok_genome_prod and prodigal as gene predictor) (Simão et al. 2015) (see Supplementary information S1).

Wolbachia Phylogenetic Analysis Based on Orthologous Proteins

Single-copy orthologous proteins for phylogenetic analysis of Wolbachia were identified from the 32 Wolbachia genomes using Orthofinder version 2.5.5 (Emms and Kelly 2015) (see Supplementary information S2). For genomes lacking an annotated protein dataset, the Prokka pipeline (version 1.14.6) predicted proteins (Seemann 2014) (see Supplementary information S1). A supermatrix of orthologous, amino acid, sequence alignments was generated, and the poorly aligned positions of the alignments were filtered using Gblocks (parameter per default) version 0.91b (Talavera and Castresana 2007), so indels were not explicitly excluded or coded. All phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the maximum-likelihood (ML) inference method with IQ-TREE version 2.2.2.9 (Minh et al. 2020) (see Supplementary information S2). ModelFinder, integrated within the IQ-TREE pipeline, was used to evaluate the most appropriate model of evolution for our dataset according to the BIC index. The robustness of each node was assessed using ultrafast bootstrap approximation with 1,000 replicates. The phylogenetic trees were edited in FigTree (https://github.com/rambaut/figtree/) and Inkscape (https://inkscape.org/).

Identification of wmk Genes and Proteins

Using BLASTN, we searched for homologs of the candidate MK gene wmk (WD0626) along with its previously described homologs (WD0622, WD0623, WD0255, and WD0508) from the wMel genome across the dataset of 32 genomes (Camacho et al. 2009). Each identified sequence was manually curated to exclude smaller matches (<40 bp) and verify CDS integrity. In addition, we performed a BLASTP homology analysis on protein sequences to identify potential additional homologs. The phylogeny of wmk and its homologs was generated from aligned DNA sequences to allow the inclusion of genes with potential frameshifts or premature stop codons that might be overlooked in protein-level analyses. DNA sequences were aligned using the Geneious Prime (version 2025.0.3) alignment pipeline with default parameters (65% similarity 5.0/−4.0 Cost matrix, 12 Gap open penalty, 3 gap extension penalty, and 2 refinement iterations). Indels were retained as gaps and not explicitly excluded or coded. An initial phylogenetic analysis was performed to identify and remove sequences that could potentially introduce artifacts to the analysis, including highly divergent sequences and those of insufficient length (see Table S1). The phylogenetic analysis was performed using IQ-TREE as described above, but the robustness of each node was evaluated using two complementary approaches: non-parametric bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates and SH-like approximate likelihood ratio tests. Different support metrics across analyses are provided in Supplementary information S2 and Fig. S10. Each selected wmk homolog was screened at the protein sequence levels for the presence of the number and types of HTH domains using ScanProsite and Interpro (de Castro et al. 2006; Blum et al. 2025). In addition, ScanProsite predicted the DNA-binding motif in the HTH domain based on matches with the PROSITE collection of motifs. Furthermore, genomic localization and neighboring genes were categorized by manual annotation. The schematics of the protein domain structure and genomic regions were produced in PowerPoint and Inkscape. The phylogenetic clade, CDS structure, and HTH domains of each wmk homolog were classified by reproductive phenotype and statistically analyzed using the R environment (package ggplot2) (R Core team 2023). Protein sequences were aligned using the PROMALS3D web server with default parameters (Pei et al. 2008). Similarities between the sequences and consensus sequences were established using Geneious Prime (version 2025.0.3). These similarities in data were plotted using the R environment (package ggplot2) (R Core team 2023).

Identification of cifA, cifB, and oscar genes

Using BLASTN and BLASTP, we identified cifA, cifB, and oscar across the 32 genome dataset based on previously described sequences in the literature (Lindsey et al. 2018; Arai et al. 2024b) (see Table S2). As described above, we produced three independent phylogenetic analyses based on the protein sequences, including the identified proteins from the 33 analyzed genomes.

Topological Congruency Between wmk and cifA Genes

Pairs of cifA and wmk genes separated by less than 5 kb and fewer than seven intervening genes were isolated to produce two ML phylogenies, as described above. The matrix of pairwise distance is calculated using MEGA (p-distance) (Tamura et al. 2021). We tested the congruency with two different methods (see Supplementary information S3). First, the global-fit method was used to establish potential codivergence patterns between cifA and wmk using the ParaFit function (Legendre et al. 2002). ParaFit uses the patristic distances derived from the two phylogenies and the association matrix to produce a matrix of principal coordinates. The ParaFitGlobal statistic is calculated based on the congruence of these principal coordinates and represents the strength of the overall congruence. The significance of the ParaFitGlobal statistic is tested using permutation testing (10,000), where the associations are randomized to generate a null distribution. Second, we used a Mantel test on the two distance matrices to determine if a significant correlation exists between the genetic relationships. The Mantel test is a nonparametric method that compares the pairwise distance matrices of the two gene groups and determines if they are significantly correlated. Together, these two methods provide a broad assessment of the evolutionary relationships between cifA and wmk homologs, with ParaFit analyzing codivergence patterns while controlling for the phylogenetic relationships of the sequences and the Mantel test testing for a correlation in genetic divergence.

Drosophila melanogaster Strains, Husbandry, and Sex Ratio Assays

Transgenic D. melanogaster with genes WD0626 (wmk), WD0622 (wmk homolog), WD0623 (wmk homolog), and WD0034 (no killing control gene) were previously published (Perlmutter et al. 2019, 2021) and codon-optimized as described earlier (Le Page et al. 2017). WD0034, a nonphage hypothetical protein-coding gene from wMel Wolbachia, serves as a control transgene that does not induce a sex-biased ratio or recapitulate CI in D. melanogaster. Here, we generated new codon-optimized transgenic lines expressing gene combinations that follow the genome organization and transcriptional direction (WD0626, WD0623, and WD0622). Three separate lines were generated: WD0623 + 622, WD0626 + 622, and WD0626 + 623, each with a T2A sequence inserted between them to allow for bicistronic expression and subsequence peptide cleavage of the two products (Table S6). The same transgenic methods and codon optimizations were followed for all lines. Briefly, each construct was synthesized as DNA and cloned into the pTIGER plasmid by GenScript Biotech (Piscataway, NJ). The plasmids were then sent to BestGene (Chino Hills, CA), which performed injections into embryos with the attP40 background line carrying PhiC31 integrase to facilitate site-specific integration at the attP40 locus. Successful transformants were identified based on the red eye marker, and homozygous lines were established through counterselection against CyO. Each transgenic line was derived from the offspring of a single isofemale transformant.

Drosophila melanogaster were reared on a standard nutritional blend of quality molasses, enriched cornmeal, and yeast (5.07% cornmeal, 1.22% cornstarch, 1.52% yeast, 4.65% dry molasses, 0.574% agar, 0.15% methylparaben, 0.39% propionic acid, and 0.52% ethanol (Archon Scientific Inc, NC). Stocks were maintained at 22 °C. During virgin collections, stocks were kept at 18 °C overnight and 22 °C during the day.

To assess the effect of the transgenes on MK, as measured by sex ratios (N = 15 replicates per cross), crosses were set up in vials with 20 uninfected, 4- to 7-d-old Arm-gal4 virgin females (BDSC 1560: w[*]; P{w[+mW.hs]=GAL4-arm.S}11) and six uninfected, 1- to 2-d-old transgenic males. They were left on the media to lay eggs for 2 d at 22 °C, after which adults were removed. The vials were maintained at 22 °C, and pupae were counted before adult emergence. Emerging adults were scored for both sex and total number. The Arm-gal4 driver line is homozygous viable, so all progeny inherit the Arm-gal4 and express the transgene. Two independent experiments have been conducted using a ratio of 20 females to 6 males to test all the crosses. An additional experiment was conducted using a ratio of one female to one male, which were left for 7 d to lay eggs. Additional control crosses have been included in this assay: Arm-gal4 female × wild-type male (BDSC 8622) to test the potential pathogenicity of Arm-gal4, and wild-type female × UAS-wmk male to test potential leaking of expression of the transgene (Table S7). Sex ratio results were visualized using R (package ggplot2) (R Core team 2023), and differences between groups were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For the total offspring counts, a Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's many-to-one post hoc test was performed, using the Arm/WD0034 offspring values as the reference (raw data and statistical analyses detailed in the Table S3 to S5 and S7 and Supplementary information S4).

Gene Expression of the wmk Homologs

We re-analyzed a previously published RNAseq dataset for Wolbachia (Bioproject PRJNA75285) (Gutzwiller et al. 2015) (see Supplementary information S5). It contains 54 libraries (SRA SRP001696) representing 30 stages of D. melanogaster. After filtering using Trim Galore! (v0.6.10) for quality control and adapter trimming (Krueger 2015), paired-end reads were trimmed using a quality threshold of 20, with a minimum read length of 20 bp. Adapter sequences were removed, and the reads were aligned to the D. melanogaster genome release 6, version 12, using STAR (version 2.7.11) (Dobin et al. 2013). The unaligned reads were mapped to the wMel reference genome (Genbank AE017196). The raw RNAseq counts were evaluated based on the bam files using the featureCounts tools (version 2.0.8) (Liao et al. 2014) (Table S8). The mean normalized read counts in units of TPM was established by: (gene length + 99), where 99 corresponds to read length (100)—1, and these values were multiplied by 1e6/(sum of values in stage) (Li 2013) (Table S9). We selected the wmk homologs WD0255, WD0508, WD0622, WD0623, and WD0626 (wmk reference), as well as the neighbor genes WD0254, WD0257, WD507, WD0509, WD0621, WD0625, WD0628, WD0631 (cifA), and WD0632 (cifB) (Table S10).
