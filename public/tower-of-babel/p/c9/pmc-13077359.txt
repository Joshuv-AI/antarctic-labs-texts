The geriatric nutritional risk index predicts pathological complete response and anastomotic leakage in esophageal cancer patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy
Authors: Jingyuan Niu, Jiahao Huang, Min Zhang, Bin Yang, Tao Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEsophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) poses a significant global health challenge. Pathological complete response (pCR) to neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgery is a crucial predictor of survival, but treatment response is highly variable. Nutritional status and systemic inflammation are key determinants of outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI), alone and combined with body mass index (BMI), as a predictor for pCR and anastomotic leak (AL) in this patient population.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 143 ESCC patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy and radical esophagectomy were enrolled. The GNRI was calculated using serum albumin and body weight. The optimal GNRI cutoff for predicting pCR was determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Patients were stratified into high (≥106.7) and low (<106.7) GNRI groups. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors for pCR and AL. The synergistic value of GNRI and BMI was assessed through collinearity analysis, mutual adjustment in multivariate models, and model fit statistics [Akaike information criterion (AIC), likelihood ratio tests].ResultsThe pCR rate was significantly higher in the high GNRI group (39.3% vs. 10.2%, P<0.001). GNRI demonstrated good predictive accuracy for pCR [area under the curve (AUC) =0.706] with high sensitivity (87.2%). Multivariate analysis confirmed high GNRI as an independent predictor for pCR [odds ratio (OR)=3.90] and a protective factor against AL (OR =0.39). The AL rate was significantly lower in the high GNRI group (16.67% vs. 33.90%, P=0.02). Despite a strong correlation between BMI and GNRI (R=0.76, P<0.001), both retained independent predictive value for pCR after mutual adjustment. A combined model (GNRI + BMI + confounders) yielded the best fit (lowest AIC=139.8) and the highest discriminative ability (AUC=0.873).ConclusionsPretreatment GNRI is a powerful, independent predictor for pCR and AL in ESCC patients. Its high negative predictive value helps identify patients with a very low probability of responding to standard neoadjuvant therapy. The combination of GNRI and BMI provides superior predictive power for pCR, as they offer complementary information. These simple, cost-effective indices should be integrated into initial patient assessment to improve risk stratification and guide personalized treatment strategies.

Key findings

• Pretreatment geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI) independently predicts pathological complete response (pCR) and anastomotic leakage in esophageal cancer patients. A low GNRI (<106.7) identifies patients with very low pCR rates (10.2% vs. 39.3% in high GNRI group) and doubled anastomotic leakage risk. GNRI and body mass index (BMI) provide complementary predictive value.

What is known and what is new?

• Nutritional status influences cancer outcomes, and GNRI has prognostic value in oncology. This study newly establishes GNRI dual predictive role for both treatment response and surgical complications in neoadjuvant therapy. It demonstrates GNRI high negative predictive value and synergistic effect with BMI.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• GNRI should be integrated into initial patient assessment to identify high-risk patients. For those with low GNRI, we should consider: treatment intensification/modification; enhanced nutritional support; and proactive surgical planning. These readily available indices enable personalized risk stratification and treatment optimization.

Introduction

Background

Esophageal cancer remains a significant global health challenge, ranking among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide (1). Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is the predominant histological subtype in many regions, including East Asia (2). The current standard of care for locally advanced ESCC often involves neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgical resection, with pathological complete response (pCR) serving as a crucial surrogate marker for treatment efficacy and long-term survival (3). Despite advances in multimodal treatment, response to neoadjuvant therapy varies considerably among patients, highlighting the need for reliable predictive biomarkers to guide personalized treatment strategies (4).

Nutritional status and systemic inflammation have emerged as important determinants of cancer outcomes (5). Malnutrition and inflammatory processes can adversely affect treatment tolerance, therapeutic efficacy, and postoperative recovery (6). The geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI), initially developed to assess nutritional risk in elderly patients, has recently gained attention in oncological research (7). This composite index, incorporating both serum albumin levels and body weight parameters, offers a comprehensive assessment of nutritional status while reflecting underlying inflammatory processes (8). Similarly, body mass index (BMI) serves as a readily available indicator of nutritional reserves and body composition (9).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Although previous studies have explored the prognostic value of nutritional indicators in esophageal cancer (10,11), several knowledge gaps remain. The combined predictive value of GNRI and BMI for treatment response has not been thoroughly investigated, and their potential collinearity requires careful statistical evaluation. Furthermore, while most research has focused on survival outcomes, the relationship between nutritional indices and specific endpoints such as pCR and surgical complications deserves further exploration.

Objective

This study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the predictive value of GNRI, both alone and in combination with BMI, for pCR and anastomotic leak (AL) in ESCC patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgery. We hypothesized that pretreatment GNRI would serve as a significant predictor of treatment response and postoperative complications, and that its combination with BMI would provide complementary predictive information. Through rigorous statistical modeling including collinearity assessment and mutual adjustment, we sought to clarify the independent contributions of these nutritional indices to treatment outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-aw-2365/rc).

Methods

Patient selection criteria

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Province Cancer Hospital (Approval No. KY2024130). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study, which involved no more than minimal risk and used only de-identified data from routine clinical care. Patients were included if they had: (I) pathologically confirmed ESCC; (II) all included patients received neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy, and that no none received neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) cases were included; and (III) available complete clinical and follow-up records. Exclusion criteria were: (I) presence of other malignant tumors; (II) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; and (III) incomplete perioperative data or loss to follow-up; (IV) patients undergo definitive chemoradiotherapy followed by salvage esophagectomy.

Data collection and definitions

Clinical and laboratory data were systematically collected from electronic medical records. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, BMI, smoking status, and alcohol use. Comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes were recorded. Tumor characteristics included location, length, and clinical tumor-nodes-metastasis (TNM) stage based on the 8th edition American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) criteria. Treatment details encompassed the type of neoadjuvant therapy, surgical approach, and pathological tumor staging after surgery (ypTNM). Nutritional assessment involved serum albumin levels, body weight, and height. The GNRI was calculated using the established formula: GNRI =1.489× albumin (g/L) +41.7× (actual body weight/ideal body weight), where ideal body weight was derived from the Lorentz equation considering height and sex. GNRI was calculated after completion of neoadjuvant therapy and immediately prior to surgery, based on serum albumin and body weight measured at the preoperative evaluation before esophagectomy. Outcome measures included pCR, defined as the absence of residual viable tumor cells in both the primary tumor and resected lymph nodes (ypT0N0), and postoperative complications such as anastomotic leakage, pulmonary infection, pleural effusion, and recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, which were graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. Tumor regression grade (TRG) was assessed according to the College of American Pathologists (CAP) tumor regression grading system.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range based on their distribution and compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies with percentages and compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors independently associated with pCR and anastomotic leakage, with variables showing P<0.10 in univariate analysis included in the multivariate model. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The predictive performance of GNRI for pCR was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the optimal cutoff value determined by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity −1). The area under the curve (AUC) was used to assess discriminative ability. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

External validation

To assess the generalizability of the GNRI cut-off derived from the primary cohort, we performed a temporal external validation using an independent cohort treated during a different period (July 2024 to June 2025) who underwent planned esophagectomy after neoadjuvant therapy. Baseline demographic, clinical, and tumor characteristics of the validation cohort are provided in Table S1. The GNRI threshold identified in the derivation cohort GNRI =106.7 was clinically rounded to 107 and was pre-specified for validation; patients in the validation cohort were stratified into high (GNRI ≥107) and low (GNRI <107) groups without re-estimating the cut-off in the validation dataset.

Results

GNRI predicts pCR in esophageal cancer

A total of 143 esophageal cancer patients who received neoadjuvant therapy and subsequent surgery were included in this study. The optimal cutoff value for GNRI, determined by ROC curve analysis, was 106.7. The ROC analysis showed that the AUC of GNRI for predicting pCR was 0.706, indicating good predictive accuracy. The corresponding sensitivity and specificity based on the Youden index were 0.872 and 0.50, respectively (Figure 1A). According to the ROC results, patients were divided into two groups: a high GNRI group (≥106.7, n=84) and a low GNRI group (<106.7, n=59). Among the entire cohort, 39 patients achieved pCR. The pCR rate in the high GNRI group was 39.3% (33/84), which was significantly higher than that in the low GNRI group (10.2%, 6/59; P<0.001) (Figure 1B).

Univariate analysis indicated that gender, smoking history, drinking history, BMI, GNRI, and biopsy differentiation grade were significantly associated with pCR (all P<0.05). Specifically, male patients were less likely to achieve pCR compared to females (OR =0.20, 95% CI: 0.09–0.45, P<0.001). Patients with a history of smoking (OR =3.17, 95% CI: 1.47–6.80, P=0.003) or drinking (OR =4.92, 95% CI: 2.12–11.42, P<0.001) had higher pCR rates. Similarly, BMI >24 (OR =4.10, 95% CI: 1.89–8.89, P<0.001) and higher GNRI (OR =5.72, 95% CI: 2.21–14.79, P<0.001) were also associated with increased likelihood of pCR. Furthermore, compared to well-differentiated tumors (Grade 1), moderately differentiated (Grade 2, OR =0.15, 95% CI: 0.06–0.40, P<0.001) and poorly differentiated tumors (Grade 3, OR =0.30, 95% CI: 0.10–0.90, P=0.03) showed significantly reduced pCR rates.

Multivariate analysis confirmed that male gender (OR =0.22, 95% CI: 0.09–0.57, P=0.002), BMI >24 (OR =3.10, 95% CI: 1.17–8.17, P=0.02), higher GNRI (OR =3.90, 95% CI: 1.24–12.26, P=0.02), and poorer tumor differentiation (Grade 2: OR =0.15, 95% CI: 0.05–0.45, P<0.001; Grade 3: OR =0.21, 95% CI: 0.06–0.78, P=0.02) were independent predictors of pCR. Although sex was associated with pCR in our model, evidence is inconsistent (12). Variables such as age, diabetes, hypertension, tumor location, tumor length, cT stage, cN stage, and adverse reaction grade did not show significant associations in either univariate or multivariate analyses.

To identify predictors for AL after neoadjuvant therapy in esophageal cancer, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed (Table S2). Univariate analysis showed that most variables, including gender, age, comorbidities, and tumor characteristics, were not significantly associated with AL. However, a higher GNRI was significantly associated with reduced risk (OR =0.39, 95% CI: 0.18–0.86, P=0.02). This association remained significant in multivariate analysis (OR =0.39, 95% CI: 0.18–0.86, P=0.02). No other variables were independent predictors. The incidence of AL was significantly associated with preoperative GNRI status. In our cohort, the overall AL rate was 23.78% (34/143), including 16.67% (14/84) in the high-GNRI group and 33.90% (20/59) in the low-GNRI group (Table 3). Reported AL rates vary substantially across studies due to differences in anastomotic location and event definition/ascertainment; series dominated by intrathoracic anastomosis and/or reporting only clinically significant leaks often yield lower rates. In the ICAN randomized trial, the overall AL rate was 34.1% with cervical anastomosis vs. 12.3% with intrathoracic anastomosis, indicating that rates around 30% can occur when cervical anastomosis is used and leaks are systematically captured (13). We additionally clarified that AL was defined and reported according to the ECCG standardized framework to facilitate cross-study comparison (14).

Synergistic predictive value of BMI and GNRI for pCR

In both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, BMI and GNRI were significantly associated with pCR. Since both are related to body weight—with BMI reflecting general nutritional and body composition status, while GNRI additionally incorporates albumin levels, accounting for both inflammatory and nutritional status—a Spearman correlation analysis indicated a strong correlation between BMI and GNRI (R=0.76, P<0.001), suggesting potential collinearity if both were included in the same model (Figure 2A). To clarify their independent predictive values, three multivariate models were constructed, adjusting for confounders including gender, smoking history, drinking history, hypertension, tumor location, tumor length, cT stage, cN stage, and biopsy tumor differentiation (Table S3).

In multivariate logistic regression, Model 1 (BMI + confounders) showed that BMI was significantly associated with pCR (OR =5.92, P<0.001). Model 2 (GNRI + confounders) indicated that GNRI was also an independent predictor of pCR (OR =3.80, P=0.002). When both were included in Model 3 (BMI + GNRI + confounders), both BMI (OR =3.51, P=0.03) and GNRI (OR =2.65, P=0.03) remained statistically significant, suggesting that each retains independent predictive value after mutual adjustment. Akaike information criterion (AIC) comparisons showed that the AIC values for Model 1, Model 2, and Model 3 were 142.84, 142.76, and 139.8, respectively, with Model 3 demonstrating the best fit (Table S3). Further likelihood ratio tests indicated that adding GNRI to a model already containing BMI significantly improved model fit (ΔDeviance =5.04, P=0.03); similarly, adding BMI to a model already containing GNRI also significantly enhanced the fit (ΔDeviance =4.95, P=0.03) (Table S4). In conclusion, both BMI and GNRI are independent predictors of pCR, and their combination provides complementary information, significantly enhancing the predictive ability of the model.

ROC curve analysis showed that the AUC of Model 1 (BMI + confounders) was 0.862, Model 2 (GNRI + confounders) was 0.859, and Model 3 (BMI + GNRI + confounders) was 0.873. All three AUC values were relatively high, indicating good discriminative ability of the models (Figure 2B). Model 3 had the highest AUC, suggesting that the combination of BMI and GNRI outperforms either indicator alone in predicting pCR. Together with the AIC and likelihood ratio test results, these findings further support that both BMI and GNRI are independent predictors and that their joint use provides complementary information, thereby improving the overall predictive performance of the model.

Discussion

The present study provides comprehensive evidence that baseline nutritional and inflammatory status, as evaluated by the GNRI, serves as a significant and independent predictor for both pCR and the risk of AL in esophageal cancer patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgery. Our most pivotal finding is that GNRI functions as a powerful negative predictor. The remarkably low pCR rate of merely 10.2% in patients with GNRI <106.7 starkly contrasts with the 39.3% rate in the high GNRI group. This indicates that patients with impaired nutritional—inflammation status have an exceedingly low probability of achieving a complete response with current neoadjuvant regimens. GNRI may serve as a pragmatic tool for pretreatment risk stratification. Patients with low GNRI appear to have a higher probability of non-pCR; this information should be used to guide nutritional optimization, enhanced supportive care, and closer response assessment, and to consider clinical trial enrollment or treatment tailoring where appropriate, rather than to withhold standard neoadjuvant therapy (15,16).

Although the overall discriminatory accuracy of GNRI alone was moderate (AUC =0.706), its exceptionally high sensitivity (87.2%) underscores its reliability in correctly identifying those patients who will achieve pCR, which is its primary clinical strength. However, the concomitant low specificity (50.0%) elucidates its limitation: good nutritional status, while necessary, is not a sufficient condition to guarantee pCR. This substantial false-positive rate implies that other biological factors, particularly intrinsic tumor characteristics such as differentiation grade (which remained a powerful independent predictor in our multivariate model), are paramount determinants of ultimate treatment sensitivity (17,18).

Nutritional indices such as GNRI may capture a composite “host-status” signal reflecting both nutritional reserve and systemic inflammation, which could plausibly influence response to neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy. Albumin, a key component of GNRI, is a negative acute-phase reactant and may decrease with inflammatory activation rather than nutrition alone (19). In ESCC treated with neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy, GNRI has been evaluated in relation to therapeutic response, supporting its plausibility as a response-associated marker (20). In addition, sarcopenia and treatment-induced skeletal muscle loss have been linked to treatment tolerance/toxicity and outcomes in this setting, suggesting that compromised physiologic reserve/systemic catabolism may reduce the likelihood of achieving pCR (21).

Beyond predicting oncologic outcome, our analysis revealed a significant protective role of a higher GNRI against AL, a serious and potentially fatal surgical complication. The prevalence of AL was halved in the High GNRI group (OR =0.39, P=0.02), and multivariate analysis confirmed GNRI as an independent protective factor (OR =0.39, P=0.02). This aligns with established literature linking malnutrition and hypoalbuminemia to impaired wound healing and collagen synthesis, thereby increasing the risk of anastomotic dehiscence (22-24). This dual role of GNRI—predicting both treatment efficacy and surgical risk—reinforces its value in the comprehensive pre-therapeutic risk assessment and multidisciplinary management of these complex patients (22).

The strong correlation observed between BMI and GNRI (R=0.76, P<0.001) was anticipated, as both are anthropometric measures related to body mass and nutriture. However, our multivariate modeling with mutual adjustment robustly demonstrated that both BMI and GNRI retain independent predictive value for pCR. The significant improvement in model fit (lowest AIC of 139.8 and significant likelihood ratio tests) and the superior discriminative ability (AUC =0.873) when both variables are combined indicate they provide complementary information. BMI is a general measure of energy reserves and body composition (25), whereas GNRI incorporates serum albumin, adding a critical dimension of systemic inflammation and visceral protein status (7,26). Prior esophageal cancer data likewise support the clinical relevance of GNRI in perioperative outcomes and prognosis (8,26,27). Complementary to clinicobiologic indices, contemporary imaging-based models can further aid pCR prediction and may be integrated with GNRI/BMI in future work (28,29). Notably, several conventional tumor-related variables (tumor length, cT, cN, and grade) were not independently associated with pCR in our cohort. Prior reports are heterogeneous (30), and the accuracy limitations of clinical staging [endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS)/computed tomography (CT)/positron emission tomography (PET)] (31,32) and biopsy-based grading discordance (33) may attenuate associations. Although sex was associated with pCR in our model, evidence is inconsistent (12); thus, it is treated as exploratory without mechanistic interpretation.

Limitations

There are several limitations in this study. (I) Our study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design. Although all patients proceeded to surgery after completing the intended neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy course, dose reductions, delays, or early discontinuation may have occurred, particularly among patients with severe dysphagia or cachexia, and such treatment modifications could confound the association between GNRI and pCR. Because treatment intensity data were not consistently captured across the cohort, residual confounding cannot be excluded. (II) Its retrospective and single-center design inherently introduces potential selection and information biases. The GNRI cutoff value, though derived objectively by ROC analysis, requires external validation in larger, prospective, multi-institutional cohorts. Our temporal validation provides external support for the GNRI cut-off; however, larger multicenter prospective studies are warranted to further confirm its clinical utility. Furthermore, the specific biological mechanisms—such as detailed immune profiles or cytokine levels—linking nutrition, inflammation, and chemo-sensitivity were not explored and remain a crucial area for future research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, pre-therapy GNRI is a potent negative predictor for pCR and a protective factor against AL in esophageal cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant therapy and surgery. Its high sensitivity allows for the early identification of patients with a very low probability of response, enabling potential treatment personalization and early supportive care interventions. The combination of GNRI and BMI, reflecting complementary aspects of host status, significantly enhances predictive accuracy for pCR. We recommend integrating these simple, cost-effective, and readily available metrics into initial patient assessments to better stratify risk, guide therapeutic decision-making, and ultimately improve outcomes in the multimodal management of esophageal cancer.
