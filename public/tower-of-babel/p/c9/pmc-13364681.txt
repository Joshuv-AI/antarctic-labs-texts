Developmental chronology of mouse embryo from 2-cell stage through birth
Authors: Shangtao Cao, Lihui Lin, Huijian Feng, Pan Chen, Guangming Wu, Baomei Cai, Mengjie Pan, Qingmei Shen, Huan Chen, Xuzhao Zhai, Quanyou Cai, Ziyu Feng, Yingying Zhao, Dingxin Li, Chuman Wu, Fuqing Jiang, Weifang Liang, Yuanbang Mai, Jincan Ke, Yao Zhang, Pinghui Zhu, Yuting Liu, Mengying Zeng, Minjing Ke, Fangru Lu, Yuan Yu, He Liu, Xiongzhi Quan, Wei Pang, Shilong Chu, Guizhong Cui, Fangfang Qu, Shen Gu, Guangdun Peng, Jiekai Chen, Duanqing Pei

ABSTRACT
Current single-cell atlases of mouse embryos are limited in temporal resolution and cellular or sequencing depth coverage. Here we report the mouse developmental Cell and Lineage Atlas (mdCLA), which covers 37 time points across the entire embryonic development from the 2-cell stage (E1.5) to birth (E19.0), with two million cells and a median of 4,500 genes detected per cell. The temporal coverage and data quality of mdCLA surpass the existing mouse embryonic atlases, enabling the identification of organ-specific cell type and gene expression. Using mdCLA, we uncovered divergent gene expression profiles between early and late-stage metanephric progenitor populations, marked by the upregulation of epithelial differentiation and immune response pathways in the late-stage population. Moreover, by analysing the newly generated cell types over time, we revealed an epithelium-specific accelerated differentiation process that occurs after E14.5. The transcription factor upregulated during late developmental stages was validated, where specific ablation of the Tub gene in the pituitary epithelium led to a reduction in the populations producing follicle-stimulating hormone. Consequently, this genetic modification resulted in postnatal obesity in mice. Together, mdCLA offers a comprehensive and high-quality reference, providing critical insights into the dynamic processes and mechanisms underlying mammalian embryogenesis.

Main

Understanding how a mammalian fertilized egg develops into a fully functional individual is a fundamental question in developmental biology. Unravelling this process requires a comprehensive understanding of lineage relationships, transcriptional regulatory networks and the spatiotemporal dynamics of different cell types. The advent of single-cell sequencing technology has enabled the collation of embryogenic knowledge into a cellular atlas at high resolution. Single-cell atlases have been compiled for mouse embryos at various stages—including the pre-implantation1,2, gastrulation3–7, organogenesis8,9 and fetal development10 stages—using diverse technologies. However, variations in temporal coverage and data quality across different technologies have hindered efforts to merge datasets from different sources for meta-analysis, leaving substantial gaps in our understanding of developmental continuum. Here we established a comprehensive single-cell atlas that spans the full range of developmental time points from embryonic day 1.5 (E1.5) to E19.0, facilitating the elucidation of developmental dynamics of mouse embryos.

Given the varying adhesion properties of different tissues and cell types, we designed optimal lysis protocols for each tissue at various developmental stages to ensure adequate sampling and cover the representative cell types in each embryo (Extended Data Fig. 1b and Methods). Specifically, cells were sampled from the whole embryos at E1.5 to E10.5, and extra-embryonic tissues from embryos older than E8.0 were discarded. Embryos at an advanced developmental stage (>E10.5) were dissected by major body parts (E11.0–11.5, head and trunk; E12.0–15.5, brain, skeleton–skin and visceral organs; E16.0–19.0, brain, skeleton, skin and visceral organs). The cell lysis protocol yielded >90% cell viability. We employed 10x Chromium v3 GEM reagents to prepare single-cell libraries. Importantly, fresh cells were used for sequencing to avoid stress-related gene expression and messenger RNA degradation caused by embryo freeze–thaw operations12,13. After quality control, we obtained 2,230,900 individual cells. We trained a classifier for sex predictions and found that most time points included both male and female samples (Extended Data Fig. 1c and Methods). The average number of reads per cell reached around 62,000, with a median of 4,512 detected genes and 16,910 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) per cell (Extended Data Fig. 1d,e), outperforming recently published prenatal mouse embryonic single-cell atlases with a median of 1,574 detected genes and 2,700 UMIs10 (Extended Data Fig. 1f).

Overview of mdCLA

To systematically characterize cell identities during mouse embryogenesis, we performed manual annotation by merging transcriptomic signatures and literature markers. Cells were classified into six groups: early embryonic cells (E1.5–7.5, Pou5f1 and Sall4)14,15, epithelial cells (Epcam and Cdh1)16,17, mesenchymal cells (Pdgfrb and Dcn)18,19, neural cells (Tubb3 and Map2)20,21, endothelial cells (Pecam1 and Kdr)22,23 and haematopoietic cells (Ptprc and Runx1)24,25 (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 2a). For each group, we performed in-depth annotation, establishing a four-level classification system composed of 108 organs or tissues (level-2), 305 cell types (level-3) and 1,316 cell subtypes (level-4; Extended Data Fig. 2b). Tissue annotations are displayed in Fig. 1c. In addition, we identified and listed the top marker genes for each tissue (Extended Data Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 2).

To validate the accuracy of annotations, we integrated our E6.5–8.5 embryonic data with a published single-cell atlas4 using the Harmony algorithm26. Cross-dataset analysis demonstrated robust consistency in cell-type assignments, confirming the reliability of our annotation (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Clustering analysis further revealed lineage relationships (Fig. 1d). For instance, the foregut-derived stomach, pancreatic and lung epithelial cells formed a cohesive cluster (groups 22, 23 and 24). Collectively, the mouse developmental Cell and Lineage Atlas (mdCLA) offers a high-resolution and temporally comprehensive transcriptomic atlas for mouse embryogenesis.

mdCLA enables identification of cell types during embryonic development

To evaluate its cell-type identification capabilities, we compared mdCLA against a recently published prenatal mouse embryo atlas (Qiu2024)10. Qiu2024 used single-cell combinatorial indexing RNA sequencing (sci-RNA-seq3) to profile the intact mouse embryos from E8.5 to E19.0, covering 11,441,407 cells, approximately five times the size of mdCLA (Extended Data Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 3). This larger cell number leads to higher cell coverage (defined as the ratio of the number of sequenced cells to the total number of expected cells per stage) from E8.5 (Extended Data Fig. 3b).

To enable a fair comparison between the two datasets, we assigned the level-2 cell labels from mdCLA to Qiu2024 and produced 89 unified cell labels of organs or tissues shared by both datasets (Extended Data Fig. 3c and Methods). Integration of these two datasets using the Harmony algorithm26 showed that cells with the same labels clustered together and exhibited highly similar gene expression profiles (Extended Data Fig. 3d–f). These results validated the high accuracy of cell-label transfer in the Qiu2024 dataset.

We investigated potential cell-type capture biases between droplet-based (mdCLA) and nucleus-based (Qiu2024) single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). We first compared the cellular composition of five lineages (endothelial, haematopoietic, epithelial, neural and mesenchymal cells). The proportions of these lineages were generally consistent between the two datasets (Extended Data Fig. 3g and Supplementary Table 3). Notably, Qiu2024 captured a slightly higher proportion of neural cells in the late developmental stages, probably a reflection of the advantage of nucleus-based sequencing in profiling mature neurons, which are large in size and may be under-represented in droplet-based approaches.

We then compared the cellular composition of unified 89 organ/tissue labels. We found that most tissues had similar cellular distributions (87.6%, 78/89), with only 12.4% (11/89) of tissues showing minor differences (Extended Data Fig. 3h and Supplementary Table 3). Specifically, Qiu2024 contained higher proportions of brain, spinal cord and skeletal muscle cells. This finding provides further support for the biased capture of neural cells by the nucleus-based sequencing method. However, this technique may also overestimate the number of multinucleated cells, such as skeletal muscle cells.

Notably, mdCLA contained a higher proportion of skin cells. To determine whether mdCLA captured more skin cell types, we integrated skin epithelial cells from both datasets. This analysis revealed that mdCLA uniquely identified skin neuroendocrine cells (also known as Merkel cells), a cell type that could not be defined in Qiu2024 (Fig. 2a). In summary, these results indicate that differences in sequencing platforms and sampling strategies lead to distinct biases in cellular composition. Although mdCLA has lower cell coverage than Qiu2024, its deeper transcript coverage confers unique advantages in discovering developmental cell types.

mdCLA facilitates the analysis of cell type-specific gene expression

A prevailing assumption in single-cell sequencing is that a lower per-cell transcript coverage can be compensated by a higher number of sequenced cells30. To assess the trade-off between increased cell number and reduced transcript depth, we generated normalized pseudobulk profiles for each unified cell label (scaled to 10,000 total counts). We found that the pseudobulk data increase the number of detected genes (nGenes) and narrow the gap in nGenes between two datasets. The median number of genes detected at the single-cell level by mdCLA was around fourfold higher than Qiu2024, whereas this difference reduced to only 1.2-fold at the pseudobulk level (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b).

To examine the relationship between cell number and gene detection, we downsampled the Qiu2024 dataset with a wide range of sampling fractions (0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10,…, 90 and 100% of 11,441,407 cells) and calculated the pseudobulk of unified cell labels (Extended Data Fig. 4c). Increased cell numbers effectively rescued the gene detection rates. However, this benefit was most pronounced at low cell abundances (<20% of total cells) and diminished to negligible levels beyond this threshold (Extended Data Fig. 4d).

Consistent with these observations, correlation analysis between the downsampled and 100% pseudobulk of Qiu2024 exhibited a similar trend: the Pearson correlation coefficient rose rapidly with increasing cell numbers at low sampling fractions (<5% of total cells) but reached a plateau at higher sampling fractions (>5% of total cells; Extended Data Fig. 4e). Furthermore, increased cell numbers in Qiu2024 were insufficient to recapitulate the gene expression patterns observed in mdCLA, with a maximum median Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.26 (Extended Data Fig. 4f).

To benchmark marker-gene detection, we established a gold standard comprising the top-100 tissue-specific markers (ranked according to the log2-tranformed fold change value) from 100% Qiu2024 and 100% mdCLA datasets, respectively. We then calculated the true positive rate (TPR) of the top-100 marker genes identified in downsampled Qiu2024 datasets (Methods). Similarly, the TPR improved with increased cell numbers at low sampling fractions (<20% of total cells) but showed minimal gains at higher fractions (>20% of total cells; Extended Data Fig. 4g,h).

Taking lung epithelial cells as a case study, we analysed the expression of four well-established developmental markers: Nkx2-1 (master transcription factor (TF) of all lung epithelial cells), Sox9 (distal alveolar progenitor marker)31, Sfta2 (surfactant associated 2)32 and Slc6a14 (solute carrier family 6)33. The pseudobulk expression level of Sox9 increased in a cell number-dependent manner at low sampling fractions, saturating once fractions exceeded 5% of total cells. Even at full sampling (100% cells), their expression levels in Qiu2024 remained below those in mdCLA. Notably, the expression levels of Nkx2-1, Sfta2 and Slc6a14 showed no clear correlation with increased cell numbers (Extended Data Fig. 4i). Among these, Sfta2 and Slc6a14 were nearly undetectable in Qiu2024 at the single-cell level (Extended Data Fig. 4j). Our analysis suggests that increasing cell numbers yields diminishing returns for marker-gene detection in low-depth sequencing data.

We hypothesize that the deep transcript coverage could facilitate the identification of TF co-expression modules (Methods). A direct comparison revealed that mdCLA yielded more differentially expressed TF modules specific to epithelial tissue than Qiu2024 (Student’s t-test, P < 0.01; Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 4). For instance, in lung and kidney epithelial cells, the TF module scores were notably higher in mdCLA than in Qiu2024 (Extended Data Fig. 4k,l). We present 13 tissue-specific TF modules identified by mdCLA, which may serve as a reference to further explore their functional roles during developmental processes (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 4).

The heterogeneity of nephron progenitor cells

We next evaluated whether mdCLA could be used to identify developmental cell states and gene expression. We focused on nephron progenitor cells (NPCs). NPCs are self-renewing populations that surround the invading epithelial ureteric bud tips, initiate nephrogenesis at around E10.0 and generate all nephrons throughout the life time of the organism34,35. The nephron progenitors persist until nephrogenesis ceases at around postnatal day 3 (P3)36. The size of the NPC pool determines the number of nephrons in adults. Reduced nephrons are associated with hypertension, glomerulosclerosis and end-stage renal disease37–39. However, the development dynamics and heterogeneity of NPCs remain unclear.

In mdCLA, NPCs were identified as kidney cells co-expressing the hallmark genes Six2 and Osr1 (refs. 34,35,40; Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 5a,b). Cluster analysis identified six NPC populations, NPC1–6, in mdCLA. RNA velocity41 analysis revealed that all NPC populations were derived from NPC1, a transient population exclusively present at E10–11 (Fig. 3b–d). NPC1 was characterized by the expression of Six1 (Fig. 2e), a TF essential for active nephrogenesis and potentially involved in establishing normal Six2 levels42. Interestingly, Six1 expression was highly enriched during early developmental stages (E9.5–11.0), after which it declined sharply and became undetectable by E13.0 (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Among the late-stage populations, NPC3 was distinguished by elevated expression of genes related to stem cell proliferation and mitotic nuclear division, including Mki67 and Cdca8 (Fig. 3e,f and Extended Data Fig. 5c). The proportion of NPC3 peaked at around E15.0 and subsequently declined (Fig. 3d), consistent with the histological observations of a diminishing NPC population in the ureteric bud tip niche during late gestation43.

We also identified NPC6, which emerges after E16.0 (Fig. 3d). NPC6 was characterized by low expression levels of genes associated with stem cell proliferation and mitotic nuclear division. Conversely, NPC6 was enriched for genes implicated in epithelial cell migration and differentiation (such as Klf4, Maff and Cebpb) and cell activation processes associated with immune responses (such as Dusp10, Il33 and Nfkbiz; Fig. 3e,f and Supplementary Table 5). Notably, NPC1 and NPC6 populations could not be identified in the Qiu2024 dataset (Extended Data Fig. 5d–g). We validated marker-gene expression in NPCs using single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (Extended Data Fig. 6a–c).

We next investigated whether distinct NPC populations were associated with specific nephrogenic niches. We profiled the spatial transcriptome of mouse kidneys at E14.5 and E18.0 using the 10x Xenium 5k platform (Fig. 3g,h). This platform enables the detection of gene expression at single-cell and spatial resolution.

We first defined NPCs and NPC neighbour cells using the k-nearest-neighbour algorithm (k = 10) based on spatial coordinates (Extended Data Fig. 6d). Genes related to mitotic nuclear division were downregulated in the NPC populations at E18.0 (for example, Cdca8). Genes associated with immune pathway signalling (for example, Il33)44 were upregulated at this stage (Extended Data Fig. 6e–g). Spatial data analysis validated the findings from single-cell data (Fig. 3e).

To further characterize niche-dependent signalling, we performed ligand–receptor interaction analyses between NPC neighbours and NPCs using the LIANA+ package45. Compared with E14.5, NPCs and their neighbours at E18.0 exhibited upregulation of pathways involved in epithelial differentiation (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Table 5). For example, the Notch pathway is known to critically regulate the epithelial differentiation of NPCs46. Immune response-related genes such as Ccn1, Thbs1 and Irf9 were also highly expressed in E18.0 niches (Fig. 3j and Extended Data Fig. 6h–j). Cellular communication network factor 1 (Ccn1) has been reported to regulate renal tissue regeneration in adult mice47 but its role during development remains elusive. Interferon (IFN) regulatory factor 9 (Irf9), a core TF in the type I IFN signalling pathway, forms a complex with STAT1/STAT2 to activate IFN-stimulated genes and orchestrate immune regulation48. The activation of Irf9 suggests changes in the immune microenvironment of the kidney during late gestation.

These analyses indicate that NPC heterogeneity may be associated with stage-specific niche signalling, linking progenitor cell states to dynamic microenvironmental regulation during kidney development.

Epithelial cells exhibit accelerated differentiation post E14.5

The analyses described so far revealed that cell differentiation during embryogenesis is a time-dependent process. This observation motivated us to globally explore the temporal dynamics of distinct cell lineages by leveraging the comprehensive temporal coverage of mdCLA. We calculated the accumulation of cell subtypes over time (Fig. 4a); the curve colour indicates the developmental rate, defined by the number of new level-4 subtypes emerging at each time point. Distinct differentiation patterns were observed across lineages: neural, mesenchymal and haematopoietic lineages underwent rapid diversification during E11.0–14.0, sharing similar developmental dynamics that peaked at around E12.0 and decelerated after E13.0. However, epithelial cells underwent a distinct acceleration process between E14.5 and E17.5. We also employed the Palantir algorithm49 to validate the phenomenon of accelerated differentiation. Palantir quantifies the probability of each cell differentiating into distinct cell-fate branches, thereby enabling the calculation of entropy over branches as cellular differentiation potential (DP). Undifferentiated cells exhibit a high DP owing to their multiple differentiation potentials, whereas the DP declines as cell fates become progressively specified. We applied Palantir to analyse the DP of 13 epithelial cell types and their temporal dynamics. The results revealed a rapid decline in DP across the most epithelial cell types at E14.5–17.5, indicating that a high rate of fate branching events occurs during this period (Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 6).

As a representative example, the endoderm-derived lung epithelial cells of E11.0–14.0 were primarily composed of progenitor cell types, including early distal and proximal progenitors. In contrast, we observed the emergence of specialized cell types, such as distal progenitor-derived alveolar type 1 and alveolar type 2 cells as well as proximal progenitor-derived ciliated, club and goblet cells at E14.5–17.5 (ref. 31; Fig. 4c). Similar patterns were observed for the pituitary epithelial cells, which originate from the ectoderm. Mature cell types rapidly emerged and expanded post E14.5 (Fig. 4d).

To investigate the molecular basis underlying this accelerated differentiation, we counted the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) during E11.0–14.0 and E14.5–17.5 across 13 epithelial cell types (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Table 6 and Methods). Notably, the number of upregulated genes at the later stages far exceeded the number of downregulated genes. Genes that were highly expressed at the later stage were linked to organ-specific functions (Fig. 4f). For instance, late-stage lung epithelial cells were enriched with pathways involved in surfactant homeostasis and lung development, whereas pituitary epithelial cells showed enrichment for processes related to peptide hormone secretion and endocrine-system development.

Together, these findings reveal a late-gestational acceleration of epithelial differentiation, characterized by a rapid expansion of cell-subtype diversity and extensive activation of organ-specific transcriptional programs.

Tub regulates hormone-producing cell specification of the pituitary gland

We next sought to investigate the functions of genes related to accelerated epithelial differentiation. Among these, our analyses identified Tub, a pituitary gland-specific TF, upregulated at late development stage (Figs. 2e, 5a,b and Supplementary Table 6), whose function in the pituitary gland remains largely uncharacterized. To further delineate the temporal and lineage-specific dynamics of Tub, we applied the optimal transport algorithm50 to infer the ancestor–descendant relationships of epithelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 9a–c) and constructed a temporal trajectory tree to visualize Tub expression across developmental time points and tissues (Extended Data Fig. 9d,e). Previous studies reported that Tub-deficient mice develop late-onset obesity51. To this end, we constructed a mouse strain with pituitary gland-specific conditional knockout (cKO) of Tub through the deletion of flanked exons 2–4 of Tub using Lhx3-driven iCRE (Fig. 5c). Lhx3 was specifically expressed in the pituitary gland from E9.0 in the Rathke’s pouch54 (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 9e). Examination of pituitary glands at E18.5 revealed that TUB was primarily expressed in the anterior lobe and that Tub-knockout mice had a smaller pituitary gland than the control mice (Fig. 5d).

We next performed scRNA-seq profiling of E18.5 pituitary glands to assess lineage composition. Notably, the population of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSHβ)-expressing cells was significantly reduced in the Tub-cKO group (Fig. 5e,f). Consistent with these findings, immunohistological analysis of E18.5 pituitary sections revealed fewer FSHβ+ cells and a thinner anterior lobe in the Tub-cKO mice, whereas its intermediate lobe seemed comparable to that of controls (Fig. 5g and Extended Data Fig. 10a–c). Together, our findings uncover a role for Tub in pituitary epithelial maturation and hormone-producing cell differentiation, provide a clue for the obesity phenotype observed in Tub-deficient mice and highlight mdCLA as a powerful resource for identifying critical developmental regulators.

Discussion

Here we present a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas of mouse embryonic development from E1.5 to E19.0. The use of a standardized sampling and sequencing pipeline ensures the generation of a high-quality dataset and eliminates batch effects inherent in integrating datasets from different laboratories. Compared with the Qiu2024 dataset, the high-transcript-coverage mdCLA has distinct advantages for cell-subtype and marker identification. We have rigorously validated the reliability of this dataset in identifying cell types during critical developmental stages, including gastrulation, and within specific organs such as the skin and kidney. Notably, our dataset revealed previously uncharacterized cell populations in NPCs, which could provide insights into the molecular evidence controlling the nephron number during embryogenesis.

This study identified lineage-specific differentiation patterns across embryonic development. For several lineages, including mesenchymal, neural, endothelial and haematopoietic cells, cellular diversity emerges predominantly in mid gestation and is subsequently maintained at relatively stable levels during late gestation. However, epithelial lineage exhibits a strikingly distinct pattern, with their diversity emerging primarily after E14.5. These differentiation patterns are likely to underpin the coordinated development of multiple cell types. For instance, early mesenchymal cell differentiation may establish the essential niche supporting subsequent epithelial cell diversification55,56. The mechanisms underlying multicellular collaborative differentiation remain largely unexplored, representing an exciting avenue for further investigation.

Beyond global lineage dynamics, mdCLA highlighted transcriptional regulators that were selectively upregulated during the late developmental stages. Among these we identified Tub as a regulator of pituitary epithelial maturation. Our functional analyses demonstrated that pituitary-specific loss of Tub impairs anterior lobe development, reduces FSHβ+ cell numbers and leads to postnatal obesity. These findings suggest that genes activated during late gestation may play critical roles in terminal cell-fate decisions, organ maturation and the establishment of physiological homeostasis after birth.

Despite its strengths, our dataset has several limitations. For instance, mdCLA contains only 2.2 million cells and the cell coverage from late developmental stages is disproportionately low relative to the total number of cells in the embryos. This discrepancy may under-represent rare cell types, thereby restricting the completeness of our observations. In addition, compared with nucleus-based sequencing approaches, for example, sci-RNA-seq3, droplet-based scRNA-seq may under-represent mature neurons due to their large size and complex morphology. Furthermore, the lack of organ-specific sampling in our dataset presents a challenge in assigning precise organ labels to certain cell types, which could hinder detailed functional characterization such as cell–cell interaction analysis.

To address these limitations, future studies could integrate mdCLA with other published single-cell datasets, specifically those generated via organ-specific sampling and datasets with shallow sequencing depth but high cellular coverage. Such integration could help overcome the challenges posed by cell-type capture bias and incomplete organ labelling, ultimately contributing to the construction of a more comprehensive and robust reference dataset.

To facilitate broad accessibility and utility of mdCLA, we have established an open-access website where users can access, analyse and download gene expression data (http://mdcla.ccla.ac.cn/). We believe that this dataset serves as an outstanding data resource for the study of embryogenesis according to the high temporal and transcript coverage.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations. All animal experiments were performed according to the Animal Protection Guidelines of Guangzhou Institutes of Biomedicine and Health, Chinese Academy of Sciences (N2022019).

Mouse embryo dissociation and single-cell suspension preparation

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from C57BL/6 mouse embryos (Charles River) collected from E1.5 (2-cell stage) to E18.5 at intervals of 0.5 days, with additional samples at E7.75 and at birth (E19.0). Each embryo was imaged to document the developmental stage previous to dissociation. To ensure efficient sampling and broad coverage of cell types across developmental stages, different dissociation protocols were applied according to embryonic age and tissue characteristics. For all stages, isolated single cells were collected into PCR tubes and resuspended in DPBS medium containing 0.04% BSA unless otherwise stated.

E1.5–2.5

Zona pellucida-free embryos were transferred into Ca2+ and Mg2+-free DPBS and dissociated into single cells by repeated aspiration using a finely drawn, mouth-controlled glass pipette. The cells were collected and washed once in M2 medium before transfer into PCR tubes.

E3.0–4.5

Zona pellucida-free embryos were collected in M2 medium and incubated in 100 µl enzyme solution (10 U ml−1 papain:accutase, 1:1 vol/vol) for 30 min. The embryos were dissociated by gentle repetitive aspiration using a finely drawn, mouth-controlled glass pipette. The cells were washed twice in M2 medium and transferred into PCR tubes.

E5.0–7.75

Embryos were washed twice with cold DPBS and incubated in 10 U ml−1 papain for 10 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Gentle pipetting was performed every 5 min. The digestion was terminated by adding DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U ml−1 DNase I, which was added to reduce viscosity. The cells were then collected into a PCR tube.

E8.0–10.5

Embryos were washed three times with cold DPBS and incubated in 10 U ml−1 papain for 20 min (37 °C and 5% CO2) with gentle pipetting every 10 min. The digestion was stopped by adding DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U ml−1 DNase I to reduce viscosity. The cell suspensions were filtered through 40-µm cell strainers on ice, centrifuged at 300g and 4 °C for 5 min and washed once with cold PBS containing 0.04% BSA. Cell number and viability were assessed using Trypan Blue exclusion. For the E10.0 and E10.5 embryos, the cells were additionally treated with ACK lysing buffer for 5 min at room temperature to remove red blood cells before collection into PCR tubes.

E11.0–11.5

Embryos were dissected into body and head parts, and washed twice with cold DPBS. The body tissues were digested in 10 U ml−1 papain and 0.05% trypsin for 15 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2 with gentle pipetting gently every 5 min. After digestion, the cells were filtered through 40-µm cell strainers on ice and centrifuged at 300g and 4 °C for 5 min. Red blood cells were removed by treatment with ACK lysis buffer for 5 min at room temperature, followed by a single wash with cold DPBS containing 0.04% BSA. Cell number and viability were determined by Trypan Blue staining before transfer into PCR tubes.

E12.0–15.0

Embryos were washed twice with cold DPBS and dissected into three parts: head, trunk and visceral organs. Head tissues were digested in 10 U ml−1 papain for 20–30 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2 with gentle pipetting every 10–15 min. Trunk tissues were digested in 0.25% trypsin for 20–30 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2 with gentle pipetting every 10–15 min. Visceral tissues were digested in 1 mg ml−1 collagenase I/II/IV for 30–60 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2 with gentle pipetting every 15–30 min. After digestion, the cell suspensions were filtered through 40-µm cell strainers on ice and centrifuged at 300g and 4 °C for 5 min. Red blood cells were removed by treatment with ACK lysis buffer for 5 min at room temperature, followed by a single wash with cold PBS containing 0.04% BSA. Cell number and viability were assessed by Trypan Blue exclusion before collection into PCR tubes.

E15.5–19.0

Embryos were washed twice with cold DPBS and dissected into four parts: brain, skin, body and viscera. Brain tissues were digested in 10 U ml−1 papain for 30 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2, with gentle pipetting every 15 min. Visceral tissues were digested in 1 mg ml−1 collagenase I/II/IV for 60–90 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2, with gentle pipetting every 30–45 min. Skin tissues were digested in 2 mg ml−1 collagenase A and B for 60 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2, with gentle pipetting every 30 min. Body tissues were first digested in 0.25% trypsin for 20–30 min and subsequently treated with 2 mg ml−1 collagenase II/D for 60 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2, with gentle pipetting every 10–15 min. After digestion, the cell suspensions were filtered through 40-µm cell strainers on ice and centrifuged at 300g and 4 °C for 5 min. Red blood cells were removed by treatment with ACK lysis buffer for 5 min at room temperature, followed by a single wash with cold PBS containing 0.04% BSA. Cell number and viability were determined using Trypan Blue staining solution. The cells were then collected into PCR tube for downstream single-cell analyses.

Single-cell RNA-seq library preparation

Single-cell suspensions were loaded onto a Chromium chip to generate single-cell gel bead-in-emulsions for capturing 500–10,000 single cells per reaction. The single-cell 3′-library was constructed using Chromium single cell reagent kits V3 (10x GENOMICS) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Following cell lysis, first-strand complementary DNA synthesis and amplification were carried out according to the instructions. Amplified cDNA was purified using SPRIselect beads (Beckman Coulter) and sheared to 250–350 base pairs (bp). A Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer and Qsep1 Bio-Fragment Analyzer (Bioptic) were used for cDNA quality control. The DNA libraries were sequenced using a Illumina Novaseq 6000 system.

Single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization

Single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization was performed following our previously published protocol57. Briefly, tissue sections (10 μm) were prepared using a Leica CM3050S cryostat, fixed in 4% (wt/vol) paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature, washed with PBS and permeabilized in 70% (vol/vol) ethanol for at least 1 h. The sections were hybridized overnight with probes (final concentration of 125 nM) in hybridization buffer at 37 °C, followed by stringent washing and counterstaining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Slides were mounted and sealed before imaging. Images were acquired using a Dragonfly 200 high-speed confocal imaging system and processed uniformly using Andor Fusion, Fiji (ImageJ) and Adobe Photoshop. Probe sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Kidney sample preparation for 10x Xenium spatial profile

E14.5 and E18 mouse embryonic kidneys were dissected, rinsed with PBS, embedded in Tissue-Tek optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound at 4 °C for 30 min and then snap-frozen on dry ice. The samples were transferred to a −80 °C ultra-low temperature freezer for long-term storage after approximately 1 h.

First, the OCT-embedded kidney tissues stored at −80 °C and the sealed Xenium slides were retrieved and equilibrated for 30 min in a cryostat (Dakewei CT520). The temperatures of the blade, sample head and cold stage were set to −20, −19 and −30 °C, respectively. The tissue block was then promptly attached to the centre of the sample holder in the correct orientation using a small amount of fresh OCT, followed by freezing to ensure tight adhesion. Next, the sample holder was mounted on the sample rack and aligned appropriately. The cryostat was switched to trimming mode (set to 30 μm) and excess OCT around the sample was gradually removed to expose the horizontal plane (E14.5) or the coronal plane (E18.0) of the kidney tissue. For quality control, approximately 50 μm tissue was collected from each sample for RNA integrity number determination and several 10-μm tissue sections were prepared for haematoxylin and eosin staining. Both assays were used to evaluate the RNA quality of the samples and only samples that passed quality control were subjected to formal section collection.

During collection, the Xenium slide ID was recorded and the flattened, intact 10-μm sections were transferred to the sample area of the Xenium slides. A finger was then pressed against the back of the slide, allowing the sample to be fully adsorbed onto the slide via cutaneous heat, and the tissue was re-frozen on the cold stage. After all sections were collected, the slides were placed in a slide box and immediately transferred to a −80 °C freezer for long-term storage, pending subsequent Xenium experiments.

Generation of Tub-conditional-knockout mouse embryonic stem cells

Mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) were derived from the inner cell mass of E3.5 blastocysts obtained from intercrosses of C57BL/6 mice. The mESCs were cultured on feeder layers in DMEM medium supplemented with 15% fetal calf serum, 1,000 U ml−1 leukemia inhibitory factor, 1 µM PD0325901 (an MEK inhibitor) and 3 µM CHIR99021 (a GSK3 inhibitor). Genome engineering of mESCs was performed using a CRISPR–Cas9-based strategy58 and carried out sequentially in two steps.

First, iCre was knocked into the Lhx3 locus. An iCre cassette linked by a P2A sequence was knocked in immediately upstream of the Lhx3 stop codon. Single-guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the Lhx3 locus (5′-GGGGCTAGCCATCCACCCAG-3′) was designed to target a site approximately 50 bp downstream of the Lhx3 stop codon. The sgRNA and homologous donor vector containing iCre and a PGK-BSD selection cassette were electroporated into wild-type C57BL/6 mESCs using a Mouse embryonic stem cell Nucleofector kKit (Lonza, VPH1001). After blasticidin selection (10 μg ml−1) for six days, single-cell-derived colonies were isolated and validated by long-range PCR and next-generation sequencing. Second, Tub-cKO mESCs were generated. Using the correctly targeted Lhx3-iCre mESCs obtained in the first step, loxP sites were inserted into the intronic regions flanking exons 2–4, and a PGK-Puro cassette flanked by FRT sites was used for selection. Two targeting sgRNAs, sgRNA1 (5′-TCTGCCTTCTTTAAGCGTCG-3′) and sgRNA2 (5′-CGTCCTAGCTTGATACTGGA-3′), together with the linearized donor vector, were introduced into mESCs by electroporation. After puromycin selection (1 μg ml−1) for six days, single-cell-derived colonies were picked and validated by long-range PCR and next-generation sequencing. Standard online tools were used to design sgRNAs for gene editing, which were synthesized by GenScript with desalting-grade purification.

Generation of Tub-mutant mice

Tetraploid embryo complementation was performed59. Briefly, 2-cell embryos were collected from the oviducts of ICR mice at 20 h following human chorionic gonadotropin injection and subsequently fused by electrofusion using a single pulse of 50 V for 35 μs in 0.3 M mannitol. The resulting tetraploid embryos were cultured overnight in potassium simplex optimized medium supplemented with 19 natural amino acids (KSOM + AA). For aggregation, 15–20 trypsinized Tub-cKO mESCs were transferred into individual wells containing drops of KSOM + AA under mineral oil. Meanwhile, batches of 30–50 embryos were briefly treated with acidified Tyrode’s solution to remove the zona pellucida. Each ESC clump was then aggregated with two tetraploid embryos and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After 24 h, 10–12 blastocyst-stage embryos were transferred into a single uterine horn of a 2.5-day post conception pseudopregnant recipient to generate mice carrying Lhx3-iCre and Tub+/+ or Tubfl/fl genotypes.

Immunofluorescence assay

Pituitary tissues were carefully dissected from the ventral side of the skull at the base of the brain, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 1–2 h and subsequently embedded in OCT compound. Cryosections were cut at a thickness of 10 μm. For immunostaining, the sections were washed with PBS and blocked with 5% donkey serum in 0.3% PBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C and secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Images were acquired using an Olympus IXplore spinSR microscope.

The primary antibodies used for immunostaining were anti-TUB (Sino Biological, 102415-T08) and anti-FSH (Abcam, ab281562; monoclonal, clone EPR24439-141), both of which were rabbit-derived and used at a dilution of 1:200. The secondary antibody was Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H+L), highly cross-adsorbed, and used at a dilution of 1:500.

Generation of single-cell expression matrices

Quality control of FASTQ data was implemented with fastp-0.20.040 (ref. 60). The sequence of Read1 was trimmed to 28 bp and Read2 was trimmed to 98 bp using TrimGalore-0.6.5. Trimmed reads were mapped to the mouse genome reference of gencode.vM21 using STAR-2.7.6a61 with the settings ‘–soloType Droplet–soloFeatures Gene–soloCBwhitelist 3M-february-2018.txt–soloCBstart 1–soloCBlen 16–soloUMIstart 17–soloUMIlen 12–soloBarcodeReadLength 0’. The 3M-february-2018.txt is the barcode whitelist provided by Cell Ranger v3. The resulting expression matrix comprised of barcodes.tsv, genes.tsv and matrix.mtx were used for downstream analysis.

Data preprocessing

A total of 322 gene expression matrices corresponding to single-cell sequencing libraries were generated. Downstream analyses were performed with the Python package Scanpy62. Given that the expression pattern could vary at different stages or cell types, quality control was performed for each of the libraries separately. The cells with low number of detected genes and total UMIs were removed. In addition, the cells with percent.mt > 0.2 were excluded, except for E1.5 embryo cells. When using 10x Genomics to isolate single cells, the partitioning oil could capture more than one cell with a certain probability, where multiple cells are labelled with a sample barcode, that is, doublet. We used Scrublet63 to predict the doublets. Scrublet sampled and combined observed transcriptomes to create the simulated doublets, which were incorporated with all cells to construct a k-nearest-neighbour graph, the cells with more doublet neighbours would obtain higher doublet score. Scrublet was implemented with the settings ‘expected_doublet_rate = 0.05, n_neighbors = round(0.5 * sqrt(n_cells)), min_counts = 3, min_cells = 3, n_prin_comps = 30, mean_center = True, normalize_variance = True’. Cells with a high doublet score were excluded. The subsequent pool of 2,230,900 cells was used for further analysis.

Sex classifier

We developed a sex prediction classifier using E19.0 data with confirmed sex labels (female/male). A logistic regression classifier was trained on log-normal transformed expression data of sex-related genes (Xist, Ddx3y, Eif2s3y, Gm29650, Kdm5d and Uty)66 and then implemented for sex determination of the remaining cells. If >90% of the cells in a library were predicted to be of a single sex, all cells in this library were defined as uniform sex label.

Two-dimensional visualization

The total count of each cell was normalized to 10,000 and then log-transformed after adding pseudocount (value = 1). A total od 3,938 highly variable genes (HVGs) were selected using scanpy.pl.highly_variable_genes, implemented in Scanpy. The expression was then scaled to unit variance and zero mean for each gene. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to reduce sequencing noise based on the selected HVGs. The top-150 components that explained most of the variance were then used as input of FFT-accelerated Interpolation-based t-SNE (FIt-SNE)67. We followed an optimized t-SNE workflow to visualize the data, which effectively avoids overcrowding embeddings when large numbers of cells are present and preserves the global geometry of the data on the two-dimensional plane68.

Cell-type annotations

For systematic cell-type classification, we used a hierarchical annotation strategy (Extended Data Fig. 2b).

Level-1 (group): Cells collected before E7.5 were assigned to the ‘early embryonic cells’. The rest were categorized into five broad lineages (epithelial, mesenchymal, endothelial, neural and haematopoietic cells) based on marker genes (Extended Data Fig. 2a).

Level-2 (organ/tissue): For level-1 annotations, we repeated the Scanpy workflow (default HVGs, 150 PCs, n_neighbors = 50, Leiden resolution = 2). Organ/tissue annotations were assigned via known marker genes (Extended Data Fig. 2c).

Level-3 (cell-type annotations): Each level-2 annotation was re-analysed with the Scanpy workflow (default HVGs, 50 PCs, n_neighbors = 20, Leiden resolution = 1). Cell types were annotated using literature marker genes.

Level-4 (cell-subtype annotations): For level-3 annotations, clusters with >50 cells were re-clustered via the Scanpy workflow (top-500 HVGs, 50 PCs, n_neighbors = 20, Leiden resolution = 0.2).

Cross-dataset cell-label prediction

We harmonized the annotation by mapping the organ/tissue cell labels (level-2 annotation) of mdCLA to Qiu2024 based on the consideration that both atlases have sampled all organs/tissues from the intact embryos and thus share consistent organ/tissue labels (Extended Data Fig. 3c).

Step 1: A random forest classifier was trained on the mdCLA dataset.

Step 2: The cell labels of Qiu2024 dataset were predicted using the random forest classifier.

Step 3: Cell clustering was performed within each cell label in the Qiu2024 dataset.

Step 4: For each predicted organ/tissue in Qiu2024, cells were scored by averaging the marker-gene expression of the top-30 organ/tissue-specifc marker genes derived from mdCLA. An average score was then calculated for each cluster. Clusters with an average score of ≥0.5 were defined as high-confidence clusters, whereas those with scores of <0.5 were designated as low-confidence cells.

Step 5: A new random forest classifier was trained using the high-confidence cluster cells of Qiu2024 and the top-30 marker genes for each cell label in the Qiu2024 dataset were identified.

Step 6: Cell labels with low-confidence cells of Qiu2024 were predicted using the new random forest classifier.

Step 7: Cell clustering was performed within each newly predicted cell label.

Step 8: For each predicted organ/tissue in Qiu2024, cells were rescored using the top-30 marker genes derived from Qiu2024 and average cluster scores were calculated. Clusters with average scores of ≥0.5 were classified as high-confidence, whereas the remaining clusters were designated as low-confidence.

Step 9: The labels of the remaining low-confidence cells were assigned as the nearest high-confidence cells in the PCA space.

Final outcome: using this annotation strategy, a total of 11,441,407 cells in the Qiu2024 dataset were assigned 89 unified organ/tissue labels. We then performed all comparative analyses of two datasets with the unified labels.

Integration of the mdCLA and Qiu2024 datasets

To visualize unified annotation of the two datasets, we performed batch integration using the Harmony algorithm26 and UMAP visualization. Given the large dataset size, we randomly subsampled cells across all 89 unified organ/tissue labels: 1,000 cells per tissue were selected from mdCLA and 5,000 cells per tissue from Qiu2024.

Subsequently, we obtained the intersection of HVGs and DEGs within each dataset and took the intersection of these gene sets between mdCLA and Qiu2024. This yielded a total of 1,881 shared genes, which we used for PCA analysis (50 PCs).

The following Harmony integration parameters were used: max_iter_harmony = 20 and max_iter_kmeans = 40. Based on the PCA embeddings adjusted by Harmony, we constructed nearest-neighbour graphs (n_neighbors = 30) and then performed UMAP visualization. We further calculated the correlation between the two datasets in the Harmony-corrected PCA space.

Transcription factor co-expressed module analysis

We performed TF co-expressed module analysis with the following steps: epithelial cell types were selected, with 1,500 cells randomly sampled from each tissue in each dataset; DEGs and HVGs were identified in each dataset and their union was used as the gene pool for module inference; TF co-expressed modules were then inferred independently for each dataset using GRNBoost2 implemented in pySCENIC69,70; co-expression scores were calculated using pyscenic.aucell.aucell; and finally, differential expressed modules were identified by Student’s t-tests, with thresholds set as adjusted P < 0.001 and log2(fold change) > 1.

Developmental rate analysis

To characterize the developmental rate of five cell groups (epithelial, mesenchymal, endothelial, neural and haematopoietic lineage cells), we first calculated the cumulative number of cell subtypes (level-4 annotation) at each time point. Specifically, this number included newly emerged cell subtypes at the current time point plus those already present at the previous time point, with subtypes containing fewer than ten cells excluded at each stage. The temporal dynamics of this cumulative number were smoothed using generalized additive models. The gradient between consecutive time points in these curves reflected the emergence rate of new subtypes, representing the developmental rate. To visualize when cell subtypes emerged, we generated pseudobulk RNA data for each cell subtype and performed PCA using Scanpy. We set the initial time point of each cell subtype as the x axis and PC1 as the y axis. Given that a cell subtype may exist across multiple time points, we only designate its earliest time point of appearance as the initial time point.

Furthermore, we identified DEGs across epithelial cell types (adjusted P < 0.001, log2(fold change) > 1). We identified DEGs for E10.0–14.0 and E14.5–17.5 (adjusted P < 0.001, log2(fold change) > 1) of each epithelial cell type. The intersection of the two DEG sets were defined as lineage- and stage-specific DEGs. We then performed Gene Ontology analysis on these DEGs using ClusterProfiler71.

Lineage tree inference

We used moscot, an optimal transport-based method for modelling time series single-cell data, to infer the ancestor–descendant relationship of epithelial cells50. The union of cell-type marker genes and highly variable genes were used as input. Data pairs from all adjacent time points were selected and subjected to canonical correlation analysis72. Subsequently, the TemporalProblem function in moscot was employed to infer the ancestor–descendant optimal transport scores using the top-20 canonical correlation analysis components (with parameters: epsilon = 0.001, tau_a = 0.99, tau_b = 0.999, scale_cost = ‘mean’). This analysis resulted in transport scores between adjacent time points; the cell types with the highest average transport score were connected. A lineage tree model was build using the Python package Toytree73, where each node is a cell type in a time point and lines connect the nodes of the same lineage. We then calculated the gene expression of each lineage node. The gene expression of each node is defined as the weighted average of single-cell gene expression on the transport score. Notably, to reduce the discovery rate of false positive gene expression, the transport score was restricted in the cells of each node; the cells that do not belong to the node will not participate in the calculation.

Statistics and reproducibility

Immunofluorescence experiments were repeated independently as indicated in the figure legends. For Fig. 5d, experiments were performed in two independent biological replicates and multiple tissue sections from different levels were analysed in each experiment, yielding similar results. Representative images are shown in Fig. 5g, with additional sections from different tissue levels and an independent biological replicate provided in Extended Data Fig. 10, all showing similar results. For Fig. 5h, images are representative of one experiment, and similar staining patterns were observed across multiple fields of view.

For datasets with n < 3 (Extended Data Fig 10c), no statistical analyses were performed and no error bars or significance annotations are shown.
