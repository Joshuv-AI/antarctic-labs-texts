Lysosomal cholesteryl ester hydrolysis drives white matter repair by reprogramming microglia into a novel reparative state
Authors: Yu Hu, Kaici Tang, Hongli Hu, Hongbo Mi, Xue Li, Hao Wang, Jiali Liu, Yiqing Xia, Wenhui Huang, Frank Kirchhoff, Guo-dong Xiao, Jia Jia, Jian Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhite matter repair relies on microglial clearance of cholesterol-rich myelin debris. Microglia have been reported to predominantly depend on de novo sterol synthesis to support this repair process. Lysosomal acid lipase (LAL) is the only known lysosomal enzyme capable of hydrolyzing cholesterol esters. In stark contrast to previous studies, we demonstrate here that LAL-mediated lysosomal lipolysis—not de novo sterol synthesis—serves as a central determinant of the microglial capacity to drive white matter repair. Using single-cell RNA sequencing, we identified a novel reparative microglial state characterized by simultaneously high expression of glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB) and LAL. Following white matter injury, GPNMB+ microglia expanded and constituted the major microglial subset responsible for myelin debris engulfment. However, GPNMB+ microglia displayed context-dependent capacity to digest internalized myelin debris and mediate remyelination, with marked differences between reparable white matter injury and nonregenerative injury induced by white matter stroke (WMS). Transcriptomic profiling identified LAL as a key regulator of the reparative phenotype in GPNMB+ microglia. Independent of cytosolic lipases—widely regarded as synergistic mediators of LAL in cholesteryl ester hydrolysis, microglial LAL was both indispensable for myelin debris clearance and spontaneous remyelination in the reparable injury model, and sufficient to restore these processes following WMS-induced irreparable white matter injury. Mechanistically, LAL-mediated lysosomal lipolysis constituted the primary pathway for cholesteryl ester hydrolysis in microglia after white matter injury. This pathway converted cholesteryl esters into free cholesterol and activates liver X receptors (LXRs), both of which were required to reprogram microglial into the reparative state. Consistently, LXR activation alone was insufficient to rescue defective white matter repair caused by LAL deficiency. Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HβCD), an FDA-approved drug carrier, effectively lowers intracellular cholesterol levels through incompletely defined mechanisms. HβCD specifically upregulated LAL expression within white matter lesions and promoted remyelination via a LAL-dependent manner following WMS, supporting its potential as a therapeutic agent for WMS. Collectively, this study identifies lysosomal cholesterol ester hydrolysis as a novel therapeutic target for the treatment of irreversible white matter injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03782-7.

Introduction

White matter injuries lead to cognitive and motor deficits, which are a primary cause of multiple neurological disorders, such as multiple sclerosis (MS) [1] and aging brains with cognitive impairment [2]. Notably, subcortical white matter stroke (WMS) is an important pathological mechanism underlying aging-associated white matter lesions. Indeed, WMS accounts for up to 25% of all strokes and ranks as the second leading cause of dementia [3, 4]. However, our current knowledge about white matter repair is derived mainly from research on inflammatory or toxic injuries to white matter. In nonischemic demyelinating diseases, such as MS, spontaneous remyelination can be initiated to partially restore functions [5], as evidenced by the maturation of oligodendrocyte progenitors (OPCs) into myelinating oligodendrocytes. However, OPCs fail to mature into oligodendrocytes following WMS. This failure represents a fundamental mechanism of irreparable lesions induced by WMS [6]. Why OPCs lose their maturation potential remains unclear.

Myelin regeneration requires a permissive environment [5]. White matter injuries generate lipids and cholesterol-enriched myelin debris that inhibits OPC maturation [7]. Microglia are central to remyelination: they phagocytose myelin debris, then mediate the intracellular metabolism of debris-derived lipids and the extracellular efflux of cholesterol [8]. Given that most of our knowledge regarding the roles of microglia in white matter repair is derived from reparable white matter disease models, some fundamental questions remain unclear. For example, in experimental MS models, sterol synthesis is exclusively activated in microglia, and microglia critically depend on de novo sterol synthesis to facilitate cholesterol efflux via desmosterol-mediated activation liver X receptors (LXR) for effective remyelination [9]. Desmosterol is a precursor to cholesterol in the process of sterol synthesis. Notably, certain oxysterols derived from cholesteryl ester hydrolysis, such as 25- or 27-hydroxycholesterol, are also potent endogenous LXR activators [10]. This fact implicates a potential role of cholesterol hydrolysis in regulating remyelination via LXR activation. However, whether cholesterol hydrolysis in microglia also actively drives the repair of demyelinated lesions remains elusive.

A systematic comparison of the mechanistic differences between reparable and irreparable white matter injury may shed light on white matter repair from a new angle. In this context, we sought to determine microglial responses by using a subcortical WMS-induced irreparable injury model and a lysolecithin (LPC)-induced reparable model. LPC causes rapid demyelination followed by almost complete repair, which mimics some aspects of MS-induced demyelination [11, 12]. On the other hand, the vasoconstrictor N5-(1-iminoethyl)-L-ornithine (L-NIO)-induced subcortical WMS in mice mimics pathological processes shared with WMS patients [6]. By comparing the single-cell transcriptomes of white matter lesions derived from these two models and conducting functional experiments, we show that lysosomal acid lipase (LAL), the only known lysosomal enzyme capable of hydrolyzing cholesterol esters, is the key determinant of a novel microglial state that drives white matter repair. While it is assumed that cytosolic lipases act synergistically with LAL to hydrolyze cholesteryl esters and only the inhibition of both pathways totally abolishes cholesteryl ester hydrolysis [13, 14], we found that LAL-mediated lysosomal cholesteryl ester hydrolysis in microglia is both required and sufficient to drive remyelination. Thus, lysosomal lipolysis represents a previously unrecognized mechanism underlying microglia’s ability to drive white matter repair and a potential therapeutic target for irreparable white matter injury.

Results

Bulk RNA-seq reveals that reduced lipid metabolism is associated with irreparable white matter lesions

Consistent with published results [15], LPC injection induced rapid demyelination at 3 days post-injection, followed by nearly complete remyelination by 14 days post-LPC injection (Fig. 1A, Supplemental Fig. 1A). In contrast, L-NIO-induced WMS caused irreparable demyelination [6], with no evidence of repair even at 3 months post-L-NIO injection (Fig. 1A, Supplemental Fig. 1B). To investigate the underlying molecular changes, we harvested white matter lesions for bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) at 4 days after LPC- or L-NIO-induced injury. We performed weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) on 13,111 variable genes and identified 25 gene modules—each consisting of genes with coordinated expression profiles and related functions (Fig. 1B-C). Genes in the MEturquoise module (correlation coefficient: 0.67, p value = 0.006) showed the strongest correlation with LPC-induced white matter repair (LPCips, Fig. 1C). Beyond genes associated with inflammatory pathways, the MEturquoise module was highly enriched for genes involved in the lysosome pathway (top 2), phagosome pathway (top 7), lipid and atherosclerosis pathway and efferocytosis (Fig. 1D). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further confirmed that biological processes linked to lysosomes, efferocytosis, and ABC transporters (key mediators of cholesterol transport) were among the most highly upregulated pathways in LPC-induced reparable lesions relative to L-NIO-induced irreparable lesions (Fig. 1E, Supplemental Fig. 1C). Given that lysosome–phagosome function and cholesterol transport are essential for lipid metabolism, these results suggest that lipid metabolism is a core mechanism associated with white matter repair.

Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion is the pathological mechanism underlying periventricular white matter lesions [5]. To investigate whether lipid metabolism is also dysregulated in clinical white matter lesions associated with hypoperfusion, we used GSEA to reanalyze a published RNA-seq dataset of white matter tissues harvested from patients with periventricular white matter lesions and from healthy controls (GEO accession: GSE157363) [2]. Among the top 20 downregulated pathways in periventricular white matter lesions relative to control tissue were the pathways linked to myelin debris clearance, including efferocytosis and phagosome functions (Supplemental Fig. 1D). Critically, positive regulation of lipid metabolism was downregulated in periventricular white matter lesions compared with control tissues (Fig. 1F).

Taken together, RNA-seq data from clinical patients and animal models indicate that enhanced lipid metabolism is associated with white matter repair, whereas impaired lipid metabolism correlates with remyelination deficits following WMS. To experimentally validate these findings, we characterized microglia-mediated lipid metabolism and myelin debris clearance at four time points (4 days, 2, 4, and 9 weeks) post white matter injury, thereby establishing a temporal profile of lipid clearance dynamics. At 4 days following LPC- and L-NIO-induced white matter injury, Bodipy and Filipin staining revealed prominent and comparable accumulation of neutral lipids (primarily sequestered in lipid droplets) and free cholesterol within microglia (Supplemental Fig. 1E, H). In LPC-induced reparable injury model, microglial neutral lipid and free cholesterol deposition began to decline by 2 weeks and was nearly undetectable at 4 and 9 weeks post-injury. In stark contrast, following L-NIO–induced irreversible ischemic white matter injury, microglial lipid droplet and free cholesterol accumulation remained significantly elevated at all time points examined (Fig. 1G–H, Supplemental Fig. 1F–G; Fig. 1I–J, Supplemental Fig. 1I–J). FluoroMyelin staining further demonstrated extensive and comparable myelin debris sequestration within microglia at 4 days post- white matter injury in both LPC- and L-NIO-treated groups (Supplemental Fig. 1K), indicating the robust internalization of myelin debris by microglia at this time point. Consistent with the lipid deposition patterns, microglial myelin debris in LPC-induced reparable lesions began to decrease at 2 weeks and was almost undetectable at 4 and 9 weeks. By contrast, myelin debris levels within microglia remained persistently elevated at all tested time points after L-NIO-induced irreparable WMS (Fig. 1K–L, Supplemental Fig. 1L–N). Collectively, temporal profiling of microglial lipid metabolism and myelin debris accumulation implicated a causal link between impaired microglial lipid clearance and the irreparability of white matter lesions following WMS.

A GPNMB⁺ microglial cluster dominates myelin debris engulfment but shows context-dependent myelin debris digestion capacity in white matter lesions

Bulk RNA-seq captured the global transcriptomic response to white matter injury. To delineate cell-specific responses, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on tissues collected from reparable versus irreparable white matter lesions. To minimize variability and ensure sufficient tissue for sequencing, we pooled corpus callosum tissues from eight mice per group. Based on transcriptomic profiling, 23,116 single cells were clustered into seven major cell classes: microglia, neurons, astrocytes, macrophages, oligodendrocytes (OLs), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), and endothelial cells (Supplemental Fig. 2A-B). Notably, the proportion of microglia was dramatically increased in white matter lesions following both LPC-induced reparable injury (57.8%) and L-NIO-induced irreparable ischemic injury (52.8%, Supplemental Fig. 2C). We validated these findings using microglial lineage tracing mice and immunofluorescence staining, following the published protocols [16]. In microglia lineage tracing mice (Cx3cr1CreERT2+/−: RosatdT/+ treated with tamoxifen), tamoxifen induction specifically labeled nearly all CNS-resident microglia—rather than peripheral macrophages in the spleen/liver—with tdTomato (Supplemental Fig. 2D). Consistent with the transcriptomic data, microglia (tdT+) were dramatically accumulated at the injury site following both LPC-induced reparable and L-NIO-induced irreparable white matter injury (Supplemental Fig. 2E–I).

Given the substantial population of microglia observed following white matter injury, we reclustered microglia and identified six subclusters (Fig. 2A-B). Cluster 1 shared a gene signature with disease-associated microglia (DAM; Supplemental Fig. 3A) [17]. Cluster 2 microglia were homeostatic, with high expression of genes such as Tmem119, P2ry12, and Cx3cr1 (Supplemental Fig. 3A). Cluster 3 was characterized by elevated expression of cell proliferation markers, injury response genes, and DAM-associated genes (Supplemental Fig. 3A), resembling a specific subtype of injury-responsive microglia (IRM2.4) in white matter lesions [15]. Clusters 4 and 5 were characterized by the downregulation of homeostatic genes and the upregulation of proliferative markers (Kcnal and Cdn19 for cluster 4; Meis2 and Sox11 for cluster 5; Supplemental Fig. 3A).

Cluster 0 microglia are characterized by downregulation of homeostatic genes and upregulation of genes linked to injury responses and DAM (Supplemental Fig. 3A). Furthermore, cluster 0 microglia highly expressed signature genes of axon tract-associated microglia (ATM; Supplemental Fig. 3A), a microglial population that emerges exclusively in white matter during a restricted developmental window [15]. Cluster 0 microglia therefore share gene signatures with DAMs and ATMs (Fig. 2C-D). However, Cluster 0 is distinct from both DAMs and ATMs, supported by two facts: (1) unlike cluster 0 microglia, DAMs exhibit extremely low expression of ATM signature genes (Supplemental Fig. 3B); and (2) ATMs do not express injury-associated genes (raw data from GSE121654; Supplemental Fig. 3C). By reanalyzing sc-RNA data from ischemic cortex tissue (GEO accession NO: GSE247391) [17], we further confirmed that that microglia with signatures analogous to Cluster 0 were absent from ischemic gray matter (Supplemental Fig. 3B). Cluster 0 likely represents a microglial subset associated with white matter injury.

Cluster 0 microglia were undetectable in the white matter of sham-operated mice (Fig. 2B, E). Following injury, the proportion of cluster 0 microglia increased dramatically and became the largest microglial subpopulation in both LPC-induced reparable lesions (52.9%) and L-NIO-induced irreparable white matter lesions (44.7%; Fig. 2E). In the developing white matter, ATMs exhibit high lipid metabolic activity and engulfment capacity, as supported by gene enrichment analyses of this population [15]. Consistently, our dataset revealed that cluster 0 microglia, like ATMs, displayed greater lipid metabolic activity than injury-induced DAMs and IRM2.4, as evidenced by the enrichment of genes linked to lipid transport, lipid catabolism, and phagocytosis in cluster 0 microglia (Fig. 2F). Together, these findings suggest that cluster 0 microglia may be critical for clearing lipid-rich myelin debris.

To investigate the contribution of cluster 0 microglia to myelin debris clearance, we first sought to identify a specific marker for cluster 0 microglia. Glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB), a key marker for ATMs, is induced in microglia/macrophages in response to inflammation or tissue injury, where it supports inflammation resolution and efferocytosis. Notably, Gpnmb was not expressed in DAMs reported in the previous study [17] (Fig. 2G; reanalyzed by us) or in DAMs from our dataset (Fig. 2H). In contrast, the majority of cluster 0 microglia in our dataset exhibited high Gpnmb expression (Fig. 2H), indicating Gpnmb as a specific marker for cluster 0 microglia.

scRNA-seq data suggested that Gpnmb was also expressed by infiltrating macrophages within white matter lesions (Supplemental Fig. 3D). However, microglia were the predominant cell type over macrophages in LPC/L-NIO-induced white matter lesions (percentage to total cells: 55.71% vs. 4.81%). Using microglia lineage tracing mice (Cx3cr1CreERT2+/−:RosatdT/+ treated with tamoxifen), we further confirmed that the density of these peripheral macrophages (IBA1+tdT−) was substantially lower than that of microglia (IBA1+tdT+) in white matter lesions following both LPC- and L-NIO-induced injury (percentage to total IBA1+ cells: 10.28% vs. 89.72% for LPC; and 10.26% vs. 89.74% for L-NIO; Supplemental Fig. 3E). Accordingly, IBA1⁺GPNMB⁺ cells in white matter lesions predominantly represented cluster 0 microglia (IBA1⁺GPNMB⁺tdT+; percentage to total IBA1⁺GPNMB⁺ cells: 85.91% for LPC and 85.12% for L-NIO), rather than GPNMB-expressing peripheral macrophages (IBA1⁺GPNMB⁺tdT−; percentage to total IBA1⁺GPNMB⁺ cells: 14.09% for LPC and 14.88% for L-NIO; Supplemental Fig. 3E).

Using GPNMB as a marker for cluster 0 microglia, we validated our scRNA-seq data, confirming that cluster 0 microglia (IBA1⁺GPNMB⁺) account for ~ 50% of the total microglial population (Fig. 2I-J). Moreover, comparable proportions of GPNMB⁺ microglia were observed in white matter lesions at 4 days post-injury, regardless of whether the injury was induced by L-NIO (irreparable) or LPC (reparable; Fig. 2I-J). Recent studies reported that a GPNMB+ microglial population is expanded and may exert protective or reparative abilities in AD and MS patients [18]. However, our results indicate that expansion of GPNMB⁺ microglia alone is insufficient to drive white matter repair, suggesting that GPNMB⁺ microglia display context-dependent reparative capacity.

We next investigated whether the context-dependent reparative capacity of GPNMB⁺ microglia stems from differential myelin debris clearance — a core process underlying white matter injury repair. We therefore quantified the proportion of GPNMB⁺ microglia and GPNMB⁻ microglia containing intracellular myelin debris within white matter lesions. Our results demonstrated that GPNMB⁺ microglia, but not GPNMB⁻ microglia, constituted the primary microglial subtype responsible for myelin debris engulfment at 4 days post-injury in both LPC-induced reparable and L-NIO-induced irreparable lesions (Fig. 2I, K–L). Importantly, there was no difference in the engulfment capacity of GPNMB⁺ microglia in LPC-induced reparable lesions versus L-NIO-induced irreparable lesions 4 days post-injury (Fig. 2I, M). Notably, by 14 days post-injury, intracellular myelin debris was barely detectable inside GPNMB⁺ microglia within LPC-induced reparable lesions. In contrast, substantial amounts of intracellular myelin debris persisted in GPNMB⁺ microglia within L-NIO-induced ischemic (irreparable) lesions (Fig. 2N-O). Collectively, these findings indicate that GPNMB⁺ microglia in irreparable white matter lesions retain intact engulfment capacity, but their ability to digest the engulfed myelin debris is impaired compared to GPNMB⁺ microglia in reparable white matter lesions.

Consistent with our immunohistochemical results, our scRNA-seq data also indicated that the lipid catabolism pathway was downregulated in cluster 0 microglia from irreparable white matter lesions relative to cluster 0 microglia from LPC-induced reparable lesions (Fig. 2P). During white matter development, ATMs exhibit high lipid metabolic activity, as supported by upregulated lipid catabolic pathways in ATMs [15]. Using GSVA, we found that several lipid catabolic pathways were downregulated in cluster 0 microglia from irreparable ischemic lesions compared with ATMs (Supplemental Fig. 3F). Moreover, both the “lipid catabolic process” pathway and the “regulation of lipid catabolic process” pathway were upregulated in cluster 0 microglia from LPC-induced reparable lesions relative to cluster 0 microglia from irreparable lesions (Supplemental Fig. 3G). In conclusion, cluster 0 microglia marked by GPNMB dominates myelin debris engulfment but shows context-dependent myelin debris digestion capacity in white matter lesions. Notably, aligning with published findings that sterol synthesis is uniquely activated in microglia after reparable injury [9], our scRNA-seq data also revealed the upregulation of cholesterol biosynthesis and sterol biosynthesis regulatory processes in cluster 0 microglia from LPC-induced reparable lesions compared with cluster 0 microglia from L-NIO-induced irreparable ischemic lesions (Supplemental Fig. 3H).

To assess the clinical relevance of this microglia subset, we reanalyzed publicly available single-nucleus RNA sequencing data from chronic active (CA) and chronic inactive (CI) white matter lesions of patients with MS (GEO accession: GSE279180) [19]. For the analysis, we used the well-established cellular identifiers reported in the publications and the cluster 0 microglia signature genes identified in this study (Supplemental Fig. 4A-E). Microglia with gene signatures analogous to cluster 0 microglia were markedly increased in CA lesions relative to CI lesions (Supplemental Fig. 4F), further supporting the clinical relevance of this microglial subset.

We next investigated the temporal dynamics of GPNMB⁺ microglia (GPNMB⁺IBA1⁺) at 4 days, and 2, 4, and 9 weeks post-white matter injury. Notably, GPNMB⁺ microglia were robustly induced to comparable levels at 4 days post-white matter injury induced by both LPC and L-NIO (Fig. 2I-J). However, GPNMB⁺ microglia dynamics displayed distinct temporal profiles between the two models: in the LPC model, GPNMB⁺ microglia numbers decreased significantly by 2 weeks post-injury and were undetectable at 4 and 9 weeks. In contrast, GPNMB⁺ microglia remained persistently elevated at all examined time points following L-NIO-induced WMS (Fig. 2O, Supplemental Fig. 5A-C). These findings demonstrate that GPNMB⁺ microglia resolve gradually over time as white matter repair proceeds, whereas they persist in irreparable white matter lesions after L-NIO-induced white matter stroke.

To validate the functional role of GPNMB⁺ microglia in white matter repair, we conducted complementary in vitro and in vivo experiments using a lentivirus-mediated Gpnmb knockdown strategy (Supplemental Fig. 5D-E). Our prior findings identified GPNMB⁺ microglia as the primary microglial subtype responsible for myelin debris engulfment after white matter injury (Fig. 2I-M). Consistently, GPNMB knockdown in BV2 microglia markedly impaired their phagocytosis of myelin debris in vitro (Supplemental Fig. 5F-G). GPNMB was predominantly expressed in GPNMB⁺ microglia within white matter lesions at 4 days after LPC-induced reparable injury, with no detectable expression in other CNS cell types (Supplemental Fig. 3D-E). We employed a lentiviral approach to knockdown GPNMB within LPC-induced white matter lesions. GPNMB knockdown impaired spontaneous repair in the LPC model, as evidenced by the persistent demyelinated lesions identified via MBP staining with DAPI nuclear counterstain (Supplemental Fig. 5H-I). GPNMB knockdown also severely compromised the recovery of cognitive and motor function in mice at 4 weeks post LPC injection (Supplemental Fig. 5J-K). In conclusion, GPNMB⁺ microglia exert an indispensable functional role in white matter repair.

LAL is the determinant of microglial lipid metabolism activity and reparative state following white matter injury

Notably, we observed the upregulation of both cholesterol (sterol) biosynthesis and lipid catabolism pathways in cluster 0 microglia (marked by GPNMB) from LPC-induced reparable lesions, compared with cluster 0 microglia from WMS-induced irreparable ischemic lesions (Supplemental Fig. 3G-H). This prompts a critical question: does lipid metabolism or sterol synthesis actively drive repair in our models? To address the issue, we sought to identify hub genes that regulate the plasticity of cluster 0 microglia in reparable versus irreparable injury, followed by functional validation of the most critical hub gene. We hypothesized that integrating multiple analytical approaches would yield the robust identification of the critical hub gene.

Accordingly, we employed a multi-method strategy to mine hub genes from both bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq datasets. GSEA aggregates the contributions of all genes within a pathway, therefore capturing subtle gene expression changes that may be undetectable when analyzing individual genes. Using GSEA, we identified upregulated pathways in LPC-induced reparable lesions relative to irreparable ischemic lesions via three distinct analyses: (1) 41 pathways analyzed from bulk RNA-seq data, (2) 50 pathways analyzed from microglial transcriptomes extracted from scRNA-seq data, and (3) 59 pathways analyzed from cluster 0 microglia transcriptome profiles derived from scRNA-seq data (Fig. 3A). These analyses overlapped at 7 pathways, with the lysosomal pathway ranking as the top common pathway (Fig. 3A). This finding highlights the critical role of microglial lysosomal function in white matter repair.

Using WGCNA on bulk RNA-seq data, we have revealed that the 956 genes in the turquoise module were strongly associated with the LPC-induced repair phenotype. Thus, we mapped these repair-associated genes in the turquoise module to the microglial lysosomal pathway (identified via GSEA of both bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq datasets), yielding 8 intersecting lysosomal genes. These 8 genes were used to construct a protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network. The network analysis indicated that LAL (encoded by Lipa), the only known lysosomal enzyme capable of hydrolyzing cholesteryl esters, is the most critical hub mediator (Fig. 3A).

Next, we characterized the expression pattern of LAL following reparable and irreparable white matter injury. At 4 days post-injury, LAL protein levels in LPC-induced reparable lesions, but not in L-NIO-induced irreparable white matter lesions, were higher than those in sham-operated white matter (Fig. 3B, C). Notably, immunohistochemical results further confirmed that this increase in LAL was restricted to white matter lesions, with no elevation observed in other brain regions after LPC-induced reparable injury (Fig. 3D). Moreover, LAL protein levels were significantly higher in microglia and GPNMB⁺ microglia within LPC-induced reparable lesions relative to those in L-NIO-induced irreparable lesions (Fig. 3E-H; Supplemental Fig. 6A-B). Importantly, this elevated LAL expression with LPC-induced lesions was localized to lysosomes of microglia (LAMP1⁺IBA1⁺, Supplemental Fig. 6A-B). Of note, the injury-enhanced LAL expression in the LPC model was markedly reduced by 2 weeks post injury (Supplemental Fig. 6C-D), a reduction likely attributable to the progression of myelin debris clearance and tissue repair.

To investigate whether the LAL is a determinant of the reparative state of microglia, we specifically overexpressed LAL in microglia as previously reported [20] (Fig. 4A). We first validated the specificity and efficiency of this approach. The lentivirus that was designed to co-express LAL and eGFP in the cells with Cre recombinase activity was injected into the corpus callosum of microglial lineage tracing mice (Cx3cr1CreERT2+/−:RosatdT/+). Two weeks after inducing Cre activity with tamoxifen treatment, many dTomato (tdT)-positive cells (microglia) presented eGFP signals, and almost all the eGFP signals were observed in the tdT-positive cells (Fig. 4B). These results implicated that the approach led to eGFP and LAL expression specifically in the microglia of Cx3cr1CreERT2+/− mice. WMS was induced in LAL-overexpressing Cx3cr1CreERT2+/− mice or control Cx3cr1CreERT2+/− mice that received the lentivirus but were not treated with tamoxifen. At 4 days after WMS induction, microglial overexpression of LAL led to increased LAL levels in GPNMB+ microglia (Fig. 4C). Consistent with prior reports [21], WMS induced long-term recognition memory deficits (≥ 4 weeks) in the novel object recognition (NOR) test, with impairments persisting for at least 3 months in control mice (Fig. 4D). In contrast, mice overexpressing LAL specifically in microglia displayed significant recovery in NOR performance relative to controls (Fig. 4D). Concomitantly, microglial LAL overexpression promoted remyelination, as evidenced by markedly reduced demyelinated lesions (Fig. 4E). Electron microscopy and immunohistochemistry further revealed microglial LAL overexpression improves myelin integrity (Fig. 4F) and reorganization (Fig. 4G) in ischemic lesions at 3 months post-WMS. Relative to controls, LAL-overexpressing microglia displayed markedly reduced free cholesterol and intracellular myelin debris deposition in microglia at 4 and 9 weeks after L-NIO injection (Fig. 4H-J), suggesting that specifically enhancing LAL expression in microglia promoted myelin debris clearance. By assessing newly generated mature oligodendrocytes (EdU⁺/CC1⁺ cells), we further confirmed that microglial LAL overexpression enhanced OPC differentiation into mature OLs following WMS (Fig. 4K-L). In conclusion, increased LAL expression in microglia determines the lipid metabolic activity and reparative capacity of microglia following irreparable white matter injury.

To investigate the contribution of LAL expressed by other cell types to white matter repair, we injected lentivirus that expressed LAL under the ubiquitous CAG promoter into the corpus callosum. Theoretically, this approach resulted in nonspecific overexpression of LAL in many cell types (Supplemental Fig. 6E-G). Non-specific LAL overexpression also reduced white matter demyelinated lesions (Supplemental Fig. 6H-I) and improved performance in both the novel object recognition (NOR) test and grid-walking assay at 3 months after L-NIO-induced WMS (Supplemental Fig. 6J-K). However, the reduction in demyelinated lesions was less pronounced with non-specific overexpression than with microglia-specific LAL overexpression (Fig. 4E versus supplemental Fig. 6H-I). These findings indicate that enhanced LAL primarily acts through microglia to promote white matter repair.

To further confirm the critical role of LAL in white matter repair, we investigated whether LAL is also indispensable to LPC-induced spontaneous white matter repair. To this end, lentivirus expressing Lipa-shRNA was injected into the left corpus callosum to knock down LAL expression (Supplemental Fig. 7A-B). LAL knockdown remarkably blocked spontaneous remyelination at 3 months post-LPC injection (Supplemental Fig. 7C-D) and exacerbated neurological deficits in the NOR test and grid-walking ability at 3 months post-injury (Supplemental Fig. 7E-F).

LAL reprograms microglia into a reparable state by metabolizing cholesterol to activate LXR

LAL is the only known lysosomal enzyme capable of hydrolyzing cholesteryl esters (CE) into free cholesterol, and free cholesterol can be further metabolized into cholesterol metabolites such as oxysterols. To investigate how LAL promotes the microglial reparative phenotype, we profiled 43 sterol species. Among the 23 detectable sterol species, we identified two differentially abundant oxysterols, 25-hydroxycholesterol (25-OHC) and 7-ketocholesterol, which were significantly elevated in LPC-induced reparable lesions relative to L-NIO-induced ischemic irreparable lesions (Fig. 5A, Supplemental Fig. 8A-B). Notably, desmosterol levels did not differ between reparable and irreparable lesions following injury (Fig. 5A).

25-OHC is the most potent endogenous activator of LXRs [10]. Consistent with the results of sterol specie profiling, GSVA results obtained from our scRNA-seq dataset al.so revealed that the “FXR/LXR regulation of cholesterol metabolism” pathway was activated in cluster 0 microglia from LPC-induced lesions compared with those from L-NIO-induced lesions (Fig. 5B). LXRs are nuclear transcription factors that regulate cholesterol, fatty acid, and glucose homeostasis by controlling the expression of lipid metabolism-related genes [12]. Similarly, bulk RNA-seq data showed that LXR/RXR target genes—including Nr1h2 (which encodes LXRβ), Apoe, Abca1, Abcg1, and cholesterol 25-hydroxylase—were upregulated in LPC-induced reparable lesions relative to L-NIO-induced ischemic lesions (Fig. 5C). Using qRT-PCR, we confirmed the upregulation of cholesterol transport genes (Apoe, Abca1, Abcg1) in LPC-induced lesions compared with L-NIO-induced lesions (Supplemental Fig. 8C). More relevantly, both microglia-specific LAL overexpression and non-specific LAL overexpression in white matter lesions increased the mRNA levels of Apoe, Abca1, and Abcg1 in L-NIO-induced lesions (Fig. 5D-F, Supplemental Fig. 8D). In conclusion, increased LAL expression in microglia promotes LXR activation and reprograms gene expression in white matter lesions.

Next, we investigated whether LXR activation drives white matter repair following L-NIO-induced irreparable WMS. Treatment with the LXR agonist GW3965 after WMS resulted in nearly complete remyelination (Fig. 5G-H) and improved myelin reorganization (Fig. 5I-J). Compared to vehicle, GW3965 also enhanced mouse performance in the novel object recognition (NOR) test and grid-walking assay at 3 months post-WMS induction (Supplemental Fig. 8E-F). Conversely, knockdown of LXRβ in the corpus callosum (Supplemental Fig. 8G-H) blocked LPC-induced spontaneous remyelination, as evidenced by persistent demyelinated lesions at 3 months post-LPC injection (Fig. 5K-L). Relative to control lentivirus, LXRβ knockdown exacerbated neurological deficits in both NOR test performance and grid-walking ability at 9 weeks post-LPC injection (Fig. 5M, Supplemental Fig. 8I).

Cholesterol 25-hydroxylase (CH25H) is the rate-limiting enzyme catalyzing the biosynthesis of 25-OHC. Microglial LAL expression and 25-OHC levels were markedly elevated in LPC-induced white matter lesions. To further validate the role of the LAL-25-OHC axis in white matter repair, we investigated whether CH25H knockdown within white matter lesions (Supplemental Fig. 9A-B) could block the spontaneous repair of LPC-induced white matter lesions. CH25H knockdown resulted in the persistence of demyelination, assessed by MBP staining with DAPI nuclear counterstain, at 4 weeks post-LPC-induced injury (Supplemental Fig. 9C-D). CH25H knockdown also markedly exacerbated neurological deficits, as evidenced by impaired performance in the novel object recognition (NOR) test grid-walking test at 4 weeks post-injury (Supplemental Fig. 9E-F). Using qRT-PCR, we confirmed that Ch25h-shRNA-mediated knockdown significantly downregulated the expression of LXR target genes Apoe, Abca1, and Abcg1 in white matter lesions at 4 days post-LPC injury, relative to non-targeting control (NC)-shRNA treatment (Supplemental Fig. 9G-I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CH25H knockdown abrogated LAL-mediated LXR activation and impaired white matter repair.

Next, we employed microglia-specific LAL knockout mice to investigate whether microglial LAL activity served as a primary source of 25-OHC in white matter lesions. We used ELISA to measure lesional 25-OHC levels. First, we validated that ELISA-measured 25-OHC levels in LPC- and L-NIO-induced white matter lesions (Supplemental Fig. 10A) were comparable to those obtained via mass spectrometry (Supplemental Fig. 8B), thus confirming the reliability of the ELISA assay. To validate the specificity and efficiency of microglial-specific LAL deletion in knockout mice (Cx3cr1CreERT2+/−: Lipaflox/flox treated with tamoxifen), we performed immunofluorescence staining to assess LAL expression at 4 days post LPC-induced demyelination. Compared with oil-treated controls, tamoxifen (TAM)-treatment did not alter LAL expression in peripheral IBA1+ monocytes in the liver and spleen (Supplemental Fig. 10B-G), demonstrating that the strategy would not result in the deletion of LAL in peripheral monocytes/macrophages. In contrast, TAM-injection resulted in a marked reduction in LAL expression in cerebral IBA1+ cells at the injury site. Notably, 25-OHC levels were significantly elevated in white matter lesions of wild-type control mice (Supplemental Fig. 10A) after LPC-induced injury; by comparison, microglia-specific LAL deletion caused a marked reduction in lesional 25-OHC levels relative to control mice (Supplemental Fig. 10H). In conclusion, microglial LAL activity constitutes a primary source of 25-OHC in white matter lesions. Collectively, these results indicate a functional regulatory link between LAL, the CH25H/25-OHC axis, and LXR signaling in the white matter repair process.

LAL-mediated lysosomal lipolysis is the primary pathway for cholesterol ester hydrolysis in microglia and required for effective remyelination following white matter injury

Several cytosolic hydrolases, including cholesterol ester hydrolase (Nceh1) and hormone-sensitive lipase (Lipe)—are known to play essential roles in cholesteryl ester (CE) hydrolysis in macrophages [13]. Importantly, it is assumed that cytosolic lipases act synergistically with LAL to hydrolyze CE and only the inhibition of both pathways totally abolishes cholesteryl ester hydrolysis [13, 14]. Our scRNA-seq data revealed that Lipe mRNA displayed extremely low expression in microglia, whereas Nceh1 mRNA expression was significantly induced in microglia (and particularly in cluster 0 microglia) following both LPC-induced reparable and L-NIO-induced irreparable white matter injury (Supplemental Fig. 11A-B). Consistent with scRNA-seq data, immunofluorescence staining revealed a significant upregulation of NCEH1 protein in microglia within white matter lesions following both LPC- and L-NIO-induced demyelination (Supplemental Fig. 11C-D). Notably, no differences in total NCEH1 expression or microglia-enriched NCEH1 expression were detected between these two injury models (Supplemental Fig. 11C-D). In line with the scRNA-seq data, immunofluorescence results revealed that LIPE expression in white matter lesions was unaltered and remained at extremely low levels following LPC or L-NIO challenge, comparable to that in sham-operated control mice (Supplemental Fig. 11E-F). These findings prompt a critical question: following white matter injury, which lipolysis pathway, i.e. that mediated by the cytosolic lipase Nceh1 or by lysosmal Lipa, serves as the primary mechanism for cholesteryl ester hydrolysis in microglia?

To address the issue, we investigated the effects of microglia-specific LAL deletion on CE hydrolase activity following white matter injury. We employed Filipin staining to quantify free cholesterol levels, which served as a surrogate marker for CE hydrolase activity. Strikingly, microglia-specific LAL deletion almost completely abolished free cholesterol production in LAL knockout mice at 4 days post-LPC-induced reparable white matter injury, a time point characterized by substantial free cholesterol accumulation in LPC-induced lesions of control mice (Fig. 6A-B). Notably, at 4 days post-LPC-induced demyelination, NCEH1 protein remained significantly upregulated in microglia within the white matter lesions of LAL-KO mice, whereas LIPE expression was sustained at extremely low levels. Both expression levels were comparable to those in control mice (Supplemental Fig. 11G–J). Given the comparable maximal upregulation of NCEH1 alongside near-complete loss of CE hydrolase activity in LAL-KO microglia within white matter lesions, these results indicate that lysosomal LAL—rather than cytosolic CE hydrolase NCEH1—acts as the primary enzyme mediating CE hydrolysis in microglia after white matter injury.

Given that LAL-mediated lysosomal lipolysis is the primary mechanism of cholesteryl ester hydrolysis, and that only free cholesterol can be transported via cholesterol reverse transport, we hypothesized that LAL deficiency—which blocks the conversion of cholesteryl esters to free cholesterol—would impair cholesterol efflux and consequently result in defective myelin debris clearance, even under conditions of LXR activation. To test this hypothesis, we induced white matter injury with LPC in microglia-specific LAL knockout mice, followed by injection of the LXR activator GW3965. Consistent with the central role of LAL in cholesteryl ester hydrolysis, free cholesterol was undetectable in lesion-resident microglia of LAL-KO mice at 4 weeks post-LPC injury, irrespective of GW3965 or vehicle treatment (Fig. 6C–D). In contrast, neutral lipid droplets accumulated markedly in lesion microglia of LAL-KO mice to levels comparable to those in control mice at 4 weeks post-injury, with no significant effect of vehicle versus GW3965 treatment (Fig. 6E–F). Thus, the concurrent accumulation of neutral lipid droplets alongside absent free cholesterol in the microglia of GW3965-treated LAL knockout mice indicates that the lack of free cholesterol arises from LAL deficiency-impaired cholesteryl ester hydrolysis, rather than from reverse cholesterol transport activated by GW3965.

Consistent with the hypothesis that LAL-mediated conversion of cholesteryl esters to free cholesterol is required for myelin debris clearance following white matter injury, microglia-specific LAL deficiency resulted in defective myelin debris clearance—even under conditions of continuous LXR activation by GW3965. This was evidenced by the persistent accumulation of myelin debris within microglia and demyelinated lesions at 3 months after LPC-induced white matter injury (Fig. 6G–J). Consequently, mice with LAL deletion exhibited exacerbated neurological deficits in the novel object recognition (NOR) test and grid-walking assay at 9 weeks post-LPC injection, irrespective of GW3965 treatment (Fig. 6K–L). In summary, LAL-mediated lysosomal cholesteryl ester hydrolysis in microglia to promote myelin debris clearance through two essential mechanisms: (1) generating 25-OHC to activate LXR, and (2) converting cholesteryl esters to free cholesterol—a prerequisite for cholesterol efflux. Thus, LXR activation alone is insufficient to rescue white matter repair deficits caused by LAL deficiency.

HβCD specifically targets LAL-mediated lysosomal lipolysis to drive remyelination following white matter stroke

Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HβCD) is an FDA-approved vehicle for drug delivery and an efficient cholesterol-lowering agent [22]. Notably, HβCD increases the expression of cytosolic hydrolase Nceh1 but not that of Lipa (LAL) or Lipe [23]. Based on these findings, we initially hypothesized that HβCD would not drive white matter repair following WMS. Strikingly, the performance of HβCD-treated mice in NOR test recovered to levels comparable to sham-operated mice at 3 months post-WMS (Supplemental Fig. 12A). Moreover, HβCD promoted nearly complete remyelination of white matter in mice at 3 months after WMS induction (Fig. 7A-B). Relative to vehicle treatment, HβCD also markedly reduced free cholesterol deposition within microglia (Fig. 7C-D) and intracellular accumulation of myelin debris inside the microglia within WMS-induced irreparable lesions (Fig. 7E-F). Staining with the paranodal markers Caspr and Pan [24] further revealed that HβCD increased myelin density and facilitated myelin reorganization in ischemic lesions (Fig. 7G-H). Besides, HβCD also markedly reduced neutral lipid droplets accumulated markedly in microglia within the lesions (Fig. 7I-J).

Currently, no drug specifically targeting LAL-mediated lysosomal lipolysis has been identified. Strikingly, despite systemic administration of HβCD via intraperitoneal injection, we unexpectedly found that HβCD significantly increased LAL expression specifically within the white matter lesions, but not in other brain regions, relative to vehicle treatment following WMS (Fig. 7N-O). Next, we showed that HβCD specifically elevated LAL levels in primary microglia only in the presence of myelin debris (Supplemental Fig. 12D–E). This in vitro finding was consistent with the in vivo observation of selective LAL upregulation by HβCD within white matter lesions, suggesting that myelin debris is required for the induction of LAL expression. Concurrently, systemic HβCD administration did not alter free cholesterol levels or lipid droplet accumulation in peripheral IBA1+ cells of the liver and spleen, nor in microglia within non-injured brain regions, all of which remained at uninduced basal levels after WMS (Supplemental Fig. 12F–O). Moreover, FluoroMyelin staining confirmed the absence of myelin debris accumulation in non-injured brain regions, and HβCD administration did not alter myelin debris accumulation in microglia of these regions post-WMS (Supplemental Fig. 13A–F). In conclusion, systemic HβCD administration exerted region-specific effects restricted to white matter lesions.

Following L-NIO-induced WMS, systemic HβCD administration potently ameliorated the inflammatory microenvironment at the white matter injury site, as evidenced by decreased levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, and increased levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Supplemental Fig. 13G–J). Notably, systemic HβCD administration had no discernible effects on inflammatory profiles in the liver, spleen, or non-lesioned brain regions, all of which remained at uninduced basal levels (Supplemental Fig. 13G–J). Furthermore, HβCD increased the transcription of LXR-targeted cholesterol efflux genes (including Apoe, Abcg1, and Abca1; Fig. 8B) in white matter lesions following WMS. Collectively, these results suggest that HβCD reprograms microglia toward a reparative state following WMS, potentially via LAL-mediated LXR activation.

To confirm that HβCD acts through microglial LAL to drive white matter repair, we used microglia-specific LAL knockout mice. Relative to HβCD-treated control mice, HβCD failed to drive remyelination or improve neurological deficits in the novel object recognition (NOR) test or grid-walking assay in LAL knockout mice (Fig. 8C-D, Supplemental Fig. 14A). Moreover, microglial LAL deficiency blocked HβCD-enhanced lipid metabolism, evidenced by neutral lipid deposition at 4 weeks post-WMS (Fig. 8E, Supplemental Fig. 14B). Microglial LAL deficiency also abrogated the HβCD-induced reduction in intracellular myelin debris accumulation inside microglia following WMS (Fig. 8F). In conclusion, enhancing LAL-mediated lysosomal lipolysis specifically within white matter lesions represents a previously unrecognized mechanism underlying HβCD’s pharmacological effects on white matter repair.

Discussion

Our findings reveal that LAL-mediated lysosomal hydrolysis of cholesteryl esters is a currently unrecognized mechanism underlying the capacity of microglia to drive white matter repair. LAL is the only known enzyme that hydrolyses cholesteryl esters in the lysosome, and its functional role in white matter repair has not been investigated thus far. Unbiased transcriptomics and functional studies with microglia-specific overexpression and deletion approaches have confirmed the critical role of LAL-mediated lysosomal lipolysis in driving white matter repair following both reparable and irreparable injury (Supplemental Fig. 15).

In addition to functioning as a structural component of all membranes of mammalian cells, cholesterol and its derivatives and precursors play essential roles in signaling pathway transduction. Here, by systematically comparing the transcriptomic profiles of LPC-induced reparable white matter injury and WMS-induced irreparable white matter injury and performing functional studies, we identified that LAL-mediated lysosomal hydrolysis of cholesterol esters drove white matter repair in part via the activation of LXR by 25-OHC. Our study revealed that microglial cholesterol metabolism is an unrecognized mechanism underlying remyelination following white matter injury. CNS microglia depend on sterol synthesis to produce desmosterol for efficient cholesterol efflux following cuprizone-induced experimental MS9. Whether different models account for the discrepancy between this publication [9] and our findings deserves further investigation. Although cytosolic hydrolases are assumed to act synergistically with LAL to hydrolyze cholesterol esters [13, 14], we found that microglial hydrolysis of cholesterol esters critically depends on the lysosomal hydrolase LAL. In conclusion, our results highlight the dispensable roles of LAL-mediated lysosomal lipolysis in microglial hydrolysis of cholesterol esters and in repair after white matter injury.

We revealed that microglial LAL is the key mediator that determines lipid metabolism activity in GPNMB+ microglia, the predominant myelin debris-engulfing microglia subset in white matter lesions. LAL deficiency has been linked to metabolic disorders and significant morbidity due to liver failure and/or atherosclerosis. Here, we showed that LAL is the master inducer of the reparative phenotype of microglia and that targeting this single regulator is sufficient to drive white matter repair. LAL is required for LXR activation during peripheral efferocytosis [25, 26]. LXR activation ameliorates glial lipid accumulation and neurodegeneration in AD disease models [27]. Here, we show that microglial LAL drives white matter repair via two essential mechanisms. First, LAL-mediated cholesterol ester hydrolysis leads to LXR activation via enhanced production of 25-OHC. The other mechanism involves LAL-mediated hydrolysis of cholesterol esters into free cholesterol, which is required for cholesterol efflux. Thus, LXR activation alone cannot rescue the LAL deficiency-induced impairment of white matter repair. Although numerous studies have shown that LXR activation is a fundamental mechanism of remyelination, our findings suggest that increased LAL expression has broader significance than does LXR activation. HβCD is an FDA-approved vehicle for drug delivery and an efficient cholesterol-lowering agent [22]. Notably, HβCD reportedly increases the expression of the cytosolic hydrolase Nceh1 but not that of Lipa or Lipe [23]. In contrast, we showed here that HβCD enhanced LAL expression specifically within WMS-induced ischemic lesions and that targeting LAL is an unrecognized mechanism underlying the pharmacological effects of HβCD.

DAMs are currently considered the major microglial subtype responsible for white matter repair [28]. Here, we employed scRNA sequencing to identify a GPNMB+ microglial subset that displayed a distinct gene signature relative to DAMs. Importantly, this subset represents the most abundant microglial population and the major subtype responsible for myelin debris clearance in white matter lesions. Notably, white matter-associated microglia (WAMs) have been identified in the white matter of aging individuals [29]. However, unlike the subset identified in this study, WAMs do not express the genes associated with injury and ATM. Analysis of publicly available datasets further revealed that a microglial subpopulation with an analogous gene signature is also present in chronic active white matter lesions from multiple sclerosis (MS) patients, suggesting that this subset may also play a functional role in MS pathogenesis.

We identified that GPNMB is one of the most robust marker gene for this microglial subset. Recent studies reported that a GPNMB+ microglial population is expanded and may exert protective or reparative abilities in AD and MS patients [18]. We found that the expansion of the GPNMB+ microglia alone was insufficient to drive white matter repair unless LAL expression is elevated. This finding underscores the indispensable functional role of LAL in GPNMB+ microglia. Whether LAL plays an equally critical role in GPNMB+ microglia under other disease conditions merits further investigation.

In the present study, we showed that GPNMB+ microglia dominates myelin debris engulfment but shows context-dependent myelin debris digestion capacity following white matter injury. As specific microglial subpopulations exhibit context-dependent functions [30], GPNMB+ microglia likewise displayed functional heterogeneity. GPNMB+ microglia dramatically expanded in both repairable and irreparable ischemic white matter lesions, with comparable myelin debris engulfment activity. However, the digestion of engulfed myelin debris, which is determined by LAL-mediated lipid catabolism, was inhibited in GPNMB+ microglia in irreparable ischemic lesions relative to those in reparable white matter lesions. Collectively, we identified a reparative microglial state characterized by simultaneously high expression of GPNMB and LAL.

GPNMB+LAL+ microglia-mediated digestion of myelin debris aids remyelination, possibly not only by clearing debris-derived remyelination inhibitors. It may also help reprogram microglia into reparable states, since the digestion of apoptotic cells has been shown to be required for epigenetic induction of reparative phenotypes of macrophages [9, 31]. Moreover, reparative phenotypes of microglia/macrophages drive OPC differentiation by secreting Activin A [32]. Thus, additional investigations are warranted to elucidate the role of Activin A in LAL-mediated repair following white matter injury. Enhanced reverse cholesterol transport may also supplies microglial cholesterol to oligodendrocytes, thereby supporting remyelination [33]. Likewise, cholesterol-enriched extracellular vesicles secreted by repair-associated macrophages boost remyelination capacity by promoting the differentiation of OPCs [34]. Thus, further investigations are warranted to clarify whether microglia donate their intracellular cholesterol pools to OPCs or oligodendrocytes to support remyelination.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not investigate the mechanism underlying the inhibition of microglial LAL expression in irreparable white matter lesions vs. reparable lesions. An investigation into this area could reveal more therapeutic targets. For example, reduced LAL expression in aging neurons is associated with RNA polymerase pausing [35]. Whether removing the transcriptional brake on LAL expression promotes white matter repair needs further investigation. Second, this study has a technical limitation related to the use of MBP staining, which only assesses demyelinated sizes and does not fully quantify tissue destruction. To more precisely evaluate white matter injury and repair, future studies should employ complementary methods such as nuclear counterstaining to assess tissue integrity at lesion sites. Third, the study was only performed in male mice. Further research is needed to investigate whether the findings can be translated into females or whether there are gender differences.

Overall, this work revealed that LAL-mediated specific lysosomal lipolysis in microglia drives white matter repair. Research holds the potential for the treatment of irreparable white matter injury.

Materials and methods

Mice and drug treatment

Adult male ICR mice purchased from the Covance Experimental Animal Company (Changzhou, China) were used in this study. To generate genetically engineered OPC reporter mice (NG2CreERT2+/−:RosatdT/+), NG2CreERT2+/+ mice [36] were crossed with RosatdT/tdT mice (Shanghai Model Organisms, Shanghai, China), resulting in NG2CreERT2+/−: RosatdT/+ mice. Cx3cr1CreERT2+/+ mice [B6.129P2(C)-CX3cr1tm2.1(cre/ERT2)Jung/J] were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. These mice were crossed with RosatdT/tdT mice to generate Cx3cr1CreERT2+/−:RosatdT/+ mice. Cx3cr1CreERT2+/+ mice were crossed with Lipaflox/flox mice (Cyagen, China) to generate Cx3cr1CreERT2+/−: Lipaflox/flox mice.

HβCD (Bidepharm, China) was dissolved in saline and administered via subcutaneous injection every other day at a dose of 400 mg/kg, starting at 24 h after the induction of white matter injury. GW3965 (Bidepharm, China) was dissolved in DMSO and corn oil (1:9, v/v) and administered via intraperitoneal (IP) injection every day at a dose of 20 mg/kg, starting at 24 h after the induction of white matter injury. Tamoxifen (Aladdin, China) was dissolved in corn oil and administered via IP injection daily at a dose of 100 mg/kg.

White Matter Stroke (WMS) and toxin-induced white matter injury

Animal protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Soochow University (Approval NO. SUDA20250720A01) and under project licenses of grants from the National Natural Science Foundation of China (82271315, 81971119, 82071469, and 32271033). Under anesthesia, mouse heads were fixed using a stereotaxic apparatus adapter (Shenzhen RWD Life Science, China). A cotton swab dipped in iodine was applied to the scalp, and the skin was incised to expose the skull. L-NIO (0.5 µL, diluted in saline to a concentration of 27 mg/mL, Abcam, USA) or LPC (2 µL, diluted in saline to a concentration of 5 mg/mL, Sigma, USA) was injected into the corpus callosum via a microinjector equipped with a glass electrode (10 µL, Sangon Biotech, China). The coordinates were set as anterior 0.8 mm (+ 0.8 mm), lateral ± 1 mm (left or right), and ventral − 1.7 mm relative to the skull surface and the reference point bregma. The injection was performed at a rate of 0.07 µL/min for L-NIO or 0.1 µL/min for LPC. After the injection, the needle was left in place for 5 min before being withdrawn slowly. The scalp was then sutured, and the mice were kept warm after surgery until they fully regained consciousness.

Behavioral tests

Novel object recognition was performed according to previously published methods [37]. In short, the main testing apparatus consisted of a polyethylene plastic chamber, in which two identical rectangular objects were placed at equal distances (8–10 cm) from the chamber walls. Each mouse was placed in the chamber and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. After 2 h, one of the rectangular objects was replaced with a novel-shaped object, and the same mouse was reintroduced into the chamber for a 5 min test session. The recognition index was calculated as the percentage of time spent exploring the novel object over the total exploration time for both objects.

The foot fault test was conducted as described previously [38]. Briefly, one week prior to surgery, a grid-walking test (5 min per mouse) was conducted for each mouse to determine baseline performance. The same test was subsequently performed at 2, 4, and 9 weeks post-surgery. The total number of steps and the number of errors made by both limbs were recorded. A limb error was defined as a slip or miss when the limb failed to properly grasp the grid. The test results were calculated as follows: (contralateral limb errors - ipsilateral limb errors)/total steps × 100%.

Fixation, cryosectioning and immunofluorescence (IF)

After the mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane (Shenzhen RWD Life Science, China), a perfusion needle was inserted into the left ventricle. The liver was perfused with 1× PBS until it turned completely white, indicating sufficient flushing. Then, 1×PFA was perfused until the liver became firm, and the signal fixation was complete. The mouse brains were then harvested and placed in freshly prepared 1× PFA for fixation for 8–12 h. After fixation, the tissues were transferred to sucrose for cryoprotection. Sections of the tissues (20 μm thickness) were obtained via a freezing microtome (Shenzhen RWD Life Science, China).

The brain sections were washed three times with 1× PBS for 5 min. The brain sections were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to block nonspecific binding with 3% BSA solution. After blocking and washing, the brain sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Then, the brain sections were treated with secondary antibodies in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. After the above steps were completed, the sections were washed with 1× PBS to remove the secondary antibody, followed by the addition of mounting medium, cover slipping, and sealing. Images were acquired via a laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

The following primary antibodies were used: IBA1 guinea pig pAb (IF: 1:500, OB-PGP049-01, Oasis, China), LAL polyclonal antibody (IF: 1:500, 12956-1-AP, Proteintech, China), CC1 rat pAb (IF: 1:200, OB-PRT039-02, Oasis, China), Filipin solution (IF: 1:10, SAE0088, Sigma, USA), MBP rabbit pAb (IF: 1:500, OB-PRB130-02, Oasis, China), CASPR rabbit polyclonal antibody (IF: 1:300, 55417-1-AP, Proteintech, China), goat anti-mouse osteoactivin/GPNMB antibody (IF: 1:200, AF2330-SP, Biotech, USA), FluoroMyelin™ Red (IF: 1:400, F34652, Thermo Fisher, USA), mouse anti-sodium Channel (PAN, 1:100, S8809, Sigma, USA), LAMP1 antibody (IF: 1:400, sc-20011, Santa Cruz, USA), LIPE polyclonal antibody (IF: 1:300, 17333-1-AP, Proteintech, China), NCEH1 polyclonal antibody (IF: 1:300, 14021-1-AP, Proteintech, China) and Bodipy 493/503 (IF: 1:2000, 121207-31-6, Amgicam, China).

Cell culture and experiment

Primary microglia were isolated from the cerebral of postnatal day 1–3 Wild type mouse. The procedure was as follows: (1) Brain were aseptically dissected, and meninges and blood vessels were carefully removed. (2) The tissue was minced and digested with 0.25% trypsin in Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution at 37 °C for 15 min. (3) Digestion was terminated with DMEM/F12 complete medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. A single-cell suspension was obtained by passing the tissue through a 70 μm cell strainer. (4) Cells were seeded into poly-D-lysine-coated culture flasks and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. (5) After 14 days of culture, microglia were harvested by gentle orbital shaking (180 rpm, 37 °C, 2 h). Harvested primary microglia were plated and allowed to adhere overnight before treatment. Primary microglia were co-treated with myelin debris (100 µg/mL) and hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HβCD, final concentration 1 mM) for 6 h. After treatment, cells were harvested for IF.

BV2 cells, an immortalized mouse microglial cell line, were routinely cultured in high-glucose DMEM complete medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. For experiments, cells were seeded in culture plates and allowed to adhere. After adhesion, cells were infected with the indicated virus for 6 h. Following infection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and cells were further cultured for 48 h. Subsequently, the medium was changed to fresh complete medium containing purified myelin debris (final concentration 100 µg/mL, stained with PKH26) for an additional 6 h. After treatment, cells were harvested for immunofluorescence (IF) analysis.

ELISA

At designated time points, mice subjected to various treatments were euthanized. Target brain regions (the corpus callosum lesion area and no-lesion brain area) and control tissues (liver, spleen) were rapidly dissected, immediately rinsed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove blood, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C until further use. Approximately 25 mg of frozen tissue was weighed and placed in a homogenization tube containing pre-cooled tissue lysis buffer. The tissue was thoroughly homogenized on ice using a tissue homogenizer. The homogenate was transferred to a microcentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully aspirated into a new tube, avoiding the pellet. The total protein concentration of each sample was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China) for subsequent data normalization. The concentrations of target cytokines were measured using high-sensitivity ELISA kits, strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions (25-OHC: Mlbio, China; inflammatory factors: Elabscience, China). The absolute cytokine concentration (pg/mL) for each sample was interpolated from the standard curve based on its mean absorbance. Finally, to account for variations in tissue extraction efficiency, the cytokine concentration for each sample was normalized to its total protein content, and the results are expressed as pg cytokine per mg total protein (pg/mg).

Western blot

The injured and contralateral tissues of the corpus callosum were collected at specified time points after white matter injury. RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) was used to lyse the tissue samples. The homogenates were centrifuged at 4 °C and 13,200 rpm for 30 min. The supernatants were collected, and the protein concentration was determined via a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). A total of 25 µg of protein per sample was denatured, separated on a 10% SDS‒PAGE gel, and then transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 10% nonfat milk for 30 min, washed, and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After overnight incubation, the membranes were washed and probed with appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA) was used to visualize the membrane. The following primary antibodies were used: LAL polyclonal antibody (WB: 1:1000, 12956-1-AP, Proteintech, China), NR1H2 mouse mAb (LXRβ, WB: 1:1000, 60345-1-lg, Proteintech, China), goat anti-mouse osteoactivin/GPNMB antibody (WB: 1:1000, AF2330-SP, Biotech, USA), CH25H rabbit polyclonal antibody (WB: 1:1000, bs-6480R, Bioss, China), β-actin mouse mAb (WB: 1:4000, FD0060, Fdbio, China), β-tubulin mouse mAb (WB: 1:4000, FD0064, Fdbio, China) and GAPDH mouse mAb (WB: 1:2000, M20006S, Abmart, China). All Western blot results are shown in Supplemental Fig. 16 and Supplemental Fig. 17.

Assessment of cell proliferation with 5-Ethynyl-2’-Deoxyuridine (EdU)

To label proliferating cells, 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU; Bidepharm, China) was dissolved in saline and administered via IP injection twice daily at a dose of 5 mg/kg from day 4 to day 14 after the induction of white matter injury. Then, the mouse brain tissues were fixed and cryosectioned into 20 μm thick sections. Then, the slices were incubated with the reaction mixture provided by the EdU kit (Beyotime, China) in a dark box at room temperature for 30 min. After incubation, the brain slices were washed three times with 1× PBS for 5 min each time to remove any unbound reagents, followed by the application of mounting medium, coverslipping, and sealing. Images were acquired under a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). EdU-positive cells fluoresce, indicating cell proliferation.

Quantitative real-time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR)

The injured and contralateral tissues were isolated from the corpus callosum at specified time points after the induction of white matter injury. RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and other experimental processes were performed as previously described [38]. Briefly, total RNA was isolated via the RNAprep Pure Tissue Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (TIANGEN, China). Reverse transcription and RT‒PCR were performed using gDNA wiper mix, HiScript III qRT SuperMix, and the SYBR Green RT‒PCR Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Transcript expression was determined in triplicate and normalized to that of the housekeeping gene GAPDH. A real-time PCR instrument (Thermo, USA) was used to perform fluorescence quantitative PCR.

Bulk RNA sequencing and analysis

The injured and contralateral tissues were isolated from the corpus callosum at 4 days after LPC-induced repair injury or L-NIO-induced WMS. The corpus callosum tissue collected from the sham-operated mice served as a control. The tissue samples were sent to a company (GENEWIZ Biotechnology, China) for high-throughput transcriptome sequencing. In brief, total RNA was extracted via TRIzol reagent and quantified, after which ribosomal RNA depletion and poly(A) purification were performed. The cDNA library was subsequently prepared via the RNA Library Prep Kit. Libraries were quantified and pooled for paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

The qualities of the paired-end sequencing data were controlled via Fastp (version 0.24.0), and the data were aligned to the reference genome via HISAT2 (version 2.2.1). The reference genome and annotation files were obtained from GRCm39. Bulk RNA-seq data were analyzed with R (version 4.4.2) for bioinformatics processing. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on the injured sides of LPC and L-NIO, with pathways filtered and retained at p < 0.05. Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) was applied to associate sample phenotypes with gene modules and explore their relationships [39]. The regulatory network among gene modules was visualized in R, and key regulatory genes were identified. The gene modules associated with the LPC-induced recovery phenotype were subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis [40], and enriched pathways were filtered at p < 0.05. The hub gene interaction network derived from bulk RNA-seq data was visualized and analyzed with Cytoscape (version 3.10.2).

Single-cell RNA sequencing and analysis

The injured and contralateral tissues were isolated from the corpus callosum at 4 days after LPC-induced repair injury or L-NIO-induced WMS. The corpus callosum tissue collected from the sham-operated mice served as a control. The tissue samples were sent to a company (CapitalBio Technology, China) for single-cell high-throughput sequencing (scRNA-seq) via the 10× Genomics platform. Briefly, viable single-cell suspensions (> 90% viability) were loaded onto microfluidic chips to generate gel bead-in-emulsions (GEMs), where cellular mRNA was barcoded and reverse transcribed. Amplified cDNA underwent fragmentation, adapter ligation, and library construction according to the manufacturer’s protocol. High-throughput sequencing was performed via the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to obtain high-quality transcriptome data. The qualities of preliminary quality were controlled by removing low-quality reads and connector sequences. Then, we utilized Cell Ranger software (version 9.0.1) provided by 10× Genomics for data decoding, UMI counting, and gene expression matrix generation.

We performed bioinformatics analysis on the scRNA-seq data via R (version 4.4.2). The Seurat package (version 5.1.0) was used to read, control quality, debatch, and perform dimensionality reduction clustering on the sequencing data. The DoubletFinder package (version 2.0.4) was used to remove double cells. Gene expression matrices were normalized by total UMI counts per cell (NormalizeData) to enable cross-cell comparisons, followed by variance stabilization (ScaleData) to mitigate the influence of highly variable or abundantly expressed genes during downstream analyses. For dimensional reduction and clustering, principal component analysis (RunPCA) was first performed, followed by batch effect correction via Harmony. Cell clusters were subsequently identified through k-nearest neighbor graph construction (FindNeighbors) and UMAP embedding (RunUMAP). Finally, cluster-specific marker genes (FindAllMarkers) were identified on the basis of differential expression analysis. The cells of each cluster were annotated according to the marker gene reference CellMarker 2.0 and the cell annotation marker genes in published articles [15, 41–54]. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was subsequently performed, and the enriched pathways were filtered and screened according to p < 0.05. The GSEA results from LPC and L-NIO microglia were compared with the GSEA results from the Bulk RNA-seq data, and the intersecting pathways were identified (p < 0.05). Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was employed to assess differences in enriched pathways among distinct subgroups within the same cell type. AddModuleScore was utilized to calculate cell type-specific gene set scores and pathway-specific gene set scores [55].

Specific expression of LAL in microglia

To induce the targeted expression of LAL and enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) in microglia expressing Cre recombinase, we used a lentivirus-mediated double-floxed inverted orientation system (LV-DIO-LIPA) and Cx3cr1CreERT2+/- mice, as previously reported [20]. The lentivirus (LV-DIO-LIPA) was injected into the corpus callosum of Cx3cr1CreERT2+/- mice. Starting from day 3 after lentiviral injection, the mice received daily IP injections of tamoxifen (100 mg/kg in corn oil) or vehicle (corn oil alone) for 5 consecutive days. Fourteen days after injection, Cx3cr1CreERT2+/- mice from both groups were subjected to L-NIO-induced WMS.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

At 9 weeks after L-NIO-induced WMS, the brain tissues were fixed and sent to a commercial service provider (Kexue Zhinan, Hangzhou Research Compass Biotech, China) for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging. In summary, axonal cross-sections were observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which captured high-resolution myelin images at magnifications > 10,000×.

Mass spectrometry detection of cholesterol and lipid profiles

The injured and contralateral tissues were isolated from the corpus callosum at 4 days after LPC-induced repair injury or L-NIO-induced WMS. The corpus callosum tissue collected from the sham-operated mice served as a control. The samples were sent to a company for mass spectrometric analysis of cholesterol metabolites (MetWare Biotechnology, China). Briefly, cholesterol species and lipids are extracted from tissues, followed by purification and derivatization. Then, cholesterol species are separated via liquid chromatography or gas chromatography. Molecules are ionized via electrospray ionization and analyzed to determine their mass‒charge ratios. Cholesterol profiles are identified by database matching (LIPID MAPS) with quantified concentrations. The mass spectrometry data were processed for quality control and analyzed with R (version 4.4.2). In brief, we standardized the data and performed differential analysis on measured data (n = 3) and converted the difference values to log2 values to screen for significantly different cholesterol and lipid profiles (abs[log2(fold change)] > 1, p value < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

Prior to statistical comparisons, the normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was evaluated via the Brown–Forsythe test. For comparisons between two independent groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’ s t tests were used when data met assumptions of normality and equal variance. When variances were unequal, Welch’ s t test was applied. For comparisons between two paired groups, paired two-tailed Student’s t tests were used. If data violated normality assumptions, the nonparametric Mann–Whitney test (for independent groups) was employed. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was performed when data met parametric assumptions, followed by Tukey’ s post hoc test for equal variances or the Games–Howell test for unequal variances. For comparisons involving two independent variables, two-way ANOVA was conducted. In cases of significant interaction in two-way ANOVA, simple effects analyses were performed with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The results are presented as the means ± standard errors of the means (SEMs). All the statistical analyses and graphing were performed via SPSS 27 and GraphPad Prism 9. Statistical significance was set at * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), and *** (P < 0.001), and “ns” indicates no significance.

Scale bars, 10μm. Data represent mean ± SEM. "ns" indicates no significance by Mann-Whitney test. Fig S15. Working model of the study. LAL reprograms GPNMB+ microglia into a reparative state to drive white matter repair, achieved by hydrolyzing cholesteryl esters (CE). Following white matter injury, a large proportion of microglia are reprogrammed into GPNMB+ microglia, which dominate myelin debris engulfment but exhibit context-dependent capacity for lipid metabolic and myelin debris clearance; critically, their capacity to digest engulfed myelin debris is LAL-dependent. Notably, microglial LAL expression was higher in LPC-induced reparable white matter lesions than in L-NIO-induced irreparable lesions. Mechanistically, microglial LAL drives myelin debris clearance and remyelination through two essential pathways: 1) LAL-mediated cholesteryl ester hydrolysis enhances the production of 25-hydroxycholesterol (25-OHC), which in turn activates LXRs. This is supported by increased transcription of genes governing cholesterol efflux—Apoe, Abca1, and Abcg1. 2) LAL-mediated hydrolysis of cholesteryl esters into free cholesterol (FC) is a prerequisite for cholesterol efflux. Consistent with these mechanisms, LXR activation alone is insufficient to rescue the impairment of white matter repair induced by LAL deficiency. Fig S16. Western Blot images. Scale bars, 10μm. Data represent mean ± SEM. "ns" indicates no significance by Mann-Whitney test. Fig S15. Working model of the study. LAL reprograms GPNMB+ microglia into a reparative state to drive white matter repair, achieved by hydrolyzing cholesteryl esters (CE). Following white matter injury, a large proportion of microglia are reprogrammed into GPNMB+ microglia, which dominate myelin debris engulfment but exhibit context-dependent capacity for lipid metabolic and myelin debris clearance; critically, their capacity to digest engulfed myelin debris is LAL-dependent. Notably, microglial LAL expression was higher in LPC-induced reparable white matter lesions than in L-NIO-induced irreparable lesions. Mechanistically, microglial LAL drives myelin debris clearance and remyelination through two essential pathways: 1) LAL-mediated cholesteryl ester hydrolysis enhances the production of 25-hydroxycholesterol (25-OHC), which in turn activates LXRs. This is supported by increased transcription of genes governing cholesterol efflux—Apoe, Abca1, and Abcg1. 2) LAL-mediated hydrolysis of cholesteryl esters into free cholesterol (FC) is a prerequisite for cholesterol efflux. Consistent with these mechanisms, LXR activation alone is insufficient to rescue the impairment of white matter repair induced by LAL deficiency. Fig S16. Western Blot images.
