Metabolic and inflammatory burden modifies muscle cardiovascular association across aging cohorts within an intrinsic capacity framework
Authors: Changqing Dong, Shengmao Liu, Linlin Wu, Wuda Huoshen, Yuexin Zhu, Ping Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study evaluates the association between intrinsic capacity domains and incident cardiovascular disease across three nationally representative aging cohorts from the United States, the United Kingdom, and China. Harmonized data from HRS, ELSA, and CHARLS were analyzed using multivariable cause-specific Cox models with time-varying covariates where available. Among intrinsic capacity domains, muscle function showed the most consistent inverse association with cardiovascular risk across cohorts. Higher levels of systemic inflammatory and metabolic burden, including elevated C-reactive protein, diabetes, and non-HDL cholesterol, were associated with higher risk and with attenuation of the modeled muscle–cardiovascular association, with the strongest modification observed in CHARLS. A three-dimensional Metabolism–Inflammation–Muscle surface was used to visualize this interaction structure. These findings support muscle function as a cross-cohort marker of lower cardiovascular risk while highlighting its context-dependent magnitude under systemic stress. The proposed surface is descriptive and requires external validation before clinical application.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in older adults worldwide. While traditional risk prediction models focus on snapshot biomarkers, such as blood pressure, glucose, and lipid levels, these metrics may not fully capture the progressive decline in functional reserves that underpin systemic resilience in aging populations. Emerging evidence suggests that incorporating measures of intrinsic physiological capacity may enhance risk stratification and improve early detection of functional decompensation preceding overt cardiovascular events.

To address this limitation, the World Health Organization (WHO) proposed the construct of intrinsic capacity (IC)—defined as “the composite of all the physical and mental capacities an individual can draw upon.”1 This framework encompasses five core domains—locomotion, vitality, cognition, psychological well being, and sensory function2,3—and emphasizes a systems biology approach to functional aging. Among these, skeletal muscle function—which is the core of locomotion, is increasingly being recognized as a metabolically active organ that mediates glucose uptake, inflammatory regulation, and mitochondrial integrity.4,5,6,7 Sarcopenia undermines these pathways, weakening the body’s ability to buffer systemic metabolic stress and increasing vulnerability to cardiovascular damage, especially in individuals with diabetes or dyslipidemia.8,9,10

Despite growing interest in IC, critical gaps remain. Many studies have relied on aggregate IC scores, which may obscure domain-specific interactions with metabolic stressors such as C-reactive protein (CRP), non-HDL cholesterol (NHDL), and hyperglycemia. Additionally, cross-cultural variability in metabolic exposure and healthcare delivery has not been adequately addressed, which limits our understanding of how skeletal muscle-based resilience is modulated across diverse aging populations.

To fill these gaps, we analyzed harmonized longitudinal data from three nationally representative aging cohorts—the Health and Retirement Study (HRS, USA), the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA, UK), and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, China)—to examine associations between the five IC domains and incident CVD and to test whether systemic inflammatory and metabolic burden (CRP, NHDL, and diabetes) modifies these associations. We further present a metabolism-inflammation-muscle (MIM) framework as an associational visualization approach to communicate how the muscle-CVD association varies across levels of systemic stress, rather than as a mechanistic or clinical prediction model. Given its consistent cross-cohort association and modifiability, we highlight the muscle domain for interaction-focused analyses; corresponding interaction results for the other IC domains are provided in Table S2.

Follow-up windows were constrained by biomarker/medication availability and harmonization rules. Median follow-up was 8 years in HRS and ELSA (range, 1–8) and 7 years in CHARLS (range, 1–7), with incident CVD events occurring in 3,653 (24.2%) participants in HRS, 1,699 (19.0%) in ELSA, and 3,207 (22.6%) in CHARLS (Table 1). These cohort-specific event proportions reflect differences in the underlying risk profiles, mortality patterns, and calendar period/context, rather than a direct measure of model performance.

Model robustness and domain-specific IC effects

Multicollinearity was minimal across all primary models, with adjusted variance inflation factors (VIFs) ≤ 2.0 for covariates (Table S1), supporting stable estimation in the multivariable setting.

We next examined time-varying associations between the five IC domains and incident CVD, using multivariable cause-specific Cox models harmonized across cohorts (Table S2 and Figure 1). Among all IC domains, the muscle composite score (integrating mass, grip strength, and gait speed) showed the most consistent and strongest inverse associations with incident CVD across the three cohorts (Figure 1). Higher muscle scores were associated with significantly lower CVD risk in CHARLS (hazards ratio [HR] = 0.51; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.35–0.74), HRS (HR = 0.58; 95% CI = 0.44–0.75), and ELSA (HR = 0.54; 95% CI = 0.38–0.77) (Figure 1 and Table S2). The psychological domain was inversely associated with incident CVD in all cohorts, with stronger estimates in HRS/ELSA and a smaller magnitude in CHARLS (Figure 1 and Table S2). In contrast, the cognition and sensory domains demonstrated weaker and more heterogeneous associations across cohorts, with CIs often spanning the null (Figure 1 and Table S2). Collectively, these domain-resolved results indicate that the IC construct contains domains with substantially different cardiometabolic relevance and that the muscle domain is the most reproducible protective component across settings in this harmonized analysis.

Covariate effects in the primary models were generally consistent with prior epidemiology. Older age and current smoking were associated with elevated CVD risk in HRS and ELSA (Figure 1 and Table S2). Female sex was protective in HRS and ELSA but not in CHARLS after adjustment (Figure 1 and Table S2). Education gradients were present in some cohorts. Systemic stressor covariates (CRP categories, diabetes, and NHDL categories) showed cohort-specific magnitudes, consistent with heterogeneity in baseline burden and clinical management (Figure 1 and Table S2).

Effect modification by systemic inflammatory and metabolic stressors

We then tested whether systemic inflammatory and metabolic burden modifies IC-CVD associations. Interaction analyses between each IC domain and CRP, diabetes, and NHDL were performed with multiplicity control; the results are reported as FDR adjusted in Table S3. Because the muscle domain showed the most consistent main association across cohorts and has clear modifiability and clinical interpretability, we prioritized muscle-stressor interactions in the main narrative, while presenting the full domain × stressor interaction matrix (including non-muscle domains) in Table S3.

Across cohorts, we observed a coherent pattern: the inverse association between muscle function and incident CVD was attenuated under higher systemic stress. Specifically, interaction terms for muscle with inflammatory burden (CRP) and metabolic burden (diabetes and/or NHDL) were directionally consistent, with strongest statistical support in CHARLS and less precise estimates in HRS and ELSA (Figures 2D–2F, and Table S3). In ELSA, evidence for muscle × non-HDL interaction was weaker than in the other cohorts, whereas muscle × CRP and muscle × diabetes interactions were more consistently supported (Table S3). Non-linear “threshold” patterns visualized by the MIM surface

To communicate how muscle-based protection varies across graded systemic inflammatory and lipid burden, we constructed a 3D MIM surface, using CHARLS-derived coefficients (Figures 2A–2C). The surface maps estimated HRs over the observed combinations of muscle score, CRP, and NHDL. Importantly, the HR = 1 contour is presented as a descriptive landmark (a model-based null crossing within the CHARLS scale and covariate set) to visualize where the inverse muscle association becomes attenuated under higher stress; it is not proposed as a clinical cutoff.

Representative landmark combinations illustrating distinct regions of the modeled interaction surface are summarized in Table 2. Under relatively low inflammatory and metabolic burden (e.g., muscle score, 1.50; CRP, 1.0 mg/L; NHDL, 130 mg/dL), the estimated relative hazard is substantially below unity (HR ≈ 0.49; Table 2). As systemic burden increases, higher muscle scores are required to maintain relative hazard near unity, as illustrated by muscle score of 1.45, with CRP level at 3.0 mg/L and NHDL at 160 mg/dL (HR ≈ 0.995; Table 2). At higher CRP (e.g., 6.6 mg/L), even elevated muscle scores may no longer offset risk (HR > 1), and similarly, at high NHDL levels (e.g., 297 mg/dL), the protective gradient attenuates (Table 2). Under extreme combined burden (e.g., muscle score, 0.50; CRP, 8.0 mg/L; NHDL, 300 mg/dL), the model estimates markedly elevated relative hazard (HR ≈ 3.25; Table 2). Sensitivity analyses

For transparency, we provide complete multivariable Cox regression tables (HRs, 95% CIs, and p values) for each cohort in Table S2. A series of sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the main findings. First, replacing diabetes with continuous HbA1c as the metabolic stressor yielded consistent patterns (Table S4). Second, additionally adjusting for systolic blood pressure and kidney function (cystatin C, where available) did not materially alter the muscle association and generally preserved the direction of key interaction patterns (Table S5). Finally, further adjustment for healthcare management and sociodemographic factors likewise did not materially change the primary conclusions (Table S8).

Discussion

Summary of key findings

Using harmonized longitudinal data from three nationally representative aging cohorts (HRS, ELSA, and CHARLS), we found that the muscle domain—operationalized as a composite of muscle mass, grip strength, and gait speed—showed the most consistent association with lower incident CVD risk among the five WHO IC domains. Beyond main effects, systemic inflammatory and metabolic burden (CRP, diabetes, and NHDL) modified the muscle-CVD association, with the most pronounced and precise interaction patterns observed in CHARLS. To improve interpretability of effect modification, we further expressed the fitted interaction structure as a descriptive 3D surface, illustrating regions in which the apparent protective association of muscle function is attenuated under higher systemic stress. Collectively, these findings support a function-centered interpretation of IC in which muscle function tracks cardiovascular resilience in a context-dependent manner, rather than acting as a uniformly protective factor across all physiological states.

Muscle function within IC as a marker of functional reserve

The cross-cohort consistency of the muscle domain relative to other IC domains suggests that muscle function may capture a broad “functional reserve” signal that is closely tied to cardiometabolic vulnerability in later life. Unlike snapshot biomarkers that reflect a momentary physiological state, muscle performance integrates multiple upstream determinants accumulated over time—nutrition, activity patterns, inflammatory tone, endocrine signaling, comorbidity burden, and subclinical disease. This integrative property may help explain why the muscle domain produced the most stable association across cohorts with heterogeneous healthcare systems, risk-factor profiles, and baseline distributions.

From a mechanistic perspective, skeletal muscle releases myokines—such as irisin, FGF21, and IL-15, which inhibit inflammatory signaling (e.g., NF-κB and IL-6),11,12,13 improve insulin sensitivity,14 preserve endothelial and mitochondrial integrity,15,16,17 and buffer oxidative stress. Thus, muscle serves as a metabolic buffer that helps modulate systemic homeostasis.

Physiological reserve (e.g., peak expiratory flow and vascular elasticity) also demonstrated protective associations across cohorts, although the interaction signals with systemic inflammatory and metabolic stressors were generally weaker than those observed for the muscle domain. One plausible interpretation is that the cardiopulmonary capacity and vascular reserve may reflect a more downstream integrative state—closely tied to existing cardiometabolic load—whereas skeletal muscle function may behave as a more proximal, modifiable component of functional reserve that buffers systemic stress in earlier stages. This interpretation remains hypothesis generating because we did not directly measure mediators along the cardiopulmonary pathway, but it is consistent with the domain pattern observed across cohorts.

In contrast, the cognitive and sensory domains showed weaker and more heterogeneous associations across cohorts. This does not imply that cognition or sensory function are unimportant for cardiovascular health; rather, it may reflect differences in measurement instruments, ceiling/floor effects, cohort-specific selection, or the possibility that these domains influence CVD through pathways that are less well captured by the current covariate set and event definition.

Systemic stress as an effect modifier and the descriptive MIM surface

A central contribution of this work is the observation that the muscle-CVD association is not constant across levels of systemic stress. In all three cohorts, higher CRP and metabolic burden tended to coincide with attenuation of the muscle-associated risk reduction, with the strongest statistical support in CHARLS. Importantly, these interaction patterns are interpreted as effect modification of associations, not proof of biological mechanism. Nevertheless, the pattern is coherent with a resilience framing: when the inflammatory and lipid burden is low, higher muscle function aligns with substantially lower observed risk; as systemic burden increases, the same increment in muscle function corresponds to a smaller apparent risk difference.

This non-linear, saturable model aligns with established evidence of the detrimental effects of chronic inflammation (IL-6 and TNF-α), impaired insulin/IGF-1 signaling, and lipid toxicity in promoting sarcopenia through catabolic pathways such as FOXO, KLF15, and CD36.18,19,20,21,22,23

To communicate this context dependence in an interpretable way, we express the fitted interaction model as a descriptive 3D surface (muscle × CRP × NHDL) and summarize representative landmark combinations (Table 2). The HR ≈ 1 boundary is presented as a risk-interpretation landmark within the analyzed data, not as a clinical cutoff. The value of this visualization is not prediction but explanation: it allows readers to see, where in the joint exposure space, the muscle association appears robust versus attenuated. This approach also helps avoid over-interpretation of single interaction coefficients by presenting the combined implications of main effects and interaction terms across plausible ranges of stressors.

Because the surface is derived from fitted coefficients, it should be interpreted as a compact summary of model-implied associations. The intended use is conceptual and communicative—illustrating context-dependent vulnerability—rather than prescriptive (e.g., defining actionable thresholds). Any translation toward individualized risk prediction would require external validation and cohort-specific calibration.

Psychological capacity and contextual buffering

Psychological capacity also showed a protective association, being stronger in HRS/ELSA than in CHARLS. Psychological capacity—measured via depressive symptomatology—exerts independent effects on CVD risk, particularly in socially disconnected environments. Western cohorts showed stronger associations (HR = 0.72–0.74) than CHARLS (HR = 0.84), potentially reflecting differences in social buffering, community integration, and mental health infrastructure.24,25 These interpretations are hypothesis generating because the relevant mediators (e.g., access to mental health services, chronic stress burden, and social participation quality) are not uniformly measured across cohorts and, therefore, cannot be directly tested here.

Cross-cohort context and interpretation

Cross-cohort differences in metabolic management and social connectedness provide a useful descriptive context for interpreting heterogeneity in effect sizes and interaction strength. Differences in treatment coverage and control patterns may contribute to differences in the distribution and persistence of systemic stressors across cohorts, influencing where participants fall on the joint exposure surface. Adjusting for the available healthcare management and social indicators did not materially alter the main domain estimates, suggesting that the primary associations are not solely explained by these measured contextual factors; however, these variables remain incomplete proxies and do not eliminate the possibility of residual confounding by broader socioeconomic or behavioral determinants.

Implications

These findings have two main implications. First, within the WHO IC framework, muscle function appears to be the most consistent IC domain across diverse cohorts, supporting its use in research settings as a function-centered marker of resilience. Second, the observed effect modification suggests that the protective association of muscle function should not be assumed to be uniform across physiological contexts. In populations or subgroups with higher inflammatory and metabolic burden, muscle function may remain relevant but appear less strongly associated with incident CVD, emphasizing the need to consider systemic stressors when interpreting functional measures.

The descriptive 3D surface is offered as an interpretability aid that may help bridge functional aging research and cardiometabolic epidemiology by showing how functional capacity and systemic stress may jointly relate to observed risk. It can motivate future work on whether interventions that target both function (e.g., strength and mobility) and systemic burden (e.g., inflammation and dyslipidemia control) yield more consistent risk reduction than function-first approaches alone—an idea that must be tested prospectively.

Across three nationally representative aging cohorts, skeletal muscle function was consistently associated with lower incident CVD risk, underscoring its relevance within the WHO IC framework. This association appeared context dependent and attenuated under higher inflammatory and metabolic burden. The proposed MIM visualization offers a descriptive framework to illustrate how muscle function and systemic stress jointly relate to the observed risk patterns, without implying clinical thresholds or causal effects. External validation and prospective calibration will be necessary to determine whether these modeled interaction patterns translate into meaningful differences in the cumulative risk and whether function-centered stratification strategies add decision value across diverse populations and healthcare contexts.

Limitations of the study

This study is observational and cannot establish causal effects. Residual confounding remains possible, including by physical activity, diet quality, socioeconomic position, and medication adherence, which were incompletely measured or not harmonized across all waves. Reverse causation is also plausible, as subclinical CVD or multimorbidity may influence muscle function and inflammatory markers prior to the recorded event.

Measurement and harmonization constraints should be acknowledged. Biomarkers and medication indicators were collected at discrete waves with varying intervals across cohorts, and domain scores may be sensitive to cohort-specific instruments and scaling decisions. Although cardiovascular outcomes were harmonized, differences in ascertainment methods across settings may contribute to heterogeneity.

The 3D interaction surface is derived from fitted Cox models and is intended for descriptive interpretation of effect modification rather than clinical prediction. It does not represent a calibrated prediction model, and uncertainty across the full surface was not designed to support prescriptive thresholding.

Finally, multiple interaction tests were performed. Although multiplicity control was applied, secondary domain findings should be interpreted as exploratory, particularly where cross-cohort consistency is limited.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and cohorts

We used harmonized longitudinal data from three nationally representative aging cohorts: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS, United States, 2006–2014; waves 8–12),26 the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA, United Kingdom, 2004–2012; waves 2–6),27 and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, China, 2011–2018; waves 1–4).28

Human subjects and ethical approval

This study analyzed human participants from three nationally representative aging cohorts: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS, United States), the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA, United Kingdom), and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, China). All original cohort studies obtained written informed consent from participants at enrollment. The present study conducted secondary analyses of de-identified publicly available data and therefore did not require additional institutional ethical approval.

HRS was approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (HUM00061128).

ELSA was approved by the London Multicentre Research Ethics Committee (MREC/01/2/91).

CHARLS was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015).

Participant characteristics and eligibility

Participants were community-dwelling adults aged ≥50 years. For the incident CVD survival analyses, participants with a history of cardiovascular disease at baseline were excluded, yielding analytical samples of 15,098 (HRS), 8,942 (ELSA), and 14,221 (CHARLS). Descriptive analyses of cardiometabolic management and social factors used all participants with available baseline data, irrespective of eligibility for the incident CVD survival analyses.

Sex was available in all three cohorts and was included as a covariate in all multivariable models. Associations with sex are reported in the Results and Figure 1. Race, ethnicity, ancestry, and gender identity were not available in harmonized form across all three cohorts and were therefore not included in the cross-cohort analyses; this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Method details

Construction of intrinsic capacity (IC) domain scores

To operationalize the WHO intrinsic capacity (IC) framework cross-nationally, we constructed five standardized IC domains using harmonized and consistently measured variables across cohorts. Domain scores were normalized to a 0–2 scale using cohort-specific percentile mapping, with higher values indicating better capacity.

Muscle composite score (0–2)

To capture muscle status comprehensively, we constructed a multidimensional muscle composite score combining structure, strength, and performance, aligned with consensus sarcopenia-related definitions (EWGSOP, AWGS).4,29,30 The score incorporated.

Muscle mass

Estimated appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) normalized by height2 using sex-specific anthropometric formulas.

Muscle strength

Average handgrip strength (left and right).

Physical performance

2.5-meter walking speed.

Each component was z-standardized; components were summed to form a latent muscle factor score and then linearly transformed to a 0–2 scale.

Physiological reserve score (0–2)

This domain reflected cardiopulmonary capacity and vascular aging by integrating.

Estimated pulse wave velocity (ePWV)

Computed from age and blood pressure using validated formulae.31,32

Peak expiratory flow (PEF) ratio

Observed/predicted PEF adjusted by age, sex, and height.33,34

Z-scores for ePWV (reverse-coded so higher indicates better reserve) and the PEF ratio were averaged and rescaled to 0–2.

Cognitive, sensory, and psychological capacity scores (each 0–2)

Cognition

Combined performance on immediate and delayed word recall, time orientation, and serial 7s subtraction.

Sensory function

Summed self-reported difficulties in vision, hearing, and reading.

Psychological capacity

Assessed using abbreviated CES-D instruments with established validity in older populations.35,36 CES-D items (8-item in HRS/ELSA; 10-item in CHARLS) were harmonized into five severity levels and rescaled to 0–2 to reflect depressive symptom burden (higher score indicating better psychological capacity/lower symptom burden as defined in the harmonization).

Outcome definition

Cardiovascular outcome

The primary outcome was incident CVD, defined as the first occurrence of myocardial infarction, angina, stroke, or cardiovascular death during follow-up. Events were identified using self-reported physician diagnoses and/or death registry information where available. Follow-up continued until the earliest occurrence of incident CVD, non-CVD death, loss to follow-up, or administrative end of follow-up.

Covariates

All primary models adjusted for harmonized covariates:

Demographics: age (continuous), sex, education (harmonized as <high school, high school, ≥college);

Behavioral factors: current smoking (yes/no), alcohol intake (≥3 times/week);

Metabolic/inflammatory stressors: diabetes status (self-reported), C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/L), and non-HDL cholesterol (mg/dL).

HbA1c (%) was used only in substitution sensitivity analyses and was not included alongside diabetes in the primary models to preserve cross-cohort comparability and avoid redundancy.

Handling of missing data

Missing data in IC domains and covariates were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), generating 10 imputed datasets per cohort. Continuous variables were imputed using linear regression models, and ordered categorical variables were imputed using ordinal logistic models. Blood biomarkers were not imputed to avoid bias from potentially non-random missingness. Model estimates were pooled across imputations using Rubin’s rules.

Construction and visualization of 3D interaction surfaces

Purpose and model inputs

To visualize how systemic inflammatory and lipid burden may modify the association between muscle function and incident CVD, we expressed the fitted interaction structure as a descriptive 3D surface over muscle score (M), C-reactive protein (CRP), and non-HDL cholesterol (non-HDL). For surface construction, CRP and non-HDL were parameterized on the natural-log scale to reduce skewness and to facilitate visualization of non-linear scaling. Coefficients were taken from the cohort-specific interaction model (primary surface shown for CHARLS; corresponding surfaces for HRS and ELSA are shown in Figures S1 and S2). Importantly, this surface parameterization is used only for visualization of modeled effect modification; the primary analyses and the transparent full-model tables (Table S2) use the prespecified main-model covariate definitions. The surface is intended for interpretability and communication, not for clinical prediction or prescriptive thresholding.

Model-implied relative hazard ratio

Let η (M, CRP, non-HDL) denote the Cox linear predictor component relevant to the surface, including main effects of ln (CRP) and ln (non-HDL) and their interactions with M. We computed a model-implied relative hazard ratio over a grid of (M, CRP, non-HDL) values and normalized it to a prespecified reference profile (M0, CRP0, non-HDL0):

HRrel (M, CRP, non-HDL) = exp [η (M, CRP, non-HDL) −η (M0, CRP0, non-HDL0)].

The reference profile was prespecified for normalization as (M0, CRP0, non-HDL0) = (1.45,3 mg/L,160 mg/dL); by construction, HRrel = 1 at this profile, and it is used only for visualization (not interpreted as a clinical cutoff).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Overview of analytical strategy

IC domain scores were treated as time-varying at each cohort wave when available, aligning functional measures with subsequent risk windows. Analyses were conducted separately by cohort and then interpreted comparatively.

Descriptive statistics

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD when approximately normally distributed and as median (IQR) when skewed. Categorical variables are presented as n (%). In this study, n denotes the number of human participants included in each cohort-specific analysis. For Figure 3, percentages were calculated using available-case denominators, which vary according to data availability for each variable. Error bars in Figure 3 represent 95% confidence intervals based on binomial proportion estimates.

Multicollinearity assessment

Multicollinearity among IC domains and covariates was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Results are reported in Table S1.

Time-varying Cox proportional hazards models

We applied time-varying cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals for associations between intrinsic capacity domains and incident cardiovascular disease. Participants were followed from baseline until the earliest occurrence of incident CVD, non-CVD death, loss to follow-up, or administrative end of follow-up. Events other than incident CVD were treated as censoring; thus, the Cox models estimated cause-specific hazards, which are appropriate when the primary objective is etiologic or risk-factor inference rather than direct modeling of cumulative incidence.37 Exact p values are reported in the figures, Results, and Table S2.

Interaction analyses

Multiplicative interaction terms were evaluated for each intrinsic capacity domain with CRP, diabetes, and non-HDL cholesterol. To minimize multicollinearity and facilitate interpretation, interaction terms were tested in separate models. False discovery rate adjustment was applied to the interaction screening matrix. A full domain-by-stressor interaction screening matrix is reported with false discovery rate control in Table S3.

Sensitivity analyses

HbA1c substitution

Diabetes was replaced with HbA1c (continuous) as the metabolic stressor to assess robustness of findings; results are in Table S4.

Additional adjustment for systolic blood pressure and kidney function

Models were further adjusted for systolic blood pressure and cystatin C (where available); results are in Table S5.

Healthcare management and social context

Metabolic management indicators and social isolation indicators were summarized descriptively (treatment/control status by cohort) in Tables S6 and S7, respectively. Descriptive analyses of cardiometabolic management and social factors were conducted using all participants with available baseline data, irrespective of eligibility for the incident CVD survival analyses. Models additionally adjusting for healthcare management and sociodemographic factors are reported in Table S8.

Software

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.2). Figures were generated in R using standard plotting packages.
