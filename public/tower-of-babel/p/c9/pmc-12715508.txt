codonGPT: reinforcement learning on a generative language model enables scalable mRNA design
Authors: Binita Rajbanshi, Anuj Guruacharya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReinforcement learning (RL) has become a powerful tool for design tasks in engineering. However, in biology, the combination of RL with language models has not been explored. Moreover, emerging generative language models for biology focus on DNA, non-coding RNA, or proteins. This ignores information hidden in coding messenger RNA (mRNA). Even though non-generative models for mRNA exist, the absence of generative models hinders scalable mRNA design for therapeutics, synthetic biology, and protein engineering. To address this, first, we built codonGPT, the first generative language model trained exclusively on 338 417 mRNA sequences of model organisms. To constrain synonymous sequences unique to mRNA, we introduce a novel inference-time masking method, enabling us to frame mRNA design as a constrained language modeling task. Second, we demonstrate a robust method for mRNA optimization with two example genes HLA-A and ACTB, by implementing RL on codonGPT with multiple biological constraints such as expression, stability, and GC-content. Our methods can be applied for optimization of widely used reporter genes such as GFP, beta-lactamase, and erythropoietin.

Introduction

Despite the central role that protein-coding messenger RNA (mRNA) sequences (codon sequences) play in practical applications such as mRNA therapeutics [1] and protein manufacturing [2], there is still a lack of a strong open-source foundational language modeling approach for codons. Recent advances in large language models have shown promise in modeling biological sequences [3–5], including proteins [6–8], non-coding RNA [9–11], and DNA [12, 13], yet a generative foundation model for coding mRNA doesn’t exist. Coding mRNA sequences have a unique latent structure, redundancy, and organism-specific bias embedded within codon usage [14–18], which necessitates a generative language model specific to protein-coding mRNA sequences.

There are astronomically many possible mRNAs that can encode the same amino acid sequence; e.g. a typical 300-amino-acid protein may have on the order of 10 100 different synonymous codon combinations. This makes its selection a combinatorics optimization problem, esp. in cross-host gene selection, in molecular research, in industrial protein engineering, and in mRNA therapeutics design. Choosing which mRNA sequence among the many possibilities to synthesize for different host systems is a recurring challenge in protein synthesis [19, 20], which has mostly been solved by traditional ad-hoc methods or classical optimization methods [1, 21–28]. Commonly used tools include GeneOptimizer® by Thermo Fisher [29] and GenSmart® by Genscript (Patent Number: WO2020024917A1) [30]. Such widely used commercial services are designed for fast, turnkey expressions with proprietary and closed-source multi-parameter scoring; they typically return one or a few designs. They do not expose objective weights or intermediate scores, so they lack inherent user customization capabilities or lack a clear framework for extension to other optimization applications besides cross-host optimization. In contrast, in this study, we provide a framework that outputs a distribution of designs and explicit, user-tunable objectives that can be fine-tuned for human therapeutics optimization.

In this study, we approach these two challenges through two unique ways. First, we build a deep language model trained on mRNA sequences of common expression systems such as humans, Caenorhabditis elegans, Mus musculus, Cricetulus griseus, Danio rerio, and Drosophila melanogaster. Second, instead of fine-tuning it using self-supervision of another large set of carefully selected mRNA sequences [31, 32], we use reinforcement learning (RL) to fine-tune it to our user requirements. We show that this method helps achieve a modular, scalable, and customizable method of designing and optimizing mRNA sequences. Our work thus introduces two novel methods.

First, we introduce codonGPT, a codon-native generative transformer language model. codonGPT is trained on a GPT-2 architecture from scratch using protein-coding nucleotide sequences, using a vocabulary of nucleotide triplets. During inference, we applied pre-softmax logit masking to constrain generation to only those codons that preserve the original protein sequence. We evaluated codonGPT by comparing its generated sequences to native codon usage patterns of housekeeping genes (HKG) [31] and an in-depth analysis of two genes of variable functions: Human Leukocyte Antigen A (HLA-A) [32] and Actin Beta (ACTB) [33]. Our results for the first time show that the model can produce biologically plausible codon sequences while maintaining synonymous fidelity without requiring explicit downstream tuning. This method can be used for optimization of other genes such as GFP, beta-lactamase, and erythropoietin that are widely used as reporter systems in microscopy.

Second, we then demonstrated that through the use of RL on a biological generative language model like codonGPT, we can perform a customizable way of codon optimization (Fig. 1). This is the first time RL on such models is being used for this task. For most therapeutic and synthetic gene design tasks, a generic optimization model that works for most proteins is not enough. Rather for such high value applications that rely on a single focused protein, we want a high-performing model for that single protein that a user can customize and optimize as they desire. Usually expression, stability, or a mix of various biological parameters must be optimized under multiple biological constraints. Thus, to further align codonGPT-generated sequences with expression-optimized and stability-optimized properties, we experimented with a fine-tuned version of codonGPT via RL on a protein-specific codon optimization task using customized reward variables. This design allowed the model to adapt to sequence-specific trade-offs such as codon usage patterns, local GC content, and RNA structural constraints that may differ significantly between proteins. Using two proteins, HLA-A and ACTB, as examples, we showed that fine-tuning codonGPT via RL generates such optimized codon sequences.

System design of biologically constrained codon sequence generation and optimization. Our foundational scale model, codonGPT, builds on a base untrained GPT-2 model, and adapts it for biology using unsupervised codon-level training with a 64-codon tokenizer with three special tokens. To ensure that only biologically valid codon sequences that match the protein translation of the initial codons are created, a constraint is added during inference. During inference, logit masking is performed before the softmax layer to generate synonymly-constrained codon sequence. The inherent generative ability of codonGPT makes it amenable to RL by generating codons as it learns the right behavior. In our study, the results of codonGPT are refined by a RL mechanism based on biological metrics such as CAI, GC content, ΔG, entropy, and trinucleotide repeats. The weights of the reward for these constraints can be changed as per protein input requirements or application requirements such as manufacturing for therapeutics or reporter genes for research grade microscopy. Learning from this customized reward mechanism, codonGPT creates an optimized codon sequence that enhances either translational efficiency or stability, without supervised labels. This removes the necessity to fine-tune codonGPT on a large corpus of high-quality data, which can be painful to obtain and methodologically out of reach for an average biological scientist to spend time training the model on.

Materials and methods

Data

To construct the training data, we extracted 338 417 protein-coding mRNA sequences (CDS) sequences: 123 845 of humans, 31 865 sequences of Caenorhabditis elegans, 66 558 sequences of Mus musculus, 28 327 sequences of Cricetulus griseus (strain, chok1gshd), 52 089 sequences of Danio rerio, 30 802 sequences of D. melanogaster, and 4931 sequences of Escherichia coli (strain, 110 957), totaling 338 417 mRNA sequences, which were acquired from Ensembl Release 64 [34], and segmented each into triplets of nucleotides. We included all nuclear protein-coding sequences and excluded mitochondrial transcripts. Sequences are used as they are annotated in Ensembl, and no extra filtering has been applied. Training is done using nucleotide data only. Organism-specific genetic codes are used only for downstream analyses to ensure correct translation of CDS to proteins. We used a custom tokenizer designed for the biological codon vocabulary, consisting of 64 standard codons and three special tokens: [PAD], [BOS], and [EOS]. Each sequence was tokenized as a flat sequence of codons, without any alignment to amino acid sequences. One amino acid codon represents one token. Sequences exceeding 1024 tokens were truncated to this length, while shorter sequences were padded with a designated [PAD] token to achieve a uniform length of 1024 tokens. The resulting fixed-length codon sequences formed the input for model training.

Model architecture, vocabulary, and tokenization

A generative pre-trained transformer (GPT-2) architecture was employed as the foundational language model. The model was constructed from scratch using the Hugging Face transformers library (version 4.47.1) [35]. This specific iteration of the GPT-2 model comprised a total of 12 transformer blocks. Each block maintained an internal hidden size of d = 64, which also served as the dimensionality for token and positional embeddings.

Within each transformer block, a multi-head self-attention mechanism was implemented with eight attention heads. This configuration allows the model to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces at different positions. Following the self-attention layer, a position-wise feedforward network (FFN) was applied. This FFN consisted of two linear transformations with a 4× expansion factor, meaning the hidden layer within the FFN expanded to 4 × d model = 4 × 64 = 256 dimensions before projecting back to d model. The GELU (Gaussian Error Linear Unit) activation function was applied after the first linear transformation in the FFN. To mitigate overfitting, dropout (with a probability P = .1) was applied after both the attention and FFN sub-layers. The model was configured to process input sequences with a maximum context window size of 1024 tokens. The total number of trainable parameters in this model was ~686 115.

The model operated with a specialized vocabulary size of 67 unique tokens. Prior to model input, raw data was tokenized into these specific tokens. During preprocessing, BOS (Beginning-of-Sentence) and EOS (End-of-Sentence) tokens were appended, and sequences were padded to the defined 1024-token context window size to ensure uniform input dimensions for batch processing.

Training details

The model was trained on a dataset comprising 338 417 sequences. Training was conducted over five epochs, with a batch size of eight samples per device. The optimization algorithm used was AdamW, a variant of Adam that incorporates weight decay regularization. The initial learning rate was set to . No explicit weight decay () or learning rate warmup steps () were applied, meaning the learning rate remained constant throughout training. All model training was performed on CUDA-enabled 3× T4 GPU, with 64 GB of memory.

For the RL fine-tuning per protein, training was performed using a single T4 GPU. Both ACTB and HLA-A proteins were trained for 1000 update steps, with peak GPU memory of 9 GB. Once trained, generation is inference-only, which can be performed in CPU-only mode and was completed within 12 min for 100 sequences generated; the RL training cost is a one-time per-protein expense.

Results

Biological structure emerges from unsupervised codon-level training of codonGPT

We first evaluated whether codonGPT, trained in a fully unsupervised manner, could learn biologically meaningful representations of codon sequences. The model was trained as a next-token predictor at the codon level, with no explicit supervision regarding amino acid identity, gene structure, or expression. We analyzed both its predictive performance and the structure of its learned embedding space to assess the extent to which codonGPT internalized biological signals.

We investigated whether codonGPT learns to group codons that encode the same amino acid by analyzing its internal representation space. After training, we extracted the 64 codon embeddings from the model’s input layer, and we projected the 64 codon embeddings using t-SNE (n = 2, perplexity = 10) (Fig. 2A). Codons encoding the same amino acid tended to cluster, though segregation was incomplete. Quantitatively, the Silhouette Score (0.327) and Davies–Bouldin Index (2.196) indicated moderate clustering quality. These findings suggest that unsupervised training captures codon–amino acid relationships in the embedding space. As an alternative, we also computed k-means directly on the codon embeddings to obtain cluster labels and then performed a two-dimensional projection with t-SNE (Supplementary Fig. S2), which revealed similar characteristics.

To determine whether this visual structure reflected a statistically meaningful pattern, we computed the average pairwise cosine similarity between codons within each synonymous group and compared it to the average similarity of non-synonymous codon pairs. The comparisons showed a statistically significant difference (t-statistic = 20.091, P < .05). Assumptions were assessed with Shapiro–Wilk normality tests (synonymous: W = 0.973, P < .05; non-synonymous: W = 0.979, P < .05) and Levene’s test for equal variances (F = 8.299, P < .05), motivating the unequal-variance Welch’s t-test. The average cosine similarity between synonymous codons (0.30 ± 0.12) was higher than between non-synonymous codon pairs (0.03 ± 0.10) (Fig. 2B). The significantly high difference between the cosine similarities between these two groups demonstrates that synonymous codons are consistently embedded more closely together than non-synonymous codons. This pattern emerged solely from the model’s unsupervised training objective, without any explicit access to amino acid labels or translation supervision. The proximity of synonymous codons in the embedding space suggests that codonGPT internalizes semantic relationships embedded in the structure of protein-coding sequences. Rather than relying solely on surface-level codon frequencies, the model appears to develop a deeper representation of codon meaning based on the broader sequence context in which codons appear. Importantly, the synonymous relationships captured in the embedding space mirror the structure of the genetic code, despite the model having no direct knowledge of amino acid translation or protein function.

These results demonstrate that codonGPT learns biologically meaningful structure at the level of codon synonymy, and that this structure is reflected both qualitatively by tSNE and quantitatively by cosine similarity in its learned representation space. The emergent clustering of synonymous codons highlights the model’s potential as a foundation for biologically constrained generation, synonym-aware optimization, and tasks requiring semantic understanding of codon usage.

To determine whether codonGPT embeddings reflect lower-level nucleotide composition, particularly GC content, which plays a key role in genome structure and gene regulation, we performed PCA on the embeddings of the model. The scree plot and cumulative variance are provided in Supplementary Fig. S3. Given that PC1 and PC2 capture only ∼19% of variance (12.66% and 6.30%), differing layouts in t-SNE and PCA are expected, as the two methods capture complementary aspects of the data, with PCA emphasizing global variance and t-SNE emphasizing local neighborhood structure.

Using the PCA components of the codon embeddings, we evaluated its GC compositional bias. We colored the codon PCA space by GC percentage and observed a smooth gradient along one of the principal axes (Fig. 2C). GC-rich codons clustered at one end of the space, while AT-rich codons were positioned at the opposite end. The continuity of this gradient suggests that compositional properties are embedded directly into the codon representations, likely due to the model’s sensitivity to local sequence features during training.

Using the PCA components of the codon embeddings, we also evaluated whether the codon embeddings align with codon-usage preferences relevant to expression. We colored the PCA of the 64 codon embeddings by the per-codon relative adaptiveness (w(c)), derived from a human high-expression [18]. Codons with a high w(c) occupy a compact region of the space, whereas low-w(c) codons are more dispersed (Fig. Fig. 2D 2). This spatial organization indicates that codonGPT captures organism-specific codon preferences without supervision, even though it never saw expression data or codon-usage labels. Because these preferences are reference-dependent, we replicated the coloring with Dorsophila melanogaster (Supplementary Fig. S4) and Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Supplementary Fig. S5) per-codon relative adaptiveness values [18] and observed a similarly consistent direction.

Together, these results indicate that codonGPT, trained without any explicit biological supervision, learns codon-level representations that encode translational relationships, compositional properties, and expression-related usage biases. This embedding geometry provides a strong foundation for downstream applications in synonymous codon selection, constrained decoding, and biologically informed optimization.

Inference-time synonymous logit masking of codonGPT ensures biologically faithful codon generation

We then enhanced our codon-level language model with inference-time, pre-softmax, synonymous logit masking. This approach constrains the model such that at each generation step, it selects only from codons that correspond to the correct amino acid, ensuring the biological meaning of the sequence is preserved. We evaluated this approach by comparing a set of native codon sequences of 100 protein-coding HKG against those generated by our model for those same genes. The 100 HKG (provided in Supplementary Table S1) were randomly selected from a list of HKG formulated by Eisenberg and Levanon [31]. The model-generated HKG sequences were standardized with the same hyperparameters of temperature, top-k, and top-p. Our evaluation focused on both fidelity to the target protein and the biological plausibility of the generated codon sequences.

The model preserved the target protein sequences with 100% accuracy in all 100 cases. This confirms that the pre-softmax logit masking mechanism effectively enforces the amino acid constraint throughout the entire decoding process. Since the model is restricted to generating only synonymous codons for each position, it ensures that the decoded protein is identical to the intended sequence. This guaranteed preservation is critical for biological applications such as codon optimization, gene synthesis, and therapeutic mRNA design, where maintaining the protein output is non-negotiable. Unlike unconstrained generation, which can result in mistranslation errors, our masking strategy fully eliminates this risk at inference time.

For each gene, we compare the generated codon sequence to its native counterpart using several sequence-level and codon-level similarity metrics (Fig. 3).

The normalized codon Hamming distance between native and model-generated sequences had a median of 0.608, highlighting that the model introduces substantial codon-level variation (Fig. 3A). Importantly, this variation was completely synonymous, with the number of synonymous substitutions also centered around a median of 225 (Q1 to Q3 range of 150 to 308.75), ensuring the translated amino acid sequence of the input genetic codon sequence unchanged (Fig. 3D).

Despite the sequence diversity, codon-level overlap remained moderate, with a median codon-level identity (match rate) of 39.2%, indicating partial conservation of specific codons (Fig. 3B). Match rate is defined as the fraction of codon positions that are identical between sequence pairs (equal-length, in-frame). More critically, the model preserved global codon usage trends per gene. Cosine similarity between codon frequency vectors of native and generated sequences yielded a tightly distributed similarity profile (median = 0.854, Q1 to Q3 range of 0.799 to 0.925) (Fig. 3C), suggesting that codonGPT effectively learns gene-specific codon biases.

We next examined whether codonGPT-generated sequences retain key biological correlations observed in native codon sequences of the HKG. Specifically, we evaluated the relationship between GC content and CAI, two metrics known to be co-regulated in many human genes. Despite never explicitly optimizing for these metrics during training, the model produced codon sequences with highly realistic profiles. In both model-generated and native sequences (Fig. 3E and F), we observed a strong positive correlation between GC content and CAI (Pearson’s r = 0.63 for model, Pearson’s r = 0.68 for native; P < 1 × 10−6 for both), suggesting that the model internalizes and reproduces this underlying biological relationship. To further quantify differences, we compared overall CAI and GC content between model generated, native sequences, and a synonymous control sequence set that we generated.

To generate synonymous control sequences, we implemented a codon‐level randomization procedure that preserves the amino acid sequence while introducing a defined number of synonymous substitutions. For each native coding sequence, we first determined the codon‐level Hamming distance between the model‐generated sequence and the native sequence. An equivalent number of positions was then selected at random from among codons with available synonymous alternatives, excluding the initiation codon to preserve translational fidelity. At each selected position, the native codon was replaced with a synonymous codon chosen uniformly from the standard genetic code. This approach ensured that the synonymous controls matched the model–native comparisons in overall codon substitution load while maintaining the identical protein sequence. These synonymous controls therefore serve as appropriate baselines for evaluating whether model‐designed codon choices confer distinct properties beyond random synonymous variation.

After confirming that the synonymous control had a similar distribution of Hamming distance (Pearson’s r = 1, P < .05) (Supplementary Fig. S6) and a similar distribution of synonymous substitutions (Pearson’s r = 1, P < .05) (Supplementary Fig. S7) as our codonGPT model, we compared the control’s codon usage similarity to the model’s codon usage similarity and found them to be significantly different (Mann-Whitney U test, P < .05) (Supplementary Fig. S8). In other words, the model tends to generate sequences with higher codon usage similarity to the native sequences than random chance, showing that the model’s outputs are not an artifact of random chance.

We compared the GC and CAI distributions between the controls, codonGPT model generated, and the native HKG sequences. Unlike the positive GC–CAI trend for model-generated sequences (Pearson’s r = 0.63, P < .05) (Fig. 3E) and the positive GC–CAI trend for the native sequences (Pearson’s r = 0.68, P < .05) (Fig. 3F), the control has weakly negative GC–CAI trend (Pearson’s r = −0.23, P = .0209) (Fig. 3G), confirming the effect is not a Hamming distance artifact. In overlaid scatter plots (Fig. 3H) for these three groups, we see that the model group and the native group closely follow each other, while the control group does not.

We then evaluated the individual CAI (Fig. 3I) and individual GC content (Fig. 3J) between the three groups. We found that the CAI of the model group was significantly higher than the control group (mean = 48% for model versus 38% for control; paired t-test, P < .05). We also found a statistically significant but small increase of 0.03 in CAI between the model and native group (mean = 48% for model versus 45% for native; paired t-test, P = .016). This small increase was present even though the model has not been fine-tuned on a high CAI dataset, nor has RL fine-tuning been applied. Similarly, GC content was not significantly different between the model and the native sets (mean = 53% for both model-generated and native; paired t-test, P = .72). However, the GC content between model and the control sets was significantly different (mean = 53% for model versus 50% for control, paired t-test, P < .05). This indicates that sequences generated from codonGPT preserve the native codon usage patterns.

These results are especially noteworthy because the model operates under hard constraints; it must choose from a subset of valid codons per amino acid, yet it still tends to favor codons that match human codon usage biases. This suggests that the model has implicitly learned organism-specific patterns of synonymous codon usage. Together, these results show that codonGPT balances codon-level novelty with biologically constrained fidelity, generating synonymous sequences that reflect endogenous usage patterns without altering protein translation.

Emergence of codon constraints across functionally distinct genes

To investigate whether codonGPT internalizes biologically meaningful constraints on synonymous codon usage, we performed a focused analysis of model-generated codon sequences for two structurally and functionally distinct human genes: a major histocompatibility complex class I gene (HLA-A) and β-actin, a HKG (ACTB). These genes were selected to represent distinct evolutionary pressures: immune-domain modularity in HLA-A and constitutive expression stability in ACTB. For each gene, we generated 100 codon candidates using synonym-constrained decoding, where the amino acid sequence is fixed and each codon position is sampled only from synonymous alternatives. These candidates were generated using the base model without top-k filtering. The temperature hyperparameter was manually tuned per gene (1.5 for HLA-A and 1.0 for ACTB) to achieve comparable candidate diversity given gene-specific constraints.

We first examined positional entropy across codon sites to evaluate per-position codon variability. ACTB exhibited higher average positional entropy (mean = 1.55) compared to HLA-A (mean = 1.29), reflecting gene-specific differences in synonymous codon flexibility (Fig. 4A). The distribution of entropy values showed moderate variability in both genes (SD = 0.64 for HLA-A, 0.67 for ACTB) (Fig. 4B). Normalized Hamming distances between generated sequences revealed non-trivial sequence-level diversity (mean = 0.49, SD = 0.05 for 4950 pairs of HLA-A sequences; mean = 0.61, SD = 0.03 for 4950 pairs of ACTB sequences) (Fig. 4C), confirming that codonGPT generates distinct synonymous variants rather than minor variations around a single motif. We also explored RNA secondary structure stability (ΔG) and repeat codon frequencies to assess structural and translational constraints. No strong patterns were observed between these two features (Fig. 4D), suggesting they are not explicitly learned or prioritized by the model in the unsupervised setting. Lastly, generated candidate sequences were well within biologically realistic bounds for CAI (0.4 to 0.55 for HLA-A and 0.38 to 0.55 for ACTB) and for GC (0.52 to 0.62 for HLA-A and 0.44 to 0.57 for ACTB) (Fig. 4E), further demonstrating that codonGPT maintains translational and compositional plausibility.

Protein-specific reinforcement fine-tuning of codonGPT optimizes a multi-objective reward function

To use codonGPT on a downstream task of codon optimization, we fine-tuned codonGPT using protein-specific RL feedback for two representative proteins: HLA-A and ACTB. We constructed a reward function that combines five different biological components: CAI, which reflects translational efficiency in the host organism; GC content, which impacts mRNA stability and synthesis; RNA folding stability (ΔG score), a proxy for the structural integrity of the transcript; codon entropy, which measures synonymous codon diversity; and the number of codon repeats. These metrics were calculated for each training step and for each candidate generated during inference.

We experimented with alternative reward formulations, including different GC shaping functions (e.g. Gaussian versus raised-cosine), local sliding-window GC terms, and various relative weights of reward components. Some of these exploratory schemes are shown in Supplementary Figs S9–S13. While certain runs yielded sequences with strong individual metrics, overall training was unstable or inconsistent, motivating the adoption of the final balanced reward design described in the main text. For optimization to balance GC content that does not cross 70%, a high CAI, a balanced stability, diversity, and a low repeat, we chose a reward for ACTB with weights of 2 for CAI, 0.2 for GC penalty, 0.05 for ΔG score, 0.01 for entropy score, and 0.01 for repeat penalty. For HLA-A, we chose a reward with weights of 1.6 for CAI, 0.1 for GC penalty, 0.05 for ΔG score, 0.05 for entropy score, and 0.02 for repeat penalty.

To evaluate the training stability and convergence behavior of our RL framework, we analyzed and corrected the weights of our reward based on the reward trajectory plot (Fig. 5). We observed that the raw reward sometimes fell into a valley, which later the model was able to extricate out of and then finally plateauing, indicating convergence to a locally optimal sequence. This consistent plateau across runs confirmed that the model was able to reliably optimize for the multi-objective reward of this shape.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that RL on a generative language model can produce codon sequences that are not only biologically interpretable and thermodynamically stable but also compositionally balanced, customizable, and robust across multiple biological objectives.

Discussion

In this work, we make two significant contributions: the first one being the building of a generative language model for protein-coding mRNA sequences and the second one being the use of RL to guide the optimization of such sequences.

The interface of RNA and proteins has recently been explored by multiple deep learning approaches such as RNNs [39, 40], VAEs [41], and GANs [42, 43]. Even though such approaches are neural network based, they don’t model genetic sequences as languages.

From a language model perspective, BERT [44] and GPT [45] have emerged as foundational language models. Several BERT-based approaches for codons have recently emerged for codon sequence understanding, such as two independent models by Li et al. [46] and by Ren et al. [47], both named codonBERT. However, BERT’s bidirectional nature and masked pretraining objective [43] make it less suited for tasks that require generative modeling. Typically, such models rely on curated datasets for task-specific fine-tuning of a base model to optimize a fixed set of parameters across all proteins. On the other hand, generative architectures, such as GPT, if modified, are able to generate full biologically valid sequences and have an inherent capability of being fine-tuned by reinforcement using any custom set of reward metrics a user might need to change as per protein. However, to date, no GPT-based model has been reported for codon-level sequence generation. While BERT-family tools like codonBERT could pursue similar endpoints (e.g. optimizing codon usage bias) through dataset-specific fine-tuning, our approach maintains a metric-agnostic pretrained codonGPT and employs RL with a user-tunable, multi-objective reward framework, allowing flexible optimization across diverse biological metrics (CAI, GC content, ΔG, repeat penalty, etc.) without task-specific data collection.

From an RL perspective, such techniques in artificial intelligence have been applied for biological sequence design of DNA [48], non-coding RNA [49], and proteins [50–52]. However, they have not been extended to protein-coding mRNA sequence design, which uses a triplet of nucleotides instead of single amino acids as its fundamental vocabulary and poses its own unique challenges in maintaining synonymous coding during RL optimization.

From a user perspective, while commercial tools are excellent in providing easy-to-use graphical, fast, and turnkey designs for routine expression, they are not intended for therapeutic or highly customizable optimization. Our codonGPT-RL framework provides objectives and weights that are transparent and user-tunable, runs are reproducible, and the method supports multi-objective optimization. Additionally, commercial codon-optimization tools’ closed-source, software-as-a-service business model limits reproducibility and auditability. Objective functions and weightings are not publicly specified, intermediate scores are not usually exposed, and bulk programmatic evaluation is typically restricted. Use often requires uploading sequences to third-party infrastructure, which may be incompatible with some data-governance requirements. These services generally return a small number of optimized sequences. In contrast, our codonGPT-RL algorithms and methods are openly testable, with explicit, user-tunable rewards, and can generate diverse panels matched to user-defined, protein-specific objectives.

This study is the first implementation of a generative architecture for codon-level biological languages. Without alignment to protein sequences, the model captures codon usage bias, synonymous codon clustering, and long-range dependencies, demonstrating its ability to learn biologically meaningful patterns from raw sequence data. While CAI was an explicit component during RL optimization of codonGPT, we also observed a small but statistically significant increase in CAI in the base codonGPT model outputs (Fig. 3I), which may reflect an emergent preference for more frequently used codons, even though the model was not explicitly trained on high-CAI sequences.

To solve for amino acid fidelity during generation, we introduced a novel method of inference-time, pre-softmax, logit masking mechanism that restricts outputs to synonymous codons per position. This ensures semantic correctness without modifying model weights. We would like to point out that the customized logits processor-based mechanism we proposed in this study also enables incorporation of additional biological constraints (e.g. restriction enzyme site detection, motif presence) at inference time itself without necessarily adding RL.

We show for the first time that mRNA sequence design is made easier by our method. We showed this by extending codonGPT via RL for various biological optimization tasks. For protein-specific optimization, we developed an RL framework that combines CAI, GC balance, ΔG, and codon entropy into a unified reward function. This approach allows tailored optimization per protein, reflecting application needs in synthetic biology and therapeutic protein design. This user-customized balance between fidelity and variability is critical for biological design robustness. Fixed reward weights were used, but future work may explore adaptive strategies informed by empirical data. codonGPT can also be fine-tuned to additional design constraints not implemented in this study, such as immunogenicity, ribosomal pausing, or tissue-specific translation.

Overall, our study is the first of its kind to offer a scalable, modular, and biologically grounded framework for codon-level sequence generation and optimization. It serves as a foundation model for biologically constrained design, supporting both general-purpose biological discovery and target-specific engineering of protein-coding DNA. Unlike DNA- or protein-centric models, codonGPT provides a foundation model for sequences at the codon level and learns to generate synonymous codon chains while adhering to user-customized biological constraints. Our study establishes a foundation for broader applications in synthetic biology, multi-host expression tuning, mRNA vaccine design, and therapeutic antibody design.
