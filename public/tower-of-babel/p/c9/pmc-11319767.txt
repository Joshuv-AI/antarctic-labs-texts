Distinctive duodenal microbiomes and bile acid profiles in duodenal tumor patients revealed by prospective observational study
Authors: Yoko Kubosawa, Tomohisa Sujino, Kentaro Miyamoto, Atsuto Kayashima, Daisuke Minezaki, Kohei Morioka, Kentaro Iwata, Kurato Miyazaki, Teppei Masunaga, Mari Mizutani, Teppei Akimoto, Yusaku Takatori, Noriko Matsuura, Atsushi Nakayama, Kaoru Takabayashi, Nobuhiro Nakamoto, Akira Honda, Motohiko Kato, Naohisa Yahagi, Takanori Kanai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe incidence of duodenal tumors (DTs) is increasing. However, the mechanisms underlying its development remain unclear. Environmental factors, including the microbiome and bile acids (BAs), are believed to influence tumor development. Therefore, we conducted a single-center, prospective, observational study to investigate the potential differences between patients with DTs and healthy controls (HCs) based on these factors. In addition, the BAs in the duodenal fluid were measured using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. We recruited 41 patients and performed 16S rRNA-seq. There was no difference in the observed ASVs or PCoA plot of Bray–Curtis dissimilarity between the DTs and HCs. The lithocholic acid concentration was significantly lower in the DT group than in the control group. The ratio of CDCA to LCA was significantly higher in patients with DTs. No significant differences in microbiota were observed between DTs and HCs. In patients with DTs, the lithocholic acid concentration in duodenal was significantly lower than in HCs.Subject terms: Gastrointestinal diseases, Oesophagogastroscopy, Gastrointestinal cancer, Microbiology

Introduction

The incidence of duodenal cancer is reported to be 3.0–3.7 per million population in North America1,2, 2.9–4.3 per million population in Europe3,4, and 23.7 per million population in Japan5, all of which are rare cancers that meet the definition of < 6 per 100,000 population. However, reports from North America2 and the Netherlands4 have indicated an increased incidence. The incidence in Japan is much higher than that in the West; the detection rate of localized lesions is also very high, at 56.4%5 compared to 10–22% in the West1,3. The influence of regional differences cannot be denied; however, the extent of endoscopic medical checkups and the increasing interest of endoscopists in duodenal lesions could have contributed to the early detection in many cases. Surgical procedures, such as pancreatoduodenectomy, are recommended for tumors that invade deeper than the submucosal layer6; however, pancreatoduodenectomy is a very invasive procedure with a postoperative morbidity rate of 30–40% and a mortality rate of 1–4%7–9.

In contrast, adenomas and intramucosal carcinomas can be cured using endoscopic procedures; therefore, early detection and treatment are desirable in the duodenum. Strategies for treating duodenal cancer have been established; however, the mechanisms of development and the risk factors for duodenal tumors remain unclear. Clarifying these factors may enable more appropriate screening and post-treatment surveillance of duodenal tumors, a rare disease. A duodenal tumor’s genetic developmental pathway is similar to that of a colon tumor, called the “adenoma-carcinoma sequence.” This indicates that environmental factors affect the second or third mutation during duodenal tumor development. Recently, microbes have been considered to be the most affected environmental factors. Notably, specific microbes trigger gastrointestinal cancer; for example, Helicobacter pylori infection increases the risk of gastric cancer10 and Fusobacterium nucleatum infection increases the growth rate of colorectal tumors11,12. Recently, microbes and their metabolites have been shown to influence host health status and disease, suggesting that modification of metabolites with disruption of the intestinal microbiome (dysbiosis) may induce various diseases13–15. In colorectal cancer, the intestinal microbiome and different metabolites are altered depending on the presence or absence of a tumor and the degree of tumor progression. Branched-chain amino acids and bile acids (BAs), especially deoxycholate, are significantly increased in intramucosal carcinoma12. BAs, which are synthesized in the liver and excreted into the bile, are vital for lipid digestion and absorption. Cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) are synthesized in the liver as primary BAs. Primary BAs are converted to secondary BAs by the intestinal microbiome.

CA is converted to deoxycholic acid (DCA), and CDCA is converted to lithocholic acid (LCA). These secondary BAs are highly cytotoxic because of their hydrophobic characteristics; however, they also exert anti-inflammatory effects and inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. There are several steps involved in the production of secondary BAs by microbes. The first step involves 7α-dehydroxylation enzymes that are exclusively present in low-abundance anaerobic bacteria. The Bai gene cluster catalyses multiple steps. Notably, most microbes in Ruminococcus have bai, especially Clostridium, with BA 7α-dehydratase encoded by baiE. BAs act as antimicrobial barriers by disrupting bacterial cell walls owing to their amphipathic detergent properties16–18. Therefore, the microbiome and BAs are closely associated.

Therefore, we considered that the gastrointestinal microbiome and metabolites, especially BA, may differ between patients with duodenal tumors and healthy donors.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A summary of this study is presented in Fig. 1. Table 1 shows the characteristics of the study population. A total of 41 patients were recruited: 24 in the duodenal tumor (DT) group and 17 in the healthy control (HC) group. The median ages in the DT and HC groups were 66 years (44–81 years) and 33 years (30–66 years), respectively, and the DT group was significantly older (P < 0.01). The proportion of males was high in both groups (DT group, 66.7%; HC group, 76.5%) (P = 0.73). Those with eradicated Helicobacter pylori infection was higher in the DT group (12% and 29% in the HC and DT groups, respectively); however, the difference was not significant. Notably, only one person in each group was administered antacids (P = 1.0). In the DT group, the median lesion size was 18 mm (range, 5–60 mm) and most lesions were located beyond the descending part. Notably, approximately 80% of the lesions were adenomas.

Profile of duodenal microbiome

We then compared the composition of mucus-associated microbes between the HC and DT groups using 16S rRNAseq. The workflow for microbiome analysis is shown in Fig. 2a. Analysis was difficult in two cases in the DT group and one in the HC group because of the small number of reads obtained. As the total number of microbiome in this study was small, we performed 16S rRNA sequencing using biopsy forceps that were passed through the endoscopic channel as the negative control (negative control). We observed 299 ASVs from samples of HC/DTs and 44 ASVs from the negative control (NCs) (Fig. 2a, right panel). We found three ASVs that overlapped between HC/DTs and NCs. In a previous paper, Ralstonia was reported as a contamination of the kit19,20. Therefore, we excluded the two Ralstonia species from HC/DTs and performed further analysis (Fig. 2b).

Profile of bile acids in duodenal fluid

Next, we analyzed the concentration and composition of BAs in the duodenal lumen. First, the median concentration of total bile acid in the duodenal fluid was 12,030 nM (155–895,031 nM) in the DT group and 3563 nM (192–964,071 nM) in the HC group (P = 0.70), showing no significant difference. Comparing the concentrations of each bile acid (CA, CDCA, DCA, LCA, and UDCA), LCA was significantly lower in the DT group (23.1 nM) than in the HC group (46.7 nM), p = 0.0242). However, the others showed no significant differences were observed in the other groups (Fig. 5a). The proportion of each bile acid in the total bile acids was compared. No significant differences were observed between the two groups for any of the BAs (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, comparison of unconjugated and conjugated BAs showed no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.86) (Fig. 5c). We next compare the ratio of primary to secondary BAs. CA: DCA showed significant differences between the two groups; however, CDCA: LCA were significantly higher in the DT group (DT group: HC group = 0.79 [0.76–1.91]:0.17 [0.13–0.39], P = 0.0001). Total primary BAs: total secondary BAs (CA + CDCA: DCA + LCA + UDCA) were comparable between the DT group and HC group. Consequently, secondary bile acid LCA decreased in patients with DT compared with that in HCs.

Discussion

In recent years, an increase in the number of DTs has emerged. However, little is known about the environmental factors in the duodenal mucosa of patients with DTs and HCs. Therefore, we explored the mucosal microbiome and its metabolites, BAs, in patients with DTs and HCs.

We performed 16S rRNAseq of the duodenal mucosa-associated microbiome in patients with DTs and HCs and observed no differences in the observed ASVs and PCoA plot of Bray–Curtis dissimilarity (Fig. 3a, b). As these are low-biomass samples with a high risk of false positives due to contamination sources, we mentioned our procedure and the risk of contaminants19,20. To avoid contamination, we analyzed the sterile forceps that passed through the endoscopic channel. 44 ASVs were detected using a sequence kit or microbe in the endoscopic channel. Three species overlapped between the HC and human samples. E.coli was common microbe in human gastric duct, so we did not remove from analysis however, we excluded Ralstonia which was reported as kit contamination19,20. Ralstonia is an aerobic, gram-negative bacterium typically found in soil-, water-, and plant-associated environments. We excluded Ralstonia from the analysis in this study. Nevertheless, we did not exclude the possibility that Ralstonia existed in the duodenum, as Ralstonia can colonize the airways and generate biofilm-causing pneumonia, especially in ventilated patients and those with cystic fibrosis21–23.

Although we detected a variety of microbiomes in the duodenum samples from both the HC and DT groups, the PCoA plot indicated that there was no difference in the composition of the microbiome between the HC and DT groups. To see the detail of the genus level, Bacteroides was detected in less than half cases in both HC and DT group. As samples from the small intestine were enriched for Firmicutes and Actinobacteriota and Bacteroides was more prevalent in the colon24–26, our data were the same as those in previous reports. Interestingly, the percentage of the patients which Actinobacteriota are detected is 75% in HC and 36.3% in DT group. In a previous report, Actinobacteriota was detected in the mucus of the small intestine, but not in fecal contents27. Further studies are needed to determine whether the accurate positive ratio of the patients in whom Actinobacteriota was detected in the DT group was lower than that in the HC group on a large scale. Moreover, we need to show that the lower detection rate of Actinobacteriota in the DT group was the cause or effect of the DT progression.

We also analyzed the concentration and composition of BAs in the duodenal lumen and found that LCA concentrations were significantly reduced in patients with DT, and the balance of CDCA/LCA was higher in patients with DT due to the LCA concentration. We did not observe the difference in the concentration of other bile acids, CA, CDCA, DCA and LCDA. Recently, it has been suggested that differences in BA composition may be involved in pathophysiology28 and that BAs, especially DCA, are also involved in carcinogenesis29,30. There have been several reports of secondary BAs and LCA in epithelial cells. Previous reports have indicated that secondary BAs, including LCAs, promote colon cancer as LCAs damage epithelial cells through reactive oxygen species production, inducing apoptosis through CD95/caspase-8 signaling, and enhancing cell proliferation31. In addition, LCA activates Erk1/2 by suppressing signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) 3 phosphorylation and promoting cell proliferation32. Notably, these reports were generated using colonic epithelial HT-29 and HCT-116 cells but not duodenal cells31,32. Based on this evidence, we suspected that reducing the LCA in patients with DT was not a DT trigger.

Further studies are required to examine the involvement of LCA reduction in DT development, especially in the epithelial duodenal cells. The connection between microbes and BAs has been well described, especially in the colon but not in the duodenum. In addition, most secondary BAs are generated in the colon because of the abundance of the microbiome in the colon but not in the duodenum. As most of the data regarding BAs and microbiomes were obtained from colon or fecal samples, it remains unknown whether microbiomes in colonic anaerobic conditions and those in duodenal microaerophilic conditions generate secondary BAs at the same rate. Our findings raise the possibility that reducing LCAs induces the expression of pathogenic proteins such as heat shock proteins in microbes. BAs can damage microbiome DNA and inhibit the production of heat shock proteins33,34. Moreover, LCA is known to reduce the pathogenesis of Clostridium difficile by reducing the growth and activity of toxins35. Therefore, LCA in HCs may reduce the pathogenesis of metabolites from the pathobionts. Further studies are required to demonstrate the expression of metabolites, including heat shock proteins and toxins, in microbes isolated from patients with DT and HCs. There are other possible explanations for this observation. First, an imbalance of CDCA/LCA in the duodenum may directly induce intestinal damage. Few studies have reported that primary BAs directly affect epithelial cells36–38; therefore, further studies are required. Second, BAs and the microbiome indirectly affect duodenal tumors; for example, high amounts of gastric acids in the duodenum suppress microbiome growth, resulting in the reduction of secondary BAs. Notably, we could not exclude the possibility of gastric acid influx into the duodenum owing to morphological factors, diet, or body position. As it is quite challenging to show the connection between gastric acids and duodenal tumors, the unique composition of BA might be a biomarker for duodenal tumors, even after compiling evidence on a large scale in a prospective study.

This study had some limitations. First, the sample size was small due to the disease’s rarity. Second, we could not obtain age-matched samples from healthy donors due to ethical issues. Therefore, we analyzed the microbiome components in alpha and beta diversity analyses, defined by age. Our data indicated that the composition of the duodenal microbiome was not significantly altered by aging. Third, we collected the BAs and intestinal microbes after fasting39. It is technically challenging to obtain samples simultaneously after gastric juice is ejected from the duodenal papilla. This may interfere with BA concentrations in patients with DT and HCs. Despite this timing, since the distribution of BA concentrations was similar in patients with DT and HC, we believe that bias in that area has been considered. Fourth, we detected bacteria using 16S rRNAseq in the duodenum; however, the total volume of microbes was lower than that in other intestinal tissues, such as the colon11. We collected mucus-related microbiome by obtaining duodenal tissue biopsies without washing. Nevertheless, we did not reveal the microbiome profiles on the three samples because of low amplification. Further studies are required to obtain sufficient mucus-related microbiome using a safe method such as an abrasion brush.

Here, we analyzed the microbiome composition and BAs in patients with DT and HCs. We did not observe a difference in the observed ASVs and PCoA plots of Bray–Curtis dissimilarity between DT and HCs. The LCA concentration in the duodenum of patients with DTs was lower than that in HCs and the balance of CDCA/LCA was higher in patients with DTs.

Methods

Study design and eligible patients

This was a single-center, prospective, observational study. Patients aged ≥ 20 years who underwent endoscopic treatment for duodenal tumor or esophagogastroduodenoscopy and willingly consented to participate in this study were included. Patients with liver disease, liver failure, antibiotic use within one month, abnormal blood coagulation, inability to manage anticoagulation or platelet medication following the guidelines for gastrointestinal endoscopy, severe organ damage, or other cases deemed inappropriate for enrollment by the physician in charge were excluded. The study was conducted between April 2021 and December 2022. This study was conducted following the 2008 revised Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by Keio University School of Medicine Ethical Review Board (20,200,185).

Sample and data collection

Patients stopped eating the night before, and the procedure was performed with at least 12 h of abstinence from food. During the procedure, benzodiazepine was used for sedation, and scopolamine butylbromide or glucagon was used as antispasmodic. Bile secretagogue was not administered. The endoscope was inserted into the descending part of the duodenum without suction. After spraying 100 mL of saline solution, the duodenal fluid was aspirated through the endoscope tube and collected, quickly frozen with liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. After duodenal fluid collection, two pieces of normal mucosa were collected from the descending part of the duodenum using sterile biopsy forceps for microbiome analysis. Furthermore, specimens were soaked overnight in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and stored at -80 °C. In patients with duodenal tumors, the tissue was collected from the normal mucosa at a distance that would not affect tumor resection. We collected individual patient information, including age, sex, medication, H. pylori eradication history, lesion characteristics, and histopathological findings, from their medical records.

Determination of the microbiome profile

DNA isolation from mucosal samples and sequencing for the duodenal microbiome analysis were outsourced to the Bioengineering Laboratory. Co., Ltd. (Kanagawa, Japan). Genomic DNA was extracted from the mucosa. The mucosa stored at − 80 °C was thawed, and Lysis Solution F (NIPPON GENE. Tokyo, Japan) was added; the mixture was crushed at 1500 rpm for 2 min using a Shake Master Neo (BIO MEDICAL SCIENCE. Tokyo, Japan). After incubation at 65 °C for 10 min, centrifugation was performed at 12,000 × g for 2 min, and the supernatant was separated. DNA was purified from the aliquoted solution using a Lab-Aid®824s DNA Extraction kit (Zeesan Biotech, Fujian Province, China). Libraries were prepared using a two-step tailed polymerase chain reaction (16S rRNA V3–V4) and sequenced at 2 × 300 bp using the MiSeq system and MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (Illumina, California, USA). Cutadapt (version 4.6) is trimming primer sequences (Forward: CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG, Reverse: GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC) from sequencing reads40. Quality filtering, merge read pairs, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) chimera removal and infers exact ASVs was used DADA2 (version 1.30.0)41. Barrnap (version 0.9) was used to remove ASVs other than bacteria (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap). The representative sequences and ASVs tables were obtained after removing the chimeric and noise sequences using the Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology (QIIME) 2 plugin (version 2023.7.0)42,43. Phylogenetic inference was performed by comparing the Silva 138 99% OTUs full-length sequences with the representative sequences obtained using the feature-classifier plugin44. Three samples were removed because the taxonomic string contained any of mitochondria, chloroplast, archaea and unclassified.

Diversity analysis was performed using QIIME2. Diversity calculations are based on sub-sampled data rarefied to 862 counts (Fig. 2c). α diversity was compared using the observed ASVs; β diversity was estimated using the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity to calculate the distances between the samples and visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). The statistical analysis of beta diversity was conducted using Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Bar plots were created using microshades (version 1.12)45. Differential microbiota abundance was analyzed by the analyzing the composition of microbiomes (ANCOM)46 using the qiime2 command.

Determination of the bile acid profile

The concentration and composition of BAs in the duodenal fluid were measured using the previously described liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)47. An internal standard mixture consisting of [2H4]CA (41.6 ng), [2H4]CDCA (57.5 ng), [2H4]DCA (32.8 ng), [2H4]LCA (22.4 ng), [2H4]ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA, 34.4 ng), [2H4]tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA, 34.4 ng), and [2H5]taurocholic acid (TCA, 200 ng) in 20 µL of ethanol–water (9:1, v/v) was added to 4–20 µL of duodenal fluid. The samples were then diluted with 1 mL of 0.5 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). BAs were extracted using Bond Elut C18 cartridges (200 mg, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and an aliquot was injected into the LC–MS/MS system. The LC–MS/MS system using the electrospray ionization (ESI) mode consisted of a TSQ Vantage triple stage quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with an HESI-II probe and a Prominence ultra-fast liquid chromatography (UFLC) system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Hypersil GOLD column (150 × 2.1 mm, 3 μm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for each BA’s chromatographic separation at 40 °C. The following gradient system was used at a flow rate of 200 µl/min: first, the mobile phase composed of 20 mM ammonium acetate buffer (pH 7.5)–acetonitrile–methanol (70:15:15, v/v/v); then it was programmed in a linear manner to 20 mM ammonium acetate buffer (pH 7.5)–acetonitrile–methanol (30:35:35, v/v/v) over 20 min. The final mobile phase was kept constant for an additional 10 min. The general MS/MS conditions for selected reaction monitoring (SRM) were as follows: spray voltage, − 2500 V; vaporizer temperature, 450 °C; sheath gas (nitrogen) pressure, 50 psi; auxiliary gas (nitrogen) flow, 15 arbitrary units; ion transfer capillary temperature, 220 °C; collision gas (argon) pressure, 1.0 mTorr; and ion polarity, negative. The monitoring ions and optimal collision energies were m/z 407 → 407 (20 V) for CA, m/z 391 → 391 (20 V) for CDCA, DCA, and UDCA, m/z 375 → 375 (20 V) for LCA, m/z 464 → 74 (35 V) for GCA, m/z 448 → 74 (35 V) for GCDCA, GDCA, and GUDCA, m/z 432 → 74 (35 V) for GLCA, m/z 514 → 80 (70 V) for TCA, m/z 498 → 80 (70 V) for TCDCA, TDCA, and TUDCA, m/z 482 → 80 (70 V) for TLCA, m/z 411 → 411 (20 V) for [2H4]CA, m/z 395 → 395 (20 V) for [2H4]CDCA, [2H4]DCA, and [2H4]UDCA, m/z 379 → 379 (20 V) for [2H4]LCA, m/z 519 → 80 (70 V) for [2H5]TCA, and m/z 502 → 80 (70 V) for [2H4]TUDCA. A calibration plot was established for each BA. Different amounts of authentic BAs were mixed with deuterated internal standards and quantified as described above. The weight ratio of each BA, relative to the corresponding deuterated internal standard, was plotted on the abscissa, and the peak area ratio of the authentic BA to the deuterated variant measured by SRM was plotted on the ordinate. Because deuterium-labeled BAs for all authentic BAs were not available, [2H4]CA was used as an internal standard for GCA, [2H4]CDCA for GCDCA, [2H4]DCA for GDCA, [2H4]LCA for GLCA, [2H4]UDCA for GUDCA, and [2H4]TUDCA for TCDCA, TDCA, and TLCA. The linearity of the calibration curves, as determined by simple linear regression, was excellent, and samples that deviated from the calibration curve were diluted and reassayed. Total BA means the sum of all unconjugated and conjugated BAs; bile acids without a prefix were defined as the unconjugated BAs in this study. For example, the reference to a CA is an unconjugated CA.

Statistical analysis

We performed univariate analyses using the Fisher exact test for categorical data and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous data; statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using the JMP Pro software (version 17.0.0; SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software).
