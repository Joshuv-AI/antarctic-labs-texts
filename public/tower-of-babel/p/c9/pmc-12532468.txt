Projection of temperature-related severe injuries under climate change: a nationwide study of South Korea
Authors: Jieun Oh, Whanhee Lee, Jiwoo Park, Cinoo Kang, Yewon Seo, Ho Kim, Seungsik Hwang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFuture warming is projected to increase the burden of severe injuries, which can cause deaths or chronic disabilities. However, the future impact of temperature on severe injuries has not been adequately addressed in previous studies.MethodsWe collected national severe injury data and daily meteorological data between the period 2016–2020 across the entire 250 districts in South Korea. We applied a two-stage approach with a time-stratified case-crossover design to derive temperature-related severe injury risk. Using the projected daily mean temperature from six general circulation models (GCMs), we projected the excess number and fraction of severe injuries due to temperature for the historical (2010–2019) and future (2020–2099) periods under three shared socioeconomic pathway (SSP) scenarios.ResultsA total of 237,606 severe injuries during 2016–2020 were identified in this study. We found that exposure to temperature was associated with severe injuries, and these associations are stronger in injuries from transport or contact with chemicals or other substances. Excess severe injuries attributable to temperature were projected to increase to 10.21–15.52% from the 2050s to the 2090s under different SSP scenarios, compared to 8.16% in the historical period. The largest future burden was projected under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, with excess fraction in the 2090s estimated to be 13.83% for injuries due to contact with chemicals or other substances, 12.56% for transport injuries, and 4.86% for falls.ConclusionThis nationwide study indicates that the temperature rise under the climate change scenario may contribute to an increase in the future burden of injuries in South Korea, particularly transport injuries and injuries due to contact with chemicals or other substances. Our findings may be informative for public health policies aiming at alleviating the health burden in the context of global warming.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24844-0.

Background

Climate change has become one of the biggest challenges to human health in the 21 st century [1]. The hazardous impacts of ambient temperature on morbidity and/or mortality have been well-documented in numerous previous studies [2–4]. A previous study in South Korea (hereafter, Korea) found that the risk of mortality due to heatwaves has increased over the past 20 years [5]. Furthermore, several recent studies have projected that the health burden attributable to temperature will further increase under climate change scenarios [6–8]. However, these studies have focused on all-cause and non-external cause mortality [6, 7], or certain causes of mortality, such as cardiovascular disease [8]. Limited number of studies have addressed the future impact of temperature on severe injuries that can cause deaths or chronic disabilities affecting the quality of life [9].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), 4.4 million people worldwide die from injuries each year, accounting for 8% of all deaths [10]. In Korea, injuries accounted for 10.1% of the total disability-adjusted life years, most of which were due to transport or other unintentional injuries [9]. There are multiple factors associated with injuries, including alcohol or substance abuse and weak legal system [11, 12]. Some injury mechanisms, such as falls, are closely related to aging, with older adults being vulnerable to injuries even from major or minor accidents due to age-related physiological changes, such as reduced muscle strength and impaired balance [13]. Also, a growing number of studies have found potential pathways through which exposure to extreme temperature may affect injury risk [11, 12, 14, 15]. Heat exposure is associated with mental fatigue, impaired physical performance, and diminished cognitive functioning [12, 15], which can lead to unintentional injuries in a variety of settings, such as roads and workplaces. Moreover, extreme weather conditions associated with climate change can damage urban infrastructure and create hazardous environments that increase the risk of injuries [16, 17]. In specific, extreme heat can cause cracking, buckling, and swelling within structures, which can deteriorate road surface and affect pavement friction, increasing risk of losing control [17]. This set of findings suggests that the burden of injuries may become more severe in the future with further global warming.

To understand and predict future climate change impacts, a comprehensive set of climate models from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP) has been widely utilized [6–8, 18–20]. This project, part of the World Climate Research Programme (WCRP)’s Working Group on Coupled Modelling (WGCM), has coordinated climate model experiments involving multiple international modeling teams [19]. These models provide historical and projected future climate variables, including global temperature, under various climate change scenarios. The latest phase, CMIP6, incorporates the shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs), a new scenario framework based on socioeconomic trajectories, in which the representative concentration pathways (RCPs) have been combined, enabling more accurate scientific projections of climate change scenarios and related health risks [18].

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the impact of temperature on injuries, particularly severe injuries that can contribute to a greater burden on society, and further quantify the future excess burden of severe injuries attributable to temperature using climate change scenarios from CMIP6 models. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first and largest study that estimated the potential impact of temperature under different climate change scenarios by using a nationwide dataset in Korea that holds all severe injury cases from 2016 to 2020.

Methods

Data collection

Severe injury data

We collected national data on severe injuries occurring between the period 2016–2020 across the entire 250 districts in Korea from the Community-Based Severe Trauma Investigation system database developed by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency [21]. This database aims to generate regional statistics on severe injuries and assess the emergency medical response capacity of health facilities in the community and local communities.

This database is based on the legally mandatory nationwide medical record investigations, conducted on total patients with severe traumas, non-traumatic severe injuries, and multiple casualties, transported by emergency medical services. In the investigation system, a case is defined as a severe injury when emergency medical services identify a high risk of severe trauma or when the revised trauma score indicates abnormalities (based on the state of consciousness, blood pressure, and respiratory rate). More detailed criteria for severe injuries and related information in this database are described in the Supplementary Materials (Description of severe injury data).

Each recorded case includes information on individual characteristics, including age, sex, address where the case occurred, intention, and mechanism. The mechanisms of severe injuries were categorized into transport-related, falls, contact with chemicals or other substances (i.e., poisonings and corrosion from solids, liquids, and gases, anaphylactic shock, and chemical injuries such as paint in the eyes or glue burns), and others (i.e., blunt or penetrating injuries, injuries from mechanical force, heat and cold-related injuries, suffocation, etc.).

Historical weather and air pollution data

We collected district-level daily meteorological and air pollution data in 2016–2020 across 16 provinces in Korea, including daily weather data on mean temperature (°C) and relative humidity (%) from the Korea Meteorological Administration [22], and daily concentrations of particulate matter smaller than 2.5 μm (PM2.5) data from the Korea Environment Corporation [23]. The daily meteorological and air pollution data were linked to each severe injury case at the district level based on the information on address and date.

Projected temperature data under climate change scenarios

District-level projected daily mean temperature data were obtained from the dataset of CMIP6 general circulation models (GCMs) [19]. We obtained the daily projected temperature series for the historical (2010–2019) and projected (2020–2099) periods under three SSP scenarios (SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5) in six GCMs (ACCESS-CM2, CanESM5, CESM2, CNRM-CM6-1, CNRM-ESM2-1, KACE-1-0-G). Specifically, the SSP2-4.5 scenario corresponds to a medium radiative forcing level within “middle of the road” socio-economic family, while the SSP3-7.0 scenario is considered a medium-high scenario within the “regional rivalry” socio-economic family. The SSP5-8.5 represents the “fossil-fueled development” scenario spectrum with high radiative forcing [18]. All the daily mean temperatures were extracted from grid cells corresponding to the coordinates of each district and recalibrated using the observed series [20].

Statistical analysis

Estimation of the observed exposure-response relationships

The analyses follow a two-stage approach to derive the pooled severe injury risk related to temperature. In the first stage, we performed a conditional logistic regression with a time-stratified case-crossover design to estimate the temperature-related severe injury risk for each of the 16 provinces. The time-stratified case-crossover design compares the exposure to the daily mean temperature on the day of severe injury occurred with the exposure during control periods on an individual level. In this study design, the same days of the week in the same calendar month were selected as control periods, and therefore, each case had three to four controls. This approach is effective in controlling for time-varying confounders, including long-term trends, seasonality, and the day of the week effect, and time-invariant confounders, including sex, age, economic conditions, and lifestyle [24, 25].

Specifically, we applied conditional logistic regression to fit the association between temperature and the onset of severe injuries. Given the low number of severe injury cases (Table S1), odds ratios derived from the conditional logistic regression can be considered reasonable approximations of relative risks. We modeled the cross-basis function of temperature and its lag using the distributed lag nonlinear models [26]. The exposure-response function was modeled using a natural cubic spline function with three internal knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the province-specific temperature distributions. Considering that injuries usually occur as acute events, we adopted a lag period of 0–2 days based on the previous study [27]. The lag-response function was modeled with a natural cubic spline with an intercept and one internal knot. To account for environmental time-varying confounders, we included natural cubic B-spline functions with three degrees of freedom, for both relative humidity and PM2.5 concentrations. Then, we reduced the high dimensional estimates to obtain the temperature-severe injury association cumulated over the lag period for each province.

In the second stage, we pooled the first-stage estimates to provide the nationwide and province-specific estimates using a multivariate meta-regression approach. Also, to investigate the sub-populations and potential effect modifications, we repeated the main analysis described earlier for stratified analysis by the main mechanism of severe injuries (transport injuries, falls, and injuries due to contact with chemicals or other substances) with a sufficient sample size for analysis. Also, in order to examine the regional difference, we calculated the province-specific risks using the best linear unbiased prediction [28].

Projection of excess severe injuries in the future

We assessed the excess severe injuries burden attributable to temperature in Korea in the future under different SSP scenarios, using the daily series of modeled temperatures and the observed severe injuries. We assumed no change in baseline population and adaptation to extreme temperature, and therefore, we used the average observed counts of severe injuries for each day of the year to represent the level for the entire period from 2010 to 2099. All these assumptions and methodologies were based on the approach in previous studies [6, 29].

We calculated the attributable number and fraction of severe injuries using the province-specific lag-cumulative odds ratio corresponding to each day’s temperature, with the 25th percentile of observed temperature distribution as the reference, in line with previous studies [27, 30]. We aggregated the excess number of severe injuries for each day by decade, covering the historical (2010–2019) and projected period (2020–2099), for three SSP scenarios (SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5) for the entirety of Korea. To address uncertainty in both the estimation of the exposure-lag-response associations and projections across six GCMs, the effects of temperature on severe injuries were estimated using the Monte Carlo simulation. For each GCM model, we generated 1,000 samples of BLUPs assuming the multivariate normal distribution. Subsequently, point estimates (i.e., mean and median) and 95% empirical confidence interval (eCI) for the attributable number and fraction of severe injuries were calculated using the entire samples of six GCM models. Additional information about the procedures is represented in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Methods). All statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.1.3 software and relevant packages, including “dlnm” for distributed lag non-linear models, “survival” for conditional logistic, and “mvmeta” for meta-analysis.

Sensitivity analyses

We performed several sensitivity analyses. First, we tested modeling specifications by altering the knots in the exposure-response function to be placed at the 10th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of temperature distributions and extending lag period to seven days. Second, we calculated the excess severe injury burden attributable to temperature for each GCM model and compared the results. Third, when estimating and projecting the excess burden, we only used daily mean temperature within the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile or the 5th to 95th percentiles of the observed temperature series. Fourth, to account for measurement error, we used population-weighted average temperature data for the analysis instead of monitored temperature data. Lastly, we estimated exposure-response relationships during the study period before the COVID-19 outbreak and projected the excess burden.

Results

Among the total severe injuries, 61.46% were males, 68.79% occurred in individuals aged 40 or older, and the majority of cases (73.42%) were unintentional injuries. Transport was the most prevalent mechanism of severe injuries (36.62%), followed by falls (25.07%), contact with chemicals or other substances (19.15%), and others (15.58%). Table S3 in Supplementary Materials shows that 99.70% and 86.19% of transport and fall injuries, respectively, occurred unintentionally, whereas 65.44% of injuries due to contact with chemicals or other substances occurred intentionally.

Observed temperature-severe injury association

Figure 1 presents the relationships between temperature and severe injuries across lag 0–2 days by mechanism. Overall, the temperature-severe injury associations were approximately linear or J-shaped. At high temperature, the risks of total and transport injuries were observed to increase steeply, while the risk of injuries from falls remained flat. Injuries due to contact with chemicals or other substances increased sharply with temperature and then remained high. By the intention of severe injuries, the cases that occurred unintentionally showed a sharp increase with temperature, while those occurring intentionally remained relatively steady and even decreased at the highest temperature (Fig. S1). These trends were also observed in the results for unintentional and intentional severe injuries resulting from contact with chemicals or other substances (Fig. S2).

Future temperature-attributable excess severe injuries

Figure 2 and Table S4 present the temporal trends in excess severe injuries attributable to temperature by decade under three SSP scenarios. Compared to the 2010 s (historical period), the overall excess risk due to temperature was projected to increase continuously during future periods in the order of SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. Specifically, excess severe injuries attributable to temperature in the 2050 s were similar under SSP2-4.5 (10.21%; 95% eCI: 3.86, 15.86) and SSP5-8.5 (10.86%; 95% eCI: 4.30, 16.90). However, the gap among scenarios gradually widened, with the excess risk in the 2090 s estimated to be 11.12% (95% eCI: 4.54, 16.93) for SSP2-4.5 and 15.52% (95% eCI: 7.41, 23.06) for SSP5-8.5.

Among different injury mechanisms, the projected burden attributable to temperature was much greater in injuries from transport or contact with chemicals or other substances than in falls in each period and SSP scenario (Fig. 2). For example, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, temperature-related excess injuries due to contact with chemicals or other substances were projected to be 11.34% (95% eCI: −9.00, 27.48) in the 2050 s and 13.83% (95% eCI: −10.41, 33.24) in the 2090 s (Table S5). Meanwhile, excess risk of transport injuries attributable to temperature showed a sharp increase under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, rising from 7.83% (95% eCI: −4.42, 19.16) in the 2050 s to 12.56% (95% eCI: −1.79, 25.55) in the 2090s. However, excess risk of falls attributable to temperature did not show a prominent increase, with 4.21% (95% eCI: −4.36, 12.00) in the 2050 s and 4.86% (95% eCI: −5.54, 14.29) in the 2090s.

Figure 3 shows excess burden of total severe injuries attributable to temperature under each GCM model. The results were consistent for most of the models, showing a gradual increase in the future period, with the magnitude of the increase varying across SSP scenarios. However, as we projected further into the future, the variability between GCM models increased, peaking in the 2090 s, and it was particularly pronounced under the SSP5-8.5 scenario; which would highlight the necessity to consider the uncertainty among different GCM models as done in this study.

Sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analyses, the changes in the location of the knots in the exposure-response function and extension of lag period could not substantially alter the association of temperature with severe injuries (Table S6). Although excess burden in the future period was estimated to be smaller when excluding more extreme temperature values, the overall trend by decade and SSP scenario was consistent with the main findings (Table S7). We did not find noticeable changes in the exposure-response association when using population-weighted average temperature data (Table S8, Fig. S3). When the COVID-19 period was excluded, the overall trend was consistent with main findings, with slightly higher estimates (Table S9, S10).

Discussion

This study evaluated the potential impact of temperature on severe injuries under different climate change scenarios using a nationwide dataset across 250 districts in Korea. Our results show that exposure to temperature was associated with severe injuries, and these associations are stronger in injuries from transport or contact with chemicals or other substances. In addition, excess severe injuries attributable to temperature were projected to increase to 10.21–15.52% from the 2050 s to the 2090 s under different SSP scenarios, compared to 8.16% in the historical period (2010s).

Some previous studies have estimated the impact of temperature on injuries, and only a few of them have projected future burdens [31–35]. One study in China found a nearly linear association between injury mortality and temperature [33], which is consistent with our findings. Although results by specific causes were mixed, deaths due to transport injuries or other unintentional injuries were consistently associated with high temperature [33, 35]. A few studies that included projection analysis estimated an increased risk of injury mortality attributable to temperature in the future, particularly under the RCP8.5, which is the highest emission scenario [33–35].

In this study, we found that the magnitude of temperature-related excess severe injuries varies depending on the extent of warming expected under alternative SSP scenarios. The greatest future burden is projected under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, which is characterized by rapid and unconstrained growth in economic output and energy use. In contrast, relatively fewer excess severe injuries are projected under the SSP2-4.5 scenario, which assumes moderate mitigation strategies with some improvements in the intensity of resource and energy use. These findings would emphasize the importance of implementing effective policies to address the impact of climate change, which can have a significant burden on health in the future.

We also revealed that the projected burden of temperature-related severe injuries was substantially heterogeneous by specific mechanisms; in particular, excess risk was pronounced in transport injuries and injuries due to contact with chemicals or other substances. This difference is likely related to the different exposure-response associations. Unlike the risk of falls, which was estimated to remain flat overall, the risk of injuries from transport or contact with chemicals or other substances was estimated to increase with temperature, contributing to a greater burden in future periods with higher daily mean temperature.

Although the exact mechanisms and pathways remain to be examined further, there would be some possible hypotheses to explain the impact of temperature on severe injuries. Consistent with previous studies, we observed increased risk in injuries that were mostly unintentional, such as those from transport or unintentional contact with chemicals or other substances [33, 35]. Many studies suggest that heat stress can impair decision-making abilities and motor skills and make individuals more vulnerable to hazardous environments, which may lead to unintentional injury events such as traffic accidents or occupational accidents [11, 14]. In addition, extreme temperature can affect roadway safety by inducing infrastructure damage (e.g., pavement buckling and road surface melting) [17], and introduce potential hazards to work settings (e.g., difficulty in gripping due to sweat, sweat in eyes, and distraction) [36]. On the other hand, we found no evident increase in temperature-related risk of falls in this study. It may be related to older adults, who are more likely to have a fear of falling, reducing their outdoor activity in extreme weather conditions (e.g., high temperature or heavy rainfall) [37], but further investigation is needed.

While not addressed in this study, the increasing frequency and intensity of natural disasters due to climate change can exacerbate the impact of temperature on severe injuries. Extreme weather events, such as floods, storms, and wildfires, not only pose direct threats to safety but also disrupt essential services and environmental conditions [16, 38]. For example, power outages resulting from floods or storms in summer season, can disable air conditioning and other temperature control mechanisms, amplifying the health impacts of extreme temperatures [38]. Further research is needed to understand this complex interplay between temperature and natural disasters and its implications for public health.

Some limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, when projecting future burdens attributable to temperature, we assumed no change in baseline population and adaptation to extreme temperature, thus our results should be interpreted carefully. Future works should be conducted considering changes in population characteristics and temperature risks with the corresponding precise scenarios. Second, due to the limitation of sample size, we could not explore all specific causes and mechanisms of severe injuries. While injuries directly related to temperature, such as burns, sunstroke, and frostbite, were also documented, the sample size was too small to be included in the analysis. Third, the analysis to estimate observed exposure-response association was limited to the period from 2016 to 2020 due to the availability of severe injury data. Although this period is relatively short, this dataset comprises all reported cases of severe injuries in Korea, resulting in a substantial number of observations (Table S1). To address the potential uncertainties, further research should be conducted with data collected over a longer period. Fourth, our study could be related to the limitations regarding estimation biases. Because we used monitored temperatures as exposures, there may have been a measurement error. However, our sensitivity analysis with population-weighted average temperature data showed consistent results, and these measurement errors, which are likely to be random, have been known to bias risk estimates towards the null [39]. Also, although we applied the time-stratified case-crossover design which can control for time-invariant confounders, there may be potential confounders that could change within a month (e.g. daily physical conditions). This limitation should be addressed in the future studies with large and precise cohorts.

Despite these limitations, our study has several notable strengths. To the best of our knowledge, relatively few studies have projected the future burden of injuries attributable to temperature, and most of them have been conducted in specific regions, not on a national scale. Furthermore, as these studies have relied on injury death records, the risk of non-fatal outcomes of injuries has not been fully assessed. Therefore, we believe that our nationwide study, which was based on national records on severe injuries, can add new evidence for the potential impact of temperature on the onset of severe injury and provide more comprehensive estimates than these prior investigations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study indicates that exposure to temperature is associated with severe injuries and the temperature rise under the climate change scenario may contribute to an increase in the future burden of injuries in Korea, particularly transport injuries and injuries due to contact with chemicals or other substances. In the context of global warming, our findings provide important information regarding the allocation of resources for interventions aimed at alleviating the health burden due to climate change.
