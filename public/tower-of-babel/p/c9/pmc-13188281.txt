Prevalence of nontuberculous mycobacteria isolates and pulmonary disease from respiratory tract samples in persons investigated for presumed tuberculosis from three West African countries, an analysis of three prospective cohort studies
Authors: Elizabeth Tabitha Abbew, Souleymane Mahamadou Bassirou, Isiyaku Ahmadu, Juliana Boampomaa Adu, Roger Laryea, Rita Patricia Frimpong Amenyo, Felix Sorvor, Yaw Adusi Poku, Dorcas Obiri-Yeboah, Tom Decroo, Lutgarde Lynen, Natalie Lorent, Leen Rigouts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn West Africa, the isolation of nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) is increasingly reported among individuals evaluated for tuberculosis (TB). However, the clinical relevance of NTM detection from respiratory tract samples remains unclear, creating uncertainty for clinicians when NTM are identified. As a first step in addressing this gap, we aimed to estimate the prevalence of NTM isolation from mycobacterial cultures among individuals with presumed TB, regardless of TB treatment history, and determine the proportion who met international diagnostic criteria for NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD).MethodsWe analysed data from three prospective cohort studies conducted in Ghana, Nigeria, and Niger using a descriptive analysis. Sputum samples were collected from all individuals presenting with presumed TB for smear microscopy and culture; additionally, Xpert MTB/RIF testing was performed in Ghana and Niger. NTM species were identified using GenoType CM/AS and/or rrs/rpoB sequencing. Drug-susceptibility testing was performed with phenotypic and genotypic methods. Clinical data were extracted from medical records and structured questionnaires, and patients underwent chest radiography.ResultsOut of 9,981 symptomatic individuals screened, 938 were subjected to mycobacterial culture, of whom 99 (Ghana: 31; Nigeria: 42; Niger: 26) had at least one NTM isolate. The prevalence of NTM detection among mycobacterial cultures was 10.6% (95%CI: 8.7%-12.7%). The mean age was 51.6 years, with 61.6% males; 46.5% had a history of TB, and 27.3% were living with HIV. Among the 99 patients, the predominant symptoms were cough (82.8%), weight loss (56.3%), fever (47.5%), and dyspnoea (28.3%). Consolidation was observed on chest x-ray in 73.7% and cavitation in 46.5%. Common NTM species included M. intracellulare (34.3%), M. fortuitum (16.2%), and M. abscessus complex (10.1%). Most M. intracellulare and M. abscessus complex were susceptible to macrolides and aminoglycosides. Of the 49 patients with two or more sputum samples processed for culture at baseline and/or follow up, 15 (30.6%) met the diagnostic criteria for NTM-PD, with all patients exhibiting smear positivity, clinical symptoms, radiological abnormalities and repeated culture of the same NTM species. Mortality for patients who were classified as NTM-PD was 43%.ConclusionThese findings provide insights into the clinical relevance of NTM isolation in TB-endemic settings. Active TB should be excluded in all symptomatic patients, as should other possible explanations such as (post-TB) bronchiectasis with bacterial superinfection, Aspergillus infection) if infrastructure allows, before attributing the clinical presentation to NTM. Until more context-specific epidemiologic and clinical data become available, adherence to NTM-PD diagnostic criteria is recommended to guide appropriate clinical decision-making.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13211-y.

Background

Nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) isolation as well as pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) is increasingly recognised worldwide, particularly in the Americas, Asia, and Europe, with Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), comprising M. intracellulare and M. avium, identified globally as the most common causative agent for NTM disease [1–3]. In these regions, NTM-PD has been associated with underlying conditions such as bronchiectasis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and other chronic lung diseases [4]. The diagnosis and management of NTM-PD is guided by the standardised ATS/IDSA/ERS/ESCMID diagnostic criteria [5]. These criteria require the presence of clinical symptoms, radiological abnormalities, and microbiological evidence. Typically, the isolation of the same NTM species from at least two positive culture results from expectorated sputum samples, or positive culture result from at least one bronchoalveolar lavage or biopsy specimen, is required.

In West Africa, increasing numbers of NTM isolates have been reported among individuals initially presumed to have tuberculosis (TB) [6]. This trend reflects the expanded use of mycobacterial culture, particularly in the monitoring of drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) and in research settings that utilise line probe assays (LPA) for NTM identification. Prior TB has been identified as the major risk factor for NTM-PD in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [7]. However, most studies from the region are limited to reporting the prevalence of NTM isolates but lack clinical and radiological descriptions of affected patients.

As a result, there is little guidance for clinicians in routine practice on how to act upon the isolation of NTM from mycobacterial culture. The diagnostic limitations, including the inability to process multiple samples for culture or to perform species identification, as well as challenges in ensuring patient follow-up and difficulties in confirming disease progression, hinder the timely and appropriate management of NTM-PD patients [8].

To better understand the clinical implications of the increasing NTM reports, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of NTM among individuals with presumed TB, evaluate their clinical and radiological features, and assess how the established diagnostic criteria for NTM-PD might apply to our resource-limited setting.

Methods

Study design and patient recruitment strategy

We analysed data from three prospective cohort studies in three West African countries: Ghana, Nigeria, and Niger.

In Ghana and Nigeria, individuals with presumed TB, regardless of TB treatment history, were recruited (Table 1). Patient recruitment and follow-up was prospective in Ghana (June 2022 - December 2024) using a symptom-based screening approach, adapted for the purpose of this study. Among those identified as presumed TB cases, Xpert MTB/RIF testing was performed; participants with Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBc) detected by Xpert were excluded from the study. In Nigeria, patients had been identified retrospectively (May 2017 - October 2018) [9, 10], with assessment of their clinical status after inclusion (retrospective for baseline, prospective for current status). Recruitment was nationwide from TB clinics in 5 regions of Ghana, while in Nigeria, patients were recruited from TB clinics in the cities of Shira, Zaria and Jama, all located in Kaduna State [9, 10].

In Niger, participants were enrolled from a prospective study conducted between June 2022 and December 2024 across the country’s four DR-TB clinics. The study included primarily a cohort of previously treated TB patients who presented with a presumed new episode of TB and had sputum smear positivity. In addition, any symptomatic individual who presented with presumed TB but who was MTBc-negative by Xpert but had acid fast bacilli (AFB) detected by smear microscopy- were also included. The Niger study population, however, was skewed towards patients with a history of prior TB treatment.

Across all sites, only individuals with NTM isolated from sputum culture at baseline, and no M. tuberculosis, were included. Upon identification of an NTM-positive person, clinical symptoms assessment was done prospectively using a structured questionnaire in Ghana and Niger, while medical records were consulted retrospectively in Nigeria (Supplementary S2). Symptoms of interest included cough, chest pain, haemoptysis, fever, weight loss, and dyspnoea. In all three countries, medical records were reviewed to assess comorbidities and sociodemographic data, which included age, weight, previous TB history, HIV status (determined using the national testing algorithm), diabetes mellitus status, occupation, and history of alcohol use and smoking. Chest radiography was performed at baseline, and images were interpreted by a radiologist or a medical doctor trained in chest X-ray interpretation.

To assess symptom evolution, clinical status, and survival outcomes, participants were followed prospectively in Ghana and Niger, while patients with NTM isolates from Nigeria were re-contacted for follow up. In Ghana, between 1 and 8 follow-up samples were obtained who were also reviewed for clinical assessment. In contrast, no follow-up sputum samples were collected from patients in Niger or Nigeria. The data for the analysis is located in the supplementary files of the repository (Supplementary S3_ntmdataallcountries.xlsx, Supplementary S3_twoormoresampledata.xlsx.

Laboratory analysis

At baseline, Xpert MTB/RIF (Ultra) was performed in Ghana and Niger, but not in Nigeria (Table 1; Fig. 1). Microscopic detection of acid-fast bacilli (AFB) was done in all three countries. Baseline sputum samples were collected differently for mycobacterial culture across the countries: one sample each in Ghana and Nigeria, and two samples in Niger, with an additional two samples in Niger reserved for direct PCR. Also, the criteria for performing mycobacterial culture varied across the study sites. In Nigeria, culture was performed for all patients with AFB-positive sputum. In Niger, it was done for patients with AFB-positive sputum in whom Xpert MTB/RIF (Ultra) did not detect MTBc. In Ghana, culture was performed for patients with no MTBc detected by Xpert, regardless of AFB status. This included those with a history of TB treatment, as well as patients for whom a clinician requested culture based on clinical presentation.

Species identification of NTM from positive cultures was performed locally in Ghana and Nigeria using the GenoType Mycobacterium CM/AS assays (Bruker, Germany). Additionally in Nigeria, isolates that could not be identified to the species level by GenoType, were characterised through sequencing of the rrs gene. In Niger, NTM species identification was conducted solely through sequencing of the rrs and rpoB genes at the Institute of Tropical Medicine (ITM) in Antwerp, Belgium, both on positive cultures and directly on sputum. In Ghana, molecular drug-susceptibility testing (DST) was performed using the GenoType NTM-DR assay (Bruker, Germany) for M. intracellulare and M. abscessus complex (MABC) isolates. For Niger isolates, phenotypic DST using broth microdilution was performed at Sciensano, the Belgian National Reference Laboratory. There was no DST performed in Nigeria.

During follow up between January 2022 to December 2024, sputum samples for mycobacterial culture were collected for the identified patients in Ghana, although not in a systematic manner.

All laboratory procedures were conducted at accredited national and regional TB laboratories: the National Tuberculosis Reference Laboratory in the Eastern Region of Ghana, the State Reference Laboratory in Zaria, Nigeria, and the Mycobacteriology laboratory at ITM and Sciensano, Belgium [11].

Study variables and statistical analysis

Variables included sociodemographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, radiological findings (including presence of consolidation and cavitations), laboratory results, treatment received, and current clinical status (categorised as alive, dead, or lost to follow-up). Data from all participating countries were collated separately in Excel spreadsheets and subsequently merged into a single dataset. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp, 2023).

The proportion of patients, along with their clinical and radiological characteristics, was described for each of the groups. Descriptive statistics was performed and stratified per country. The identified NTM species were evaluated for their potential clinical relevance in causing pulmonary disease using published criteria and reference databases of clinically significant NTM species. (https://bccm.belspo.be/Images/ITM/ClinicalRelevanceNTM_v24apr2024.xlsx).

In addition, analysis was performed on patients from whom one or more NTM was grown from sputum. We stratified the identified patients as NTM-PD (two NTM isolates, symptoms and radiological presentation) versus those with a single isolate among those who had at least two cultures done, either at baseline, either one at baseline and other(s) during follow up. NTM-PD is defined as a clinical condition caused by NTM, characterised by compatible respiratory symptoms such as chronic cough, weight loss, and haemoptysis, and by supportive radiological findings, such as nodular or cavitary lesions, and repeated isolation of the same NTM species from sputum culture.

In this paper, we define presumed TB as patients presenting with clinical features such as chronic cough, fever, weight loss, and night sweats, who are undergoing diagnostic evaluation for TB, regardless of eventual microbiological confirmation. Also, potentially clinically relevant NTM is defined as NTM isolates from sputum that are considered to have potential pathogenic significance as reported in the literature.

Results

Participant characteristics and baseline culture and PCR findings

Out of 9,981 symptomatic individuals screened, 938 were subjected to mycobacterial culture, of whom 99 (Ghana: 31; Nigeria: 42; Niger: 26) had at least one NTM isolate (Fig. 1). A total of 355 culture-negative cases, 390 MPT Positive (MTBc cases), and 45 patients with contaminated cultures were excluded from the analysis. An additional 49 cases were excluded due to missing clinical data or lack of NTM speciation. The remaining 99 patients were included in the analysis, amongst which parallel direct PCR and sequencing on sputa from patients in Niger revealed 54 NTM, 5 MTBc, 5 negative cultures, and 6 invalid or empty results (Fig. 1). Hence, in Niger, culture revealed 26 NTM, while direct sequencing identified 54 NTM.

Baseline clinical characteristics

The majority of participants (61.6%) were male (Table 2). The mean age was 51.6 years (standard deviation [SD] = 14.7), and the average body weight was 50.4 kg (SD = 13.2). Among the participants, 46.5% had a history of TB, acknowledging a broad variety across the three countries, ranging from 14.3% in Nigeria to 76.9% in Niger. Almost half had alcohol use disorder (45.4%) or reported smoking (42.9%), while around one third (27.3%) were living with HIV, and a minority (7.1%) had measured or self-reported diabetes mellitus. The most frequently reported symptom at baseline was cough, present in 82.8% of cases. Other common symptoms included weight loss (56.3%), fever (47.5%,), dyspnoea (28.3%), chest pain (19.2%) and haemoptysis (13.1%). Consolidation was found on 73.7% of chest radiography whereas cavitary lesions were observed in 46.5% of cases.

Identified NTM species and their drug-resistance patterns

Potentially clinically relevant NTM species were identified in 86.9% (86/99) of all those with at least one isolate. The most frequently identified species was M. intracellulare, accounting for 34 isolates (34.3%), and was present in all three countries. Other species included M. fortuitum (14.1%), predominantly from Ghana and Nigeria but absent in Niger, and M. abscessus complex (10.2%). M. palustre was also relatively common, with 7 isolates (7.1%), all originating from Niger. Less frequent species with potential clinical relevance included M. malmoense (5.1%) and M. avium (2.0%). Of note, several of the species identified in Niger or Nigeria (M. palustre, M. chitae, M. margaritense, and M. paraense) are not covered by the GenoType CM/AS. Their detection was only possible through sequencing, which highlights the methodological differences across study sites.

DST revealed that most isolates (M. intracellulare, M. abscessus complex, M. palustre, M. paraense) were susceptible to macrolides and aminoglycosides. Clinical data for individuals with NTM isolation and NTM-pulmonary disease

Among the 99 patients, we could assess the bacteriological ATS criterium for NTM-PD for only half of the patients. Indeed, only 49 patients had at least two sputum samples taken for mycobacterial culture: 26 patients from Niger with two samples cultured at baseline, and 23/49 patients from Ghana with one sputum cultured at baseline and one or more during follow up. Fifteen (30.6%) of 49 fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for NTM-PD: 11 from Niger and four from Ghana [5]. An additional two patients had repeated NTM isolation, but with a different species.

The mean age for the 15 persons with NTM-PD was 57.5 years (Table 4). The most frequently isolated species among NTM-PD patients was M. intracellulare (46.7%), followed by M. palustre (20.0%). All patients with NTM-PD were smear-positive at baseline, compared to 82.4% (28/34) in the group with no NTM-PD. Cough was equally reported across both groups. Other symptoms, such as fever (66.7% vs. 61.8%), haemoptysis (20.0% vs. 11.8%), dyspnoea (60.0% vs. 35.3%), and weight loss (93.3% vs. 79.4%) were more frequently observed among NTM-PD patients, yet none of these differences was statistically significant. Radiological abnormalities were documented in all NTM-PD patients, with all patients having consolidation at baseline compared to 85.3% of those with no NTM-PD. Cavitation on chest x-ray was more frequent in NTM-PD (80.0% vs. 64.7%). Also, these differences were not statistically significant.

Discussion

In this multi-country study, we provide insights into the prevalence and clinical relevance of NTM isolates in patients with presumed TB across three West African countries. Among the individuals with at least one mycobacterial culture done, 10.6% had at least one NTM isolate, and amongst those who had two or more sputum samples collected at baseline or during follow up, one third (30.6%) met the diagnostic criteria for NTM-PD [3, 12]. This proportion is comparable to that reported in Europe, where up to 25% of patients with NTM isolates met the ATS diagnostic criteria [13]. All 15 patients with NTM-PD presented with radiological evidence of consolidation, and most showed cavitation. Clinical symptoms at baseline were predominantly cough, fever, and dyspnoea; yet, these were also observed among patients with only a single NTM isolate, suggesting that some of the single-isolate patients may have had NTM-PD, not being bacteriologically confirmed due to the low number of cultures done [6]. However, these symptoms are also common in individuals presenting with chronic respiratory disease; therefore, a thorough evaluation is required for differential diagnosis [8, 14].

Most study participants had abnormalities on radiography, yet had a single NTM isolate at baseline, making it difficult to ascertain the clinical relevance of the NTM species, particularly because only half of the analysed population had multiple sputum samples cultured, and besides mycobacteria, no other culture was performed. Because fulfilling the microbiological criteria can be challenging, less stringent microbiological criteria have been suggested in case of MAC, particularly in settings with limited clinical infrastructure [14]. Among the 70 patients from Niger who were AFB-positive but MTBc-negative by Xpert, NTM detection by direct PCR and sequencing identified almost double the number of NTM compared to culture. These findings suggest that reliance on culture alone may have underestimated the burden of NTM-PD in Niger, which may be attributable to issues with sample transport and storage prior to culture initiation. It would be prudent to incorporate direct PCR and sequencing into diagnostic algorithms for patients with AFB-positive, Xpert MTBc-negative sputum, and consider NTM-positive results for classification as NTM-PD to avoid misclassification and unnecessary TB retreatment.

The 15 patients identified as NTM-PD, were all smear-positive, presented with cough and had an abnormality on chest radiography; however, none were initiated on pharmacological treatment. This may reflect uncertainty about its clinical relevance or structural barriers, including the limited availability and high cost of recommended multidrug therapy. The clinical benefit of NTM-PD treatment appears to vary depending on the patient population and treatment approach. In a high TB/HIV-burden setting, treatment outcomes for NTM disease were often poor, characterised by high rates of treatment failure, mortality, and loss to follow-up [15, 16]. The HIV-positivity in our study was 27.6% of the 99 overall and 10.2% among the 47 patients who had multiple samples cultured, yet none of the patients with NTM-PD was HIV positive. For patients with extensive NTM-PD, early adjuvant surgical intervention, regardless of the specific surgical approach, has demonstrated significant clinical benefit, leading to high rates of sputum culture conversion and a 80% two-year probability of being free from unfavourable outcomes [17].

Among our 15 NTM-PD cases, M. intracellulare (46.7%) was the predominant isolate, consistent with global trends [3]. For the patients with this isolate, the standard proposed treatment would apply, given the demonstrated susceptibility to macrolides and amikacin, which form the cornerstone of standard regimens [18]. The standard regimen for MAC-PD, requires a 12 to 18-month course of rifampicin, ethambutol, and a macrolide, with or without an aminoglycoside, but is difficult to implement in these resource-limited settings [5]. Macrolides, although available in some facilities, are not covered under national insurance when prescribed for NTM, requiring patients to pay out of pocket [8]. Injectable aminoglycosides, such as amikacin, are even less accessible in peripheral facilities. These challenges reflect broader systemic issues in delivering NTM-PD care in low-resource settings. M. palustre (20.0%), although rarely reported in the literature, was detected multiple times among patients from Niger. This slow-growing species has previously been described in Mali as causing NTM-PD, although it has mostly been associated with animal diseases and is not common in Europe [19]. DST for M. intracellulare and MABC confirmed susceptibility to clarithromycin and amikacin. This suggests that macrolide-based regimens could be effective, although treatment remains more difficult due to the absence of standardised guidelines and limited clinical experience. M. fortuitum (6.7%) presents a greater therapeutic challenge since treatment requires individualised, DST-guided regimens and often prolonged use of parenteral and oral agents. Similarly, an isolate related to M. asiaticum (6.7%) demonstrated susceptibility to clarithromycin and amikacin but resistance to rifampicin, making therapy possible but less straightforward due to the scarcity of clinical data. One isolate reported only as “Mycobacterium species” (6.7%) highlights the limitations of incomplete speciation, complicating rational treatment initiation without drug-susceptibility results. Overall, while preserved susceptibility to clarithromycin and amikacin across most isolates indicates that effective regimens are achievable, the ease of treatment varies considerably, with M. intracellulare being the most manageable, and rarer species such as M. palustre, M. fortuitum, and M. asiaticum posing more significant challenges for clinicians [5].

While most patients survived the follow-up period of up to two years, persistent respiratory symptoms remained common, even among those who did not meet the ATS criteria (whether or not due to limited microbiological data). These chronic symptoms may not be directly attributable to NTM but instead reflect the sequelae of prior pulmonary TB or other chronic lung conditions [14, 20, 21]. In our study, of the 57 patients with follow-up data, 25 were alive, and 76% of these survivors had a history of previous TB and chronic pulmonary symptoms. The absence of structured monitoring for such patients poses a diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma, especially in contexts where AFB smear positivity may result in repeated empirical TB treatment [19].

Even though numbers were too small to show significancy, our data suggest high mortality among NTM-PD patients, with deaths mostly attributed to respiratory causes. This aligns with previously published data for the overall mortality associated with NTM-PD ranging between 10 and 48% [22, 23]. Recently published data from the US Registry on NTM-PD reported the 5-year mortality as 12.6% [24]. Our data should however be interpreted with caution given the low numbers of patients with follow up data. Additionally, the study documented a higher rate of loss to follow-up in the NTM-PD group, though not statistically significant. This further reflects challenges in patient retention and continuity of care in low-resource settings. Ideally, patients with chronic respiratory symptoms and NTM isolation but who are not meeting the ATS/IDSA diagnostic thresholds or lack access to treatment must remain under care, which is difficult in low-resource setting.

International guidelines recommend serial sputum cultures and imaging to assess disease progression and inform treatment decisions [5, 18]. However, such follow-up protocols are rarely feasible in resource-limited settings without subsidised diagnostic services. The financial burden of routine investigations typically falls on patients or overstretched health systems, contributing to care discontinuity and delayed disease recognition. There is an urgent need for sustainable care models that include routine monitoring, patient education, and integration of pulmonary rehabilitation services, regardless of whether patients ultimately qualify for treatment. Strengthening health system capacity to manage people with chronic respiratory symptoms across the diagnostic, therapeutic, and follow-up continuum is essential to reduce avoidable morbidity and mortality in these settings, whether or not related to NTM. In addition, training clinicians on the diagnosis of NTM-PD, ensuring that patients are not misdiagnosed with TB, and identifying those who require treatment is essential. The species identified in our study, together with their drug-susceptibility patterns, indicate a potential window of opportunity for effective treatment.

This study has some limitations. First, the heterogeneity of the study population, including individuals with presumed TB but with different TB treatment backgrounds. The higher yield of NTM isolates observed in Niger, compared to Ghana and Nigeria, may be attributable to a more selective patient population who had previously been treated for TB in that setting, suggesting the potential benefit of refining inclusion criteria for future surveillance. Second, a potential source of bias in the study is the selective inclusion of mainly AFB-positive individuals from Niger and Nigeria, which may have excluded patients with NTM disease who are smear-negative. Third, the laboratory analysis was not uniform across all sites; notably the collection of one or two sputum specimens for cultures at baseline, the variety in methods for NTM species identification and NTM DST that was not performed in all countries. Fourth, the classification of patients with only a single identified NTM isolate as not having NTM-PD. In the study settings, repeated culture sampling was not consistently feasible due to technical and programmatic constraints, which limited full application of microbiological criteria for NTM-PD. While this approach was necessary to maintain diagnostic specificity, it may have resulted in some degree of misclassification and a conservative estimate of the true burden of NTM-PD. On the other hand, NTM are environmental opportunistic pathogens and a too loose interpretation of the microbiological criteria may lead to overdiagnosis and potential unnecessary exposure to antibiotics. And finally, follow-up over time with repeated clinical assessments and sputum sampling, which is required to establish a diagnosis of NTM-PD, was not done consistently, hence the true prevalence of NTM-PD may have been underestimated in our study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the presence and clinical relevance of NTM isolates in individuals with presumed TB in West Africa, with M. intracellulare being the most frequently isolated species among patients who fulfilled diagnostic criteria for NTM-PD. Despite the presence of characteristic radiographic features and persistent respiratory symptoms, none of the patients received treatment. The observed mortality and high loss to follow-up rates further show the need for health systems to develop context-appropriate strategies for diagnosis, management, and monitoring of post-TB sequelae. Strengthening diagnostic infrastructure, ensuring equitable access to treatment, and incorporating follow-up protocols into national TB programs may be critical steps toward improving outcomes for individuals who may have been affected by NTM-PD in resource-limited settings.
