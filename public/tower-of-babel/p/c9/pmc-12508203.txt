3D-LAKES: Three-Dimensional Global Lake and Reservoir Bathymetry from ICESat-2 Altimetry and Landsat Imagery
Authors: Chi Hsiang Huang, Shuai Zhang, Deep Shah, Anshul Yadav, Yao Li, Gang Zhao, Huilin Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Quantification of the water storage dynamics in global lakes and reservoirs is pivotal for understanding the roles of surface water in regional climatology, mitigating natural disasters, and preserving ecosystems. However, the ability to accurately comprehend these storage dynamics is significantly hindered by the lack of reliable and cost-effective global bathymetry information. This study introduces the 3D-LAKES dataset, which contains the area-elevation (A-E) relationship and three-dimensional (3D) bathymetry information for 510,530 global lakes and reservoirs, representing 98.9% of global surface water storage capacity. This dataset was validated using 214 A-E relationships and 12 bathymetry maps collected from in-situ measurements, showing strong agreement. The A-E relationships yield an RMSE of 0.60 m, a NRMSE of 0.14, and an R2 of 0.61; while the 3D bathymetry maps have an RMSE of 1.37 m and a NRMSE of 0.26. This dataset has the potential to support many applications, from monitoring lake/reservoir storage variations to parameterizing hydraulic/hydrological models. Such integration provides essential information for global hydrological studies, water management programs, and disaster mitigation.

Background & Summary

Global lakes and reservoirs play important roles in regulating regional climates, mitigating natural disasters, and preserving vital ecosystem functions. It has been long recognized that lakes can influence regional climates by changing the surface energy budget and the hydrologic budget1,2. Some recent studies have found that the dynamic storage variations of these water bodies can also increase carbon emissions3–5. Furthermore, lakes serve as natural barriers against natural disasters, by enhancing drought resilience and preventing fires from spreading6,7. In addition, the interconnected networks of lakes are crucial for facilitating global species migration, which, in turn, is essential for maintaining global ecosystem health8. Geometric characteristics of these lakes and reservoirs—from A-E relationships to 3D bathymetry maps—are essential for accurately quantifying these roles. This dataset also provides crucial inputs for monitoring and modeling water storage variability9–11, reservoir sediment flux12,13, and predicting phytoplankton dynamics14. However, the limited availability of information about these geometric characteristics—both from the lack of in situ measurements and the lack of data sharing (when they do exist)—has been a major barrier to research and applications, from local to regional to global scales15.

With the ongoing advances in satellite remote sensing for monitoring surface water, the ability for developing A-E relationships has improved significantly. The most common method is to pair up lake area estimations from imagery classifications with elevation observations from radar/lidar altimetry collected at about the same time16. Despite its good accuracy, this method has been primarily used only for large lakes, due to the limited temporal and spatial coverage of the altimetry data. Another method leverages the above-water topographic information represented by Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data, such as that from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM)17. By pairing each elevation with its corresponding cumulative area (accomplished by counting all pixels at or below this elevation), the regression A-E relationship can be established18. However, the range of the A-E relationship is limited to the water level during the DEM acquisition time. In addition, some simulation methods have been developed to expand A-E relationships based on publicly available geometric data (e.g., average reservoir length and maximum water depth values)15,19. While such approaches can usually provide results for most locations, these results often involve larger errors and uncertainties (due to necessary assumptions and over-simplifications).

Compared with establishing A-E relationships, creating 3D bathymetry maps is more challenging—especially with regard to accurately estimating the elevation values for each pixel. This significantly constrains the capability of developing 3D bathymetry maps for lakes and reservoirs when using current techniques. Some simulation methods predict elevation values by assuming the shape of the bathymetry19,20. By leveraging the slopes from DEMs and predicted maximum depth values, 3D bathymetry maps can be developed for most locations. However, these methods result in large errors and uncertainties due to their assumptions and over-simplifications. Another approach combines the A-E relationships with historical water extent data to generate bathymetry maps21. Nevertheless, the accuracy of these models strongly relies on the precision of the A-E relationships.

There are two major constraints with the current datasets: First, there is a scarcity of available datasets for medium and small-sized lakes. Therefore, global-scale surface water storage analyses—which rely on A-E relationships as critical inputs—are limited to either large lakes or primarily reservoirs10,22,23. More comprehensive analyses incorporating all waterbodies have only focused on evaluating water level variations and area changes24,25. Regardless, this scarcity of data sources (e.g., smaller lakes) undermines comprehensive environmental assessment and water resource management, making the acquisition of such data not only challenging but critically necessary for informed decision-making26,27. Second, the current capability to produce 3D bathymetry maps on a global scale remains significantly constrained. This directly limits studies that require detailed 3D bathymetry maps as crucial inputs. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to develop a comprehensive dataset that includes both the A-E relationships and 3D bathymetry maps for global lakes. This dataset31—referred to as “3D-LAKES” thereafter—contains 505,332 (natural) lakes and 5,198 reservoirs (Fig. 1). The total water storage capacity of 3D-LAKES31 is 185,742,569 km3, which represents 98.9% of the global capacity (according to the HydroLAKES dataset32). Furthermore, the number of lakes/reservoirs in 3D-LAKES31 is significantly larger than those in previous studies (e.g., 7,250 in GRDL33, and 27,000 in GloLakes23). This capability is attributed to the development of a novel algorithm which leverages high spatial resolution data from the Ice, Cloud, and land Elevation Satellite-2 (ICESat-2) and Landsat surface water occurrence (SWO) images. We further validated 214 A-E relationships and 12 bathymetry maps against in-situ measurements. This dataset can be used for supporting hydrological, hydraulic, geomorphological, ecological modeling, lake/reservoir remote sensing, and water resources management across scales. Methods

The methods developed in this study are illustrated using a flowchart in Fig. 2. This process leveraged three datasets: HydroLAKES32, Global Surface Water (GSW)25, and the ICESat-2 L3A Land and Vegetation Height product (ATL08)34,35. These datasets provided the polygons of the global lakes and reservoirs, their SWO images, and the terrain elevations associated with the accompanying domains respectively. Overall, this study enhanced the previous algorithm21,36, which combined SWO contours with elevation data to derive A-E relationships and generate 3D bathymetry maps. First, for each given lake of interest, the SWO images and elevation data were paired in order to extract the initial data points needed to establish the “Preliminary A-E Data Points”. Secondly, the “Quality Controlled A-E Relationship” was established at each lake. Instead of generating the A-E relationship using a linear regression based on all preliminary data points21,36, we adopted a machine learning algorithm to produce a nonlinear quality-controlled A-E relationship. This relationship is then used to establish Level 1 (L1) and Level 2 (L2) A-E products, representing the result before and after interpolation/extrapolation, respectively. Lastly, the “3D Bathymetry Map” was generated for each given lake by combining the L2 A-E relationship with the SWO image.Fig. 2Flowchart depicting the processes for generating the A-E relationship and the 3D bathymetry map for a given lake.

Flowchart depicting the processes for generating the A-E relationship and the 3D bathymetry map for a given lake.

Lake/Reservoir Polygons from HydroLAKES Database

The HydroLAKES dataset32 is comprised of polygons for 1.42 million lakes and reservoirs (>10 ha) on a global scale, covering a total surface area of 2.67 million km2. Among these, over 1 million are small lakes (with water areas from 0.1 km2 to 1 km2). In this study, all HydroLAKES32 waterbodies were first selected as the areas of study. Furthermore, to encompass all possible historical water coverage areas, we applied a buffer to each polygon according to the size of the lake area (see Supplementary Table 1 for more details).

SWO Images from the GSW dataset

Leveraging over four million Landsat images collected from 1984 to 2021, the GSW dataset25 provides 30-m resolution monthly water classifications at the global scale. The frequency with which water was present on the surface was captured in a single variable called the “SWO”. For a given pixel, its SWO value was calculated as the ratio of the number of times it was detected as water over the total number of valid observation times from 1984 to 2021. In other words, the SWO value represents the likelihood that water elevation surpasses land elevation. By assuming a quiescent water surface within the lake, pixels with the same SWO value should also share the same bathymetry value21. Over a lake area, the SWO image provides a remotely sensed contour map similar to the bathymetry map—except that the contours are labeled by frequencies (in percentage) rather than elevation values (Fig. 3(a)).Fig. 3An example showing the generation of the A-E relationship and the 3D bathymetry map at the Wickiup Reservoir, Oregon (USA): (a) Landsat-based water occurrence image and locations of ICESat-2 ATL08 data points; (b) Preliminary A-E points, L1 A-E relationship, and L2 A-E relationship; and (c) the L2 3D bathymetry map.

Elevations from the ICESat-2 ATL08 Product

The ICESat-2 ATL08 product during the period from 2018 to 2022 was utilized to provide the elevation values. ICESat-2 employs laser pulses to take along-track measurements with a footprint of 17 m, a sampling interval of 0.7 m, and a 91-day repeat cycle34. Because of the equipment (which uses three pairs of beams), ICESat-2 is capable of detecting elevations in smaller water bodies34,37. The ATL08 product provides measurements of along-track heights at an interval of every 100 meters above the WGS84 ellipsoid (based on the ITRF2014 reference frame) for both ground and canopy surfaces. The vertical accuracy for terrain height retrievals along a single transect is 0.3m38. In this study, two ATL08 variables —“h_te_best_fit” for the best fit terrain elevation, and “segment_watermask” for the inland water mask—were extracted for each reservoir or lake (see Fig. 3(a) for an example).

Extraction of Preliminary A-E Data Points

The process of extracting the preliminary A-E points involves three steps (Fig. 2; Process 1): First, the SWO-area (SWO-A) relationship was established at each lake/reservoir. For each SWO percentile value (from 0% to 100%), its corresponding area value was identified by calculating the cumulative water area from pixels within the percentile contour.

Second, the ICESat-2 ATL08 terrain elevation values were linked to their respective SWO percentile values. At each lake, all ATL08 data points which were within the buffered HydroLAKES32 polygon (see Supplementary Table 1)—but not identified as water (i.e., segment_watermask ≠ 1)—were extracted. Then, based on the locations of these points, the respective SWO percentile values were derived from the SWO image. If the respective SWO percentile values were not found, the ATL08 data point was excluded. By using the elevation values from the topography around the lake (instead of the water levels), this approach21 allowed us to extract bathymetric information from a limited number of ground tracks. As indicated in Fig. 3(b), the SWO-E relationship may have contained some outliers. These outliers could be attributed to the errors and uncertainties from both the SWO pixel values and the ATL08 terrain elevation values (see “Sources of Uncertainties and Limitations”) or to the erroneous inclusion of data from nearby small water bodies.

Lastly, by linking the two relationships (SWO-E and SWO-A), preliminary A-E data points were assembled (albeit with some outliers inherited from the SWO-E relationship—see Fig. 3(b) for an example). Since cloud contaminations and image misclassifications tend to affect the high and low ends of the SWO values more significantly, only data points within 5%–95% SWO values were used.

Generation of Quality Controlled A-E Relationships

To eliminate the outliers and establish a more reliable A-E relationship at each lake, a repeated two-step procedure was applied to the preliminary A-E points (Fig. 2; Process 2). Firstly, the Random Sample Consensus algorithm (RANSAC)39 was employed to identify outliers. This machine learning algorithm operates by randomly selecting data points to determine the optimal fitting results (or “zones”) that effectively differentiate inliers from outliers. This approach was selected because it can accurately identify inliers, even when a substantial number of outliers are present in the dataset. Because of the high stability, it is suitable for screening out outliers from the preliminary A-E points—particularly given that such relationships may involve changes in elevation values, which cannot be ignored due to reservoir sediment deposition. Next, the Isotonic Regression algorithm was applied to produce the quality-controlled A-E relationship. By fitting a free-form line through data via a non-decreasing function, this regression approach has been commonly adapted across various models and applications in different domains40–42. It is well-suitable for developing the A-E relationships in situations like ours, where area increases with elevation. Furthermore, this method has proven to be effective for identifying nonlinear A-E relationships15,43.

In this study, this procedure (the combined use of RANSAC and Isotonic Regression) was repeated 2,000 times at each lake. This process aims to maximize the range of each A-E relationship, and to address the challenge of local minima encountered during the training phase44. During each simulation, two indices were recorded: the Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE), and the range of the predicted elevation values. The best fitting A-E relationship was identified from the 2000 simulations based on the following criteria: First, the 200 simulations (10%) with the lowest NRMSE values were selected. Second, all of the selected simulations that contained only one or two points were excluded (Fig. 3(b)). In cases where none of the 200 simulations fit the criteria, the A-E relationship could not be determined for the lake. Finally, the simulation which had the largest elevation range was chosen as the best fitting A-E relationship for the lake. Based on this process, L1 A-E relationships (i.e., the L1 product), represented by the best fitting A-E relationship, were developed for 510,530 lakes and reservoirs (out of the 1.42 million locations). This L1 product includes only the most reliable area-elevation points derived from the machine learning algorithms (see Fig. 3(b) for example).

Furthermore, a L2 product, which contains the A-E relationship from 5% to 95% at a 1% SWO increment, was generated by applying a linear interpolation (when within the L1 range) and extrapolation (when outside of the L1 range) procedure to the L1 product. This allows for the prediction of elevation values from 5% to 95% at a 1% SWO increment (see Fig. 3(b) for an example). This method overcomes the limitation imposed by the fact that ICESat-2 data has a much shorter temporal coverage than GSW25; therefore, it may not be able to represent the full range of dynamic variations observed by Landsat. The L2 A-E relationships also serve as the basis for generating the 3D bathymetry maps.

Generation of the 3D Bathymetry Maps

By linking the SWO-A relationship with the A-E relationship, the one-to-one SWO-E relationship was established for each lake/reservoir (Fig. 2; Process 3). Subsequently, this one-to-one SWO-E relationship was utilized to transfer the SWO values to elevations in the SWO images, resulting in a 3D bathymetry map for the waterbody of interest. Figure 3(c) shows an example of a 3D bathymetric map.

Data Records

The 3D-LAKES dataset31 provides the A-E relationships and 3D bathymetry maps for 510,530 locations (505,332 lakes and 5,198 reservoirs), as illustrated in Fig. 1. This 3D-LAKES dataset31 accounts for 98.9% of global capacity according to the storage estimates in HydroLAKES32. The dataset includes the L1 and L2 products for A-E relationships, as well as the L2 product for 3D bathymetry maps. The L1 product and L2 product is based on the results before and after interpolation/extrapolation, respectively. A detailed description of these products can be found in “Extraction of Preliminary A-E Data Points”. Furthermore, the potential uncertainty associated with the product for each lake is provided, as detailed in “Sources of Uncertainties and Limitations” and in Supplementary Text 1.

The A-E relationships were made publicly accessible via Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/records/13107867). The 3D bathymetry maps are available for visualization and download through a Google Earth Engine (GEE) application (https://planet-test-projectchi.projects.earthengine.app/view/d-lakes). The codes related to this study can be found at the GitHub repository (https://github.com/ChiHsiang-67/3D-LAKES). Detailed guidance on how to access and utilize the application and code is provided in the “Usage Notes” and “Code Availability” sections.

Technical Validation

The performance of the A-E relationships and the bathymetry maps was evaluated comprehensively using in-situ measurements. These in-situ data include the A-E relationships for 208 reservoirs and 6 lakes, and 12 bathymetry maps, which were provided by the U.S. Bureau of Recreation (USBR, https://www.usbr.gov/lc/region/g4000/hourly/mead-elv.html), the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE, https://www.nwd-mr.usace.army.mil/rcc//), and the Texas Water Development Board (TWDB; https://www.twdb.texas.gov/surfacewater/surveys/completed/list/index.asp). Additionally, we also compared the A-E relationships from this study with those from two existing global datasets: GLOBathy19 and GRDL33. GLOBathy19 provides 1.42 million A-E relationships for lakes and reservoirs based on an empirical equation, and GRDL33 adopts deep machine learning techniques to reconstruct the bathymetry maps for 7,250 reservoirs. The coefficient of determination (R2), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and Normalized Root Mean Squared Error (NRMSE), were employed to evaluate the performance of the A-E relationships and bathymetry values against in-situ measurements.

Sources of Uncertainties and Limitations

The uncertainties of the 3D-LAKES31 dataset arise from three sources: sediment deposition, the assumption of a quiescent water surface, and the input data. Sediment deposition, which alters bathymetry at an annual storage sedimentation rate of 0.18%, poses a challenge in generating accurate bathymetric maps22. However, this issue has often been overlooked in previous studies and is difficult to correct—especially with the methods that use global DEM datasets like SRTM17 for bathymetry estimation15,19,20,45,46. In this study, we have attempted to minimize the errors due to sediment deposition by using two key components. First, we adopted the ICESat-2 elevation data from 2018 to 2022 to ensure that we have the latest elevation information. Next, we addressed potential sediment deposition impacts on the historical SWO image in the following manner. The locations that were heavily impacted by sediment deposition showed varying relationships between SWO and elevation values, and the outliers were removed through machine learning (as explained in the “Generation of Quality Controlled A-E Relationships” section). Since this method cannot estimate the A-E relationships (and their corresponding bathymetries) outside of the dynamic ranges, the sedimentation that occurred outside of these ranges could not be accounted for.

The second source of uncertainty is attributed to the assumption that the water surface is quiescent. This uncertainty may be negligible in small waterbodies but becomes more significant in larger ones—particularly when the water level differences between upstream and downstream are large. Currently, the algorithm cannot differentiate this uncertainty, as it assumes that the elevations of different points on the same SWO percentile are the same, regardless of being upstream or downstream of each other. As a result, 3D-LAKES31 may slightly overestimate the range of water surface levels in large water bodies. However, 3D-LAKES31 still performs better than datasets from previous studies (as demonstrated in Fig. 5).

The last major source of uncertainty stems from the spatial, temporal resolution and accuracy of the input dataset. These bathymetric maps are generated using data from SWO images and ICESat-2 elevation measurements. The SWO images offer a resolution of 30 m, providing 91 contour lines (with SWO values ranging from 5% to 95%) for each given lake/reservoir. This uncertainty causes the error that is discussed in the “Validation of the 3D Bathymetry” section—vertical data points in Fig. 6d–f. However, the precision of these contour lines could be enhanced through future integration of additional satellite imagery classifications. Moreover, since the ICESat-2 elevations are only available after 2018, the L1 A-E relationship may not be able to represent the full dynamic variations that occurred over the period of 1984–2021. To address these limitations, a L2 A-E relationship is generated by applying linear interpolation/ extrapolation to the L1 A-E relationship. It should be noted, however, that additional uncertainties are introduced from this linear assumption. Finally, the low accuracy of ICESat-2 elevations over steeper terrain slopes may reduce the reliability of the bathymetry values (see Supplementary Fig. 1).

To address these uncertainty issues, we have provided the NRMSE values (Model_NRMSE) and three quality flags (QA)—QA_RMSE, QA_NRMSE, and QA_Extrapolation—at each lake. Model_NRMSE, derived from the machine learning procedure in the “Generation of Quality-Controlled A–E Relationships” section, represents the uncertainties associated with the machine learning model and the input data. QA_RMSE represents the uncertainty of the overall bathymetry values, and QA_NRMSE is used to evaluate the quality of the A-E relationships. Based on these flags, statistical error ranges for RMSE and NRMSE were estimated at each lake. Finally, QA_Extrapolation was used to classify the extrapolation errors of the L2 product (see Supplementary Text 1 for more details).

Finally, although more than half a million lakes/reservoirs are included in 3D-LAKES31, the A-E relationships and 3D bathymetry maps are still not available for most of the very small lakes (less than 1 km2) (Fig. 1(d)). This limitation is primarily caused by the spatial and temporal resolution constraints of ICESat-2 data. The recently launched Surface Water and Ocean Topography Satellite (SWOT) offers the potential to fill in this data gap by measuring both the area and surface elevation of lakes larger than 250 × 250 m47,48. Nevertheless, generating complete bathymetry requires multiple measurements over years, especially considering the SWOT 21-day revisit time.

Usage Notes

In this study, the dataset includes L1 and L2 products for A-E relationships, and a 3D bathymetry map. Detailed descriptions of these products can be found in the section titled “Extraction of Preliminary A-E Data Points”. In addition, the uncertainty quantification of the bathymetry values at each lake is provided in Supplementary Text 1.

Two options are provided for accessing the dataset. The first option utilizes the GEE application, which offers rapid visualization and straightforward downloading capabilities. Users can search for a specific lake/reservoir by using either the HydroLAKES ID32, the GRanD ID49, the Prior Lake Database (PLD) ID50, or/and directly search the address. However, this option only supports downloading of the A-E relationship and the 3D bathymetry map of one lake/reservoir each time. The second option allows users to efficiently reuse all of the data. The L1 and L2 A-E relationships for all of the lakes and reservoirs can be downloaded through Zendo. Due to the large size of the 3D bathymetry maps, a GEE code is provided to download all of the L2 3D bathymetries within the areas of interest.

The 3D-LAKES dataset31 has a variety of potential applications. It can be used to monitor historical and recent lake/reservoir storage dynamics by combining the A-E relationships from 3D-LAKES31 with water surface area and elevation data. The Harmonized Landsat Sentinel-2 (HLS) instrument provides water area data every 3–5 days51,52, and the newly launched SWOT satellite measures water elevations every 21 days (even under cloudy conditions). This data helps to analyze historical droughts, and supports water management strategies45,53. Furthermore, 3D bathymetry maps are essential for sedimentary and hydrological modeling12,13,28, and for simulating turbidity currents in reservoirs. Additionally, the dataset advances knowledge of water temperature profiles, which play a critical role in assessing water quality, evaporation rates, and CO2 emissions in lakes and reservoirs54,55.
