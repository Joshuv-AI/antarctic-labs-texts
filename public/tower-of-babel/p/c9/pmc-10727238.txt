Assessment of the Effects of Sodium Nitroprusside Administered Via Intracranial Subdural Catheters on the Cerebral Blood Flow and Lactate Using Dynamic Susceptibility Contrast Magnetic Resonance Imaging and Proton Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy in a Pig Cardiac Arrest Model
Authors: Hyoung Youn Lee, Yong Hun Jung, Najmiddin Mamadjonov, Kyung Woon Jeung, Byung Kook Lee, Tae‐Hoon Kim, Hyung Joong Kim, Jorge Antonio Gumucio, David D. Salcido
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral blood flow (CBF) is impaired in the early phase after return of spontaneous circulation. Sodium nitroprusside (SNP) administration via intracranial subdural catheters improves cerebral cortical microcirculation. We determined whether the SNP treatment improves CBF in the subcortical tissue and evaluated the effects of this treatment on cerebral lactate.Methods and ResultsSixty minutes after return of spontaneous circulation following 14 minutes of untreated cardiac arrest, 14 minipigs randomly received 4 mg SNP or saline via intracranial subdural catheters. CBF was measured in regions of interest within the cerebrum and thalamus using dynamic susceptibility contrast‐magnetic resonance imaging. After return of spontaneous circulation, CBF was expressed as a percentage of the baseline value. In the saline group, the %CBF in the regions of interest within the cerebrum remained at approximately 50% until 3.5 hours after return of spontaneous circulation, whereas %CBF in the thalamic regions of interest recovered to approximately 73% at this time point. The percentages of the baseline values in the cortical gray matter and subcortical white matter were higher in the SNP group (group effect P=0.026 and 0.025, respectively) but not in the thalamus. The cerebral lactate/creatine ratio measured using magnetic resonance spectroscopy increased over time in the saline group but not in the SNP group (group–time interaction P=0.035). The thalamic lactate/creatine ratio was similar in the 2 groups.ConclusionsSNP administered via intracranial subdural catheters improved CBF not only in the cortical gray matter but also in the subcortical white matter. The CBF improvement by SNP was accompanied by a decrease in cerebral lactate.

The clinical outcomes of patients who are comatose after resuscitation from cardiac arrest remain poor despite advances in post‐cardiac arrest care, and <50% of these patients achieve a meaningful neurologic recovery. 1 , 2 , 3 Hypoxic–ischemic brain injury (HIBI) is a major factor that limits full recovery in comatose cardiac arrest survivors, 1 , 4 and there remains a great unmet need for novel, effective therapies to mitigate post‐cardiac arrest HIBI.

Cerebral blood flow (CBF) is a major determinant of cerebral energy substrate delivery. CBF impairment following return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), commonly referred to as postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion, has been observed in numerous experimental and clinical studies. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Despite the ambiguity on whether postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion is detrimental or merely an epiphenomenon of decreased cerebral metabolic requirements, 9 , 10 , 11 the HIBI‐mitigating potential of therapies that improve cerebral perfusion in the post‐ROSC acute phase has been reported. 12 , 13 In the treatment of postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion, intrathecal vasodilator administration has several advantages over systemic delivery. It bypasses systemic circulation and facilitates ready access of a high concentration of vasodilator drugs to the spastic vessels on the surface of the brain, thus resulting in enhanced therapeutic efficacy and a reduction in adverse effects associated with systemic administration, such as arterial hypotension. In a previous study using a porcine cardiac arrest model, 14 sodium nitroprusside (SNP) introduced into the subarachnoid space through intracranial subdural catheters during the early post‐ROSC period effectively improved the pial microcirculation and cerebral cortex tissue oxygenation. This intervention alleviated post‐ROSC cerebral cortical hypoperfusion and decreased the duration of exposure to cortical tissue hypoxia. Conversely, intravenous administration of SNP led to profound hypotension and failed to improve pial microcirculation and tissue oxygenation. 14 However, the efficacy of this SNP treatment for improving deep‐brain CBF was undetermined, because the cerebral perfusion measurements were limited to the surface of the cerebral cortex. To determine the potential applicability of the abovementioned treatment for postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion, the impact of the SNP treatment on the deeper cerebral structures as well as the pathophysiologic significance of postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion need to be investigated.

We hypothesized that SNP administration into the subarachnoid space would improve CBF in the subcortical tissue and reduce cerebral lactate levels. Thus, in the present study, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques, including dynamic susceptibility contrast (DSC)‐MRI and proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H‐MRS), were used to: (1) determine whether SNP administered via intracranial subdural catheters improves CBF even in subcortical tissues, and (2) evaluate the effects of this treatment on the cerebral lactate level, which is an indicator of anaerobic metabolic activity.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. This prospective experimental study included 16 female minipigs weighing 22.5 (20.8–28.1) kg. The animal care procedures and experiments were performed in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and the protocol for this study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology (KRIBB‐AEC‐21277).

Preparation

Following a 5‐day acclimatization with ad libitum access to tap water and commercial feed, the subjects were sedated with a single intramuscular injection comprising xylazine (2.2 mg/kg), tiletamine and zolazepam (6 mg/kg), and atropine (0.04 mg/kg). Next, the animals were anesthetized with isoflurane, intubated orotracheally, and ventilated. During the preparatory stage, isoflurane anesthesia was maintained and titrated (0.5%–2.0%) to maintain appropriate anesthetic depth (characterized by no withdrawal reflex and no increase in blood pressure or heart rate). In addition to the isoflurane anesthesia, local anesthesia with subcutaneous lidocaine infiltration was administered before all surgical incisions. Peripheral oxygen saturation and end‐tidal carbon dioxide levels were continuously monitored and maintained within the normal physiologic range by adjusting the oxygen flow and ventilation rates. The rectal temperature was monitored continuously and maintained at 37.5±0.5 °C by using a heating pad. A 7F catheter was inserted into the left external jugular vein for fluid and drug administration, and another 7F catheter was inserted into the right femoral artery for arterial pressure monitoring and blood sampling. To correct the intraoperative volume loss, saline was intravenously infused (flow rate 5 mL/kg per hour) throughout the experiment. After positioning the animals in the prone position, a scalp incision was made to expose the right and left parietal regions, and a cranial burr hole was made bilaterally over each parietal cortex; however, the dura was kept intact until the post‐ROSC intracranial subdural catheter placement.

Experimental Protocol

Figure 1 depicts the timeline of the experiment. The animals were positioned supine on a U‐shaped trough. A 2‐cm skin incision was made immediately below the xiphoid process and, using blunt dissection, a subcutaneous pocket that extended approximately 3 cm beneath the xyphoid process was created. After a 5‐minute isoflurane washout period, ventricular fibrillation was induced by applying an electrical current (60 Hz/30 mA) through a pacing wire that was inserted via the subcutaneous pocket and situated near the right ventricle. Ventilation was stopped immediately after the onset of ventricular fibrillation. After 12 minutes of untreated cardiac arrest, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was initiated with intravenous administration of vasopressin (0.6 U/kg), mechanical chest compressions (Life‐Stat; Michigan Instruments, Grand Rapids, MI; depth, 20% of the anteroposterior chest diameter; rate, 100 compressions/min), and manual ventilation (rate, 10 ventilations/min) with high‐flow oxygen (15 L/min). The 12‐minute duration of untreated cardiac arrest was chosen to induce a clinically relevant post‐ROSC cerebral pathophysiologic state. 15 Compared with epinephrine, vasopressin is associated with a higher ROSC rate after a prolonged cardiac arrest in pigs. 16 Vasopressin was thus chosen as the initial vasopressor to increase the ROSC rate and thus reduce the number of experimental animals for this study. A single biphasic 150‐J electrical shock was applied 2 minutes after CPR initiation and was repeated every 2 minutes for up to 14 minutes after CPR commencement, if necessary. If ROSC was not achieved after 2 minutes of CPR, an intravenous bolus injection of epinephrine (0.02 mg/kg) was administered at 3 minutes after CPR initiation and was repeated every 3 minutes.

After achieving ROSC, the animals underwent a 4‐hour period of intensive care, including hemodynamic and ventilatory support. Isoflurane anesthesia was resumed 15 minutes after ROSC and was maintained until the end of the experiment. The fraction of inspired oxygen and the ventilation rate were titrated to maintain a peripheral oxygen saturation of 94% to 98% and an end‐tidal carbon dioxide level of 35 to 40 mm Hg, respectively. If the mean arterial pressure (MAP) decreased to <65 mm Hg, a norepinephrine infusion was initiated and titrated to maintain the MAP to >65 mm Hg. Immediately after ROSC, an investigator randomly allocated the test subjects to the saline or the SNP group using the sealed envelope method and prepared an equal volume (1 mL) of saline placebo or SNP (4 mg) solution. The SNP dose in the present study was identical to that in a previous study. 14 All other investigators were blinded to group assignment. A 20‐gauge catheter was then inserted through each burr hole into the subarachnoid space for intrathecal drug administration. Immediately before the DSC‐MRI scan that was performed 1 hour after ROSC, the SNP solution or saline placebo was injected over 1 minute via the intracranial subdural catheters (0.5 mL through each catheter). The intracranial subdural catheters were removed following the intrathecal drug administration, and the burr holes were filled with ultrasound gel to minimize magnetic susceptibility artifacts and cerebrospinal fluid leak from the catheter insertion sites. No animals reached the predetermined humane end points for euthanasia (seizure, systolic arterial pressure <60 mm Hg, or heart rate <40 bpm) until the completion of the 4‐hour intensive care period. Immediately after the completion of the 4‐hour intensive care period, the animals were euthanized by infusing a rapid intravenous injection of euthanasia agent (T61; MSD Animal Health, Kenilworth, NJ) under isoflurane anesthesia.

Measurements

The primary outcomes were the CBF and cerebral lactate/creatine ratio that were measured with DSC‐MRI and 1H‐MRS, respectively. Coronal DSC‐MRI and 1H‐MRS were conducted at the prearrest baseline and at 1 and 3.5 hours after ROSC. After immobilization using a vacuum immobilization mat, the animals were positioned inside a 3.0T MRI scanner (Achieva 3.0T [TX] Quasar Dual System; Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands). The animals' heads were then placed within a 32‐channel coil (32‐channel SENSE Torso/Cardiac Coil; Philips Healthcare). During the MRI scans, the arterial blood pressure, heart rate, and peripheral oxygen saturation were monitored, and isoflurane anesthesia was maintained. Following the anatomic scanning (T2‐weighted imaging), the DSC‐MRI was promptly performed usng a gradient‐echo pulse sequence with the following parameters: repetition time/echo time=2000/40 ms, number of averaging=1, flip angle=75°, field of view=200×200 mm2, matrix size=128×128, voxel volume=1.56×1.56×4.00 mm3, and 15 slices with a slice thickness of 4 mm (no gap). For the DSC‐MRI, a 4‐mL bolus of gadobutrol (623 mg/mL, Gadobrix; Taejoon Pharma, Seoul, Republic of Korea), a gadolinium‐based MRI contrast agent, was intravenously injected (flow rate 3 mL/s) using an MRI‐compatible power injector, followed by a 20 mL 0.9% saline flush at the same flow rate. The proton magnetic resonance spectra were acquired using a single‐voxel point resolved spectroscopy pulse sequence from voxels of interest (VOI) in the cerebrum (parietal lobe) and thalamus (Figure 2). The imaging parameters of the 1H‐MRS were as follows: repetition time/echo time=2000/35 ms, flip angle=90°, bandwidth=2000 Hz, number of data points=1024, number of averaging=192, number of excitations=96, voxel size=20×10×30 mm3. A relatively large voxel size was used to achieve an adequate signal‐to‐noise ratio for the 1H‐MRS. As shown in Figure 2, the VOI for cerebral 1H‐MRS were positioned in the parietal lobe containing both cortical gray matter (GM) and subcortical white matter (WM) to ensure the inclusion of the cerebral parenchyma while circumventing the cerebrospinal fluid space. Among the diencephalic structures, the thalamus was selected as the site for the VOI for 1H‐MRS, given its central role in consciousness recovery and its ample size for VOI placement. In a study using a rat model of cardiac arrest, 17 Guo et al reported a robust correlation between CBF recovery in the thalamus and favorable neurologic outcomes. A researcher analyzed the DSC‐MRI data using a perfusion analysis program (DPTools; Denis Ducreux, Paris, France) without any information on group allocation. On the coronal slice at the midthalamic level, the CBF was measured in the following 4 separate regions of interest (ROI): (1) 10 mm2 area in the cortical GM (cortical GM CBF), (2) 10 mm2 area in the periventricular WM (subcortical WM CBF), (3) 61 mm2 area within the VOI for cerebral 1H‐MRS (CBF measured in this area was named GM+WM CBF, because this area contained both cortical GM and subcortical WM), and (4) 61 mm2 area within the VOI for thalamic 1H‐MRS (thalamic CBF) (Figure 2). Unlike in traumatic brain injury or cerebral infarction, which are usually unilateral, HIBI after cardiac arrest is bilateral. In our preliminary analysis, there was no difference in the CBF between the right and left hemispheres. Thus, the CBF was measured only in the right hemisphere in the present study. The arterial input function for the cortical GM CBF, subcortical WM CBF, and GM+WM CBF measurements was determined from voxels over the M3 segment of the middle cerebral artery, and that for the thalamic CBF measurement was determined from voxels over the paramedian thalamic artery. Given the small sample used in this study, considerable interanimal variation in CBF at baseline may have undermined our ability to detect a significant intergroup difference in the CBF. Therefore, the post‐ROSC CBF was expressed as a percentage of the measured value relative to the baseline value (%cortical GM CBF, %subcortical WM CBF, %GM+WM CBF, and %thalamic CBF) to account for potential interanimal variations. The obtained magnetic resonance spectra were processed and analyzed with Java‐based Magnetic Resonance User Interface software (version 5.2, MRUI Consortium) for the quantification of magnetic resonance spectroscopy data. The free induction decay data were multiplied by a 2‐Hz Gaussian broadening function, followed by 0‐filling (2 times, 4096 data points) and Fourier transformation to the frequency domain. Then, the water signal was suppressed through the Hankel‐Lanczos singular value decomposition and the spectral baseline was corrected, followed by 0‐ and first‐order phase correction. The metabolite peaks of interest were quantified using the Advanced Method for Accurate, Robust, and Efficient Spectral algorithm. Lactate and creatine levels were measured at 1.3 ppm and 3.0 ppm, respectively, and the lactate/creatine ratio was calculated using these measurements. Monitoring of arterial blood pressure and heart rate was maintained throughout the experimental procedure, with sampling conducted at predefined intervals: the prearrest baseline, 30 minutes post‐ROSC, and immediately following each MRI session after ROSC. Arterial blood gas (VetStat Electrolyte Blood Gas Analyzer; IDEXX, Westbrook, ME) and lactate (Unicel DXC 800; Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA) measurements were obtained immediately after each MRI session.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size for this study was calculated based on the microvascular flow index data (mean±SD, 2.79±0.26) of a previous study, 14 where the microvascular flow index values were 2.61±0.27 and 2.96±0.08 in the saline and SNP groups, respectively, and the intragroup variance was 0.04. We calculated that 6 animals would be needed per group to reach a power of 80% at α=0.05 and thus included 8 animals per group to minimize the impact of possible data loss. Continuous data were expressed as medians along with interquartile ranges. For comparing continuous data between the 2 groups, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was used, whereas the Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to compare 2 paired measurements (prearrest baseline versus 30 minutes, 1 hour, or 3 hours post‐ROSC) within each group. Linear mixed‐effects models were constructed to analyze group effects and group–time interactions in serially obtained hemodynamic, blood gas, and MRI parameters (Table S1). The linear mixed‐effects models contained group, time, and group–time interaction as fixed‐effects covariates with random effects of intercept that originated from repeated measurements. The prearrest baseline data of each parameter, except for MAP during CPR, %cortical GM CBF, %subcortical WM CBF, %GM+WM CBF, and %thalamic CBF, were used for covariate adjustment in the mixed‐effects models. Post hoc intergroup comparisons at each time point were conducted with Bonferroni adjustments. Two‐tailed P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and the T&F program version 4.0 (YooJin BioSoft, Goyang, Republic of Korea).

Results

Of the 16 animals, 14 achieved ROSC and were successfully randomized (7 in each group) and survived to the completion of the study protocol. No significant intergroup differences in the baseline hemodynamic, laboratory, and MRI measurements were found (Table 1). The MAP during CPR (Figure 3), number of epinephrine doses, CPR duration, and hemodynamic and laboratory values at 30 minutes before the allocated treatment did not differ between the 2 groups (Table 2).

Figure 4 shows changes in the hemodynamic and laboratory parameters during the experiment. Neither group effects nor group–time interactions were found in these hemodynamic and laboratory parameters. In both groups, the heart rate exhibited a marked increase at 30 minutes post‐ROSC (P=0.047 versus baseline in both groups), a phenomenon likely attributable to post‐cardiac arrest myocardial dysfunction. This tachycardia persisted until the experiment's conclusion. However, throughout the experiment, the MAP remained clearly ≥70 mm Hg in all animals. Neither the total dose of norepinephrine administered during the post‐ROSC period (P=0.647) nor the duration of norepinephrine administration (P=0.296) differed between the 2 groups. The arterial lactate levels exhibited a substantial increase in both groups at 30 minutes post‐ROSC (P=0.047 versus baseline in both groups), followed by a gradual decrease.

Figure 6 shows the %cortical GM CBF, %subcortical WM CBF, %GM+WM CBF, and %thalamic CBF at 1 and 3.5 hours after ROSC. In the saline group, the CBF at 1 hour post‐ROSC was reduced to approximately 40% to 50% of baseline levels in all ROI within the cerebrum (%cortical GM CBF, 39.5% [33.8%–43.8%]; %subcortical WM CBF, 52.6% [44.3%–67.9%]; %GM+WM CBF, 44.0% [41.6%–50.3%]) and remained decreased at 3.5 hours post‐ROSC (%cortical GM CBF, 43.4% [35.0%–63.0%]; %subcortical WM CBF, 52.9% [41.8%–64.6]; %GM+WM CBF, 52.0% [38.6%–76.7%]). In the SNP group, CBF at 1 hour post‐ROSC increased to approximately 100% to 120% of baseline levels in all ROI within the cerebrum (%cortical GM CBF, 126.1% [87.0%–180.4%]; %subcortical WM CBF, 113.5% [106.5%–149.2]; %GM+WM CBF, 97.6% [71.0%–129.5%]) and remained increased at 3.5 hours post‐ROSC (%cortical GM CBF, 110.9% [91.0%–147.3%]; %subcortical WM CBF, 130.1% [112.6%–207.8%]; %GM+WM CBF, 160.5% [146.9%–223.5%]). The mixed‐effects models revealed significant group effects for the %cortical GM CBF and %subcortical WM CBF (P=0.026 and 0.025, respectively) and a significant group–time interaction for %GM+WM CBF (P=0.021). The thalamic CBF at 1 hour post‐ROSC was reduced to 52.1% (37.6%–74.1%) of the baseline level in the saline group. However, unlike the CBF measured in ROI within the cerebrum, the thalamic CBF in the saline group recovered to 72.9% (50.5%–100.9%) of the baseline levels at 3.5 hours post‐ROSC. The %thalamic CBF in the SNP group was higher than that in the saline group at both 1 hour (82.2% [58.8%–91.3%]) and 3.5 hours (109.7% [65.3%–161.8%]) after ROSC; however, the differences did not reach statistical significance (group effect P=0.325, group–time interaction P=0.706). A significant group–time interaction was observed with regard to the cerebral lactate/creatine ratio (P=0.035). Post hoc analyses revealed a significantly lower cerebral lactate/creatine ratio in the SNP group at 3.5 hours post‐ROSC (P=0.033). Cerebral lactate/creatine ratio at 1 hour post‐ROSC could not be obtained in 1 animal in each group due to poor spectral quality. *P<0.05 vs the saline group using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. ROI indicates regions of interest; ROSC, return of spontaneous circulation; and SNP, sodium nitroprusside.

Discussion

In this study, we found that administering SNP into the subarachnoid space markedly improved blood flow in both the cortical GM and subcortical WM. Our approach diverged from an earlier study that evaluated the effects of SNP administration into the subarachnoid space on the pial microcirculation and cortical tissue oxygenation; the divergence was primarily in terms of intervention timing, cerebral perfusion measurement site, and measurement technique. In the previous study, intrathecal drug administration took place 30 minutes post‐ROSC, and cerebral perfusion was quantified by visualizing microcirculation on the cerebral cortex surface and determining the microvascular flow index, number of perfused microvessels, and arteriole diameter. 14 Despite these methodological differences, our cerebral perfusion measurement findings agreed with those of the previous study. Administering SNP into the subarachnoid space led to an immediate increase in CBF (including cortical GM CBF, subcortical WM CBF, and GM+WM CBF) and pial arteriole diameter in the current and previous studies, respectively, and the effects were sustained for a minimum of 2 hours. 14 The collective evidence from the present investigation and earlier research suggests that SNP administration via intracranial subdural catheters may serve as an effective treatment strategy for postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion following cardiac arrest. 14

The improvement of CBF by SNP in the present study is consistent with previously documented angiographic reversal of cerebral vasospasm after intrathecal SNP administration in patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage. 18 , 19 Cerebral vasoconstriction, along with the no‐reflow phenomenon, 20 causes postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion. 21 The imbalance between vasoconstrictive and vasodilatory mediators within the cerebral circulation has been identified as a primary cause of this phenomenon. More specifically, this includes an increase in vasoconstrictors such as endothelin and a decrease in vasodilators such as nitric oxide following cardiac arrest. 8 , 22 , 23 SNP causes vascular smooth muscle relaxation by releasing nitric oxide.

The capability of intrathecally administered SNP to improve blood flow in the subcortical tissue could play a significant role in optimizing neurologic recovery. Although subcortical WM is less susceptible to HIBI compared with cortical GM, it is similarly affected by postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion following cardiac arrest. 24 , 25 WM injury has been associated with poor neurologic outcomes after cardiac arrest. 26 , 27 In the present study, SNP likely improved blood flow in the subcortical tissue by acting on pial arterioles rather than through penetrating arterioles or capillaries. In the present study, blood flow in the subcortical tissue was markedly increased in the DSC‐MRI scan performed immediately after SNP administration (1 hour post‐ROSC). SNP administered into the subarachnoid space is expected to readily reach the smooth muscle cells of the pial arterioles through the adventitia of the arterioles. Several studies have suggested that the pial arterioles on the surface of the brain play a key role in determining the CBF. 28 , 29 In a study that investigated cerebral microvascular changes during the first hour after ROSC in rats resuscitated from asphyxia‐induced cardiac arrest, 29 constriction of the pial arterioles, but not of the penetrating arterioles, was observed, suggesting that the pial arteriolar constriction is responsible for the postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion after cardiac arrest.

Several studies have indicated that postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion leads to inadequate substrate delivery for meeting the metabolic requirement, 30 , 31 , 32 thus rendering the brain at risk for secondary ischemic injury. However, in contrast to these studies, 30 , 31 , 32 other studies have suggested that postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion is a consequence of reduced cerebral metabolic requirements following ischemia, 10 , 11 suggesting that postischemic hypoperfusion itself does not contribute to HIBI. In the present study, the CBF improvement by SNP was accompanied by a decreased cerebral lactate/creatine ratio. This finding suggests that in the saline group, cerebral perfusion was diminished to below the threshold level required for fulfilling cerebral oxygen demands, leading to anaerobic metabolism. In contrast, the enhancement in CBF by SNP was associated with reduced anaerobic metabolism. Cerebral lactate, which is a measure of anaerobic metabolic activity, correlates well with the HIBI severity. 33 , 34 Our study, in conjunction with previous research suggesting the beneficial effects of treatments aimed at mitigating cerebral hypoperfusion on neurologic recovery, 12 , 13 , 35 reinforces the notion that postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion is an important therapeutic target for minimizing HIBI following cardiac arrest.

The %thalamic CBF was higher in the SNP group than in the saline group; however, the intergroup difference did not reach statistical significance. In the present study, the SNP was administered into the subarachnoid space over the cerebral cortex. This likely provided a high concentration of SNP to pial arterioles on the surface of cerebral cortex that supply the cerebrum. However, SNP administered in this way might not effectively reach arterioles that supply central structures, such as the thalamus. In the saline group, unlike the CBF measured within the cerebrum, which remained at approximately 50% of the baseline levels until 3.5 hours post‐ROSC, the thalamic CBF recovered to approximately 73% of the baseline level at 3.5 hours post‐ROSC. This finding is in alignment with the reports from several studies that cerebral hypoperfusion was less pronounced in the central region than in the cortical region. 5 , 6 , 36 In the present study, the thalamic lactate/creatine ratio decreased over time even in the saline group, and the thalamic lactate/creatine ratio at 3.5 hours post‐ROSC was similar to that at the baseline. These findings suggest that, in both groups, the thalamic CBF recovered to levels sufficient to meet the thalamic oxygen requirements at 3.5 hours post‐ROSC.

Several questions on the intrathecal SNP administration need to be answered before the application of this treatment to human patients with cardiac arrest. The optimal dose of SNP administered intrathecally has not yet been determined. In the present study, the SNP administration increased CBF above the prearrest baseline level. The same dose of SNP led to an increase in brain tissue oxygen tension above the prearrest baseline level in a previous study. 14 The exposure to supraphysiologic levels of blood flow and oxygen might be detrimental by aggravating reperfusion injury. In contrast, this might be beneficial; supraphysiologic cerebral perfusion may be necessary for neurologic recovery from the ischemic insult caused by cardiac arrest. 13 Moreover, the therapeutic time window for this treatment has not been determined. Postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion occurs within the first hour after ROSC and persists for at least several hours. 37 , 38 , 39 Therefore, it would be desirable to perform this treatment within the first hour after ROSC, preferably before or shortly after the onset of postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion, to minimize the detrimental effects of postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion. However, in real‐world clinical practice, it would be difficult to administer SNP intrathecally within the first few hours after ROSC as multiple life‐saving procedures, such as percutaneous coronary intervention, are performed during this period. Delay in SNP delivery may reduce or extinguish the effectiveness of this novel treatment for reducing HIBI. Further studies addressing these issues are required to determine the optimal treatment protocol for intrathecal SNP administration after cardiac arrest.

This study has several important limitations. First, this study was performed using young minipigs without any known underlying disease. Therefore, our findings may not be directly extrapolatable to human patients with cardiac arrest. Second, we exclusively used female minipigs to mitigate sex‐related differences that could impact the results. 40 Consequently, our findings require validation in samples that include male animals. Third, isoflurane anesthesia was maintained throughout the study, and this might have affected our cerebral measurements. Several studies suggested that isoflurane anesthesia increased CBF and cerebral lactate. 41 , 42 Fourth, although not statistically significant, the SNP group had lower CBF values at the prearrest baseline compared with the saline group. This might have affected the comparisons of the percentages of CBF values between the 2 groups. However, when the measured CBF values were inserted instead of the percentages of the measured values relative to the baseline values in the mixed‐effects models, consistent results (significant group effects for the cortical GM CBF [P=0.007] and subcortical WM CBF [P=0.011]; no significant group effect or group–time interaction for the thalamic CBF) were shown except for the GM+WM CBF (group effect P=0.196 and group–time interaction P=0.460). Fifth, the cerebral measurements were performed solely within the ROI in the parietal lobe and thalamus. The hippocampus, which exhibits particular vulnerability to HIBI, was not evaluated due to its small size, which precluded the placement of our ROI within this structure. Sixth, we did not assess functional neurologic or histologic outcome. Further study is required to determine if the improved CBF after the intrathecal SNP administration leads to improved neurologic recovery.

Conclusions

In the present study that investigated the effects of SNP administered into the subarachnoid space on cerebral perfusion and metabolism using MRI techniques, SNP administration ameliorated hypoperfusion not only in the cortical GM but also in the subcortical WM. The CBF improvement with SNP administration was accompanied by a decrease in cerebral lactate. Thus, SNP administration via intracranial subdural catheters may be an effective means to treat postischemic cerebral hypoperfusion after cardiac arrest.
