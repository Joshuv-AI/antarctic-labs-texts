Exploring internal and external drivers of hydrogen deployment in the energy transition
Authors: Zhiyuan Xie, Ricardo Fernandes, Gorm Bruun Andresen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHydrogen is increasingly recognized as important for deep decarbonization, but its future deployment remains highly uncertain. Here, we extend the open-source PyPSA-Eur model with country-level carbon constraints and simulate European energy transition pathways from 2025 to 2050 under three carbon budgets. We distinguish external drivers, such as hydrogen use in transport and industry, from internal drivers, such as electrolyzer costs, CO2 capture rates, and sequestration availability. Results show that, within our scenario design, internal drivers and carbon budget stringency explain most variation in EU hydrogen deployment. Endogenous 2050 hydrogen demand ranges from nearly zero to 2,200 TWh/year, and widespread green hydrogen uptake could raise demand to 3,200 TWh/year. Blue hydrogen remains more limited, constrained by sequestration scarcity and tighter carbon budgets, implying competition with other hard-to-abate sectors and greater asset-stranding risk. By quantifying the boundaries and potential evolution of hydrogen deployment, this study provides system-level insights beyond simplified hydrogen roadmap assumptions.

Introduction

Hydrogen plays a crucial role in today’s energy landscape and is poised to become even more integral in the future energy transition.1,2,3 Its production can be low-carbon or even zero-carbon, facilitated by carbon capture technologies and electrolysis powered by renewable energy.4 In line with this outlook, the IEA’s Net Zero by 2050 scenario projects that global demand for low-carbon hydrogen could reach around 520 Mt by 2050 (17,300 TWhH2/year, lower heating value (LHV)) Hydrogen mass-to-energy conversions use LHV = 120 GJ/t (≈33.33 MWh/t). USD values are converted to EUR using the 2025 annual average exchange rate (1 EUR = 1.1306 USD), while low-carbon hydrogen and hydrogen-based fuels together supply about 13% of global final energy consumption.5 Moreover, its versatility allows it to be used as a fuel, an energy storage medium, or a feedstock for various industrial processes.6,7 These green and multifunctional attributes reinforce hydrogen’s indispensable role in the energy transition.

Based on the above vision of hydrogen, various sectors of society have shown strong support or increased attention to its development.8,9 For instance, the EU has established ambitious hydrogen targets, aiming to increase its share in Europe’s energy mix from the current 2% to 13–14% by 2050.10 China aims for carbon neutrality by 2060 with initiatives like deploying 50,000 hydrogen fuel-cell vehicles by 2025 and producing up to 200,000 tonnes of green hydrogen annually (≈6.7 TWhH2/year).11 The USA’s Department of Energy targets cost reduction of clean hydrogen to $1/kg by 2030 (≈26.5 EUR/MWhH2), with large investments in research and infrastructure.12 Around the world, large-scale projects for green hydrogen and blue hydrogen production are being developed and gradually coming online.13,14,15,16,17 Additionally, various commercial applications of hydrogen are maturing, particularly in agricultural trucks and long-distance transportation.18

However, despite these ambitious plans, the actual deployment of hydrogen production capacity remains slow. As of 2023, only 7% of the expected global green hydrogen capacity had been realized.19 A key factor behind this slow deployment is the significant disagreement over hydrogen’s role in the energy transition, particularly regarding the external drivers that influence its deployment relative to other energy sources and the extent to which blue hydrogen should be relied upon. While hydrogen’s role has been increasingly acknowledged, some studies still advocate for complete decarbonization through direct electrification.20,21 Recent research, however, highlights hydrogen’s potential in hard-to-decarbonize sectors,22,23 with stakeholders even promoting the broader use of electronic fuels.24,25,26 For instance, Schreyer et al.27 estimate that hydrogen could account for approximately 15% of the final energy share. Blanco et al.25 suggest that the EU’s hydrogen demand could range from 20 to 120 million tons per year (≈670–4,000 TWhH2/year), while Seck et al.28 envision large-scale hydrogen deployment spanning the entire energy transition. These studies establish hydrogen’s role in complementing direct electrification for deep decarbonization, while its final demand remains highly uncertain.

The debate over whether blue or green hydrogen will dominate the future energy transition remains highly controversial.29,30,31,32,33 While numerous studies have examined this topic in depth, their findings often diverge and, in some cases, contradict one another. Since Howarth et al.34 raised concerns about blue hydrogen’s emissions due to upstream methane leakage—further exacerbated by the natural gas price spike in 2022—perceptions of blue hydrogen have become more mixed in recent years. Additional concerns include low carbon capture efficiency across entire plants (typically ranging from 50% to 90%),35,36 long-term dependency on fossil fuels, the risk of stranded assets, and uncertainties regarding the scalability and safety of CCS technologies.37 These challenges have reinforced calls for an accelerated transition to large-scale green hydrogen deployment.38 However, achieving this remains difficult due to the significant renewable energy demand required for green hydrogen production, which may divert resources from other decarbonization efforts.25,38 Additionally, high electrolyzer investment costs and conversion losses along electricity-to-hydrogen pathways (relative to direct electrification) necessitate further technological improvements.39 For example, a recent techno-economic assessment of import supply chains reports present-day delivered green hydrogen costs on the order of 5–9 EUR/kg H2 (≈150–270 EUR/MWhH2) for selected routes to Germany, depending on region and transport vector.40 Collectively, these uncertainties indicate that it is still unclear whether blue or green hydrogen will emerge as the dominant pathway for the future energy transition.

Despite the extensive attention hydrogen has received in the literature, projections of its future demand and deployment pathways vary widely—often shaped by the specific assumptions and modeling frameworks employed in these studies. Many of these studies are based on fixed hydrogen demand or predefined conditions that favor or disadvantage either blue or green hydrogen, significantly influencing their conclusions. Moreover, they often fail to account for the potential large-scale deployment of hydrogen driven by substantial technological advancements. For instance, recent research suggests that electrolyzer costs could follow a trajectory similar to the decline in solar panel costs,41 but this potential has not been fully integrated into energy transition planning. Simply put, comprehensive reviews that systematically examine the various internal drivers influencing blue and green hydrogen deployment remain relatively scarce. While studies such as Ueckerdt42 and Schreyer27 have employed integrated assessment model (IAM) to analyze these factors, IAM frameworks typically rely on more aggregated representations of technologies, geography, and sector coupling. As a result, key drivers are often imposed exogenously or linked through simplified relationships, which can limit the representation of endogenous feedbacks and cross-sectoral resource constraints within an interdependent energy system. Additionally, many studies focus on either a single country or impose a uniform carbon reduction target over an entire region, overlooking the heterogeneity of national decarbonization pathways.

To address these research gaps, this study proposes several targeted approaches. Firstly, we systematically integrate key factors affecting hydrogen deployment and categorize them into two main types: external drivers and internal drivers. External drivers refer to sectoral hydrogen adoption pathways and end-use conditions that influence the overall uptake of hydrogen and hydrogen-based fuels across sectors, without prescribing how hydrogen is produced. They include, for example, the share of hydrogen in land-based transport, the proportion of hydrogen and methanol used in shipping, the share of direct reduced iron (DRI) in steel decarbonization, as well as techno-economic parameters of end-use technologies such as fuel cell truck costs, hydrogen tank storage costs, and competing gas boiler costs. In line with European climate targets and sectoral roadmaps, all external-driver settings assume that heavy-duty road freight, maritime shipping and primary steel production are fully decarbonized by 2050, while the high, moderate, and limited cases differ in the speed at which hydrogen and hydrogen-based fuels scale up along this pathway. Internal drivers, by contrast, are defined as the direct and technology-specific factors that determine the relative competitiveness of green and blue hydrogen supply options and thus shape their respective deployment pathways. For blue hydrogen, key factors include carbon capture efficiency, upstream methane leakage rates, natural gas prices, and carbon storage potential. For green hydrogen, the most critical factors are the expansion rate of renewable energy and electrolyzer costs.

Based on this key classification, we systematically explore the following research questions: (1) How do these drivers influence the timing, scale, and spatial patterns of green, blue, and gray hydrogen deployment across different stages of the transition? (2) How do internal and external drivers shape the scale and sectoral distribution of hydrogen demand in the European energy transition? (3) How does the interplay between hydrogen drivers and carbon budget stringency affect system costs, price stability, sequestration resource allocation, and infrastructure needs? This study provides a comprehensive analysis of hydrogen’s evolving role within Europe’s energy transition pathway, highlighting the critical interactions between policy ambition, technology competitiveness, and cross-sectoral system dynamics.

To account for the interactions between external and internal hydrogen competition under different carbon budget pathways, as well as the heterogeneity of national decarbonization efforts, this study develops an enhanced version of the PyPSA-Eur model that incorporates country-specific carbon constraints. PyPSA-Eur is an open-source, bottom-up energy system model that optimizes the European energy transition while minimizing system costs. The model operates under a myopic foresight approach, simulating the transition from 2025 to 2050 in five-year time steps. This myopic setting is well-suited to studying path dependence by capturing sequential investment decisions and the potential lock-in or stranding of hydrogen assets under tightening constraints, which may be less visible under perfect-foresight planning.43 It represents Europe at the country level (one node per country). Additionally, PyPSA-Eur employs a hybrid approach of exogenous assumptions and endogenous optimization to determine the final hydrogen demand and the corresponding hydrogen infrastructure. For details on hydrogen production and utilization in the model, refer to Section 3 in the methodology.

Each country’s carbon emissions are treated as individual constraints rather than imposing a single EU-wide target. These constraints are defined based on nationally declared decarbonization goals and further adjusted by considering each country’s historical progress and economic development. This approach establishes three distinct carbon reduction pathways with final cumulative carbon budgets of 34 Gt (+1.7 °C), 44 Gt (+1.85 °C), and 56 Gt (+2 °C), allowing for a more refined assessment of the geographical feasibility of hydrogen deployment. To systematically explore hydrogen’s competitive landscape, we classify external drivers, green hydrogen drivers, and blue hydrogen drivers into three levels. The three levels of external competition are high (H), moderate (M), and limited (L). Green hydrogen (G) and blue hydrogen (B) competitiveness are categorized as aggressive (A), target (T), and conservative (C). The specific factors influencing each level of competitiveness are detailed in Section 3 of the methodology. By freely combining different levels of external and internal drivers with the three carbon budget pathways, this study explores a total of 81 scenarios. The scenario naming convention follows the format “External Drivers – Internal Drivers – Carbon Budget”. For example, the “H-GABC-44” scenario represents a case where external drivers are at the High level, green hydrogen drivers are Aggressive, blue hydrogen drivers are Conservative, and the cumulative carbon budget is 44 Gt. Results

Quantifying the impact of internal and external drivers on hydrogen demand

Figure 4 presents hydrogen demand in 2050 across 27 scenarios under a 44 Gt cumulative carbon budget, categorized into Target, Boundary, and Potential groups. The stacked circular bar chart classifies hydrogen demand into two main categories: exogenous hydrogen demand, which is set by sector-specific assumptions, and endogenous hydrogen demand, which is determined by cost optimization and technological feasibility. Two supplemental bar charts further break down these categories. Exogenous hydrogen demand includes hydrogen for industry, methanol production, and shipping. In contrast, endogenous hydrogen demand emerges through system optimization, covering synthetic fuel production, power generation (fuel cell and hydrogen turbine), and methane production. Notably, hydrogen demand is not strictly divided into endogenous and exogenous components. The interaction between exogenous assumptions and endogenous optimization can lead to mixed effects, as discussed in Section 3 of the methodology. Hydrogen’s external drivers are shaped by exogenous hydrogen demand assumptions. However, external drivers extend beyond these assumptions, as they also include cost and technology factors that facilitate hydrogen deployment. Hydrogen demand in 2050 varies significantly across scenarios, ranging from 1,100 TWh/year to 2,200 TWh/year under the 44 Gt carbon reduction pathway. In mass terms, this corresponds to approximately 33–66 Mt H2/year, based on the (LHV, 120 GJ/t). Exogenous hydrogen demand spans from 800 TWh/year to 1,200 TWh/year, increasing in 200 TWh/year increments, and could result in up to 400 TWh/year variation in total hydrogen demand. In contrast, endogenous hydrogen demand fluctuates between 350 TWh/year and 1600 TWh/year.

As illustrated in Figure 4C, a comparison of scenarios with identical internal drivers but varying external drivers reveals that within our scenario design external drivers have minimal influence on endogenous hydrogen demand. Across such scenarios, endogenous hydrogen demand variation remains within 5%, whereas, under the 44 Gt carbon reduction pathway, external drivers can create at most 400 TWh/year of additional hydrogen demand, and variations in internal drivers can drive demand differences of up to 1250 TWh/year—a roughly 3-fold difference. These results indicate that, given our stylized net-zero assumptions for heavy-duty road freight, maritime shipping, and primary steel, internal drivers and carbon budget stringency are the dominant sources of variation in endogenous hydrogen deployment in this scenario set and underscore that, in this context, a favorable external-driver setting alone is insufficient to ensure large-scale hydrogen deployment. Furthermore, the small variation in endogenous hydrogen demand across external-driver cases highlights the limited interaction between internal and external hydrogen drivers under our assumptions. At the same time, our analysis does not consider cases in which these sectors fail to fully decarbonize or adopt hydrogen to the extent assumed here. In such cases, uncertainty in external drivers could lead to much larger changes in total hydrogen demand, potentially comparable to those driven by internal drivers.

Under the 44 Gt carbon budget constraint, the internal drivers for green and blue hydrogen show a complex interplay in shaping endogenous hydrogen demand across sectors. Strong internal drivers for green hydrogen tend to stimulate hydrogen use for synthetic fuel production, which aligns with its role in enabling carbon-neutral liquid fuels. In scenarios where blue hydrogen is least competitive, particularly in the high-natural-gas-price stress-test variant of the Conservative blue-hydrogen setting, hydrogen use for methane synthesis and even for power generation increases sharply. In this variant, fossil methane becomes prohibitively expensive while a residual demand for methane remains, so the model responds by relying more heavily on synthetic methane produced from green hydrogen (and, to a lesser extent, direct hydrogen use in the power sector). This behavior therefore reflects the combined effect of stringent carbon constraints and strongly elevated natural gas prices, rather than a generic property of conservative blue-hydrogen technology assumptions alone. More generally, blue hydrogen alone does not determine the level of hydrogen-based methane synthesis: When green hydrogen remains competitive, the system prefers to split hydrogen roughly between synthetic fuels and methane production. By contrast, when both blue and green hydrogen lose their competitiveness, synthetic fuel production vanishes, and hydrogen use for methane surges. This reflects the inflexible pressure imposed by the 44 Gt carbon budget, which forces the system to meet climate targets through costly substitutions—a dynamic further discussed in Section 2.3.

Hydrogen deployment under internal and external drivers

Figure 5 illustrates the installed capacity, generation, and capacity factor of gray, blue, and green hydrogen across 27 scenarios under a 44 Gt cumulative carbon budget. The heatmaps reveal two critical trends regarding the deployment of green and blue hydrogen. First, green hydrogen emerges as the dominant energy carrier by 2050, with large-scale deployment across all scenarios, regardless of the strength of internal drivers for green and blue hydrogen. Second, blue hydrogen serves as a transitional technology under specific conditions. As shown in Figure 5E, no blue hydrogen deployment is observed in 2050. However, in certain scenarios, blue hydrogen emerges between 2035 and 2045, highlighting its transitional role under the 44 Gt carbon budget. Blue hydrogen primarily appears in scenarios where internal drivers for blue hydrogen are strong, while internal drivers for green hydrogen are weak. In the most extreme case, where blue hydrogen drivers are maximized and green hydrogen drivers are minimized, blue hydrogen production peaks at approximately 120 TWh/year in 2040 but declines sharply to 9.5 TWh/year by 2045. This rapid decline suggests that a significant portion of blue hydrogen capacity constructed in 2040—around 92%—becomes stranded within just five years.

Figure 5 further reveals that within our scenario design, internal drivers of green and blue hydrogen determine the timing and scale of green hydrogen deployment. A closer examination of Figure 5 indicates that when internal drivers for blue hydrogen are weak, green hydrogen is deployed earlier and at larger scales, as the system increasingly relies on green hydrogen and hydrogen-based fuels to deliver deep decarbonization when blue hydrogen is less competitive. Conversely, as internal drivers for blue hydrogen strengthen, the deployment of green hydrogen is delayed, and its scale is reduced. Additionally, Figure 5 shows that, under our assumptions, scenarios sharing the same internal-driver combinations but differing in external drivers remain clustered within the same regions of the deployment space, indicating that external drivers mainly modulate rather than determine the overall hydrogen deployment patterns. This finding aligns with the quantitative results obtained in the previous section.

A key conclusion from Figure 6 is that ensuring at least one of blue hydrogen or green hydrogen retains strong internal drivers is critical for the energy transition. In terms of system costs, comparing across all scenarios, we use the H-GABA-44 scenario—where both internal and external drivers are at their most favorable conditions—as a baseline. The weakening of internal drivers for green hydrogen or blue hydrogen alone leads to an increase in system costs by 10% and 8%, respectively. Notably, when internal drivers for both lose strength simultaneously, total system costs rise by 40%. Similar trends are observed for carbon prices and hydrogen prices. Under the simultaneous weakening of internal drivers for blue and green hydrogen, the average carbon price and hydrogen price increase by 460% and 300%, respectively. Furthermore, when internal drivers for blue hydrogen are strongest, the energy transition becomes reliant on natural gas and necessitates the deployment of negative emissions technologies and carbon capture. This reliance also significantly alleviates the pressure on renewable electricity expansion, reducing renewable capacity investments by approximately 25%. However, the long-term viability of this pathway is also subject to considerable uncertainty, including whether negative emissions technologies like direct air capture can become commercially viable and whether natural gas prices can remain stable over time.

Spatial deployment of hydrogen and its infrastructure implications

Figure 7 presents the geographic distribution of hydrogen production and infrastructure deployment in two representative scenarios: H-GCBA-44, characterized by the strongest internal drivers for blue hydrogen, and H-GABC-44, which drives early and large-scale green hydrogen deployment. Additionally, Figure 7C examines the development of hydrogen transmission pipeline infrastructure under these contrasting scenarios. Overall, green hydrogen tends to concentrate in regions with abundant low-cost renewable electricity for electrolysis, including wind-rich areas (e.g., Denmark and the United Kingdom) and solar-rich areas (e.g., Spain and Italy). By contrast, in our stylized, self-sufficient European system, blue hydrogen is primarily deployed in Spain, with occasional smaller contributions from countries such as Norway and the United Kingdom. This pattern is not driven by exceptional domestic gas production or storage endowments, but by the interaction between heterogeneous country-level carbon budgets and scarce sequestration capacity in the cost-minimizing solution: In our input data, each country must satisfy its own long-term emissions constraint, and the optimization tends to allocate most sequestration potential in central and northern Europe to hard-to-abate industrial processes, aviation, and shipping, while Spain retains some residual allowance for positive emissions in 2050, leading to lower CO2 shadow prices and making blue hydrogen comparatively more attractive there. Hydrogen demand is predominantly concentrated in central Europe (Figures S26–S28), with hydrogen-based re-electrification occurring primarily in high-demand, high-ambition countries such as Germany. Hydrogen transmission infrastructure in Spain and its neighboring regions remains close to the European average, and our assessment of national sequestration capacities indicates that, within this study, Spain’s practical domestic carbon sequestration potential is negligible; blue hydrogen deployment in Spain is therefore not linked to exceptional local infrastructure or storage availability but emerges endogenously from the model’s allocation of scarce sequestration resources under heterogeneous national carbon constraints.Figure 7Spatial hydrogen deployment and infrastructure under contrasting hydrogen pathwaysUnder a 44 Gt cumulative carbon budget, (A and B) show the geographic distribution of hydrogen generation—blue hydrogen for 2035, 2040, and 2045, and green hydrogen for 2025–2050—while (C) presents a dual y axis chart of H2 pipeline capacity (MW) and capital cost (Billion Euro). Across all scenarios, national sequestration capacities are consistently fully utilized. This underscores their role as a scarce and system-critical resource, shared and competitively allocated among various sectors, including hard-to-abate industrial processes, shipping, and aviation, rather than being dedicated to hydrogen production alone. Notably, our results indicate that blue hydrogen generally holds a lower priority in the competition for limited sequestration resources, with other sectors often taking precedence. Regarding infrastructure needs, green hydrogen pathways require a much steeper initial build-out of hydrogen transmission pipelines—approximately 320 GW by 2030, or about three times the level required under blue hydrogen pathways—with comparatively little additional capacity added between 2030 and 2045, and a total of about 460 GW by 2050. In contrast, blue hydrogen scenarios show a more gradual pipeline build-up, reaching approximately 320 GW by 2050, illustrating the higher early-stage infrastructure requirements associated with large-scale green hydrogen deployment.

Decarbonization pathways and hydrogen deployment drivers

The impact of different carbon budgets on hydrogen deployment is substantial. This study examines three carbon budget scenarios: 34 Gt, 44 Gt, and 56 Gt, and finds that stricter carbon constraints lead to higher hydrogen demand, a stronger reliance on green hydrogen, reduced fossil fuel lock-in, and an increased need for hydrogen infrastructure. By 2050, total hydrogen demand peaks at 3,100 TWh/year (≈93 Mt H2/year), 2600 TWh/year (≈78 Mt H2/year), and 2100 TWh/year (≈63 Mt H2/year) for the 34 Gt, 44 Gt, and 56 Gt pathways, respectively, with average annual hydrogen demand following the same pattern, decreasing from 2300 TWh/year to 1960 TWh/year to 1,230 TWh/year. Blue hydrogen retains its transitional role in the 34 Gt and 44 Gt scenarios, appearing primarily between 2035 and 2045, while under the 56 Gt scenario, it remains deployed even in 2050. However, it does not play a dominant role, with gray and green hydrogen continuing to provide the majority of the hydrogen supply in this pathway. Gray hydrogen will only be fully phased out by 2050 in the most stringent 34 Gt scenario.

The carbon budget and the internal drivers for hydrogen are in delicate interplay, together reconfiguring hydrogen’s role across the energy system. While the previous discussion focused on the 44 Gt pathway, Figures S7 and S8 illustrate how this interplay shifts under different carbon constraints. For example, under more stringent budgets (e.g., 34 Gt), green hydrogen is increasingly directed toward synthetic fuel production, whereas under looser constraints (e.g., 56 Gt), the same green hydrogen drivers do not necessarily lead to significant uptake. Likewise, the consequences of weak blue hydrogen drivers differ across carbon targets, depending on the extent to which the natural gas system remains viable. These observations highlight that hydrogen pathways emerge from a coupled system response to both policy ambition and technology competitiveness.

Despite variations in total hydrogen deployment, the geographic distribution of hydrogen production remains largely unchanged across different carbon budgets. In our results, Spain emerges as the primary location for blue hydrogen production under certain scenarios, not due to domestic sequestration availability or superior infrastructure, but rather as a result of its relatively relaxed carbon reduction target. However, under more ambitious carbon budgets (e.g., 34 Gt), blue hydrogen deployment in Spain diminishes, reflecting the broader system trend where limited sequestration capacity is increasingly prioritized for hard-to-abate sectors beyond hydrogen production. Similarly, regions rich in wind resources (e.g., Denmark and the United Kingdom) and/or solar resources (e.g., Spain and Italy) remain key hubs for green hydrogen production. On the economic side, a stricter carbon budget results in a higher energy transition cost, with the system’s average cost increasing by approximately 12.5% for each level of carbon reduction target. Regardless of the carbon reduction target, maintaining the competitiveness of at least one hydrogen pathway—either blue or green—is crucial. Scenarios where both internal drivers for blue and green hydrogen are weak show a sharp surge in both carbon and hydrogen prices. Notably, the demand for hydrogen transmission infrastructure remains largely unchanged between the 44 Gt and 34 Gt pathways, while a more conservative 56 Gt carbon budget leads to a 50% reduction in infrastructure needs, reflecting the possibility of the EU shifting from its current carbon reduction targets to more ambitious goals.

In this context, Europe will consistently benefit from strengthening internal drivers for green hydrogen, as this enhances system flexibility, reduces reliance on scarce sequestration resources, supports deep sector coupling, and helps mitigate the risk of carbon and hydrogen price volatility.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the role of hydrogen in Europe’s energy transition by systematically analyzing how internal and external drivers, carbon budget stringency, and cross-sectoral resource constraints shape hydrogen demand, deployment timing, and spatial patterns. By leveraging a bottom-up optimization framework and explicitly incorporating country-level carbon budgets, our work offers a perspective on the complex interdependencies governing hydrogen’s evolution. In the following discussion, we reflect on how these findings complement and extend existing literature, explore the implications for hydrogen’s competitiveness and system costs, and assess their relevance for infrastructure planning and policy development.

Hydrogen demand in context

The discussion on hydrogen demand can be divided into two parts: direct hydrogen use and total hydrogen demand. This distinction arises because some energy models lack sector coupling capabilities. Let us first focus on direct hydrogen use. Van der Zwaan et al.44 examined global hydrogen demand using eight IAM models and found that direct hydrogen use accounts for approximately 6% of global final energy demand, which is broadly consistent in magnitude with our model results for Europe, where direct hydrogen use represents only a few percent of final energy demand. This comparison serves as an external point of reference for the order of magnitude of direct hydrogen use, and motivates our use of an exogenous hydrogen-demand share in the external-competition setting as a scenario input for benchmarking. Additionally, our study highlights that hydrogen’s role in electrification-based applications in Europe only emerges when internal drivers for both green and blue hydrogen are weak. Regarding total hydrogen demand, van der Zwaan et al. provided a rough estimate that e-fuel demand is about twice direct hydrogen use, and many studies still argue that direct hydrogen use should outweigh hydrogen-based synthetic liquid fuels. Oshiro and Fujimori45 similarly find, in a global context, that synfuels account for roughly half of hydrogen-based energy carriers in 2050, implying an approximate 1:1 relationship between e-fuels and direct hydrogen use. Our study challenges this perspective for Europe. In short, e-fuel demand in our European scenarios is highly sensitive to the carbon reduction pathway and variation in internal drivers, ranging from about 1.2 times to as much as 4 times that of direct hydrogen use, suggesting that e-fuels can play an even more significant role than direct hydrogen consumption in Europe’s energy transition.

For final hydrogen demand in 2050, our findings align closely with those of Schreyer et al.,27 who estimated a European range of 1,000–2,600 TWh/year, whereas our study suggests 1,300–2,700 TWh/year under Europe’s net-zero target for 2050 (approximately 40–80 Mt H2/year). Blanco25 and Seck et al.28 project that European hydrogen demand could reach up to about 120 Mt H2/year by 2050, while the upper bound across our scenarios is around 100 Mt H2/year. From a global perspective, the IEA’s Net Zero by 2050 scenario and the analysis of Oshiro and Fujimori both indicate that hydrogen and hydrogen-based fuels together supply on the order of 5–15% of global final energy demand by mid-century.5,45 By contrast, Schreyer et al.27 and our study suggest that the share of hydrogen and hydrogen-based fuels in final energy in Europe is likely to be substantially higher than this global average. Moreover, several studies highlight that the stringency of climate targets is a key driver of hydrogen demand: Stricter cumulative carbon budgets or more ambitious climate policies systematically increase the share of hydrogen in final energy.45,46 This pattern is consistent with our European results, where more stringent carbon reduction pathways systematically push up final hydrogen demand.

Beyond these comparisons, it is important to clarify how the treatment of negative emission technologies affects hydrogen demand. In the PyPSA-Eur setup used here, both BECCS and DAC are available based on techno-economic data from the Danish Energy Agency. For DAC, investment costs decline from 7,000,000 to 4,000,000 EUR/(tCO2/h) between 2025 and 2050, which corresponds to levelized capital costs of roughly 195–110 EUR/tCO2 under standard assumptions (4% discount rate, 25-year lifetime, 30% capacity factor), before adding operating and energy costs. BECCS technologies are parameterized with similarly optimistic cost assumptions, but sustainable biomass and CO2 storage are constrained by explicit potential caps, so both BECCS and DAC deploy only at modest levels across the scenarios. Consequently, the relatively high hydrogen and e-fuel demands in the results arise not from excluding negative emission technologies, but from their competition for limited biomass and storage resources and their still-limited techno-economic competitiveness under the given assumptions.

Competition between blue and green hydrogen

Regarding the competition between blue hydrogen and green hydrogen in the future energy transition, different studies have provided varying answers depending on their assumptions. For instance, George et al.33 argue that blue hydrogen could be a deeply relied-upon technology in Germany, while Alhumaidan et al.47 even suggest that blue hydrogen is essential for the energy transition. However, these studies often overlook methane emissions or place excessive confidence in carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technology, despite persistent uncertainties around the long-term integrity and governance of CO2 storage (e.g., monitoring and verification requirements, long-term liability, and the risk—however low—of leakage over multi-decadal horizons). Ueckerdt et al.42 evaluated a broad range of parameters influencing blue and green hydrogen deployment within a comprehensive framework. Their findings indicate that blue hydrogen and green hydrogen deployment are highly sensitive to parameter assumptions, and blue hydrogen can only maintain a long-term open deployment window under optimistic conditions. Our study reaches a similar conclusion: When blue hydrogen loses competitiveness, it may become entirely unviable for deployment. Furthermore, within a cost-optimal energy transition pathway, we explore the feasibility of both blue and green hydrogen deployment and their impact on final hydrogen demand and the broader energy transition. We find that under a 56 Gt carbon budget, blue hydrogen could serve as a long-term solution, but only if it maintains at least moderate competitiveness—this requires low natural gas prices, an upstream methane leakage rate below 1.5%, and a carbon capture rate above 90%. Under Europe’s targeted carbon budget or a highly ambitious carbon budget, blue hydrogen could function as a bridging technology, but this would necessitate the strongest internal drivers while also facing the risk of stranded assets. Additionally, our study finds that across all carbon budget scenarios, if blue hydrogen is highly competitive, it leads to a reduction in final hydrogen demand and maintain reliance on gray hydrogen and fossil fuels. Interestingly, the European Commission released a communication on the hydrogen strategy roadmap, projecting that hydrogen demand will increase to 13–14% by 2050 and acknowledging the transitional role of blue hydrogen.48 In our study, under the same carbon emission targets and scenarios where blue hydrogen acts as a bridging technology, the final hydrogen demand is around 1,300–1,500 TWh/year, aligning with the EU’s plan.

System implications of large-scale green deployment

While green hydrogen is widely regarded as a key pillar of deep decarbonization, its large-scale deployment presents significant challenges for the energy transition, particularly in its early stages. In scenarios that fully rely on green hydrogen, the required capacity of renewable energy generators in the early phase is twice that of scenarios relying on blue hydrogen, while the capacity requirement for hydrogen transmission networks is 2–3 times higher. Additionally, the energy transition cost remains 10–15% higher from 2025 to 2050. This underscores the need for greater early-stage investments and policy support when the EU pursues more ambitious carbon reduction targets or aims for greater green hydrogen deployment. Interestingly, although the overall capacity of the hydrogen transmission network increases with large-scale green hydrogen deployment, the pipeline capacity involving Spain decreases significantly. Under the 2050 net-zero target, this capacity drops from 12 GW to 8 GW.

Hydrogen cost and price uncertainty

There is also considerable uncertainty regarding the future costs and prices of hydrogen. Forward-looking syntheses suggest that this uncertainty is likely to persist by mid-century: A recent review of 89 projections49 reports a median 2050 electrolysis LCOH of about 2.7 EUR/kg H2, with estimates spanning roughly 0.36 to more than 10 EUR/kg H2 and favorable regional or technology averages around 1.8–2.0 EUR/kg H2. The IEA likewise highlights that renewable hydrogen remains significantly more expensive than unabated fossil-based hydrogen and that costs are highly sensitive to electricity prices and location,50 while many studies treat power prices, curtailment, duty cycles, storage, and network access exogenously, contributing to wide cost dispersion. Expressed per kilogram of hydrogen (assuming an LHV of 33.3 kWh/kg), the endogenous 2050 hydrogen marginal prices in our scenarios fall broadly within these ranges but show a strong dependence on carbon budget stringency and internal drivers: Under the 44 Gt and 56 Gt carbon budgets, prices mostly lie between 1.4 and 6.7 EUR/kg H2, while only under the most stringent 34 Gt pathway do unfavorable internal drivers (e.g., high natural gas prices and limited CO2 storage) push 2050 hydrogen prices up to about 16.7 EUR/kg H2, which should be interpreted as an upper-bound stress test rather than a central cost expectation.

Limitations of the study

Our study provides a detailed investigation into the external and internal drivers of hydrogen, examining how these dynamics influence the spatial and temporal deployment of blue and green hydrogen and their impact on the energy transition. However, several limitations remain: (1) Cost-minimization approach—our model minimizes system costs, a widely used methodology, but one that does not fully capture real-world decision-making processes, where factors such as market uncertainty and risk aversion play a crucial role. Beyond a single least-cost solution, future work could explore (1) near-optimal solution spaces (i.e., alternative solutions within a small cost tolerance of the optimum) and (2) stochastic optimization approaches that explicitly represent uncertainty, to assess the robustness of the competition between green and blue hydrogen under alternative decision-making and uncertainty representations. (2) Lack of explicit policy modeling—our study does not explicitly model policy interventions, such as green hydrogen certification, carbon pricing, and subsidies, all of which could significantly influence hydrogen deployment trajectories. Integrating such mechanisms into future research would provide a more policy-relevant perspective. (3) Assumption of a closed European energy system—our model assumes that Europe operates as a self-contained system, excluding potential hydrogen imports from North Africa or green methanol imports from China, both of which could serve as long-term energy storage solutions. Incorporating international trade could alleviate decarbonization pressures and reshape hydrogen deployment patterns. (4) Ambitious sectoral decarbonization assumptions—our scenarios assume that heavy-duty road freight, maritime shipping and primary steel production are fully decarbonized by 2050, with hydrogen and hydrogen-based fuels playing a major role in these sectors even in the “Limited” external-driver cases. In reality, adoption levels in these sectors could remain substantially below the lower bounds assumed here. In such cases, uncertainty in external drivers could induce much larger changes in total hydrogen demand than those explored in our scenario set, potentially making the relative importance of external versus internal drivers more balanced than suggested by our results. Addressing these limitations in future research would enhance the robustness and practical relevance of our conclusions.

Method details

Model description

PyPSA-Eur is an open-source model designed to optimize the capacity and dispatch of generation, storage, and transmission to minimize costs. It can address energy and feedstock demands in the electricity, heating, transport, and industry sectors. In this study, we use the model to simulate the transformation of the European energy system from 2025 to 2050, with a five-year time step and a temporal resolution of 3 h, while each country is represented as a single node geographically. The optimization follows a myopic approach, where each time step builds upon the previous one, considering existing capacities and past investment decisions. This method allows for a more realistic and dynamically evolving energy transition. For detailed information on energy flows and the single-node energy model, including comprehensive descriptions of each sector and the mathematical formulations, please refer to Methods S1. PyPSA-Eur has been widely used and validated in various energy system studies. However, many previous applications imposed a single CO2 emission cap for all of Europe, neglecting the heterogeneity in national economic development levels and decarbonization commitments. This led to unrealistically carbon reduction requirements for certain countries.

To address these limitations, we employ a version of PyPSA-Eur that incorporates country-specific CO2 emission targets, ensuring a more realistic and policy-aligned representation of decarbonization efforts across Europe. This study considers three carbon budget pathways—34 Gt, 44 Gt, and 56 Gt—which determine the cumulative CO2 emissions allowed until 2050. These pathways reflect different levels of climate ambition, with 34 Gt representing an aggressive decarbonization trajectory (Proactive), 44 Gt aligning with current policy targets (Target), and 56 Gt corresponding to a more gradual transition (Conservative). The 44 Gt “Target” pathway is derived from publicly stated national decarbonization targets and is therefore intended to reflect the current European climate-governance context. For EU member states, it is broadly consistent with the direction of the Fit-for-55 package, which operationalizes the EU-wide 2030 ambition across member states. The 44 Gt pathway is derived from the 2030 and 2050 decarbonization commitments of 34 European countries, where, for nations with undefined 2050 targets, we assume a 40% reduction relative to their 2030 goals (expressed as a fraction of 1990 emissions). For instance, if a country aims to reduce its 1990 emissions to 60% by 2030, its 2050 target is assumed to be 20%. The 34 Gt (Proactive) pathway imposes stricter near-term carbon reductions and requires net-zero emissions by 2050 across all countries. Conversely, the 56 Gt (Conservative) pathway allows more flexibility by relaxing decarbonization constraints for high-emission countries, those further from their targets, or those with below-average per capita incomes. Figure S4 illustrates these pathways, where six major economies—including Germany, Italy, and Spain—account for approximately 65% of the total carbon budget. For details on carbon flows in the model, including CCUS, refer to Figure S3 in Methods S1.

Hydrogen production and usage

Hydrogen is a crucial energy carrier that links multiple sectors of the energy system. In the model used in this paper, hydrogen can originate from fossil sources to generate gray hydrogen, where it can be combined with carbon capture to produce blue hydrogen, or it can be generated green hydrogen via electrolysis. The model also allows hydrogen to be utilized as a raw feedstock or energy carrier across the power, heat, transportation, and industrial sectors.

In the power sector, hydrogen can be supplied by fuel cells or used to generate electricity through modified OCGT or CCGT plants. In the heat sector, the model provides options to retrofit boilers to burn hydrogen for heat production or to use hydrogen to produce synthetic natural gas, which can then provide heat through CHP systems or boilers. Notably, CHP systems also generate electricity. The model assumes that CHP operates with backpressure, with a fixed ratio of electricity to heat output. The use of hydrogen in the power and heat sectors is endogenously optimized, meaning the model determines the optimal usage.

In the transportation sector, the model divides transportation into land transport, shipping, and aviation. For land transport, the model assumes that long-distance trucks will use hydrogen for decarbonization. For passenger vehicles, two decarbonization options are provided: battery electric vehicles (BEVs) and fuel cell vehicles (FCVs). The shift in the proportion of BEVs and FCVs by mid-century is an exogenous assumption. In the shipping industry, the model offers hydrogen and methanol as decarbonization options, both of which are also exogenously determined. However, it is important to note that in this model, hydrogen is a potential feedstock for the green production of methanol. In the aviation sector, kerosene is consumed, either synthetically produced or derived from fossil sources, with hydrogen being one of the raw materials for synthetic kerosene.

In the industrial sector, hydrogen has a variety of applications, such as producing iron through direct reduction, creating liquid hydrocarbons via the Fischer-Tropsch process, or synthesizing methane, ammonia, and methanol. Methane can also serve as a link between the power and heat sectors. It is important to note that while the proportion of hydrogen used in the direct reduction of iron is an exogenous assumption, the other processes are endogenously optimized by the model.

By integrating these processes, it becomes evident that the model also involves a mixture of endogenous optimization and exogenous assumptions. For instance, the demand for oil-based products in land transportation is an exogenous assumption, but these products may be green oil-based products derived from hydrogen, which is endogenously optimized by the model. Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative results in this study are obtained from deterministic capacity-expansion and dispatch optimization using the enhanced PyPSA-Eur model under predefined scenario assumptions. The scenario set comprises 81 scenarios, formed by combining three external-driver levels, three green-hydrogen internal-driver levels, three blue-hydrogen internal-driver levels, and three carbon budget pathways. Scenario comparisons, rather than statistical hypothesis testing, are used to assess how hydrogen drivers and carbon budget stringency affect hydrogen demand, deployment, system costs, carbon prices, hydrogen marginal prices, and infrastructure requirements.

No experimental sample size, randomization, blinding, p values, confidence intervals, standard deviations, standard errors, or formal statistical tests are applicable. Where relevant, the value of n corresponds to the number of modeled scenarios, countries, technologies, or model years represented in the corresponding figure, table, or legend. For comparisons under a single carbon budget pathway, n = 27 scenarios; for the full scenario ensemble, n = 81 scenarios; and for country-level distributions, n corresponds to the 34 modeled European countries. Hydrogen marginal prices are calculated from the time-averaged marginal prices of hydrogen buses, while carbon prices are derived from the shadow prices of country-level carbon constraints. Hydrogen mass-to-energy conversions use the lower heating value of hydrogen, with 33.3 kWh/kg H2, equivalent to 120 GJ/t H2. Data processing and visualization were performed using Python scripts, and the relevant software, data, and code resources are listed in the key resources table and the data and code availability section.
