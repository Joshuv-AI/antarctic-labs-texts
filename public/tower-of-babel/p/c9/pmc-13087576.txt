Perceptual organization and its relationship to functioning in patients with chronic schizophrenia
Authors: Sang Kyoung Kim, Jungsun Lee, Sung Woo Joo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the associations between perceptual organization (PO), other neurocognitive domains, and general functioning in patients with chronic schizophrenia.MethodPO was assessed using the Rey–Osterrieth Complex Figure (ROCF) fragmentation score, and a battery of neurocognitive tests was administered to 53 clinically stable patients with chronic schizophrenia. Correlation and exploratory factor analyses were conducted to examine the relationships between PO and other neurocognitive domains. Correlation, partial correlation, and multiple linear regression analyses were performed to investigate the associations between cognitive variables and general functioning, measured by the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) score.ResultsIn the exploratory factor analysis, the ROCF fragmentation score was significantly correlated with the ROCF copy score and loaded on the same factor as the ROCF copy, Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT), and Stroop test scores. Both ROCF fragmentation and AVLT scores were positively correlated with the GAF score. However, after adjusting for overall psychiatric severity, as measured by the Clinical Global Impression–Overall score, only the ROCF fragmentation score remained significantly associated with functioning in the partial correlation and multiple linear regression analyses.ConclusionsPO, as measured by the ROCF fragmentation score, is associated with general functioning in patients with chronic schizophrenia and may partly explain the variability in functioning among patients.Keywords: Chronic schizophrenia, Perceptual organization, Neurocognition, Rey–Osterrieth Complex Figure test, Functioning

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe mental illness characterized by abnormalities in thought, perception, and behavior, commonly manifesting through clinical symptoms such as hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized speech. These symptoms often result in significant impairments in social and occupational functioning (Andreasen, 1995). Chronic schizophrenia is associated with recurrent relapses and necessitates long-term treatment. Despite reintegration into the community, patients with chronic schizophrenia frequently experience persistent social and occupational challenges (Vargas et al., 2014). The disorder is highly complex and heterogeneous, with varying degrees of symptom severity and functional impairment among patients (Millan et al., 2014). In particular, cognitive function deficits have been identified as critical predictors of social functioning and overall quality of life (Addington and Addington, 2000). These findings highlight the need to investigate the variability in cognitive function deficits in chronic schizophrenia to inform the development of personalized treatment strategies for patients with schizophrenia.

Neurocognition (NC) is an essential cognitive process that facilitates learning, academic achievement, mental well-being, and the ability to engage in adaptive and goal-directed behaviors (Rabinovici et al., 2015). These higher-order cognitive abilities facilitate self-regulation, problem-solving, and the execution of complex tasks necessary for effective functioning in various domains of life. Patients with chronic schizophrenia exhibit impairments across various NC domains, including processing speed, attention, working memory, verbal and visual learning, and reasoning and problem-solving (Nuechterlein et al., 2004). Impairments in NC are associated with several demographic and clinical factors, including age, depressive symptoms, negative symptoms, and the dosage of antipsychotic medication (O'Grada and Dinan, 2007). These impairments are also associated with functional outcomes, such as employment status (Kessler et al., 2007; Penadés et al., 2010) and overall well-being (Tolman and Kurtz, 2012). Several studies have emphasized the critical role of NC impairments in functional deficits and poor outcomes in chronic schizophrenia (Green et al., 2000), with these cognitive impairments potentially reflecting the direct effects of the disease process (Moritz et al., 2021). However, the extent to which these impairments contribute to the variability in functional outcomes remains unclear. This is because previous research on this issue is limited by the inconsistent methodologies and neuropsychological tests used across studies. Additionally, there is limited empirical evidence on validating changes in cognitive function and tracking symptom fluctuations over time in individuals with schizophrenia (Lim et al., 2021).

Perceptual organization (PO), defined as the cognitive process by which visual information is structured into coherent patterns, such as groups, contours, perceptual wholes, and object representations (Van der Helm, 2012), has also been identified as a key feature of schizophrenia. PO is typically disrupted in schizophrenia and is linked to impairments in early-stage perceptual processing, rather than in complex or higher-order cognitive functions (Silverstein and Keane, 2011). Previous findings have shown that deficits in PO are associated with disorganized thought (Silverstein et al., 2000), negative symptoms (Kéri et al., 2005), and cognitive impairment (Olivier et al., 2017). Furthermore, PO deficits are related specifically in patients with a history of poor, but not good, premorbid social functioning among those with schizophrenia (Phillips and Singer, 1997). Patients with deficits in PO tend to focus on the detailed features of an object, particularly showing difficulty in perceiving the object as a whole structure (Silverstein et al., 2000; Uhlhaas et al., 2006). Parnas et al. (2001) administered the PO test to patients with chronic schizophrenia, those with first-episode schizophrenia, and a high-risk group with prodromal symptoms. This study showed that the chronic patients performed poorly on the PO task, while those with prodromal symptoms showed relatively better performance. These findings suggest that PO function may vary across different stages of schizophrenia. In addition, PO has been found to vary by sex among patients with schizophrenia (Joseph et al., 2013), suggesting that PO deficits may present differently among patients with schizophrenia. Although it is plausible that PO indirectly influences the course of schizophrenia over time, its contribution to changes in functioning in chronic schizophrenia remains unclear.

Among the various tools that measure PO, the Rey–Osterrieth Complex Figure (ROCF; Rey, 1941) test, originally developed as a neuropsychological assessment to evaluate cognitive functions such as attention, non-verbal memory, and planning and organization (Lezak et al., 2012), has been widely utilized in clinical settings. Specifically, the ROCF fragmentation score was proposed to measure PO by assessing the extent to which participants perceive a stimulus in a gestalt manner. A low fragmentation score indicates a fragmented approach to the figure, characterized by poorly connected elements (Stern et al., 1999). During the test, participants are asked to reproduce a complex figure, and they often focus on key features or interpret the figure as a meaningful gestalt (Johnson et al., 2005; Inami and Kirino, 2004).

Numerous studies have provided evidence linking PO with various cognitive domains in schizophrenia, including social cognition (McCleery et al., 2020), working memory (Woodman et al., 2003), problem-solving (Kahneman and Henik, 2017), and verbal learning (Stothers and Klein, 2010). A previous study suggested that PO and processing speed may represent distinct cognitive domains in schizophrenia, based on their differing correlations with activity in the lateral occipital complex, which is involved in visual and organizational processing (Sehatpour et al., 2010). Another study reported that patients with schizophrenia have difficulty memorizing and recalling a given figure using the PO strategy, suggesting that PO and working memory may function independently (Tek et al., 2002). In chronic schizophrenia, cognitive heterogeneity may manifest within specific domains or across multiple domains, resulting in diverse patterns of performance on various cognitive tests (Kuperberg and Heckers, 2000). PO may contribute to the cognitive heterogeneity in chronic schizophrenia, either as a distinct cognitive domain or in interaction with other domains. It also remains unclear to what extent impairments in PO are associated with functional outcomes in patients with chronic schizophrenia.

To address this gap, we aimed to examine PO in patients with chronic schizophrenia, using the ROCF fragmentation score. Specifically, we investigated the relationships between ROCF fragmentation scores and other NC test scores and evaluated whether the ROCF fragmentation score was associated with general functioning in patients with chronic schizophrenia.

Methods

Participants

We recruited patients with chronic schizophrenia from the Asan Medical Center, a university-affiliated hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a diagnosis of schizophrenia based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), and (2) an illness duration exceeding 5 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients <20 or >80 years, (2) recent substance abuse within the last 6 months, (3) head injury causing unconsciousness for >3 min, and (4) a history of neurological or physical conditions that may affect brain function. A total of 53 patients with chronic schizophrenia were included in this study. Our study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (IRB No. 2021–1128, 2022-1193).

Assessments of clinical symptoms and NC functions

Demographic information, including marital status and educational level, was collected from all participants. The severity of psychiatric symptoms was measured using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS; Kay et al., 1989) and the Clinical Global Impression (CGI; Guy, 1976). Daily functioning was evaluated by using the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) scale (Endicott et al., 1976) and social functioning was assessed using the Personal and Social Performance Scale (PSP; Morosini et al., 2000).

To assess NC function, we administered a battery of NC tests to all the participants. According to the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB; Nuechterlein et al., 2008), we selected an individual NC test according to each cognitive domain in the MCCB. Processing speed was assessed using the phonemic fluency test, in which participants were asked to generate as many words as possible within a set time limit, based on specific starting phonemes (e.g., A, B, C) as prompts (Spreen and Benton, 1969). Attention and vigilance were assessed using the Stroop test (MacLeod, 1992), which quantifies the number of color stimuli correctly identified within a restricted time. Working memory was evaluated using the number-letter sequencing test from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS–IV; Wechsler, 1997), in which the participants had to recall increasingly complex sequences over 10 trials. Verbal learning was assessed using the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT), in which participants were required to recall a list of 15 words and perform a delayed recognition task, with the total AVLT score recorded for analysis (Rey, 1958). Reasoning and problem-solving were measured using the five-point test, where participants were instructed to connect patterns of five dots into different shapes within a time limit (Regard et al., 1982).

PO and visual learning were measured using the Korean version of the ROCF test and the Complex Figure Test from the Rey–Kim Memory Test (Kim, 2001; Rey, 1941). The participants were asked to copy the figure and then reproduce it immediately from memory, and again after a 20-min delay. The ROCF fragmentation test assessed participants' ability to perceive an integrated figure in a gestalt manner, in which PO was measured by the first session of drawing the ROCF figure given to the participants. Visual learning was measured using the ROCF copy test, which involved reproducing a given figure immediately after the initial trial. Scoring for the ROCF test was conducted according to the Boston Qualitative Scoring System (Stern et al., 1999).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 25.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). We set an alpha value of 0.05 for the statistical significance. Continuous or categorical variables in the demographic and clinical characteristics were presented as means and standard deviations or numbers and percentages, respectively. We performed Pearson's correlation analyses to investigate the relationships between the ROCF fragmentation score and the other NC test scores. Exploratory factor analysis was conducted to examine the factorial structures of the ROCF fragmentation and other NC test scores. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) index and the Bartlett test were used to evaluate the suitability of the models. Robust maximum likelihood estimation (RML) and direct oblimin methods were used to extract the factors. A factor loading value over an absolute value of 0.4 was applied to identify independent factors (Tabachnick and Fidell, 2007).

We performed Pearson's correlation analysis to determine the relationship between ROCF fragmentation and the other NC test scores on the GAF score. To account for the effects of overall clinical symptoms on functioning, we included the CGI-overall score as a mediator in partial correlation analyses for the correlations of ROCF fragmentation and NC test scores with the GAF score. Furthermore, we performed multiple linear regression analyses to more rigorously adjust the covarying effects on the GAF score. All NC test scores were included as independent variables, with covariates of age, sex, illness duration, education attainment, and the CGI-overall score for the dependent variable of the GAF score. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were used to assess multicollinearity between the explanatory variables based on a criterion of a value greater than 10.

Exploratory factor analyses were conducted to reveal the factorial structures between ROCF fragmentation and NC test scores (Table 3). The KMO measure of sampling adequacy was 0.637, indicating a mediocre level of adequacy. Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (χ2 (21) = 40.061, p < 0.01), suggesting that the correlation matrix was not an identity matrix and the factor analysis was appropriate for the data. We identified three distinct factors based on an eigenvalue of 0.4. The first factor, which explained 15.15% of the total variance, included the WAIS–number-letter test. The second factor, which accounted for 20.43% of the total variance, included the five-point test. The third factor comprised ROCF fragmentation, the Stroop test, AVLT, and ROCF copy, accounting for 11.64% of the total variance. These three factors explained 47.22% of the total variance.

Associations of the ROCF fragmentation and other NC test scores with the GAF score

We explored the impact of ROCF fragmentation and NC test scores on the GAF score using correlation, partial correlation, and multiple linear regression analyses (Table 4). ROCF fragmentation (r = 0.342, p = 0.012) and AVLT scores (r = 0.273, p = 0.048) were positively correlated with the GAF score. In the partial correlation analyses, the ROCF fragmentation score was the only variable associated with the GAF score (r = 0.339, p = 0.014). Multiple linear regression models were adopted to adjust the covarying effects on the GAF score more rigorously. The VIF score for all the independent variables was below 10. The ROCF fragmentation score was positively associated with the GAF score (β = 0.199, p = 0.028). However, other NC test scores were not significantly associated with the GAF score.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the relationship between the ROCF fragmentation and other NC test scores in patients with chronic schizophrenia. The ROCF fragmentation score had a significant relationship with the ROCF copy score. Exploratory factor analysis showed the three factors of the ROCF fragmentation and other NC test scores. The ROCF fragmentation score was included in the third factor, along with the Stroop, AVLT, and ROCF copy scores. As for associations of the ROCF fragmentation and other NC test scores with the GAF score, the ROCF fragmentation score was the only variable positively associated with the GAF score.

First, we observed a significant relationship between the ROCF fragmentation and ROCF copy scores. Previous studies suggested that deficit of memory in patients with schizophrenia could be affected by insufficient organizational processing, reporting that patients with gestalt learning showed higher scores in copy and memory compared to patients with a fragmented perception (Cirillo and Seidman, 2003; Kalinowski et al., 2003; Rempfer et al., 2012). It is consistent with the suggestion that PO plays a role in organizing visual components into a meaningful subject in intermediate stage of processing (Kurylo et al., 2018). In addition, significant correlations were observed among ROCF copy, AVLT, Stroop, and WAIS-number-letter, which is in line with previous reports that neurocognitive domains within the MCCB are interrelated (Nuechterlein et al., 2004). Overall, these results indicate that each cognitive task partly involves the functions of other NC areas. Accordingly, exploratory factor analysis was conducted to explore the structural relationship between ROCF fragmentation and NC tests.

Exploratory factor analysis of the ROCF fragmentation score and other NC test scores revealed a three-factor structure, with the ROCF fragmentation score loading on the same factor as the ROCF copy, AVLT, and Stroop test scores. Our results may be related to how long each patient was exposed to the given stimulus. The WAIS–number-letter test is a representative short-term and working memory test (Egeland, 2015) that requires participants to remember and appropriately order stimuli given by researchers within a limited time. The five-point test also requires a high level of short-term and working memory functions (Tucha et al., 2012), as participants must fill a sheet containing 40 squares of five dots by connecting at least two dots in each square.

However, the Stroop test is a simple color-identification task, allowing participants to respond more easily. In the AVLT, the stimulus words were repeated five times by the researcher, which may have increased the participants' comfort and familiarity during task performance. The ROCF fragmentation and copy tests also allowed the participants to complete the copying tasks without time pressure. Compared to the WAIS–number-letter and five-point tests, these tests may have required relatively lower levels of working memory. Taken together, our results may reflect the influence of stimulus exposure time and the demands on working memory during the test performance. Nevertheless, considering the modest sample size (N = 53) and the KMO value of 0.637, the EFA results should be interpreted tentatively. The three factors explained only 47.22% of the total variance, and the variables loaded on the same factor may separate into distinct factors in a larger sample.

Regarding associations of the ROCF fragmentation and other NC scores with the GAF score, we conducted correlation, partial correlation, and multiple linear regression analyses. While both the ROCF fragmentation and AVLT scores were positively correlated with the GAF score, only the ROCF fragmentation score remained a significant predictor when accounting for the CGI-Overall score as a mediator, reflecting overall psychiatric symptom severity. The association between the ROCF fragmentation and GAF scores was further supported by multiple linear regression analyses.

According to the Cognitive Neuroscience Treatment Research to Improve Cognition in Schizophrenia (CNTRICS) initiative (Butler et al., 2008), perceptual organization operates at an earlier processing level distinct from the higher-order neurocognitive domains included in the MCCB. Consistent with this perspective, previous studies have reported that patients with chronic schizophrenia tend to focus on detailed features, resulting in fragmented and overly localized perceptions (Cutting, 1989; Happé and Frith, 2006). Silverstein et al. (1998) also reported that patients with chronic schizophrenia tended to use pathological strategies when copying figures in the ROCF test. According to a previous study conducted by Kim et al. (2008), patients with schizophrenia had lower ROCF fragmentation scores than healthy controls, supporting the notion that detail-oriented processing reflects a deficit in interpreting whole objects as meaningful gestalts (Johnson et al., 2005; Inami and Kirino, 2004). Uhlhaas et al. (2005) examined deficits in PO in patients with chronic schizophrenia over a three-week inpatient treatment course. Among these patients, those with disorganized schizophrenia exhibited significant deficits in PO, and improvements in PO were significantly associated with a reduction in symptoms, which is consistent with the results in our study.

However, some limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, as all assessments were conducted during a single visit, it was difficult to establish a causal relationship between deficits in PO and general functioning in patients with chronic schizophrenia. Considering that PO deficits are associated with premorbid functioning and independent living skills (Silverstein and Keane, 2011; Herrera et al., 2021), the future study should employ longitudinal designs to clarify this relationship. Second, psychotropic medications may have influenced cognitive functioning of the patients. To mitigate this, we included only clinically stable patients, defined by no changes in antipsychotic medication dosage for at least six months prior to enrollment. However, antipsychotic medications may differentially affect cognitive performance across patients (Harvey et al., 2022), medication type and dosage will be carefully considered in future analyses. Third, the sample size was relatively small to explore the relationship between PO, NC and functioning, suggesting the need for larger samples to ensure adequate statistical power in the future study. Fourth, the mean GAF score in our study was 64.05 (SD = 11.31), which is higher than the typical range of 40 to 50 reported in schizophrenia samples. This may be due to that patients in our study were recruited as part of a biomarker discovery study that involved blood sampling, genetic testing, and MRI scanning, which required participants to be clinically stable and cooperative. Additionally, inclusion was restricted to patients whose antipsychotic medication dosage had remained unchanged for at least six months. The mean PSP score was 62.74 (SD = 12.50), which was highly correlated with GAF scores (r = 0.90), supporting that patients in our study were relatively high functioning. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted as limited to chronic schizophrenia patients with relatively high functioning, and future studies with more functionally diverse samples are needed to generalize these results.

In conclusion, we assessed ROCF fragmentation and other NC test scores in 53 patients with chronic schizophrenia. The ROCF fragmentation score was significantly correlated with the ROCF copy score and loaded on the same factor as the ROCF copy, AVLT, and Stroop test scores in EFA. Notably, the ROCF fragmentation score was the only significant predictor of the GAF score. Considering the sample size and the relatively high-functioning nature of this sample, these findings suggest that PO may limitedly explain the variability in functioning across patients. Future studies with larger samples, longitudinal designs, and more functionally diverse populations are needed to validate these findings.
