Optical characterization analysis of surface modified-plastics (SMP) induced by atmospheric cold plasma system
Authors: Mark Nickole H Tabafa, Aldrin P Bonto, Jose M Esmeria, Patrick Joshua Emannuel Montales, James Roy P Lesidan, Jejomar A Bulan, Jumar G Cadondon, Tatsuo Shiina, Maria Cecilia D Galvez, Edgar A Vallar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface modification of polymers is essential for improving adhesion, coating performance, and wettability in various industrial and environmental applications. In this study, uniformly cut samples of common plastics such as polypropylene (PP), polystyrene (PS), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and high-density polyethylene (HDPE) were exposed to a low-cost atmospheric cold plasma (ACP) system using a zero-voltage switching (ZVS). The plasma system operated at an output of 10–20 kV, using argon (Ar), nitrogen (N₂), and oxygen (O₂) as working gases under atmospheric conditions. Samples were treated for 5 and 10 min of exposure. Surface modifications were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy/energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX), atomic force microscopy (AFM), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and contact angle measurements (CAM). A pronounced roughness enhancement was found in PP and HDPE. Moderate changes were seen in PET and PVC, while PS had a minimal response. FTIR confirmed the creation of oxygen-containing functional groups (–OH, C= O), enhancing surface polarity. The contact angle of PP decreased from 108° to 47°, while HDPE decreased from 80° to 62°, showing that improvements in wettability arise from the combined effects of chemical functionalization and increased surface roughness. These findings demonstrate that the low-cost ACP system effectively tailors polymer surface chemistry and morphology, providing a scalable and eco-friendly approach for adhesion, coating, and sustainable polymer processing applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41387-5.Keywords: Atmospheric cold plasma (ACP), Polymer surface modification, Surface chemistry and functionalization, Wettability enhancement, Adhesion and coatingsSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Plastic production has more than doubled between 2000 and 2019, increasing from 234 to 460 million tonnes (Mt). Without stronger regulation policies, global plastics production and use are projected to continue rising, reaching 736 million tonnes by 2040, a 70% increase from 2020 levels1. This growth is expected to continue outpacing global population trends, driven by sustained demand in both developed and emerging economies across Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, and Latin America1–4.This high growth rate is fueled by the extensive application of polymers like polypropylene (PP), polystyrene (PS), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and high-density polyethylene (HDPE) in various fields, including packaging, construction, automobile, electronics, and biomedicine, owing to their ruggedness, affordability, and flexibility5. However, their hydrophobic and chemically inert surfaces tend to restrict interfacial adhesion, compatibility of coatings, and wettability, limiting their applications in high-end applications like functional materials, biomedical devices, and green recycling6.

To overcome these restrictions, various polymer surface modification methods have been developed. Conventional approaches, such as chemical etching, ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, flame treatment, and corona discharge, can improve adhesion and wettability but often involve environmental concerns, high energy consumption, or limited chemical control7,8. In contrast, atmospheric cold plasma (ACP) provides a clean, tunable, and solvent-free approach that modifies polymer surfaces without altering their bulk structure. During treatment, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) interact with polymer backbones, causing bond scission and the incorporation of polar functional groups such as hydroxyl (–OH), carbonyl (C = O), and carboxyl (–COOH)9–13. These molecular-level changes enhance surface energy, adhesion, and coating performance, making ACP a scalable and environmentally sustainable technology for polymer surface engineering.

Despite the wide application and numerous advantages of ACP in surface modification, its adoption remains limited due to the high cost of the commercial system, making it inaccessible for small industries and research institutions8,13. Several studies have explored the effects of ACP on common plastics; they often focus on only one or two materials (e.g., PP, PS, or PET)8–10,12,14. Investigating several polymer types under the same plasma conditions enables direct correlation between surface modification behavior and intrinsic polymer properties, providing broader insight and practical relevance compared with single-material studies. In contrast, this study examines the surface modification behavior of five common plastics subjected to plasma treatment. Furthermore, most studies utilized only a few of the techniques mentioned above to address material-specific surface changes across multiple polymers.

This study built a low-cost ACP system based on zero-voltage switching (ZVS) for modifying the surface properties of PP, PS, PVC, PET, and HDPE. The ZVS-based driver differs from conventional RF or pulsed plasma systems by achieving efficient high-voltage generation with minimal energy loss and simplified circuitry, offering a low-cost yet stable alternative for atmospheric plasma applications. Scanning electron microscopy/energy-dispersive X-ray (SEM-EDX), atomic force microscopy (AFM), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and contact angle measurement (CAM) were employed to characterize the treated plastics. Material properties, including surface roughness, chemical functionalization, and wettability, were investigated before and after plasma exposure.

Experimental workflow.

Plastic materials used

Five types of plastics were cut into uniform pieces, each measuring 20 mm by 20 mm, to maintain consistency throughout the experiment. Three replicates were performed on each type of plastic to boost the reliability of the findings and account for any variability. Prior to plasma exposure, the plastic surfaces were gently cleaned using ethanol-wetted Kimwipes to remove any superficial dust or handling residues. No ultrasonic cleaning or chemical etchants were applied, as the samples were supplied pre-cleaned, stored in sealed containers, and handled under controlled conditions. After cleaning, the samples were placed in compartment boxes to prevent recontamination and were subsequently exposed to atmospheric cold plasma (ACP). Development of the ZVS-based plasma set-up

Figure 2 illustrates the schematic diagram of the plasma system, showing the internal and external components of the setup. The high-voltage power supply is connected to a 1.6 mm diameter tungsten electrode, which is insulated by a quartz tube acting as a dielectric barrier. This tube separates the electrode from the grounded copper spring wrapped around the quartz tube, ensuring controlled and uniform plasma discharge through the Dielectric Barrier Discharge (DBD) mechanism. The DBD configuration prevents electrical breakdown while maintaining a stable plasma generation process. To sustain plasma discharge, a gas inlet system supplies argon, nitrogen, and oxygen, which are introduced above the nozzle shown in Fig. 2(a). The argon gas was added since it can enhance the plasma stability by reducing electron loss and improving ionization. This positioning ensures a secure seal, prevents gas leakage and enhances plasma stability for improved performance. The system is designed to transition from a larger diameter at the top to a smaller diameter at the nozzle outlet, thereby achieving plasma jet formation. A 10 mm long plastic tube (5 mm inner diameter) is positioned after the quartz tube to narrow the plasma flow. The quartz tube has an inner diameter of 12 mm, providing sufficient space for plasma generation before being constricted at the outlet. The ACP setup, shown in Fig. 2(b), uses a series of power supplies, each providing 12 V and 30 A. The power supplies were connected in series, allowing the output voltage to be switched between 12 V and 24 V, while the current remained constant. The input power was 720 W, and the output voltage ranged from 10 kV to 20 kV. This configuration provided flexibility in adjusting the input voltage to the ZVS driver. The ZVS driver, which powered the flyback transformer, consisted of two IRFP250N MOSFETs, a 200 µH inductor, and a 0.33 µF resonant capacitor, operating at a switching frequency of approximately 30–35 kHz. This design minimized switching losses, improved power efficiency, and ensured stable high-voltage generation for plasma discharge. The high-voltage output (red wire) was connected to the tungsten electrode, while the ground (black wire) was attached to the copper ring around the quartz tube. To keep the nozzle stable and stationary, it was secured with a stand and clamp, maintaining a constant distance from the plastic target during the experiment. Electrical characteristics, including voltage and discharge current waveforms, have been previously reported and validated for a comparable ZVS-driven atmospheric plasma jet configuration16 and therefore were not remeasured in the present study.

Shows the schematic diagram of (a) Top view of the nozzle and (b) Overall set-up of the atmospheric cold plasma system.

The plasma plume exhibited an average length of approximately 20 mm (measured from the nozzle exit) and a width of 5 mm under a fixed operating condition of a 1 L/min total gas flow rate supplied through a single hose upstream of the nozzle. This flow rate was maintained constant for all experiments to ensure repeatability and stable plasma operation. The plasma gas temperature, measured using a Fluke 52 II digital thermometer positioned near the plume–sample interaction region, was 109.3 ± 11.7 °C, representing the steady-state temperature during continuous plasma operation. Under the fixed electrical input and nozzle geometry employed in this study, moderate variations in gas flow are expected to primarily influence plume morphology rather than the near-surface plasma–polymer interaction. Consequently, surface modification outcomes are dominated by exposure time and material-dependent interactions under constant flow conditions.

In atmospheric cold plasma systems, the gas temperature typically rises rapidly after plasma ignition and stabilizes under constant electrical and gas flow conditions, as commonly reported for dielectric barrier discharge and plasma jet configurations17,18. Although time-resolved measurements were not performed, the reported temperature corresponds to this stable operating regime. Plasma plume characteristics are known to depend on the working gas composition under atmospheric-pressure operation19,20. In this study, all experiments were conducted under a fixed mixed-gas condition and constant operating parameters, and the reported plume dimensions and temperature correspond to these controlled conditions. The plasma treatment durations of 5 and 10 min were selected to examine time-dependent surface modification under stable plasma operation while minimizing thermal and structural damage to the polymer substrates. A 5-minute exposure represents a moderate treatment condition sufficient to initiate surface activation and functional group formation without excessive etching. The 10-minute exposure was chosen as the maximum continuous operating time of the developed ACP system, beyond which overheating of system components may occur, potentially affecting plasma stability and experimental repeatability. Prolonged plasma exposure has been reported to intensify surface etching and degradation mechanisms in polymers, highlighting the importance of optimizing treatment duration21–23. Shorter treatment times may result in incomplete surface activation due to limited interaction between reactive plasma species and the polymer surface, while longer exposures may lead to excessive etching, surface degradation, or thermal effects with diminishing improvements in wettability. Throughout the experiments, all other plasma parameters, including applied voltage, gas composition, and flow rate, nozzle-to-sample distance, and ambient conditions, were kept constant, ensuring that the observed surface modifications were primarily governed by treatment duration.

Techniques for surface characterization

SEM-EDX characterization

The parameters used during SEM-EDX imaging of the plasma-treated plastic surfaces include the use of the Secondary Electron Detector (SED) under high-vacuum conditions to capture detailed surface morphology. The accelerating voltages were set to 3.0 kV for non-conductive polymer materials to minimize the charging effects. To ensure optimal focus and depth of field, a working distance of 9.4 mm was set. Imaging was performed at a magnification of ×1500, enabling the observation of micro-roughness features introduced after exposure to plasma. All samples were prepared with a standard mounting configuration and captured under consistent conditions to ensure comparability between untreated and treated samples.

AFM surface roughness

Surface topography of untreated and treated plastic samples, PP, PS, PVC, PET, and HDPE, was analyzed using an NX10 Atomic Force Microscope from Park System. The NX10, equipped with a high-resolution scanner and silicon cantilever probes featuring a tip radius of less than 10 nm, operates in non-contact mode to minimize sample damage and ensure precise topography mapping. The 20 × 20 mm samples were mounted on a steel disc with adhesive glue. Scans covered a 50 × 50 μm area with a resolution of pixels and a scan rate of 0.09 Hz. The cantilever oscillation was set to approximately 10 nm, and the set point was adjusted to maintain stable imaging. Imaging occurred under normal conditions, and the data were processed using XEI software to create 3D topography maps and calculate surface roughness parameters such as root mean square roughness, Rq.

FTIR characterization

The mid-infrared spectra of pristine and ACP-treated plastic samples were obtained using a Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrophotometer (IRSpirit-X, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a QATR-S diamond single-reflection accessory. Spectra were recorded over the 4000–400 cm⁻¹ range at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹, with 64 scans averaged per sample. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Contact angle measurement (CAM)

Surface wettability was assessed by measuring the static contact angle of distilled water droplets on untreated and treated plastics, i.e., 5 and 10 min, using a digital microscope. The system, equipped with a magnification lens and LED illumination, was mounted with a tape to maintain a fixed distance from the plastic sample, ensuring stability during imaging. Samples were stabilized at 20 ± 2 °C and 40 ± 5% relative humidity for 24 h post-treatments to achieve consistent surface conditions. A 5 µL distilled water droplet was dispensed onto each sample using a micro syringe. Images were captured immediately using the microscope’s integrated camera, connected to a laptop running ImageJ (free software) for analysis using the drop analysis LD-ABSA plug-in24. The contact angle was measured by manually fitting a tangent to the droplet profile in ImageJ. All contact angle measurements were performed within 30 s of droplet deposition, following standard practice for evaluating the static wettability of plasma-modified polymer surfaces.

Results and discussion

SEM-EDX analysis

The scanning electron microscopy energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM) analysis presented in Fig. 3 (untreated samples) and Fig. 4 (treated samples) reveal distinct plasma-induced surface morphological changes in PP, PS, PVC, PET, and HDPE following treatment with the atmospheric cold plasma (ACP) system. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the untreated PP exhibited a smooth and featureless surface morphology, consistent with previous SEM reports for untreated polypropylene9,26. After ACP exposure (Fig. 4(a)), PP developed a granular or globular surface microstructure, indicative of surface activation and localized etching induced by energetic plasma species. Similar surface morphologies have been widely attributed to plasma-induced chain scission and oxidation, leading to the formation of low-molecular-weight oxidized material (LMWOM) that migrates, phase-separates, and agglomerates at the surface due to interfacial free-energy differences26–29. The corresponding SEM–EDX elemental surface mapping confirms that carbon (C) remains the dominant element on PP surfaces both before and after ACP treatment. The untreated PP surface exhibited 100.00 ± 0.17 at% and 100.00 ± 0.17 wt% carbon, while the ACP-treated PP showed a similarly carbon-rich composition with 99.97 ± 0.16 at% and 99.93 ± 0.16 wt% carbon, accompanied by only a trace aluminum signal (0.03 ± 0.01 at% and 0.07 ± 0.01 wt%). No measurable oxygen enrichment was detected by EDX, which is expected given the highly surface-localized nature of plasma functionalization. Atmospheric plasma treatments typically modify only the outermost few nanometers of the polymer surface, whereas SEM–EDX probes interaction volumes on the order of hundreds of nanometers to several micrometers. Consequently, nanoscale chemical modifications responsible for enhanced wettability and surface activation may not be resolved by bulk-sensitive EDX analysis, as widely reported in plasma-modified polymer studies27,30,31.

Untreated PS exhibited a relatively smooth surface with minimal topographical features. Following ACP treatment (Fig. 4), the PS surface developed pronounced pits, micro-pores, and localized roughness. Comparable AFM and SEM observations have been reported for plasma-treated PS, where ion bombardment and reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) preferentially etch the near-surface region, resulting in increased roughness and micro-porosity without significant bulk degradation32,33. Mechanistic XPS studies further demonstrate that plasma treatment of PS induces surface oxidation and bond scission at aromatic ring sites, with oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., C–O and C = O) confined to the top few nanometers of the surface, evidencing oxidative bond cleavage localized at the polymer surface30,34. SEM–EDX mapping shows the presence of trace Ca and Ti species (< 1 at%), attributed to inorganic fillers or stabilizers commonly incorporated during polymer processing. Importantly, their atomic and mass percentages remained within experimental uncertainty after ACP treatment, indicating that plasma exposure did not introduce foreign elemental contamination.

PVC’s untreated surface was flat and smooth, consistent with its chlorinated polymer backbone. Treatment resulted in a finely roughened texture with porous structures (pores 0.1–1 μm), likely due to dehydrochlorination (loss of HCl) and etching of the outer layers35. SEM–EDX analysis confirms the presence of chlorine (Cl) in both untreated and treated samples, with comparable atomic fractions (21.75 at% untreated vs. 23.32 at% treated), demonstrating that ACP treatment did not cause bulk dichlorination. This finding supports previous studies showing that plasma-induced PVC modification is largely surface-confined, while the polymer backbone remains chemically stable at the bulk scale34,35.

Untreated PET exhibited pronounced linear surface features associated with film manufacturing processes, as observed. Following ACP exposure, these linear features remained discernible; however, localized surface modification became evident in the form of subtle roughening and the appearance of scattered pinhole-like features. Such pinholes have been reported in previous plasma studies on polymers and ceramics and are commonly attributed to localized bombardment by energetic ions and reactive plasma species, leading to near-surface bond scission and material removal at defect-prone sites36–38. SEM–EDX results indicate that carbon remains the dominant element (> 99.9 at%), with only trace Ca detected, likely originating from processing additives. The absence of detectable oxygen enrichment by EDX further confirms that plasma-induced functionalization is limited to the near-surface region, consistent with the semicrystalline nature of PET, which suppresses aggressive ablation while enabling controlled surface activation39.

For HDPE, the untreated surface exhibited a relatively smooth morphology with low inherent roughness, characteristic of polyethylene-based polymers processed under conventional film-forming conditions. Following ACP treatment, the HDPE surface showed moderate roughening and the development of shallow texture features, indicative of plasma-induced surface activation. Similar morphological changes have been widely reported for HDPE and related polyethylene materials exposed to atmospheric and low-pressure plasma, where ion bombardment and reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) induce chain scission and surface oxidation primarily within the near-surface region31,40. SEM–EDX mapping revealed trace Ca and Ti species (< 1 at%) in both untreated and treated samples, with no systematic increase after plasma exposure. These elements are attributed to stabilizers or filler residues rather than plasma deposition. The stable elemental composition, combined with the observed morphological changes, confirms that ACP interaction was confined to the surface layers without inducing bulk chemical alteration. Similar behavior has been widely reported for plasma-treated HDPE and polyethylene systems41,42.

Overall, the SEM–EDX results demonstrate that ACP treatment induces significant surface morphological modification while preserving bulk chemical composition. The enhanced wettability and roughness observed in treated polymers therefore arise from nanoscale surface restructuring and chemical functionalization rather than from bulk elemental changes, highlighting the controlled and surface-specific nature of the ACP process.

To evaluate the spatial uniformity of plasma-induced surface modification, SEM observations were complemented with SEM–EDX elemental surface mapping for both untreated and ACP-treated samples. The SEM–EDX overlay maps demonstrate a homogeneous elemental distribution across the analyzed surface areas, with carbon remaining the dominant element and no evidence of localized elemental enrichment or depletion following plasma exposure. Minor aluminum signals detected in some samples are attributed to extrinsic contamination from aluminum mounting fixtures rather than intrinsic plasma-induced chemical changes.

For PP, faint raster-like line features were observed in some SEM images, which are attributed to localized surface charging effects during electron beam scanning. Such charging artifacts are commonly reported for low-conductivity polymer surfaces and may persist even after thin conductive coatings (e.g., Pt sputtering), particularly when surface roughness and oxidation increase after plasma treatment43.

Importantly, these raster patterns are not reflected in the corresponding SEM–EDX elemental maps, confirming that they originate from imaging effects rather than non-uniform plasma modification. Variations in image contrast, particularly observed for PS, are primarily associated with SEM imaging conditions, as confirmed by the corresponding EDX maps. Collectively, the SEM images and SEM–EDX mapping results confirm that the ACP system produces spatially uniform surface modification, supporting its applicability for surface engineering applications such as adhesion enhancement and wettability control.

AFM surface roughness

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) was employed to evaluate the nanoscale roughness of untreated and ACP-treated plastics. Figure 4 presents the 3D topography images for both untreated and treated plastics in this study. The images showed the difference after the plastics were exposed to plasma. At the same time, the quantified roughness parameters and average roughness (Ra), are present in Fig. 5.

3D AFM topography images of plastics, with untreated samples shown on the top and treated samples shown below.

ACP treatment generally increased surface roughness across all tested plastics, as reflected by both the average roughness (Ra) and root-mean-square roughness (Rq) values. Quantitative AFM roughness analysis was expanded to explicitly discuss both parameters, with Ra emphasized as the primary roughness descriptor due to its direct relevance to surface wettability, adhesion, and coating compatibility, while Rq is presented as a complementary parameter that is more sensitive to extreme surface features such as peaks, pits, and pinholes. PP exhibited the most pronounced roughening, with Rq increasing from 64.35 nm to 119.70 nm and Ra from 50.25 nm to 95.00 nm after ACP exposure. A similar trend was observed for HDPE, where Rq increased from 111.24 nm to 144.36 nm and Ra from 90.20 nm to 117.83 nm, indicating substantial development of surface texture. These trends are consistent with plasma-induced nanoscale etching, chain scission, and micro-ablation reported for polyolefins under oxygen-containing plasma environments31,40,42. PS exhibited comparatively minor changes in surface roughness, with Rq values of 134.35 nm (untreated) and 136.13 nm (treated) and nearly unchanged Ra values, suggesting that PS is more resistant to ACP-induced nanoscale roughening within the applied treatment duration30,32. In contrast, PET and PVC showed moderate increases in roughness following ACP treatment. PET exhibited an increase in Rq from 15.22 nm to 27.52 nm and Ra from 10.75 nm to 18.52 nm, while PVC showed increases in Rq from 11.82 nm to 16.71 nm and Ra from 6.90 nm to 12.01 nm. Similar increases in surface roughness after plasma treatment have been reported for PET films, where AFM measurements show significant elevation of Ra values and the development of a nanostructured surface due to plasma exposure, indicating near-surface modification and enhanced topography after treatment44,45. Reviews of polymer plasma modification also describe increased roughness as a common outcome of ion bombardment and etching processes on initially smooth polymer surfaces46. These observations indicate effective near-surface modification even for relatively smooth plastics, although the extent of modification remains less severe than that observed for PP and HDPE.

The observed increase in surface roughness for PP and HDPE compared to PS, PVC, and PET can be attributed to differences in polymer crystallinity and chemical composition. Both PP and HDPE are semi-crystalline polyolefins with non-polar hydrocarbon backbones. During plasma exposure, energetic ions and reactive species preferentially attack the amorphous regions, resulting in localized etching and the formation of microgrooves at the crystalline–amorphous interfaces. In contrast, PS, PVC, and PET contain aromatic or polar functional groups (e.g., C = Cl, C = O) that favor chemical oxidation rather than physical ablation, resulting in smoother surface morphologies after treatment. The higher susceptibility of PP and HDPE to ion bombardment and chain scission, therefore, explains their more pronounced increase in surface roughness following ACP treatment26,27. Although an overall decrease in contact angle was observed after ACP treatment, the correlation between roughness and wettability was not strictly linear. For instance, PP and HDPE exhibited the highest increase in surface roughness, but only moderate reductions in contact angle, while PET and PVC showed smaller roughness changes yet stronger improvements in wettability. This indicates that surface chemistry, particularly the formation of polar oxygenated groups during plasma oxidation, plays a more dominant role than roughness in determining wetting behavior. Nonetheless, the general trend shows that increased roughness and chemical activation together enhance surface hydrophilicity, as reflected by the decreased contact angle values in the following section.

FTIR measurements

The FTIR spectra (as shown in Fig. 6) clearly indicates that atmospheric cold plasma (ACP) treatment introduces new functional groups on the surface of common plastics. In PP and PET, broad O–H stretching bands (~ 3200–3500 cm⁻¹) and a new carbonyl band (~ 1700–1750 cm⁻¹), signifying oxidative incorporation of hydroxyl and carbonyl groups. Similar observations have been reported, where plasma treatment increased the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups in fiber-reinforced PP composites and in commercial PET derived from colorless plastic bottles47. PS exhibits enhanced C = C stretching vibrations (~ 1600 cm⁻¹), which may result from aromatic ring modification or partial chain scission caused by energetic plasma species. Such changes can lead to the formation of conjugated double bonds or oxygenated aromatic structures (e.g., phenolic or carbonyl derivatives) on the surface48,49. This suggests that plasma exposure induces localized rearrangements and oxidation within the PS backbone, even though the bulk aromatic structure remains largely intact. PVC develops carbonyl absorption, likely due to plasticizer degradation, alongside maintained C–Cl bands. In HDPE, weaker but noticeable carbonyl bands appear. The relative enhancement of O–H and C = O absorption bands suggest surface oxidation of varying degrees, with PET and PVC showing more pronounced modifications than PP and HDPE. These qualitative differences indicate polymer-dependent reactivity toward plasma-induced oxidation.

This reaction accounts for the observed oxidative functionalization while preserving most chemical backbone, suggesting that ACP treatment can effectively tailor the surface chemistry of polymers without causing significant bulk degradation51.

Contact angle measurements

The wettability behavior of the untreated and plasma-treated PP, PS, PVC, PET, and HDPE surfaces is quantitatively summarized in Fig. 8. Untreated plastic surfaces were predominantly hydrophobic, exhibiting water contact angles greater than 70°, consistent with typical polymer surface characteristics11. Following exposure to the atmospheric cold plasma (ACP) system for 5 and 10 min, a clear reduction in contact angle was observed for all polymers, indicating enhanced surface hydrophilicity.

Effects of plasma treatment on contact angle measurement.

In particular, the untreated PP surface exhibited a high contact angle of 108°, indicative of hydrophobicity. After plasma treatment, the contact angle decreased markedly to 61° after 5 min and further to 47° after 10 min of exposure, corresponding to an overall reduction of 47°. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) confirmed that the reductions in contact angle for PP after plasma treatment were statistically significant (p < 0.001). The pronounced change in wettability is consistent with the granular microstructure observed in the SEM images (Fig. 3), suggesting increased surface roughness and surface activation, in agreement with previous studies by Kwon et al.9,27. Similarly, PS exhibited a statistically significant decrease in contact angle from 79° to 52° and 49° following 5 and 10 min of plasma exposure, respectively (p < 0.001), corresponding to the pitted and textured surface features observed in the SEM results. PVC showed a reduction from 82° to 67° and 55° after 5 and 10 min of treatment, while PET decreased from 72° to 58° and 54°, with both polymers exhibiting statistically significant changes (p < 0.01). HDPE displayed the smallest wettability change, decreasing from 80° to 75° and 62° after 5 and 10 min, respectively; nevertheless, these reductions were also statistically significant (p < 0.05). The error bars represent the standard deviation calculated from three independent measurements taken at different surface locations for each polymer, confirming the reproducibility of the results. Overall, these findings demonstrate that atmospheric cold plasma treatment effectively enhances surface wettability across all investigated polymers, with PP and PET showing the most pronounced responses.

The increase in surface energy with treatment duration indicates enhanced wettability, adhesion potential, and coating compatibility of the polymer surfaces. The improved surface energy also implies enhanced coating compatibility. Surfaces with higher energy facilitate uniform spreading and anchoring of coating materials, leading to improved coating homogeneity and durability. The observed surface energy values fall within the optimal range reported for effective adhesion and coating performance on polymer substrates, suggesting suitability for applications such as protective coatings, functional thin films, printing, and biomedical surface modification.

Previous works have primarily focused on single-polymer systems or relied on low-pressure plasma treatments, emphasizing surface chemical modification and wettability enhancement using techniques such as XPS and contact angle analysis. Recent studies have demonstrated that appropriate selection of plasma precursors can lead to substantial changes in surface wettability, roughness, and adhesion performance. For instance, non-thermal plasma treatments using inert or reactive gas environments have been shown to significantly enhance surface hydrophilicity and adhesion through the introduction of oxygen-containing functional groups and controlled surface etching52,53. Similar improvements in surface activation and bonding characteristics have also been reported for plasma-modified engineering polymers and composites, highlighting the importance of plasma chemistry in tailoring surface properties without affecting bulk material integrity54,55. These findings support the relevance of the present study, where atmospheric cold plasma treatment under controlled precursor and operating conditions resulted in enhanced surface wettability and roughness across multiple polymer types. In contrast, the present study systematically investigates multiple commercially relevant polymers (PP, PS, PVC, PET, and HDPE) under atmospheric cold plasma conditions. By combining surface morphology (SEM), nanoscale roughness (AFM), chemical bonding (FTIR), and wettability analysis, this work provides a unified comparison of polymer-dependent plasma responses.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate the potential of the developed low-cost atmospheric cold plasma (ACP) system for effectively modifying the surfaces of common polymers. The treatment enhanced surface roughness and wettability, suggesting improved adhesion and coating compatibility without compromising bulk integrity. FTIR analysis indicated the incorporation of oxygen-containing functional groups, while SEM and AFM confirmed nanoscale morphological alterations characteristic of plasma-induced activation. The zero-voltage switching (ZVS)-based ACP design offers a cost-effective and energy-efficient alternative to conventional plasma systems, enabling stable plasma generation with simplified circuitry. This approach enables controlled surface modification and is suitable for various industrial applications, including packaging, printing, and material bonding. Future work will focus on quantifying the long-term stability and controlled surface degradation mechanisms of plasma-treated polymers, as well as extending the system’s use to environmentally sustainable polymer processing.
