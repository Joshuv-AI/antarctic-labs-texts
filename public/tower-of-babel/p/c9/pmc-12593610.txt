A dataset of exocentric images capturing hand gestures in multiplayer games
Authors: Talukder Hasnat Zadid, Sharia Arfin Tanim, MF Mridha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset presents 7221 exocentric (third-person) frame images of hand gestures recorded during multiplayer tabletop games including Ludo, Poker, and Snakes and Ladders. The recordings were made using a Google Pixel 6a smartphone at 30 frames per second (FPS), and frame images were extracted at a fixed interval of one frame every four seconds. Each extracted image was resized to 512 × 512 pixels. The dataset captures two-, three-, and four-person gameplay sessions under four distinct lighting conditions (natural, white, yellow, and dim), reflecting realistic and varied visual environments. The frames include natural gesture dynamics with motion blur, occlusions, and object manipulations, offering authentic interaction data. This dataset supports research in computer vision, human–computer interaction, and Extended Reality (XR), enabling studies in gesture recognition, hand-object interaction, and occlusion handling. It can also be applied in supervised and unsupervised learning tasks, evaluation of gesture-tracking algorithms, and XR benchmarking for real-world gaming interactions. By providing authentic multi-person interaction footage from consistent top-down viewpoints, this dataset facilitates advancements of gesture recognition systems designed for collaborative environments and interactive gaming applications.Keywords: Exocentric view, Human-computer interaction (HCI), Frame images, Extended reality, Computer vision

Background

Hand gesture recognition has emerged as a pivotal component in advancing human-computer interaction (HCI), particularly within immersive environments such as virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR). The transition from traditional input devices to gesture-based interfaces offers users a more natural and intuitive means of interaction, thereby enhancing the overall user experience [1].

Recent studies have explored various methodologies for capturing and interpreting hand gesture data. For instance, Zhang et al. utilized skin detection in the YCbCr color space combined with Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) features and Support Vector Machines (SVM) to achieve real-time hand gesture recognition in VR settings. Similarly, Zhang highlighted the efficacy of HOG features in enhancing gesture recognition accuracy within virtual environments [2,3].

The integration of AI-driven tools, such as MediaPipe, has further streamlined the development of gesture recognition systems. Jo et al. demonstrated the application of MediaPipe in classifying multiple hand gestures with high accuracy, emphasizing its potential for VR and AR applications [4,5].

Despite these advancements, there remains a scarcity of datasets capturing hand gestures from an exocentric (third-person) perspective, especially in real-world multi-user gaming scenarios. The dataset presented in this study addresses this gap by providing frame images of participants engaged in interactive games such as Ludo, Poker, and Snakes and Ladders under various lighting conditions. Such data is invaluable for training and evaluating gesture recognition models that can operate effectively in dynamic, real-world environments.

Data Description

The dataset consists of an Exocentric View (third-person perspective) folder, which contains subfolders categorized by the number of participants: two, three, and four persons. Each of these subfolders is further divided based on the lighting conditions, including natural, white, yellow, and dim light. Each lighting-specific folder contained three game folders. The subfolders in the exocentric view (third-person perspective) store the frame images extracted from the recorded videos. The frames are named numerically. For example, “Ludo_4_dim00241,” where Ludo corresponds to the game name, the next 4 represents the number of participants, dim indicates the lighting condition, and finally, 00,241 is the frame number (241st frame). Subsequently, all other frame files across the dataset were named following the same format. The original videos were recorded at 30 frames per second (FPS) using a Google Pixel 6a smartphone. Frame images were extracted at a fixed interval of 1 frame every 120 video frames, resulting in approximately one frame every 4 s. Each extracted frame image was resized to 512 × 512 pixels during the export process using VLC Media Player. This method provides representative gesture snapshots while managing data volume effectively. Moreover, the lighting conditions in the dataset naturally varied over time during the recordings, creating distinct illumination levels for groups of different sizes. For two-person interactions, natural lighting remained normal; for three-person interactions, it became weaker; and for four-person interactions, it appeared darker. The dim lighting originated from ceiling fixtures opposite the window, and its intensity was affected by the participants' seating positions, with more people creating greater light obstruction due to increased physical blockage. Fig. 2 demonstrates these variations through three representative cases: (a) two players engaged in three different games under normal natural light, (b) three players participating in three games under weakened natural light, and (c) four players competing in three games under dark natural light conditions.

Individual frames typically contain multiple overlapping gestures, such as simultaneous piece-moving motions and hand-shape deformations during the checkers gameplay, as systematically categorized in Table 3a, Table 3b, and Table 3c. The extracted frames preserve these complex interactions, exhibiting numerous blurred hand gestures from rapid motions, while variable lighting conditions introduce additional challenges with near-dark hand appearances and pronounced shadows from light interference, as shown in Fig. 2.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The study involved four volunteers playing Ludo, card games (Poker), and Snakes and Ladders to analyze their hand interactions during gameplay. They completed sets for two-, three-, and four-player games.

The first step in the process is to prepare the equipment. Google Pixel 6a mounted on a floor-standing tripod recorded all sessions, capturing key elements such as piece movement, card handling, and dice rolls. The setup focused on documenting fast motions (such as shuffling cards or rolling dice), hand occlusions (blocking game pieces), and multiplayer interactions around the game board. With only one camera angle available from the tripod, the experiment prioritized clear footage of hand movements, rotations, and object manipulations across the three distinct game types under consistent lighting conditions. The second necessary step was to arrange the filming environment with the necessary furniture. This includes tools and game boards. The phone was set up on a tripod at the top for a top view and a proper view of the participants to capture the required portion of the scene.

The third step was video capturing; one Pixel 6a camera was deployed in the default configuration on a floor tripod from the top vertically to capture third-person viewpoint recordings. The overall layout is illustrated in Fig. 3. The lighting environment was carefully controlled using multiple sources: natural illumination from wall-mounted windows, bright white and soft yellow lighting from ceiling fixtures, and dim lighting from bulbs positioned opposite the windows. All filming occurred indoors with four distinct lighting conditions maintained throughout the sessions - natural, bright white, soft yellow, and dim - as visually documented in Fig. 2.

Setup Scene.

The fourth step involved converting the video into continuous images. For this process, the export Scene Video Filter feature of the VLC Media Player was used. Individual video frames were extracted using the scene video filter of the VLC Media Player. A recording ratio of 120 was configured, which means that one frame was saved every 120 frames of the video. Given a standard frame rate (e.g., 30 fps), this corresponds to a frame captured approximately every 4 s. The extracted frames were saved in the PNG format in a designated folder for further analysis.

The simplified approach using minimal equipment still allowed the observation of critical gameplay interactions, such as piece movements (up/down, left/right), card deformations during play, and shared access to game components among the four participants.

Limitations

Despite careful design, the dataset has several limitations. First, some players occasionally leaned outside the capture zone, reducing hand visibility. Second, dim lighting conditions increased motion blur during rapid movements. Third, game-specific dynamics affected data quality—for example, compact card exchanges in Poker produced more hand occlusions compared to Ludo’s wider board play. While 30 FPS recording and controlled lighting reduced these issues, they were not fully eliminated. Additionally, the dataset is limited to an exocentric viewpoint; incorporating egocentric (first-person) data could provide a richer perspective for future studies.

Ethics Statement

The dataset was collected with the voluntary participation of individuals during interactive gameplay sessions. All participants were informed in advance about the nature and purpose of the study, and written informed consent was obtained using a standardized form. The consent covered the use of non-identifiable data. No photographs or video recordings containing identifiable features, such as participants’ faces, were used; only hand gestures were recorded, ensuring that participants' privacy was fully protected. Formal ethics approval from an institutional review board or ethics committee was not sought, as the study posed minimal risk, involved no medical intervention or sensitive personal data, and was conducted in a separate residential room. The data collection process adhered to ethical standards consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki and common research practices for low-risk observational studies.
