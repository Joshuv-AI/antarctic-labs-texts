Contrast negation increases face pareidolia rates in natural scenes
Authors: Benjamin Balas, Myra Morton, Molly Setchfield, Lily Roshau, Emily Westrick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Face pareidolia, the phenomenon of seeing face-like patterns in non-face images, has a dual nature: Pareidolic patterns are experienced as face-like, even while observers can recognize the true nature of the stimulus (Stuart et al., 2025). Although pareidolic faces seem to result largely from the canonical arrangement of eye spots and a mouth, we hypothesized that competition between veridical and face-like interpretations of pareidolic patterns may constrain face pareidolia in natural scenes and textures. Specifically, we predicted that contrast negation, which disrupts multiple aspects of mid- to high-level recognition, may increase rates of face pareidolia in complex natural textures by weakening the veridical, non-face stimulus interpretation. We presented adult participants (n = 27) and 5- to 12-year-old children (n = 67) with a series of natural images depicting textures such as grass, leaves, shells, and rocks. We asked participants to circle any patterns in each image that looked face-like, with no constraints on response time or pattern size, position, and orientation. We found that, across our adult and child samples, contrast-negated images yielded more pareidolic face detections than positive images. We conclude that disrupting veridical object and texture recognition enhances pareidolia in children and adults by compromising half of the dual nature of a pareidolic pattern.

Introduction

Face pareidolia refers to a common-place phenomenon in which random patterns or particular configurations of objects look sufficiently face-like to be noteworthy to casual observers. Besides yielding many funny and whimsical images, face pareidolia is interesting from a vision science perspective because it reveals properties of the mechanisms of the human visual system for face detection. Specifically, that non-face patterns sometimes look like faces to us reflects the broad tuning of mechanisms that provide high sensitivity to faces in natural images. The so-called first-order configuration of eye spots positioned over a nose and mouth, for example, is directly linked to the experience of face pareidolia (Omer, Sapir, Hatuka, & Yovel, 2019). Understanding the stimulus conditions that support face pareidolia in more depth has the potential to provide further insights into the tuning of face detection and the decision-making processes that lead observers to recognize something face-like in rocks Bednarik (2016), clouds, and other everyday objects and textures.

Although many recent studies of face pareidolia rely on images selected due to their high resemblance to faces (see the examples in Figure 1), examining face pareidolia using images that are not known or guaranteed to have face-like patterns in them is an important means of exploring the stimulus features that support seeing faces when they are not there. An excellent example of this approach is the use by Paras and Webster (2013) of symmetric “totem pole” stimuli created from 1/f noise patterns In their study, they found that making mirror-symmetric patterns from fractal noise images by copying and flipping one half of the image across the vertical midline led to the perception of many face-like patterns along this axis. Their conclusion (subsequent to multiple experiments examining the contribution of anti-symmetric and chromatic contrast, among other image properties) was that dark eye spots appeared to be sufficient to drive pareidolic face perception, suggesting a low-level triggering feature that led to a higher level inference about faces. Using similar noise patterns, Balas et al. (2024) and Peterson, Sarna, Spehar, and Clifford (2025) manipulated the power spectrum of fractal noise to examine how changing the statistics of random patterns might affect the perception of animate forms more broadly, and faces in particular. Balas et al. (2024) reported reduced rates of face pareidolia when fractal noise patterns were bandpass filtered to limit either horizontal or vertical contrast energy and when steeper fall-off coefficients were applied to the power spectrum slope. Peterson et al. (2025) allowed participants to make more open-ended responses to fractal noise varying in both spatial and temporal statistics, with and without vertical symmetry imposed a la Paras and Webster (2013). They found the highest response rates of pareidolia (including faces) for images with a spectral fall-off coefficient closest to that of natural scenes. Considered together, these studies provide important evidence that the detection of face-like structure in larger patterns depends on specific local features (in the case of dark eye spots) and also on global image statistics (in the case of spectral power coefficients and orientation energy).

Of course, real-world images do not look like fractal noise but include a range of natural objects and textures. This means that the face-like patterns that emerge from such images have an interesting duality to consider that does not apply to truly random noise patterns: Pareidolic faces in natural scenes look like faces but are ultimately recognizable as the objects and textures that they truly are. Consider the image shown in Figure 2 that depicts rather wicked-looking red peppers. Although your initial impression of this picture may be that it looks like a pair of laughing faces, you are also probably not tricked into thinking that these are actually social beings lying on a kitchen counter. Instead, your visual system provides you with an initial signal of “face-ness” in this picture that quickly gives way to the recognition of these objects as vegetables. This sequence of events was demonstrated elegantly by Wardle, Taubert, Teichmann, and Baker (2020) in a study in which they recorded concurrent functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) data while participants were presented with face-like objects or matched objects that did not appear face-like. They found that, although the initial presentation of face-like objects elicited responses from face-sensitive areas such as the fusiform face area (FFA) and occipital face area (OFA), later neural activity bore a closer resemblance to matched objects rather than true human faces. Using a rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP) paradigm, Stuart, Saurels, Robinson, and Taubert (2025) found behavioral evidence that further supports this dual-natured perception of pareidolic objects. Specifically, they found that manipulating the amount of time available to respond to pareidolic objects changed the extent to which face or object identity dominated observers’ perceptions of these stimuli. Whereas shorter amounts of processing time favored the face-ness of such objects, longer amounts of time favored object-ness. Thus the experience of face pareidolia “in the wild” not only depends on feed-forward processing of critical low-level image features but also has a temporal evolution: Face pareidolia is an experience of initial impression, with further processing yielding veridical processing of object and texture categories.

In the current study, we chose to use contrast negation (reversing light- and dark-intensity values in an image) as a means of examining how pareidolia is affected by the presence of interpretable visual structure and naturalistic scene appearance. This simple manipulation preserves a great deal of low-level visual structure in natural images while profoundly disrupting a range of mid- to high-level recognition processes. With regard to low-level visual structure, reversing intensity values via contrast negation does nothing to change the spectral power of an image, nor does it change the distribution of orientations present in an image. Instead, contrast negation flips the polarity (or sign) of each edge, such that a light-to-dark edge becomes a dark-to-light edge. A range of statistical properties of the original image are thus unaltered by this change. However, despite the image properties that are not affected by contrast negation, it is highly relevant to the phenomenology of face pareidolia due to the profound deleterious effect it has on face recognition accuracy (Galper, 1970). Why should negation affect face recognition so strongly? Although judgments about feature spacing in unfamiliar faces are less accurate in negated images (Kemp, McManus, & Pigott, 1990), more recent accounts focus on the specific disruption of surface pigmentation in face images (Russell, Sinha, Biederman, & Nederhouser, 2006; Vuong, Peissig, Harrison, & Tarr, 2005) independent from compromised inferences about illumination and shape-from-shading (Kemp, Pike, White, & Musselman, 1996). This is consistent with studies demonstrating that contrast negation also disrupts texture discrimination (Balas, 2012; Balas & Conlin, 2015) and material recognition abilities (Fleming & Bulthoff, 2005), both of which contribute to the processing of the surface pigmentation of face images.

Besides the fact that mid-level and high-level visual recognition is measurably disrupted by contrast negation, negative images also simply look odd. It is generally easy to tell if one is looking at a negated image due to the many ways in which typical illumination patterns, shading, and natural contrast relationships within discrete objects have been altered. If we accept the premise that the visual system is tuned to images of the natural world, then the subjective un-naturalness of negative images signals a substantial departure from the diet of images the visual system is sensitive to and suggests generally less efficient processing of these stimuli. These various aspects of negated images, their clearly unnatural appearance, the disruption of recognition abilities, and the preservation of many low-level image statistics lead to different predictions about how face pareidolia may be manifest in negated images relative to positive contrast images. First, to the extent that face pareidolia may be tuned to natural image statistics (Balas, 2025), negation may either (1) do little to affect pareidolia rates due to the preservation of spectral power, etc., or (2) lead to lower rates of pareidolia due either to the globally unnatural appearance of negated images or sensitivity to phase statistics in natural scenes (although, for evidence that adults are generally not very sensitive to these relationships, see Balas, 2006). Second, however, to the extent that pareidolic percepts must compete with veridical interpretations of the same patterns (e.g., seeing Figure 2 as red peppers vs. faces), negation may increase rates of face pareidolia by weakening the strength of the veridical percept. That is, if negation makes it more challenging to recognize objects and textures for what they really are due to disruptions of texture/pigmentation processing, then pareidolic interpretations of the same patterns may be more robust.

These predictions regarding the impact of contrast negation on face pareidolia are also interesting to examine from a developmental perspective. In general, children's face pareidolia has not been studied in great depth. Flessert, Taubert, and Beran (2022) reported that children interpreted pareidolic stimuli as face-like but non-human primates did not, providing a clear demonstration of measurable face pareidolia in childhood. In terms of how pareidolic patterns are categorized, Wardle, Ewing, Malcolm, Paranjape, and Baker (2023) found that children exhibit the same overwhelming male bias when categorizing pareidolic faces as adults (Wardle, Paranjape, Taubert, & Baker, 2022). These results tentatively suggest pareidolic percepts in childhood that resemble those of adults, but near-ceiling response rates for pareidolic stimuli in Wardle et al. (2023) make it difficult to draw a firm conclusion about the incidence of pareidolia as a function of age.

What might we expect in terms of children's tendency to report faces in non-face stimuli as a function of age, including comparisons to adults? Children's visual development with regard to different aspects of face recognition, perceptual organization, and texture processing unfolds at different time scales, and each of these may be a critical contributor to the experience of face pareidolia. Although some aspects of face recognition, such as holistic processing of face patterns (de Heering, Houthuys, & Rossion, 2007; Pellicano & Rhodes, 2003) and the apparent structure of face space (Crookes & McKone, 2009), appear to be mature early in childhood, other features of adult face recognition develop more slowly during middle childhood. The ability to discriminate faces based on feature spacing, for example, is not adult-like until after at least 8 years of age (Mondloch, Dobson, Parsons, & Maurer, 2004), and children also do not have an adult-like bias favoring horizontal orientation energy and diagnostic micro-patterns in face images until around 10 years of age (Balas, Auen, Saville, Schmidt, & Harel, 2020; Balas, Harel, Auen, & Saville, 2019; Balas, Schmidt, & Saville, 2015). The effects of contrast negation on face discrimination also appear to be weaker at younger ages (8 years of age) than at 14 years of age (Itier & Taylor, 2004).

With regard to perceptual organization, children exhibit remarkably poor abilities at recognizing two-tone images (Milne et al., 2024), suggesting slow development of adult-like segmentation and visual closure abilities. Finally, with regard to texture processing, children's ability to categorize materials using texture properties also matures slowly during middle childhood (Balas, 2017), as does their sensitivity to natural texture statistics (Ellemberg, Hansen, & Johnson, 2012). Given that adult-like face pareidolia likely depends on the maintenance of a sensitive face template, visual closure abilities, and the visual processing of texture (especially when we consider noise stimuli or other stochastic patterns), these varying developmental trajectories make it challenging to guess how face pareidolia is likely to develop during middle childhood in terms of both the raw likelihood that non-face patterns may look face-like and the sensitivity of this experience to variation in the appearance of non-face patterns. On one hand, the early development of holistic face processing supports rapid development of face pareidolia in childhood, for example. On the other, the slow development of perceptual organization skills suggests that face pareidolia may emerge gradually during middle childhood.

We examined the incidence of face pareidolia as a function of positive and negative image contrast using an unconstrained pareidolic face detection task administered to adults and school-age children (5–11 years of age). This task made it possible to use a range of natural textures as the basis for eliciting face pareidolia and also to study the behavior of adults (Experiment 1) and school-age children (Experiment 2) using a common intuitive paradigm. Our primary question was whether and how negating natural image contrast affected participants’ rates of reporting pareidolic faces. Regarding contrast negation, we predicted that negating our stimulus images would either disrupt the natural appearance of these scenes sufficiently that the mechanisms that support face pareidolia (or face detection) do not operate as effectively, or that contrast negation may limit the competition between a veridical interpretation of the image and the pareidolic interpretation of different image regions, leading to increased pareidolia. Whichever effect we observed in adults, we hypothesized that the effects of contrast negation would not be evident early in our developmental sample but would instead emerge gradually with increasing age such that only older children would have adult-like sensitivity to positive vs. negative contrast.

Experiment 1

In Experiment 1, we measured adults’ rates of pareidolic face pattern perception in natural images that were displayed in positive and negative contrast. We predicted that negation may weaken the veridical interpretation of these images, thereby increasing pareidolia rates in negative images relative to positive ones or, alternatively, that the disruption of naturalistic appearance may reduce pareidolia in negative scenes due to the tuning of face pareidolia to typical scene structure.

Methods

Participants

We recruited a total of 27 adults (17 women, 10 men) from the North Dakota State University (NDSU) Undergraduate Psychology Study Pool. All participants were between 19 and 23 years of age and self-reported normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity. We granted course credit as compensation for their participation in the study, and all participants provided informed consent prior to the beginning of the study session. All recruitment and testing procedures were approved by the NDSU institutional review board.

Stimuli

We created images likely to elicit high rates of pareidolia according to the manipulation described in Paras and Webster (2013). We first selected eight unique images of natural textures depicting grass, rocks, seashells, and other elements. These were selected such that the spatial scale of texture elements varied across images and so that each image did not have a clear modal orientation (i.e., no strongly directional textures were included). These images ranged in size and aspect ratio (see full stimulus set on the Open Science Framework at https://osf.io/d9t2z/) but had a minimum size of 1000 × 700 pixels.

To create our pareidolic stimuli, each original image was converted to grayscale and then cut in half at the vertical midline. The left half of the image was then copied, flipped horizontally, and pasted on top of the original right half of the image. The result in each case was an image with forced symmetry across the vertical meridian. We then created a contrast-negated counterpart for each symmetric image (Figure 3) for a total of 16 stimuli.

(Top row) An example of a mirror-symmetric natural image with original contrast (left) and contrast-negated in the original color palette. (Bottom row) The same images in grayscale as they were presented in Experiments 1 and 2.

Procedure

We presented grayscale versions of the positive and negative contrast symmetric images described above to each participant via a custom interface developed using InQuisit 6.6.3. We instructed participants to examine each image to determine if they saw any face-like patterns in the texture and to circle any such patterns using a stylus. Participants’ responses were drawn on top of each image as they completed the task using a 3-point-width red line, which remained on the screen as they continued to look for more face-like patterns in each stimulus. Participants were given unlimited time to view each image, and the images were presented to each participant in a new pseudo-randomized order. The task was administered on an 11-inch iPad (Apple, Cupertino, CA). For each participant, we captured a screenshot of their complete set of circled patterns per image (raw data available at https://osf.io/d9t2z/), and an example of one of these screen shots is included in Figure 4.

An example of one participant's results from circling perceived pareidolic faces on one of our contrast-negated stimuli. Like Paras and Webster (2013), we found that participants had a strong bias to circle faces on the midline, although some faces were identified off to either side.

Results

Two raters hand-counted the number of unique circles each participant marked per image, and there were no disagreements between raters regarding the final count per image across participants. We analyzed this count data with a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) using a Poisson distribution and log linking function implemented in JASP 3.0. We included the count data per image as the dependent variable along with a fixed effect of image contrast (positive or negative) and random effects for participant ID and image ID. In Figure 5, we display boxplot-jitter graphs for the positive and negative contrast conditions, which reveal the substantial variability we observed across individuals and images.

Box-jitter plots depicting summary statistics and the distribution of individual data points for the per-image count data obtained from enumerating participants’ circled pareidolic face patterns in our stimulus set. The solid and dashed “box” elements to the left of each collection of jittered dots indicate the median and IQR of the data. There is a great deal of variability across images and participants, consistent with prior reports describing substantial individual differences in face pareidolia.

This analysis revealed a significant effect of image contrast on the number of pareidolic face patterns reported by our participants, χ2(1) = 6.00, p = 0.014. The estimated marginal means indicate that this significant result belies a modest difference between our two contrast conditions. Positive contrast images yielded an average of 4.35 faces per image (SEM = 0.65) and negative contrast images yielded a larger average of 4.86 faces per image (SEM = 0.69).

Regarding the substantial variability we can observe in Figure 5, we also examined the data both with regard to participants and with regard to the individual stimulus images. Regarding participants, given that there is clearly high variability across observers in the two contrast conditions, we were curious how correlated participant data were across the positive and negative versions of our stimulus images. To some extent, we expect that this must be true to enough of an extent to observe the significant effect of contrast that we have reported, but nonetheless this offers an additional datapoint regarding participant consistency across image appearance. For each participant, we calculated the average count of pareidolic faces reported per image for the positive and negative conditions and calculated the correlation between these values using Spearman's rho. This analysis revealed a strong significant correlation between these values (Spearman's rho = 0.928, p < 0.001), suggesting that individual participants are consistent in reporting either relatively few or relatively many pareidolic faces in our task.

Regarding variability across images, we similarly calculated the total count of pareidolic faces reported per stimulus across all participants to see the extent to which individual images produced high versus low rates of face pareidolia (Table 1). Examining Table 1, it is evident that there are some potentially meaningful differences across images, with the lowest and highest rates differing by more than a 2:1 ratio. Although this study was not sufficiently powered to make a strong statement about these potential image effects, examining these extremal stimuli suggests that images with more fine-grained structure lead to more reported pareidolic faces. This is consistent with prior work examining pareidolia rates subject to varying power spectrum coefficients (Balas, 2025) in which similar results were observed for shallow versus steep spectral power curves.

Discussion

The results of our first experiment suggest that contrast negation may indeed weaken the interpretation of natural textures sufficiently to strengthen pareidolic face perception. This effect was relatively small but measurable despite the high variability in pareidolia rates across participants. This effect differs from the null results reported by Paras and Webster (2013) in their work with negated noise images (also with symmetry across the vertical meridian), but the use of natural texture images in our study is critical to this difference. In noise images, contrast negation turns one uninterpretable pattern into another uninterpretable pattern, presumably with similar likelihoods of encountering appropriately scaled dark blobs that can serve as candidate eye and mouth spots for a pareidolic face. In natural scenes, however, contrast negation is asymmetric insofar as the starting image is more easily interpretable than the negated one. Although contrast negation has been show to disproportionately affect human faces more than non-face objects (Vuong et al., 2005), there are measurable effects of contrast negation on texture and material perception that support our claim that negated textures such as the ones we have used will be more difficult to categorize than their positive contrast counterparts. Thus, our study does not directly conflict with prior work but rather extends our understanding of face pareidolia via the use of manipulated natural scenes.

An important possibility to consider, however, is whether or not it could be the case that contrast negation inadvertently introduced more objectively face-like patterns into our stimuli. Were this the case, our results would speak more to a curious artifact of our stimulus manipulation than to a feature of the human visual system. This is a tricky issue to address given that pareidolic faces are by their nature quite subjective and may differ substantially from the typical structure of a human face. However, to offer some insight into this issue we applied the Viola–Jones face detection model (Viola & Jones, 2001) to our stimulus images using routines in the Python implementation of OpenCV. This analysis revealed no differences in the incidence of face-like patterns in our positive and negative images, offering further support for our conclusion that it is the disruption of easily recognizable objects and textures that increases pareidolia rates in these images.

We continue by examining how contrast negation affects face pareidolia in a school-age population. As we mentioned previously, developmental trajectories for texture and material processing, sensitivity to statistical properties of natural scenes, and some aspects of face recognition extend through this age range. On the other hand, sensitivity to contrast negation as applied to simple face detection is even evident in infancy (Farroni et al., 2005). The varying trajectories of these distinct aspects of visual development thus suggest at least two clear hypotheses regarding when we might observe adult-like effects of contrast negation on face pareidolia in natural images. On one hand, we might expect that early maturity of contrast-specific templates for face detection by middle childhood would lead to adult-like increases in face pareidolia across our age range. On the other hand, the relatively slow development of texture and material processing may weaken the impact of negation on veridical object and texture interpretations of pareidolic patterns leading to an interaction between age and image contrast. Finally, independent from the effects of negation, the relatively slow development of perceptual organization abilities and the tuning of face processing to specific bands of critical features may lead to gradual increases in face pareidolia rates with age.

Experiment 2

In our second experiment, we examined how contrast negation affected rates of face pareidolia in school-age children. Using the same testing paradigm, we investigated whether adult-like impacts of negation were evident across middle childhood or if the effects of contrast negation on pareidolia only emerged later in our target age range.

Participants

We recruited a total of 72 children between the ages of 5 years and 12 years of age from the Fargo–Moorhead community. We excluded data from five participants due to technical failures during testing, participant non-compliance, or incomplete data, leading to a final sample consisting of 67 children. This sample included 40 male children (median age,7.5 years; range, 5.083–12.83; interquartile range [IQR] = 2.75) and 27 female children (median age, 7.83 years; range, 5.33–11.67 years; IQR = 3.67). All participants had either normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity as assessed using a brief HOTV eye test conducted in the lab following informed consent. A caregiver accompanying each child provided informed consent for their child to participate, and children over the age of 7 years also provided verbal assent. Participants were compensated with $10 in cash and their choice of NDSU Balas Lab stickers following completion of the task. All recruitment and testing procedures were approved by the NDSU institutional review board.

Stimuli

We used a subset of the natural texture images described in Experiment 1. Specifically, we selected half of the stimuli at random from Experiment 1 in order to shorten the overall testing time for young children. Participants in Experiment 2 thus saw positive and negative contrast versions of four unique natural texture images for a total of eight stimuli in the pareidolia task. In addition to these digital stimuli, we also asked children to complete a short warm-up pareidolia task using a Perlin noise pattern printed out on paper. This gave us the opportunity to scaffold the full experiment as administered on the iPad while also collecting independent data about pareidolia rates in an unconstrained task. This image was printed out at approximately 6 × 6 inches square in grayscale.

Procedure

As described in Experiment 1, we presented positive and negative contrast symmetric images to each participant via a custom interface developed using InQuisit 6.6.3. We instructed participants to examine each image to determine if they saw any face-like patterns in the texture and to circle any such patterns using a stylus. Prior to this task, children additionally completed a visual acuity assessment using an HOTV eye chart viewed at a distance of 10 feet, a standardized assessment of visual closure included in the Test of Visual Perceptual Skills, 4th Edition (TVPS-4), and a warm-up pareidolia task using the Perlin noise stimulus described above. Participants were given unlimited time to complete each task and were able to request breaks as needed to alleviate boredom.

Results

Visual closure and perlin noise pareidolia

First, we examined the relationship between individual variation in pareidolia rates in our warm-up Perlin noise stimulus, participant age, and visual closure abilities as assessed by the TVPS-4 test. In particular, we were interested in whether or not visual closure would make an independent contribution to explaining variation in pareidolia rates over and above the effects of age. To analyze this relationship, we carried out a generalized linear model analysis using a Poisson distribution with a log link function in which the count data from the warm-up pareidolia task was the dependent variable and participant age and performance in the visual closure task were included as covariates. Because we were specifically interested in the contribution of visual closure independent from participant age, we included age in the null model. This analysis revealed that the model including visual closure performance was significantly better than the null model, χ2(1) = 50.59, p < 0.001, and that the overall model fit was significant, R(67) = 769.68, p < 0.001. This indicates that visual closure abilities do predict face pareidolia rates independent from the effects of age. The model coefficients for all predictors are provided in Table 2.

Contrast negation in natural texture stimuli

To examine the impact of contrast negation on children's face pareidolia rates as a function of visual development, we continued by carrying out a GLMM analysis similar to that reported in Experiment 1 for our adult data. This analysis also used a Poisson distribution and a log link function, with the count data of pareidolic face reports serving as the dependent variable, image contrast and participant age as fixed effects, and item ID and participant ID as random effects. This analysis revealed significant effects of image contrast, χ2(1) = 12.55, p < 0.001, and participant age, χ2(1) = 7.90, p = 0.005. As in Experiment 1, we note that there is clearly substantial variability across observers with regard to the number of pareidolic faces reported (Figure 6). To examine participants’ consistency with regard to their individual rates of pareidolia, we carried out the same correlational analysis across observers that we described in Experiment 1: Per participant, we calculated the average number of faces reported per image in the positive and negative conditions and then examined the strength of this correlation using Spearman's rho. This analysis revealed a significant correlation between these values (Spearman's rho = 0.912, p < 0.001), demonstrating a high degree of consistency across observers with regard to pareidolia rates in our two stimulus conditions.

The results of Experiment 2 align closely with the outcome we reported in Experiment 1: Like adults, children also reported more pareidolic faces in contrast-negated images of natural textures. We interpret this as further support that negation weakens the veridical interpretation of objects and textures in natural scenes, making pareidolic interpretations more robust.

General discussion

In two experiments, we observed that contrast negation applied to natural texture images led to increased rates of face pareidolia. We interpret this to mean that, although contrast negation dramatically disrupts natural appearance, any tuning of face detection mechanisms to natural scene structure makes less of a contribution to the experience of pareidolia than the removal of competing percepts of the real objects, surfaces, and textures in a real-world scene.

Developmentally, we interpret the consistency of this result across our middle-childhood sample as evidence that pareidolia is likely best understood in terms of the development of coarse templates for face detection rather than more complex mechanisms that support face identification or holistic processing. This is consistent with the results of Omer et al. (2019) demonstrating that the presence of putative eye and mouth features in pareidolic stimuli was critical for perceiving face-ness in non-face images, as well as with the early sensitivity to similar schematic stimuli in infancy that typically only contain dark spot triads arranged in an eye-and-mouth first-order configuration (Farroni et al., 2005). Furthermore, although sensitivity to the redundancy of natural scenes exhibits slow development during middle childhood (Ellemberg et al., 2012), sensitivity to contrast polarity in natural scenes and textures appears to develop on a faster timescale. For example, Balas and Woods (2014) reported that 9-month-old infants were sensitive to the contrast polarity of natural texture patches in a visual preference task, exhibiting weaker preferences for natural textures (as opposed to synthetic textures) when stimuli were negated. Using event-related potentials with a middle-childhood sample similar to that reported here, Balas, Saville, and Schmidt (2018) reported consistent effects of contrast negation on the N1 response to natural texture patches across a 5- to 10-year-old age range, with only sensitivity at the earlier P1 component showing signs of emerging sensitivity to negation with increased age. Although there remains a great deal of work yet to be done to characterize infants’ and children's sensitivity to natural scene statistics over the course of visual development (Newman, McAdams, Yuill, & Skelton, 2025), we take the present results as evidence that the sensitivity of face pareidolia to contrast negation in adults and children reflects the application of a template for face detection that is sufficiently mature as early as infancy (Kato & Mugitani, 2015; Otsuka et al., 2013) to natural scenes and textures that are processed in a manner that incorporates information about contrast polarity into processes for recognition across our age range. That is, pareidolic faces are detected efficiently by all age groups due to the presence of candidate eye and mouth features, and veridical interpretations are weakened for all age groups due to an early maturation of sensitivity to reversed contrast in natural images.

In addition to the effects of negation on rates of face pareidolia, our finding that these rates increase steadily with age across our middle-childhood sample is also, to our knowledge, a novel result. Whereas Wardle et al. (2023) reported a modest difference in pareidolic responses to non-face images when comparing their youngest age group (4–8 years old) to adults, this difference was small in magnitude (∼4%) and potentially subject to ceiling effects, given the very high rate of pareidolic responses to non-face images across all age groups (even the youngest children responded “face” 88% of the time). The present data do not allow us to determine if this reflects perceptual development (either face-specific or more generally related to the development of visual search) (Meissner et al., 2018) or changes in response bias with age, which we suggest is an important question for future developmental studies of face pareidolia. Although our unconstrained task has several advantages, one obvious disadvantage of the approach with children is that changes in children's willingness to spend more time on-task could easily lead to more frequent reports of pareidolic faces. To better understand the nature of this developmental trend, complementing our current approach with psychophysical testing using isolated stimuli is likely an important idea. Another advantage of single-image assessment of face pareidolia in children is the potential to apply reverse correlation methods to estimate the perceptual template children use to make decisions about pareidolic stimuli (see Hansen, Thompson, Hess, & Ellemberg, 2010). We note, however, that one of the advantages of our unconstrained task is the potential for children and adults to identify pareidolic faces with a structure that differs from real human face configurations, sometimes profoundly. Peterson et al. (2025), for example, found that an open-ended pareidolia task led to participant reports of face and non-face objects, with some faces described as being “alien” or “insect”-like in appearance. This suggests that the experience of face pareidolia may have a broader subjective profile than that assumed by adopting the proposal that templates for human face detection and its typical configuration are fundamental to the experience of face pareidolia. Examining and comparing the micropatterns children and adults identify in a range of natural and unnatural images may reveal important insights into the bandwidth of the perceptual template that governs the experience of face pareidolia.

Finally, we note that it is important to consider our results in light of the specific stimuli that we used to examine face pareidolia. First, like Peterson et al. (2025), this study was limited to grayscale images, thereby removing any potential contribution of color information to the pareidolic experience. Exploring the role of chromatic appearance both in its own right and subject to negation would be an intriguing extension of the current approach. Although face color generally does not contribute much to face identification (Yip & Sinha, 2002), Simpson, Maylott, Leonard, Lazo, and Jakobsen (2019) found that infants exhibited less sensitivity to color in a face detection task than adults, suggesting tuning for human face appearance that may be broader with regard to chroma than adults. The large gap between their 3- to 5-month-old participants and adults raises the obvious question of how children in our target age range would perform in a similar task, as well as what this might suggest with regard to the patterns they would endorse as pareidolic faces compared with adults when color information is available. Another key feature of our design is the use of artificially imposed bilateral symmetry, which we opted to use as a means of increasing overall pareidolia rates per Paras and Webster (2013). Although this manipulation cannot solely account for the difference we reported between positive and negative contrast conditions (see Paras and Webster's results regarding negation in their noise stimuli), it is an open question as to how removing this symmetry would affect our results. Given the strength of pareidolia induced by bilateral symmetry, it is possible that removing this would lower overall pareidolia rates sufficiently to make our effect more difficult to measure. Still, examining this question using images without symmetry, or with parametrically varying symmetry, would be a useful extension of this work. Likewise, further examination of the variation in pareidolia rates across natural texture images would be a useful means of bridging results obtained from fractal noise textures and with more naturalistic stimuli. Finally, our images (and other stimuli used in prior pareidolia studies) differ substantially from images that depict objects or object parts (for examples, see our Figures 1 and 2) that elicit strong pareidolic percepts. Compared with these stimuli, our images offer far less constraint on what the observer is likely to see in terms of face-like structure, thereby allowing much more potential for high variability across observers and images. This offers both an opportunity and a challenge, and we suggest that a particularly useful direction for future work is to examine the relationship between face pareidolia as elicited by more directed stimuli (objects and object–part images) and relatively unconstrained stimuli like those used here. Although the outcome of seeing something face-like is intuitively the same, the mechanisms that support pareidolia in these two settings may in fact differ in important ways.

In sum, the current study reveals both potential development change in the perception of face pareidolia while also identifying mechanisms supporting pareidolic face perception that appear to be mature early in childhood. Our approach to measuring face pareidolia rates via an unconstrained task offers a useful means of collecting developmental data with an engaging and intuitive task while giving participants sufficient flexibility of response to accommodate individual variability in what does and does not constitute a face-like pattern. Leveraging the richness of data collected with this paradigm is an important direction for future work, potentially revealing deeper insights into how the perceptual templates and decision-making processes that underlie perceiving meaningful visual structure in natural scenes develop.
