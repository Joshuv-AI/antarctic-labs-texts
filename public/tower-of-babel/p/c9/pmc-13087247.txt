Association of park features with total and prolonged sedentary behavior among older adults: An observational study in Macau
Authors: Yashan Chen, Junming Zheng, Linsheng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChanging the park features can contribute to increasing park-based physical activity, but many older adults still engage in sedentary behavior (SB). The relationship between park-based SB and park features remains unclear. This cross-sectional study aimed to examine total and prolonged SB in older adults in urban parks and to explore the associations between such behavior and specific park features. Using systematic observation and covert video recording, this study collected behavioral data from older adults across 51 sub-areas within three Macau parks. The primary type of prolonged SB was identified using analysis of variance. To test these associations, negative binomial generalized linear mixed models and Firth’s penalized logistic regression were employed. The results indicated that card/board game playing was associated with the most prolonged sedentary bouts. Shade (IRR = 4.98, p < 0.05) was the only feature significantly predictive of total SB and served as a necessary condition for prolonged SB. The presence of outdoor table and chairs showed a significant and robust association with prolonged SB (aOR = 10.16, p < 0.001). Current parks are often designed for passive sitting rather than promoting intermittent activity. Therefore, this study proposes design strategies to encourage active use, offering a novel approach to park design.Keywords: Green space, Sedentary bout, Park use, System for Observing Play and Recreation in Communities (SOPARC), Physical activity, Health behaviorSubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Health care, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Global population aging, driven by continuous improvements in longevity, is fundamentally reshaping contemporary demographic profiles. East Asia, especially Japan, South Korea, and China, leads the world in terms of the speed and scale of aging1. Older adults are the group with the highest prevalence and incidence of chronic diseases2. In the context of aging, unprecedented attention has been paid to the concern and prevention of chronic diseases. High levels of sedentary behavior (SB) have become widespread and are associated with an increased risk of some chronic diseases3. Older adults are the most sedentary group4, spending 60%−70% of their waking time in SB5, with the majority occurring during leisure time6. To change this situation, the World Health Organization calls for replacing sedentary time with physical activity (PA) of any intensity.

Urban parks are the main place for recreation and PA among older adults in high-density cities in Asia7. Different park activities can have varying degrees of positive effects on the physical and mental health of older adults. Yet, other activities do not achieve the equivalent effects of PA8. As a result, a large number of studies have focused on park features related to the promotion of PA9,10; however, people who increase their PA levels may even become more sedentary because they feel content having achieved adequate PA levels11. This suggests that interventions to promote PA have had limited effectiveness in changing SB12. In contrast, this study shifts the focus from PA itself to the types and patterns of SB in parks.

SB refers to any waking activity characterized by an energy expenditure of ≦ 1.5 metabolic equivalents (METs) while in a sitting, reclining, or lying position13. Different types of SB may have different health effects. Some outdoor sitting activities, such as chatting with friends, and participating in group activities, play an important role in the social well-being of older adults14. However, either activity, when it results in regular prolonged SB, may have a negative impact on the physical health among older adults15. Thus, the temporal attributes of park-based SB are key to determining whether it constitutes active rest or a potential risk.

SB patterns are an important measure, referring to the accumulation of SB through bouts and breaks. A period of continuous sedentary time is called a sedentary bout, and the time between two bouts is called a break16. Some studies suggest that reducing sedentary bouts and increasing breaks yield health benefits17. Only prolonged SB exceeding a certain threshold will significantly increase the incidence of non-communicable diseases and all-cause mortality18. Although there is no unified standard for the threshold, a bout lasting ≥ 30 min is generally considered prolonged16. It is more feasible to reduce sedentary bout duration and increase breaks through intervention than to promote PA5.

Studies of urban planning tend to suggest that a higher density of parks, shorter distances to reach them, and better street walkability are associated with reduced sedentary time and increased breaks for SB19–21. But this involves the assumption that proximity to parks equals park use22, and park use equals more PA. This is not entirely true23. In contrast, a considerable proportion of older adults are sedentary in parks24,25. Preferences for PA and SB may coexist in older adults’ outdoor activities. As for the park environment, some studies have concluded that older adults generally prefer areas in the parks with an adequate and favorable supply of seating, and tend to sit more in areas with more seating26–28. Another view is that easily accessible seating may promote PA among older adults because of their decreased mobility, and that the lack of such support is likely to limit their outdoor activities29. The area size is also a factor affecting the use. A larger activity area may attract more users or make the activity less dense30,31. To my knowledge, only one Australian study has specifically examined the relationship between park environments and park-based SB. However, this study was limited to analyzing landcover features associated with SB and did not incorporate other environmental factors10. Although the above factors may be related to the frequency of sitting activities, their relationship to prolonged SB remains unclear. We know very little about park-based sedentary patterns. Currently, few studies have specifically measured sedentary bouts in parks and examined their related features, which hinders the development of corresponding interventions.

To narrow this gap, this study examines the characteristics of SB among older adults in parks and identifies the types of prolonged SB along with their associated park features. Based on this, this study addresses the following research questions:

(1) What are the characteristics of park-based SB among older adults in terms of frequency of occurrence, type, and duration of bouts?

(2) Which types of park-based SB are associated with prolonged bouts? Is SB among older adults an active choice or a passive acceptance?

(3) Which park features are associated with total and prolonged SB in the park, respectively?

Data and methodology

Study Area

Macau, a typical high-density city in Asia, accommodates 686,000 people in a land area of 32.9 km2, with a population density of about 20,900 people per square kilometer. The older population (age ≧ 65) accounts for 17.6%, indicating that it is a deeply aging region. The most densely populated area of Macau, the Macau Peninsula, with a population density of 56,000 people per square kilometer, was chosen for this study.

There is a total of 25 urban parks in the Macau Peninsula, with areas ranging from 0.1 hm2 to 12.4hm2 and a median of 1.0hm2. Considering that Macau is a tourist city, parks near tourist attractions are visited by more tourists, which may affect the accuracy of the data. Variations in its spatial layout, landscape, and facility configurations can directly shape usage patterns and frequency. Boundaries were delineated based on clear features like pavement edges or building outlines. Where such features were absent, the observed area of human activity defined the boundary. It was observed that seated older adults predominantly occupied paved open spaces, with minimal use of lawns, slopes, or paths. Consequently, these sub-areas included nearly all such paved open spaces in the parks, and the data collected from these sub-areas captured approximately 95% of all observed SB among older adults, indicating their representativeness. Of the 51 sub-areas, 21 were from Luis de Camoes Park (Figure. 1b), 22 were from Hac Sa Van Park(Figure. 1c), and the remaining 8 were from Hac Sa Van Triangular Park (Figure. 1 d).

Data collection and measurements

This study employed a cross-sectional observational design. During September and October 2024, systematic observation was used to collect data on sub-areas’ features and older adults’ activities, while covert video recording was employed to capture the duration of sedentary bouts.

Regarding the measured features of the sub-areas, four variables were included: seating density, seat type, presence of shade, and sub-area size. Their measurement methods and justification are provided in Table 1. The procedure begins by obtaining the park master plan, followed by field visits where observers compared and updated the plan with missing information.

Older adults’ activity data were collected by the System for Observing Play and Recreation in Communities (SOPARC), including the number of older adults, gender, and their behavior within each sub-area. The SOPARC protocol is extensively applied in park visitor behavior observation, with established validity and reliability24. For the Macau context, we made two modifications to the standard SOPARC tool: (1)removing the “Race” column, as nearly all observed older adults were Asian; (2)adding more detailed codes for SB, particularly the accompanying activities performed while sitting37.

Before data collection, observers were trained for two days on how to code activities and how to distinguish between older and middle-aged people based on observable physical characteristics. This requires a high degree of consistency among post-training observers38. Inter-observer reliability was assessed by having two observers independently code the same park. The kappa statistic was calculated at 0.83, indicating substantial agreement.

The formal survey was conducted on four days (two weekdays, two weekend days), with four observation periods per day, for a total of 16 observation periods of older adults’ activities in each sub-area. To ensure data coverage across variations in park visitor flow, observations were divided into four periods: Period 1 (6:30–8:30), Period 2 (8:30–10:30), Period 3 (14:00–16:00), and Period 4 (16:00–18:00). The observation period excluded midday and nighttime. This exclusion was made because park visitation is typically low at midday due to intense sunlight and high temperatures34, and because older adult activity in parks at night is minimal due to insufficient lighting. Based on the SOPARC manual, two observers independently scanned each sub-area and recorded data using a modified SOPARC tool during observation.

Sedentary bouts were collected by covert video recording and data was extracted by reviewing the video. The data included: gender, type of SB, and duration of a sedentary bout. This method was chosen because studies have found that older adults are often unable to accurately measure sedentary time39. Moreover, the more sedentary people are, the more likely they are to conceal, avoid, or misreport their actual hours40. Covert non-participant observation avoids the Hawthorne effect and is therefore an ideal data collection method41.

Sub-areas where SB occurred were randomly selected during different observation periods, and observers were recorded in an unnoticeable manner from a certain distance away. The duration of the filming session was consistently 90 min. There may be people who enter in the middle of these 90-minute periods, as well as people who stop their SB and leave. During data extraction, video-recorded sedentary bouts were classified as valid if they met the following criterion: a complete cycle of an older adult being seated (from sitting to standing) within a single continuous video segment. To ensure that the data are easily comparable, at least 10% of the total number of older adults were collected for each type of SB as a sample size.

Declaration of informed consent

This study, including the use of covert video recording for behavioral observation, was reviewed and approved by the Putian University Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2024035). The covert video recording procedure was designed for naturalistic observation. To preserve the authenticity of behaviors, the recording was conducted without prior notification. Verbal consent was subsequently obtained from the older adults observed, immediately after each recording session concluded. The observers asked older adults whether they agreed to be observed, video-recorded, and included in the research data. They also promised that all videos and photographic materials would be used only for academic research and that identifying information would not be made public. While we could not obtain individual consent from every incidental park visitor recorded, we secured general verbal consent from the core group of older adults who frequented the study areas and were the focus of our observations. Videos and photographs of any observer who did not consent were removed from the study. No personal information was published directly in this paper, and the necessary images have been privacy-protected so that personal information cannot be obtained indirectly from the images.

Statistical analyses

First, descriptive statistics were used to analyze the overall characteristics of SB among older adults in 51 sub-areas. The chi-square test was used to examine differences by gender, observation period, and type of SB.

Second, a one-way ANOVA was used to test for differences in bout duration among SB types. Based on significant ANOVA results, post hoc HSD tests, in conjunction with descriptive analyses, identified the SB type most likely to be associated with prolonged SB.

Third, the total SB data represent repeated measures nested within sub-areas. To address the issue of sub-area non-independence, we employed a negative binomial generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with random intercepts to examine the association between park features and total SB. The negative binomial distribution was chosen over Poisson to account for overdispersion (variance > mean). Sub-area ID was included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements. Fixed effects included seating density, seat types, the presence of shade, and sub-area size. An observation period was also included to adjust for potential temporal confounding. To examine whether the influences of these features were specific to SB, we fitted separate models for total SB and PA counts. To further investigate features associated with prolonged SB, we examined the associations between park features and the occurrence of this specific behavior type. The outcome was a binary variable indicating whether prolonged SB occurred at a sub-area. To control for potential confounders and address estimation issues due to complete separation (zero-frequency cells), we performed multivariable logistic regression using Firth’s penalized likelihood method. This approach provides bias-reduced estimates and effectively handles small-sample bias and complete separation. All continuous predictors were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) to facilitate interpretation and model convergence. Statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.1 using the “lme4” package for GLMMs and the “logistf” package for Firth regression.

Occurrence and types of park-based SB in parks

The chi-square test showed significant differences in the percentage of SB by gender, observation period, and behavior type (p < 0.001). Older sedentary males (33.41%) had a higher percentage than older sedentary females (23.81%). The period with the highest SB ratio mainly occurred in the late afternoon (16:00–18:00), and the lowest ratio occurred in the early morning (6:30 − 8:30). SB types were classified into five categories based on accompanying activities, including seated chatting (SC), seated mobile phone use (SMP), playing seated card/board games (SCB), onlooking in a seated position (SO), and seated rest or scenic viewing (SR). It is worth pointing out that the SO observed in parks were for the purpose of watching cards/board games. That is, two types of SB were triggered by cards/board games, the sum of SCB and SO, which accounted for 16.51% of the total, second only to SC. In the 51 sub-areas, the mean number of older users was 6.38 (SD 7.72, rang 0–43), with an average of 3.65 observed to be sedentary (SD 5.57, rang 0–33).

For the other four types, there were many points clustered on the 0-value line and a few larger values. It suggested that these behaviors do not occur in many sub-areas, but are frequent in a few. SCB, in particular, were uncommon (occurring in only 11 sub-areas), but when they did occur, they usually clustered in larger numbers of older adults (Fig. 4).

Informal observation of cards/board games

In addition to the high level of clustering (both onlookers and players) and the prolonged sedentary bouts shown by the study data, informal observations revealed that most people do not leave at the end of a game, but continue or take a break and start the next game. If attended continuously, an extremely prolonged sedentary bout will occur. Longitudinally, the occurrence of card/board games was also highly regular, with the same group of participants generally gathering spontaneously at fixed times of the day to play in fixed areas of the park, so the accumulation of sedentary time may be at its peak.

Furthermore, it was observed that some of the older adults showed intermittent body posture shifts (Fig. 6), such as squatting and standing bent over, in addition to sitting during the card/board games. Through the interviews, it was learned that they use this approach to relieve discomfort such as stiffness and soreness associated with prolonged sitting, and a small number of them hold the perception of the health hazards of being sedentary, and therefore wish to increase their PA to minimise the negative effects. Considering this additional information, card/board games are the type most likely to lead older adults to accumulate prolonged sedentary time, due to their long duration, high social clustering, and high regularity of play.

Differentiated influences park features on total versus prolonged SB in parks

Model diagnostics indicated that the negative binomial distribution adequately captured over-dispersion in both models (model 1: θ = 11.14; model 2: θ = 4.67), and residual checks supported the respective model assumptions. Table 4 presents the results of the negative binomial mixed-effects models. In model 1, after controlling for observation period and sub-area random effects, the presence of shade was significantly associated with total SB (IRR = 4.98, 95% CI [1.37, 18.10], p = 0.015), indicating that the incidence of SB in shaded areas was nearly five times higher than in non-shaded areas. The effects of seating density, seat type, and sub-area size were all insignificant. Compared with observation period 1 (reference), the incidence of SB was significantly higher in all subsequent periods, with incidence rate ratios (IRRs) ranging from 3.65 to 5.61 (all p < 0.001). In Model 2, sub-area size had a significant positive effect on PA (IRR = 1.61, 95% CI [1.15,2.26], p = 0.005). In both models, the standard deviations of the random intercepts for the observed sub-areas were 1.38 and 1.03, indicating substantial residual variation in baseline SB and PA levels across these units, even after controlling for all predictor variables.

Discussion

Regarding the first research question, the prevalence of park-based SB among older adults in Macau was generally consistent with levels observed in other East Asian cities such as Guangzhou (47%) and Hong Kong (50%)25,42, while remaining lower than rates reported in Western contexts like Australia (70%) and the United States (72%)24,33. This pattern aligns with the broader observation that older adults in East Asia tend to be more active park users. However, a study in Taipei reported a notably lower proportion of only 9%37. This contrast suggests that there remains meaningful potential for further reduction in Macau, as the Taipei example demonstrates that well-equipped parks with organized, volunteer-led activities can more effectively engage older adults and reduce SB. The amount of park-based SB showed significant gender and period differences. In China, most studies support a higher frequency of SB in males, consistent with the results of this study43,44. The study also found that older adults’ park usage and PA exhibited a distinct “double-peak” pattern, with high usage occurring in the morning (8:00–10:30) and late afternoon (16:00–18:00). PA was minimal during the midday heat (14:00–16:00), consistent with thermal avoidance43,45. SB, however, showed no concurrent decline, arguably because it often occurs in shaded areas. Among the five types of park-based SB, chatting and rest or scenic viewing are common worldwide33,46. However, activities such as card/board games and onlooking are rooted in specific cultural leisure traditions44. These activities are particularly prevalent in Chinese parks34,47, which distinguishes them from park activities common in other cultural contexts. In addition, measurements of sedentary bouts revealed that the average sedentary bout among older adults in the park exceeded 30 min, surpassing the threshold commonly associated with prolonged SB. This finding not only confirms the observation that older adults who prefer sitting indeed remain sedentary for extended periods in park settings, but also suggests that the park environment primarily fulfills the need for “comfortable sitting” while failing to encourage “intermittent activity” effectively. This inference is supported by previous studies, which suggest that there is a closer association between single sedentary episodes lasting around 20–30 min or longer and environmental factors21.

It was found that card/board games are the activity most strongly associated with prolonged sedentary bouts (exceeding 30 min) among older adults. This finding directly addresses our second research question. Such uninterrupted bouts of 30 min or more accounted for approximately 58% of total sedentary time21,48. Furthermore, the occurrence of card/board games has also been found to have be highly regular. The impact of this park-based SB on the health of older adults is mixed. These types of outdoor social recreational activities play a beneficial role in the cognitive performance, mental health, and social connectedness of older adults10,49. However, current social norms and the physical environments of parks often make such activities only possible while sitting, and the persistent lack of daily energy expenditure and muscle activation resulting from habitual SB may accelerate physical limitations and increase the risk of disease and disability that threaten older adults’ health32. In fact, it was not that they subjectively chose to be sedentary. Informal observations showed that they tried to change their sitting posture. These may once again indicate that the park environment has failed to provide better features, rather than that they do not need them50.

In response to the third research question, shade was the only park feature that showed a significant and strong association with total SB. Previous studies support this finding, as shade provides the necessary comfort environment for older adults to engage in leisure activities33. This also explains why SB among the older adults did not decrease significantly even during the peak heat of midday. In contrast, only sub-area size showed a significant positive association with PA; shade had no significant effect. This indicates that park features exert distinct effects on the two behaviors, further confirming that “reducing SB and promoting PA require distinct intervention strategies”. It also highlights the importance of this study’s specific focus on SB. Reasonable improvements to park features related to SB need not raise excessive concern about potentially altering levels of PA. Additionally, this study found a strong and robust association between table and chairs and prolonged SB. Group leisure activities such as card/board games rely more heavily on centripetal furniture layouts, as they facilitate face-to-face interaction and thereby extend the duration of stay. Existing studies have noted the role of table and chairs in promoting leisure activities34,51. This study is the first to quantify their significant association with SB among older adults through regression models, further supporting this conclusion. Although the estimated effect size for shaded was large (OR = 16.53), its 95% confidence interval was extremely wide (2.30–2097.86). This statistical uncertainty stemmed from the observed “complete segregation” in the data, where all 92 prolonged SB incidents occurred in shaded areas. Therefore, shading is more like a necessary but insufficient condition for prolonged SB. It provides the fundamental environmental precondition for avoiding sun exposure and improving thermal comfort. Yet, the actual occurrence of this behavior remains influenced by multiple factors such as seat type, time period, and individual characteristics. The significant impact of observation periods on SB reflects the temporal patterns of leisure activities among older adults. Since period effects are difficult to address directly through park design, the focus should be on modifiable physical features. Specifically, optimizing shade and seating configurations can provide older adults with more supportive outdoor spaces that encourage breaks in prolonged SB.

There are still some shortcomings in this study. First, this study incorporated the four controversial park features identified in the literature. However, model results indicated that significant baseline differences between sites persisted even after controlling for these observable features (SB: 1.38; PA: 1.03). This suggests that unmeasured factors such as accessibility and safety perceptions may be at play, highlighting a need for future research to explore other features. Second, the sample size of this study was not large enough, and more robust results and more findings might have been possible if more urban parks had been included. Third, the prolonged SB examined in this study has exhibited distinct cultural specificity influenced by regional leisure habits among older adults. This particular context not only serves as the empirical starting point for this research but also implies that relevant conclusions require careful consideration when extended to other cultural settings. Finally, SB was measured using on-site observation, which may not capture the full extent of sedentary time across different periods and settings; future research could incorporate accelerometer-based measurements for greater precision.

Future research should more comprehensively investigate the patterns of park-based SB among older adults and more diverse populations, and compare the behavioral characteristics in different types of parks and under the influence of different features. More usefully, if intervention studies on park-based SB were conducted through natural experiments, this would help provide stronger evidence and more effective measures. In addition, encouraging qualitative research to more deeply analyze the real intentions and value judgments of sedentary people will provide important new insights.

Implications

Building upon the data-driven interpretations above, the ideas presented below explore possible directions for future design and investigative work. They are presented as conceptual implications that extend beyond our immediate findings, aiming to highlight the potential of applying these insights to age-friendly park design. We put them forward as speculative starting points for planners and researchers to consider, with the goal of inspiring and structuring future inquiry and innovation.

Directly increasing PA levels can be particularly challenging for older adults, especially those accustomed to being sedentary and play games52. While frequent sit-to-stand transitions are an effective way to break up sedentary time, existing park facilities often fail to support older adults in performing such movements during sedentary activities. With reference to intervention strategies in the workplace53, attempts can be made to bring in removable high-legged table and stools that are height-appropriate for older adults in areas where the cards/board games take place (Fig. 7). Older adults can choose to remove the stool and stand for a while after they have sat down enough, or they can sit back down at any time. By raising the height of the table, it enables freer sit-to-stand movement during activities. In addition, it has been found that the standing position does not negatively affect task performance53, so this form of furniture is less likely to interfere with the normal conduct of recreational activities.

Application scenarios of high-legged table and stools. (Image source: drawn by the author).

Another strategy can be seating optimization, where fitness equipment is combined with seating furniture to increase energy expenditure in the seated state of older adults. Combining a mini elliptical device with a chair may be a meaningful attempt to allow older adults to shift SB to low-intensity PA by increasing energy expenditure through leg activity during sedentary times (Fig. 8). Studies have demonstrated that this device can be used effectively in older adults’ leisure time, and that energy expenditure for seated use of the device can reach 2–2.6 METs54. Since this device allows users to continue their primary seated activity while pedaling, it demonstrates better application prospects.

Application scenarios for seat combined with mini elliptical device. (Image source: drawn by the author).

Drawing inspiration from park fitness stations, establish “outdoor leisure stations” that integrate high-legged table and stools, seat combined mini elliptical devices, and traditional furniture. Additionally, incorporate smart screens to track sitting duration and provide posture reminders, thereby optimizing the activity environment for older adults. Furthermore, more flexible and mixed spatial layouts can be adopted. Placing amenities such as water fountains and restrooms near seating areas improves accessibility and can naturally prompt more frequent movement. Integrating these “outdoor recreation stations” with low-intensity exercise facilities—and connecting them to walking paths—enables easier transitions from sitting to light activity. Furthermore, offering structured group activities led by trained facilitators can effectively attract older adults and promote sustained participation55.

Conclusion

This study focuses on the characteristics and correlates of total and prolonged SB among older adults in urban parks of Macau. The findings indicate that more than half of park-based SB occurs among older adults. The average bout duration of SB exceeded 30 min. Among five common park-based sedentary activities, playing cards/board games is most likely to be associated with prolonged sedentary time. Older adults also attempted postural changes during prolonged SB, yet no park facilities supported sit-to-stand movement. Among four park features, shade showed a significant positive correlation with total SB and provided an environmental foundation for prolonged SB. The availability of outdoor table and chairs was associated with more frequent prolonged SB. We offer a set of conceptual ideas for future age-friendly park design based on our data-driven interpretations, such as seat furniture that facilitates sit-to-stand movement and flexible site layouts, with the goal of reducing sedentary time among older adults. This study provides novel insights into achieving healthy aging in urban parks in Asia, with the hope that adapting park features to create environmental support can offer older adults more suitable and healthier options, rather than forcing passive changes upon them.
