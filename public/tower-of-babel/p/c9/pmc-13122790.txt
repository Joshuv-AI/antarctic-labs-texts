Ambulance clinicians exposure to out-of-hospital cardiac arrest over 14 years in London, UK: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Rachael Fothergill, Matthew Dodd, Tim Clayton, Adam Mellett-Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•EMS clinicians in London attend OHCA on average 4 times per year, once every 63 days.•The rate of exposure to cardiac arrest is declining in London.•Specialist clinicians have a very high rate of exposure to these incidents.•Staff attend children in cardiac arrest only once every 6 years on average.•Traumatic cardiac arrests are attended on average once every 3.7 years.

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) continues to be a major global health concern, with incidence levels increasing and survival rates remaining persistently low. Registry data from North America, Europe, Asia, and Oceania demonstrate an increase in Emergency Medical Services (EMS)-treated OHCA from 30.0–97.1 per 100,000 population in 2015, to 40.8–100.2 per 100,000 in 2017.1 Paradoxically, despite the increasing burden of OHCA, individual paramedic exposure to such events is relatively infrequent and may be declining.

Research examining paramedic exposure to OHCA is limited both in volume and geographical scope, with few studies published in the past decade. Observational data from North America report that paramedics attended an average of 8–10 OHCAs annually.2, 3, 4 Substantially lower rates were observed in Australia, where clinicians were found to attend 1–2 OHCAs per year on average, along with declining rates over time and prolonged periods of no OHCA exposure.5, 6

Low levels of paramedic exposure to OHCA have important implications for resuscitation performance and patient outcomes. Advanced life support skills can decay rapidly,7 with a lack of exposure to such events being a recognised barrier to maintaining resuscitation competency.8 Consistent with this, higher cumulative levels of OHCA experience have been associated with better resuscitation outcomes.4, 6, 9 Recency of OHCA exposure also appears to be an important determinant of outcome.6 Accordingly, the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) recommend that EMS systems monitor clinician exposure to OHCA and ensure that they maintain recent experience of such events.10

Understanding the frequency and nature of paramedic exposure to OHCA is essential to informing training strategies, mitigating skill decay and improving patient outcomes. Additionally, EMS systems and the paramedic workforce have undergone substantial change in recent years, including the introduction of specialist critical care paramedics who are specifically tasked with responding to high-acuity patients such as those experiencing OHCA. The effect of these system-level changes on individual clinician exposure to OHCA is unclear. This study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of OHCA exposure rates among ambulance clinicians within a UK EMS system and addresses a long-standing gap in the literature.

Methods

Design

This retrospective observational study examined exposure to OHCA among full-time clinical staff employed by the London Ambulance Service NHS Trust (LAS) over a 14-year period (1 January 2010–31 December 2023). Pseudo-anonymised personnel data were linked to routinely collected, anonymised LAS OHCA registry data. Neither Health Research Authority nor NHS Research Ethics Committee approval were required as the study involved the secondary use of routinely collected non-identifiable data. Local approval, under the Trust’s research governance process, was granted. No specific funding was received from public, commercial, or not-for-profit funding bodies.

Study setting

The LAS is the primary provider of unscheduled urgent and emergency out-of-hospital care across Greater London, UK, serving an estimated population of 8.95 million people in 2023 over approximately 607 square miles.11 More than 3500 clinicians are employed by LAS in patient-facing frontline roles. They collectively attend over 1.2 million incidents each year, which includes attempting the resuscitation of over 4000 cardiac arrest patients. Clinical staff include registered paramedics, who are autonomous healthcare professionals trained to deliver Advanced Life Support (ALS), non-registered clinicians who provide Immediate Life Support (ILS) interventions, and specialist clinicians such as clinical managers and advanced critical care paramedics.

Advanced critical care paramedics were introduced within the LAS in 2014, with the programme providing up to five of these specialist clinicians across London at any one time. Critical care paramedics undertake additional post-graduate education and training, operate with an extended scope of practice, and are selectively dispatched to critically unwell patients where their extended skills and advanced interventions may be beneficial.

Emergency calls triaged as a potential cardiac arrest receive a standardised response involving a minimum of four clinicians deployed via double-crewed ambulance and/or solo responder vehicles. This response includes at least one registered paramedic (ALS provider) and an ambulance suitable for patient transport. Clinical managers may be dispatched to provide managerial support and clinical leadership. Critical care paramedics are dispatched following secondary call interrogation by another critical care paramedic in the ambulance control centre or at the request of on-scene clinicians. Resuscitation is performed in accordance with international guidelines and is withheld in cases where obvious signs of death or conditions associated with futility are identified.12, 13, 14

Data collection

Pseudo-anonymised data were extracted from the LAS personnel database for all clinicians in full-time employment between 1st January 2010 and 31st December 2023. Extracted data included a unique staff identifier, dates of employment, and clinical grade, but contained no identifiable data nor data capable of re-identifying individual staff members. The study population comprised of patient-facing ambulance staff, including registered paramedics, non-registered clinicians, clinical managers, and critical care paramedics. For analytical purposes registered paramedics and non-registered clinicians were grouped as ‘frontline clinicians’ due to shared dispatch criteria.

Staff, or periods of employment, in non-patient facing roles and in other specialist teams with distinct operating criteria (e.g. Hazardous Area Response Team) were excluded, as OHCA exposure in these roles is not comparable to the frontline ambulance workforce. To account for clinicians holding multiple roles with differing dispatch profiles, and initial non-patient-facing training periods, we excluded any periods of employment in eligible roles that were below full-time equivalent contracted hours (37.5 h per week). This approach was necessary because for clinicians with multiple roles, it was not possible to determine which role they were undertaking on the days they attended an OHCA incident. In addition, part-time clinical staff have a proportionally greater amount of non-patient-facing time (e.g. mandatory training and administration duties) than full-time staff, which could not be accurately quantified nor adjusted for in our analysis. Remaining eligible periods of employment for these employees were retained in the analysis. Staff employed for less than six months during the study period were also excluded.

Personnel data were linked to pseudonymised patient-level cardiac arrest data from the LAS Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest Registry using the unique staff identifier and routinely collected vehicle dispatch and staffing records. Only cases in which resuscitation was attempted were included in the linkage and our analysis. For personnel records that matched to an OHCA event, data including age, aetiology of cardiac arrest, and initial arrest rhythm were extracted from the registry.

Statistical analysis

For each included staff member, the total number of OHCA attendances during eligible (full-time) periods of employment in the study period was calculated. Data were summarised using the median with interquartile range (IQR). Mean rates were also calculated and presented along with standard deviations (SD) where appropriate.

Annual attendance rates were calculated by dividing the number of OHCA attendances by the duration of employment and expressed per year (365 days). The mean annual attendance rate and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated. Given potential skewness, the median annual rate and its 95% CI were also estimated using non-parametric bootstrap resampling (2000 replications of size 200).

Annual attendance rates were stratified by patient age group (adult ≥18 years, versus paediatric), OHCA cause (traumatic versus non-traumatic), initial recorded rhythm (shockable versus non-shockable), and staff role (frontline ambulance clinician, clinical manager, or critical care paramedic). Temporal trends in annual rates were plotted over calendar time and assessed using linear regression, with year as a continuous predictor to estimate the average change in rate per year.

The mean interval between OHCA attendances was calculated as the total number of follow-up days divided by the total number of attendances across all included staff. Corresponding 95% CIs were estimated using delta method approximations. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

OHCA incidents

During the study period, 61,862 OHCA incidents in which resuscitation was attempted were recorded in the LAS Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest Registry. Of these, 61,412 (99.3%) were attended by at least one clinician included in the study population. The number of unique OHCA incidents attended by clinicians in our analysis increased from 4212 in 2010, to 4531 in 2023.

Most OHCA incidents involved adult patients (58,151/59,936; 97.0%), with paediatric cases accounting for a much smaller proportion (1785/59,936; 3.0%). Non-traumatic causes of OHCA accounted for 95.7% of all incidents, and most patients presented in a non-shockable initial rhythm (79.9%).

Overall exposure

Of the 8340 clinicians included in the analysis, 8149 (97.7%) attended at least one OHCA incident in which resuscitation was attempted during their eligible period of employment. Across the study period, clinicians collectively attended 230,301 OHCA incidents (Table 1).

Temporal trends

A decline in annual OHCA exposure rates was observed over calendar time when all staff were considered together. The median annual exposure rate decreased by 0.16 incidents per year (95% CI 0.12–0.20; p < 0.0001), falling from 5.42 incidents in 2010 to 3.24 per year in 2023. Exposure by clinical skill level

OHCA exposure varied by clinical role. Frontline ambulance clinicians (registered paramedics and non-registered clinicians) had a median annual OHCA attendance rate of 4.00 incidents (95% CI 3.26–4.74). This rate declined over the study period by an average of 0.19 incidents per year (95% CI 0.16–0.23; p < 0.0001; Fig. 4, Fig. S1).Fig. 4Annual rates by staff group.

Annual rates by staff group.

Clinical managers attended more OHCA incidents than frontline clinicians, with a median annual attendance rate of 5.98 incidents (95% CI 4.86–7.11), which increased towards the end of the study period (from 4.00 in 2018 to 12.56 in 2023). This is equivalent to an average increase of 1.69 incidents per year (95% 0.86 to 2.52; p = 0.005; Fig. S2).

Critical care paramedics attended the highest number of OHCAs, with a median annual exposure of 55.00 incidents per year (95% CI 51.30–58.70). These rates of exposure did not appear to change over time (95% CI −2.42 to 0.30; Fig. S3).

The length of time between OHCA attendances varied by clinical role. Frontline ambulance clinicians attended an OHCA on average once every 69 days. In contrast, clinical managers and critical care paramedics experienced OHCA events once every 43 and 6.3 days respectively (Table 1).

Discussion

By analysing more than 230,000 attendances at over 61,000 OHCA incidents, involving more than 8000 ambulance clinicians and almost 40,000 clinician-years of observation, this study is, to our knowledge, the largest analysis of OHCA exposure in an EMS setting. Nearly all full-time clinicians (98%) in our analysis attended at least one OHCA during the study period, with a median exposure of 4 OHCAs per clinician per year. This rate is higher than that observed in Victoria, Australia (1.4 per year) and lower than reported in the USA (8–10 per year).2, 3, 4, 5 These differences are likely attributable to variation in EMS system organisation, geography, population density, clinician demography and workload.

Over the study period, the total number of patients receiving a resuscitation attempt increased modestly over time (from 4212 in 2010 to 4531 in 2023). In contrast, individual clinician exposure rates declined significantly, with median annual exposure falling from 5.42 to 3.24 incidents per year. This decline is consistent with observations from Australia despite differences in the time period, setting, and EMS operational model.5 In London, the reduction in OHCA exposure is most plausibly explained by substantial growth in the clinical workforce, which increased by almost 60% between 2010 and 2023.

Notably, the mean rate of OHCA exposure declined more slowly than the median. This divergence reflects increasing heterogeneity in exposure across the different staff groups, driven by changes in London’s OHCA response model, which has resulted in high or very high rates of exposure among small groups of specialist clinicians. Critical care paramedics attended an average of 55 OHCAs per year, which contrasts markedly with frontline ambulance clinicians who attended an average of 4 incidents annually. Clinical managers saw an average of 6 OHCAs per year, with exposure increasing from 4 per year in 2018 to almost 13 per year in 2023. This increase coincided with changes in role utilisation of that starting during the COVID-19 pandemic, when OHCA incidence in London rose significantly and increased emphasis was placed on dispatching clinical managers to support clinicians on scene.15 These changes continued following the pandemic, with further refinements to the dispatch model for this group of clinicians, resulting in increased exposure. Importantly, among frontline ambulance clinicians – the largest staff group in this study – both mean and median exposure declined (Fig. 4B), indicating that exposure reduced for the majority of clinicians in our study.

An association between clinician exposure to OHCA and patient outcomes has been reported previously.4, 6, 9, 16 While this relationship was not examined in the present study, our findings support EMS models that use targeted dispatch to deploy specialist clinicians with higher OHCA exposure, thereby maximising the benefits of enhanced skills and accumulated experience.

Clinicians were most frequently exposed to adult, non-traumatic OHCAs attending such incidents on average once every 58 days. In contrast, paediatric OHCAs were rare, with clinicians encountering these approximately once every 6 years. Traumatic OHCAs were similarly uncommon, with frontline clinicians attending these incidents on average once every 4 years. Given that the median follow-up time of frontline clinicians in our study was 3.6 years, many ambulance clinicians in London are unlikely to ever attend such incidents during their careers.

OHCA is a time-critical emergency requiring the delivery of high-quality resuscitation by skilled clinicians to maximise survival.17 Maintaining resuscitation competence is particularly challenging for frontline clinicians in the context of low exposure to OHCA events. This challenge is further compounded by evolving EMS service delivery models that increasingly focus on the delivery of urgent care for lower acuity patients.18 Further research investigating feasible and effective approaches to retaining resuscitation skills and mitigating the effects of declining exposure is urgently needed.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted within a single EMS system serving a densely populated metropolitan area, which may limit generalisability to other systems or settings. Second, we excluded staff in part-time roles, in other specialist teams, and those with less than six months employment; while pragmatic, this decision should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, this study focussed on exposure to OHCA and did not examine the relationship between the rates of exposure and outcome, which remains an important area for future research.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that OHCA incidents are a relatively rare – and declining – event for full-time frontline EMS clinicians in London, with marked differences in exposure between clinical skill levels and to specific patient cohorts. Attendance at paediatric OHCAs and those of traumatic aetiology is particularly rare. Whilst ambulance clinicians have a critical role in the management of OHCA, these events constitute a small proportion of their overall workload, which presents challenges for preparedness and skill retention. Certain groups of clinicians in London have higher rates of exposure and further work is required to explore if higher rates of exposure are associated with improved outcomes following OHCA.
