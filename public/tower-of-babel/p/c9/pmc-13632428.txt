Online modulation of neural drive to lower motor neurons with high-definition transcranial alternating current stimulation (HD-tACS)
Authors: Michael Houston, Hao Meng, Nicholas Dias, Yingchun Zhang, Sheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) is a form of non-invasive brain stimulation that entrains cortical neuronal activity through exogenously delivered sinusoidal currents. However, it remains unclear whether tACS can effectively modulate the common input to lower motor neurons. This study aims to address this gap by applying high-definition tACS (HD-tACS) at varying stimulation frequencies (11 Hz, 21 Hz, 40 Hz, and Sham) and intensities (1 mA and 2 mA).Materials and methodsFifteen healthy, young adults produced 10% maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) isometric forces for 100 s with their flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) while HD-tACS was applied to the motor hotspot of FDS during the initial 60 s. High-definition surface electromyography (HD-sEMG) was decomposed into motor unit (MU) spike trains. The number of identified MUs, amplitude, firing rate, and MU connectivity (MUC) were assessed during the 60-s HD-tACS period. Cortico-muscular connectivity (CMC) of the FDS motor hotspot and FDS was quantified during the 40 s post-HD-tACS period.ResultsHD-tACS delivered at 2 mA versus 1 mA elicited significantly greater delta-band (δ: 1–4 Hz) MUC (p = 0.033). Additionally, when HD-tACS was applied at 21 Hz and 2 mA, both δ MUC (p = 0.003) and α MUC (p = 0.015) were greater in magnitude than during Sham. However, no significant differences to CMC after HD-tACS were identified (p = 0.135).ConclusionHD-tACS delivered at 21 Hz and 2 mA can produce online modulation of the corticospinal inputs to lower motor neurons. However, 60 s of online HD-tACS may not be ample time for corticospinal entrainment to persist post-stimulation.Keywords: Neuromodulation, High-definition transcranial alternating current stimulation, High-definition surface electromyography, Motor unit, Common input

Introduction

Transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) is a non-invasive brain stimulation technique with great potential in neuromotor applications. The working mechanism of tACS involves the periodic modulation of the neuron transmembrane potential via the exogenous introduction of a sinusoidal current towards a target cortical area, entraining endogenous neuronal network oscillations towards alignment with the stimulation frequency [1–3]. Whereas a more established method such as transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) works by tonically modulating the neuronal cell membrane polarity [4–6], tACS is promising due to its working mechanism being more closely aligned with the brain’s naturally occurring oscillatory dynamics [7, 8].

There is evidence to support that tACS can modulate motor function, but there is not yet an agreed upon convention of ideal stimulation parameters for neuromotor applications. A review from Rostami et al. illustrated that tACS at intensities above 1 mA peak-to-peak as well as tACS at personalized frequencies within alpha range could increase corticospinal excitability whereas alpha, beta, and gamma tACS applied to the motor cortex could improve motor function [9]. Our own previous work [10–13] has also shown heightened peak-to-peak amplitude of motor evoked potentials (MEPs) of the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) after applying HD-tACS at the FDS hotspot as compared to the conventional C3/C4 position at 21 Hz and 2 mA peak-to-peak amplitude. We also reported modulation to the variability of motor unit firing rates and force output of FDS following HD-tACS applied to the FDS hotspot. On the other hand, Tingting et al. [14] applied tACS at beta- and gamma-range frequencies at 2 mA peak-to-peak amplitude for 20 min to healthy young adults at rest and observed that gamma-tACS enhanced cortical excitability, as seen with increased motor evoked potential amplitudes, whereas beta-tACS did not. While the neuromodulatory effects of tACS are still varied across literature, it is demonstrable that tACS elicits a frequency-dependent nature on upper motor neuron entrainment, therefore the need for appropriate stimulation settings requires further exploration.

The neuromodulatory effect tACS may have on the neural drive to lower motor neurons is poorly understood. A prior study from Ibáñez et al. [15] attempted to evaluate the efficacy of conventional tACS at 1 mA peak-to-peak amplitude with frequencies of 7, 21, and 40 Hz on a simulated group of MU spike trains as well as empirical MU spike trains from the digiti minimi and tibialis anterior muscles with intra-muscular connectivity analysis of cumulative spike trains derived from motor unit decomposition. While their computational simulations of motor neuron pools suggested beta-band entrainment, empirical data did not show any modulation to intra-muscular connectivity, thus rejecting the hypothesis of tACS-driven corticospinal entrainment at 1 mA. However, these results are not consistent with our previous findings regarding the reduction in MU firing rate variability when beta HD-tACS at 2 mA peak-to-peak amplitude was applied. While there is some evidence to suggest that tACS can induce MU changes, there is a lack in understanding of optimized stimulation parameters for neuromotor applications. Elucidating the neuromodulatory effects of tACS on lower motor neuron firing patterns may prove vital in expanding our understanding of neurophysiology and open the way for improved neurorehabilitation efforts.

There is a clear and present gap in knowledge with respect to the neuromodulatory effects tACS has on lower motor neurons in neuromotor applications. This study aims to address this key issue by implementing HD-tACS along with high-density surface electromyography (HD-sEMG) to evaluate the entrainment of corticospinal inputs through analysis of decomposed motor unit spike trains of the FDS. We hypothesized that HD-tACS can provide online modulation of corticospinal drive to lower motor neurons. Specifically, HD-tACS is expected to elicit stronger neuromodulation on the motor unit connectivity at 2 mA over 1 mA, given the dose-dependent nature of tACS [16]. Beta-range HD-tACS is also expected to provide stronger neuromodulatory effects during isometric contraction over other stimulation frequencies when compared to sham considering the relevance of beta band in cortico-muscular connectivity [17], inter-muscular connectivity [18, 19], and motor-unit connectivity [20] with respect to isometric force production.

Materials and methods

Participant demographics

Fifteen young, healthy, and right-handed participants (9 males: 28 ± 5.3 y/o; 6 females: 30 ± 8.7 y/o) participated in this study. The study protocol was approved by the Committee for the Protection of Human Participants (CPHS) of the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston and was conducted in adherence to the standards established by the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental setup

Participants were seated comfortably with four fingers resting on four force transducers (PCB Piezotronics Inc., Depew, NY, USA) and allowed them to rearrange the transducers for the most ergonomical position as seen in Fig. 1. Participants were then asked to perform three repetitions of maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) with their index, middle, ring, and little fingers on the four force transducers simultaneously for 5 s. The maximum force value of the three MVC trials was used to identify the 10% MVC threshold. Flexible printed circuit board (PCB) HD-sEMG grids (64-channel, 8 × 8) were placed on the muscle belly of the flexor digitorum superficialis and a ground wristband was placed on the contralateral wrist. HD-sEMG signals were sampled at 2048 Hz with a Refa-136 amplifier (TMSi, Enschede, The Netherlands). An Actichamp Plus system (Brain Products GmBH, Germany) was used to sample 64-channel EEG at 2500 Hz. Both amplifier systems were synchronized with a BNC splitter cable connection to receive event triggers. HD-tACS was delivered using the MxN-9 HD-tES (Soterix, Woodbridge, NJ, USA) in a 4 × 1 configuration. The center electrode was placed at the FCC3h position, according to the 10 − 5 system [21], as it was the closest standardized position to the functional motor hotspot of FDS as determined by MEPs from transcranial magnetic stimulation delivered with a figure-of-eight coil connected to a Magstim Bistim2 200 stimulator (Magstim Company, Whitland, Wales, UK). The surrounding ring of HD-tACS electrodes were placed at the FFC3h, FCC1h, CCP3h, and FCC5h positions. HD-tACS was chosen over tACS due to the focality of current distribution HD-tACS provides [22], ensuring precise neuromodulation of the FDS functional motor hotspot.

Participants were given 10 s to reach the 10% MVC force threshold and asked to maintain a stable force output while HD-tACS was applied for 60 s and another 40 s after HD-tACS was turned off

Concurrent HD-tACS and 10% MVC protocol

The experimental protocol is illustrated in Fig. 1. Participants were asked to lightly rest their fingers above the force transducers and stay at rest until they were instructed to begin the 10% MVC task. Participants were given a fixed 10 s window to reach and maintain the 10% MVC force threshold as guided by the visual feedback of the real-time force signals. The HD-tACS was then turned on to deliver stimulation at a randomized combination of frequency (11 Hz, 21 Hz, and 40 Hz) and intensity (1 mA and 2 mA peak-to-peak amplitude) or sham for 60 s as the participants maintained 10% MVC force. A stimulation ramping period of 1 s was applied to all active and Sham HD-tACS conditions. The Sham HD-tACS condition was delivered with an initial, faint tingling sensation during the stimulation ramping periods at the beginning and end of the online HD-tACS delivery. After 60 s of concurrent HD-tACS and 10% MVC force production, the HD-tACS system was turned off as the participants maintained their 10% MVC force for 40 more seconds. Finally, participants were allowed to rest for 5 min before proceeding to the next trial to avoid fatigue and facilitate washout of neuromodulatory effects.

Signal pre-processing

Monopolar HD-sEMG signals recorded from the FDS were bandpass filtered (10–500 Hz) and notch filtered at 60 Hz (58–62 Hz bandstop) with 2nd order Butterworth filters to eliminate motion artifacts, high-frequency noise, and powerline artifacts. A bipolar EMG signal was derived from a pair of central electrodes from the HD-sEMG grid, located on the muscle belly, for cortico-muscular connectivity (CMC) analysis. EEG signals were bandpass filtered (1–50 Hz) with a 2nd order Butterworth filter. EEG signals were then decomposed with independent component analysis (ICA) to reject ocular motion artifacts (2–3 components removed per participant). Finally, EEG signals were re-referenced to the common average reference and resampled to 2048 Hz to match the sampling rate of the HD-sEMG.

Motor unit decomposition

Monopolar HD-sEMG signals were decomposed using the K-means Clustering and Convolution Kernel Compensation (KmCKC) method [23]. Briefly, the KmCMC approach reconstructs innervation pulse trains by assessing the global activity index of the HD-sEMG signals to determine when multiple MUs are active simultaneously. The K-means clustering method is then used to group similar instances for evaluation as an initial MUAP pulse train. A multi-step iterative convolution kernel compensation step is utilized to update the estimated MUAP pulse trains until convergence is reached.

MUAP characteristics were quantified after decomposition including: the number of identified MUs, MUAP amplitude, instantaneous firing rate (FR) of MUs and variability of motor unit firing rate (FRV). MUAP amplitudes were calculated as the highest root-mean squared (RMS) amplitude of the template MUAP waveform across the entire HD-sEMG grid. Instantaneous firing rates were determined by convolving binary MU spike trains with 1-valued windows one second in duration and smoothing with a Gaussian filter. The average instantaneous firing rates were calculated across three windows during the 10% MVC + HD-tACS phase of the trials: 0–20, 20–40, and 40–60 s. The FRV was quantified with the coefficient of variability as done previously [12] by normalizing the standard deviation of FR with the mean of FR. The FRV was also calculated within the 0–20, 20–40, and 40–60 s windows during the delivery of HD-tACS.

Functional connectivity

Magnitude-squared coherence was implemented to assess the motor unit connectivity (MUC) and cortico-muscular connectivity (CMC). Magnitude-squared coherence is calculated with cross-spectrum of two binary cumulative spike trains x and y, Pxy, normalized by the auto-spectra, Px and Py (1). Hanning windows of 1 s with no overlap were used for MUC and CMC analyses.

MUC was quantified with magnitude-squared coherence of cumulative spike trains [15, 24]. Cumulative spike trains were derived by summing the binary spike trains of half of the MUs identified selected at random. Cumulative spike trains were implemented for MUC over pair-wise connectivity of individual MU spike trains as individual MU discharge rates influence the full frequency content of the common input signal between two MU spike trains [25]. Cumulative spike trains are also considered to reflect the common input signal [26]. MUC was calculated across one hundred randomly generated permutations of cumulative spike trains and then averaged. Band-average MUC was extracted from the delta- (δ:1–4 Hz), alpha- (α: 8–12 Hz), and beta- (β: 15–30 Hz) frequency bands within the 0–20, 20–40, and 40–60 s windows of online HD-tACS, resulting in 20 epochs of MUC analyzed per 20 s window (60 epochs total). While Ibáñez et al. [15] focused solely on β MUC, other studies have also implicated lower frequency bands such as delta and alpha with respect to motor unit connectivity [27–29] and cortico-motoneuronal connectivity [30].

CMC was calculated in the β-band across the entire 40 s post-stimulation period (40 epochs of MUC analyzed) as participants maintained the 10% MVC finger flexor force from FDS. CMC was not calculated in the 60 s window when HD-tACS was active due to the severe stimulation artifacts inherent with HD-tACS. CMC was calculated and averaged with EEG from the FCC3h, FC3, FC1, C3, and C1 location and bipolar EMG from FDS.

Power spectrum density

EEG power analysis was conducted to evaluate any shifts in frequency power related to the stimulation frequency of HD-tACS. Band-average power spectrum densities (PSD) from α, β, and gamma (γ: 30–50 Hz) frequency bands were derived and averaged from the FCC3h, FC3, FC1, C3, and C1 electrodes. Spectral estimation of EEG frequency power was conducted with Hanning windows (1 s) with no window overlap, resulting in 40 analyzed epochs. Band-average PSD was assessed for the frequency range the stimulation frequency resided in at both stimulation intensities (1 and 2 mA) and compared to Sham.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) tests were performed with in SPSS (IBM Corp. Released 2023. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0.2.0 Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Normality of data residuals were verified with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilkes tests) for ANOVA. Multiple comparisons were corrected for with Tukey Honest Significant Difference procedure with the Bonferroni confidence interval adjustment method. Independent variables analyzed involved: Frequency (Sham, 11, 21, and 40 Hz), Intensity (1 and 2 mA), and Time (0–20, 20–40, and 40–60 s). The critical value was set to < 0.05 for statistical significance.

Results

Motor unit characteristics

Motor unit properties including the number of identified MUs, MUAP amplitude, MU firing rate (FR), and firing rate variability (FRV) were assessed during the application of online HD-tACS to ascertain whether the recruitment of MUs or their firing activities are modulated as an effect of the corticomotoneuronal entrainment. The number of identified MUs and RMS amplitude of MUAPs are summarized in Table 1. The effects of HD-tACS Frequency on the number of MUs and RMS amplitude of MUAPs were compared with a one-way ANOVA. No significant differences in either the number of identified MUs or the RMS amplitudes of MUAPs were found when comparing HD-tACS (11, 21, and 40 Hz) at 2 mA versus Sham. A two-way ANOVA applied to assess the interaction between HD-tACS Frequency (11, 21, and 40 Hz) and Intensity (1 and 2 mA) on the number of identified MUs and RMS amplitude of MUAPs did not identify any significant findings among main factors of Frequency and Intensity. The lack of modulation to the number of MUs and MUAP amplitudes suggests that the recruitment of MUs remained unaffected as a result of the online HD-tACS [31].

Motor unit connectivity

Motor unit connectivity (MUC) was quantified across one hundred random permutations of cumulative spike trains. MUC was assessed across relevant frequency bands including δ, α, and β frequency ranges to understand the online neuromodulatory effects of HD-tACS on the descending common neural drive of motor unit pools and corroborate the widely proposed mechanism of neural entrainment. A three-way ANOVA with factors of Frequency (11, 21, and 40 Hz), Intensity, and Time was applied to MUC at each frequency band (δ, α, and β) to determine whether the neural drive of the FDS MU pool is affected in a stimulation intensity-dependent manner. The three-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Intensity on δ MUC (F(1,243) = 4.581; p = 0.033) but not for the factors of Frequency or Time. δ MUC was greater when HD-tACS was applied at 2 mA versus 1 mA (p = 0.033) (Fig. 2). No significant findings were identified when assessing either α MUC or β MUC.

MUC of cumulative spike trains during HD-tACS assessed at 1 versus 2 mA. Error bars indicate 95% confidence interval. * indicates p < 0.05

After confirming the stimulation-intensity dependent nature of HD-tACS, MUC was compared across HD-tACS delivered at 2 mA with Frequency (Sham, 11, 21, and 40 Hz) and Time as the factors of a two-way ANOVA. A significant main effect of Frequency (F(3165) = 4.45; p = 0.005) was identified for δ MUC. Post-hoc tests further showed that HD-tACS applied at 2 mA and 21 Hz (p = 0.003) had greater δ MUC than the Sham condition (Fig. 3A). When assessing α MUC, Frequency was also identified as a significant main effect (F(3,165) = 3.101; p = 0.028) while Time was not. Once again, post-hoc analyses indicated a significantly greater α MUC when HD-tACS was applied at 21 Hz and 2 mA over the Sham condition (p = 0.015) (Fig. 3B). No significant findings were determined for β MUC. These results introduce an interesting perspective into motor control and the frequency and intensity-specific neuromodulatory effects of HD-tACS.

Cortico-muscular connectivity

The β CMC between FCC3h and bipolar EMG from FDS was analyzed to determine whether corticomotoneuronal entrainment was still evident immediately after online HD-tACS. A one-way ANOVA was used to determine whether CMC was modulated immediately after applying online HD-tACS at 2 mA across Frequency (Sham, 11, 21, and 40 Hz). While a main effect of Frequency was identified (F(3, 60) = 2.967; p = 0.041), no significant pairwise differences in CMC were observed after multiple-comparisons correction, as seen in Table 3. Two-way ANOVA tests were used to compare the magnitude of CMC between Frequency (11, 21, and 40 Hz) and Intensity factors of HD-tACS. However, neither Frequency nor Intensity was found to have significant main effects on CMC. These results, in conjunction with the previous MUC findings, would suggest that while modulation to lower motor neuron neural drive occurred, entrainment of the upper motor neurons was not retained after sixty seconds of online HD-tACS.

Discussion

The ability of tACS to entrain cortico-spinal inputs to lower motor neurons is unclear. This study implemented HD-tACS over the FDS hotspot with varying stimulation intensities and frequencies and characterized motor unit recruitment and neural drive. No neuromodulation-specific changes were identified with respect to the number of identified motor units, motor unit action potential amplitude, firing rate, or variability of firing rate. However, neuromodulation-specific increases in neural drive of motor units, as quantified by the connectivity of cumulative spike trains, were identified when HD-tACS was applied at 21 Hz and 2 mA versus Sham. Additionally, greater magnitude of neural drive was identified when HD-tACS was applied at 2 mA versus 1 mA, illustrating a dose-dependent nature of neuromodulation.

Basic motor unit properties such as the number of identified MUs, MUAP amplitude, FR, and FRV were not affected due to neuromodulation. This finding is partially in line with Meng et al. [10] where an increased variability of force output but no changes to the number of identified MUs or their MUAP amplitude was observed. While FRV was found to be significantly reduced in the 20–60 s versus 0–20 s windows, this finding was also observed during Sham and HD-tACS, indicating a non-specificity towards the neuromodulation. However, Meng et al. identified a significant decrease in FRV after a 20-minute session of HD-tACS applied at 21 Hz and 2 mA to the FDS hotspot versus another session of HD-tACS applied to the standard C3/C4 scalp location [10]. It is likely that the decreases in FRV identified in the current study are associated with participants’ continued efforts to acclimate to the visual target line of 10% MVC and stabilize their fingertip forces. Considering the dose-dependent nature of tACS [16], similar neuromodulation-specific alterations to FRV as observed in Meng et al. should be realized after neuromodulation periods greater in duration than what was applied in the current study. Overall, the working mechanism of tACS involves entrainment of cortical oscillations via the exogenous introduction of an external sinusoidal current [1–3, 7]. Thus, these findings suggest that the neuromodulatory mechanisms of short-term tACS do not affect the recruitment of MUs nor the MUAP waveform characteristics, only MU oscillatory activities [10] and their synchronization.

MUC was shown to be greater when stimulation intensity was set to 2 mA instead of 1 mA, and at a stimulation frequency of 21 Hz versus Sham. These findings, while at odds with the empirical findings of Ibáñez et al. [15], are exciting and indeed corroborated by other MU literature. While no significant modulation of β MUC was identified, similarly to Ibáñez et al. [15], the rejection of corticospinal entrainment of lower motor neuron oscillations cannot be rejected at present. Increases to δ MUC were identified at a key motor-related stimulation frequency (21 Hz) when applying HD-tACS at 2 mA versus 1 mA. The δ MUC and α MUC were also found to be greater during online HD-tACS at 21 Hz and 2 mA versus the Sham condition. Indeed, the δ band is proposed to reflect common modulation of MU firing rates [32, 33]. Additionally, MUC in the α-band is thought to be affected by feedback from muscle spindles and potential from rhythmic activities of the spinal reflex loop [34, 35]. While β band MUC can have cortical contribution [36], it is unreasonable to reject the notion that the motor neuron pool of FDS was indeed modulated by HD-tACS in this study as seen with δ MUC. Furthermore, one possible explanation as to why neither Ibáñez et al. nor we could observe any notable increases in β MUC may be that tACS may be more likely to affect the dominant, slow-varying common synaptic input of lower motor neuron pools as identified by Negro et al. [28]. Moreover, De Luca & Erim have implicated frequencies < 5 Hz with force generation [32]. Castronovo et al. also observed that δ MUC increased under conditions of increased net excitatory drive, including increased force levels and the presence of fatigue [29]. Another factor affecting MUC is the duration of tACS neuromodulation. HD-tACS was applied in this study concurrently during 10% MVC force output for a 60-s duration, similar to Ibáñez et al. [15], and β MUC was not observed to increase in either study. Additionally, while Meng et al. [10] observed reduced FRV after 20 min of HD-tACS at 21 Hz, the present study was unable to produce similar findings after 1 min of HD-tACS with identical parameters. On the other end, Strüber et al. applied alpha-band tACS intermittently for 1 s duration and did not observe any modulation to EEG amplitude or phase [37]. Thus, a longer duration neuromodulation may be needed in order to elicit β MUC modulation. This may be observed. Lastly, while 21 Hz HD-tACS at 2 mA appeared the be the most ideal application in healthy young adults, more research is required to validate these settings in populations such as stroke [27, 38] and Parkinson’s disease [39], for example.

The corticospinal drive to the FDS, as observed with CMC, appeared unchanged immediately after HD-tACS. While the online modulation to MUC during HD-tACS indirectly addressed that HD-tACS affected the upper motor neurons of the neuro-navigated hotspot of FDS, prior literature has observed similar results. Kudo et al. applied individualized-frequency based oscillatory transcranial direction current stimulation (otDCS), tACS, and tDCS and found only individualized otDCS at 20 Hz affected CMC when the CMC peak frequency was near 20 Hz [40]. Additionally, Krause et al. [39] applied 10 Hz and 20 Hz tACS at 1 mA for 15 min to the primary motor area of participants with Parkinson’s disease and healthy controls and evaluated the effects of tACS with CMC analyses. Their findings indicated that 20 Hz tACS was associated decreased β CMC and variability of finger tapping movement for the Parkinson’s disease patients but not for the healthy controls. Their findings did not include any significant effects related to 10 Hz tACS either, in accordance with the results of the present study. However, Wach et al. [41] assessed CMC of the extensor digitorum communis after tACS was applied at 10 and 20 Hz for 10 min with an intensity of 1 mA and identified a reduction in CMC of the low gamma range after 10 Hz tACS. Also, Yamaguchi et al. [42] applied tACS at peak beta-band frequencies per participant after a 10 min visuomotor ankle dorsiflexion task and identified increases in beta CMC, but not intra-muscular connectivity, one day after neuromodulation, suggesting a role of tACS in facilitating consolidation of motor skills. Regardless, It is clear that the choice in muscle, functional signal of cortical oscillatory activity (electroencephalogram versus magnetoencephalogram), neuromodulation intensity, frequency, duration, and location all play a significant role in the determination of tACS entrainment, thus yielding a heterogenous selection of outcomes in prior literature [9].

No modulation in band-average PSD was detected immediately after HD-tACS. The lack of any meaningful changes in EEG spectral estimates, in conjunction with the absence of similar findings in CMC and the presence of increased MUC during HD-tACS suggest that the neuromodulatory effects of online HD-tACS may not persist after a short duration of neuromodulation. Previous studies have observed increases in frequency power and shifts in individual peak frequencies after applying offline tACS [7, 37, 43, 44]. However, these studies differ from the present study due to their implementation of offline tACS for 10–20 min neuromodulation sessions.

This study benefits from the analysis of motor unit activity during neuromodulation. tACS produces severe artifacts that render EEG signals unusable during the neuromodulation itself. However, there have been some recent attempts at denoising EEG during tACS that appear to hold some promise [45, 46]. Ideally, future studies may attempt to evaluate both MUC and CMC during and after HD-tACS after applying these artifact removal strategies to further improve our understanding of the mechanism of HD-tACS from the upper and lower motor neuron perspective. Future studies may also consider advancing the understanding of how HD-tACS modulates cortical activity of the sensorimotor network under intact and damaged corticospinal tract conditions such as stroke, spinal cord injury, and cerebral palsy. The application of tACS to the ipsilesional sensorimotor cortex may be vital in improving neuro-rehabilitation outcomes after corticospinal tract damage. Post-stroke survivors experience reduced corticospinal tract integrity [18], leading to negative consequences on motor function [47–49]. An imbalance between the damaged corticospinal tract and subsequently upregulated reticulospinal tract in chronic stroke survivors, along with maladaptive motor control strategies to compensate for functional losses, are largely at play [50]. While functional motor deficits post-stroke are indeed visible from inter-muscular connectivity studies [19, 51, 52], understanding the alterations to the neural drive of affected muscles at the motor unit level is crucial for advancing neurophysiology. Dai et al. [27] illustrated upregulated connectivity of motor units from the paretic first dorsal interosseous in stroke in the delta (1–4 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and beta (15–30 Hz) bands; these results indicated an increased common synaptic input to motor neurons across spinal and supraspinal levels. HD-tACS applied concurrently with physical rehabilitation after corticospinal tract damage may hold advantageous outcomes akin to tDCS [38, 53–55].

Conclusion

HD-tACS can produce online neuromodulation of the activity of lower motor neurons when delivered at the appropriate current frequency and intensity. Online HD-tACS delivered at 21 Hz and 2 mA can modulate the corticospinal inputs to lower motor neurons. However, 60 s of online HD-tACS may not be ample time for corticospinal entrainment to persist post-stimulation. These findings support the application of HD-tACS in the context of neurorehabilitation to target the impaired function of descending neural drive after corticospinal tract damage.
