Cerebellum-inspired memtransistors enable emergent differentiation for hardware-efficient novelty detection
Authors: Min-A Kang, Spencer T Brown, Nethmi Jayasinghe, Meghana R Holla, Thang T Pham, Thomas T Zeng, Ruiqin Wu, Zachary J Trdinich, Xudong Zhuang, Vinayak P Dravid, Indira M Raman, Amit R Trivedi, Vinod K Sangwan, Mark C Hersam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI) algorithms are currently executed using silicon-based hardware, resulting in excessively high energy demand for data centers. Edge computing AI for healthcare, robotics, and autonomous vehicles presents even stricter power and latency constraints, which are unmet by incumbent computing architectures. With vastly superior energy efficiency, biological neuronal networks provide hints towards alternative computational approaches including memory-logic colocation, asynchronous parallelism, and spike-triggered computation. Here, we draw inspiration from the cerebellum to demonstrate asymmetric-contact-gated MoS2 memtransistors that exhibit bias-polarity-dependent excitatory/inhibitory short-term plasticity. Arrays of these cerebellum-inspired memtransistors exploit the evolving interplay between excitatory and inhibitory responses to emulate the emergent synaptic differentiation of the cerebellum, enabling rapid identification of novel events. When applied to electrocardiogram data, arrhythmias are detected within a single heartbeat with 10,000-fold fewer operations than existing silicon-based approaches. In this manner, cerebellum-inspired neuromorphic hardware provides a pathway to computationally efficient, high-speed novelty detection for edge intelligence.Subject terms: Electronic devices, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

The brain offers a powerful model for rapid and adaptive inference. Of particular interest is the cerebellum, which orchestrates real-time motor corrections within milliseconds through a continuous monitoring of sensorimotor data arising from other brain regions, detecting and responding to deviations from predicted sensory or action states1–4. For instance, in the everyday experience of reaching for an object whose weight differs from expectation, the cerebellum corrects transient errors by triggering short-latency adjustments to muscles, thereby maintaining the smooth execution of movement. This rapid adaptive motor coordination emerges from signaling in cerebellar circuits that have specialized properties (Fig. 1a, b)5. Sensory signals reach the cerebellar cortex input layer through mossy fibers (MFs) that synaptically connect to granule cells (GrCs), which carry out pattern separation6–10. Granule cells excite inhibitory Purkinje cells, the principal neurons of the cerebellar cortex, as well as molecular layer interneurons (MLIs), which provide feed-forward inhibition to Purkinje cells. Granule-Purkinje synapses have the strongest short-term facilitation known in the brain11, so synaptic excitation begins weakly but grows with repeated activation. Conversely, granule-MLI synapses are potent, and MLI-Purkinje synapses are stable12. Consequently, the onset of sensory input activates granule cells, initially producing weak excitation and triggering potent MLI-mediated inhibition in Purkinje cells, suppressing spiking. The persistence of sensory input leads to a strengthening of excitation through facilitation, overriding inhibition, and terminating the Purkinje suppression12. Thus, at the onset of sensory input, spike suppression results from synaptic differentiation, emerging from the integration of facilitating excitation with stable inhibition. This differentiation is used by Purkinje cells for event detection, translating novel or anomalous sensory events into transient spike suppressions that act as motor commands, disinhibiting downstream motor circuits. Given their critical role in adaptive control of motor behavior, these cerebellar circuits offer a rich repertoire of computational solutions that can serve as an architectural blueprint for neuromorphic computing hardware.

Here, we present a neuromorphic computing device that employs these biological principles, using the solid-state platform of a cerebellum-inspired memtransistor (CIM) for fast, computationally-efficient novelty detection. CIMs are fabricated using a self-aligned, asymmetric-contact gating scheme where one of the electrodes overlaps the polycrystalline MoS2 channel via a metal oxide dielectric layer. This geometry produces substantive changes in the lateral electric field near the contact regions upon reversal of the bias polarity (i.e., when the bias and ground electrodes are interchanged). Based on finite element calculations, grounding (biasing) the overlapping or top electrode induces depletion regions near both source and drain contacts (only the drain contact), thereby inducing qualitatively distinct memristive switching in each case. In this manner, CIMs provide voltage-polarity-dependent modes of operation that differ in short-term plasticity (STP) and decay time of the output read current. With repeated activation, the sum of the bias-gated (excitatory, E) and ground-gated (inhibitory, I) outputs mimics the distinct time course of synaptic plasticity in Purkinje cells, with the added flexibility of gate-dependent gain modulation. Using the demonstrated CIM current-voltage characteristics and distinct STP responses, simulated CIM synaptic elements are embedded into a cerebellum-inspired hybrid neural network to demonstrate an efficient novelty detection system5,12. Since this implementation leverages the temporal characteristics of the short-term memory of the E and I counterparts, it is qualitatively distinct from widespread analog compute-in-memory implementations using memristors, memtransistors, and other emerging non-volatile memories13–17. Although long-term learning in the cerebellum involves teaching signals from climbing fibers (Fig. 1a), only short-term memory based on STP is necessary for the demonstrated novelty detection5,12. An encoder layer is used for dimensionality expansion, and a CIM-based N×1 crossbar array is proposed as the output layer, executing E/I interplay for novelty (anomaly) detection in a wide range of datasets, including electrocardiogram (ECG), visual, and audio signals. The same device can be switched between E and I counterparts simply by tailoring the voltage pulse polarities, thus enabling on-chip reconfigurability of the network hardware. The performance of the resulting bio-inspired neuronal network is benchmarked against classical ECG monitoring baselines, state-of-the-art neural networks, transformers, and machine learning algorithms, ultimately revealing the ability to detect arrhythmia in standard ECG datasets within a fraction of a heartbeat with 10,000-fold fewer computing operations. The comparison against silicon-based computing is performed in terms of computing operations, which demonstrates the benefits of a highly parallelized cerebellum-inspired algorithm for rapid novelty detection, where the network response speed approaches the transients of individual devices.

Results and Discussion

Asymmetric-contact-gated MoS2 memtransistor

We first discuss key features of the asymmetric-contact-gated MoS2 memtransistor design and electrical characteristics that are critical to emulate the E/I interplay of cerebellar Purkinje cells (Fig. 1a, b). The memtransistors are fabricated by transferring a polycrystalline monolayer of MoS2 (with < 10% coverage of bilayer flakes) grown by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) onto local bottom gates (Ti/Au) that are covered with a 25 nm-thick Al2O3 gate dielectric layer grown by atomic layer deposition (ALD) (Fig. 1c)18–21. The details of the growth conditions and materials characterization are provided in the Methods section and Suppl. Note 1. Lateral force microscopy reveals grain boundaries with one-dimensional grain sizes ranging from 50 to 500 nm, while atomic force microscopy (AFM) shows isolated MoS2 bilayers with less than 10% areal coverage (Suppl. Figs. 1, 2). Raman and photoluminescence spectroscopy confirm a predominant coverage of MoS2 monolayer, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy reveals a sub-stoichiometric Mo:S ratio of 1:1.66 (Suppl. Figs. 3, 4)18,21,22. After the transfer onto the patterned gate and gate dielectric layer, MoS2 is etched into channel geometries by reactive ion etching (Suppl. Fig. 5). Then, a self-aligned lithography step is used to pattern contact electrodes (2 nm Ti/20 nm Au) with a MoS2 channel length defined by a dielectric extension layer (25 nm-thick Al2O3) that electrostatically couples the channel to an overlapping contact electrode, which can be either a source or drain electrode, depending on the biasing configuration (see Methods section and Suppl. Note 2 for fabrication details). The device symbols in Fig. 1c show the bias polarity and current flow direction in each case. This fabrication process achieves an approximate yield of working devices of 70%. Although all steps of lithography affect the overall device yield, the most critical steps are the CVD MoS2 wet transfer and liftoff of self-aligned ALD Al2O3 that grows conformally on the resist. The electrical data in Fig. 1d are representative of 12 measured devices. The electrostatic effects from the asymmetric channel contacts allow two distinct modes of operation (Fig. 1c)23. Both modes show bipolar memristive switching in output current-voltage characteristics (i.e., drain current (ID) versus drain voltage (VD)), where conductance can be tuned by a factor of 105 by the gate voltage (VG) (Fig. 1d). However, the application of a bias on the overlapping electrode (i.e., bias-gated CIM) achieves a switching ratio of ≈ 10 (ratio of resistances at VD < 0.3 V) that results in an excitatory response, as discussed later. On the other hand, grounding the overlapping electrode (i.e., ground-gated CIM) achieves a larger memristive switching ratio ( ≈ 103), resulting in an inhibitory response.

We next discuss the memristive switching mechanism in self-aligned CIMs. First, low-voltage operation is achieved by scaling the channel length to 400–500 nm, while the channel width (20 μm) exceeds the MoS2 grain size to ensure the intersection of multiple grain boundaries with the Schottky contacts, which results in substantive memristive switching20,24. Cross-sectional scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and AFM confirm the targeted geometry of the MoS2 channel, Al2O3 extension layer, and relative alignment of all the metal layers (Fig. 1c bottom, Suppl. Figs. 5, 6). Second, a clockwise memristive loop results from the rearrangement of sulfur vacancies (n-type dopants) in the pinch-off regions. Previously, MoS2 memtransistors on SiO2 using Schottky contacts have shown an anti-clockwise memristive loop that was analytically modeled as a dopant rearrangement near the reverse-biased Schottky diode at the source18,19,25. On the other hand, single- or dual-gated MoS2 memtransistors using Al2O3 as the dielectric layer have shown clockwise memristive loops that are consistent with dopant rearrangement under high field in the pitch-off region near the drain contact20,21. In particular, ALD-grown Al2O3 induces additional n-type doping ( > 1013 cm–2) to CVD-grown MoS2, and thus the reduced Debye length attenuates resistive switching at the Schottky contacts compared to the pinch-off region21,26. Highly non-linear ID-VD curves of these memtransistors (Fig. 1d) are qualitatively similar to the space-charge-limited current in previously reported memtransistors21. Our recently reported drift-diffusion model of free electrons and mobile defects (i.e., sulfur vacancies as n-type dopants) in lateral MoS2 memristors shows that resistive switching can arise from both the modulation of the Schottky barrier height by mobile dopants or the dynamic rearrangement of mobile dopants in the channel with or without a Schottky barrier25. Both density functional theory (DFT) calculations and experiments have shown sulfur vacancies in MoS2 as n-type dopants27,28. Furthermore, Kelvin probe force microscopy and Auger electron spectroscopy have shown that the sulfur vacancies can redistribute near metal contacts under high electric fields in lateral MoS2 memristors29,30.

First-principles studies of monolayer MoS2 have shown different energy barriers for the migration of S vacancies along and across grain boundaries depending on the atomic structure of the defects and grain boundaries31–34. For example, a (4 | 6) dislocation-V2S complex can glide in monolayer MoS2 by overcoming an energy barrier as low as 0.61 eV31. Scanning tunneling microscopy and STEM have also been used to extract temperature-dependent sulfur vacancy diffusion coefficients, demonstrating that defects can diffuse, coalesce, and migrate with an energy barrier of 0.6 eV35. Recently, molecular dynamics simulations using machine learning interatomic potentials have shown cooperative vacancy transport that forms line defects spanning tens of nanometers within a single MoS2 crystal lattice36. However, this process of creating new defect states requires a larger energy barrier of 2–3 eV and thus is less relevant to polycrystalline MoS2. Nevertheless, additional thermal and strain effects at the large electric field at the contact ( > 1 MV/cm, discussed later) cannot be ruled out. Since ALD Al2O3 serves as an effective encapsulation layer37,38, we can exclude adsorption/desorption of ambient species in the memristive mechanism of these MoS2 memtransistors, where the channel is fully encapsulated by Al2O3. While bilayer MoS2 flakes can introduce spatial inhomogeneities in the MoS2 electronic structure within the channel, a < 10% coverage of bilayer MoS2 is not expected to affect the memristive behavior significantly, especially since memristive switching is localized at the metal contacts. Note that we chose Al2O3 over other higher-κ dielectrics such as HfO2 and ZrO2 because low-temperature ALD (100 ⁰C) of Al2O3 is compatible with liftoff processes. Different degrees of doping (minimum in the case of ZrO2) are expected to influence memristive switching through the modulation of depletion regions39.

The top Al2O3 extension layer also plays a critical role in memristive switching beyond defining the self-aligned MoS2 channel. A 100-fold larger memristive switching ratio (larger loops in output curves, Fig. 1d) in the ground-gated CIM case compared to the bias-gated CIM case can be understood from the asymmetric electrostatic effects of the self-aligned overlapping electrode23. Finite element electrostatic calculations using COMSOL Multiphysics, presented here (Fig. 1e, f, Suppl. Fig. 7, and Suppl. Table S1) and those using Sentaurus TCAD (Synopsys) in ref. 23 elucidate the key differences in the two modes of operation. Ground-gated CIMs show the formation of depletion (pinch-off) regions near both source and drain contacts for VD > 0 V, in contrast to a depletion region forming only near the drain electrode in the bias-gated configuration (Fig. 1f)23. Note that the band-bending in the bias-gated configuration is qualitatively similar to a standard bottom-gated field-effect transistor with a pinch-off near the drain in the high VD saturation regime, except that the bias-gated geometry further increases the local electric fields. Indeed, the maximum lateral electric fields Ex, |E | > 1 MV/cm in both bias-gated and ground-gated CIMs (Suppl. Fig. 7) are comparable to previous experimental reports of the fields required for space charge-limited current in MoS223,40. However, large fields near both contacts in the ground-gated CIM facilitate dynamic rearrangement of mobile defects near both contacts to produce a stronger non-linearity in the ID-VD characteristics (i.e., a delayed onset of high current in the high-resistance state (HRS) and a saturating current in the low-resistance state (LRS)), ultimately resulting in a larger memristive ratio than bias-gated CIMs. Since the same memtransistor device geometry supports both excitatory and inhibitory modes of operation, fabrication steps can be minimized when implementing CIM-based neural networks. Hysteresis in the transfer characteristics (Suppl. Fig. 8) also indicates charge trapping in the Al2O3 dielectric or at trap states at the Al2O3/MoS2 interface, enabled by grain boundaries. Since similar self-aligned field-effect transistors on CVD-grown individual MoS2 flakes do not show hysteresis in transfer curves, we hypothesize that grain boundaries play a critical role in memristive switching and hysteretic behavior. A lower threshold voltage in bias-gated CIMs (average Vth = −2.25 V) compared to ground-gated CIMs (average Vth = 3.75 V) agrees with previous simulations, pointing to larger vertical electric fields as the source of this extrinsic doping23. However, bias-gated CIMs also show a larger hysteretic window (ΔVH = 7.5 V) compared to the ground-gated case (4 V) (Suppl. Fig. 8), which can be explained by increased charge trapping events as a result of larger vertical fields in bias-gated CIMs. As we discuss next, this short-term memory is central to the cerebellum-inspired novelty detection and distinguishes this work from conventional in-memory computing inference relying on analog non-volatile memories.

Bias-polarity-dependent excitatory-inhibitory synapses

We explored pulse-dependent conductance modulation (i.e., synaptic behavior) of bias-gated and ground-gated CIMs at different VG values following the pulse protocols shown in Fig. 2a, b. We first make three correlated observations about differences in the synaptic behavior of two CIMs. Then, we describe the dynamic synaptic behavior of CIMs in terms of bias-polarity-dependent quasi-static charge transport in Fig. 1d. First, CIMs display differences in long-term neuromorphic synaptic changes, including long-term potentiation (LTP) in response to VD, POT pulses (2 V, 5 ms), and long-term depression (LTD) in response to VD, DEP pulses (−1 V, 5 ms). In response to repeated potentiation pulses (at 100 Hz) at a given gate voltage, bias-gated CIMs show a gradual increase in baseline-subtracted read current (ΔIread) over repeated stimulation cycles (Fig. 2a). In contrast, ground-gated CIMs show an abrupt increase in ΔIread upon the first potentiation pulse, followed by a weaker modulation with additional potentiation pulses (Fig. 2b). Suppl. Fig. 9 shows the absolute Iread values over multiple pulsing cycles that were used to extract ΔIread in Fig. 2a, b. Second, bias-gated CIMs (ten-fold ΔIread change) are more sensitive than ground-gated CIMs (two-fold ΔIread change) to changes in the gate voltage, over the tested range of −2 V to +1 V (Fig. 2a, b). Notably, the gate tunability of ΔIread provides a means of mimicking graded changes in synaptic strength, such as those resulting from climbing fiber-instructed plasticity in the cerebellum (Fig. 2c)41,42. Third, the bias-polarity leads to asymmetric transient current responses to a single voltage pulse, resulting in decay constants that are tenfold slower for the bias-gated versus ground-gated configuration (τE = 2.43 s and τI = 0.28 s; Fig. 2d).

The gradual conductance change (ΔGE,I) in bias-gated CIMs in Fig. 2a arises from a larger band-bending confined at the drain, whereas the step-like change in conductance in ground-gated CIMs results from the net band-bending distributed at both the source and drain contacts (Fig. 1f, Suppl. Fig. 7). Larger fields in the MoS2 depletion region and top Al2O3 extension layer near the drain facilitate gradual conductance modulation upon persistent pulsing of the bias-gated CIM. In addition, asymmetric charge trapping due to the vertical fields in the top Al2O3 layer cannot be ruled out since these synaptic characteristics also embody asymmetric STP behavior, as seen by exponential current decays upon a single pulse in Fig. 2d. Indeed, retention test of HRS and LRS shows memory lasting for a few hours (Suppl. Fig. 10). A shorter duration of conductance retention in bias-gated CIM is consistent with larger ID-VG hysteresis owing to larger vertical fields in the top Al2O3 layer, which increases the relative fraction of charge trapping compared to non-volatile memristive switching. The relevant STP characteristics are incremental changes in baseline-subtracted conductance ΔGE,I, ∂(ΔGE,I), or baseline-subtracted current, ∂(ΔIread), upon application of a pulse that depends on their pre-pulse values, GE,I, or Iread in Fig. 2a, b, respectively, as discussed later. These characteristics are sufficient to model bias-gated CIMs as excitatory to emulate the short-term facilitation of granule-to-Purkinje synapses, while ground-gated CIMs are inhibitory to emulate the relative stability of MLI-to-Purkinje synapses12,43. Emulating an interplay of excitatory and inhibitory synapses for novelty detection does not require non-volatile memory. Therefore, a pair of oppositely biased CIMs can model the interaction between synaptic excitation and feed-forward inhibition observed in Purkinje cells during behaviorally relevant neural processing.

Cerebellum-inspired neural network for novelty detection

Cerebellum-inspired memtransistors replicate key sub-components of the larger cerebellar network that underlie novelty detection and unsupervised learning. To test the efficacy of CIMs for novelty detection, we incorporated CIMs into a larger cerebellum-inspired neural network, based on the multiple layers of the cerebellum, which comprise a series of processing stages that serve complementary roles (Fig. 3)6–10. In the biological cerebellum, mossy fibers relay sensorimotor information to the granule-Golgi cell layer (Fig. 3a). This layer performs expansion recoding, transforming mossy fiber signals into a sparse, decorrelated, high-dimensional representation, thereby enhancing pattern separation6–10. Next, granule cell outputs are relayed to Purkinje cell dendrites in the molecular layer through excitatory and feed-forward inhibitory synapses (via MLIs) with distinct STP profiles, which, when integrated, result in the sensitivity of Purkinje cells to new input patterns. This output layer performs novelty detection through a differentiating E/I interplay. Signals from N neurons converging onto a single Purkinje cell can be embodied by an N × 1 crossbar array of bias-gated and ground-gated CIMs (Fig. 3b). Any of the N neurons can be made excitatory or inhibitory by selecting relative bias polarity at relevant rows and the Purkinje column, whereas independent gates can further modulate synaptic strength.

With this cerebellar inspiration, our CIM-based neural network consists of two core stages: a projection-expansion stage and a convergence-detection stage (Fig. 3c). The projection stage temporally encodes input signals into a high-dimensional latent space in an unsupervised manner, promoting pattern separation6–10. Because this projection enhances separability but does not inherently detect novelties, additional mechanisms are required to suppress known patterns and enhance sensitivity to novel inputs7. Therefore, we coupled the encoder outputs of the first stage to a CIM-based convergence stage. Specifically, CIM asymmetric plasticity allows for the selective amplification of transient deviations while suppressing redundant activity by STP- and synaptic-decay-mediated adaptive filtering. We fabricated a 10 × 1 crossbar array to demonstrate the feasibility of the practical realization of the output layer shown in Fig. 3b. Figure 3d, e show optical images of the crossbar where respective layers on a CIM are highlighted (see the Methods section for the detailed fabrication protocol). First, a gate line is fabricated column-wise, and then the granule-Golgi cells (or MLI, depending on biasing conditions) are fabricated as bottom electrodes (BEs) row-wise on the prepatterned MoS2 channels. Finally, a Purkinje cell line is fabricated as an overlapping or top electrode (TE) column-wise, parallel to the gate line, defining CIMs at the intersections with the BEs, thus summing currents from all 10 granule-Golgi or MLI synaptic connections. Note that a crossbar node includes two CIMs connected in parallel, defined by two self-aligned Al2O3 extension layers at the two edges of the bottom electrode, resulting in a twofold increase in the effective channel width of the CIM in a crossbar compared to that of an individual CIM shown in Fig. 1c and Suppl. Fig. 5.

We tested the utility of our CIM-based neural network for real-time arrhythmia detection by using ECG recordings from the publicly available Physikalisch-Technische Bundesanstalt (PTB) dataset44. The computation begins by temporally segmenting input from a single ECG channel using a sliding window and projecting it into a 64-dimensional latent space using a transformer-based encoder6,7. To achieve energy-efficient spike-based computation, the encoder outputs are passed to 64 neurons, which integrate input features over time and emit spike trains based on a leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) threshold mechanism. The neurons that are most active with the presentation of normal (rhythmic) data are labeled as excitatory. Neurons labeled as inhibitory are randomly selected from the 64 neurons. Excitatory and inhibitory neurons are synaptically connected to a Purkinje output cell with the attributes of the bias-gated and ground-gated CIMs, respectively. We explicitly establish the correspondence between device metrics and quantities used to simulate the network. The experimentally measured device response is mapped to the network variables through a conductance-to-current-to-voltage signal chain. A presynaptic voltage spike updates the device conductance state (Eq. 1) according to the pulse-history-dependent conductance modulation measured in Fig. 2a, b, while the single-pulse decay in Fig. 2d sets the relaxation constants τE and τI. Under a fixed read voltage (VD,read = 0.1 V), these conductance states generate synaptic currents, I(t)=VD,readG(t), which are summed in column-wise top electrode in Fig. 3d–f (the Purkinje cell) across all active excitatory or inhibitory synapses. The summed currents are then converted into postsynaptic voltage-domain variables through leaky integration, yielding VE(t) and VI(t). These variables are therefore simulated postsynaptic signals derived from measured conductance/current dynamics as opposed to direct terminal-voltage measurements of individual devices. In a hardware implementation of a crossbar in Fig. 3d–f, the current-to-voltage conversion could be implemented using a transimpedance/integrating amplifier or a capacitive membrane/RC leaky integrator, followed by a comparator to generate the Purkinje-like spike output. Overall, the present work experimentally demonstrates the CIM synaptic dynamics, while the encoder, spike conversion, leaky integration, and Purkinje-like decision stage are modeled at the system level to illustrate the architecture-level advantages provided by CIMs.

ECG data that included stretches of normal rhythms and arrhythmic episodes were presented to the model. Figure 4–f illustrates the ECG input (c), the output of the latent nodes (d), the synaptic inputs to Purkinje cells (e), and the Purkinje spike output (f). During normal ECG patterns, excitatory CIM inputs to Purkinje neurons dominate, due to continuous drive from expected patterns, leading to an accumulation of potentiating and slowly decaying synaptic responses. Consequently, the system exists within a near-saturated state, curtailing minor fluctuations in inhibition, thereby reducing false positives detected by the network. In contrast, inhibitory CIM inputs, designed for high-gain, rapidly decaying responses, react sensitively to deviations. Hence, when arrhythmia occurs, previously active (excitatory) neurons decrease their firing rates and previously inactive (inhibitory) neurons become active, generating sharp, transient rises of VI(t). The Purkinje cell fires if VE(t) > VI(t); otherwise, it is silenced. During transitions from normal to arrhythmic ECG phases, the signals from the latent nodes indeed show feature deviations, associated with net inhibition surpassing excitation, and a corresponding transient suppression of Purkinje cell firing, serving as a flag for novelties. This behavior embodies cerebellar emergent differentiation with rapid, transient suppression of Purkinje cell output at the onset of novel input12.

Robustness was ensured by a duration-based threshold rule, in which only sustained dominance of inhibition triggered novelty classification. We further extended this system to a multiple-cell setup, in which 16 Purkinje cells are connected to the latent space, and a novelty was flagged if K out of N cells are simultaneously silenced (Suppl. Fig. 12 and Suppl. Note 4). This attribute of neuronal consensus, or synchrony, occurs within populations of biological Purkinje cells, thereby increasing the precision of cerebellar output5,12,45,46. Short-latency accuracy increases when multiple Purkinje cells, rather than just one, are considered for novelty assessment. The ideal number of Purkinje cells depends on the prominence of false positives associated with particular datasets.

We first benchmarked our cerebellum-inspired neuromorphic system for arrhythmia detection with classical ECG monitoring baselines that act as a low-latency novelty detector intended to operate as a front-end module within broader monitoring pipelines (Suppl. Note 5 and Suppl. Fig. 13). The CIM framework achieves nearly > 98% accuracy in detecting arrhythmia within 1/5 of a heartbeat, whereas all other baselines stay between 70 and 80% within the same fraction of a beat duration (Suppl. Fig. 13). To reveal the quantitative advantages of the CIM approach, we next benchmarked the performance of our CIM framework against deep learning baselines, including Transformer47, long short-term memory (LSTM)48, gated recurrent unit (GRU)49, generative adversarial network (GAN)50, recurrent neural network (RNN)48, and convolutional neural network (CNN)51. All benchmarking is performed using a comparable number of trainable parameters. Detection performance (accuracy) was evaluated as a function of the fractional heartbeat interval in 2.5% increments. For our cerebellum-inspired system, if inhibition dominated throughout the fractional interval, the event was classed as an arrhythmia. For standard neural network models, events were classified as arrhythmias when the decoder activation exceeded chance at 0.5 (Fig. 5a). For our system and the baseline models, true positives and true negatives were determined at each detection interval, and accuracy was averaged over repeated trials using different sets of heartbeats. Notably, the CIM system exhibits a sharper accuracy-latency transition, reaching 100% accuracy 2.4 times faster than the best baseline model.

We next compared the performance of the CIM decoding stage (excitatory and inhibitory neurons and Purkinje-like cells of Fig. 3c) to the standard cross-attention-based decoder of the Transformer model47, with both models configured to use the same input projection scheme (i.e., pattern separation stage of Fig. 3c). We varied two parameters (i.e., the temporal resolution of signal sampling and the latent dimension of the Transformer) and measured two key outputs (i.e., the time to arrhythmia detection and the number of operations required) (Fig. 5b). A parametric study across Transformer configurations showed that operations are reduced as continuous-to-spike conversion rates are increased from 1 ms to 25 ms, although with slight latency tradeoffs. Moreover, the time to detection drops with increasing latent dimensions, with the tradeoff of significantly higher multiply-and-accumulate operations. However, even with the highest latent dimension (64) and highest sampling rate (1 ms), the Transformer model showed more than two-fold slower detection latency than the CIM system. The competitive advantage of the CIM system can be traced to its ability to process inputs at high temporal resolution but only encode the most informative spikes, where both the count and timing of the spikes influence the downstream response. This selective encoding sharply reduces computation without compromising detection fidelity, with accuracy consistently in the 98–100% range across all selected parameter settings. Overall, the CIM system consistently maintains accuracy comparable to the Transformer model with 10,000-fold fewer operations, demonstrating a markedly improved computation-latency tradeoff for real-time novelty detection. The reduction in inference-stage operations reflects computational complexity rather than system-level energy consumption. Although operation count influences energy usage, realized energy metrics additionally depend on memory access, interconnect activity, and peripheral circuitry. The present comparison, therefore, establishes operation-level computational efficiency arising from the experimentally characterized device dynamics and local state evolution of CIM synapses.

To further evaluate the functional contributions of individual synaptic mechanisms, we conducted a series of ablation studies. In the CIM system, STP results from the transient modulation of synaptic strength based on recent activity, causing spikes to be weighted by their temporal novelty rather than just their occurrence. When combined with a synaptic temporal decay (τ), which imposes a gradually fading memory trace, context-sensitive filtering is achieved that prioritizes recent deviations over stale or redundant input. As shown in Fig. 5c, removing either STP or τ degrades accuracy, underscoring the critical role of their combination in supporting novelty detection. We also assessed the importance of the asymmetric excitatory/inhibitory synaptic configuration (Fig. 5d). Specifically, we compared the original configuration (where bias-gated CIM governs excitatory and ground-gated CIM governs inhibitory dynamics) with cases in which both pathways use the same (bias-gated or ground-gated) configuration. In both symmetric configurations, accuracy decreased at short latencies, further demonstrating the dependence of rapid differentiation on the bias-polarity-dependent synaptic behavior of CIMs. In addition to clinical arrhythmia detection from ECG signals, we generalized the application of our cerebellum-inspired system across diverse modalities by detecting novelties in handwritten images (Suppl. Note 6 and Suppl. Fig. 14) and free-spoken digit dataset (FSDD) audio waveforms (Suppl. Note 7 and Suppl. Fig. 15) that could enable intelligent vision and audio processing52. Collectively, these results demonstrate that CIM-based neuromorphic architecture can be generalized to multiple applications requiring robust, computationally-efficient novelty detection.

Drawing inspiration from the cerebellum, an asymmetric-contact-gated MoS2 memtransistor has been developed that enables hardware architectures with the excitatory/inhibitory interplay of biological cerebellar circuits. Asymmetric electrostatic effects in memtransistors produce bias-polarity-dependent STP. In particular, these CIMs emulate the dynamics of granule-to-Purkinje (excitatory) and MLI-to-Purkinje (inhibitory) synapses, enabling emergent differentiation and temporal gating in hardware. Using the CIM pulse-controlled synaptic modulation, we conduct real-time, computationally-efficient arrhythmia detection of ECG signals with an approach that is generalizable to other types of temporally evolving data, such as visual and audio streams. Unlike conventional neuromorphic approaches, our cerebellum-inspired architecture achieves rapid, low-overhead inference with a reduction in computational overhead by 10,000-fold. This work demonstrates how biologically grounded hardware-software co-design can unlock adaptive signal processing, particularly for edge applications requiring high speed, robustness, and efficiency. In a broader context, these findings further offer a scalable, interpretable framework for edge intelligence in autonomous control and cyber-physical security where rapid, low-power novelty detection is essential.

Methods

Chemical vapor deposition MoS2 synthesis

Polycrystalline MoS2 was synthesized via chemical vapor deposition (CVD) on a sapphire substrate19,20. Prior to growth, sapphire substrates were thoroughly cleaned using bath sonication in acetone and isopropyl alcohol for 10 min each. Sequentially, the substrates were treated with O2 plasma (Harrick Plasma) at a base pressure of 200 mTorr and a power of 10 W for 2 min to remove organic contaminants. Following the cleaning step, a 1-inch-diameter two-zone CVD system (Lindberg/Blue) was used for MoS2 growth. In this setup, an alumina boat containing 4.0 mg sulfur powder (99.98%, Millipore-Sigma) was placed in the upstream zone while another alumina boat with 150 mg MoO3 powder (99.98% trace metal, Sigma-Aldrich) was positioned downstream adjacent to the sapphire substrates, with a distance of 26 cm between the two sources. The cleaned sapphire substrate was placed near the MoO3 source, and the furnace was purged at 300 °C with ultrahigh-purity argon (35 sccm) for 20 min. The furnace was then ramped to 800 °C at a controlled rate of 23 °C·min−1 and held at this temperature for 10 min to promote MoS2 growth. Upon completion of synthesis, the CVD system was allowed to cool to room temperature naturally under a steady Ar flow to minimize oxidation of the MoS2 films during cooling.

Polycarbonate-mediated MoS2 transfer

CVD-grown MoS2 films were transferred onto the desired undoped Si wafer using polycarbonate (PC) films19,20. First, a PC solution was prepared by dissolving 1.75 g of PC in 20 ml of chloroform (0.5–1.0% ethanol as stabilizer, Millipore-Sigma). Then, the mixture was stirred overnight at 70 °C using a magnetic stirrer to ensure complete dissolution and uniform dispersion. This PC solution was then spin-coated onto the MoS2/sapphire substrate at 3000 rpm (acceleration = 1000 rpm/s), followed by drying at 120 °C for 1 min. The edges of the PC film were carefully cut using a razor blade to define the boundaries of the transferred MoS2. The substrate was then repeatedly dipped in and out of deionized water to separate the PC/MoS2 film from the sapphire substrate. The PC/MoS2 film was then picked up from the water beaker using an undoped Si substrate. The substrate was then dried at 80 °C, followed by stepwise heating at a rate of 5 °C per minute up to 180 °C. After reaching this temperature, the sample was cooled naturally in ambient conditions. To remove the PC layer, the substrate was then soaked in chloroform solution for 3 h and subsequently cleaned with acetone and isopropanol to complete the transfer process.

Fabrication of cerebellum-inspired memtransistors

The MoS2 cerebellum-inspired memtransistor (CIM) fabrication process begins with patterning large contact pads by electron beam lithography (EBL, Raith Voyager 100) using polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) as a positive tone e-beam resist polymer. A (2 nm Ti)/(50 nm Au) stack was deposited via thermal evaporation (Ångstrom Engineering COVAP Thermal Evaporator Ⅱ) and patterned by liftoff in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) at 60 °C. Next, the gate electrodes were patterned using a (2 nm Ti)/(20 nm Au)/(2 nm Al) stack, with the 2 nm Al (immediately oxidized to Al2O3 in ambient) serving as a seeding layer for the subsequent growth of Al2O3 as the gate dielectric layer by atomic layer deposition (ALD). The gate dielectric layer of 25 nm-thick Al2O3 was patterned by a liftoff process where ALD growth was performed on patterned PMMA at 80 °C (ALD Cambridge Nanotech Savannah S100) to avoid overbaking the polymer. Throughout these steps, 950 K PMMA A5 e-beam resist (Kayaku Advanced Materials) was spin-coated and baked at 180 °C for 90 s after coating. For development, we used a mixture of methyl isobutyl ketone (MIBK) and isopropanol (IPA) with an MIBK:IPA ratio of 1:3, with the addition of 2 % deionized water that helped control the development rate. Subsequently, CVD-grown MoS2 was transferred onto the chip and patterned by EBL using a negative-tone e-beam resist polymer. First, an adhesion promoter SurPass 4000TM (DisChem, Inc.) was spin-coated and rinsed with deionized water. Then, a mixture of a negative tone resist AZ®nLOF™ 2035 (MicroChemicals GmbH) and propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA) with an nLOF2035:PGMEA ratio of 1:2 were spin-coated at 3,000 rpm for 30 s with an acceleration of 1,000 rpm/s, followed by drying at 110 °C for 1 min. After exposure, a post-bake step was performed at 100 °C for 1 min, after which the substrate was rinsed in AZ® 300 MIF Developer (MicroChemicals GmbH) for 18 s, followed by rinsing in deionized water. Exposed MoS2 areas were etched by reactive ion etching (RIE, Samco RIE-10NR) using Argon plasma (50 sccm, 50 W, 13.3 Pa) for 2 min, and the resist was removed by lift-off in NMP at 60 °C for overnight. The next step is fabrication of the bottom electrode using a self-aligned methodology23. For this step, we used an MMA/PMMA bilayer to achieve well-defined undercuts. An MMA(8.5)MAA EL6 (Kayaku Advanced Materials) was spin-coated at 2500 rpm (2500 rpm/s acceleration) for 45 s, followed by baking at 180 °C for 90 s. Then, a 495 K PMMA A5 layer was spin-coated at 4,000 rpm (acceleration = 1000 rpm/s) for 45 s and baked at 180 °C for 90 s. This patterning process was followed by development in MIBK:IPA (1:3) with 2% deionized water for 1 min, followed by rinsing in IPA five times to achieve a well-defined undercut geometry. Then, we deposited a (2 nm Ti)/(20 nm Au)/(2 nm Al) metal stack using a slow deposition rate of 0.05–0.1 nm/s while the sample was rotated during deposition. Again, 2 nm-thick Al was immediately oxidized to Al2O3 in ambient. The metal deposition was followed by ALD-growth of 20 nm-thick Al2O3 at 80 °C before performing liftoff in NMP at 60 °C for overnight. This step leaves an Al2O3 coating over the whole bottom electrode and an Al2O3 extension of 400–500 nm beyond the metal edges that defines the channel length. For the top electrode, 950 K PMMA A5 was spin-coated at 4000 rpm (acceleration = 4000 rpm/s) for 45 s and baked at 180 °C for 90 s. After exposure, the resist was developed in MIBK:IPA (1:3) for 90 s. Finally, a metal stack of (2 nm Ti)/(50 nm Au) was deposited via thermal evaporation, followed by the liftoff to complete the device fabrication.

A 10 × 1 CIM crossbar was fabricated using photolithography with a SUSS MABA6 Mask Aligner. First, the gate electrode was patterned on undoped Si substrates coated with a 300 nm-thick oxide layer using a negative photoresist NR9-1000 PY (Futurrex, Inc.). The photoresist was exposed with a wavelength of 365 nm, followed by development, deposition of a metal stack of (2 nm Ti)/(20 nm Au)/(2 nm Al), and liftoff. Atomic layer deposition was used to grow a 25 nm-thick Al2O3 gate dielectric layer over the entire chip. Then, CVD-grown MoS2 was transferred and patterned by photolithography in MABA6 using a bilayer of polydimethylglutarimide (PMGI) and Microposit S1813 positive resist, since this bilayer minimizes polymeric residues on MoS218. Notch features in the gate electrode (Fig. 3d, e) were used to define the area for the etched MoS2 for subsequent alignment. Using patterned resist as a mask, MoS2 was etched by RIE using the same recipe as individual CIMs above (Ar plasma, 50 sccm, 50 W, 13.3 Pa, 2 min). Next, the bottom electrodes (BEs) were patterned on MoS2 using the negative photoresist and by aligning with notches in the gate line since MoS2 does not have any optical contrast under the photoresist. The dielectric extension on the BEs was grown in the same manner as the individual CIMs described above. After growing a (2 nm Ti)/(20 nm Au)/(2 nm Al) metal stack, a 20 nm-thick Al2O3 layer was grown by ALD, followed by liftoff. Finally, an overlapping or top electrode (TE) consisting of a metal stack of (2 nm Ti)/(50 nm Au) was patterned in the middle of the gate line using negative photoresist. The width of the gate line is 20 μm, while the widths of TE and BE are 10 μm each. The area of the etched MoS2 region was 20 μm × 30 μm (Fig. 3e). This self-alignment procedure by photolithography follows prior literature23 with the resulting dielectric extension and corresponding channel length being approximately 1 μm. Two regions of dielectric extension on either side of the BE define two CIMs at each node of the crossbar.

EDS data were collected at 200 kV with a Dual SSD EDS detector (1/7 sr). Data processing (denoising, background subtraction, and signal mapping) was conducted using Gatan GMS software.

Device simulations

Electrical characteristics were modeled using finite element analysis software (COMSOL Multiphysics). The simulations were performed utilizing the Semiconductor Module. The physical model is based on drift-diffusion theory, which self-consistently solves the Poisson Eq. (2) and the carrier transport via two mechanisms: drift and diffusion (Eqs. (3) and (4)). Drift and diffusion are related through the Einstein relation (Eq. (5)).

Here, ϕ is the electrostatic potential, ε is the local permittivity, q is the elementary charge, n and p are the electron and hole concentrations, and ND+ and NA− are the ionized donor and acceptor concentrations, respectively. Jn and Jp represent the current densities for electrons and holes.

The MoS2 channel was defined as an n-type semiconductor with a background doping concentration of 1018 cm−3. To accurately model the carrier statistics in this heavily doped scenario, Fermi-Dirac statistics were employed. Under non-equilibrium conditions (i.e., when a drain bias is applied), the system is described by solving for the quasi-Fermi levels of electrons and holes; more parameter details are provided in Suppl. For the numerical solution, a finite element mesh was generated over the entire device domain. The mesh was locally refined at critical regions, such as the Schottky interfaces and the channel/dielectric interface, to ensure numerical accuracy and convergence. A stationary solver was used to compute the static (DC) characteristics of the device under various source, drain, and gate bias conditions.

Neural network architecture overview

For our cerebellum-inspired neuromorphic system, we developed a simulation pipeline that integrates transformer-based temporal encoding with a biologically grounded convergence-detection mechanism. This dual-stage architecture is designed to emulate key cerebellar principles of emergent differentiation and spike-efficient signal processing for the task of real-time electrocardiogram (ECG) novelty detection. All data processing, model development, and analysis were performed in Python with key libraries including NumPy, PyTorch, and SciPy.

Signal preprocessing and encoding

ECG data were obtained from the Physikalisch-Technische Bundesanstalt (PTB) Diagnostic ECG Database, available through PhysioNet. The PTB database, provided by the National Metrology Institute of Germany, comprises high-resolution ECG recordings from 290 subjects, including healthy individuals and patients with cardiac abnormalities44,53. As a source of normal ECG signals, we selected a healthy control (Patient 233) who has five ECG recordings, each approximately two minutes long. Anomalous ECG data were drawn from the dysrhythmia category within the dataset. The input to the neuromorphic system consisted of ECG signals derived from the Lead II channel, which is clinically recognized for its alignment with the electrical axis of the heart. The signals underwent standard preprocessing to remove DC offsets and normalize the amplitude range to [0, 1]. This step stabilized the feature space and avoiding spurious activations during inference.

To capture the temporal dynamics of cardiac cycles, each ECG signal was segmented into overlapping windows of 500 milliseconds using a sliding window approach with a step size of 1 ms for continuous evaluation. Each window is initially passed through a linear projection layer that maps the 1-dimensional signal into a 64-dimensional latent feature space. The dimensionality was selected to strike a balance between representation richness and computational efficiency, as informed by cerebellar expansion recoding6,7. The projected sequences are processed by a transformer encoder comprised of two stacked layers with four parallel attention heads per layer and a hidden feedforward dimension of 128. This configuration enabled the model to learn long-range temporal dependencies within each window, capturing the fine-grained rhythmic patterns of normal cardiac activity. The encoder was trained using 1,000 normal ECG waveforms, minimizing the mean squared reconstruction error across 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer and a learning rate of 0.001. No abnormal data were introduced during training.

Spike-based representation

Upon completion of the encoding phase, the model produces a temporal trajectory of latent activations that reflect the dynamics of the input signal. To enable downstream spike-efficient computation, these activations were transformed into binary spike trains using a leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron model, mimicking the event-driven nature of biological spiking neurons. The LIF model operates by integrating the latent feature magnitude over time; when the integrated membrane potential exceeds a predefined threshold (set at 0.95), a spike is emitted, and the potential is reset. A subset of the most active spiking units under normal data conditions was selected and labeled as excitatory neurons, whereas inhibitory neurons were randomly sampled across the latent space. Note that although an LIF spiking neuron model was used in the present demonstration, we recently demonstrated bio-realistic spiking neurons based on printed MoS2 films that can physically activate neuronal circuits in vitro in brain slices from the mouse cerebellum54.

Cerebellum-inspired convergence detection

The spike trains derived from the latent representations served as inputs to the convergence-detection stage, which was modeled after cerebellar Purkinje cells. Here, we deployed a set of conductance-based synapses implemented via CIMs. These memtransistors operate in two distinct modes, bias-gated and ground-gated, to emulate synapses arising from excitatory and inhibitory neurons, respectively. Excitatory synapses were configured to respond gradually and saturate under sustained input, representing expected or familiar patterns. In contrast, inhibitory synapses were tuned for rapid, high-gain responses to novel input, decaying quickly to prevent redundant suppression. Each synapse updates its conductance based on a nonlinear, spike-triggered rule with an exponential decay as mentioned in Eq. (1).

To parameterize the synaptic model, we extracted characteristics from bias-gated and ground-gated CIM outputs acquired during multi-pulse and single-pulse experiments. We fit exponential functions to the multi-pulse post-synaptic current (ΔIPSC) across the tested gate voltages (1 V, 0 V, –1 V, –2 V) for each device, resulting in 8 fit equations, to obtain the pulse history-dependent amplitude of the modeled conductances under the condition of repeated presynaptic firing. Single-pulse transient responses for each gate condition and device type were fit to obtain the temporal decay constants (τE, τI) that occur when a single presynaptic spike fires following a period of quiescence. Collectively, these parameters defined the gate-dependent and device-dependent synaptic update rules under sustained and isolated spike inputs within the network.

Modeled Purkinje cell membrane potential changes associated with excitatory VE(t) and inhibitory VI (t) channels were computed from the integrated sums of their respective conductance values. Detection of an anomalous event was triggered when the net inhibitory potential exceeded the net excitatory potential continuously for a pre-specified temporal window of 130 ms, which signaled that the incoming pattern deviated significantly from learned norms. This inhibitory dominance and resultant output suppression mirror the physiological behavior of biological Purkinje cells upon the occurrence of novel sensory input12.

Multi-cell integration

To improve robustness and emulate the population-level processing observed in biological cerebellar circuits, we extended the decision-making architecture to a multi-cell ensemble composed of 16 Purkinje units. Each Purkinje unit received excitatory and inhibitory inputs from the same presynaptic, latent space neurons and was initialized randomly at one of the tested gate voltages (1 V, 0 V, –1 V, –2 V), which was applied to all connected CIMs. Since gate voltage affects the amplitude and temporal decay constant of the CIM synapses, each Purkinje unit had a different sensitivity profile. During inference, a novelty was flagged if one or more Purkinje units detected a deviation from normal, a strategy that aligns with the concept of neural consensus or synchrony in populations of biological Purkinje cells5,12,45. Our experiments reveal that setting the decision threshold as low as one out of sixteen Purkinje units significantly enhances detection speed and sensitivity, making this configuration optimal for real-time operation.

Evaluation

During testing, the model is evaluated under continuous streaming conditions. All baseline models were evaluated under identical preprocessing, training regimes, model-scale constraints, and streaming novelty-detection protocols to ensure fair comparison across architectures. Each input sequence is synthesized by concatenating 5 seconds of normal ECG data with 2 s of abnormal data to simulate arrhythmia onset. The system processes these data online, applying the trained encoder and LIF converter in a stepwise manner and updating the Purkinje ensemble state after each window. All relevant model and training parameters can be found in Suppl. Table 2.

We benchmarked the system using 500 True Positive (TP) samples, each containing a normal-to-abnormal transition, and 500 True Negative (TN) samples composed of normal data. To assess the efficacy of our cerebellum-inspired novelty detection framework, we conducted a systematic comparison against a suite of widely adopted deep learning architectures. These baseline models included a Transformer47, long short-term memory (LSTM)48, gated recurrent unit (GRU)49, generative adversarial network (GAN)50, recurrent neural network (RNN)48, and convolutional neural network (CNN)51, all evaluated using identical true-positive and true-negative definitions, fractional beat-based detection criteria, and accuracy metrics.

The training procedure for each model was standardized: all models were trained exclusively on normal ECG waveforms using the same dataset, identical preprocessing steps, and a fixed sliding window duration of 500 ms. Each baseline model was implemented with architecture-specific configurations, maintaining comparable numbers of trainable parameters and matched training budgets to ensure fair evaluation across models. The Transformer model used a model dimension of 64, four attention heads, and two encoder layers, with a feedforward layer of size 64. Training was performed with a batch size of 32 over 10 epochs. The GRU-based architecture consisted of a two-layer GRU autoencoder with a hidden layer size of 110 and a latent dimension of 48, followed by a binary classifier that projected the latent representation to a novelty score using a fully connected layer and sigmoid activation. The LSTM model featured a three-layer bidirectional LSTM with a hidden layer size of 64 and a dropout rate of 0.3, feeding the hidden state from the final timestep into a sigmoid classifier. The CNN model utilized stacked, 1D convolutional layers with increasing filter depths (32–256) and max pooling for temporal down-sampling, followed by a symmetric decoder using transposed convolutions to reconstruct the input waveform. The RNN model was comprised of a four-layer vanilla RNN with a hidden layer size of 200, trained to predict the next value in the ECG sequence as a reconstruction-based novelty signal. The GAN-based model included a generator that mapped a 500-dimensional latent vector to ECG sequences via a GRU and a discriminator composed of a two-layer GRU that classified input sequences as real or synthetic based on the final hidden state. During inference, each model was exposed to sequences containing both normal and abnormal signals. A threshold-based decision rule was used across all architectures to maintain consistency in detection logic.
