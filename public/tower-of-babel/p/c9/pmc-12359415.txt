Uncovering systemic barriers related to equity, diversity and inclusion in child health research: a scoping review addressing marginalised communities
Authors: Ulises Charles-Rodriguez, Deliwe P Ngwezi, Suha Damag, Nicole Johnson, Aleem Bharwani, Tehseen Ladha, Oluwabukola Salami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite abundant evidence illustrating the impact of social determinants of health on children and youth from marginalised communities, their continued marginalisation in research contributes to a negative feedback loop that perpetuates health inequities. Previous reviews have identified barriers in marginalised adult communities. However, no comprehensive review outlines the scope of barriers to equity, diversity and inclusion (EDI) in child health research across multiple marginalised communities, particularly as they are defined in Canada.MethodsThe purpose of this review is to scope and summarise research discussing systemic barriers influencing EDI in child health research, focusing on racialised and black individuals, 2SLGBTQIA+ individuals, Indigenous peoples, women and girls and individuals with disabilities (identified as priority communities in the Canadian government’s research policy). Our team followed the steps proposed by Arksey and O’Malley for scoping reviews.ResultsFrom 3336 identified records, 53 publications met the inclusion criteria. Most studies were based in the USA (n=40) and/or other English-speaking countries (n=14), emphasising the need for global perspectives. Some publications were based in more than one country; others addressed more than one marginalised community. We identified more publications discussing racialised individuals (n=30) and black individuals (n=20) than women and girls (n=10), Indigenous peoples (n=9), children with disabilities (n=7) or 2SLGBTQIA+ individuals (n=4). Publications increased from 3 in 2020 to 15 in 2022, reflecting heightened awareness of structural racism and health inequities during the COVID-19 pandemic. Our findings suggest systemic under-recruitment and tokenism. Other factors in the research ecosystem include misleading conceptualisations of race and other social categories, power dynamics, lack of cultural safety and discrimination. Finally, we recommend applying the socio-ecological model to systematically map barriers and develop tailored, multilevel solutions that promote equity and inclusivity in research.ConclusionTo foster a more equitable and impactful child health research ecosystem, institutions must address individual, interpersonal, organisational and policy-level barriers by embedding community-driven priorities, promoting diverse and inclusive practices, and ensuring long-term, reciprocal relationships with historically marginalised communities.Keywords: Child health

Introduction

UNICEF reports that Canada has the poorest health outcomes for children and youth among rich countries.1 For example, despite having the necessary economic and environmental conditions for healthy development,1 the United Nations estimates that North America loses more than US$68 billion in human capital annually due to mental health conditions in children, and places Canada as one of the countries with the highest health disparities between Indigenous and non-Indigenous populations.2

Poor child health outcomes are driven by social determinants of health.3 Social determinants affect communities,4 families and children.5,7 However, the unequal distribution of determinants showcases underlying constellations of social, political and environmental forces that drive health inequities,8 namely systemic discrimination. Systemic discrimination and racism adversely affect communities,9 10 children911,13 and youth.12,15

In North America, marginalised communities are under-represented in clinical research.16 17 Barriers to recruitment include logistical challenges, limited access to research opportunities, homogeneous strategies, financial constraints, language and cultural barriers, stigma, mistrust and concerns about adverse effects.18,20 The exclusion of historically marginalised populations in research contributes to a negative feedback loop that perpetuates health inequities.18 The lack of population-specific data can lead to policies, programmes and treatments that fail to address the unique needs of marginalised groups, further reinforcing distrust and discouraging participation in research.

Barriers to equitable and inclusive research permeate the whole research process. A systematic review to improve health and medical research with socially disadvantaged groups identified 31 reviews of the literature, almost exclusively focusing on adults, that demonstrated barriers to sampling, recruitment, data collection and measurement, intervention participation and fidelity, and ethical considerations.21

Lessons from the adult population have deepened our understanding of barriers and led to strategies to diversify community engagement22 and embed equity, diversity and inclusion (EDI) in research design and practice.23 Journal publishers,24 25 medical associations26 and paediatric hospitals,27 among other healthcare and research institutions, have adopted EDI as a strategic priority. However, despite these efforts, the research culture has not been transformed,22 particularly regarding how these principles are applied in child health research.

Meanwhile, barriers faced by marginalised children are vastly under-explored in the literature. Research with children must adhere to strict ethical guidelines to protect children’s well-being through the research process, meaning lessons from adult literature may not be entirely transferable, even if effective. To our knowledge, there is only one review on barriers related to research that included children,28 but it does not focus on children alone or on historically marginalised communities within child health research. This underscores the urgent need for a comprehensive understanding of the unique systemic barriers affecting diverse groups of children, especially those from marginalised communities.

The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child emphasises the right of every child to the best prevention and healthcare possible.29 Researchers need a nuanced understanding of the full range of systemic barriers affecting equitable and inclusive child health research. Our scoping review uniquely contributes to this effort by synthesising evidence focusing explicitly on systemic barriers that impede EDI in child health research, both in process and throughout the research ecosystem (ie, institutions, funding mechanisms, publication systems, culture). This work lays the foundation for actionable strategies to address these barriers and align research practices with EDI principles.

Methods

We conducted a scoping review to map the types of systemic barriers and identify existing gaps, as well as potential lines of research and interventions. We followed the steps suggested by Arksey and O’Malley:30 identify the research question, identify relevant studies, select studies, chart the data and collate, summarise and report the results—all of which are described in more detail in the sections below. Given the sensitivity of the topic, the team opted to conduct a broader community consultation using a more robust methodology, which extends beyond the scope of this manuscript. To address this in the short term, we included scholars with research and lived experience in EDI on the research team, as well as patient and public contributions.

Patient and public involvement

We established a community advisory committee to oversee this project, consisting of leaders from community organisations representing Canadian marginalised communities and a patient contributor. The committee provided valuable guidance during the initial phases of the project, informing the decision to use a scoping review design and contributing to the development of the research question and search strategy to ensure alignment with their lived experiences. Alongside the findings from the review, their contributions have also shaped the development of an EDI Action Plan as part of a broader initiative that brings together researchers, community partners, healthcare providers, equity-deserving groups, educators and Indigenous communities to improve child health outcomes in Canada and beyond.

Research question

What are the systemic barriers affecting EDI in child health research among prioritised historically marginalised communities?

What are the systemic barriers affecting EDI in child health research among prioritised historically marginalised communities?

We followed the Canadian government research policy31 32 to select and conceptualise the prioritised historically marginalised communities, which included racialised and black individuals, 2SLGBTQIA+ individuals—including Two-Spirit (2S) identities, a culturally specific term within North American Indigenous communities—, Indigenous peoples, women and girls, and people living with disabilities. Although this review uses Canadian legislation to conceptualise these communities, their experiences and challenges are deeply rooted in global systems of colonialism, patriarchy, ableism, racism and other intersecting structures of oppression, underscoring their worldwide relevance and implications.

We combined keywords with controlled vocabulary or subject headings when possible and strategically to balance sensitivity and specificity across databases (eg, Emtree in Embase, APA Thesaurus in PsycINFO and CINAHL Subject Headings), while relying on keyword searches in databases with indexing limitations. This approach was particularly focused on capturing literature related to the selected marginalised populations. We adapted this strategy for Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, PubMed and Web of Science in August 2023, without applying any time restrictions to include studies (search syntaxes can be found in the online supplemental material). We then summarised key terms and used them to search the grey and potentially missing literature on Google, ProQuest, The Lancet, Statistics Canada and The Canadian Institute for Health Information. Given that databases prioritise highly relevant results, we screened only the first 100 entries to capture the most pertinent studies while maintaining feasibility. Finally, we cross-referenced key articles and screened records identified in a parallel scoping review focused on EDI strategies. While we had a strong interest in the Canadian context, we chose not to restrict our search to Canada. Recognising that systemic barriers are not unique to Canada, exploring multiple contexts allows for a more comprehensive understanding and offers broader insights for public benefit.

Selecting studies

We included all publications focusing on child and youth health research, defined as research focusing on individuals 18 years of age or younger or youth groups that include minors alongside individuals up to 25 years of age—excluding studies where youth groups did not include minors or included individuals older than 25. We included original studies, reviews, theoretical papers, editorials and grey literature. We excluded abstracts, conference proceedings and publications lacking information about marginalised communities and those where barriers were not the central focus of discussion or the connection between identified barriers and their impact on EDI was not clearly articulated. Two reviewers screened titles and abstracts and assessed articles for inclusion. Disagreements at all stages were resolved through consensus or third-reviewer mediation.

Charting and summarising the data

One reviewer charted data and a second reviewer assessed the quality of the charts. We used Covidence, a management software that facilitates record management and data extraction. We charted the title, year of publication, community of focus, study design and methods, in addition to the type, stage, description, evidence and discussion points about the discussed systemic barriers. Extracted data were thematically organised by one author and stratified by marginalised groups to identify the scope of systemic barriers they face, as well as to explore similarities, differences and those barriers specifically affecting individuals with intersectional identities. On 18 March 2025, we conducted a search for retracted studies and found that no studies included in our review had been retracted since the original search date. We used the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews) to report findings.33 As a knowledge synthesis project without the participation of human subjects, this project did not require ethical approval.

Results

From 3336 identified records, 53 publications met the inclusion criteria (figure 1). Most studies were based in the USA (n=40) and/or other English-speaking countries (n=14), emphasising the need for global perspectives (figure 2). Some publications (mainly editorials and reviews) were based in more than one country; others addressed more than one marginalised community. We encountered more research discussing racialised (n=30) and black individuals (n=20) than women and girls (n=10), Indigenous peoples (n=9), children with disabilities (n=7) or 2SLGBTQIA+individuals (n=4) (online supplemental figure 1). 26 publications used categories that aggregated more than one identity or community when describing the subject of systemic barriers (eg, some US-based studies used the term BIPOC—Black, Indigenous, and People of Colour—a term not used in this paper but cited to reflect the original study’s terminology). Publications increased from 3 in 2020 to 15 in 2022, with 9 more published by August 2023 (figure 3). Original studies primarily used cross-sectional (n=9) and qualitative (n=8) designs. Many publications were opinion, commentaries or other text forms (n=13), indicating the need for more robust evidence. More than one-third of the publications declared multiple sources of funding, with governmental grants as the primary funding source (n=27), followed by academic institutions (n=18) and philanthropic organisations (n=11); 19 publications did not receive or report funding.

Equity, diversity and inclusion

We initially coded the articles using the Guide on Equity, Diversity and Inclusion Terminology by the Government of Canada.34 According to this guide, diversity refers to the variety of identities present within an organisation, group or society; equity involves recognising people’s unique experiences and situations and ensuring equitable access to resources and opportunities to achieve fair outcomes; and inclusion emphasises fostering environments where individuals feel valued, respected and experience a sense of belonging and engagement. The definition of inclusion also highlights providing opportunities and resources for full participation, which, in the context of research, translates to enabling individuals to contribute meaningfully to prioritising, designing and executing research.

Based on this definition of inclusion, papers discussing the inclusion of participants in the research sample were categorised as addressing diversity issues, often co-categorised under equity. Our initial observation revealed that publications rarely fit into one single EDI category (9/53). Most papers overlapped on equity and diversity (39/53) or equity and inclusion (10/53) (online supplemental table 1), underscoring the interconnectedness of these categories. Overall, 50 papers addressed equity, 42 discussed diversity and only 10 focused on inclusion—highlighting the structural barriers that members of historically marginalised communities face in achieving meaningful inclusion in research processes (eg, research design and implementation) and the broader research ecosystem (eg, representation in leadership, editorial roles and funding institutions).

Although significant trends in the literature were observed, such as the persistent under-representation of marginalised groups, most studies explored the nuanced barriers faced by specific communities. In the sections that follow, we present the main systemic barriers as they pertain to each of these communities.

Barriers across marginalised communities

Publications used different conceptualisations of racialised communities (eg, African American, Latino and Hispanic in the US context), 2SLGBTQIA+ individuals (eg, gender-diverse, LGBTQ and queer youth) and Indigenous peoples (eg, Māori in New Zealand and American Indian/Alaska Native in the US context), reflecting the social, cultural and political context in which they were published. Two studies used male/female (sex) as gender, overlooking the difference between a biological characteristic and a socially constructed identity. While records are not evenly distributed among marginalised communities, the existing publications highlight pressing issues unique to or shared by multiple communities.

Black and racialised individuals

Our conceptualisation of racialised individuals includes diverse ethnic backgrounds, immigrants and non-English speakers (in North America). In this context, racialised individuals encounter challenges ranging from under-recruitment to problematic demographic conceptualisations, power dynamics in research and publication biases (online supplemental table 2). Similar barriers are faced by black individuals, who also criticise misleading conceptualisations of race, limitations in measuring racism and discrimination, and oversight of ethical considerations (online supplemental table 3). Additionally, biases in peer-review processes and pervasive discrimination and under-representation within the broader research ecosystem.

Under-representation in research

Evidence suggested persistent under-representation of non-English speakers35 and Hispanic children36 in US-based paediatric research. Two studies37 38 highlighted cultural factors and communication gaps affecting recruitment. Language,39 40 immigration policies,39 lack of time and mobility40 were identified as significant challenges.

Barriers and enablers can differ among ethnic groups. One study41 observed that, compared with Asians, Hispanic parents showed more trust in providers when approached for research purposes. In contrast, Asian participants exhibit cultural barriers, such as different norms and preferences when disclosing or discussing issues related to illness or disability, that hinder health communication and participation. Barriers manifested at the intrapersonal, interpersonal and community levels,42 showcasing the complexity of under-representation and the need for comprehensive recruitment strategies.

One study identified that black parents were twice as likely to refuse consent for their child’s participation in a clinical trial when compared with parents of white children.35 Additionally, there were 65% lower odds of enrolment in a clinical trial for black parents compared with white parents.43 A US-based study suggested significant under-representation of African Americans in Autism Genetic Research registries.44 Similar trends were observed for black, Asian American and Hispanic participants in US paediatric clinical trials.45 Various barriers contributing to this under-representation were identified, including distrust, safety concerns, complex consent forms, difficulties engaging potential participants, geographical access (eg, distance to study sites), and lack of insurance (in the US context).46,48

Race, racism and biases

Scoping reviews49 50 challenged how race and racism are defined and measured. One study discussed potential biases in adiposity measurements, emphasising the failure to account for the diverse body compositions prevalent within certain ethnicities.51 This highlighted the intricate implicit biases underlying the conceptualisation and measurement of health variables.

Two publications49 52 pointed to a scarcity of psychometrically evaluated measures for racial discrimination in children. One paper52 exposed common erroneous practices, such as using race as a proxy for genetics or economic disadvantage. Biases in cognitive assessments were particularly detrimental to black communities.53 Finally, one study54 emphasised not only the scarcity of studies addressing racial/ethnic inequities but also the absence of conceptualisation of race as a social construct.

Discrimination in the research ecosystem

Cultural differences, communication and power dynamics can challenge research processes. One paper revealed a lack of cultural competence—the ability to engage effectively across diverse cultures—in research with immigrant children.55 Others suggested challenges in consent/assent as an ongoing process, highlighting the importance of paying attention to non-verbal cues in children.56 At a higher societal level, scholars suggested unequal power dynamics in international collaborations between researchers from minority and majority countries—an alternative conceptualisation that challenges deficit-based narratives rooted in development or income world classifications—highlighting that power dynamics exist not only between researchers and participants but also between researchers with different ranks and locations.57

Racialised communities encounter biases in peer review and publication. One study exposed biases related to race and ethnicity in peer review,58 suggesting there have been limited efforts to diversify editorial teams and limited transparency regarding the diversity of reviewers, editors, editorial boards and measures to address biases. Other publications discussed the challenges in publishing child development research from majority countries, facing disparities in review processes, publication biases, adaptation dilemmas in measurement tools, and funding obstacles, resulting in non-representative child development research.57

Racialised academics can face exclusion, isolation, microaggressions and other challenges throughout their careers in child health scholarship. Multiple publications59,62 discussed overt and covert racism experienced by racialised scholars. One publication60 exposed the ‘minority tax’, which is a load of extra responsibilities that faculty carry due to often being asked to be ‘the minority voice’.

Indigenous peoples

We observed fewer publications dedicated to Indigenous peoples than black and other racialised individuals (online supplemental table 4). This scarcity, however, does not diminish the significance of the available evidence. One publication underscored the lack of Indigenous health equity in the vision and aims of health registers (eg, databases) in Aotearoa New Zealand, pointing to difficulties in ensuring high-quality data for research.63 Moreover, the study suggested that the limited Indigenous representation and workforce capacity represent a barrier to addressing health disparities within Indigenous communities.

Cultural difference and reciprocity

A study conducted in Canada64 noted systemic and cultural barriers throughout the research process and difficulties in securing sustainable funding. The study elucidated challenges emerging from the absence of predetermined and precise metrics and outcomes. The interests of funding agencies and scholars can conflict with the demands of Indigenous communities for autonomy, particularly in deciding what to prioritise and how to measure its success. A US study65 emphasised building trust over time and fostering long-term collaborative and mutually beneficial relationships within Indigenous communities. Other challenges emerged with respect to setting timelines, values and expectations and illustrated the complexity of community partnerships.64

Finally, one study37 reported Māori parents’ perceptions of research projects, and specifically their belief that projects rarely articulate Indigenous beliefs and values. They also explored additional challenges, including difficulties explaining the difference between research processes and routine medical care to Indigenous participants, concerns about invasion of privacy and confidentiality, and instances of insensitive communication by staff.

Families and children with disabilities

For families and children with disabilities, studies highlighted intertwined barriers that include engagement complexities, the prevalence of discriminatory language and barriers to accessing research opportunities (online supplemental table 5).

Exclusion and challenges to inclusion

One review revealed a concerning 89.9% exclusion rate of children with disabilities in child developmental research.66 A systematic review67 suggested similar exclusion in oral health research, along with discriminatory language and reliance on parents or caregivers as proxies for children.

Barriers can vary according to the type of disability. In visual impairment research,68 language, trust, loss of contact information, internal migration and sociocultural factors correlated with diminished visibility of families from socioeconomically deprived and ethnic minority backgrounds. Conversely, a study focusing on participation of pregnant individuals with substance use disorder in child development research reported observations of distrust towards academic staff, shame, guilt, stigma and fear of potential legal consequences.

Certain aspects inherent to the research procedures, such as language choices and the depth of engagement, posed obstacles to the complete and respectful involvement of children with disabilities. An examination of studies on attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder suggests that only 42.1% adhered to person-centred language guidelines while 47.6% reproduced stigmatising language.69 Additionally, a review70 identified only a handful of examples of the inclusion of children with disabilities in the design and implementation of qualitative research. Most studies treated children with disabilities as subjects rather than active research participants.

Women and girls

The evidence on barriers for women and girls revealed persistent gender biases and sexism (online supplemental table 6).

Gender biases

Replicated experimental studies on pain research71 72 revealed persistent gender biases in adult ratings of paediatric pain. Due to the biased perception that girls express pain more overtly than boys, girls were constantly rated as experiencing less pain than boys. These biases, perpetuated by societal stereotypes, challenged accurate assessment and overall diagnoses.

Sexism in the research ecosystem

Numerous publications underscored pervasive sexism and gender prejudices in paediatric scholarship.5962 73,75 A paediatric faculty survey uncovered prevalent harassment that disproportionately affects women, who often experienced higher emotional tolls and fear of retaliation.73 Qualitative research62 suggested work-life balance challenges in faculty members, primarily among women. Additionally, studies demonstrated under-representation of women in academic leadership, editorial roles and clinical research, emphasising the systemic nature of gender inequities in academia.74 75

2SLGBTQIA+ individuals

Research studies examining barriers for 2SLGBTQIA+ individuals highlighted under-reporting and significant attitudinal and ethical challenges (online supplemental table 7).

Under-reporting

A cross-sectional study50 suggested substantial under-reporting of sexual orientation and gender identity in school psychology research, potentially introducing systematic biases. Similarly, a recent review76 identified similar under-representation challenges, pointing out inconsistencies and data aggregation of sexual orientation and behaviour.

Attitudinal and ethical challenges

Two studies identified attitudinal barriers, including participants’ concerns about being ‘outed’ and facing criticism for their LGBTQ identity.42 77 Furthermore, potential recruitment barriers related to the need for parental consent were identified as impacting participation in gender and sexual behaviour research.76 77 For example, some boards still request parental consent, which tends to be a barrier to participation among sexually diverse youth from marginalised communities. The lack of a nuanced understanding of the youth context has contributed to discrepancies and challenges in obtaining ethics approval.78

Intersectionality

Individuals and communities with intersecting social conditions and identities experienced combined challenges ranging from discrimination to biases, a lack of disaggregated data, a misunderstanding of ethical considerations and a lack of intersectional approaches to research.

Socioeconomic status

Some publications addressing barriers faced by racialised and black individuals discussed the intricate intersectionality of socioeconomic status (SES) and the nuances of systemic under-representation, misleading conceptualisations and non-representative conclusions. Under-representation of children from low socioeconomic backgrounds may be a critical gap in our understanding of child health.50 Furthermore, analytical challenges arise from the diverse terminologies used to describe and measure socioeconomic conditions, such as social class, social stratification and SES, which are often used interchangeably.62 Additionally, some suggested the existence of general misinterpretations of the skills of children and youth from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. This inadequacy came from misinterpreting expected stress-adaptive responses as ‘deficits’, which contributed to biases in our understanding of socioeconomic influences on cognition and behaviour.53

Lack of disaggregated data

Another significant barrier is the lack of disaggregated data. For instance, authors revealed unreported race and ethnicity in sexual behaviour research on gender and sexually diverse youth,76 and significant gaps capturing comprehensive data on intersectional identities in maternal and child health research.79 An examination of the use of intersectionality in research on childhood exposure to intimate partner violence, observed a deficit of intersectionality frameworks and practical challenges, including the lack of practical guidelines on incorporating intersectionality in research.80

Intersecting barriers in the research ecosystem

In the context of academic paediatrics, scholars emphasised the combined effects of racism and sexism on racialised scholars.62 The findings highlight the importance of examining the nuanced effects of intersecting barriers, providing a basis for critically exploring how these factors affect privilege and oppression within child health scholarship.

Discussion

This article aimed to explore the available literature on systemic barriers affecting equity, diversity and inclusion in child health research addressing various marginalised communities, including racialised and black individuals, 2SLGBTQIA+ individuals, Indigenous peoples, women and girls, and children and families living with disabilities. We chose a scoping method to map the types of systemic barriers and identify existing gaps in the literature, future research questions and potential interventions. We identified multiple and intersecting barriers, emphasising the need for inclusive and comprehensive strategies that address the compounded effects of these barriers.

Systemic barriers

We identified an increased number of publications during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, reflecting a heightened awareness of structural racism and health inequities evident during this time. Members of marginalised communities navigate multiple challenges, starting from under-representation in research samples and recruitment barriers. Power dynamics and lack of cultural safety—which extends beyond mere cultural competence to encompass inclusive environments—are crucial in research where communication and power dynamics pose challenges. Black and racialised individuals, like gender and sexually diverse youth, can feel ‘othered’ in the research processes. Evidence suggests gender81 and race82 concordance in care influences patients’ degree of control and comfort. Similarly, demographic concordance in research can facilitate communication, cultural safety, trust and community links. Additionally, academic pipelines for black and racialised individuals have unique challenges. As researchers, black and racialised individuals can experience racism, harassment and particularly tokenism and the ‘minority tax’. Recognising the interconnected struggles of black and racialised individuals underscores the need for antiracist methods spread throughout the research process.83

Various social and demographic characteristics are conceptualised and used in problematic ways. Our results suggest few studies conceptualise race as a social construct. Race can also be used as a proxy for SES and even be categorised as a risk factor. Racism, not race, should be conceptualised as a risk factor;84 if there is no alternative, race can be used as a risk marker.52 Future research should focus on developing and improving paediatric psychometric measurements of racism49 and measurement of structural racism.85 Other forms of discrimination, such as Islamophobia,86 have a profound impact on mental health. Future research could inquire into how these forms of discrimination (eg, Islamophobia, xenophobia, transphobia) impact child health.

Our findings suggest Indigenous health equity is not part of the vision and aims of health databases. Similar gaps could exist in child health research institutes and initiatives. Indigenous scholars highlight difficulties securing long-term funding, culturally insensitive research(ers) and limited Indigenous workforce capacity. These issues further underscore the need for culturally sensitive and ethically grounded Indigenous research. One Child Every Child—a Canadian-led international interdisciplinary research initiative—has embraced Parallel Paths to advance Indigenous scholarship and perspectives alongside non-Indigenous research.87 Similar approaches could ensure institutional support and resources needed for long-term collaborative research relationships. In Canada, the First Nations Information Governance Centre has suggested adopting the principles of Ownership, Control, Access and Possession to honour Indigenous traditions as information and story gatherers and ensure Indigenous self-determination over data collection processes, management, and use.88

Evidence from reviews on developmental and oral research66 67 suggests children with disabilities are systemically under-represented, hindering their participation rights and challenging our understanding of different developmental pathways. The literature underscores the need for inclusive methodologies to increase representation and promote more active involvement throughout the research process. Future research should evaluate the exclusion rate in other fields of child health research and adapt and innovate inclusive methodologies. In doing so, researchers need to be mindful about the different types of barriers children can face according to their impairment (ie, physical, blindness or low vision, deaf or hearing, or cognitive), as well as the different methodological tools and considerations to overcome these barriers.89 The variety of barriers and strategies underscores that there is no one-size-fits-all for inclusive research. Most importantly, researchers must anticipate inclusion strategies, commit to methodological flexibility and person-centred language, and, when possible, include children with disabilities as active participants in the research process.70

Addressing the unique needs of 2SLGBTQIA+ youth in health research demands various ethical and methodological considerations. A predominant systemic barrier is the under-reporting50 or aggregation76 of gender and sexuality categories, undermining a comprehensive understanding of diverse health realities. Sex-diverse and gender-diverse youth often grapple with the fear of being ‘othered’ or criticised for their identity, highlighting the importance of creating inclusive and gender-affirming research environments. To foster respectful and inclusive approaches, research needs to confront the impact of historical and present-day cis-centrism and structural transphobia90 and be reflective of how these impact research processes and, ultimately, health outcomes.

Evidence suggests sexism rooted in the child health research ecosystem.9 62 73 Women can experience harassment, particularly in early career stages. However, they also experience other forms of systemic discrimination, such as negative views of pregnancy, tensions between their public and private lives, difficulties finding work-life balance and the pressure to postpone family decisions. A contributing factor to systemic sexism is the lack of representation of women in leadership positions.75 Although a more representative leadership does not guarantee rapid systemic and cultural change, proper representation seems like a necessary step before changes can occur in the foreseeable future.

In our review, multiple studies substituted gender with sex or used these terms interchangeably. Child health research must be critical to the misuse of these categories as sex refers to biological characteristics while gender encompasses identity and expression—both of which are distinct yet essential determinants of health. As with race, if researchers ignore gender as a social construct, they can miss the mechanisms that drive health inequities and systemic discrimination. Previous research highlights combined barriers for intersecting individuals and communities, such as under-represented women in clinical trials,91 underscoring the importance of integrating intersectional lenses in research design and practice.

Intersectional research has the potential to surpass mere unidimensional analysis of marginalised communities and provide a nuanced understanding of prevalent barriers and health realities. Employing intersectional lenses in research entails considerations in study conceptualisation, participant recruitment, data collection and data analysis.92 The use of intersectional lenses in epidemiological research is limited,93 but emerging statistical methods, such as multilevel analysis of individual heterogeneity and discriminatory accuracy,94 95 are promising tools for advancing intersectional quantitative health research.

Inclusive strategies

Inclusion in the research process has improved in the last decades, largely driven by the adoption of participatory methodologies.96 97 These approaches offer flexible frameworks that allow research teams to determine the level of stakeholder and community engagement, ranging from informing and consulting to deeper collaboration, co-production and empowerment.96 This shift reflects a growing recognition of the importance of equitable knowledge production and the need to reduce power imbalances between researchers and historically marginalised communities. Community-based participatory research has the potential to effectively integrate research and action,98 enhance recruitment capacity, conduct culturally and logistically appropriate research, develop partner(ship) capacity and increase sustainability and system change capacity.99 Approaches such as Patient-Oriented Research have been institutionalised to promote inclusive processes in health research. The Canadian Strategy for Patient‐Oriented Research launched by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research reflects this approach. However, institutionalising such strategies can obscure superficial engagement,100 disregarding the broader structural forces that shape health and participation in healthcare systems.101 Critiques suggest expanding other participatory and critical forms of research100 or shifting from patient to public involvement or community engagement.101

Clinical trials scholars have effectively used socio-ecological models to understand barriers and identify strategies.102 Similarly, we adopted this approach to map existing barriers (figure 4) and potential strategies (figure 5). This framework enables the organisation of barriers and strategies in a manner that facilitates their application in real-world settings.

By contrasting barriers (figure 4) and strategies (figure 5), we make the following suggestions. To improve research processes, barriers at the individual level, such as logistical challenges, could be addressed through methodological flexibility, adequate compensation and logistical support (eg, transportation, childcare). At the interpersonal level, the lack of diverse research teams and the absence of cultural safety could be mitigated by fostering more diverse teams and providing training in EDI, cultural safety and accessibility. Organisational challenges, such as weak community relationships, could benefit from approaches such as participatory methods and university or faculty-wide community-driven research agendas. Finally, at the policy level, issues such as predetermined research agendas, predefined outcomes and short-term funding cycles could be addressed by creating funding streams that support long-term community relationships and community-led prioritisation processes.

Similarly, to improve the research ecosystem, barriers could be addressed through comprehensive revisions to institutional policies and practices. These barriers include work-life imbalances at the individual level, robust reporting mechanisms to address discrimination by peers, supervisors and authorities—particularly during training—at the interpersonal level, and inequitable compensation and advancement at the organisational level. International initiatives, such as the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment, have advocated for evaluation practices that recognise the societal impacts of research alongside the generation of knowledge.103 104 Additionally, debiasing committee compositions and adopting deliberative approaches to hiring, promotion, tenure and funding processes could further reduce inequities.105

Collaborating with Indigenous peoples and other historically marginalised communities requires that we consider where and how decisions are made in research institutions.106 The academy must ensure decision-making embeds strong community voice, trust and reciprocity. Models of inclusive governance106 107 could close the equity gap by forging common values, priorities and strategic directions and enshrining diverse and Indigenous communities in research governance. In addition to equity outcomes, the principle nothing about us without us can also drive health innovation outcomes.107

Limitations

Several limitations were identified in this study. First, our search strategy allowed us to investigate the scope of barriers to EDI in child health research in a cost and time-effective fashion. By prioritising breadth over depth, we may have missed publications using language not reflected in the search strategy (eg, ethical concerns or considerations), more rigorous use of controlled vocabulary and subject headings or in other databases. Future reviews could include additional keywords and terms that are proximal to the health realities of specific communities. Additional keywords, such as recruitment, retention, engagement, participation, career advancement, leadership or ethics, could retrieve publications about barriers not reached with the present search strategy. Furthermore, some keywords could be further deconstructed, such as using assent, consent, power, ownership, sovereignty and other terms to inquire about ethical considerations.

Second, we did not include low income as a prioritised community but as an intersecting factor of other social categories. By doing this, we maintained a more focused exploration of specific communities but missed the opportunity to identify and address systemic issues related to poverty and financial hardship.

Last, readers should exercise caution as our search strategy was conceptualised in English using categories used in Canadian research policy.31 Recognising the diverse and context-dependent nature of social categories and identities is crucial for a nuanced understanding of EDI in child health global research. Systemic barriers exist within and across national borders. Child health research endeavours should consider systemic barriers in their contexts. To improve equitable and inclusive child health research, future endeavours must enact EDI policies and processes within their teams and enhance participation from diverse human experiences, conditions, locations and developmental pathways, including fostering collaboration with local communities and researchers from underrepresented geographies.

Our review also has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the first scoping review examining systemic barriers to equity, diversity and inclusion in child health research across multiple marginalised communities and their intersections. As such, it can assist researchers in identifying potential strategies to foster equitable and inclusive research. Second, we identified an unequal distribution of publications across communities, underscoring both the focus and gaps in academic discussions. The absence of literature on specific issues may reflect under-reporting, a lack of research focus or systemic biases within the research ecosystem. Third, we adopted a systemic approach, scoping and mapping barriers across multiple levels of the socio-ecological model throughout the research process and broader research ecosystem. This systemic perspective can facilitate the development of comprehensive strategies that address multiple and interconnected barriers simultaneously, creating opportunities for more effective and sustainable solutions. Finally, while our study aligns with Canadian research policies on EDI, it also provides globally relevant insights, particularly regarding the need for disaggregated data, intersectional research methodologies and structural reforms to advance inclusivity in child health research.

Conclusion

This scoping review explored the literature addressing barriers to equity, diversity and inclusion in child health research. We observed multiple barriers for black and racialised individuals, Indigenous peoples, families and children with disabilities, 2SLGBTQIA+ youth and women and girls. Barriers are present throughout the research process and the research ecosystem and demonstrate systemic under-representation, exclusion, discrimination, biases, stereotypes and other challenges. A systematic investigation of barriers needs to be conducted into the scope, prevalence and nuances of each. In this context, our review has only mapped structural challenges and uncovered potential gaps and lines of research.

Health equity and inclusive research are closely intertwined. Child health research endeavours should report on barriers experienced and strategies applied to facilitate methodological innovation and cross-fertilisation. As child health research moves forward, it is imperative to collectively commit to dismantling systemic barriers to equitable participation. Researchers must focus on equitable inclusion within and across borders, highlighting the need for broader representation from diverse human experiences, social conditions, locations and developmental pathways in child health research. To achieve this, stakeholders must establish systematic mechanisms for identifying, addressing and reporting barriers. We suggest co-creating multilevel approaches with marginalised communities. In doing so, the principle of nothing about us without us must be enacted, engaging marginalised communities in the governance, planning and implementation of comprehensive multilevel strategies aiming to improve research processes and outcomes.
