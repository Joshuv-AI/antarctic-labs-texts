Integrin-β1/FAK signaling is involved in electrical stimulation to prevent disuse muscular atrophy induced by tail suspension in mice
Authors: Yizheng Huang, Jia He, Xiaofeng Duan, Jiahao Chen, Zilong Zhao, Hao Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectrical stimulation is a widely used method for preventing and treating disuse muscle atrophy; however, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely examined. Integrins, a class of transmembrane cell adhesion receptors, are considered primary mechanosensors involved in load-induced muscle growth. However, no previous studies have demonstrated whether integrin β1 and its downstream FAK signaling pathway are involved in the prevention of disuse muscle atrophy via electrical stimulation. Our study hypothesized that mechanical signals generated by electrical induced muscle contraction activate integrins, thereby contributing to muscle atrophy prevention.Materials and methodsA disuse muscle atrophy model was constructed by tail suspending C57BL/6 mice for 2 weeks. The average physiological cross-sectional area, muscle strength, muscle fiber type, integrin β1, p-FAK, and p-p70S6K protein expression in the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles were measured following electrical stimulation.ResultsMice in the tail suspension with electrical stimulation (TS + E) group exhibited significantly greater physiological cross-sectional area and muscle strength compared with the tail suspension (TS) group (p < 0.05). Additionally, integrin β1 expression was lower in the TS group compared with the control group (p < 0.05). In contrast, in the TS + E group, expression levels of integrin β1, FAK, p-mTOR, and p-p70s6k were elevated compared with the TS group (p < 0.05). In GAS and SOL, the p-p70s6k/p70s6k ratio was significantly higher in the TS + E group compared with the TS group (p < 0.05). In GAS, the p-FAK/FAK ratio was higher in the TS + E group compared with the TS group (p < 0.01). However, no significant difference in p-FAK/FAK ratio was observed between the two groups in SOL.ConclusionElectrical stimulation upregulates the expression of the mechanosensor integrin β1 and enhances the phosphorylation of its downstream effector, FAK, in skeletal muscle. These effects may contribute to the attenuation of disuse muscle atrophy, providing preclinical evidence supporting the role of the integrin-β1/FAK signaling pathway in electrical stimulation-mediated muscle preservation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10190-8.Keywords: Disuse muscle atrophy, Electrical stimulation, Integrin β1, FAK, Tail suspension

Introduction

Disuse muscle atrophy is characterized by a reduction in muscle strength and quality resulting from decreased or absent muscle activity and is accompanied by alterations in muscle fiber composition. This condition leads to functional impairment, increased fracture incidence, and a higher likelihood of falls, thereby compromising the ability to maintain normal daily activities [1, 2]. Furthermore, muscle disuse results in reduced glucose tolerance and increased insulin resistance, thereby increasing the risk of type II diabetes mellitus [3, 4]. In patients with pre-existing comorbidities, reduced muscle mass further accelerates disease progression [5]. One of the primary external contributors to muscle atrophy is the reduction in mechanical loading of skeletal muscle, and it commonly occurs in conditions such as fracture, injury-induced immobilization, systemic cachexia leading to long-term bedridden states, and weightlessness in astronauts. The decrease in muscle protein synthesis rate, combined with an increase in muscle protein breakdown rate, creates an imbalance that directly contributes to disuse muscle atrophy [6].

Electrical stimulation mimics endogenous bioelectricity by applying an electric current to nerves or muscles, thereby activating proprioceptors and mechanosensors and inducing rhythmic muscle contractions [7]. The resultant muscle pump effect enhances local blood circulation, improves nutrients delivery, and may prevent, delay, or attenuate the development of muscle atrophy [8].

Integrins are widely recognized as the primary mechanosensors that mediate responses to mechanical loading and play critical roles in signal transduction between cells and the extracellular environment [9]. Each integrin heterodimer in mammals consists of an α subunit and a β subunit. Structurally, integrins are type I transmembrane proteins, each containing a relatively large extracellular domain and a mostly short cytoplasmic domain. A total of 8 β-subunits and 18 α-subunits combine to form 24 functionally distinct heterodimeric transmembrane receptors [10]. Mechanical signals activate integrins by exposing recessive binding and phosphorylation sites, increasing their affinity for ligands [11], and enabling the formation of adhesion complexes with other protein assemblies, termed integrin adhesion complexes (IACs). These IACs activate key intracellular downstream signaling mediators, including focal adhesion kinase (FAK), Src family protein tyrosine kinase, and integrin-linked kinase, thereby transmitting mechanical and biochemical signals and promoting cell proliferation, adhesion, diffusion, survival, and ECM assembly and remodeling [10, 12]. Integrins are involved in various skeletal muscle disorders. Studies have demonstrated that mice with integrin β1 knockout exhibit congenital myopathy and myotonic dystrophy [13]. Overexpressing α7β1-integrin in C2C12 cells enhances myofibroblast adhesion and proliferation while decreasing apoptosis, suggesting a potential therapeutic role in myotonic dystrophy [14]. Integrin β1 also plays a key role in maintaining skeletal muscle satellite cell homeostasis [13]. During exercise-induced skeletal muscle hypertrophy, α7β1 integrin is upregulated following centrifugal contraction, thereby enhancing the structural integrity of skeletal muscle and protecting against repetitive mechanical injury [15, 16]. Collectively, these findings suggest a protective role for integrin β1 in skeletal muscle. It has been demonstrated that FAK, a downstream effector of integrin signaling, regulates the AKT/m-TOR pathway [17, 18]. Specifically, the AKT/ mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1(mTORC1) signaling pathway is involved in skeletal muscle protein metabolism. The mTORC1 complex modulates the phosphorylation of downstream substrates involved in translational initiation (for example, p70S6K and 4EBP1) and elongation (for example, eEF2), thereby enhancing muscle protein synthesis [19], thereby improving muscle atrophy.

Previous studies have reported that integrin α7 mRNA expression decreased in skeletal muscle following disuse [20, 21]. Electrical stimulation attenuates the suspension-induced decrease in ribosomal RNA and protein synthesis-related proteins in mice, promotes skeletal muscle recovery, and prevents the loss of nuclei and satellite cells in disuse muscle atrophy [22–24]. Given that integrins are mechanosensitive receptors, interventions such as exercise, massage, and other forms of external mechanical stimulation may provoke skeletal muscle regeneration through the integrin-mediated pathway [19, 25]. However, no study has yet indicated whether integrin β1 and its downstream FAK signaling are involved in the prevention of disuse muscle atrophy by electrical stimulation. Therefore, this study hypothesized that electrical stimulation produces mechanical signals that activate integrin β1, which is involved in the prevention of disuse muscle atrophy by electrical stimulation.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Thirty 7-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Wuhan Beisai Model Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qualification No: BC-20240322-YQ-001). Animals were housed at room temperature, 22–24 ℃ under a 12-h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to water and food. Mice were acclimatized for 1 week before the experiment. The study protocol was evaluated and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hubei Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Ethical batch No. HBZY2022-C03-02). All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ARRIVE guidelines [26].

Grouping and tail suspension

The mice were randomly assigned to three groups: (1) Control group (C, n = 10), which received no intervention; (2) tail suspension group (TS, n = 10), which underwent tail suspension for 14 days; (3) tail suspension and electrical stimulation group (TS + E, n = 10). The sample size was determined a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1) with a type I error rate (α) of 0.05, statistical power (1–β) of 0.80, and a medium effect size (f = 0.25). Based on previous studies in this field [22–24], as well as ethical considerations and experimental feasibility, a final sample size of 10 animals per group (30 animals in total) was ultimately determined. Mice were suspended for 14 days, and electrical stimulation intervention was performed at the same time. TS is a recognized standardized method for inducing disuse muscle atrophy [27]. Briefly, based on the tail length of the mice, the tail was secured and wrapped with medical adhesive tape, thick rope and a movable hook, and the hook was hung on the cage cover to ensure that the hindlimbs leave the ground and the forelimbs touch the ground. The long axis of the mouse’s body was maintained at approximately a 30° angle relative to the ground, allowing for limited mobility. Animals were monitored daily for food intake, urination, and defecation.

Electrical stimulation program

On the second day of tail suspension, the mice in the TS + E group were placed in a prone positioned, and the body and hindlimbs were immobilized using medical breathable adhesive tape. Their feet were maintained in plantarflexion (parallel to the tibia) to keep the hindlimb muscles in a constantly shortened position. The electrical stimulation (KWD-808I, Changzhou, China) was applied using the seven-point stimulation method with electrodes placed 3–4 mm apart on both sides of the left gastrocnemius muscle. Stimulation consisted of alternating sparse-dense waves, cycling between 2 and 100 Hz (sparse to dense) every 3 s, with a pulse width of 200 µs and a current intensity of 1 mA. The stimulation intensity was set at the motor threshold (visible twitching of the gastrocnemius muscle). Each stimulation session lasted 30 min, administered once daily, 6 days per week, for 2 weeks. To avoid errors, the tail suspension group and control group were also fixed in a prone position for 30 min.

Grip force test

Hindlimb muscle strength was measured using the claw grip tester (deep intelligent push-pull tester, SC series, China). Mice were allowed to rest on the mesh until they firmly grasped the midpoint of the grid length using only two hindlimbs, and their tails were gently pulled backwards until the hindlimb was separated from the mesh surface. The grip strength was calculated as follows: force divided by body weight (N/g).

Collection and storage of experimental samples

All mice were euthanized 24 h after the last intervention (day 14). Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 30 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital. Samples of soleus (SOL) and gastrocnemius (GAS) muscles were then collected after the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, for H&E and immunofluorescence staining. GAS and SOL were taken from each group of mice after internal fixation, fixed with paraformaldehyde, and prepared for staining. Additional fresh muscle samples, which were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 ℃ for subsequent molecular analyses.

Histopathology

All samples were paraffin-embedded after fixation with paraformaldehyde, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Histochemical analyses were performed afterwards, including H&E staining and immunofluorescence staining. The left GAS and SOL muscles of each group underwent H&E stained, were photographed, and analyzed using a microscope (Sportop, EX33). Sections were blocked in immunofluorescence staining with 10% goat serum in PBS for 30 min at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the following primary antibodies, integrin β1 (12594-1-AP, 1:3000), MyHC I (GB111857, 1:200), and MyHC II (GB112130, 1:400). Integrin β1 antibody was obtained from China Three Eagles, MyHC antibody was obtained from China Sevier. After dropwise addition of the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody was incubated in a wet box at 20–37 ℃ for 1 h, including fluorescent (Cy3)-labeled sheep anti-rabbit IgG (BA1032, 1:100); fluorescent (FITC)-labeled sheep anti-mouse IgG (BA1101, 1:100). Images were captured using fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japanese company, BX53). Three fields of view were taken from each section and analyzed. Image analysis was performed using ProPlus (version 6.0) and ImageJ software, and outliers (maximum and minimum values) were excluded before statistical analysis.

Western blotting

GAS and SOL muscles were lysed on ice with RIPA lysate (BL504A, Biosharp, China) containing a protease inhibitor mixture (G2006, Servicebio, China) and a phosphatase inhibitor (G2007, Servicebio, China). After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was quantified. Equal amounts of proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk or bovine serum albumin for 1 h, and then incubated overnight with primary antibodies, including 1:1000 [anti-p70S6K (AF6226, affinity), anti-p-p70S6K (Thr389, AF3227, affinity), anti-akt (AF0806, affinity), anti-p-akt (Ser473, AF8355, affinity), anti-p-mTOR (5536T, CST, United States of America), anti-integrin β1 (#34971, Cell Signaling, United States of America), anti-FAK (12636-1-AP, Sanying, Wuhan), anti-p-FAK (Tyr398 83933-1-RR, Sanying, Wuhan)], and 1:5000 [anti-GAPDH (60004-1‐Ig)]. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies. Protein expression levels were quantified using ImageJ software.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from fresh gastrocnemius muscle and soleus muscle homogenate with 1 mL Trizol (Vazyme, R401-01, China). According to the instructions of the reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, R223-01, China), the total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA products. Next, the specific transcription products were quantitatively detected by qRT-PCR. Primer sequences are demonstrated in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (version 25.0). Statistical analysis was conducted using analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test. When a normal distribution was not met, a nonparametric rank sum test (Kruskal–Wallis test) was performed. The analyses were performed with a minimum of three replicates using the R software. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation of ≥ 3 independent experiments. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Weight, hindlimb muscle strength, and CSA

The mice were weighed before and after the intervention. No significant differences in body weight among the mice in each group at baseline. After the intervention, body weights of the TS and TS + E group were both lower than those of the control group (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively) (Fig. 1A).

Muscle strength testing revealed that the TS group exhibited lower muscle strength compared with the C group (p < 0.01). In contrast, the TS་E group demonstrated higher muscle strength than the TS group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1B).

H&E staining demonstrated that the CSA of GAS and SOL were significantly lower in the TS group compared with the C group (p < 0.001 for both groups) (Figs. 1C–E), representing approximately 28% and 52% reductions, respectively. In contrast, CSA values in GAS and SOL were significantly increased in the TS་E group compared with the TS group (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively) (Figs. 1C–E), corresponding to approximately 25% and 34% increases.

Muscle fiber types

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that, compared with the C group, the CSA of both type I and type II muscle fibers in the SOL of the TS group was lower (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2B), with reductions of approximately 55% and 53%, respectively. Conversely, in the TS + E group, the CSA of both fiber types was significantly higher compared with the TS group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B), demonstrating increases of approximately 30% and 29%, respectively. Additionally, compared with the C group, the TS group exhibited a significantly lower proportion of type I muscle fibers (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2C), accompanied by a marked increase in the proportion of type II muscle fibers (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2D). Notably, the reduction in the proportion of type I fibers in SOL of the TS + E group was no significant difference (p > 0.05) compared with the TS group (Figs. 2A and C). These findings indicate that disuse induces a shift in muscle fiber type from I to II, which is not reversed by electrical stimulation.

Therefore, the phosphorylation levels of mTOR and its upstream factor AKT, downstream factor p70s6k, were assessed. The results demonstrated that the phosphorylated expression of AKT in GAS and SOL was significantly lower in the TS group than in the C group (p < 0.01) (Figs. 3A, C, G, and I). In contrast, it was significantly higher in the TS + E group than in the TS group (p < 0.001) (Figs. 3A, C, G, and I). Compared with the C group, the phosphorylated expression of mTOR and p70s6k in GAS was significantly lower in the TS group (p < 0.01) (Figs. 3A, D, and E). In contrast, the TS + E group was significantly higher compared with the TS group (p < 0.05 and p < 0.001) (Figs. 3A, D, and E). Compared with the C group, the phosphorylation of mTOR and p70s6k in SOL was lower in the TS group (p < 0.01, p > 0.05) (Figs. 3G, J, and K), while the TS + E group was higher compared with the TS group (p < 0.01) (Figs. 3G, J, and K). In GAS, p-AKT/AKT was higher in the TS group relative to the C group (p < 0.01) (Figure S1 A), which was lower in the TS + E group relative to the TS group (p < 0.01) (Figure S1 A). In SOL, compared with the C group, p-AKT/AKT was higher in the TS group (p < 0.05) (Figure S1 D); no difference was found in the TS + E group compared with the TS group (p > 0.05) (Figure S1 D). In GAS and SOL, no difference was observed in p-p70s6k/ p70s6k change in the TS group compared with the C group (p > 0.05) (Figures S1 B and E), whereas an increase was identified in p-p70s6k/ p70s6k in the TS + E group compared with the TS group (p < 0.05) (Figures S1 B and E).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated whether integrin β1 is involved in the protective mechanism of electrical stimulation against disuse muscle atrophy. Our findings demonstrate that electrical stimulation upregulates integrin β1 expression and activates its downstream effector FAK, which may contribute to the attenuation of muscle atrophy, thereby providing a more robust theoretical basis for its broader clinical application.

Integrins play a crucial role in mechanical load-induced skeletal muscle hypertrophy [19]. Our study demonstrated that integrin β1 expression decreased following 2 weeks of tail suspension. External mechanical means are the primary basis for stimulating integrin activation, and it has been extensively demonstrated that resistance and other forms of active exercise can effectively activate integrin and FAK expression, thereby mediating skeletal muscle hypertrophy [29, 30]. Integrin α7β1 attenuates the phosphorylation of injury-related signaling molecules (for example, JNK, p38, and ERK) activated by downhill running exercise and thereby prevents exercise-induced muscle damage in skeletal muscle [31]. Zou K et al. [32] demonstrated that compared with the sedentary group of α7 BX2-integrin transgenic (α7 Tg) mice, the activation of AKT, mTOR, and p70 (S6K) in the exercise group of α7 Tg was higher, and the overall muscle hypertrophy, relative muscle weight, myofibrillar protein, and strength were significantly increased. Previous studies have also demonstrated that mechanical stimuli other than exercise can likewise mediate skeletal muscle regeneration through the integrin pathway. Liu Q et al. demonstrated that massage can reduce muscle damage from overuse through integrins and laminin [25]. In a study by Riki Ogasawara et al., integrin β1 expression was increased after 18 sessions of electrical stimulation applied to the gastrocnemius muscle in normal Sprague-Dawley rats [33]. However, it has not been reported whether integrins are involved in the treatment of skeletal muscle atrophy by electrical stimulation. Li Y et al. studied integrin and electrical stimulation in stress urinary incontinence (SUI). They revealed that the use of an integrin β1 inhibitor reduced the protective effect of electrical stimulation on SUI. In contrast, electrical stimulation combined with recombinant human integrin β1 could protect female vaginal wall fibroblasts from SUI, indicating that integrin may be a potential target of electrical stimulation in SUI treatment [34, 35]. Our results demonstrate that the mechanosensor integrin β1 is increased in mouse skeletal muscle after electrical stimulation and may be involved in the promotion of skeletal muscle protein synthesis, which is consistent, to some extent, with findings of Li Y et al.

The mouse hindlimb suspension model simulates the reduction of mechanical loading of skeletal muscle in populations with weightlessness, fractures, or prolonged bed rest after surgery, thereby resulting in decreased muscle mass and decreased muscle strength [36]. Vanmunster M et al. suspended the hindlimbs of male C57BL/6J mice for 14 days. They demonstrated that suspended mice had significantly lower body weights and CSA of skeletal muscle fibers in GAS and SOL muscles compared with the controls [37]. Our results demonstrated that a 2-week period of suspension reduced CSA and muscle strength in the hindlimbs of mice and also induced changes in the muscle fiber types, consistent with the results of Vanmunster M et al. The cortical motor pathway activated by electrical stimulation as a physical means is functionally similar to that activated by voluntary contraction [38]. Related studies have demonstrated that both high-frequency and low-frequency electrical stimulation can effectively improve the severity of muscle atrophy [22, 39]. Our study demonstrated that the CSA of SOL and GAS and muscle strength in mice were improved after electrical stimulation. Liu Z et al. demonstrated that in disuse muscle atrophy, the fiber type transitions from type I to type II, while the number of muscle cells does not decrease [40]. Leterme D et al. used a rat hindlimb suspension model to simulate changes in the soleus muscle in a non-weight-bearing state, and their study revealed a decrease in the proportion of slow muscle fibers and an increase in the proportion of fast muscle fibers [41]. Jun Che et al. subjected mice to 2 weeks of hindlimb suspension and revealed that the proportion of MyHC-I muscle fibers was significantly reduced in the suspension group, while the proportion of MyHC-IIb muscle fibers was significantly increased in the suspension group compared with the control group [42]. The proportion of fast muscle fibers in mouse SOL was also analyzed in a study by Mengyuan Chang et al., in which the results demonstrated that the proportion of fast muscle fibers in the suspension group was significantly higher than that in the control group [43]. Our results demonstrated that after suspension, the proportion of type I fibers in SOL decreased, while after electrical stimulation, the recovery of muscle fibers from type II to type I was not restored. This may be attributable to the inability of electrical stimulation to fully affect the relevant pathways intrinsically involved in myofiber transformation, such as gene expression, activation of intracellular signaling pathways, and others. Notably, it may also be related to our stimulation modality, or to the possibility that specific typing changes were not detected. In our study, after suspension, the expression of integrins appeared to differ in gastrocnemius and soleus muscles, with lower integrin β1 expression in the soleus than in the gastrocnemius, which may suggest that integrins may behave differently in different muscle fiber types. Accordingly, whether the decrease in integrins is related to fiber type remains an important question for further investigation.

Correlative studies have demonstrated that muscle atrophy correlates with p70S6K and rpS6 phosphorylation levels and protein content downstream of mTORC1 [44, 45]. Uno H et al. demonstrated that belt electrode-skeletal muscle electrical stimulation enhanced the phosphorylation of p70S6K downstream of mTORC1 by performing banded electrode skeletal muscle stimulation on the GAS and tibialis anterior (TA) muscles of denervated rats [46]. Our results also demonstrate that electrical stimulation can improve muscle atrophy by promoting skeletal muscle protein synthesis. FAK is a downstream factor of integrins that binds to integrins and promotes phosphorylation of many downstream targets [47, 48], which was significantly upregulated in the induction of skeletal muscle hypertrophy [49]—notably, de Boer MD et al. performed 24 days of unilateral calf suspension in young men. During the 21st day of disuse, a significant decrease in the synthesis rate of myogenic fibers and tendon proteins was observed, accompanied by a decrease in FAK phosphorylation [50]. FAK overexpression by transfection of a pCMV-FAK plasmid enhanced the phosphorylation and activity of p70S6K in the tibialis anterior muscle (TA) [51]. FAK is also critically involved in the activation of the TSC2/mTOR/S6K1 pathway [52]. Our results demonstrated a decrease in protein expression of p-FAK after suspension, and an increase after electrical stimulation intervention, suggesting its involvement in both atrophy and the recovery process. However, in GAS and SOL, p-AKT/AKT was higher after suspension, but there was no difference between p-FAK/FAK and p-p70s6k/p70s6k. This may be due to a higher p/total ratio caused by a lower total protein amount in suspension conditions, which may reflect a physiological compensation or stress response, as described in the experiments of Miller et al.; similar phenomena have been reported [20]. Differences that occur in the TS group versus the TS་E group may also be due to the above scenario. Interestingly, in GAS, the reduction of p-AKT/AKT in the TS + E group compared with the TS group may also be because the expression of AKT, a key factor in the activation of multiple signaling pathways, is subject to complex multi-signal regulation. Our study revealed that FAK expression demonstrated inconsistent trends at the gene and protein levels in the suspension group. This difference may be attributable to the inhibition of the mRNA translation process, such as the lack of necessary translation initiation factors, an insufficient number of ribosomes, or other factors, or to the binding of miRNAs to mRNAs and inhibiting their translation level, which ultimately leads to discrepancies between the mRNA and the protein levels. In addition, it may be associated with protein stability as well as feedback regulation mechanisms. Further research can be conducted to examine these mechanisms at a later stage.

Fan-Yen Lee et al. [53] used shock wave stimulation to stimulate rat mesenchymal stem cells and revealed that the level of FAK phosphorylation increased, but FAK phosphorylation decreased after rapamycin inhibited m-TORC1 activity. Indicating that mTORC1 may have a negative regulatory effect on FAK. Consequently, during the process of muscle hypertrophy, whether muscle will also negatively regulate the expression of FAK through some pathways or secrete some cytokines, to ensure the stable growth of skeletal muscle (Fig. 6) remains to be examined. Moreover, although we assume that electrical stimulation stimulates the muscle to generate action potentials, causing muscle contraction, the mechanical signals generated by muscle contraction can activate integrin β1 and subsequently mediate the synthesis of skeletal muscle proteins. However, there is also a possibility that electrical stimulation can directly stimulate the activation of integrins, thereby mediating downstream signals. In future studies, we will further investigate the mechanism in greater depth.

This study has several limitations. First, the 7-week-old C57BL/6 mice we selected were at the stage of growth and development; accordingly, their performance after suspension modeling, in addition to reflecting atrophy due to disuse, may indicate a slowing of developmental growth caused by disuse. Age should be considered in future studies to explore its relationship with disuse muscle atrophy in mice. Second, although positive effects of electrical stimulation were observed in immobilized animals, the absence of a control group receiving electrical stimulation limits the ability to discern whether these improvements are due solely to the intervention or whether they instead reflect a higher baseline muscle status compared with untreated animals. Future studies including a control + electrical stimulation group are necessary to determine the extent to which electrical stimulation contributes to the observed improvements and whether it independently influences muscle phenotype in the absence of immobilization.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study demonstrates for the first time that integrin β1 and its downstream molecule FAK may be involved in the preventive effect of electrical stimulation against disuse muscle atrophy. In future studies, further confirmatory studies should be conducted to explore the precise role of integrins in skeletal muscle protection mechanisms by using knockout mice or modulating integrin expression, or by applying electrical stimulation to directly stimulate muscle cells.
