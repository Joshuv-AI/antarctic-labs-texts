Liver Transplantation as a Metabolic Treatment in Glycogen Storage Disease Type Ia
Authors: Deniz Turkum Atikcan Simsek, Elif Melek Avcı Dursun, Merve Senay Ozcalik, Ozlem Turhan Iyidir, Asli Nar, Neslihan Bascil Tutuncu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlycogen storage disease type Ia (GSD Ia) is a rare autosomal recessive metabolic disorder characterized by glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency, leading to severe fasting hypoglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, hyperuricemia, hepatic adenomas, and osteoporosis. While intensive dietary and pharmacologic therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment, a subset of patients fails to achieve metabolic control and develops life-altering complications. We report the case of a 20-year-old male with genetically confirmed GSD Ia who exhibited persistent hypoglycemia, severe hyperlipidemia, hepatic adenomas, and osteoporosis despite optimal medical management. Due to the progression of hepatic and systemic complications, the patient underwent living donor liver transplantation using his father as the donor. Posttransplant follow-up demonstrated complete resolution of metabolic abnormalities, including hypoglycemia and dyslipidemia, normalization of liver function tests, and significant improvement in bone mineral density and quality of life. This case highlights the role of liver transplantation as a definitive metabolic treatment in selected patients with GSD Ia, offering not only biochemical correction but also prevention of neoplastic transformation and reversal of skeletal complications.

Introduction

Glycogen storage diseases (GSDs) comprise a heterogeneous group of inherited metabolic disorders characterized by impaired glycogen synthesis or degradation, resulting in abnormal glycogen accumulation predominantly in the liver and skeletal muscle [1, 2]. Among these, GSD type Ia (von Gierke disease) arises from a deficiency of glucose-6-phosphatase, an enzyme expressed in the liver, kidney, and intestinal mucosa, crucial for maintaining euglycemia during fasting. Enzymatic deficiency leads to severe metabolic derangements, including hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, and impaired growth. Long-term complications may involve gout, osteopenia or osteoporosis, hepatic adenomas, and short stature [3-5].

Although contemporary dietary interventions—including uncooked cornstarch supplementation and nocturnal gastric glucose infusions—have substantially improved metabolic outcomes in GSD Ia, a subset of patients remains unresponsive to conservative management [2]. For these individuals, liver transplantation provides a curative therapeutic modality, correcting the underlying enzymatic defect and preventing progression to hepatic or systemic complications [1].

This report presents a case of a young adult with genetically confirmed GSD Ia who exhibited persistent metabolic instability despite optimal dietary and pharmacologic therapy and subsequently underwent successful living donor liver transplantation. The case highlights the potential of liver transplantation as a definitive metabolic treatment and is complemented by a concise review of the current literature.

Case Presentation

A 20-year-old male with genetically confirmed GSD Ia had been under regular follow-up at our metabolic disorders clinic since infancy. Despite rigorous dietary management—including uncooked cornstarch (1.75 g/kg/dose every 4 hours orally) and a low-fat, high-complex carbohydrate diet—he continued to experience poor metabolic control during adolescence. He reported frequent episodes of fasting hypoglycemia (1-2 per week), often triggered by intercurrent illness or physical activity. Hypoglycemia episodes were monitored both at home using a glucometer and in the hospital via plasma glucose measurements. At home, the patient's fasting capillary glucose values typically ranged between 70 and 80 mg/dL. During inpatient evaluation, the lowest documented fasting plasma glucose was 55 mg/dL (3.05 mmol/L) (normal reference range, 70-100 mg/dL; 3.9-5.6 mmol/L), which is below the normal fasting range of 70 to 100 mg/dL. All documented hypoglycemic episodes occurred in the fasting state, and there were no reported postprandial hypoglycemic events.

The patient had long-standing severe hypertriglyceridemia despite treatment with gemfibrozil (600 mg twice daily for 4 years prior to liver transplantation). Recent fasting triglyceride levels prior to transplantation were as follows: 1384 mg/dL (76.8 mmol/L) (normal reference range, <150 mg/dL; < .3 mmol/L) in September 2023, 1240 mg/dL (68.8 mmol/L) on a separate occasion also in September 2023, and 932 mg/dL (51.7 mmol/L) in November 2023. After liver transplantation, a marked improvement in triglyceride levels was observed. The highest postoperative triglyceride level was 146 mg/dL (8.1 mmol/L), and the most recent level was 110 mg/dL (6.1 mmol/L), indicating excellent metabolic response. Uric acid levels remained elevated, with a peak value of 6.5 mg/dL (386 µmol/L) (normal reference range, 3.5-7.2 mg/dL; 208-428 µmol/L) while on daily allopurinol (300 mg). Liver enzymes were chronically elevated: alanine transaminase (ALT): 158 U/L (reference range, 0-55 U/L), aspartate transaminase (AST): 161 U/L (reference range, 5-34 U/L), γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT): 168 U/L (reference range, 12-64 U/L), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP): 127 U/L (reference range, 40-150 U/L).

At the time of initial evaluation in July 2023, the patient's height was 156 cm and weight was 80 kg. By January 2025, following transplantation, height was recorded as 150 cm and weight as 60 kg; this discrepancy is likely due to an earlier mismeasurement or rounding error and has been clarified in the revised manuscript. The patient exhibited signs of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. Testicular examination revealed a small left testis (12 mL) and a larger right testis (25 mL), with pubic hair development corresponding to early puberty. Abdominal ultrasound showed hepatomegaly with a liver span of 210 mm and grade 2 to 3 hepatic steatosis, along with mild splenomegaly (spleen measuring 116 mm). No xanthomas were detected on physical examination. Liver function tests revealed elevated transaminases (ALT, AST), reflecting hepatic involvement, while coagulation parameters remained within normal limits (Table 1). Bilirubin levels (both total and direct) were also within the normal range.

The diagnosis of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism was considered and confirmed during the pretransplant period. Our patient was found to have low total testosterone levels (121.96 ng/dL; 4.23 nmol/L; normal reference range, 142-923 ng/dL; 4.9-32.0 nmol/L) with inappropriately normal gonadotropins (follicle-stimulating hormone: 4.11 U/L, luteinizing hormone: 4.27 U/L), consistent with central hypogonadism. This condition was interpreted as secondary to chronic illness and long-standing metabolic imbalance associated with GSD Ia. In response, testosterone replacement therapy was initiated and maintained until the time of liver transplantation [6].

Diagnostic Assessment

Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated hepatomegaly and multiple hepatic adenomas, the largest measuring 3.2 cm. These lesions remained radiologically stable but raised concern for malignant transformation. However, biopsy ultimately confirmed the lesion to be benign. The first dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry revealed severe osteoporosis (Z score: −6.0 at L1-L4), and 2 years ago at age 18 he had sustained a low-trauma femoral fracture requiring surgical fixation. The patient's 25-hydroxyvitamin D level was 27 µg/L (reference range, 30-100 µg/L), indicating insufficiency. Corrected serum calcium was elevated at 11.3 mg/dL (0.62 nmol/L) (normal reference range, 8.6-10.3 mg/dL; 0.47-0.57 nmol/L), while phosphate remained within normal limits at 3.7 mg/dL (0.2 mmol/L) (normal reference range, 2.5-4.5 mg/dL; 0.13-0.24 mmol/L). Albumin was slightly above the upper reference limit at 5.4 g/dL (54 g/L) (normal reference range, 3.5-5.2 g/dL; 35-52 g/L). Parathyroid hormone (PTH) was measured at 22 ng/L (22 pg/mL) (normal reference range, 15-65 ng/L; 15-65 pg/mL), falling within the normal range.

Treatment

Given the combination of refractory metabolic instability, progressive hepatic pathology, and debilitating skeletal complications, liver transplantation was considered. Pretransplant evaluation confirmed preserved renal function and absence of extrahepatic malignancy. Liver transplantation was performed in January 2024 using his father as the donor. The perioperative and postoperative course was uneventful.

Outcome and Follow-up

Posttransplant follow-up demonstrated dramatic clinical and biochemical improvements. Hypoglycemia resolved, cornstarch therapy was discontinued, and triglyceride and uric acid levels normalized without pharmacological intervention. Liver function tests normalized within 6 weeks (see Table 1). Immunosuppression was maintained with tacrolimus monotherapy.

Osteoporosis management included weekly cholecalciferol (20 000 IU), calcium supplementation, and physiotherapy. At 6 months, bone mineral density had improved (Z score at L1-L4: −4.2). The patient resumed university education and reported marked improvement in physical capacity and psychosocial well-being.

Discussion

Liver transplantation has emerged as a transformative therapeutic option in GSD Ia, particularly in patients with refractory metabolic control or hepatic complications despite intensive medical therapy. In the present case, living donor liver transplantation led to resolution of persistent hypoglycemia, severe hyperlipidemia, and hepatic adenomas, highlighting its utility not only as a structural hepatic intervention but also as a definitive metabolic cure.

Post transplantation, our patient achieved complete biochemical stabilization with cessation of cornstarch therapy and normalization of lipid and uric acid levels, consistent with previously published outcomes in similar cohorts [1-5]. Moreover, the absence of adenoma progression or malignant transformation during follow-up supports the emerging consensus regarding liver transplantation's protective role against hepatocellular neoplasia in GSD patients with adenomas [7].

Importantly, our case also illustrates substantial extrahepatic benefits following liver transplantation, including improvement in bone mineral density and physical function. These gains are attributable to the restoration of energy homeostasis, alleviation of dietary restrictions, and overall enhancement of metabolic balance, as also documented by Lee et al and Matern et al [4, 5].

However, liver transplantation does not correct extrahepatic enzyme deficiencies, and thus, renal involvement—a known complication in GSD Ia—remains a critical consideration. Our patient did not exhibit nephropathy prior to liver transplantation, but ongoing surveillance is essential. Long-term follow-up should include renal monitoring and immunosuppressive management, as recommended by expert guidelines [8].

Patient selection and optimal timing for transplantation remain key determinants of outcome. Identifying individuals with refractory metabolic profiles, progressive hepatic pathology, or early signs of systemic complications can allow for timely intervention before irreversible damage occurs. The long-term success of liver transplantation in GSD Ia hinges on a multidisciplinary approach that integrates metabolic, hepatic, and systemic risk assessment.

In conclusion, liver transplantation in this case resulted in a profound clinical turnaround, reaffirming the role of liver transplantation as a definitive treatment strategy in carefully selected patients with GSD Ia. While the risks associated with surgery and lifelong immunosuppression should not be overlooked, the ability to halt hepatic neoplastic risk, normalize metabolic parameters, and improve overall quality of life supports broader consideration of liver transplantation in this population. Future prospective studies and long-term registries are warranted to delineate the full scope of posttransplant outcomes and to optimize timing, eligibility, and monitoring strategies.
