The Association Between Human Papillomavirus and Prostate Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Sarah Botting‐Provost, Kristy Ng, Cassandra Laurie, Rodrigo Noorani, Mariam El‐Zein, Eduardo L. Franco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomavirus (HPV), the necessary cause of cervical cancer, is associated with an increased risk of other anogenital cancers. While HPV has frequently been detected in prostate specimens, its role in prostate carcinogenesis remains controversial. We synthesized and meta‐analyzed the epidemiological literature on the association between HPV and prostate cancer. We completed a systematic search of Embase, MEDLINE, Scopus and Cochrane on January 31, 2025. Two reviewers independently screened articles and extracted data on study design and population, sample characteristics, HPV and prostate cancer ascertainment methods, and results. We pooled risk estimates using a random effects model and performed subgroup meta‐analyses by HPV risk level, genotype, and control group. Our search identified 1874 unique records. Of 84 included articles (1990–2025), 57 were cross‐sectional, 15 case–control, 6 nested case–control, and 6 were cohort studies. HPV detection methods reported were HPV DNA (n = 57); HPV serology (n = 17); subjects' self‐report (n = 5); chart review, registry or claims data (n = 4); RNA (n = 3); and immunohistochemistry (n = 2). Most studies (58.3%) reported HPV prevalence below 20%. Our meta‐analysis (39 studies) produced a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 2.35 (95% CI: 1.87–2.95). When restricted to high‐risk HPV, the pooled OR was 2.75 (95% CI: 1.69–4.46). HPV16, HPV18, and HPV33 had positive associations with prostate cancer (pooled ORs: 2.28, 95% CI: 1.18–4.40; 2.37, 95% CI: 1.16–4.87; 3.73, 95% CI: 0.46–30.30, respectively) and the association was strongest when using normal tissue as controls (pooled OR:4.81, 95% CI: 1.83–12.69). Our findings support a positive association between the detection of oncogenic HPV DNA in prostate specimens and prostate cancer, although its etiologic significance remains unclear.

Introduction

Persistent infection with carcinogenic human papillomavirus (HPV) is a necessary cause of cervical cancer [1, 2] and is associated with an increased risk of other anogenital [3] and head and neck cancers [4]. HPV is the most common sexually transmitted infection globally, with the lifetime cumulative risk of acquiring the infection exceeding 80% [5]. Although most HPV infections clear or become undetectable on their own, persistent infections with high‐risk HPV types, such as HPV16 and HPV18, can, under the right conditions, lead to cellular transformations that may progress to cancer.

Many studies have detected HPV in prostate tissue, but its role in prostate carcinogenesis remains unclear and controversial. Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer and the fourth most lethal cancer among males globally [3]. Despite extensive research and its public health burden, little is known about risk factors for prostate cancer. Age, family history, germline genetic mutations, and ethnicity are established risk factors, but hereditary factors likely account for only about 5% of cases [6]. Screening for prostate cancer via serum‐based prostate specific antigen (PSA) is widely used in clinical practice. However, its effectiveness at detecting prostate cancer at an early stage remains problematic due to the high proportion of false positives that represent benign conditions and the harms associated with subsequent biopsies [7]. Identifying additional risk factors could improve risk stratification and reduce screening‐related harms.

While some sexually transmitted infectious agents have been hypothesized to induce prostate carcinogenesis through inflammatory pathways [8], beginning with chronic prostatitis, HPV is known to initially suppress inflammation in cervical carcinogenesis [9]. A different mechanism, such as modification of the cell cycle by inhibiting tumor suppressor and pro‐apoptotic signaling pathways, may be more likely in this case.

The simple presence of HPV DNA in prostate tissue does not indicate biological plausibility; understanding HPV's carcinogenic mechanisms is essential when investigating its potential role in prostate cancer. A recent review, using Bradford Hill's criteria for causality to evaluate the relationship between HPV and prostate cancer, highlighted the plausibility of the association but noted that the overall quality of the evidence is low [10]. The authors did not provide any summary estimates of the association. Another 2023 review by Tsydenova et al. [11] which restricted to case–control studies using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to detect HPV and included normal prostate tissue as controls, only included 27 studies out of the large volume of literature on the topic.

With this systematic review and meta‐analysis, we aim to provide the most exhaustive summary to date on the association between HPV and prostate cancer, considering all study designs, ascertainment methods, HPV types, and prostate tissue control types. Our subgroup meta‐analyses provide a breakdown of the evidence that considers the potential biological mechanisms, HPV's oncogenic potential, and implications of the HPV detection method used.

Methods

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

The protocol was registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD42023396539). We followed the PRISMA guidelines for reporting [12]. We developed the search strategy (Table S1) for MEDLINE with the help of a librarian from McGill's Schulich Library, which we then translated into equivalent search terms for Embase, SCOPUS, and Cochrane Library using Systematic Review Accelerator [13]. We neither restricted our search by date of publication nor by language to maximize the retrieval of potentially relevant records; however, language eligibility was applied during screening. The initial search was run on March 19, 2022, and updated on January 31, 2025.

Across both searches, a total of four authors (SBP, KN, CL, RN) independently screened titles/abstracts in pairs, and three (SBP, KN, CL) independently screened full texts for inclusion in pairs. Disagreements or discrepancies between assessors at each stage were resolved through discussion and re‐examination of the relevant articles. We included peer‐reviewed empirical studies conducted on humans, published in English, and that compared exposure to HPV between prostate cancer cases and a control group. Records were not eligible and excluded (Figure S1) if they were non‐English, nonhuman studies, not peer‐reviewed (i.e., editorials, conference abstracts and proceedings, book chapters), did not investigate HPV as the exposure and prostate cancer as the putative outcome, or did not provide a measure of HPV in their study population that could be stratified for case–control comparison.

Overall, three authors (SBP, KN, CL) independently conducted full duplicate data extraction and validated each other's work in pairs, with disagreements resolved through discussion. Extracted data included information on the study (country, year, journal), population (age, source), HPV (type, ascertainment method), prostate cancer (stage, ascertainment method), and statistical analyses (statistical test, variables used for adjustment).

Quality Assessment

To assess the quality of studies included in the systematic review, two authors (SBP, CL) adapted the Newcastle‐Ottawa scale [14] to our research question (Data S1), tailoring it to the high prevalence of cross‐sectional studies in our review. Our adaptations prioritized reporting quality and methodological rigor, especially for HPV detection and prostate cancer diagnosis. For example, we introduced a hierarchy of HPV detection methods, as this methodological difference across studies may be an important source of exposure misclassification. The most points (represented by number of stars) were attributed to studies that used methods for detecting HPV in the prostate (i.e., PCR, in situ hybridization [ISH]), fewer points to those using nonspecific or less sensitive methods to detect HPV infection (i.e., serology), and no points for studies relying on uncertain measurements (i.e., self‐report or history of genital warts). Quality assessment was conducted by one author (SBP) and validated by another (CL or KN). As the adapted Newcastle‐Ottawa scale prioritized concrete and objective criteria, any disagreements were resolved by consensus between reviewers.

Data Analysis

Our main meta‐analysis only included studies that explicitly tested for HPV DNA in prostate tissue, since we considered this HPV ascertainment method to hold the highest certainty of exposure to HPV in the prostate. For studies that did not report an odds ratio (OR) or risk ratio (RR), we calculated a crude OR when sufficient data were available. We contacted the corresponding author for one article requesting data for a 2 × 2 table needed to calculate an OR but received no response [15]. For studies that reported crude and adjusted estimates, we used the adjusted estimate in our analysis. We pooled ORs and RRs separately and calculated 95% confidence intervals (CI). For studies that reported a zero count for exposed/unexposed cases or controls, we added 0.5 to each cell to enable estimation of association and allow inclusion of these studies. The random‐effects model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) for unbiased estimates of between‐study variance. Between‐study heterogeneity was assessed with τ 2 and Higgins and Thompson's I 2 statistic. We conducted a sensitivity analysis by repeating the main meta‐analysis excluding studies reporting zero counts for exposed/unexposed cases or controls. We performed leave‐one‐out analyses to assess the effect of individual studies on the main meta‐analysis, using the numerically robust maximum likelihood (ML) estimation method [16] to address REML convergence issues arising from an asymmetric CI reported in an included study [17]. We used Egger's test to assess publication bias.

Using ML to allow for τ 2 estimation convergence, we stratified by control definition: participants with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) only, normal prostate only, or a potential mix of both (where studies either specified that controls were a combination of participants with BPH and normal prostates, without separate reporting, or did not specify the results of prostate biopsies for their controls). Results from ML analyses were validated by repeating the meta‐analysis using REML after excluding the study causing convergence issues.

We conducted additional stratified analyses by HPV type (low‐risk, high‐risk, type‐specific) to assess whether the association varied by HPV type carcinogenicity. The pooled estimate for high‐risk HPVs was based on individual study estimates that included one or more high‐risk types, excluding low‐risk types. We considered 12 HPV types as high‐risk: HPVs 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, and 59 [18]. The reverse was true for the pooled estimate for low‐risk types, including studies that provided estimates for low‐risk types (i.e., HPVs 6, 11) separately from high‐risk types. Only one estimate was included per study.

We conducted all meta‐analyses and sensitivity tests using Stata version 18.

Finally, we performed a meta‐regression using the inverse variance method to exclusively pool the type‐specific serology results using the R metafor package (version 4.2.0, Core Group, Vienna) [19]. Effect estimates were analyzed on the log scale, and pooled separately by effect measure (ORs and RRs).

Based on available data and comparability of HPV ascertainment methods, 55 records were included in quantitative analyses following data extraction. The forest plot in Figure 2 shows the main meta‐analysis results among studies assessing HPV DNA in prostate tissue; pooled estimates from case–control (OR 2.94, CI: 1.22–7.06) and cross‐sectional (OR 2.33, CI: 1.84–2.95) studies suggest a statistically significant positive association between HPV DNA presence in the prostate and prostate cancer. Given the inclusion of few case–control studies and lack of heterogeneity observed between study designs, their combination in subsequent analyses was justified. The overall pooled estimate when both study designs were combined (Table S8) was 2.35 (CI: 1.87–2.95; Figure 2).

Excluding studies that reported at least one zero cell (for which 0.5 was added to the counts in the 2 × 2 tables) did not have a significant impact on the overall estimate from the main meta‐analysis; the sensitivity analysis yielded a pooled OR of 2.27 (CI: 1.80–2.85; Figure S2). Moreover, no single study significantly influenced the pooled effect size when using the random‐effect ML model (Figure S3) or REML model (excluding the asymmetric observation; results not shown) in the leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis. Finally, as shown in the funnel plot (Figure S4), publication bias was unlikely, and no significant small‐study effect was detected (p = 0.23).

The results stratified by control type are shown in Figure 3. The pooled effect estimate remained positive across all control groups, with the strongest association observed in studies that used normal prostate tissue as controls (OR 4.81, CI: 1.83–12.69), followed by mixed or likely mixed tissue as controls (OR 2.72, CI: 1.21–6.10). When analyses were restricted to studies using BPH tissue as controls, the association remained statistically significant, though further attenuated (pooled OR 2.17, CI: 1.73–2.73). Similar results were seen using the random‐effect REML model, excluding the asymmetric observation (results not shown).

Using extracted data for specific high‐risk HPV types, we were able to calculate pooled estimates for HPVs 16, 18, and 33 (Figure S5). Detection of HPV16 and HPV18 showed significant positive associations with prostate cancer cases (pooled ORs 2.28, CI: 1.18–4.40 and 2.37, CI: 1.16–4.87, respectively). While HPV33 DNA detection was also positively associated with prostate cancer cases (OR 3.73, CI: 0.46–30.30), the limited number of participants contributing to the analysis reduced the precision of the estimate. In addition, only one study reported an estimate for HPV31, showing a significant positive association with prostate cancer (OR 22.68, CI: 1.20–428.80); however, the wide CI suggests that results must be interpreted with caution.

Since serological results were HPV type‐specific and many studies assessed multiple HPV types, we provided type‐specific pooled estimates in (Table S9). Case–control and cross‐sectional studies were combined, as both reported ORs, while cohort studies, reporting RRs, were analyzed separately by HPV type. Sufficient data allowed pooling results from cross‐sectional and case–control studies for HPV16, HPV18, HPV31, and HPV33; all four pooled estimates were around the null value. Estimates for other types were based on single studies. Notably, one cohort study reported a significant association between HPV18 seropositivity and prostate cancer (RR 2.71, CI: 1.20–6.12). Consistent with type‐specific findings, the meta‐regression revealed no association between HPV seropositivity and prostate cancer.

Discussion

Our systematic review and meta‐analysis suggest that there is an association between HPV DNA in prostate tissue and prostate cancer, with consistent positive associations observed when comparing cancerous tissue to both normal and BPH prostate tissue as controls. Stratified analyses showed the strongest association for high‐risk HPV types, supporting the biologically plausible hypothesis that oncogenic HPV types may contribute to prostate carcinogenesis. Notably, HPV16 and HPV18, responsible for over 70% of cervical cancers [22], showed the strongest associations with prostate cancer, though possible links were also seen with HPV31 and HPV33. In contrast, HPV type‐specific seropositivity was not significantly associated with prostate cancer. This was expected, as serology reflects humoral immunity triggered by past HPV exposure in any mucosal surfaces that do not necessarily reflect an active infection or presence of HPV DNA in the prostate tissue. However, we cannot rule out that the null finding may be due to sparse data and our inability to pool estimates for certain HPV types.

While previous reviews have explored the potential association between HPV and prostate cancer, their strict inclusion criteria, such as restricting on study design, HPV ascertainment method, and/or HPV detection method [10, 11, 23], have limited the comprehensiveness of their findings and resulted in inconsistent evidence. Our study addresses these limitations by including a broader range of studies with judicious stratification by study design and control type, with an emphasis on examining the relationship by HPV oncogenic potential.

Across the 84 studies included in our review, almost 60% of studies reported an overall HPV prevalence below 20%, and 20% reported prevalence between 20% and 50%. We expected this lower prevalence in men at risk for or diagnosed with prostate cancer, as HPV infection rates decrease with age [24, 25] and prostate cancer is considered an age‐dependent disease, commonly diagnosed in men over age 65 [11].

No individual study in our meta‐analysis reported a statistically significant negative association between HPV and prostate cancer. Whether statistically significant or not, most estimates indicated a positive association. Pooling data from 2 case–control and 37 cross‐sectional studies that used HPV DNA testing in prostate tissue as the HPV ascertainment method, we observed a statistically significant positive association between HPV DNA detected in prostate tissue and prostate cancer. This finding is concordant with the conclusions of most prior meta‐analyses [11, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30].

Some studies have speculated that the control type used may influence observed associations, with several reporting no significant difference in high‐risk HPV prevalence between BPH samples and prostate cancer samples, questioning HPV's causal role [11]. However, other reviews reported that HPV prevalence was elevated in prostate cancer cases relative to BPH and normal tissues. Specifically, a 2020 systematic review reported that high‐risk HPV prevalence was substantially higher in prostate cancer (22.6%) compared to normal or BPH samples (8.6%) [31], while a 2023 meta‐analysis reported significant associations when normal prostate tissue (OR 3.07, 95% CI 1.80–5.21) or BPH tissue (OR 1.94, 95% CI 1.43–2.63) was used as control [11], with no association between HPV and BPH. Similarly, in this study, the positive association between HPV and prostate cancer persisted when restricting controls to normal tissue, mixed or likely mixed tissue, or BPH, though the strength of the association was attenuated in this order. This may reflect the differences in the biological and clinical context of control tissues. BPH is biologically distinct from normal tissue and may exhibit differences in sampling conditions and inflammation [32], which could influence HPV persistence or detectability independent of carcinogenesis. For similar reasons, the heterogeneity of mixed control tissue may dilute contrasts if HPV detectability varies across tissue types. Conversely, normal prostate tissue controls may originate from men undergoing biopsy for suspected cancer, introducing potential selection bias. Together, these factors suggest that control selection may influence the magnitude of observed associations rather than reflecting true causality.

A key strength of our study is inclusion of HPV type‐specific analyses. Few previous meta‐analyses have examined the association between individual types and prostate cancer, and those that did focused exclusively on HPV16 and HPV18. While some found HPV16 to be generally associated with prostate cancer [28, 33, 34, 35], HPV18 was not [35]. However, findings varied based on HPV detection methods. For example, a 2011 review found the association with HPV16 disappeared when DNA and serological detection methods were combined [34]. In our study, stratifying by HPV risk type revealed a significant association between high‐risk HPVs and prostate cancer, but not with low‐risk types. This aligns with a meta‐analysis in Mexican men that found a significantly higher prevalence of high‐risk (77%) compared to low‐risk (23%) HPVs in prostate tissue from patients with prostate cancer, though this was based on results from only three studies and pooled estimates of association were not calculated [26]. Similarly, others found that most HPV infections in individuals with prostate cancer included high‐risk types [28].

To our knowledge, ours is the first meta‐analysis to report pooled and type‐specific associations between multiple high‐risk HPV types and prostate cancer. Our findings of statistically significant positive associations between prostate cancer and HPVs 16 and 18, and possible links with HPVs 31 and 33, are consistent with results from previous studies that identified these same four types as having the highest prevalence in prostate cancer [36].

Early‐Stage Oncogenesis

The biological role of HPV in prostate cancer remains unclear. It is uncertain whether HPV can induce morphological transformations in the sparse glandular epithelial cells of the prostate, which HPV typically targets in other tissues, like the cervix [11]. Some studies report comparable HPV levels in BPH and prostate cancer samples and low viral loads in advanced prostate cancer cases [11], suggesting HPV may influence early prostate carcinogenesis rather than late‐stage disease progression. Evidence supporting this hypothesis includes detection of HPV‐associated koilocytes in some prostate cancer samples, a known marker for early oncogenesis in cervical cancer [37]. Furthermore, E6/E7 HPV oncogene expression was shown to be elevated in benign tissue but to decrease substantially, or was not expressed once the disease progressed to prostate cancer, indicating that HPV‐related oncogenesis may wane in later stages [38]. Together, these findings suggest that, unlike in cervical cancer, HPV could have indirect oncogenic effects on the progression of prostate cancer.

Indirect Mechanisms

Unlike cervical cancer, where incidence increases in immunocompromised women, prostate cancer incidence does not increase in immunocompromised men, suggesting HPV may contribute to prostate cancer development via indirect rather than direct mechanisms observed in cervical carcinogenesis (i.e., HPV persistence) [31]. For example, HPV may suppress host antiviral mechanisms by inhibiting APOBEC enzymes and indirectly influence oncogenesis, as mutations of these proteins have been observed in prostate cancer [31]. Supporting this, Nahand et al. [36] noted that although no statistically significant association was found between HPV DNA presence and prostate cancer, the expression level of anti‐apoptotic mediators and those with a role in angiogenesis and inflammation was significantly higher in the HPV‐infected prostate cancer group compared to the HPV‐negative prostate cancer and control groups. Furthermore, they found that a large proportion of HPV‐positive prostate cancer tumors contained purely integrated HPV DNA in the host genome relative to the episomal form of HPV DNA, a common observation in advanced stages of cervical cancer. Presence of viral genome was also more frequently seen in late stage (III/IV) prostate cancer, with HPVs 16, 18, and 33 being the most commonly integrated types. HPV E6/E7 oncogenes were also significantly higher in HPV‐positive prostate cancer tissues, further supporting the hypothesis that high‐risk HPVs may initiate oncogenic transformation through viral DNA integration. The authors proposed a “hit and run” hypothesis, whereby viral infection itself may be cleared post‐integration, but the retained viral DNA continues to exert oncogenic effects in the later stages of prostate cancer [36, 38]. This proposed mechanism differs from the model of persistent HPV infection required for cervical carcinogenesis. Overall, there is limited evidence on whether HPV is biologically linked to prostate carcinogenesis; further research is needed to clarify what the plausible mechanisms may be.

Limitations

Our results must be interpreted cautiously. While our study included a broader range of studies than previous syntheses, restricting inclusion to English‐language publications may have introduced language bias, potentially inflating pooled estimates, since significant findings may be more likely published in English‐language journals (i.e., international journals) while nonsignificant findings may appear only in non‐English journals [39]. However, only 5/108 records assessed for eligibility (4.6%) were excluded for being non‐English, suggesting limited impact on our results. Another limitation relates to the design of the available evidence. Most studies included were cross‐sectional and cannot establish whether HPV infection preceded onset of prostate cancer or occurred during a plausible etiologic window. The long latency period of cancer (i.e., HPV infection takes ~15–20 years to progress to cervical cancer [40]) often poses challenges when it comes to studying modifiable risk factors, as establishing past exposure would require either retrospective measurement from decades in the past, or prospective studies with a very long follow‐up period. The latter is logistically and financially demanding, and the former becomes especially challenging when relying on biological evidence. For instance, detecting HPV DNA would require biopsy samples taken from healthy prostate tissue many years before the onset of prostate cancer, which is not only ethically questionable but would also face substantial challenges in participant recruitment. It is therefore to be expected that DNA tests were most often performed on the same biopsy specimen used to diagnose prostate cancer, limiting our ability to infer causality from HPV presence. Moreover, while analyses were conducted according to HPV ascertainment method, variability may still exist within methods, including differences in PCR or non‐PCR assays, primer selection, specimen processing, and tissue preservation type, which may affect DNA quality [41]. These factors can influence HPV detection rates across studies and further complicate the interpretation of pooled estimates [28]. Lastly, while we considered detection of HPV DNA in prostate tissue to represent the highest certainty of HPV presence in the prostate, HPV DNA positivity does not necessarily indicate a true active infection. Nevertheless, accounting for ascertainment method in the analysis likely strengthened the robustness and interpretability of the pooled estimates by acknowledging a key source of measurement variability.

Conclusion

Despite the predominance of cross‐sectional studies in our analysis, this comprehensive and rigorous meta‐analysis provides valuable insights into a potential association between high‐risk HPV in prostate carcinogenesis, a link that has not been widely accepted. By considering various levels of evidence, our findings support the plausibility that HPV, particularly high‐risk types such as HPVs 16 and 18, may contribute to prostate cancer development; however, the available evidence remains insufficient to establish a causal relationship. Given that hereditary risk factors of prostate cancer account for only approximately 5% of prostate cancer cases, elucidating the potential HPV‐related biological mechanisms may improve our understanding of disease etiology and help to better identify people at higher risk of developing prostate cancer.
