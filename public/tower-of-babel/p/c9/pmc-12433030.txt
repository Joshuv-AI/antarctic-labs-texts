Effects of nutritional support during neoadjuvant therapy on perioperative outcome in esophageal cancer: a real-world cohort study
Authors: Yongbo Yang, Yan He, Wan Wei, Yaya Wu, Hong Chen, Yimei Gao, Wanpu Yan, Mengying Fan, Shaohua Ma, Zhen Liang, Hongchao Xiong, Liang Dai, Ke-Neng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile postoperative nutritional support for esophageal cancer is well-documented, the impact of nutritional intervention during neoadjuvant therapy remains understudied. This study investigates the role of nutritional support during neoadjuvant therapy in improving perioperative outcomes for esophageal cancer patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 454 esophagectomy patients following neoadjuvant therapy was conducted. Patients were categorized into a nutrition effective group (weight maintenance or gain, n=304) and a control group (weight loss, n=150) according to their weight change after nutritional intervention. Preoperative nutritional status, therapy completion, adverse reactions, postoperative pathological outcomes, and complications were compared between groups.ResultsCompared to the control group, the nutrition effective group demonstrated (I) improved nutritional status during neoadjuvant therapy with higher body mass index (BMI) changes (0.85±0.88 vs. −0.90±0.65 kg/m2, P<0.001) and reduced Nutritional Risk Screening (NRS) scores (0.72±0.89 vs. 0.53±0.82, P=0.03); (II) higher neoadjuvant therapy completion rate (82.9% vs. 74.7%, P=0.04); (III) lower incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse reactions during neoadjuvant therapy (13.8% vs. 21.3%, P=0.04); and (IV) reduced postoperative anastomotic leakage rates (10.2% vs. 17.3%, P=0.03). No significant differences were observed in postoperative hospital stay, grade 3 or higher complications, or pathological response rates [tumor regression grade (TRG) ≤2, major pathological remission (mPR), and pathological complete remission (pCR)].ConclusionsNasogastric tube feeding plays a pivotal role in effective nutritional support during neoadjuvant therapy. Effective nutritional intervention not only improves preoperative nutritional status and enhances neoadjuvant therapy completion but also mitigates grade 3 or higher adverse events and reduces the incidence of anastomotic leakage. Additionally, it may potentially contribute to improved postoperative pathological remission.

What is known and what is new?

• Maintaining or improving preoperative nutritional status is essential for esophageal cancer patients.

• Nasogastric tube feeding is pivotal to delivering effective nutritional support during neoadjuvant therapy for esophageal cancer. Such interventions not only optimize preoperative nutritional status and enhance treatment completion rates but also mitigate the incidence of grade 3+ adverse events and reduce postoperative anastomotic leakage. Notably, robust nutritional support may further contribute to improved postoperative pathological remission rates, underscoring its multifaceted role in perioperative oncology care.

• Thoracic surgeons should prioritize nutritional support therapy during the neoadjuvant treatment phase for esophageal cancer patients to optimize their perioperative nutritional status.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is a prevalent malignancy of the upper gastrointestinal tract, ranking 6th in incidence and 4th in mortality among cancers in China (1). Surgery-based multimodal treatment is the mainstream approach for resectable locally advanced esophageal carcinoma. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy significantly improves long-term survival in patients with locally advanced disease (2-5). Ensuring the completion of neoadjuvant therapy while minimizing treatment-related toxicities is critical to maximizing its efficacy.

Patients with esophageal cancer often experience progressive dysphagia and weight loss due to inadequate nutrition intake. Nearly 80% of patients exhibit weight loss exceeding 10–15% prior to diagnosis (6,7), which negatively impacts nutritional status and increases nutritional risk. Moreover, toxic side effects from prolonged antitumor therapy further impair nutritional intake, compounding the risk. Hence, effective nutritional interventions are vital during the multidisciplinary treatment of esophageal cancer (8-10).

While current guidelines primarily emphasize postoperative enteral nutrition, nutritional support during neoadjuvant therapy has received insufficient attention (8,11). Emerging studies suggest that preoperative nutritional interventions can improve patients’ nutritional status, enhance immune function, and reduce surgical risk (8,12). However, significant debate remains regarding the value of nutritional support during neoadjuvant therapy (13,14).

At the First Department of Thoracic Surgery, Peking University Cancer Hospital, we prioritize comprehensive nutritional support for esophageal cancer patients, particularly during neoadjuvant therapy. This study retrospectively analyzed esophageal cancer patients who underwent surgery after neoadjuvant therapy, comparing clinical outcomes related to nutritional support during therapy and the perioperative period. The aim was to evaluate the impact of nutritional intervention during neoadjuvant therapy on patient outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-355/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective study analyzed patients who underwent surgery for esophageal cancer between April 2017 and August 2023 from our prospective esophageal cancer database. Inclusion criteria included: (I) histologically confirmed esophageal squamous carcinoma or adenocarcinoma of esophagogastric junction; (II) preoperative neoadjuvant chemotherapy with or without immune checkpoint inhibitors; and (III) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status ≤1. Exclusion criteria included: (I) small-cell carcinoma of the esophagus; (II) neoadjuvant therapy involving radiotherapy; (III) patients who did not receive surgical treatment or only had exploratory surgery; (IV) loss to follow-up during the treatment period due to any reasons; and (V) neoadjuvant therapy not performed in the First Department of Thoracic Surgery, Peking University Cancer Hospital. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University Cancer Hospital (No. 2023YJZ02) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Neoadjuvant therapy and surgery

Patients with esophageal cancer staged cT1b–2N+ and cT3–4aNany were treated with the following neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens at the First Department of Thoracic Surgery, Peking University Cancer Hospital: paclitaxel (175 mg/m2)/Nab-paclitaxel (260 mg/m2) combined with cisplatin (75 mg/m2)/carboplatin [area under the curve (AUC) =5] for esophageal squamous carcinoma, and oxaliplatin (130 mg/m2) combined with tegafur, gimeracil, and oteracil potassium capsules (50 mg orally twice daily on days 1–14) for esophagogastric junction adenocarcinoma with or without immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Surgery was performed 4–6 weeks after completing neoadjuvant therapy. Depending on patient-specific factors, either minimally invasive McKeown surgery (transabdominal, right thoracic, and left cervical anastomosis) or Ivor Lewis surgery (transabdominal and right thoracic, with right thoracic anastomosis) was performed. Routine thoracic and abdominal lymph node dissection was conducted, including three-field lymph node dissection for patients with cervical lymph node metastasis. A jejunal nutritional nasogastric tube was routinely placed intraoperatively for postoperative enteral nutritional support.

Nutritional support during neoadjuvant therapy

All patients received individualized nutritional guidance throughout treatment. At the first outpatient visit, nutrition risk was assessed using the Nutritional Risk Screening (NRS)-2002 scale. Nutritionists and specialized nurses provided tailored nutritional management, including one-on-one nutritional guidance and weekly follow-ups (via telephone or outpatient visits) to monitor nutritional support, adverse reactions, dietary intake, and weight changes and dynamically adjust to nutritional plans based on patient progress.

Patients with a pretreatment NRS-2002 score ≥3 received nutritional intervention from diagnosis to surgery. Patients with ≥10% weight loss or significant dysphagia were provided nasogastric tube feeding, while others received oral nutritional support. Daily energy intake targets were >1,500 kcal for tube feeding and 1,000–1,500 kcal for oral feeding, ensuring a total daily energy intake of 30–40 kcal/kg without restricting the normal daily diet. Nasogastric feeding was administrated using either a large-volume syringe with slow, repeated pushes of nutrient fluid or a continuous pump. Those who either had no nasogastric tube inserted or self-dislodged the tube due to any discomfort after insertion were classified into the no nasogastric tube feeding group. Patients were classified based on weight change during neoadjuvant therapy: those maintaining or gaining weight were deemed to have effective nutritional support, while those experiencing continued weight loss were categorized as malnourished and assigned to the control group.

Postoperative nutritional therapy

Postoperative enteral nutrition via a nasogastric tube was commenced within 24 hours postoperatively in the absence of contraindications, administered at 20 mL/h for the initial 48 hours with intravenous fluid and caloric supplementation. In the absence of abdominal distension, diarrhea, or other adverse events, the infusion rate was gradually escalated to 40–60 mL/h by days 3–4 (1,000–1,500 kcal/day, tapering intravenous support), followed by a subsequent increment to 60–80 mL/h by days 5–6 (2,000–2,500 kcal/day, with intravenous energy support systematically withdrawn).

After hospital discharge, patients continued enteral nutrition using a home nutrition pump, with a progressive adjustment to increase the daytime pumping rate while decreasing the nighttime rate to ensure adequate rest. Nutrition specialist nurses conducted weekly telephone follow-ups to monitor adverse reactions and provide guidance on nutritional intake (Figure 1).

Evaluation indicators

Evaluation indicators included changes in nutritional status [weight, body mass index (BMI), and NRS score] during neoadjuvant therapy, the completion rate of neoadjuvant therapy, and the incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse reactions as per Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) criteria. Adverse reactions assessed included myelosuppression, gastrointestinal side effects (e.g., bleeding, nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension, diarrhea, etc.), respiratory side effects (lung infection, dyspnea, etc.), cardiovascular side effects (shock, heart failure, myocarditis, etc.), and hepatic and biliary side effects (elevated liver enzymes, bilirubin, etc.). Additional indicators included postoperative hospitalization duration, Clavien-Dindo grade 3 or higher complications, and tumor regression metrics such as National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) tumor regression grade (TRG), major pathological remission (mPR), and pathologic complete remission (pCR).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 27.0. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed and ordinal data were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-squared test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were used for analyzing the risk factors for the predictor of effective nutritional support. Statistical significance was set at P≤0.05.

Results

General information

Between April 2017 and August 2023, 1,166 esophagectomies for esophageal malignancies were performed at the Department of Thoracic Surgery I, Peking University Cancer Hospital. Based on the inclusion criteria, 454 patients were selected for analysis, with 304 in the nutrition effective group and 150 in the control group (Figure 2). The nutrition effective group had a lower proportion of N0 cases before neoadjuvant therapy (8.9% vs. 15.3%), lower prevalence of hypertension (26.6% vs. 36.7%), and fewer cases of diabetes (9.9% vs. 18.7%). The nasogastric tube feeding rate was higher in the nutrition effective group (83.2% vs. 33.3%) (Table 1).

Nutritional status

In the nutrition effective group, the median BMI increased from 23.08 kg/m2 (IQR, 21.08–25.17 kg/m2) before neoadjuvant therapy to 23.93 kg/m2 (IQR, 21.53–25.62 kg/m2). In contrast, the control group experienced a decrease in median BMI from 24.45 (IQR, 21.23–26.64) to 23.55 (IQR, 21.88–27.04) kg/m2. The mean BMI change was significantly different between the groups, with an increase of 0.85±0.88 kg/m2 in the nutrition effective group and a decrease of −0.90±0.65 kg/m2 in the control group (P<0.001). Comparison of neoadjuvant treatment complications

During neoadjuvant therapy, the nutrition effective group demonstrated a higher rate of completing the treatment regimen on schedule compared to the control group (82.9% vs. 74.7%, P=0.04). Additionally, the incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse reactions was significantly lower in the nutrition effective group (13.8% vs. 21.3%, P=0.04). However, there were no significant differences between the two groups in the incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse reactions related to myelosuppression, gastrointestinal reactions, respiratory complications, cardiovascular issues, and hepatobiliary disorders (Table 3).

Postoperative complications, hospital stay, and tumor regression

The overall rate of postoperative grade 3 or higher complications did not differ significantly between the nutrition effective and control groups (31.6% vs. 34.7%, P=0.51). However, the incidence of postoperative anastomotic leakage was significantly lower in the nutritional effective group (10.2% vs. 17.3%, P=0.03). While the nutrition effective group showed higher proportions of mPR (31.9% vs. 23.3%, P=0.058), pCR (17.1% vs. 14.0%, P=0.40), and TRG ≤2 (60.5% vs. 56.7%, P=0.43), these differences were not statistically significant. Additionally, there was no significant difference in postoperative hospital stay between the two groups (Table 4).

Risk factor for the efficacy of nutritional support

Univariate analysis showed that age, hypertension, diabetes, N stage and nasogastric tube feeding were statistically significant effective factors in nutritional support. Multivariate analysis found age [odds ratio (OR) 0.244, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.109–0.510; P<0.001] and nasogastric tube feeding (OR 12.392, 95% CI: 7.488–21.097; P<0.001) to be the independent predictive factor for effective nutritional support (Table 5).

Discussion

This single-center retrospective analysis demonstrated that nasogastric tube feeding plays a pivotal role in effective nutritional support during neoadjuvant therapy and effective nutritional intervention during neoadjuvant therapy improved preoperative nutritional status, increased the completion rate of neoadjuvant therapy, reduced grade 3 or higher adverse events, and effectively decreased postoperative anastomotic leakage in patients with esophageal cancer. These findings highlight the critical role of nutritional support in the multidisciplinary treatment of esophageal cancer.

The persistence of weight loss in the control group despite nutritional support in this study of esophageal cancer patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy is associated with the following distinct determinants. First, reliance on oral nutritional supplements without nasogastric feeding tube placement was observed in a subset of patients, a strategy that may have compromised caloric delivery efficiency due to treatment-related gastrointestinal impairments, including post-chemotherapy dysphagia or reduced intestinal absorptive capacity. Second, early discontinuation of enteral access occurred in individuals experiencing procedural complications or discomfort with nasogastric tubes, such as pharyngeal irritation, tube dislocation, or local mucosal injury, directly disrupting the continuity of enteral nutrition delivery. Third, symptom-driven termination of nutritional support was documented in patients with gastrointestinal toxicities, including abdominal distension and diarrhea, conditions significantly correlated with reduced nutrient tolerance and treatment adherence. Finally, incomplete outpatient nutritional compliance was identified with suboptimal comprehension of medical instructions by patients or caregivers, underscoring the critical role of health literacy and structured nutritional counseling in ensuring treatment continuity.

Maintaining or improving preoperative nutritional status is essential for patients with esophageal cancer. Previous studies, such as Davies et al. (15) showed that preoperative weight loss ≥10% significantly predicts 1-year mortality, while Yoon et al. (16) found excessive muscle loss as an important prognostic factor for OS and RFS. Consistently, our study observed an increase in BMI and a reduction in NRS-2002 scores in the nutritional effective group, indicating improved nutritional status. These results align with the findings of Kita et al. (17) and Matsumoto et al. (18), further supporting the benefits of nutritional intervention in preventing weight and BMI loss, as reported by Qiu et al. (12).

Adequate neoadjuvant therapy is crucial in the multidisciplinary treatment of esophageal cancer, with proper nutrition being essential to enhance patient tolerance and ensure effective systemic or local therapy (19,20). In our study, the completion rate of neoadjuvant therapy was significantly higher in the nutrition effective group compared to the control group (83.8% vs. 72.5%, P=0.01), aligning with findings by Bower et al. (21) (71% vs. 53%). Additionally, the incidence of TRG ≤2 was higher in the nutrition effective group (61.6% vs. 51.0%, P=0.054). Although no significant differences were observed in mPR and pCR between the groups, it is notable that the nutrition intervention group had more advanced preoperative T-staging and poorer baseline nutritional status, which may have underestimated the true impact of nutritional intervention on mPR and pCR outcomes.

With evolving treatment concepts, the role of neoadjuvant therapy in the multidisciplinary management of esophageal cancer has gained prominence (2-5). Neoadjuvant therapy contributes to down-staging tumors, eliminating micro metastases, reducing intraoperative metastasis risks, and increasing the R0 resection rate. However, it often induces adverse reactions such as myelosuppression, vomiting, diarrhea, and pneumonia. Weight loss and malnutrition not only decrease the patients’ quality of life, but more importantly, reduce their tolerance to anti-tumor treatment, leading to a worse prognosis (22). Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that the nutrition effective group experienced a significantly lower incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse reactions during neoadjuvant therapy compared to the control group. This reduction in severe adverse events likely contributed to the higher completion rate of neoadjuvant therapy in the nutrition effective group.

Anastomotic leakage is a serious and potentially fatal complication following esophagectomy, leading to prolonged hospitalization, delayed oral feeding, and increased risk of mediastinal or thoracic infections that can result in hemorrhage. This complication severely impacts patients’ quality of life and survival. The preoperative nutritional status of esophageal cancer patients plays a crucial role in the occurrence of postoperative anastomotic leakage. Poor nutritional status increases the risk, with several underlying mechanisms: (I) edema in the local tissues around the anastomosis, which increases anastomotic tension and may lead to tearing and leakage due to mechanical factors; (II) insufficient raw materials for anastomosis healing, weakening local tissues and delaying the healing process; and (III) diminished immune function, which raises the risk of postoperative wound and anastomotic infections. Given these factors, it is essential for thoracic surgeons to assess the nutritional status of esophageal cancer patients before surgery. Patients identified as malnourished should receive nutritional support preoperatively to improve their status and reduce the risk of postoperative anastomotic leakage (6,23,24).

A retrospective study by Davies et al. (15) found no significant differences between the early nutritional support group (nutritional support during neoadjuvant therapy) and the late nutritional support group (postoperative nutritional support) regarding Clavien-Dindo complication classification ≥ 3b, length of hospitalization, or 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year mortality. Similarly, in our study, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of postoperative hospitalization duration and the occurrence of overall grade 3 or higher Clavien-Dindo complications. While there was no significant difference in postoperative pathological remission after neoadjuvant therapy between the nutrition effective group and the control group, such as in pCR, mPR, and TRG ≤2, a trend toward higher rates of pathological remission was observed. This suggests that improving nutritional status during neoadjuvant therapy for esophageal cancer may enhance the rate of postoperative pathological remission.

There are some limitations in this study. Being a retrospective analysis based on a single-center prospective database, it is subjected to potential selective bias. Consequently, further large-scale randomized controlled trials are needed for validation. Currently, Cao et al. (25) are conducting a multicenter, unblinded randomized controlled trial to further assess whether nutritional therapy during neoadjuvant therapy can reduce perioperative complications and improve perioperative outcomes, with the trial in clinical enrollment status.

Conclusions

Nasogastric tube feeding plays a pivotal role in effective nutritional support during neoadjuvant therapy. Effective nutritional intervention not only improves preoperative nutritional status and enhances neoadjuvant therapy completion but also mitigates grade 3 or higher adverse events and reduces the incidence of anastomotic leakage. Additionally, it may potentially contribute to improved postoperative pathological remission. Therefore, thoracic surgeons should prioritize nutritional support therapy during the preoperative neoadjuvant treatment phase for esophageal cancer patients to optimize their perioperative nutritional status.
