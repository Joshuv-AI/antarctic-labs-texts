Piloting measures of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion among reproductive-aged contraceptive users in Kisumu, Kenya: a cross-sectional, population-based study
Authors: Brooke W. Bullington, Stephanie Chung, Dickens Otieno Onyango, Leigh Senderowicz, Emilia Goland, Claire W. Rothschild, Abigael Mwanyiro, Ben Wekesa, Brian Frizzelle, Ginger Golub, Katherine Tumlinson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLittle research has focused on measuring free contraceptive choice, or whether individuals can make decisions about contraception without barriers or coercion. To advance measurement, we pilot survey questions focused on experiences of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion using population-based data collected among contraceptive users in Kisumu, Kenya.MethodsWe describe the percentage of women who reported provider-imposed coercion-related outcomes. We then examine demographic and facility-level correlates of feeling pressured or unable to say no to contraception and compare the extent that measures of feeling pressured and unable to say no to contraception overlap using 2 × 2 tables and Cohen’s Kappa Statistic.ResultsThe percentage of participants who reported provider-imposed coercion ranged by question from 1% to 57%. There were no associations between demographic or facility characteristics, including age, marital status, parity, and facility type (public or private), and either reporting feeling pressured or reporting feeling unable to say no to using contraception. There was no agreement between measures of feeling pressured and unable to say no to contraception (Kappa statistic − 0.11).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that many women in Kisumu report experiencing some form of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion, though the proportion who report such experiences varies based on survey question. Further research is needed to improve reliable measurement of free contraceptive choice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40834-025-00394-z.

Introduction

In recent years, family planning programs have increasingly highlighted the importance of autonomy during contraceptive counseling. The Kenyan National Family Planning Guidelines for service delivery, for example, underline the importance of “never pressuring a client to choose one family planning method over another, or otherwise limiting clients’ choices for any reason other than medical eligibility” and include “volunteerism and informed choice” as one of the guiding principles for the family planning program [1]. These guidelines therefore establish autonomous decision-making as a critical element of high-quality contraceptive service delivery.

Family planning scholars have increasingly focused their efforts on measuring autonomy in contraceptive decision-making [2–5]. Researchers have proposed a flurry of new indicators that intend to assess autonomous contraceptive decision-making, providing potential outcome measures for programs that aim to promote person-centered service delivery. For example, Dehlendorf’s person-centered contraceptive counseling measure captures patient perceptions of quality of contraceptive care [6], Holt’s preference-aligned fertility management indicator measures whether an individual’s stated contraceptive desires are met [7], and Senderowicz’s contraceptive autonomy indicator quantifies whether one has the factors that need to be in place in order to make one’s own decision about contraceptive use and realize that decision [2]. These measures, in addition to others [3, 8–11], fill an important gap in the family planning literature that has traditionally evaluated success based on measures of contraceptive use that fail to take women’s individual reproductive preferences and desires into account.

New measures also have the potential to identify and elevate concerns around contraceptive coercion. For example, Senderowicz’s proposed indicator breaks contraceptive autonomy down into three subdomains: informed choice, full choice, and free choice. Informed choice captures whether a person has enough information about a variety of family planning options, full choice is whether a person has access to a range of methods, while free choice focuses on whether an individual can make a decision about contraception, free from coercion. Until recently, the vast majority of family planning literature on quality of care has primarily centered informed choice and full choice. Measures attempting to track informed choice for example, like the Method Information Index, and full choice, like measures of method availability, have been integrated into population-based surveys around the world, like the Demographic and Health Surveys.

While a growing body of qualitative research has documented experiences of coercion in women’s interactions with health service providers during contraceptive counseling [11–16], considerably less work has attempted to quantify the prevalence of such experiences at the facility or population levels [10, 17]. This is likely, in part, because of difficulties of capturing such nuanced experiences on a quantitative survey. In their first attempt to measure the contraceptive autonomy indicator, Senderowicz and colleagues discussed some of the pitfalls of their measure of free choice [17]. They note that because coercion falls along a spectrum and experiences can be subjective, it may be difficult to capture more subtle forms of coercion [11]. Further, experiences of provider pressure can be normalized to a point where they are not considered a problem, which may lead women not to report such experiences on questionnaires [18]. Additionally, social desirability bias and post-hoc rationalization may come into play, leading people to underreport experiences of violations to free choice. Senderowicz et al. therefore call for further refinement of measures of free choice, and particularly highlight the need for new indicators that focus specifically on experiences of coercion during interactions with providers.

Accurately capturing free contraceptive choice is imperative to understanding whether one of the key guiding principles of the Kenyan National Family Planning guidelines – voluntariness – is adhered to. Additionally, research shows that people without reproductive autonomy face adverse health outcomes, including depression [19–21] and delivery of low-birth-weight infants [22], and that negative contraceptive counseling experiences increase medical mistrust and hinder contraceptive use [23, 24]. This study builds off previous work by Senderowicz and colleagues [2, 17] by attempting to refine measurement of free choice. We do this by piloting a variety of questions focused on provider-imposed contraceptive coercion in a sample of reproductive-aged contraceptive users in Kisumu, Kenya. By asking a range of questions focused on different aspects of provider-imposed coercion, we seek to understand whether reports of coercion may vary depending on which aspect of coercion is queried and question wording. We also examine predictors of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion and compare different measures to further refine and improve measurement of free contraceptive choice.

Methods

This is a secondary analysis of a study designed to assess an intervention to increase contraceptive access in Kisumu, Kenya. The study included multiple facility-level instruments as well as an individual-level questionnaire with reproductive-aged women. Because we aim to examine women’s perceptions of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion, here we present data from the women’s questionnaire using pre-intervention data.

Sampling

We drew a random sample of households in Kisumu county, Kenya. To do this we first used the results from the 2014 Kenyan DHS to estimate individual and household response rates and the proportion of houses likely to be occupied. Because the interventions for this study are facility-based, we created enumeration areas that correspond to the likely catchment area (sometimes referred to as the facility’s “community units”) of each public-sector health facility using 137 Thiessen polygons, which served as mutually exclusive and exhaustive enumeration areas.

To identify all structures in each polygon, we used publicly available data described in Sirko 2021 [25]. Sirko and colleagues applied deep learning feature extraction methods to more than 100,000 high-resolution satellite images across Africa to delineate over 1.75 million buildings for the entire continent. For our purposes, we limited our use of these data to building structures located in Kisumu County, where our study takes place.

To take a random sample of structures in each polygon, we gave each building a unique identifier and selected a random sample of 50 structures in each polygon. In the three most urban catchment polygons, we took a random sample of 250 structures given the high percentage of commercial buildings. Enumerators approached structures in the order of the random sample in which they were drawn until they had enrolled 18 women of reproductive age per polygon; If more than one woman of reproductive age lived in the household, one woman was randomly sampled.

Data collection

All enumerators were female and attended a week-long training and participated in three days of pilot testing, in which they practiced identifying the randomly selected structures and completing the women’s questionnaire. Because the women’s questionnaire asked about sensitive information, including current and past family planning use, experiences of quality of care at facilities, and contraceptive decision-making, enumerators were instructed to find a private area to complete the survey. Enumerators verbally asked participants questions in English, Swahili, or Luo and recorded answers on encrypted and password protected tablets. Data collection took place between May and August 2022.

Study population

For the purposes of this analysis, we limit our sample to only women who reported current contraceptive use, as only these participants were asked all the questions about provider-imposed contraceptive coercion. We exclude n = 848 participants who did not report current contraceptive use. We also exclude participants who reported obtaining or receiving information about their current method outside of health service facilities (n = 73), as our goal is to assess provider-imposed coercion, which necessitates interaction with a healthcare provider.

Measures

We developed measures of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion based on existing literature. Most questions were taken directly from Senderowicz’s contraceptive autonomy indicator [2]. Questions from the contraceptive autonomy indicator underwent a rigorous development process in Burkina Faso, including literature review, incorporating formative qualitative research, seeking input from reproductive health experts, and cognitive interviews for novel survey items [17]. We also used an additional question on autonomy from a person-centered scale developed by Sudhinaraset et al. (2018) and validated in the Kenyan context [26]. These measures of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion rely on patient perceptions of their interactions with providers during contraceptive counseling; we therefore rely on and trust participant’s interpretations of their experiences [27]. We refined these measures with our team of local investigators. All questions were piloted over a three-day pilot testing period and revised to improve clarity and comprehension.

Measures of provider-imposed coercion were all binary (yes/no). We opted for binary response options as opposed to a Likert scale given that prior research has found reliability is similar between the two, yet a binary response option allows for faster response time and therefore reduces the burden of the survey on the participant [28]. We show a summary of these measures in Supplemental Table 1. All participants who reported current contraceptive use were asked to answer each of the following questions about their interaction with providers when they obtained their family planning method: “Did you feel like the doctors, nurses, or other staff at the facility involved you in decisions about your family planning choice?”, “When you obtained [current method] did you feel like you had enough information to make a decision that’s good for you?”, “When you obtained [current method] did you feel pressure from the provider to use a method of contraception in general?”, “When you obtained [current method] did you feel pressure from the provider to adopt this specific method?”, and “When you obtained [current method], did you feel like you could say no to using this method?”

Participants were also asked the following questions about their lifetime experiences with coercion: “Has a provider ever coerced you to use a specific contraceptive method?”, “Has a provider ever insisted on giving you a contraceptive method that you didn’t want?”, “Has a provider ever showed disapproval when you decided not to use a particular contraceptive method?”, “Has a provider ever threatened to withhold other health services if you did not accept a particular contraceptive method?”

Analysis

We first describe the sociodemographic characteristics of our sample, including age, marital status, and parity, as well as information on the current method participants reported using, where they obtained or received information about this method, and time since they most recently obtained a method. We then describe the number and percentage of participants who reported each measure of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion, including measures focused on the time when the participant most recently obtained their current method of contraception and measures focused on lifetime experiences of provider-imposed coercion.

To assess potential recall bias, we descriptively examined outcomes of provider-imposed coercion by the time since women most recently reported obtaining family planning (less than a year versus one year or longer). We further stratified by method type (long-acting methods, including the implant, intrauterine device, or sterilization versus other methods) given that long-acting method users were more likely to receive their method more than one year ago.

We then conducted exploratory analyses focused on two measures of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion that were common among our respondents: whether a participant felt pressured to use contraception and whether they felt able to say no to using contraception. We chose these measures because we do not yet understand how to measure provider-imposed coercion and therefore we do not know how sensitive responses are to wording. We also created two binary composite measures of these two outcomes. The first classified a participant as experiencing provider-imposed coercion if they either reported feeling pressured to use contraception or reported feeling unable to say no to using contraception. The second classified a participant as experiencing provider-imposed coercion if they both reported feeling pressure and unable to say no to using contraception. We used logistic regression to examine correlates of all four of these outcomes separately. We examined age (< 25, 25–34, > 34), marital status (married or living with partner, unmarried and not living with partner), parity (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5+), and facility type (public, private) as correlates and report adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We ran stratified logistic regression models to examine whether correlates of outcomes differed among women who reported using long-acting methods and other who reported using other methods.

Finally, we compare the measure of whether a participant felt pressured to use contraception in general and whether they were able to say no to using their method using a 2 × 2 table. We examine percent agreement and disagreement and report Cohen’s Kappa statistic [29]. We use Cohen’s suggested interpretation of the Kappa Statistic, with values ≤0 indicating no agreement, 0.01–0.20 indicating no to slight agreement, 0.21–0.40 indicating fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 indicating moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 indicating substantial agreement, and 0.81-1.00 indicating almost perfect agreement.

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

We included 1,228 reproductive-aged contraceptive users in this analysis, which was 57% of the full study sample and 94% of contraceptive users in the study sample. Most women (80%) were married or living with their partner (Table 1). About 26% of women were younger than 25 years old, 44% were between 25 and 34 years old, and 30% were 35 or older. Approximately 4% of women had not given birth, 16% had birthed one child, 20% had birthed two children, 19% had birthed three children, 15% had birthed four children, and 27% had birthed five or more children. The most commonly used contraceptive methods in our sample were the implant (42%) and the injectable (38%), followed by the pill (7%) and the external condom (7%). Most women in our sample (76%) reported obtaining or receiving information about their method from a public facility. Just under three quarters of participants reported obtaining family planning within the last year.

The proportion of participants who reported provider-imposed contraceptive coercion varied substantially depending on the survey question (Table 2). Looking first at measures of provider-imposed coercion that occurred when most recently obtaining a method, 15% of women reported that they did not feel involved in the decision about family planning by providers. 19% reported that they did not feel they had enough information to make a good decision about family planning. 14% of women reported they felt pressured by a provider to adopt family planning in general, and 12% reported they felt pressured by a provider to adopt a specific contraceptive method. More than half (57%) of women said they felt unable to say no to using family planning. Regarding measures of lifetime prevalence of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion, some women reported that a provider had ever coerced them to use a specific contraceptive method (2%), insisted they use family planning when it was not wanted (2%), showed disapproval when they decided not to use family planning (1%), or threatened to withhold services if a method was not accepted (1%).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional, population-based study of contraceptive users in Kisumu, Kenya, we piloted a variety of questions about provider-imposed contraceptive coercion. We found that a substantial proportion of participants (ranging from 12% to 57%) reported instances of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion when they obtained their family planning method. Considerably fewer participants (1–2%) reported ever experiencing specific instances of lifetime provider-imposed coercion, such as a provider ever coercing them to use a specific method or threatening to withhold services if they did not accept a method. Age, marital status, parity, and facility type were not associated with reports of provider pressure to adopt contraception nor feeling unable to say no to using contraception. Though measures of provider pressure and ability to say no to using contraception were designed to capture the same underlying construct of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion, they showed little agreement, indicating a need for further refinement of the measurement approach. Our findings represent an early attempt to capture the important underlying construct of free contraceptive choice; future work addressing the many challenges to measuring free choice is needed to ensure measurement validity.

Our study builds off an existing evidence base that finds that women in low- and middle-income countries experience violations to free choice in contraceptive decision-making. Studies in sub-Saharan Africa have found that women are sometimes encouraged, pressured, or even forced by providers to use contraception [10, 11, 30]. Additionally, a wide evidence base shows that women face barriers to removal of long-acting reversible contraception, including unaffordable removal fees and provider refusal to remove methods prior to their expiration [12, 15, 31, 32]. This body of literature demonstrates the importance of accurately measuring violations of autonomy. While Senderowicz and colleagues’ first attempt to measure contraceptive autonomy indicators found that fewer than 5% of contraceptive users had experienced violations to free choice, our study found a considerably higher proportion of participants who reported that they were pressured to adopt contraception (14%) or unable to say no to using contraception (57%). This may be due to the different settings of the two studies (Burkina Faso versus Kenya) or to differences in measurement approach. For example, our study built off Senderowicz’s work by specifying that experiences of nonautonomy occurred during the health service interaction. Still, additional measurement refinement is needed, including qualitative research and cognitive interviewing in this setting and others, to ensure measures accurately capture underlying constructs.

Age, marital status, parity, and facility type have previously been associated with upward or downward provider bias [10, 33–39]. We did not find evidence of associations between any patient-level sociodemographic factors and reports of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion in our study, though obtaining a method from a public facility was associated with feeling unable to say no to using contraception among long-acting method users. We posit that provider-imposed contraceptive coercion may be less influenced by characteristics of individual women or provider beliefs about who “should” be using contraception and perhaps instead are driven by the idea that family planning is universally good for all women and that larger public health system goals supersede a woman’s individual contraceptive desires. For example, providers may believe they know what’s best for an individual woman, even if this counters what the woman wants or needs. Future qualitative work that digs into provider motivations in their promotion of contraception is imperative to further understand this phenomenon and pinpoint potential interventions to eliminate coercion in contraceptive counseling.

Our findings also indicate that measures of reported provider-imposed contraceptive coercion that are attempting to capture the same underlying construct are interpreted differently and therefore yield drastically different results. For example, our measure of pressure to adopt contraception in general and inability to say no to contraception had no agreement, with more than half of participants reporting they couldn’t say no to contraception but not feeling pressured to use contraception. These results highlight the need for further refinement of questions related to coercion and free choice in contraceptive decision-making. Future qualitative work and cognitive interviewing is needed to better understand and adapt existing measures, as well as develop new measures that accurately capture coercion. In development of these measures, researchers must consider that these questions may perform differently in different settings, and thus language tweaks may be needed to capture the same underlying construct in a range of settings.

This study has several limitations. First, while we attempted to improve upon previous measurement of free choice and provider-imposed coercion, our approach is subject to several biases previously identified in the literature, including recall bias, social desirability bias, difficulty capturing experiences of coercion that are normalized, and issues with measuring more subtle experiences of coercion [11, 17]. Additionally, while we conducted pilot testing with our measures of provider-imposed coercion, most are novel indicators. A significant strength of this study is the range of novel indicators measured; by asking about a variety of provider behaviors, we can begin to identify which survey questions could better capture more subtle vs. more overt experiences of coercion, or aspects of coercion that could have been normalized for our participants in their contexts. Future work refining and validating measures of coercion, including conducting cognitive interviews in a variety of settings, is an imperative next step. Our survey question about whether a participant felt they could say no to using contraception could be interpreted in a variety of ways; cognitive interviews are needed to capture whether this question is well understood. Further, for the purposes of this study, we limited our sample to only current contraceptive users, as this was the group that all questions about provider-imposed coercion were asked. However, contraceptive non-users can also experience contraceptive coercion and future work should account for this. Finally, we found different results when we asked participants about experiences with provider-imposed contraceptive coercion the last time they obtained their method compared to any time in their lives. This may be because we asked different questions about provider-imposed coercion to capture coercion when the participant obtained their method and lifetime experiences of coercion. It may also be that questions on lifetime experience are subject to further recall bias. Future research that captures provider-induced coercion questions for both current method and lifetime prevalence estimates would allow for comparison.

In conclusion, in our pilot test of new measures of provider-imposed contraceptive coercion, we find that a considerable number of contraceptive users report experiences of nonautonomy when obtaining their contraceptive method. Given that we pilot novel measures of provider-imposed coercion, future work to understand how these questions are interpreted and whether they capture the underlying constructs they intend is vital to improving our understanding of such experiences. As such, the present research represents an early attempt in part of a longer journey to more accurately capture free contraceptive choice. All people should be able to make informed, full, and free choices about their contraceptive use and realize their reproductive goals. To intervene on and prevent instances of contraceptive coercion, it is imperative that we further develop and refine indicators to accurately capture contraceptive coercion at the population-level.
