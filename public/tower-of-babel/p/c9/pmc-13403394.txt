Molecular marker of memory formation reveals complex mechanisms of developmental learning
Authors: Rebecca M Butler, Konstance D Delis, Kruthika V Maheshwar, Karelys D Montañez, Selin Yalcindag, Sarah E London
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe intersection of age- and experience-dependent processes influence learning throughout development, and developmental learning can have long-term effects on behavior. Phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 phosphorylation (pS6) is required in active ribosomes, and new protein synthesis is a conserved mechanism that supports long term memory formation. As such, pS6 fluctuations in brain regions processing experience can provide insight into shifts in the ability for learning and memory. Juvenile male and female zebra finch songbirds (Taeniopygia guttata) perform sensory song learning in ways that affect their adult behaviors. As adults, both sexes perform song recognition learning. Both juvenile and adult types of sensory learning invoke the auditory forebrain. Prior reports established a pS6 song response in the auditory forebrain in Posthatch day 30 juvenile males but not females, and not in younger birds. This was intriguing because the experience-dependent pS6 increase tracked with the onset of the critical period for juvenile sensory song learning in males, and behavioral data indicated that females also effectively learn at P30, though they may not have a critical period. Further, by adulthood (> Posthatch day 90), both male and female auditory forebrains showed an equivalent increase in pS6 after hearing song in patterns consistent with effective recognition learning. Here, to further test the relationships between a crucial molecular marker of active learning processes and the developmental trajectory of juvenile sensory song learning and the emergence of adult-like song recognition learning, we assessed the effect of age and sex, as well as the absence of tutor experience, a manipulation that extends the critical period for developmental learning in males, on the phosphorylation of S6 within the auditory forebrain. Outcomes highlight the complexity of molecular mechanisms across developmental learning and reveal questions to be addressed by further inquiry.Keywords: Songbird, Critical period, Sex differences, pS6, Translation, mTOR, Learning and memory, Sensitive period

Introduction

Learning is a way that individuals can acquire patterns of behavior appropriate for their environment. When these behavioral patterns are learned during development, they can persist throughout the lifespan. Thus, developmental learning can be an essential factor to understand adult behaviors. The intersection of age- and experience-dependent processes influence learning throughout development. Molecular mechanisms of learning and memory can be used to determine when there is sufficient neural organization to support learning. For example, for long-term stable memory formation to occur, experience must initiate new protein synthesis [1–3]. One molecular indication of new protein synthesis is the phosphorylation of S6 (pS6; [4–7]. pS6 has been used previously as a molecular indicator of learning and memory, and is directly implicated in these processes [8–16].

We can therefore leverage pS6 to investigate how age, sex, and prior experience influence developmental learning. Zebra finch songbirds (Taeniopygia guttata) are a strong model system in which to interrogate these intersections. Adult males sing but adult females do not. However, both male and female zebra finches form memories of song during development. In juvenile males, early memories of song are important for the male to develop and sing a stereotypical zebra finch song. Between post-hatch day 30 (P30) and P65, juvenile males interact with an adult tutor male to form an auditory memory of the tutor’s song, and juvenile males use this memory to create their own song [17–25]. Male sensory song learning is restricted to a critical period; juvenile males isolated from song between P30 and P65 have the ability to learn song beyond P65 [19–21]. In juvenile females, the early memories of song formed during a similar overlapping period of development, P25 – P70, inform adult mate choice and mate recognition, though have not been rigorously tested for a critical period [18, 26–31].

Existing pS6 data from juvenile and adult zebra finch auditory forebrains left a disconnect between pS6 measures and effective learning in juvenile females, and raised questions of the developmental trajectory of sex differences in experience-dependent increases in pS6 [12, 13]. When pS6 levels were assessed after song playbacks in juvenile males and females, there was a significant increase in the auditory forebrains of P30 males, as they entered the critical period for sensory song learning, but not one week prior [13, 22]. However, despite behavioral evidence that P30 females successfully perform sensory song learning in ways that affect their adult song preferences, song exposure did not increase the density of pS6 + cells [13, 18, 26, 28–31]. The next stage at which pS6 was evaluated was adulthood, when males and females perform song recognition learning [12, 32]. Indeed, pS6+ cell densities revealed indistinguishable signatures of recognition learning in the auditory forebrains of both sexes [12].

What can explain these data? One possibility is that the sex-specific adult behaviors may arise from differential utilization of molecular and electrophysiological mechanisms of sensory song learning during development. This is interesting because, in contrast to brain regions that direct motor output of song, there are no gross morphological sex differences in the auditory forebrain, a brain region required for song learning in juvenile males and implicated in song learning in juvenile females [13, 33–38]. In fact, even though the organized and interconnected neural circuitry required to support song acquisition and the behavioral expression of song preference appears equivalent in males and females, hearing song does not always affect molecular and electrophysiological signatures of learning within the auditory forebrain in the same way across sex or across development [13, 35–37, 39]. However, by adulthood, hearing song invokes indistinguishable electrophysiological and molecular signatures of learning in males and females.

Here, we assessed the extent to which the abundance of pS6 + cells in juvenile males and females fill in some of the gaps in understanding the trajectory of sensory song learning, and the types of learning that occur, across relevant ages of developmental learning. We used immunohistochemistry to measure the pS6 cell density within the auditory forebrain of juvenile male and female zebra finches (Fig. 1a, b). We assayed normally-reared birds at ages P30, P45, P60, and P67 to cover the critical period defined by male sensory song learning. To test the role of accumulated tutor song experience, independent of age, on the ability to form song memories at the end of the critical period, we also included two additional groups of P67 males: Tutored and Isolate. We used two song playback paradigms, one acute that invokes rapid S6 phosphorylation in ways that indicate the initiation of learning and memory processes, and one protracted paradigm that permits evaluation of recognition learning [8–13, 16], 40– [14]. Our results confirm that at least one molecular mechanism of song learning within the auditory forebrain of males may be different from that of females during this period of development. More comprehensive studies exploring other molecular markers of learning in males compared to females at these ages would need to be done to elucidate processes that support juvenile sensory song learning in both sexes.

Methods

Subjects

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the NIH guidelines for the care and use of animals for experimentation and were approved by the University of Chicago Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUP no. 72220).

Rearing

All zebra finches were housed on a 14 h:10 h light: dark cycle, with seed and water provided ad libitum. The juveniles used in this study hatched in flight aviaries, amongst males and females of all ages. The “Normal” juvenile males and females, 24 per sex (n = 3 per experimental group), were reared in a breeding aviary and were tutored under normal social conditions (i.e., by their dad or other adult males in the breeding colony) until the start of song playback paradigms (below) and collection at P30, P45, P60, and P67 (Fig. 1). The “Tutor” and “Isolate” experimental males, n = 6 per condition, were removed from the aviaries and placed in a sound attenuated chambers at P23 with one to two other juveniles and two foster females (Fig. 1). At P30, all of the males were moved. The “Tutor” males were placed in a new chamber with one tutor male and one adult female and the “Isolate” males were placed in a chamber with two new adult females. This procedure not only establishes behavioral differences but also neuroanatomical and epigenetic differences in the auditory forebrain [19–21, 44, 45]. All birds remained in their respective rearing conditions until the night prior to collection.

Song playback paradigms

All experimental birds were either exposed to a song playback or left in silence. For the overnight acute playback paradigm, birds were moved into individual acoustic chambers 3 h before lights off to facilitate the song playback paradigm the morning of collection, on their target ages. 2–3 h after lights-on, birds assigned to the “Song” group experienced playbacks of a novel conspecific song, “zf101,” one bout repeated every 10 s for a total of 30 min, followed immediately by transcardial perfusion and brain dissection. Age-, sex-, and rearing condition-matched controls were placed in chambers but heard no song playback the following morning before transcardial perfusion and brain dissection 2–3 h after lights-on (“Silence” condition). This acute playback paradigm is established and has previously revealed age and sex differences in pS6+ cell density [12, 13, 46]. There were three pairs of Song and Silence condition birds for all age, sex, and rearing condition combinations (e.g. P30 male Song and P30 male Silence groups).

For the 3-day song recognition playback paradigm, birds of both sexes (n = 36 total, n = 3 for each age/sex/song playback condition group) were placed into individual acoustic chambers 3 h before lights off on Day 1, when they were either P58 or P65. On Day 2, 3 h after lights-on, all birds were exposed to a playback featuring one of two recorded songs, “zf101” or “R6.” For either song, one bout was repeated every 10 s for a total of 3 h. This paradigm is sufficient for habituation learning to occur in adults, as reflected in pS6+ cell densities and other molecular markers of learning and memory, and is termed “training” [12, 42, 47]. On Day 3, 24 h after the initiation of Day 2 song playbacks, birds were exposed to the same song heard on Day 2 (Trained-Familiar), or exposed to the other song (Trained-Novel), for 30 min. zf101 and R6 playbacks were counterbalanced across conditions. Immediately after the 30-min playback on Day 3, or within 1 h in the case of the Trained-Silence group, which heard no playbacks on Day 3, brains were collected after transcardial perfusion for immunohistochemical analysis. Playbacks were played at 70dB, measured with a sound pressure meter (model 33-2055, RadioShack) to simulate the natural amplitude of zebra finch vocalizations. Both zf101 and R6 song recordings were provided by Dr. Susan Volman’s laboratory at the Ohio State University and were thus initially novel to all subjects.

Immunohistochemistry

Immediately after hearing a song playback or being left in silence, the birds were anesthetized with Isoflurane and were transcardially perfused with ice-cold 0.1 M PBS, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.025 M PBS. Brains were dissected and post-fixed overnight at 4 °C. They were then embedded in gelatin (8% in 0.1 M PBS) and fixed overnight at 4 °C. Gelatin embedded brains were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in 0.1 M PBS. Brains were sectioned into 50 μm sagittal sections on a Thermo NX50 Cryostat, collected in a series of three into a solution of 0.1 M PBS and 0.01% Sodium Azide to protect from fungal growth until processing.

We performed immunohistochemistry with all sections from the right hemisphere from a single series from midline to ~ 990 μm lateral for each bird to capture the extent of auditory forebrain. Sections were initially washed three times in 0.01 M PBS for 10 min each to clear the Sodium Azide from the tissue. After permeabilization with 0.3% Triton-X in 0.1 M PBS for 30 min, endogenous peroxidases were exhausted with 2% H2O2 in 0.1 M PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST) for 15 min. After extensive washes in PBST, sections were blocked with 3% normal serum for 60 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by PBST washes and a 60 min room-temperature incubation with biotinylated secondary antibodies. After washing with PBST, sections were incubated in avidin-biotin complex (Vectastain Elite ABC Kit; Vector Laboratories) for 30 min–1 h at room temperature. The peroxidase complex was visualized with 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) containing 0.003% H2O2 in 0.1 M PBS. Sections were then mounted, dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped with Permount (Fisher Scientific).

Antibodies and serums were previously validated in our system and are as follows [12, 13]. pS6: rabbit anti-pS6 primary antibody (1:500 in 1% NGS-PBST; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 2211, RRID: AB_331679) blocked in normal goat serum (NGS), with a biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary (1:500; Vector Laboratories); S6: goat anti-S6 primary antibody (1:2000 in 1% NHS-PBST; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, E-13) blocked in normal goat serum (NGS), with a goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:500; Vector Laboratories) or mouse anti-S6 primary antibody (1:250 in 1% NHS-PBST; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 2317, RRID: AB_2238583) blocked in normal horse serum (NHS), with a horse anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody. Before use here, specificity of each antibody was again confirmed with Western blot analysis.

Image acquisition and processing

To assess the density of phosphorylated S6-positive (pS6+) and total S6-positive (S6+) immuno-stained cells, we captured images using a Zeiss Axiovert 200 m microscope with a Zeiss Axiocam digital color CCD camera (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Thornwood, NY, USA) running Slidebook 5.5 software (Intelligent Imaging Innovations, Denver, CO, USA). We obtained images that contain auditory forebrain, which is comprised of three main sub-regions; caudomedial nidopallium (NCM) and caudomedial mesopallium (CM) and primary auditory forebrain (Field L) plus the adjacent non-auditory hippocampus (HP) with a 2.5X or 5X objective (Figs. 1 and 2). The HP does not display immediate early gene induction or S6 phosphorylation after song playbacks [13, 48] but see [49]), and thus can be useful to control for inter-section variation in immunostaining intensity. Neuroanatomical landmarks to identify each of these brain regions are visible; specific boundaries used to consistently quantify auditory forebrain, NCM, CM, and HP across brain sections and individuals were informed by the Histological Atlas on the Zebra Finch Expression Brain Atlas (ZEBrA, Oregon Health and Science University, Portland, OR, USA: zebrafinchatlas.org) [13, 46, 48, 50–53].

In FIJI, we first applied a threshold to exclude background staining, then acquired particle count data (i.e., positively-stained cells) for each region [54, 55]. Positively-stained cell counts were divided by the region of interest area to calculate a cell density measure. To assess the pS6+ cell density, we used a normalized measure of phosphorylated S6 (pS6+/S6+ cell density). First, the density of pS6+ cells within the auditory forebrain area of interest was normalized by the density of pS6+ cells in the HP of the same section. The same process was performed for the S6 staining. The average, normalized pS6+ cell densities for each bird were then divided by the same measure for total S6+ cell densities to account for overall cell density differences (as in [13]).

Statistics

All birds were included in the analysis. We first performed either 3-way ANOVAs (for data from acute playback and song recognition paradigms in Normal birds) or a 2-way ANOVA (for the P67 male rearing conditions (Normal, Tutored, Isolate) data) to check that pS6+ and S6+ cell densities in the HP did not vary by age, sex, or song playback, permitting this normalization for technical staining variation.

For the acute playback experiment, we also used a t-tests to directly compare the normalized density of pS6-labeled cells in the NCM and CM of birds in the Song condition to their age-, sex-, and rearing condition-matched birds from the Silence condition. For both playback experiments, normalized NCM and CM (acute playback), or auditory forebrain (song recognition paradigm) cell densities were also analyzed with either 2- or 3-way ANOVAs (SPSS; α = 0.05) to test for main effects of age, acute song experience, rearing condition, and sex, and their interactions. Three-way ANOVAs were performed on data from normally-reared birds (acute playbacks and song recognition paradigm) and two-way ANOVAs were performed on data from P67 male Normal, Isolate, and Tutored groups.

Results

The HP S6 + and pS6 + cell densities showed no differences across age, sex, rearing condition, or song experience

We first confirmed that neither S6+ nor pS6+ cell densities in the HP changed across the main factors – age, sex, playback experience, and rearing condition – with ANOVAs. For the normally-reared birds who experienced acute song playbacks, a 3-way ANOVA returned no significant main effects of age (F(3,32) = 2.88, p = 0.06), sex (F(1,32) = 0.82, p = 0.37), or acute song experience (F(1,32) = 0.60, p = 0.44) for S6-labeled cells or for pS6-labeled cells (age: F(3,32) = 0.53, p = 0.67; sex: F(1,32) = 2.56, p = 0.12; song condition: F(1,32) = 0.19, p = 0.67). A 2-way ANOVA conducted on the cell densities in the HP of the P67 male normally-reared, Tutored, and Isolate groups returned no significant main effects of rearing condition (S6+: F(1,11) = 1.18, p = 0.31; pS6+: F(1,11) = 1.00, p = 0.35) or acute song experience (S6+: F(1,11) = 0.72, p = 0.42; pS6+: F(1,11) = 0.51, p = 0.53). Additionally, a 3-way ANOVA on HP cell densities measured in the song recognition experiment returned no significant effects of age (F(1, 24) = 2.41, p = 0.13), sex (F(1, 24) = 4.04, p = 0.06), or song condition (F(2, 24) = 3.22, p = 0.06) for S6 + cell density or pS6 + measures (age: F(1, 24) = 1.09, p = 0.31; sex: F(1, 24) = 1.09, p = 0.31; song condition: F(2, 24) = 0.08, p = 0.92).

No differences in the pS6+/S6+ ratio of cell densities of birds in the Silence condition across age and sex, or male rearing condition

Prior reports showed that baseline pS6+ cell densities could differ early in juvenile development [13]. To assess that possibility here, we performed a two-way ANOVA on the normalized pS6+ cell densities in NCM and CM of Normal juvenile males and females in the Silence condition (Fig. 3). There was no significant main effect of age (NCM: F(3,22) = 1.09, p = 0.38; CM: F(3,22) = 0.49, p = 0.69), sex (NCM: F(1,22) = 0.10, p = 0.76; CM: F(1,22) = 2.06, p = 0.17), or the interaction between the two (NCM: F(3,22) = 2.03, p = 0.14; CM: F(3,22) = 0.55, p = 0.65) in either region. To examine if there is an effect of rearing condition on the normalized pS6+ cell densities across the P67 male groups, we conducted a one-way ANOVA on the Normal, Tutored, and Isolate Silence data. There was not a significant effect of rearing condition in either NCM (F(2,6) = 2.28, p = 0.18) or CM (F(2,6) = 1.63, p = 0.27).

In the NCM of Normally reared birds, sex and age influence the effect of hearing acute song playbacks

To investigate the potential for a song-induced increase in the pS6+ cell density in the NCM within sex and age, we ran t-tests comparing the pS6+/S6+ cell densities in the NCM of birds in the Song condition to those in the Silence condition in normally-reared males and females at P30, P45, P60 and P67 (Fig. 3). In males, the NCM pS6+/S6+ cell densities in the Song condition are significantly greater than those in the Silence condition at P30, but not at any other age (P30 Male: p = 0.03, P45 Male: p = 0.07, P60 Male: p = 0.12, P67 Male: p = 0.08). Because of the visible trends in the data, we ran power analysis which indicated that 1–2 more samples may have been sufficient to reach α < 0.05 in normally-reared males (Fig. 3). In females, the pS6+/S6+ cell densities are significantly greater in the Song compared to Silence condition at P67, but not at any other age (P30 Female: p = 0.45, P45 Female: p = 0.43, P60 Female: p = 0.53, P67 Female: p = 0.03).

To test the collective effects of age, sex and acute song exposure on the pS6+/S6+ cell density in the NCM of the normally-reared birds, we then conducted a 3-way ANOVA. We found significant main effects of age (F(3,35) = 6.94, p < 0.01), sex (F(1,35) = 9.17, p < 0.01) and acute song experience (F(3,35) = 10.27, p < 0.01). Post-hoc Tukey’s HSD analysis of the age data showed that the P30 NCM was significantly different from all other ages (P30-P45: p < 0.01, P30-P60: p = 0.01, P30-P67: p < 0.01), that males were significantly different than females (p < 0.01), and that the cell densities in the Song condition were significantly greater than in the Silence condition (p < 0.01). Two- and three-way interactions revealed a significant interaction between age and sex (F(3,35) = 6.48, p < 0.01), and between sex and acute song experience (F(1, 35) = 9.01, p = 0.01), but not between age and acute song experience (F(3,35) = 2.23, p = 0.10). Post-hoc Tukey’s HSD analysis for the age by sex interaction showed that the NCM of P30 males were significantly different than the NCM of females at all ages (P30 Male-P30 Female: p < 0.01, P30 Male-P45 Female: p < 0.01, P30 Male-P60 Female: p < 0.01, P30 Male-P67 Female: p < 0.01) and compared to the NCM of males at all other ages (P30 Male-P45 Male: p < 0.01, P30 Male-P60 Male: p < 0.01, P30 Male-P67 Male: p < 0.01). Post-hoc Tukey’s HSD analysis for the sex by acute song exposure interaction revealed that the pS6+/S6+ cell densities of males in the Song condition were significantly different than those of male birds left in silence, and of female birds in both the Song and Silence conditions (Male Song-Male Sil: p < 0.01, Male Song-Female Song: p < 0.01, Male Song-Female Sil: p < 0.01). The three-way interaction between age, sex and rearing condition was not significant (F(3,35) = 0.89, p = 0.45).

In the CM of normally reared birds, hearing acute song playbacks increases pS6+/S6+ cell densities in males across age, but only in older juvenile females

To investigate the potential for a song response in the pS6+/S6+ cell density in the CM within sex and across age, we ran t-tests to directly compare measures from birds in the Song and Silence conditions in Normal males and females at P30, P45, P60 and P67 (Fig. 3). In males, the pS6+/S6+ cell densities in the Song condition were significantly greater than those in the Silence condition at all four ages (P30 Male: p = 0.04, P45 Male: p = 0.01, P60 Male: p = 0.05, P67 Male: p = 0.05). In females, the same comparisons revealed significantly greater pS6+/S6+ cell densities in the Song versus the Silence condition at P67, but not at any other age (P30 Female: p = 0.92, P45 Female: p = 0.88, P60 Female: p = 0.48, P67 Female: p = 0.03).

A three-way ANOVA was used to test for combined effects of age, sex and acute song exposure on the pS6+/S6+ cell density in the CM of the normally-reared birds (Fig. 3). We found a significant main effect of acute song exposure (F(1,35) = 10.77, p < 0.01), but not of age (F(3,35) = 2.78, p = 0.06) or sex (F(1,35) = 1.81, p = 0.19). Post-hoc Tukey’s HSD analysis for acute song experience showed that the CM of birds in the Song condition were significantly different than the CM of birds in the Silence condition (p < 0.01). We found a significant interaction between age and sex (F(3,35) = 3.17, p = 0.04), and between sex and acute song experience (F(1, 35) = 11.36, p < 0.01), but not between age and acute song experience (F(3,35) = 0.80, p = 0.50) or in the 3-way interaction between age, sex and rearing condition (F(3,35) = 0.49, p = 0.69). Post-hoc Tukey’s HSD analysis for the age by sex interaction showed that the CM of P30 males were significantly different than the CM of P30 females, P67 females and P67 males (P30 Male-P30 Female: p = 0.05, P30 Male-P67 Female: p = 0.02, P30 Male-P67 Male: p = 0.02). Post-hoc Tukey’s HSD analysis of the sex by acute song exposure comparison showed that the pS6+/S6+ cell densities in Song males were significantly different than those males in the Silence condition and those of females in both the Song and Silence conditions (Male Song-Male Sil: p < 0.01, Male Song-Female Song: p = 0.01, Male Song-Female Sil: p = 0.01).

In P67 male NCM, a significant increase in pS6+/S6+ cell density after acute song playback experience depends on rearing condition

To investigate a potential for a song response in the pS6+/S6+ cell density in the NCM across P67 Normal, P67 Tutored, and P67 Isolate males, we ran t-tests (Fig. 4). This analysis showed greater pS6+/S6+ cell densities in the Song condition compared to the Silence condition in Tutored and Isolate, but not Normal males (P67 Normal: p = 0.08, P67 Tutored: p = 0.02, P67 Isolate: p = 0.01).

In P67 male CM, the significant increase in pS6+/S6+ cell density after acute song playback experience was not significantly altered by rearing condition

To investigate a potential song response in the pS6+ cell density in the CM within P67 males across rearing condition, we ran t-tests. The pS6+/S6+ cell densities in the Song condition are significantly greater than those in the Silence condition in all three rearing conditions (P67 Normal: p = 0.05, P67 Tutored: p = 0.01, P67 Isolate: p < 0.01; Fig. 4).

Data do not support the hypothesis that recognition learning is occurring at P60 or P67

It was not yet tested if adult-like song recognition learning occurred in juveniles, too, and if recognition learning could overlap with the juvenile-type learning that affected adult behaviors (song structure in males, song preference in females) [12, 41–43, 56, 57]. If so, that may complicate some of the findings from the acute paradigm. We thus also used the 3-day playback paradigm to check for the molecular signature of recognition learning in P60 and P67 males and females, comparing normalized pS6+ cell densities in the Trained-Silence, Trained-Novel, and Trained-Familiar conditions (Fig. 5).

Discussion

These experiments were motivated to more completely understand juvenile sensory song learning mechanisms by considering the experience-dependent pS6 profiles in male and female auditory forebrain, and to test the possibility that two types of learning overlap in ways that would complicate interpretation of the acute experiments. Because males possess a well-established critical period for learning, and because evidence of mechanistic sex differences within the auditory forebrain despite no overt sex difference in volume indicate the possibility of two successful processes for effective long-term learning, these results inform on broader questions of how developmental learning is regulated.

Our results from male auditory forebrains reinforced the robust increase in pS6+/S6+ cell density upon acute song playback exposure at P30 [13]. Despite a focus on NCM as a primary site of song processing and learning, our data indicate that CM is also molecularly responsive to hearing song throughout the critical period; males continue to perform sensory song learning across the critical period, including overwriting memories of early tutors with subsequent tutors [20, 23, 39, 58, 59]. There was a more robust effect of song experience on NCM pS6+/S6+ cell density in the younger ages, though with trends towards statistically significant effects towards the end of the critical period. It is thus possible that molecular responsivity in NCM is especially important to enable learning at the onset of the critical period, while CM pS6 supports learning throughout [22]. Notably, NCM and CM have distinct functions but all auditory forebrain subregions are highly interconnected, indicating a cohesive functional network [50]. Indeed, manipulation of mTOR signaling, which regulates the phosphorylation of S6, within either NCM or CM during tutor experiences prevents high fidelity song copying [13].

The absence of tutor experience during the critical period extends the learning phase, and auditory forebrains from Isolate males show distinct epigenetic landscapes, functional connectivity measures, and song metrics compared to auditory forebrains from age-matched Tutored males [20, 21, 44, 45]. We took advantage of the possibility to investigate the relationship of song-induced S6 phosphorylation in age-matched males who differed in learning ability by comparing three different age-matched male groups: those normally-reared in the aviary, those tutored under controlled conditions (Tutored) and those that were not allowed to experience a tutor during the critical period (Isolate). Generally, we detected an increase in pS6+/S6+ cell densities in NCM and CM after song playback in all rearing conditions. There was no main effect of rearing condition. We tested the possibility that recognition learning may begin as early as P67, and would confound the interpretation of these data if only juvenile sensory song learning was considered. However, the emergence of song recognition learning does not explain these results, as our data reveal no indication of habituation-based recognition learning. Interestingly, isolation from tutor song before the onset of the critical period enhances the pS6 response after hearing song playbacks compared to normally-reared males, but we see no indication of that effect here [13]. It is curious that pS6+/S6+ cell densities do not obviously distinguish male auditory forebrains reared under conditions that change several other metrics; it is clear that more comprehensive mechanistic investigation will be needed to disambiguate the capacity for males to learn at the end of the critical period.

We also confirmed the reported sex difference in pS6+/S6+ cell densities after acute song experience at P30 [13]. Interestingly, we did not detect a song response in female auditory forebrain until P67, in contrast to males who responded robustly starting at P30. This suggests that females may rely on delayed or distinct molecular mechanisms during early song exposure because behavioral data indicate that song experience at the ages tested shapes later song preferences [18, 28–30, 60–62]. There are no data directly addressing the existence of a female critical period, but pS6, its role for protein synthesis, and the relationship with memory formation is not exclusive to critical period learning. In fact, the simplified playback stimulus used here to measure acute responses to song is likely even more effective for learning in juvenile females than in males [30, 60, 63]. Because we did not detect the pS6 signature of song recognition learning in females even at P67, it is possible that (a) females use alternative learning mechanisms, or (b) that the auditory forebrain is not a locus of sensory song learning – although all evidence is to the contrary (e.g [16, 34–37]). Thus, understanding the biological mechanisms for female juvenile song learning requires additional investigation.

It is possible that increased sample sizes would result in additional significant differences; ad hoc power analysis did support strong trends in some cases. Other technical alterations might also shift these results, but, our normalized density measures were within the range of prior reports, the stimuli paradigms and molecular methods were previously established, self-vocalizations do not induce molecular and genomic activation in the auditory forebrain, and despite some level of regulated and lateralized responsiveness, the right auditory forebrain responds to song exposure and our use of a consistent hemisphere for analysis precludes direct hemispheric confounds [64–66]. Learning is a complex process, and while pS6 is a powerful indicator of mechanisms that support learning and memory, our findings highlight that pS6 is a valuable but incomplete marker of mechanisms that support learning and memory. By revealing sex-specific and experience-dependent conditions, our work provides insight for future studies to identify complementary molecular indicators that drive developmental learning. In particular, the discrepancy between song-induced S6 phosphorylation and behavioral demonstrations of learning in juvenile females is intriguing. Future studies that take advantage of this uniquely powerful system of juvenile learning that has long-term behavioral consequences will be needed to more completely explain the underlying neurobiology.
