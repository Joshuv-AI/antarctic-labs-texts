Development and Validation of a Provider-Specific Anticipated Stigma Scale for People Who Inject Drugs
Authors: Roman Ivasiy, Valerie A Earnshaw, Jimi Huh, Charles M Cleland, Samuel R Friedman, John A Schneider, Danielle Ompad, Ricky N Bluthenthal, Suzan M Walters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStigma in healthcare settings is a critical barrier to HIV prevention and treatment among people who inject drugs (PWID). While previous tools have measured anticipated stigma, few account for the intersectional and provider-specific experiences of PWID—particularly from syringe service programs (SSPs). We developed and validated the Substance Use Anticipated Provider Stigma Scale (SU-APSS), a multidimensional instrument assessing anticipated stigma from four provider types: healthcare workers, substance use treatment staff, pharmacists, and SSP personnel. Data were drawn from a cross-sectional survey of 264 PWID who were 18 or older, HIV-negative, had injected drugs and used opioids within the past 30 days, and showed visible signs of recent injection. We conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) on responses from 218 participants to evaluate structural validity and used Cronbach’s alpha to assess internal consistency. The CFA supported a four-factor structure with strong model fit indices (CFI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.09 [0.07, 0.11], SRMR = 0.04). All items significantly loaded onto their respective factors (loadings: 0.62–1.06). Internal consistency was high across all subscales (α = 0.85–0.96) and for the overall scale (α = 0.87). Attribution analysis revealed drug use, physical appearance, and income level as the most common perceived reasons for anticipated stigma. The SU-APSS offers a practical tool for identifying provider-specific stigma, informing stigma-reduction interventions, and evaluating implementation strategies to improve HIV prevention and care engagement among PWID.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10461-026-05071-z.Keywords: Stigma, Anticipated stigma, Intersectional stigma, People who inject drugs (PWID), Healthcare discrimination, Stigma measurement, Scale validation

Introduction

Since 2014, there have been more than a dozen Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) clusters and outbreaks among people who inject drugs (PWID) across the U.S. attributed to injection drug use [1–16]. Many outbreaks occurred in urban areas with high syringe service program (SSP) coverage and where medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD) was widely available [1, 17, 18]. These trends are particularly pronounced among intersectional subgroups, including women and sexual minority men, as well as among other marginalized populations in underserved regions [19, 20]. Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a highly effective biomedical HIV prevention method [21], yet its uptake among PWID remains disproportionately low, despite many PWID meeting Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) eligibility criteria for the treatment [22–24]. One of the primary barriers to PrEP uptake among PWID is stigma, which involves the social devaluation and discrediting of individuals [25]. This stigma is not limited to drug use; PWID often face multiple intersecting forms of stigma related to race, ethnicity, sexual identity, gender, poverty, and others, which further compound their vulnerability to HIV and hinder their access to prevention services [26, 27]. Stigma experienced in healthcare settings from healthcare professionals, in particular, may contribute to the underutilization of PrEP among vulnerable populations [28–30]. Therefore, improved measurement of social stigma is critical for advancing HIV prevention efforts among PWID, especially as recent research highlights the role of structural stigma embedded in healthcare systems, policies, and criminal legal responses that shape experiences at the point of care [31–35].

The Stigma Framework distinguishes enacted (past experiences of discrimination), internalized (self-directed stigma) mechanisms, and anticipated stigma, each functioning independently yet contributing to health-related outcomes [25]. Anticipated stigma refers to an individual's expectation of experiencing stereotyping, prejudice, or discrimination in the future due to a stigmatized attribute—in this case, injection drug use [25, 36]. Among PWID, anticipated stigma is shaped both by prior personal encounters with discrimination and by observing or being aware of broader societal stigma. In the context of healthcare, PWID anticipate negative treatment from providers, including being disbelieved, treated with suspicion, or dehumanized [37, 38]. Research applying the Stigma Framework among PWID demonstrates that enacted and anticipated stigma are associated with poorer clinical outcomes, including suboptimal medication adherence, lower CD4 counts among people with HIV and a higher burden of chronic illness comorbidity [39, 40]. Anticipated mistreatment by healthcare providers, such as dismissal or dehumanization, further contributes to delayed care seeking, nondisclosure of drug use, and symptom minimization [41].

Despite growing recognition of stigma's negative impact on PWID, there is a notable gap in the availability of comprehensive instruments to measure intersectional stigma among PWID [26, 42]. According to the most recent systematic review of scales measuring substance use stigma [43], six tools assess internalized stigma, three include a focus on anticipated or fear of enacted stigma [37, 38, 44], and only two capture stigma from diverse sources such as family, friends, and healthcare providers [37, 38]. However, none of these instruments include subscales specifically addressing anticipated stigma from syringe service program staff, despite documented stigma in these settings [45–48]. This is a notable gap, as SSPs could serve as a critical linkage point to other healthcare services for PWID [49–51], making it essential to understand how stigma may affect their engagement. Regarding intersectional anticipated stigma, most tools target specific socio-demographic intersections using tailored measures [52, 53]. An exception is the Intersectional Discrimination Index scale, which allows participants to write in their own attributions, though it does not capture the specific source of the anticipated stigma [54].

PWID often face intersecting forms of stigma [27, 55–57], which are not adequately captured by currently available measurement tools. This study aims to develop and validate a comprehensive instrument—the Substance Use Anticipated Provider Stigma Scale (SU-APSS)—for assessing intersectional stigma experienced by PWID, with a particular emphasis on anticipated stigma within healthcare environments. It builds on existing tools informed by the Stigma Framework measuring anticipated stigma among PWID by incorporating an often-overlooked source of stigma: syringe service programs. Building on this gap, the SU-APSS introduces a provider-specific, theoretically grounded approach to measuring anticipated stigma that distinguishes between healthcare, treatment, pharmacy, and syringe service program contexts while allowing for intersectional attribution. Given their central role in HIV prevention and their potential to serve as gateways to other health services [58], incorporating SSPs as a distinct stigma source is critical for understanding and addressing barriers to care. Intersectional stigma attributed to drug use, race/ethnicity, sexual identity, gender, and poverty is measured in this study using adapted items from the Substance Use Stigma Scale and the Everyday Discrimination Scale [38, 59]. Since anticipated stigma is closely linked to both healthcare avoidance and the severity of substance use disorders [40, 60, 61], capturing anticipated intersectional stigma among PWID can inform interventions that specifically leverage peer support to normalize use, and restructure healthcare environments, like SSPs, to offer low-barrier, nonjudgmental prevention (i.e. PrEP) care access [55, 62–65].

Methods

Study Design

This study employs a cross-sectional design to develop, by revising an existing scale, a measure of anticipated intersectional stigma among PWID in healthcare and SSP settings and evaluate the structural validity and reliability of the scale. The instrument builds upon the Substance Use Stigma Mechanisms Scale (SU-SMS) and is grounded in the Substance Use Stigma Framework [25, 38]. Structural validity was assessed through a hypothesized 4-factor structure that addresses anticipated stigma in healthcare settings, focusing specifically on interactions with healthcare providers, substance use disorder specialists, pharmacists, and SSP personnel. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha to evaluate both general internal consistency and consistency within individual factors.

Study Sample

Data for this analysis are from the NIDA-funded study “Cannabis use and health outcomes among opioid-using people who inject drugs” (R01 DA046049). PWID were recruited between April 2021 and November 2022 from syringe service programs, homeless services, and other community sites in Los Angeles, CA (3 sites) and Denver, CO (2 sites). Participants completed a 45-min computer-assisted personal interview using the Questionnaire Design System (NOVA Research, Bethesda, MD), which included 351 items described in detail elsewhere [66]. Additionally, measures of anticipated stigma experiences were collected (Table 2) using Likert-scale items. The analysis focuses on data from the interviews (n = 264). PWID were eligible to participate if they self-reported injection drug use, confirmed by visual inspection of injection marks or “track” marks [67], opioid use in the past 30 days, were 18 years or older, were not living with HIV, and were capable of providing informed consent.

Measures

Sociodemographic Characteristics

The sociodemographic characteristics of participants were characterized by assessing factors such as study site, gender, race, age, sexual orientation, educational attainment, homelessness in the last three months, drug use duration (stratified by the lower quartile), and income level.

Anticipated Stigma Scale

The Substance Use Anticipated Provider Stigma Scale was developed based on the Substance Use Stigma Framework and revises the structure of the SU-SMS [38]. Initial SU-SMS items were developed in parallel with the HIV Stigma Mechanisms Scale (HIV-SMS) and informed by literature and expert consultation, then refined using cognitive interviews with methadone maintenance treatment clients and people with HIV [39]. This process highlighted themes like loss of trust, which shaped how anticipated stigma was conceptualized, particularly from family members. Similarly, like the SU-SMS, the SU-APSS focuses on measuring anticipated stigma that PWID expect to face in the future from care providers, such as healthcare workers, substance use disorder treatment staff, pharmacists, and extends this structure to include SSP personnel, using the same item format applied to the original stigma sources in the SU-SMS. The SU-APSS assesses how individuals anticipate being treated by these professionals, capturing their perceptions of future interactions with these stigma sources.

The SU-APSS includes items related to anticipated stigma from key healthcare sources. For example, participants are asked about their expectations regarding healthcare workers not listening to their concerns (e.g., “Healthcare workers will not listen to my concerns”) or assuming they are engaging in pill shopping (e.g., “Healthcare workers will think that I’m pill shopping, or trying to con them into giving me prescription medications to get high or sell.”). Similarly, anticipated stigma from substance use disorder treatment staff is assessed with items like “Drug treatment staff will not take my concerns seriously or help me with an issue because of my drug use.” and “Drug treatment staff will treat me poorly.” Anticipated stigma related to pharmacy interactions is also captured, with items such as “Pharmacists or pharmacy staff will not listen to my concerns.” and “Pharmacist or pharmacy staff will treat me poorly.” Additionally, the scale includes items reflecting anticipated stigma from SSP personnel, with questions such as “Syringe exchange or harm reduction staff will not listen to my concerns.” and “Syringe exchange or harm reduction staff will treat me poorly.” Item responses were recorded using a 5-point Likert-type scale with the options: Very Unlikely, Unlikely, Neither Unlikely nor Likely, Likely, and Very Likely, where higher scores reflected greater levels of anticipated provider-side stigma.

One of the 12 items in the original stigma scale (“Drug treatment staff will not take my concerns seriously or help me with an issue because of my drug use”) was inadvertently omitted during survey programming. This item was expected to load on the substance use disorder treatment personnel factor. In the present analysis, the SUD Tx factor was nonetheless identified by the two remaining items assessing anticipated poor treatment and dismissive behavior from drug treatment staff, which demonstrated coherent factor loadings. However, omission of this item may have attenuated the full conceptual coverage of anticipated stigma from SUD treatment providers. In particular, inclusion of this item may strengthen assessment of perceived invalidation by treatment staff and may also help clarify whether a shared wording structure (e.g., expectations that providers will not take concerns seriously) contributes to a potential method factor across stigma sources. Accordingly, future research should include the complete item set to further evaluate the scale’s factor structure, internal consistency, and construct validity.

Stigma attributed to drug use, race/ethnicity, sexual identity, gender, and poverty is assessed using items adapted from the Substance Use Stigma Scale [38] and the Everyday Discrimination Scale [59]. Following the anticipated stigma items, participants are asked to indicate what they believe are the reasons syringe exchange or harm reduction staff might treat them in a stigmatizing way in the future. The question reads: “What do you think are the reasons syringe exchange or harm reduction staff will treat you this way in the future?” Participants can select multiple attributions from a list that includes drug use, race/ethnicity, sexual identity, gender, income level, education, age, and physical appearance, as well as options for some other reason, don’t know, refuse to answer, and not applicable. These responses capture perceived stigma across multiple intersecting identities and structural factors. For analysis, each selected attribution is incorporated into the relevant stigma domain, with response values summed to ensure all participants are included regardless of the number of attributions endorsed.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics of the demographic characteristics of the sample are reported. The analysis was conducted using data from 218 valid records, a subset of the full sample (N = 264). Participants who did not complete the SU-APSS items (n = 46) were excluded from confirmatory factor analysis. We employed Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to validate the factor structure of the SU-APSS. CFA was conducted using the covariance matrix and maximum likelihood (ML) estimation. The CFA model evaluated how well the observed items align with the hypothesized four latent factors. Factor variances were fixed at 1, with a lower bound of 0.01 set for the unique item variances. The model’s goodness of fit was evaluated using several fit indices, including the chi-square statistic (χ2) to assess overall model fit, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) with values greater than 0.90 indicating acceptable fit, the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) with values below 0.08 indicating acceptable fit, and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) with values below 0.08 indicating a good fit. Unstandardized factor loadings were scrutinized, with items showing loadings below 0.4 considered for removal. Internal consistency of the scale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, with a value above 0.7 indicating acceptable reliability for each domain. All analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 [68].

Results

Participant Characteristics

Based on the demographic data provided in Table 1, the study sample consisted of 264 participants, with the majority being male (76.5%) and a mean age of 41.7 ± 10.7 years. Participants were primarily White (54.2%), followed by Latinx (23.9%), Black (5.3%), and other racial/ethnic backgrounds. The age distribution showed that 34.1% were aged 30–39, and 25.8% were aged 40–49. A significant portion of participants (79.9%) had at least a high school education. Regarding sexual orientation, the majority identified as heterosexual (81%), with smaller groups identifying as gay or lesbian (4.1%), bisexual (12.1%), and asexual (1.5%). Income data revealed that 52.7% earned less than $1,000 per month, with the remaining participants earning between $1,000 and $2,100 per month. Most participants had used drugs for seven or more years (78.8%) and reported experiencing homelessness in the past three months (81.1%). Descriptive comparisons between the full sample (n = 264) and the analytic subset (n = 218) showed no meaningful differences across key demographic characteristics, supporting the assumption that data were missing at random (MAR).

Structural Validity

Supplementary Table 1 provides descriptive statistics for the 11 items of the SU-APSS. As shown in Fig. 1, a four-factor model was tested to capture participants' anticipated stigma experiences across four provider types: (1) healthcare workers (HCW), (2) substance use disorder treatment staff (SUD Tx), (3) pharmacy staff (Pharm), and (4) syringe service program (SSP) personnel. The four-factor model demonstrated good overall fit, with RMSEA = 0.09 [0.07, 0.11], CFI = 0.97, and SRMR = 0.04. Although the RMSEA slightly exceeded the conventional cutoff of 0.08, the 90% confidence interval for the RMSEA included values below 0.08, indicating that the degree of misfit was modest and within an acceptable range. Moreover, other fit indices, including the CFI and SRMR, indicated good model fit, and all items showed strong and statistically significant factor loadings. Consistent with recommendations for a holistic evaluation of model fit, the RMSEA was interpreted alongside these complementary indices rather than in isolation. Unstandardized parameter estimates, shown in Table 2, reveal that all items significantly (p < 0.001) and substantially (factor loadings ranging from 0.62 to 1.06) load onto their respective latent factors. Table 2 also presents the error variances for the observed variables in the 4-factor model. Most items have moderate error variances, suggesting that the latent factors account for a substantial portion of the observed variance, consistent with strong factor loadings. Item SSP3 appears to be almost fully explained by the factor, with very little unique error variance.

We also examined the correlations between the subscales of the SU-APSS. The Factor Correlation Matrix in Table 3 reveals the relationships between the four latent factors in the model: HCW, SUD Tx, Pharm, and SSP. Strong positive correlations are observed between HCW and SUD Tx (0.71) and HCW and Pharm (0.64), indicating a significant association between stigma expected from healthcare workers, substance use disorder treatment staff, and pharmacists. A moderate correlation is found between SUD Tx and Pharm (0.56), suggesting a notable connection between these two sources of stigma. However, the correlations with SSP are relatively weaker, with HCW correlating at 0.22, SUD Tx at 0.37, and Pharm at 0.24, indicating that stigma from syringe service programs is less strongly related to these other two stigma sources in the model. Overall, the data show that anticipated stigma from healthcare workers, substance use disorder treatment staff, and pharmacists are more closely related, while anticipated stigma from SSP is less connected to the other factors.

Despite the omission of one of the original items of the survey, the remaining 11 items demonstrated strong internal consistency and validity. Further analysis tested several model constraints, including tau-equivalence within each factor, equality of factor covariances, and equality of error variances across and within factors. Results indicated that relaxing these assumptions did not improve model fit, thereby supporting the structural validity of the 4-factor Substance Use Anticipated Provider Stigma Scale.

In future work with larger samples and the full item set, it may be possible to explicitly model item-level method effects (e.g., shared wording related to anticipated invalidation by providers), which may partially account for the slightly elevated RMSEA observed in the present analysis.

Attributions

Supplementary Table 2 summarizes participants’ attributions for anticipated stigma across four healthcare provider types. The most cited attribution was drug use (86%), followed by physical appearance (69%) and income level (49%). Attributions were most frequently assigned to healthcare staff, with notably fewer participants attributing stigma to syringe service program staff.

Scale Reliability

To assess reliability, we evaluated the internal consistency of the SU-APSS and its subscales using Cronbach’s alpha. Internal consistency was high, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87 for the overall scale (N = 218). Reliability was strong across all subscales: 0.85 for healthcare workers, 0.85 for substance use disorder treatment staff, 0.93 for pharmacy staff, and 0.96 for SSP personnel. These results indicate that the overall scale and subscales demonstrated strong internal consistency.

Discussion

The findings of this study contribute to intersectional stigma research among people who inject drugs by providing initial evidence of the structural validity, internal consistency, and overall model fit of the Substance Use Anticipated Provider Stigma Scale, grounded in the Stigma Framework. While the proposed factor structure is supported, additional psychometric testing, such as assessing construct validity through correlations with related psychosocial constructs, is needed to determine whether the identified factors reflect meaningful psychological or social dimensions and to evaluate the scale’s practical utility in predicting stigma-related outcomes. The factor analysis showed that the four potential anticipated stigma sources—healthcare workers, substance use disorder treatment staff, pharmacy staff, and syringe service program personnel—were well represented by their respective items, with strong and significant factor loadings. Despite weaker correlations between the syringe service program anticipated stigma factor and the other domains, the overall pattern of inter-factor correlations suggests a general anticipated stigma construct, driven primarily by stigma experienced in non-SSP healthcare settings. These findings further support the use of the scale for assessing stigma in healthcare interactions involving people who inject drugs.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess a scale designed to measure anticipated stigma from a range of healthcare provider types—specifically among PWID, moving beyond general stigma measures by disaggregating stigma sources across distinct provider roles. This work extends prior research that has focused on the development of standardized instruments assessing anticipated stigma faced by PWID in healthcare and social service settings [38, 69]. It also draws on and parallels research on anticipated healthcare stigma experienced by individuals with chronic illnesses, offering an important point of comparison and reinforcing the relevance of this scale in broader stigma research [69].

Building on a provider-specific approach grounded in the Stigma Framework, this study moves beyond parallel, siloed assessments of stigma attributed to single identities. Anticipated stigma was assessed separately by provider type, and participants were then asked to attribute each experience to one or more intersecting identities or structural factors. These attributions were adapted from established measures, including the Substance Use Stigma Scale and the Everyday Discrimination Scale, and included drug use, race/ethnicity, sexual identity, gender, income, education, age, and physical appearance [38, 59]. This approach retained the contextual specificity of healthcare settings while capturing the complexity of intersectional stigma in a more integrated manner.

As evidence documenting the harmful effects of stigma toward people who inject drugs continues to grow, the validation of this tool underscores the need for precise, context-sensitive instruments capable of capturing stigma across different points of healthcare contact [70–72]. The scale also has relevance beyond the specific U.S. settings in which it was developed. By organizing anticipated stigma around types of providers rather than particular health systems or policies, the SU-APSS can be adapted for use in different cultural and healthcare contexts where service delivery structures and professional roles vary. In applied research, the scale may help identify which points of care contribute most strongly to anticipated stigma in a given context, supporting the development of more focused interventions such as provider training or changes in service organization. When used longitudinally, the SU-APSS may also be useful for assessing whether interventions lead to reductions in anticipated stigma over time, particularly within specific provider encounters that are most relevant for healthcare engagement among PWID.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample was limited to urban areas in Los Angeles and Denver, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to non-urban populations. The recruitment through service settings may further limit the applicability of findings to the broader population of people who inject drugs. While the scale was administered to a recommended sample size, the validation was based on 218 participants [73], potentially limiting statistical power for subgroup analyses. Although the scale distinguishes stigma by provider type, conceptual overlap between subscales may affect discriminant validity, underscoring the need for further psychometric evaluation, including assessments of construct validity, test–retest reliability, and predictive validity. Finally, because enacted stigma questions were only asked of participants who had used each service within the past six months, the eligible sample for those items was substantially restricted. As a result, we were unable to validate the enacted stigma construct within this study or assess the correlation between enacted and anticipated stigma constructs. Despite these limitations, the study possesses several important strengths. It is the first to examine the psychometric properties of a multidimensional tool measuring anticipated stigma from distinct types of healthcare providers specifically among PWID, a population often neglected in psychometric research. The SU-APSS is grounded in established theoretical frameworks and builds on a prior validated stigma measure, reinforcing conceptual robustness [38]. The study employed rigorous statistical methods, including confirmatory factor analysis and multiple fit indices, to assess structural validity and internal consistency across domains. Collectively, these strengths underscore the SU-APSS’s potential as a valuable and innovative instrument for future stigma research and intervention evaluation in healthcare settings involving PWID.

Conclusion

This study provides a critical step forward in measuring anticipated stigma among people who inject drugs by validating a theoretically grounded, provider-specific scale that captures both the structural and intersectional dimensions of stigma. The SU-APSS offers a reliable and context-sensitive tool that reflects the real-world complexity of healthcare experiences for PWID, moving beyond generic or single-source stigma measures. While further psychometric testing is needed, including assessments of construct and predictive validity, this scale has the potential to inform targeted research, guide stigma-reduction interventions, and support policy efforts aimed at improving healthcare engagement and outcomes for this highly stigmatized population.
