Communicable diseases genomics network: promoting and harmonising pathogen genomics implementation for public health in Australia
Authors: Chantel Lin, Amy V Jennison, Lex EX Leong, David J Speers, Ella M Meumann, Louise Cooley, Karina Kennedy, Alicia Arnott, David Winter, William Rawlinson, Jennifer Robson, Patrick Harris, Angela Donald, Torsten Seemann, Susan A Ballard, Martyn Kirk, Vitali Sintchenko, Deborah A Williamson, Benjamin P Howden, Communicable Diseases Genomics Network
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryGlobally, the utility of pathogen genomics for public health was highlighted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Approaches to enhance coordination and improve implementation of pathogen genomics for public health are needed. The Communicable Diseases Genomics Network (CDGN) was established in 2015 in Australia. The network, embedded at the public health laboratory interface and supported by the Australian Government, has facilitated a coordinated model in Australia for public health pathogen genomics. CDGN activities have facilitated pilot projects to demonstrate use cases, harmonisation of data sharing and governance arrangements, outbreak and pandemic response, translational research, policy development and workforce capacity building. The impact of CDGN has been enabling the significant progress towards public health genomics implementation in Australia, and providing a model that could be applied in other federated settings, aligned with international best practice.Keywords: Pathogen genomics, Public health, Networking, Implementation

Introduction

Pathogen whole genome sequencing (pathogen genomics) has transformed infectious diseases management and public health by enhancing routine surveillance, outbreak investigation, emerging threat detection, and response, through the ability to more rapidly and accurately characterise pathogens.1,2

Internationally, pathogen genomics has been a disruptive technology, where integration of genomic data with other available data has facilitated impactful analyses, and access to the technology across diverse settings and populations has allowed use of these data for public health responses at sub-national, national and global levels.3 To ensure that the power of pathogen genomics is fully realised, initiatives that support implementation for public health need to be embedded and coordinated by public health laboratories, in partnership with public health agencies and stakeholders. Mechanisms for harmonisation of data analysis, approaches to data sharing, and reporting of results are required, to facilitate implementation of an end to end framework for use in public health.

Like several countries globally, Australia's public health system operates within a complex public health governance structure of a federated system of states and territories, with shared health responsibilities across state and national agencies. This structure has formed a public health system that is largely state and territory-based, where disease surveillance systems and associated data governance (including analysis, access, storage, sharing and reporting), and capacity, vary significantly across the country. National surveillance and outbreak response is reliant on the development of formal and informal coordination mechanisms to support a cohesive national picture.

To ensure harmonisation of public health implementation of pathogen genomics, there was early recognition by the jurisdictional-based public health laboratories of the need for a coordinated national approach. Here we describe the Communicable Diseases Genomics Network (CDGN), a unique, national collaborative network of public health laboratories across Australia. This viewpoint is focused on how the innovation of a public health laboratory-driven networking approach has accelerated and facilitated genomics implementation since 2015, including the role of the network to support the pandemic response. The Australian experience described in this paper will be of relevance to other federated countries planning to implement genomic technologies for public health.

Recognising the need for a nationally coordinated approach to pathogen genomics

Over the last decade, the power of genomics to enhance Australia's surveillance and response systems has been demonstrated across diverse infectious disease challenges, including foodborne diseases,4, 5, 6 pathogens with antimicrobial resistance (AMR),7,8 sexually transmitted infections,9 vector-borne diseases,10 respiratory diseases,11 and emerging threats. By 2015, there was general recognition from Australian public health laboratories and agencies that to remain at the forefront of best practice infectious disease surveillance, a shift towards genomics-based responses was required. However, only three of the eight states and territories in Australia had whole genome sequencing (WGS) infrastructure or personnel, with most relying on a referral-based approach. Processes to facilitate implementation and use of pathogen genomics for public health were not well defined, and there was a lack of harmonisation of analysis and reporting approaches. Additionally, laboratories making advancements towards the use of pathogen genomics for public health were doing this independently without national coordination, resulting in a risk of inconsistent surveillance approaches, impacting the ability for results to be integrated at the national level, as well as potential for inequitable access to the technology nationally.

Pathway towards establishing the communicable diseases genomics network (CDGN) Australia and New Zealand

Recognising the need for coordination of the increasing public health pathogen genomics activities across Australia, public health laboratory leaders agreed to establish a network to enhance communication in July 2015. In 2016, public health laboratories across Australia, and a public health laboratory in New Zealand, signed Memorandums of Understanding to contribute to nationally-coordinated genomics activities.

The identity and cohesiveness of the network was enhanced when the federal Department of Health and Aged Care supported two key initiatives to identify a suitable national approach to genomics implementation in 2016; 1) contracted Australian public health laboratories that formed CDGN to undertake a landscape analysis and develop a roadmap for establishing communicable diseases genomics in Australia, and 2) established a whole genome sequencing working group under the long-standing Australian Public Health Laboratory Network.12

The roadmap identified the need for a funded centralised coordination mechanism that supports planning and implementation, standardisation of laboratory approaches for integration into surveillance and response systems, workforce training, national data governance and sharing mechanisms, and communication across laboratories with public health genomics responsibilities. The working group defined similar key priorities, and importantly, demonstrated the value of knowledge sharing across states and territory laboratories (e.g. preparation for accreditation of pathogen genomics workflows).

Concurrently, public health laboratories and key experts across academic institutions that were at the forefront of translating the use of genomics for research to public health recognised the opportunity for coordination across initiatives and convened symposia and workshops to enhance information sharing nationally.

The combined “top down” (support and investment from the federal Department of Health and Aged Care) and “bottom up” (initiatives from public health laboratories) approach generated interest and strong commitment from public health laboratories and government stakeholders to form a network, by highlighting the role an established laboratory network could have in shaping the landscape of public health pathogen genomics in Australia.

Governance and responsibilities of the network in the Australian health system

The CDGN was formalised as an Expert Reference Panel in the Australian Government's health system in 2018, under the Public Health Laboratory Network,12 reporting up to the Australian Health Protection Committee (Fig. 1). The Australian Government Department of Health, Disability and Ageing provides funding to support the secretariat and management of the network.

The vision of the CDGN13 is to advance public health through the implementation of pathogen genomics to support communicable diseases activities. The Network's roles and responsibilities are to; i) facilitate consistency in the use of genomics for public health, ii) facilitate sharing of data and knowledge, and iii) act as a unified coordination mechanism to work with government and other stakeholders to advance implementation of genomics for public health.

Laboratories with primary public health responsibility formed the Network and, since inception, membership has been open to clinical microbiologists and laboratory scientists, with expansion to include epidemiologists, bioinformaticians and policy makers. The CDGN also engages with academic and research partners where required, particularly to expand expertise to progress activities, or participate in translational research. The CDGN consists of members from all public health laboratories across Australia, Australian Government Department of Health, and expert members from private pathology, clinical microbiology laboratories, and academic institutions, with inclusion of a public health laboratory in New Zealand to support a broadened community of practice (Fig. 1).

The CDGN has a formal reporting structure to the Department of Health and provides regular updates to the Public Health Laboratory Network (Fig. 1).

Communicable Diseases Genomics Network operational framework

The CDGN operational framework supports strategic oversight, management and coordination, and structure to support the scope of activities. Specifically, the CDGN develops an annual workplan to set the direction of the network, in alignment with Australia's National Microbial Genomics Framework.14

Implementation of the workplan is supported by monthly meetings and working groups. Working groups consist of members from core CDGN, as well as additional technical experts and stakeholders as required. Working groups can be ongoing for key thematic areas (e.g. teaching and training, metagenomics), time-limited to support implementation, harmonisation and data sharing for key priority pathogens (e.g. Salmonella spp., and Mycobacterium tuberculosis), or convened to respond to emerging infectious disease threats or public health emergencies (e.g. SARS-CoV-2, Japanese encephalitis virus, and Mpox).

In addition to the delivery of the workplan, the CDGN provides advice to government, and responds to requests for support to contribute to outbreak investigation, surveillance, and translational research activities.

Milestones and impact

Since the formalisation of the CDGN as an Expert Reference Panel, there has been major progress to advance pathogen genomics for public health in Australia (Fig. 2). This has been accelerated by the global recognition of the importance of genomic surveillance through the COVID-19 pandemic response. Key milestones and impact of the CDGN are described below.

Pilot projects to demonstrate use cases

The CDGN developed a list of high priority pathogens for genomics implementation in 2018, underpinned by a review of disease burden in Australia, number of samples received by CDGN laboratories, and survey distributed to CDGN laboratories to understand pathogens being sequenced routinely or ad hoc for translational research projects. Priority pathogens included enteric pathogens (Salmonella, Shiga-toxin producing Escherichia coli, Listeria, hepatitis A virus), Neisseria meningitidis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, measles, and AMR organisms. Based on the capacity and capabilities across laboratories, and potential contribution to surveillance programs, the Network identified a number of broad areas of focus. Pilot projects were developed for a number of the priority pathogens (Listeria monocytogenes, Salmonella enterica serovar Enteritidis, M. tuberculosis, N. meningitidis, and pathogens with AMR). The objectives of the pilot projects were to; establish protocols for sharing of genomic data, including required metadata, establish harmonised approaches for analysis and reporting, and assess the feasibility and utility of nationally coordinated genomic surveillance.

CDGN laboratories were able to participate on an opt-in basis, and participating laboratories jointly defined approaches to data sharing, analysis and reporting. Projects largely used retrospective datasets or data being generated already through public health service delivery for ease of accessibility. The impact of the pilot projects was a tested framework on how the development of genomic surveillance for key pathogens could occur nationally through a collaborative translational research approach, however identified that data solutions were required to enhance genomic data sharing in the federated model.

Data sharing and governance to support national integration

While CDGN laboratories were able to share data for public health investigations through existing public health legislation and legal frameworks, data sharing to support development and optimisation of a national framework required a data sharing agreement. CDGN laboratories jointly developed an agreement, and each laboratory sought approval from their respective health agencies. The data sharing agreement allowed sharing of pathogen sequences and isolates, including agreed minimal metadata, to support development of a nationally integrated pathogen genomics surveillance system. The agreement facilitated the development of a secure web-based platform (AusTrakka) for integrated, real-time analysis, sharing and comparison of pathogen genomes for public health in Australia.15

Readiness to respond to the COVID-19 pandemic

The value of an established national genomics network was demonstrated during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, where the CDGN was able to support rapid response and mobilisation of genomics capabilities and systems to support the pandemic response. The CDGN formed COVID-19 specific working groups to support Australia's national response.16 Existing CDGN partnerships also facilitated the rapid establishment of a translational research program to support national sequencing of SARS-CoV-2 and national, real-time analysis and reporting.17,18 Following on from the COVID-19 pandemic, the CDGN has used this established model to support coordination of national responses to other emerging threats, including Mpox and Japanese encephalitis (JEV).

Translational research for implementation

The CDGN has facilitated the inception, design and implementation of a large Australian Government-funded competitive translational research program, the Australian Pathogen Genomics Program,19,20 that aims to implement and evaluate a nationally synchronised approach to public health pathogen genomics in Australia. The program includes all CDGN laboratories and demonstrates the value of translational research funding as an essential driver of progress towards public health implementation.

Translation into policy and action

The roadmap drafted by CDGN laboratories in 2016 guided the development of Australia's first National Microbial Genomics Framework (2019–2022)14 and associated Implementation Plan,21 with CDGN taking lead responsibility for key strategic objectives.

The CDGN supports development and implementation of policies and position papers to inform public health action, including application of microbial genomics to control infectious caused by M. tuberculosis22 and SARS-CoV-2,23 use of wastewater surveillance,24 and provides ongoing advice to government on outbreaks and emerging infectious diseases detection and management.

Workforce development and training

Facilitated by the CDGN, with the Australian Pathogen Genomics Program, all states and territories in Australia now have genomics infrastructure and a dedicated workforce to support pathogen genomics in public health laboratory settings. The CDGN acts as an established community of practice to share knowledge and resources and continue to advance the use of genomics for public health, in alignment with national microbiology certification requirements. The CDGN facilitates workforce training opportunities, including; short term placements for laboratory, genomic epidemiology or bioinformatics personnel across CDGN laboratories, end-to-end genomics webinar series and workshops targeted at both the genomics workforce and end users, and symposia to highlight progress and promote implementation of pathogen genomics for public health. Training materials and guidance documents are available publicly on the CDGN's website,13 with a suite of resources that cover basic introduction to pathogen genomics, to exploring application of metagenomics in public health. Workforce development and training topics covered by CDGN are proposed by members based on identified gaps and needs, and included in the Network's annual workplan.

Challenges and learnings

While the CDGN is now established and functional, it is important to highlight the challenges faced during development of the network. In the early stages, activities relied on the willingness of laboratories to participate and contribute their own time and resources, resulting in slow progress over some periods. The formalisation of CDGN as an Expert Reference Panel and ongoing funding support from the Australian Department of Health for the management and coordination of the Network has been important and remains essential to maintain the function of the Network.

In Australia's federated system, funding for national level public health initiatives is not straightforward. Without secure funding to dedicate to development of national genomic surveillance approaches, it has been difficult for CDGN laboratories to contribute the required time and resources to support national integration. The translational research funding and opportunity to work collaboratively as a Network to contribute to the Australian Pathogen Genomics Program19 has been a turning point to facilitate progress towards the integration of pathogen genomics into public health.

Approaches and challenges to data sharing for genomic surveillance have been well described internationally e.g. by the World Health Organisation,25 which describes principles to overcome operational, governance and legal requirements across different pathogens and settings. Establishing the CDGN data sharing agreement has been a complex and iterative process, that initially allowed general sharing of genomic data and limited associated metadata, with specific data sharing and data governance principles to be determined on a pathogen-by-pathogen basis. This allowed CDGN to progress with developing implementation models for key pathogens. Through this work and facilitated by the implementation of the national genomics data sharing platform (AusTrakka),19 CDGN is now moving towards supporting a pathogen-agnostic data sharing and governance framework endorsed by Australia's national health committee—the Australian Health Protection Committee.

Recommendations

The model described for establishing a national public health pathogen genomics network may be applicable and adaptable to other countries internationally seeking to enhance their public health genomics surveillance and response through a coordinated national approach.

Key recommendations for establishing a national public health pathogen genomics network (Table 1) based on the Australian experience are: i) focus on clear objectives and defined purpose, ii) open approach to membership and engagement, iii) operationalisation within national public health structure, iv) opportunities for collaboration across members, and v) established coordination mechanisms. These factors were pivotal to the successful establishment and long-term function of the CDGN, and have formed the foundation of the Network's ability to delivery outputs with public health impact.

The Network continues to evolve with the public health pathogen genomics landscape in Australia, and there are opportunities for the CDGN to consider expansion to a multi-sectoral approach, build on workforce development activities to consider formally supporting advanced training programs, and to further define opportunities to contribute to research.

Conclusion

Establishment of the CDGN was driven from commitment and recognition from public health laboratories that an integrated and cohesive approach to pathogen genomics was required and would be beneficial to building capacity and capabilities nationally. The novelty of the Network is demonstrated through the unique collaborative approach, initially driven from public health laboratories that recognised the need for a harmonised and coordinated national approach. This enhanced engagement of relevant stakeholders and the utility of the Network as it became embedded in the national committee structure. The success of the Network is facilitated by clear workplans that address key gaps and challenges to genomics implementation as recognised by CDGN members, and funding support from government and translational research programs. The CDGN fosters partnerships to advance key activities, including with government and research institutions, policy makers, and ensures alignment with national pathogen genomics policies.

Formalisation of the Network under Australia's Department of Health advisory committee structures has given the CDGN a clear role to support public health genomics implementation, with funded support from the Department of Health for the management of the Network, contributing to sustainability.

The implementation of a coordinated model in Australia has supported significant progress towards public health genomics implementation nationally, harmonising data sharing and data governance approaches, and developing a skilled genomics workforce. The learnings from the Australian experience, particularly; public health laboratories being the central drivers and coordinators, the model for open collaboration and partnership, operationalisation within national public health structures, and supported coordination mechanisms, may be applicable and adaptable in other settings.
