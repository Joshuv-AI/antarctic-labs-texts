Distinctive features of the tumor and immune microenvironment in glioblastoma
Authors: Elena Shklovskaya, Bernadette Pedersen, Su Yin Lim, Mal Irvine, Jesse R. Brown, Andreia V. Pinho, Brindha Shivalingam, Alexander M. Menzies, Laveniya Satgunaseelan, Kimberley L. Alexander, Antonio Di Ieva, Georgina V. Long, Wei Yen Chan, Helen Rizos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) have improved outcomes for asymptomatic patients with melanoma brain metastases (MBM) but have delivered limited benefit in glioblastoma (GBM). Defining context-dependent features of ICI response is critical for tailoring effective therapeutic strategies and improving GBM patient outcomes. Multiparameter flow cytometry analyses of immune composition and activation status were performed on 110 tumor cell suspensions obtained from cavitron ultrasonic surgical aspirates (CUSA) specimens (n = 40), and surgically resected GBM (n = 21) and MBM (n = 49) tumors. GBM tumors exhibited marked phenotypic heterogeneity, frequently co-expressing stem-like and differentiation markers. GBM stemness correlated with increased macrophage infiltration and reduced T cell abundance, implicating plasticity in the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Compared to MBM, GBM showed a marked paucity of T cells, an enrichment of myeloid cells, downregulated MHC-I, and elevated PD-L2 expression on tumor cells. Multiplex immunostaining performed on a subset of GBM samples, confirmed myeloid predominance and indicated pronounced spatial heterogeneity. However, the presence of antigen-experienced T cells and preserved IFNγ signaling indicates a latent potential for immune engagement. These findings provide a rationale for ICI-based combinations designed to restore effective anti-tumor immunity in GBM.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and aggressive primary brain tumor, with a median overall survival of only 14.6 months following current treatment of maximal safe resection and adjuvant chemotherapy plus radiotherapy (the Stupp protocol)1. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including PD-1, CTLA4, and LAG3 inhibitors, have revolutionized the management of many cancer types, including melanoma2. Although ICIs can induce immune activation and promote immune cell infiltration in GBM3, clinical benefits remain limited. In phase 3 randomized trials, adjuvant anti-PD-1 administered in combination with radiotherapy (with or without chemotherapy), showed no benefit over the standard chemoradiotherapy4,5. The lack of GBM response to ICIs is thought to reflect its low tumor mutational burden and the immunosuppressive microenvironment of the brain, including the parenchyma and border-associated niches with abundant microglia/macrophages and limited natural killer and T cells6–8.

Although GBMs are generally resistant to ICIs, brain metastases from melanoma (MBM) have shown significant sensitivity to ICIs. The rate of intracranial response to combination PD-1 and CTLA4 inhibitors in patients with active asymptomatic, untreated MBMs (who are not on corticosteroids) was 55%, with 26% of patients showing a complete intracranial response9. Significantly, intracranial and extracranial clinical benefits were equivalent in these melanoma patients9. Intracranial and systemic responses were also concordant in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with PD-L1 positive (PD-L1 ≥ 1%) brain metastases, with 29.7% of patients showing partial or complete intracranial response to PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy, and 33% of patients remaining progression-free in the brain at one year10. Importantly, no intracranial response to PD-1 blockade was seen in NSCLC patients with PD-L1-negative (PD-L1 < 1%) brain metastases10. These results suggest that tumor-intrinsic features contribute to ICI sensitivity in the brain.

We present a comprehensive comparative analysis of GBM and MBM, focusing on features of ICI response and resistance. We characterized the immune contexture of these tumors, and profiled tumor expression of the major histocompatibility complexes (MHC-I and MHC-II)11,12, markers of differentiation, PD-L1 and PD-L2 checkpoints and interferon-gamma (IFNγ) signaling13. GBMs were enriched for myeloid cells and exhibited a paucity of T lymphocytes compared to MBM. Notably, despite the overall low T cell abundance in GBM, a subset of T effector cells exhibits markers (PD-1, Ki-67) indicative of potential activation and tissue residency (CD103), suggesting recent antigen exposure. Additionally, although GBM cells display an immunosuppressive phenotype, characterized by elevated PD-L2 and downregulated MHC-I expression, they retain functional IFNγ signaling. These findings reveal that, despite multiple barriers to immune response, GBM tumors harbor antigen-experienced T cells and preserved IFNγ signaling, highlighting opportunities to refine ICI combinations and overcome local immunosuppression.

Results

Patient cohort and cellular heterogeneity of glioblastoma

Single-cell tumor suspensions from 60 patients with primary or relapsed GBM, derived from surgically resected tissues (n = 21 patients; Table S1) and CUSA aspirates (n = 39 patients; Table S2), were included in this study. One GBM patient (Pt#17) had matched primary (CUSA17a) and recurrence (CUSA17b) specimens; the recurrent tumor was removed from a peripheral enhancing area adjacent to the original tumor resection site 7 months later. The tumor microenvironment was examined using multiparameter flow cytometric analysis on 61 single-cell suspensions. Initially, samples were examined for markers commonly used to identify GBM stem cells, including SOX2, CD133, CD44 and neuroepithelial stem cell protein (Nestin)14–16, along with the astrocytic differentiation antigen, glial fibrillary acid protein (GFAP). Using an unbiased approach, GBM cells in each sample were defined as positive for either SOX2, CD133, CD44, Nestin or GFAP using Boolean “OR” combination gates (Fig. S1 and Fig. 1a). t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) analysis of the combined GBM cell populations revealed substantial variation in the distribution of these markers (Fig. 1a and Fig. S1b). Notably, marker expression patterns clearly differentiated surgical from CUSA-derived specimens (Fig. 1a, b, Fig. S2a, b). The vast majority of CUSA-derived GBM cells expressed GFAP (median 91.6%, range: 31.8–99.8%), while the proportions of Nestin-, SOX2-, CD44-, and CD133-positive cells were substantially lower compared to surgical GBM specimens (Fig. 1b, Fig. S2a). Notably, only 0.25% (range: 0.0–34.9%) of CUSA-derived GBM cells co-expressed all five markers, compared to 19.2% of cells from surgically resected GBM tissue (range: 0.1–48.2%; Fig. S2a). Despite these differences, GFAP staining on three patient-matched CUSA and tissue sections demonstrated concordant proportions of GFAP-positive tumor cells (Fig. S2c), indicating that CUSA-derived cells retain key features of the original tumor (Fig. S2c, d).

Although the expression of individual markers effectively distinguished CUSA- and surgery-derived GBM samples by principal component analysis (Fig. S2b), the co-expression patterns still provided meaningful insights. We observed a strong positive correlation between the percentage of cells expressing SOX2, CD133, CD44 and Nestin (Spearman’s correlation coefficients SOX2 vs Nestin expression, r = 0.844, p < 0.0001; SOX2 vs CD44 expression, r = 0.536, p < 0.001; SOX2 vs CD133 expression, r = 0.75, p < 0.0001, Fig. 1d) and these stemness markers were negatively correlated with GFAP expression (Spearman’s correlation coefficient SOX2 vs GFAP expression, r = -0.652, p < 0.0001; Nestin vs GFAP, r = −0.64, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1d).

Low MHC expression and frequent PD-L2 upregulation distinguish GBM from MBM

We next analyzed GBM tumors specifically for features of ICI response and resistance. Flow cytometry was used to compare the expression of ICI response determinants, MHC-I, MHC-II and the actionable immune checkpoint ligands PD-L1 and PD-L2 (Fig. 2a). To contextualize marker expression in GBM, we compared the expression of these markers in MBM, an intracranial malignancy that responds to ICI. We examined 49 MBM specimens (obtained from 38 patients, including seven patients with tissues collected at different timepoints; Table S3). Flow cytometry was performed on 21 OFF-treatment MBM samples (patients not on systemic therapy at the time of biopsy; two patients have previously received selective BRAFV600 inhibitors, and one patient received combination anti-PD-1 plus anti-CTLA4). The remaining 28 brain melanoma specimens were classified as ON-treatment and included 9 samples from six patients treated with PD-1 monotherapy, and 19 samples from 11 patients treated with combination PD-1 plus CTLA4 and/or MAPK inhibitors17. The fraction of tumor cells expressing MHC-I, MHC-II, and PD-L1 was significantly lower in GBM compared to MBM (Fig. 2b and Fig. S3a), although complete loss of all three markers was rare and only observed in two MBM samples (MBM9 and MBM18; Fig. 2b and Fig. S3b). Across the 61 GBM specimens, MHC-I expression was inversely correlated with GFAP expression while positively correlated with glioma stemness markers SOX2 (Fig. 2c), Nestin, CD44, and CD133 (Fig. S3c). Consistent with their higher GFAP expression, CUSA samples showed significantly lower levels of MHC-I compared to GBM and MBM tissue (Fig. 2b), but we did not observe complete loss of MHC-I expression in GBM. Among the four markers studied, only PD-L2 was significantly elevated in GBM compared to MBM (Fig. 2b). Taken together, low MHC-I expression and frequent upregulation of PD-L2 were commonly found in GBM compared to MBM tumors.

GBM and MBM immune microenvironments diverge by T cell abundance and myeloid composition

Analysis of the GBM immune microenvironment by flow cytometry (Fig. S4a–d) focussed on 24 samples with sufficient CD45+ immune content (at least 400 CD45+ cells) and minimal blood contamination. Samples were selected based on a predominance of tumor-resident (SSC-Aintermediate-high CD39high) macrophages, and less than 50% blood-derived monocytes (SSC-Aintermediate CD39intermediate), ensuring an accurate representation of the tumor-associated immune landscape. Final immune microenvironment analysis included 19 surgical and 5 CUSA GBM specimens (Fig. S4b).

Expression of the GBM stemness markers (SOX2) was negatively correlated with T cell abundance (Spearman’s correlation coefficient r = −0.65, p = 0.005) but positively correlated with macrophage presence (r = 0.45, p = 0.027, Fig. 3a), suggesting that stem-like GBM phenotypes are associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment18. GBM and MBM samples demonstrated similar immune content within live cell (tumor plus immune) fractions (Fig. S4c), but with relative paucity of T cells and higher abundance of macrophages in GBM, compared to 49 MBM specimens (Fig. S4c). Unbiased clustering of major immune subsets—including myeloid cells (macrophages/monocytes) and lymphoid cells (CD4+ conventional T cells [Tconv, CD4+FOXP3-], CD4 regulatory T cells [Treg, CD4+FOXP3+], and CD8+ T cells)—was performed with 24 GBM and 48 MBM samples (MBM21a was excluded due to insufficient lymphoid content) and revealed two distinct tumor microenvironment clusters (Fig. S4d). Cluster 1 included most GBMs (23/24, 96%) and a minority of MBMs (6/48, 13%) and was characterized by abundant tumor-associated macrophages and relatively few T cells (Fig. S4d). Cluster 2 comprised the majority of MBMs (42/48, 88%) but only one GBM (4%) and was enriched in T cells (Fig. S4d).

Although relatively deficient in T cells, GBM contained PD-1⁺Ki-67⁺ tumor-infiltrating CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ effector T cells, features consistent with potential T cell activation and proliferation, similar to MBM (Fig. 3b). The relative proportion of PD-1⁺Ki-67⁺ CD4⁺ effector T cells did not differ between MBM and GBM, whereas PD-1⁺Ki-67⁺ CD8⁺ effector T cells were significantly lower in GBM (Fig. 3b). GBM samples also displayed approximately 2-fold lower expression of the CD8⁺ T cell tissue-residency marker CD103 compared to MBM (Fig. 3c); however, CD103 expression correlated with PD-1/Ki-67 levels (Fig. 3c), suggesting that a subset of tissue-resident CD8⁺ T cells may have a proliferative, activation-associated phenotype. Importantly, integration of multiplex immunofluorescence data (across seven GBM tissue specimens) revealed that only a minority of CD8⁺ T cells expressed the cytotoxic effector molecule granzyme B, and co-expression of granzyme B and Ki-67 was infrequent (Fig. S4e), consistent with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

To provide spatial context for these findings, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence staining on nine FFPE GBM tissue sections using a 15-plex antibody panel. GBM tumor cells (GFAP⁺) and immune populations, including T cell subsets (CD4⁺, CD8⁺), macrophages (CD163⁺/CD163⁻), and microglia (CD163-TMEM119+), were analyzed across all samples (Fig. 3d, e and Fig. S5).

Consistent with GBM biology, we observed marked inter- and intra-tumor heterogeneity in immune cell composition and spatial distribution across samples. GFAP⁺ tumor cells were the most abundant population (median 686.9 cells/mm2, range: 54.7–3419). CD163⁻ pro-inflammatory (M1-like) macrophages (median 18.6 cells/mm2, range: 0.2–386.4), CD163⁺ immunoregulatory (M2-like) macrophages (median 31.4 cells/mm2, range 6.6–304.9), and CD163-TMEM119+ microglia (median 60.1 cells/mm2, range 0.5–190) were present at moderate, albeit varying, densities, whereas CD4+ T cells (median 6.4 cells/mm2, range 0–63.3) and CD8+ T cells (median 6.1 cells/mm2, range 0.4–181.6) were comparatively sparse (Fig. 3d and Fig. S5a), consistent with flow cytometry data.

Spatial proximity analysis revealed that CD163+ macrophages were located closest to GFAP+ tumor cells (median distance 101.4 μm, interquartile range (IQR) 91.56–223.0 µm, n = 9 independent GBM samples), followed by CD163- macrophages (median distance 159.9 μm, IQR 47.74–352.7 μm), CD163-TMEM119+ microglia (median distance 206.1 μm, IQR 71.65–255.0 μm), CD8+ T cells (median distance 230.6 μm, IQR 193.5–517.6 µm) and CD4+ T cells (median distance 286.2 μm, IQR 188.8–673.7 µm) (Fig. 3e, Fig. S5b). Notably, substantial variability in cell–cell distances and spatial organization was observed across all tissues, underscoring the highly heterogenous spatial architecture of the GBM tumor microenvironment.

Comparative IFNγ response reveals elevated PD-L2 expression in GBM vs MBM

To gain further insights into mechanisms of GBM-mediated immune suppression, we examined the integrity of IFNγ signaling in a series of published and newly derived GBM cell lines (n = 17)19 and in 8 MBM cell lines17. Loss of response to interferon gamma (IFNγ) is a well-established mechanism of resistance to ICI therapies13,20.

GBM and MBM cell lines were treated with 1000 U/ml IFNγ for 24 h and the expression of MHC-I, MHC-II, PD-L1, and PD-L2 was examined with flow cytometry (Fig. 4a). All GBM and MBM cell lines responded to exogenous IFNγ with induced expression (IFNγ-treated/control-treated ≥1.5) of at least three markers. Hence, loss of IFNγ response is not a feature of GBM tumors (Fig. 4a). PD-L1 and PD-L2 levels were considerably higher in GBM compared to MBM cell lines after IFNγ stimulation, and not surprisingly, the levels of induction (IFNγ-treated/control) in GBM cells were also significantly higher (Fig. S6). Elevated induction of PD-L2 in GBM cells presumably reflected their high baseline expression compared to MBM cells (Fig. 4a). During analysis, we also observed that PD-L2 expression on tumor-infiltrating T cells (TILs) was elevated in GBM compared to MBM (Fig. 4b). PD-L2 expression on TILs positively correlated with tumor PD-L2 expression in both GBM and MBM (Fig. 4b). Given the high myeloid cell content in brain dissociates, we next compared the expression levels of PD-1 ligands on tumor and myeloid cells within each dissociate, using autologous T cells as a reference population (Fig. 4c and Fig. S7). Tumor-associated macrophages were uniformly PD-L2 positive and expressed higher PD-L2 levels than tumor cells in both GBM and MBM dissociates, independent of patient treatment status (Fig. 4c). Together, our findings from brain tumor dissociates and short-term cell lines highlight tumor-intrinsic PD-L2 expression and IFNγ-inducible PD-L1/PD-L2 as shared immunosuppressive features of GBM. Our results also support a role for further immunosuppression via myeloid tumor microenvironment.

Suppression of T cell activation by PD-L1 and PD-L2 is reversed by checkpoint inhibition

To evaluate the relative importance of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in PD-1 engagement and signaling, we utilized a T cell reporter platform, comprising the human Jurkat E6.1T cell line transduced to express the PD-1 receptor and a transcriptional NF-kB::eGFP reporter, and T cell stimulator cells (TCS) expressing a CD3 agonist (aCD3, signal 1) with or without CD86 co-stimulator (signal 2), together with PD-L1 or PD-L2 (Fig. 4d)21,22. Co-culture of reporter T cells with TCS expressing aCD3/CD86 resulted in NF-kB reporter activation in 59% of the T cells as opposed to 6% with aCD3 alone. As expected, T cell activation was diminished following PD-1 engagement after T cell coculture with TCS expressing aCD3/CD86 and PD-L1 or PD-L2, with only 49% and 32% NF-kB reporter activation, respectively. Importantly, addition of the PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab to the coculture restored T cell activation and NF-kB signaling in both cases. These data confirm that immune checkpoint inhibitors acting at the PD-1 receptor, can overcome PD-L1 and PD-L2-imposed suppression with similar efficacy.

Discussion

The immunological and cellular complexity of GBM remains a significant barrier to effective immune-based therapies. Here, we provide a comprehensive characterization of the GBM tumor microenvironment, integrating phenotypic profiling of tumor cells and immune infiltrates from primary surgical and CUSA-derived tumor samples. Our findings highlight substantial intratumoral heterogeneity and reveal a clear dichotomy between CUSA- and surgical-derived GBM cell populations. While surgical specimens displayed a higher proportion of tumor cells co-expressing multiple stem-associated markers (SOX2, CD133, CD44, and Nestin), CUSA-derived specimens were dominated by GFAP-positive cells, indicating a differentiated phenotype. There was also a difference in immune populations, with CUSA samples displaying lower myeloid content and a larger T cell fraction that remarkably contained few cells with tissue retention or local activation features. Consistent with our concordance analyses demonstrating preservation of core tumor cell features, this shift may reflect technical differences in tumor sampling or spatial heterogeneity within the tumor, given the CUSA device is often used to resect the invasive edge, rather than the tumor core. Additionally, a small margin is often resected if the surrounding brain is not evident23, potentially including bystander T cells, likely derived from peripheral circulation.

Immune profiling identified two distinct immune microenvironmental states: a myeloid-dominant, T cell-low GBM-predominant cluster, and a T cell-inflamed MBM-predominant cluster, emphasizing the distinct immunological environments of these intracranial tumors. This contrast is clinically relevant as patients with asymptomatic, active melanoma brain metastases exhibit substantial responses to ICIs. Specifically, these patients show an intracranial response rate of 51% and a 7-year overall survival of 48% following treatment with the combined immune checkpoint blockade of ipilimumab and nivolumab9.

Despite the low abundance of CD8⁺ T cells in GBM, a subset expressed activation (PD-1, Ki-67) and tissue residency (CD103) markers, indicative of recent antigen encounter and potential local retention. These T cells may represent a poised, albeit restrained, immune population capable of responding to immune checkpoint blockade under permissive conditions. Recent data supports this notion, revealing that neoadjuvant combination immunotherapy (combination anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA4 and anti-LAG3) increased the infiltration and activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells into the GBM of a newly diagnosed patient3. However, sustained T cell activation may be restricted, as evidenced by the low proportion of proliferating CD8+ T cells with cytotoxic effector potential, and by GBM-associated macrophages that overwhelmingly predominate in GBM immune infiltrates. Indeed, spatial distribution analyses of GBM tumor tissues confirmed close proximity of CD163+ and CD163- macrophages to tumor cells, while CD4+ and CD8+ T cells appear excluded from the GBM tumor region.

At the tumor cell level, GBM specimens exhibited reduced expression of MHC class I and II molecules and PD-L1, key components of antigen presentation and adaptive immune recognition. This downregulation of MHC expression appears to be tumor-extrinsic, as short-term derived GBM cells express significant levels of inducible MHC and PD-L1. This suggests that an immunosuppressive cytokine milieu, including IL-10 and TGFß24, combined with low levels of T cell-derived IFNγ, may contribute to low MHC expression in GBM. Notably, PD-L2 was upregulated in a substantial fraction of GBM samples, exceeding expression in MBMs. PD-L2 expression was also prominent in GBM-derived cell lines and further enhanced by IFNγ stimulation, in contrast to MBM cell lines. Tumor-intrinsic PD-L2 expression is a prognostic marker of poor outcomes in glioma25,26 and has been positively correlated with macrophage and regulatory T cell infiltration26,27. This PD-L2-associated immunosuppressive environment in GBM may play a key role in limiting the effectiveness of ICIs28. Surprisingly, T cells infiltrating GBM tumors also exhibited high levels of PD-L2, which correlated with reduced immune cell retention. PD-L2 is expressed on human T cells upon activation and has been reported to downmodulate cell proliferation and cytokine production, consistent with functional impairment29. Although PD-L2 expression on tumor-infiltrating T cells has been reported in isolated cases, it is more commonly associated with myeloid cells. The observed correlation between PD-L2 expression on T cells and tumor cells suggests shared regulation to microenvironmental cues, and PD-L2 expression is induced by CSF1 and IL-4, key drivers of macrophage polarization towards M2 phenotype30. Shared PD-L2 expression may also reflect local acquisition, potentially via small extracellular vesicles31 or direct cell–cell contact, as has been described for PD-L132. Such transfer may drive T cell apoptosis, or functional suppression through PD-1 engagement, further underscored by higher PD-1 binding affinity for PD-L2 compared with PD-L133,34. Importantly, our functional experiments show that both PD-L1 and PD-L2 can suppress T cell activation via PD-1 engagement, and that this suppression is effectively reversed by nivolumab. These findings suggest that PD-L2, although not routinely assessed in clinical practice, may play a critical role in immune evasion in GBM and potentially other tumor types with high PD-L2 expression.

The clinical importance of PD-L2 expression with response to therapy remains to be investigated, and the precise contribution of PD-L2 to immune evasion in GBM will require future studies using patient-matched co-culture systems with PD-L2 blocking antibodies or in vivo models. Nonetheless, these data provide a strong rationale to consider PD-L2 as a candidate biomarker and therapeutic target in GBM and support continued efforts to dissect and therapeutically modulate the immune landscape of this aggressive tumor.

Collectively, these findings highlight both tumor-intrinsic and immune-related features of GBM that may contribute to the limited clinical efficacy of ICIs. Impaired antigen presentation, a predominance of immunosuppressive myeloid cells that appear to impede T cell infiltration, and high expression of PD-L2 converge to create an environment that limits effective T cell-mediated tumor clearance. Nevertheless, the presence of antigen-experienced, tissue-resident CD8⁺ T cells in a subset of GBMs indicates that immune suppression is not absolute. These insights support the development of rational therapeutic strategies that combine myeloid-targeting agents, enhancers of antigen presentation, or checkpoint inhibitors that also block PD-L2, offering new avenues to improve immunotherapy outcomes in GBM.

Methods

Patients and treatment

The study includes 60 patients with glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, CNS (central nervous system) WHO (World Health Organisation) Grade 4 (GBM)—either at first occurrence (“primary”) or at recurrence—classified according to the 2021 WHO Classification System35. Tissue specimens were obtained from 21 patients who underwent tumor resection at Macquarie University Hospital (Table S1). Cavitron ultrasonic surgical aspirate (CUSA) samples were collected and viable cell populations cryopreserved from 39 patients at Royal Prince Alfred Hospital and Chris O’Brien Lifehouse (Table S2). Among the 21 patients from Macquarie University Hospital, the median age was 72 years (range 33–84) with a predominance of male patients (76%). The majority of GBMs (86%) had conventional histological appearances, while the remaining 14% showed gliosarcoma morphology, a histological subtype of GBM. MGMT promoter methylation was detected in 43% of cases. Most tumors (91%) were primary, while 9.5% were recurrences. CUSA samples were derived from 39 patients with similar clinical characteristics. The median age was 65 years (range 31–85), with 75% male predominance. GBM accounted for 97% of cases, and MGMT promoter methylation was present in 23%. Primary tumors made up 82% of this cohort, while 18% were recurrent cases.

In addition to the GBM cohorts, this study included 38 patients with brain metastases from cutaneous melanoma (MBM; Table S3). The median age at the time of the earliest MBM biopsy was 60 years (range 29–84), and 76% were male (Table S3). At initial melanoma diagnosis, most patients presented with early-stage disease (stage I/II: 79%), and the most common histological subtype of the primary tumor was superficial spreading (47%), followed by nodular melanoma (29%). MBM specimens were categorized based on treatment timing. Samples were classified as ‘ON-treatment’ if obtained during systemic therapy or within three weeks of drug cessation. This allowed for drug clearance, which is particularly relevant for therapies with long half-life, such as ICIs36. MBM tissue specimens collected more than three weeks after the last treatment dose, or from patients with no prior therapy, were classified as ‘OFF-treatment’. Based on this definition, 21 of 49 MBM specimens (43%) were OFF-treatment, while the remaining 28 MBMs (57%) were ‘ON-treatment’ specimens. The ‘ON-treatment’ samples were collected while patients were receiving anti-PD-1 therapy (pembrolizumab 2 mg/kg every 3 weeks or nivolumab 3 mg/kg every 2 weeks), either as monotherapy (18%) or in combination with anti-CTLA4 and/or BRAF/MEK inhibitors (27%).

Biospecimen samples and clinical data were acquired with informed consent from the Sydney Brain Tumour Bank (2019/ETH08929), the Melanoma Biospecimen Tissue Bank (HREC/11/RPAH/444), and the Macquarie University Cancer Biobank (HREC2793). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients gave written informed consent to participate in the study. Patient demographics and clinicopathologic features, including age, sex, histological subtype, mutation status, disease sites and stage were collected (Tables S1–3).

Tissue processing, cell isolation, and culture

Tumor surgical specimens collected from patients affected by GBM or MBM, were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using the human tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotech, Cat# 130-095-929) and gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Single-cell suspensions were viably frozen as previously described12. CUSA GBM samples were strained to remove tissue fragments and centrifuged (450 × g; 10 min, 4 °C). CUSA cell pellets were resuspended in an erythrocyte lysis buffer (Qiagen, Cat# 79217) for 10 min, washed in cold PBS (400 × g; 10 min; 4 °C) and viably frozen in 10% (v/v) DMSO in RPMI medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# R0883).

Five short-term GBM cell cultures were isolated from single-cell dissociate suspensions. An additional 12 GBM cell lines were generated and characterized as part of the Q-Cell project (QIMR Berghofer Medical Institute, Australia)19. GBM cells were cultured as adherent monolayers on matrigel basement membrane (Corning, Cat# CLS354234)-coated vessels in GBM medium comprising KnockOut DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, Cat# 12660-012) supplemented with 10 ng/ml FGF-basic (Gibco, Cat# PHG0024), 10 ng/ml EGF (Gibco, Cat# PHG0314) and StemPro Neural Supplement (Gibco, Cat# A10508-01), and containing 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Gibco, Cat# 15140-122) and 50 µg/ml Gentamicin (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# G1397). Eight MBM cell lines were generated as previously described17, derived from tumor biopsies of melanoma patients who progressed on ICIs. MBM cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Life Technologies, Cat# 10099141) and 4 mM L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 59202C).

Flow cytometry

Cryopreserved GBM tumor dissociates and CUSA cells were thawed in GBM medium (see above) containing 20 µg/ml DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# D4513). Cryopreserved MBM tumor dissociates were thawed in RPMI/10% FBS containing 20 µg/ml DNase I. Dissociates were washed in PBS prior to staining.

Dissociates were incubated with Live dead near-IR fixable dye and Fc-block for 20 min. Antibody staining of all samples was performed in flow cytometry buffer (PBS supplemented with 5% FBS, 10 mM EDTA, and 0.05% sodium azide). Cells were incubated on ice for 30 min with fluorescently labeled monoclonal antibodies (Table S4), except that AlexaFluor700-labeled antibody against HLA-ABC was added in a separate step to avoid the potential cross-reactivity with the anti-PD-L1 antibody. After extracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized with the Fixation/Permeabilization buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 00-5523-00) and stained with antibodies against intracellular targets and Fc block in the permeabilization buffer. Cells were washed extensively prior to acquisition on the 5-laser LSR Fortessa X20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

For flow cytometric profiling of GBM and MBM cell lines following IFNγ treatment, cells plated at 1 × 105/well in 6-well plate were treated the next day with 1000 U/ml IFNγ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 300-02) or vehicle control (0.1% BSA, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A4737, in PBS) for 24 h. Cells were collected and incubated for 30 min on ice with mouse anti-human antibodies (Table S4) in flow cytometry buffer. Cell viability was determined using 5 µM DAPI. Samples were acquired on the LSR Fortessa X-20 as described above.

FlowJo v10.10 software (BD, RRID:SCR_008520) was used for data analyses. General gating strategy included forward scatter and side scatter to exclude debris, time parameter to exclude electronic noise, forward scatter and height to exclude doublets and gating on viable cells (Fig. S1). To ensure consistency across experiments, a control sample (MMK-1 GBM cell line positive for all five GBM markers19, mixed with control PBMCs) was included with each experiment (Fig. S1c). Specific gating strategies are summarized in Table S5.

Histopathology

GFAP IHC was performed on CUSA FFPE tissue sections using a Leica BOND-MAX automated stainer (Leica Biosystems). Sections were deparaffinised on instrument, followed by antigen retrieval using BOND Enzyme 1 for 10 min. Slides were incubated with polyclonal rabbit anti-GFAP antibody (Agilent/Dako; Cat# Z0334) diluted 1:1000 for 20 min. Detection was performed using the Leica BOND polymer-based detection system with DAB chromogen, and sections were counterstained with haematoxylin.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

FFPE sections of GBM were stained using the COMET system (Lunaphore Technologies). Deparaffinization and antigen retrieval were performed using the Epredia Lab Vision PT module instrument in Dewax and HIER Buffer H pH 9 (Epredia) for 1 h at 102 °C. Slides were incubated in multistaining buffer (MSB, BU06, Lunaphore) and loaded onto the COMET instrument using the COMET chip (MK03, Lunaphore). Each tissue section was sequentially stained with primary and secondary antibodies (all antibodies diluted in MSB; clone, dilution and incubation times listed in Table S6) with imaging after each cycle. Antibodies were eluted between cycles using the Elution buffer (BU07, Lunaphore). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000, Lunaphore Technologies). Images were acquired at 20× magnification within a 12.5 × 12.5 mm imaging area.

A multi-layer OME-TIFF image was generated for each tissue section and imported into the Horizon software (Lunaphore) for background subtraction, before export for downstream analysis in HALO and HALO AI image analysis software (v4.2.6399.90, Indica Labs, NM; RRID:SCR_018350). For image analyses, tissue regions were selected to exclude artefacts and areas of high autofluorescence. Nuclear detection was performed using the Nuclei Seg V2 (HALO AI) FL module v1.0.0 and membrane detection performed using Membrane Seg V1 (HALO AI) FL module v1.0.0. with cytoplasm radius set to 3. Quantification was performed using the HALO Highplex FL module v5.2.2. Marker-specific fluorescence intensity thresholds were set manually to define cell types of interest and adjusted for each tissue sample to account for staining variability. Cell phenotypes including GBM tumor cells, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, CD163- M1-like and CD163+ M2-like macrophages and CD163-TMEM119+ microglia cells were defined using canonical lineage markers (Table S7). Object metadata (counts, x and y coordinates, marker intensity) were exported for analysis in Prism v10 (GraphPad). Nearest neighbor analysis was performed by calculating the distance between each GFAP⁺ tumor cell and its nearest immune cell of a given subtype (CD163-TMEM119+ microglia, CD163- macrophage, CD163+ macrophage, CD8⁺ T cell, or CD4⁺ T cell) within each sample. For each cell population, distances were summarised at the sample level by calculating the median distance of each immune cell type to tumor cell, and statistics are reported as the median distance and interquartile range (IQR) across the nine GBM samples.

T cell reporter platform

T cell activation was measured using a T cell reporter platform comprising Jurkat E6.1T cells constitutively expressing the PD-1 receptor and a transcriptional NF-kB::eGFP reporter, and T cell stimulator cells (TCS) expressing CD3 agonist with or without CD86 co-stimulator, PD-L1 or PD-L221,22. Cell lines were maintained in the RPMI medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 4 mM glutamine and 20 mM HEPES. All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free. For T-cell activation, 5 × 104 Jurkat E6.1 cells were co-cultured with 5 × 103 TCS cells for 24 h. Cells were then stained with BUV395-labeled anti-mouse CD45 to exclude TCS cells and 5 μM DAPI to exclude dead cells. Cells were analyzed by flow cytometry to measure T-cell GFP fluorescence.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Principal component analysis was performed with Clustvis (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis; RRID:SCR_017133). Heatmap visualization and clustering analysis were performed with Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus; RRID:SCR_017386). For all other analyses, Prism v10.6.1 (GraphPad; RRID:SCR_002798) was used. Values were considered significant if p < 0.05. Statistical analyses are specified in the respective Figure legends.
