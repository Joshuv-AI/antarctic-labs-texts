Assessing the performance, egg quality, serum analysis, heavy metals and essential trace metals accumulation in laying hen eggs and tissues fed black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) larvae meal
Authors: Samiullah Khan, Xiaoli Shi, Renlian Cai, Shuai Zhao, Xialin Li, Ibrar Muhammad Khan, Zhiyong Yin, Hongpei Lu, Mian Gul Hilal, Ren Yi, Yonggui Wu, Jianjun Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlack soldier fly (BSF) larvae convert wastes into protein, playing a vital role in addressing the challenge of sustainable poultry production. These larvae accumulate toxic substances, posing a risk to feed and food safety. This study investigates the effects of substituting soybean meal with different levels of BSF larvae meal on laying performance, egg quality, serum analysis, and the deposition of various metals in eggs, meat, and excreta. A total of 1,008 Lohmann hens (age 48 wk) were randomly assigned to 4 treatments. The treatments consisted of corn-soybean meal (CK) diet replaced with 7% (BSF7), 14% (BSF14), and 21% (BSF21) BSF larvae meal. Each treatment consisted of 6 replicates with 42 hens each and the trial lasted for 8 wk. Dietary BSF larvae meal treatments increased (linear, P < 0.05) the laying rate (1.52 to 1.95%) and decreased (linear, P < 0.01) the feed intake (3.64–3.86 g) during the entire experiment. During 48 to 52 wk, egg weight was decreased (P < 0.001) 0.93 g for the BSF21 group compared to CK group, however, during 52-56 wk, no differences in egg weight were observed among treatments. The addition of BSF larvae meal enhanced the eggshell strength (linear, P < 0.05), with no effect on the albumen height and yolk weight (P > 0.05). Low transfer of arsenic, lead, and cadmium concentration was observed in the egg yolk and egg white across different treatments (P > 0.05). Conversely, the concentrations of these metals and iron showed an increase, while Zinc exhibited a decrease in excreta as the dietary intake of BSF larvae meal increased. The chromium and iron increased linearly (P < 0.001) in the egg white with the inclusion of BSF larvae meal. Egg white iron and chromium showed a strong positive correlation with the dietary zinc and copper. Taken together, BSF larvae can replace soybean meal completely in laying diet, however, careful attention requires to elevated metal levels in diet and excreta.Key words: Black soldier fly larvae meal, performance, serum profile, heavy metal transfer, laying hen

INTRODUCTION

Several countries heavily rely on soybean imports, prompting the need for a new protein-rich feed ingredient for sustainable animal production (Hartinger et al., 2022). Insect biomass emerged as an eco-friendly feed choice to tackle these issues, offering a valuable protein source for livestock husbandry (Kierończyk et al., 2022; Malematja et al., 2023). In 2021, the European Commission authorized the utilization of processed insect biomass as feed for chickens and pigs, aiming to ensure food supply stability and strengthen the sustainability of food systems (Schäfer et al., 2023). One strategy to improve sustainability in the animal farming operations involves the incorporation of black soldier fly (BSF) larvae meal into the animal feed (Ewald et al., 2020; de Souza Vilela et al., 2021).

BSF larvae meal are rich in protein (40–53%), fats (18–33%) and minerals such as calcium offers a potential alternative feed ingredient (Patterson et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2022). The incorporation of BSF larvae offers numerous health advantages due to the presence of lauric acid, which accounts for about 64% of the overall fatty acid composition (Zulkifli et al., 2022). For example, a substantial reduction was seen by adding 0.03% lauric acid in Escherichia coli and total bacterial counts that ultimately improved intestinal health and chicken performance (Fortuoso et al., 2019). Significant attention has been given to the use of BSF larvae as a substitute to soybean meal in poultry feed (Patterson et al., 2021; Heuel et al., 2023). Substituting 15 to 16% of BSF larvae meal for soybean meal showed no negative effects on egg-laying rates (Mwaniki et al., 2020; Patterson et al., 2021; Zawisza et al., 2023). In egg-laying hens, a complete replacement of soybean meal with BSF larvae meal resulted in decreased feed intake and egg weight (Marono et al., 2017; Patterson et al., 2021). Feeding hens a diet containing 5% BSF larvae meal resulted in a decrease in the laying rate (Mwaniki et al., 2018). There may be several reasons for these discrepancies such as the bird's age, short experimental period, chitin in the exoskeleton causing low digestibility, or substrate source feeding to BSF larvae (Marono et al., 2017; Bejaei and Cheng, 2020).

Black soldier fly larvae can accumulate toxic substances, such as heavy metals, when reared on organic waste substrates, posing a significant feed and food safety risk (Zhang et al., 2021). Consumer acceptance may become a concern if levels of toxic substances in meat and eggs from chickens fed with BSF larvae surpass a specific threshold (Bessa et al., 2021; Khaemba et al., 2022; Heuel et al., 2023). To confirm the utilization of insects as feed for poultry, it is imperative to thoroughly analyze and validate them prior to implementation (Malematja et al., 2023). These heavy metals cause toxicity inside the body, even though they do not have any biological roles, and disturbs proper functioning of other essential minerals (Jaishankar et al., 2014). Cadmium, which is highly accumulative in BSF larvae (Bessa et al., 2021), if exceeds the maximum level recommended in food, causing severe health concerns. Lead damages erythrocytes and hepatocytes, decreases resistance to diseases, impairs bone structure and disrupts the regulation of other minerals (Cunningham et al., 2001). Therefore, understanding transfer of these metals from BSF larvae meal to poultry products is important for ensuring nutritional safety.

This study evaluated the effect of incorporating BSF larvae meal on the laying performance, egg quality, serum analysis, heavy metals and essential trace metals in various organ tissues, eggs and excreta. Feeding BSF larval meal to laying birds has been the focus of several studies. However, to the best of our knowledge, there has not been any study conducted on the effects of feeding BSF larval meal to a large number of the flock to assess the performance and metal levels in tissues and eggs.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Experiments on laying chicken used in this study were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Guizhou University, Guiyang, China (No. EAE-GZU-2023-T095).

Birds, Housing and Experimental Diets

A total of 1,008 Lohmann laying hens (age 48 wk), were randomly distributed into 4 treatments with 6 replicates per treatment and 42 birds per replicate. BSF larvae meal of same batch was purchased from Sichuan Qianlang Ecological Agriculture Co., Ltd, Chengdu, China. In the CK group, the laying hens were provided with a basal diet formulated with corn-soybean meal. Soybean meal in the CK was replaced with BSF larvae meal at levels of 7, 14, and 21%, identified as BSF7, BSF14, and BSF21, respectively. After a 7-d acclimation period, all hens were fed their respective diets for a period of 8 wk. The nutrient, amino acid, and mineral content of the BSF larvae meal is provided in Table 1. The composition of the experimental diets, including ingredients, chemical composition, and heavy metal content, is presented in Table 2. Feed and water were provided ad libitum during the entire experimental period. The lighting schedule was maintained for 16 h from 06:00 to 22:00. The experimental birds were kept under optimal and standard bioclimatic and welfare conditions.

Sample Collection and Analysis

At the end of the 8th wk of the study, birds with uniform weight were selected and blood samples were taken and stored at -20°C for serum biochemical analysis. The excreta, meat and liver samples were stored in liquid nitrogen for various metal analysis. Samples of BSF larval meal, soybean meal, experimental diets and eggs of the hen (n = 3 samples per dietary treatment) were collected and transported to the laboratory for analysis of various metals.

The analysis techniques for CP (GB/T 6432-2018), crude fat (EE; GB/T 6433-2006), crude fiber (CF; GB/T 6434-2022), crude ash (GB/T 23742-2009) and amino acids (GB/T 18246-2019) were used for the BSF larvae meal. Minerals including Zinc, iron and copper in the BSF larvae meal and diets heavy metals including arsenic, cadmium, lead and chromium in experimental diets were analyzed using inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Thermo Scientific iCAP 7000 Series ICP Spectrometer, USA; Supplementary Table 1). Calcium (GB/T 6436-2018), and Phosphorus (GB/T 6437-2018) in the BSF larvae meal and diet adhere to the corresponding China National Standards.

Performance of Laying Hen Fed BSF Larvae Meal Diet

Egg production and individual egg weight were recorded daily, and feed intake was recorded weekly for all treatments. The data obtained was used to calculate the egg production rate, mean egg weight and average daily feed intake. At the end of 52th and 56th week, eggs were randomly selected from all the replicates of treatments (n = 30 eggs/treatment) to measure the egg width and length (Deli, DL3944, Ningbo, China).

Egg Quality Measurement of Laying Hen Fed BSF Larvae Meal Diet

At the end of the experiment, 120 normal eggs (n = 30 eggs/treatment) were randomly selected for the evaluation of the egg quality characteristics. An eggshell strength test (EFG-0503, Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan) was used to determine the eggshell strength. An electronic Vernier caliper (Deli, DL3944, Ningbo, China) was used to measure the longitudinal and transverse dimensions of the eggs, as well as the thickness of the pointy, middle, and rounded shells. Each egg sample's absolute egg yolk weight was determined using an electronic balance (BL-320H, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Furthermore, the albumen height and Haugh unit were measured using an Egg Analyzer (EMT-5200, Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan).

Serum Biochemical Analysis of Hen Fed BSF Larvae Meal Diet

At the end of the experiment, hens of similar weight were selected from each treatment (n = 6 hens/treatment) and 5 mL of blood was collected and stored in blood collection tubes. The serum obtained after centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 15 to 30 min was stored at -20°C. The amylase (AM), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), azelaic transaminase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB) and triglycerides (TG) were measured by chemistry analyzer (BS-240 Vet, Mindray, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China).

Analytical Procedure for Essential Trace Minerals and Heavy Metals Analysis

Sample Preparation for Essential Trace Minerals and Heavy Metals in Meat, Eggs and Excreta

Fresh samples were washed with distilled water. Chicken breast and liver were cut into small pieces with a clean stainless steel knife. The eggs boiled were separated the egg white, egg yolk, and eggshell. The feed samples were thoroughly mixed using a 4-part method and set aside. The samples for soybean meal and BSF larvae meal were selected at 3 points for metal analysis. The prepared samples were placed in a freeze dryer (CTFD-12S-U, Qingdao Yonghe Chuangxin, China) for freeze-drying. Subsequently, they were ground using a fully automated sample homogenizer (JXFSTPRP-32L, Shanghai Jingxin, China), sieved through a 200-mesh screen, and stored at -20°C until analysis.

Samples Digestion for Minerals and Heavy Metals of Chicken Meat, Eggs and Excreta

Sample acid digestion was performed by slowly heating 1g of each homogenized sample in 6 mL of concentrated HNO3 (69%, analytical grade). The solution was evaporated to near dryness. Then, 2mL of H2O2 (30%, analytical grade) was used for further digestion of the pre-digested samples, followed by evaporation to near dryness to ensure that the residue after digestion contained no organic matter, as organic matter could act as impurities in metal analysis. The sample residues were filtered through a 0.45 μm aqueous filter membrane and diluted to a final volume of 25 mL. Matrix spikes were tested for all kind of samples, and recoveries of the heavy metals and essential trace minerals were obtained. Recoveries observed were within 85 to 105% via comparison of the sum of the fractions with total metal concentration (Supplementary Table 2).

The investigated metals including arsenic, cadmium, lead, chromium, copper, iron and Zinc in each sample were detected using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Thermo Scientific iCAP 7000 Series ICP Spectrometer, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of the experimental data was performed using SPSS 18.0 statistical software. The experimental data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). In cases where ANOVA indicated significant differences, the Tukey HSD test was applied to separate the treatment means. Polynomial contrasts were used to analyze linear and quadratic responses to evaluate the impact of dietary supplementation with varying levels of BSF larvae meal. Data were reported as means of 6 replicate samples along with their pooled standard errors (SEM). P value < 0.05 indicate a significant difference with different lower case letters. To differentiate the possible transfers of metals, redundancy analysis (RDA) and Pearson correlation coefficient was applied to analyze the relationship between metals transfer from BSF larvae based-diet to chicken meat, egg components, liver and excreta. The ordination plots illustrated the relationship between dietary metals content and egg components, organ tissues and excreta.

RESULTS

Laying Performance of Hens Fed BSF Larvae Meal

The effect of BSF larvae meal inclusion on the performance including laying rate, average egg weight, feed intake and egg size (length and width) during 48 to 52 and 52 to 56 wk are presented (Table 3). The laying rate of hens was linearly increased (P < 0.05) with the increase in BSF larvae meal incorporation during the entire experimental period. The feed intake reduced exhibiting a linear trend (P < 0.001) with the increased in the inclusion of BSF larvae meal during 48 to 52 wk, and 52 to 56 wk. During wk 48 to 52, the BSF21 group hens showed a decreased average egg weight compared to those in other groups (P < 0.001). However, no significant difference (P > 0.05, linear, P > 0.05) in the average egg weight was shown among treatments during 52 to 56 wk. During 48 to 52 wk, laying hens fed BSF21 group tended to decrease the egg length compared to those in CK group (P < 0.001; linear, P < 0.001), while no differences were observed in egg length and width among treatments during 52 to 56 wk (P > 0.05).

Heavy Metal Concentration in Laying Hens Tissues and Eggs Fed BSF Larvae Meal

The levels of arsenic, cadmium, lead, and chromium in the excreta, meat, eggs, and liver are shown in Tables 6, 7, 8, and 9, respectively. There were no effects of dietary treatments on the level arsenic content in meat tissues, egg yolk, egg white and eggshell (P > 0.05; Table 6). Laying hen fed on BSF21 group exhibited a linear rise (linear, P < 0.001) with maximum arsenic concentrations recorded for BSF21 (0.675 mg/kg), which was 2.23 fold higher than CK (0.302 mg/kg). The liver showed a decline pattern for arsenic contents with the increased BSF larvae meal in the dietary treatments (P < 0.001).

Lead concentrations in laying hen excreta rose linearly (linear, P < 0.001), with the maximum concentration (0.87 mg/kg) reported in the BSF21 group, which was 93.33% higher than the CK (0.45mg/kg) diet (Table 8). Interestingly, the lead contents in meat tissues showed a decreasing and then increasing trend (P < 0.001) with the highest concentration level observed for CK (2.0 mg/kg) followed by BSF21 (0.12 mg/kg) group. However, egg yolk, egg white and eggshell were unaffected and remained stable for the lead contents across all treatments (P > 0.05), suggesting that these metals may be less responsive.

The concentration of chromium in meat showed a linear increase (linear, P < 0.001) in the BSF21 group (1.99 mg/kg), reaching approximately twice the level compared to the CK (0.86 mg/kg) group (Table 9). Similar findings of rising levels of chromium contents were also shown for the egg white among the dietary regimens (P < 0.05). Orthogonal polynomial comparisons revealed a significant linear relationship between chromium levels and eggshell (P < 0.001). More specifically, the liver at BSF7 and the eggshell at BSF21 groups had higher levels of chromium.

DISCUSSION

The utilization of protein-rich biomass generated by black soldier fly larvae as a source of feed for chickens has received a lot of interest (Marono et al., 2017). Several studies showed the effect of substituting soybean meal with BSF larvae meal on the production performance of laying hens. BSF larvae grown on diverse organic waste materials, which are known to accumulate several toxic substances, including heavy metals. Therefore, understanding the fate of transfer of these contaminants in chicken products is essential. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of replacing soybean meal with BSF larvae meal on performance, egg quality, serum analysis, as well as the levels of trace metals and heavy metals in chicken meat, eggs (egg yolk, egg white, and eggshell), liver, and excreta.

The results showed that the addition of BSF larvae meal showed no negative effects on the performance and even improved laying rate of 1.16 to 1.53% for the BSF14 and BSF21 groups as compared to the CK group. Previous studies showed no negative effect on production performance by substituting 15 to 16% or even 24% of BSF larvae meal (Mwaniki et al., 2020; Patterson et al., 2021; Zawisza et al., 2023). A significant reduced feed intake was observed with the increase BSF larvae meal inclusion, which is line with the previous studies (Patterson et al., 2021; Marono et al., 2017). However, Maurer et al. (2016) reported no effect on feed intake with a high-quality meal (59% CP, 3.09 % lysine, and 0.98% methionine) at 12 and 24% of the diet replacing soybean cake. The discrepancies may be due to the duration of trail period, the age and breed of the birds, composition of BSF meal, or the limited number of laying hens.

Various dietary minerals, amino acids and other components such as chitin in the diet (Kawasaki et al., 2019; Park et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2022) can determine the egg quality of hen. The present study showed that the addition of BSF larvae meal in laying hen's feed increased eggshell strength and eggshell thickness. Previous studies also observed an increased eggshell thickness (Patterson et al., 2021) and eggshell strength (Kawasaki et al., 2019) by incorporating BSF larvae meal into the laying hen diet. Similarly, long-term replacement of BSF larvae meal for fishmeal has shown an increased eggshell strength (Zhao et al., 2022). Furthermore, our study observed an improved eggshell thickness; however, this difference was not significant. Mwaniki et al. (2018) reported an increased eggshell thickness following the inclusion of 5 to 7.5% BSF larvae meal in the hen's diet. An increased eggshell strength in eggs might possibly be an indicative of either improved calcium absorption and metabolism due to the breakdown of chitin in the gut of hens fed BSF larvae meal (Mwanaki et al., 2018; Ruhnke; Park et al., 2021). For instance, Marono et al. (2017) demonstrated that feeding hens (24–45 wk age) 17% BSF larvae meal resulted in higher circulating serum calcium compared to the control group with no BSF larvae meal, despite both diets were similar concentration of calcium. The dietary fiber nature of chitin and other bioactive compounds present in the BSF larvae resulting in a higher cecal production of butyric acid, which may further support gut health and optimize nutrient absorption, and thus better egg shell quality in laying birds fed BSF larvae meal (Cutrignelli et al., 2018). Other parameters such as Haugh units, albumen height, and egg yolk weight remained unaffected by the dietary treatments, in line with findings from prior research (Patterson et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2022).

The present study showed that incorporation of BSF larvae meal had no effect on the ALT, AST, TP, ALB and TG of hen's serum. However, amylase level significantly increased in the BSF7 group as compared to the laying hen fed on BSF14 and BSF21 group. Zawisza et al. (2023) also noticed a rise in serum amylase of hens fed with 5% of defatted BSF larvae meal, which subsequently fallen when the inclusion rate was increased to 10% and 15%. BSF larvae meal contain lauric acid and chitin, and their addition at low level may resulted in optimum amylase activity stimulation. In accordance with current research, lauric acid facilitated the breakdown of starch by enhancing the α-amylase activity in ruminants (Zhang et al., 2024). Similarly, the inclusion of 1% chitin in poultry feed has demonstrated a rise in plasma amylase activity (Borland, et a., 2024). The decline in amylase activity at high BSF larvae meal inclusion could be associated with decreased feed consumption because of the fibrous nature of chitin from black soldier fly larvae, resulting in reduced demand to induce initial enzyme production.

BSF larvae accumulate various toxic substances from their rearing substrate (Diener et al., 2015; Biancarosa et al., 2018; Heuel et al., 2023). The results of the current study showed a minimal transfer for the arsenic, lead and cadmium contents from the BSF larvae based meal to the laying hen egg yolk and egg white. However, the concentration of heavy metals increased in excreta, with the increase supplementation of BSF larvae meal. An effective detoxification and excretion system is necessary to avoid the accumulation of heavy metals (Alotaibi et al., 2021). These metals such as cadmium and lead, which are toxic, have the ability to disrupt important biological processes in organisms, leading to tissue damage, oxidative stress, and disturbances in enzyme functions (Jaishankar et al., 2014). Various biological pathways such as metallothioneins metal-binding proteins expressed in all organisms to counter the effects of these metals. The elimination of these heavy metals through excreta is an essential approach to reduce their overall load in the organism's body (Brugger and Windisch, 2015).

The levels of these heavy metals enhanced with the increased in BSF larvae meal into the diet as compared to the control diet. The cadmium concentration in eggs ranged from 0.010 to 0.015 mg per kg (Atique et al., 2022, Samad et al., 2023), which is almost similar to the present study. The European commission (EC), Chinese National Standards (GB 2762-2022) has established a limit level for cadmium in liver of 0.5 mg/kg, and a comparable maximum level for lead in meat of 0.2 mg/kg. If these values are exceeded, then affected tissue cannot be marketable. In our study, both lead and cadmium in chicken meat did not cross the permissible level. Similar results were also shown in a study conducted by Elsharawy and Elsharawy (2015), who detected cadmium and lead in meat and other organs from the local slaughterhouse.

The chromium content in meat increased with the addition of BSF larvae meal, from 0.86 mg/kg in the CK group to 1.99 mg/kg in the BSF21 group. Previous studies reported similar findings, with meat samples containing 1.4 mg/kg (Ahmed et al., 2015) and 2.17 mg/kg (Shaheen et al., 2016). The recent findings indicate that laying hens fed BSF larval feed may collect heavy metals from their rearing substrate, yet their eggs and meat remain suitable for consumption. However, it is critical to assess these heavy metals before incorporating BSF larvae into the chicken diet especially if these larvae grow on contaminated substrate.

This study showed a decreased Zinc content in the excreta, which is similar to the results obtained by previous studies (Zhu et al., 2022, Sun et al., 2023). Sun et al. (2023) showed that the inclusion of 130 mg/kg or even 150 mg/kg of zinc into the broiler diet optimizes intestinal zinc absorption, leading to a reduction in zinc excretion as compared to control group. Organic Zinc could reduce negative environmental impact by decreasing the excretion of Zinc in the manure (Zhu et al., 2022). Maintaining physiological balance in organisms depends on the homeostatic control of essential metals such as Zinc and iron. Through a complex network of transporters, binding proteins, transport peptides and storage mechanisms, the body regulate the concentrations of these metals either through absorption from the gastrointestinal tract or elimination processes through excretion (Brugger and Windisch, 2015). For instance, zinc and copper levels are closely managed by metallothioneins and binding proteins, which sequester these metals in surplus and release them as necessary (Kambe et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2022). Fe levels are governed by hepcidin, a hormone that modulates iron absorption and recycling based on the body's requirements (Camaschella et al., 2020). The intricate balance of these metals is indispensable for various enzymatic reactions and immune responses (Prasad, 2013).

In our study, Zinc accumulation in the egg yolk was highly positively correlated with the chromium, cadmium, and lead in the feed. The accumulation of iron and chromium in muscle were highly related is highly correlated to their dietary intake. Iron contents in broiler chicken organs has shown a highly positively correlation to their dietary intake (Shahla et al., 2012). The accumulation of arsenic in muscle only has a strong correlation with feed arsenic and lead. The contaminant level of arsenic in the diet of fish has shown a positive association with the arsenic in fish muscles (Khellaf et al., 2023). Our results showed that arsenic, cadmium, chromium, lead and Fe increased in the excreta of laying hens. However, when compared to all the heavy metals, Zinc and iron are higher than other metals. Higher levels of metals including copper, chromium, cadmium, lead, and arsenic were observed in the manure of swine and were much higher levels than those in feed (Cang et al., 2004). As a result, sufficient precautions are necessary to be taken during substrate selection for growing BSF larvae to provide food for long-term animal production.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that dietary incorporation of BSF larvae meal in a laying hen diet has no negative effects on the laying rate and egg weight. Reduced feed intake and an increased eggshell strength were observed with the increase in the incorporation of BSF larvae meal into laying hen diet. Further studies are needed to explore the underlying mechanism of the observed increase in egg shell strength, with a particular emphasis on calcium absorption and metabolism. In addition, low transfer with limited variation for cadmium, lead, arsenic and chromium for the meat and egg content among the dietary treatments was observed. Iron content increased in the egg white and excreta of the laying hens with the increase in the BSF larvae meal. The incorporation of BSF larvae tended to decrease the zinc content in the excreta showed optimum intestinal absorption. Therefore, the inclusion of BSF larvae meal in the diet of laying hens has no adverse impact on the quality of eggs and meat, making it a safe ingredient.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

These results are primarily relevant to conditions involving an 8-week BSF larvae meal-feeding regimen to aged laying hens. It is possible that raising BSF on other known substrates will reduce the detected contamination levels. However, further exploring those dietary differences and expanding the research to broiler birds (from the day started to the finisher period) are critical for a complete understanding of the fate of the transfer of heavy metals. For assessing the baseline accumulation of heavy metals, it is also necessary to pre-evaluate birds or pick laying starting hens before the experiment. This would provide a more accurate assessment of heavy metal accumulation and transfer pathways. Future research should quantitatively monitor the transfer of contaminants from the diets fed to BSF larvae to the chickens in order to provide a thorough picture of the transfer of contaminants from BSF larvae meal along the food chain. This approach will shed light on the dynamics and processes of heavy metal transfer in the food chain.
