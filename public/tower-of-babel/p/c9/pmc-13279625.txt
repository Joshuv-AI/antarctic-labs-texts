Interhemispheric Metabolic Effects of Transcranial Infrared Laser Stimulation of the Prefrontal Cortex
Authors: Patrick O'Connor, Turner Lime, Douglas W Barrett, Li‐da Huang, F Gonzalez‐Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhotobiomodulation is the use of low‐power, non‐ionizing light to stimulate cellular processes. Transcranial infrared laser stimulation (TILS) is a method for prefrontal photobiomodulation that produces dose‐dependent metabolic effects in vivo. However, interhemispheric differences in metabolic responses to unilateral right versus left TILS are unknown. Investigating interhemispheric effects of TILS is important because right–left differences are observed with other forms of transcranial stimulation, including magnetic and electrical stimulation. TILS penetration was first characterized using Monte Carlo simulations of photon propagation in the Colin27 MRI‐based brain model. Simulations confirmed that 1%–5% photon penetration was confined unilaterally to the stimulated side of the prefrontal cortex. Eighty‐one healthy adults were then randomized to receive unilateral right TILS, unilateral left TILS, or sham stimulation (laser off). Active TILS parameters were selected based on prior studies demonstrating metabolic efficacy (1064 nm continuous wave, 250 mW/cm2 irradiance, 440 s exposure, 110 J/cm2 fluence, and 1496 J total energy). Bilateral changes in cytochrome‐c‐oxidase (CCO), oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO), and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HHb) were measured with in vivo spectroscopy before, during, and after unilateral TILS or sham. No adverse effects were reported, and no significant metabolic or hemodynamic changes were observed in the sham condition. Unilateral TILS produced ipsilateral (TILS side) increases in CCO and HbO that were similar regardless of whether the right or left prefrontal cortex was stimulated. In contrast, contralateral (opposite side) metabolic responses differed by stimulation side. Contralateral CCO effects emerged later than ipsilateral effects and were greater in the right hemisphere following TILS to the left. This study is the first to demonstrate an interhemispheric difference in the human brain response to photobiomodulation, specifically contralateral CCO effects. Although ipsilateral metabolic effects were similar for right and left prefrontal stimulation, contralateral responses exhibited hemispheric asymmetry. It is possible that these interhemispheric differences may contribute to the different cognitive and emotional effects of right versus left transcranial photobiomodulation.Keywords: cytochrome‐c‐oxidase, hemispheric lateralization, oxygenated hemoglobin, photobiomodulation, transcranial infrared penetration

Introduction

This study employed transcranial photobiomodulation (tPBM), which provides a noninvasive method to deliver red‐to‐near infrared photons to the cerebral cortex. tPBM involves the transcranial application of low‐level light in the red to near‐infrared spectrum (600–1100 nm) to stimulate cellular function, typically through the absorption of photons by mitochondrial chromophores. For detailed reviews of tPBM mechanisms and applications, see (Rojas 2011; Gonzalez‐Lima 2021; Frankowski et al. 2025). A specific application of tPBM is transcranial infrared laser stimulation (TILS) of the human prefrontal cortex (PFC) to neuromodulate cognitive and emotional functions, which our group introduced in 2013 (Barrett and Gonzalez‐Lima 2013; Wang et al. 2024). Over the past twelve years, we have shown that TILS of the PFC induces a variety of beneficial neurological and psychological effects without side effects or contraindications (Gonzalez‐Lima 2021; Barrett and Gonzalez‐Lima 2013; Rojas and Gonzalez‐Lima 2013; O'Donnell et al. 2022; Zaizar et al. 2023; Barrett, Davis, et al. 2025; Barrett, Beevers, and Gonzalez‐Lima 2025). TILS delivers directional low‐irradiance and high‐fluence monochromatic infrared light at 1064 nm to the forehead. A small proportion of this light penetrates the forehead and is absorbed by mitochondria‐rich cortical tissue, resulting in increased cerebral energy metabolism and hemodynamics, among other previously described effects (Frankowski et al. 2025; Barrett and Gonzalez‐Lima 2013; Gonzalez‐Lima and Barrett 2014; Tedford et al. 2015; Tian et al. 2020; Wang, Tian, et al. 2017).

Specifically, in the case of TILS to the human forehead, there is consistent evidence for photo‐oxidation of mitochondrial respiratory enzymes and increased oxygenated hemoglobin in the PFC. Six recent studies have confirmed the ability of broadband near‐infrared spectroscopy (bbNIRS) to non‐invasively quantify in vivo concentration changes of oxidized cytochrome‐c‐oxidase (CCO), oxyhemoglobin (HbO), and deoxyhemoglobin (HHb) in human cerebral cortex in response to TILS (Wang, Tian, et al. 2017; O'Donnell et al. 2023; Pruitt et al. 2020, 2022; Saucedo et al. 2021), and these effects were not due to skin heating (Wang, Reddy, et al. 2017). In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that subjects are unable to reliably discern whether they received TILS or sham to the forehead (Barrett and Gonzalez‐Lima 2013; Zaizar et al. 2023). This indicates that a perceptual difference is unlikely to account for TILS effects in the PFC.

Of particular interest is the effect of TILS on the metabolic enzyme CCO, the terminal rate‐limiting enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, which receives the oxygen carried by HbO and catalyzes oxygen consumption coupled with ATP production (Cardoso et al. 2022; Eells et al. 2004; Wong‐Riley et al. 2005; Wong‐Riley 2026). However, all the previous bbNIRS‐TILS studies were limited to only demonstrating CCO effects on the stimulated side (ipsilateral side). There are no bbNIRS‐TILS studies of right versus left side stimulation investigating CCO concentration changes in the side opposite to the stimulated hemisphere (contralateral side). Investigating these interhemispheric response differences is important both for advancing mechanistic understanding of tPBM and for informing stimulation parameters in future studies, particularly given that lateralized effects have been well documented with other forms of transcranial stimulation.

Several anatomical features of the cerebral hemispheres provide a basis for interhemispheric effects of lateralized stimulation. The cerebral hemispheres of the human brain differ in their anatomical characteristics and connections, and these differences give rise to functional hemispheric asymmetries. For example, the right cerebral cortex has a smaller number of inhibitory interneurons as compared to the left cortex (Rosen et al. 1993; Rosen 1996; Schenker et al. 2007). This alters the excitatory‐inhibitory balance and may result in greater interhemispheric network excitation in the right cortex relative to the left. Interhemispheric communication permits transfer between one hemisphere and the other side through direct connections, primarily via the corpus callosum (Gazzaniga 2000). Therefore, interhemispheric communication permits that stimulation of one side (ipsilateral side) may result in changes in the opposite side to the stimulation (contralateral side).

Understanding lateralized effects of brain stimulation is important because right versus left hemispheric differences in neuromodulation of emotional and cognitive functions have been found with transcranial magnetic and electrical stimulation of the human brain (Cobb et al. 2021; Gibson et al. 2022). Similarly, TILS shows different emotional and cognitive effects depending on the hemisphere stimulated. For example, TILS to the right, but not to the left, PFC reduces depression symptoms (Disner et al. 2016) and enhances working memory performance (Zhao et al. 2022). However, whether there are different PFC metabolic responses to right versus left tPBM has not been demonstrated before.

Therefore, this study had three aims: (1) to model TILS light penetration in a standard brain atlas to verify that photon propagation was confined to the stimulated hemisphere, (2) to compare ipsilateral metabolic effects of right versus left prefrontal TILS, and (3) to test whether contralateral metabolic responses differ by stimulation side. We found that TILS penetration was confined ipsilaterally, that ipsilateral CCO and HbO increases were similar for right and left stimulation, and that contralateral CCO responses showed hemispheric asymmetry, emerging later than ipsilateral effects and only in the right hemisphere following left‐sided TILS. These contralateral differences are interpreted below in the context of known hemispheric asymmetries in cortical anatomy and interhemispheric connectivity.

Materials and Methods

Simulation of Transcranial Light Penetration

To test the prediction for contralateral stimulation effects, a simulation of the density of light penetration was required to estimate how much light would reach the underlying tissues. The Colin27 MRI model is a widely used standard digital human neuroanatomical atlas with high quality (Holmes et al. 1998). The Colin27 template was adopted in this simulation and processed using the same platform and parameters described in detail by Huang et al. (Huang et al. 2021). Briefly, five types of tissue including scalp, skull, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), gray matter (GM), and white matter (WM) were registered. Optical parameters included the absorption coefficient (μa) and the reduced scattering coefficient (μ′s) that are needed to perform Monte Carlo simulations of photon transport based on ex vivo or in vivo intra‐operative measurements. The specific geometric and optical parameters used in our modeling of the multilayer structure are the same as those specified in figure 1 of Huang et al. (2021). We used an open‐source software package, the Monte Carlo Extreme (MCX), to track the photon propagation trajectory in 3D head models and recorded the photon power absorbed in each voxel. There was a total of 65 sets of optical parameters used in MCX simulations. Two hundred million photons were used for each simulation condition to obtain stable results.

Our collimated top‐hat laser beam used for TILS was implemented by the uniform disk source of MCX. The 4‐cm diameter of the laser disk source was readily assigned in MCX and the direction of irradiation was set to be perpendicular to the scalp surface at the frontal polar position. The output of MCX simulations was the absorbed photon weight in each voxel. Since the total number of launched photons could represent the total optical power or energy of the source, the power or energy absorbed in each voxel of the head model could be readily obtained. The power delivered to the GM was quantified by summing the photon power density (fluence) absorbed in all voxels of GM and dividing the sum by μa of GM to obtain the volume‐integrated fluence rate in the GM at 1064 nm on the Colin27 model of the human brain.

The strength of this modeling procedure was that our results are directly comparable to the previous model done by Huang et al. (2021), which used MRIs from six different individuals. However, the model presented in this paper applied exactly the same modeling procedures to a combined brain model, the Colin27 MRI model, which represents the average of 27 MRI scans and is widely used as the standard digital human brain atlas.

Study Participants and Safety

Adult participants were recruited among healthy young undergraduates at the University of Texas at Austin (UT). Procedures were approved by the UT Institutional Review Board and complied with U.S. National Institute of Health (NIH) and Declaration of Helsinki guidelines on human research. Written informed consent was obtained prior to starting the experiment. Participants were prescreened with health questionnaires and met the following inclusionary criteria: (1) 18 years of age or older, (2) not pregnant, (3) no prior TILS history. Beyond these criteria, participants were not excluded on the basis of neurological, psychiatric, or medical history. This decision was made to maximize both recruitment feasibility and generalizability, with random assignment to conditions serving to distribute potential confounding variables across groups.

Laser operators completed UT laser safety training, and the standard operating procedure was approved by UT Laser Safety Office. Protective eyewear was worn by both participants and researchers during TILS/sham. Participants also closed their eyes during TILS/sham. However, there is no concern about eye safety and visual function from this TILS to the forehead, because previous TILS studies confirmed the safety of this procedure, using visual signal detection tasks and visual memory tasks (Barrett and Gonzalez‐Lima 2013; Zhao et al. 2022; Holmes et al. 2019).

A statistical power analysis was conducted for sample size estimation, using alpha = 0.05 and power = 0.80 based on previous TILS studies (Wang, Tian, et al. 2017; O'Donnell et al. 2023; Pruitt et al. 2020, 2022; Saucedo et al. 2021). Data were analyzed for 81 subjects (53.1% female; Mean = 19.76 years old, SD = 1.39).

Adverse events were assessed via verbal inquiry immediately following the experimental session. Participants were also provided a copy of the informed consent document with PI contact information and were encouraged to reach out with any additional questions or concerns. No adverse effects were reported by any participant.

Experimental Design

Using a single‐blind sham‐controlled design, participants were randomized to condition using block randomization stratified by sex. Randomization occurred after consent on the day of testing. When sex is evenly distributed across conditions, any observed effects are more likely to be due to the manipulation of the independent variable. Subjects were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: (1) TILS to left PFC (n = 19), (2) TILS to right PFC (n = 21), (3) sham to left PFC (n = 20), or (4) sham to right PFC (n = 21). TILS groups had an invisible infrared laser at 1064 nm turned on, while sham groups had this infrared laser turned off. Procedures were otherwise identical across each condition. A red guiding light at 650 nm (0.08 mW/cm2) provided a visual aid in targeting the forehead in all participants. This red light served as the control light for the sham condition, with 3125 times lower power density than the infrared light used for TILS. Sham proceeded identically to TILS with the difference that only the 650 nm targeting light was used (i.e., without TILS at 1064 nm).

TILS Stimulation Parameters

The TILS and bbNIRS equipment were optically based, using different light sources with non‐overlapping wavelengths: TILS used 1064 nm and bbNIRS used 400–1000 nm. TILS used a well‐collimated laser diode custom‐made for our lab (CytonPro‐5000, CytonSys Inc., Austin, Texas). This device is exactly equivalent to the CG‐5000 device used in previous TILS studies (Wang, Tian, et al. 2017; O'Donnell et al. 2023; Pruitt et al. 2020, 2022; Saucedo et al. 2021), but it was made by a different manufacturer. Both devices have FDA clearance as safe for use in humans. The monochromatic, continuous‐wave (CW) light was emitted as a 4‐cm circular flattop beam (Amaroli et al. 2021) with an area of 13.6 cm2. Power output was 3.4 W and irradiance (power density) was 250 mW/cm2. Total laser exposure consisted of 440 s (eight 55‐s applications), 110 J/cm2 fluence and 1496 J energy. TILS was centered at left or right forehead sites (Figure 1, red circle) corresponding to frontopolar EEG electrode placements (Fp1 and Fp2) over the anterior PFC (Brodmann area 10). We chose the stimulation parameters and points Fp1 and Fp2 as sites of stimulation, because these parameters and locations on the forehead are the same that we have used in previous studies that have shown both cognitive and brain metabolic effects of TILS. There is no evidence in the literature of in vivo changes in CCO resulting from a different set of stimulation parameters or different wavelengths. The laser aperture was fixed in place with a clamp such that the distance to the forehead measured 5 cm.

bbNIRS Measurement Parameters

Broadband near‐infrared spectroscopy (bbNIRS) is a novel optical methodology used to non‐invasively quantify in vivo concentration changes of chromophores in the brain. We used the same type of bbNIRS equipment and method for quantification of CCO, HbO, and HHb described in detail by Wang, Tian, et al. (2017) that has been replicated in six previous studies (Wang, Tian, et al. 2017; O'Donnell et al. 2023; Pruitt et al. 2020; Pruitt et al. 2022; Saucedo et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2016). However, our current bbNIRS device differed from previous studies in that it has two monitoring probes instead of one, with one probe for the right side and another for the left side. This upgrade allowed simultaneous monitoring of the two hemispheres during TILS (Figure 1). Concentration changes of CCO, HbO, and HHb were measured via detected light from four high‐sensitivity charge‐coupled device spectrometers capable of quantifying light with a spectral range of 735–1100 nm (QE‐Pro, Ocean Optics Inc., Orlando, Florida), with one light source and two detectors over each hemisphere. Sources transmitted broadband light emitted from a tungsten‐halogen lamp with a spectral range of 400–1500 nm (Model 3900, Illumination Technologies Inc., East Syracuse, New York). A low‐pass filter with a cutoff of 1000 nm was installed between the bulb and the proximal optical bundle tip to avoid overlapping with the laser light from TILS and to mitigate heat. A Y‐shaped optical fiber bundle was fitted to the lamp to allow for two light source probes with equivalent intensities and spectral signatures. Detector pairs quantified the spectral signature of diffuse light at 3 cm lateral from each source. Only detector pairs situated 3 cm from the light source were used for calculating chromophore concentration changes. Detector pairs situated at 1 cm from the light source were used exclusively to verify motion artifacts during the recordings. A motion artifact was verified by finding that it affected both the 1 and 3 cm source‐detector pairs, instead of only 3 cm source‐detector pairs. The terminal ends of the source and detector probes were inserted into custom I‐shaped 3D‐printed housings fixed to the forehead immediately inferior to the hairline via an elastic band secured behind the head. A laptop computer was used to acquire, display, and save all bbNIRS data.

Preprocessing and Statistical Analyses of bbNIRS Data

Calculations of Δ[oxidized CCO], Δ[HbO] and Δ[HHb] from bbNIRS raw spectral data were generated in MATLAB using algorithms based on the modified Beer–Lambert Law as described in detail by Wang, Tian, et al. (2017) and Wang et al. (2024). Briefly, the MATLAB script determined the relative optical density (ΔOD) at each wavelength, λ, of data obtained from the broadband spectrometers to compute relative chromophore concentration changes compared to the initial baseline. ΔOD at each λ was expressed as a sum of optical absorbance contributed by each chromophore. The chromophore extinction coefficients and wavelength‐dependent differential pathlength factors were used in the computations. We utilized multiple linear regression analysis in the wavelength range of 740–900 nm, comprising a total of 161 wavelengths, within a MATLAB‐based function. This regression algorithm aimed to achieve the best fit of chromophore‐specific concentrations to the measured ΔOD(λ) spectrum by minimizing the squared residual or the objective function (Wang, Tian, et al. 2017).

Signal data (TILS on) was adjusted by subtracting the average sham control signal (TILS off) originating from the corresponding hemisphere at each timepoint. This sham‐subtraction procedure controls for non‐TILS‐related temporal trends in bbNIRS signals, such as physiological drift over the course of the recording session, which are present regardless of stimulation condition. This temporal trend would not be controlled with a more direct change‐from‐baseline approach. A detrending approach has also been previously used to study TILS effects in bbNIRS (O'Donnell et al. 2023) and electrophysiological studies (Wang et al. 2021, 2019; Wang, Dmochowski, et al. 2017). Importantly, sham values did not significantly differ from baseline for any chromophore or hemisphere (Table 1), confirming that the subtraction removes non‐specific temporal trends rather than introducing systematic bias.

Results

Transcranial Infrared Light Penetration Showed Effective Unilateral Propagation

The Monte Carlo simulations (Figure 3) showed that 1064 nm light penetration of 1%–5% of incident light was confined unilaterally to the targeted PFC, while the contralateral hemisphere showed orders of magnitude less light penetration (0.1%–0.001%). The results from the simulations served to verify that any contralateral TILS effects on the PFC could not be due to significant light penetration reaching the opposite hemisphere. Furthermore, significant light penetration did not propagate through other parts of the brain or reach the retina or the optic nerves.

Sex Differences in Metabolic Effects Were Not Found

Mixed repeated‐measures ANOVAs for each chromophore (CCO, HbO, HHb) with Time (stimulation vs. post‐stimulation) as a within‐subject factor and Sex (male vs. female) as a between‐subject factor revealed no significant main effects of Sex or Time × Sex interactions in either TILS or sham groups. Therefore, all subsequent data analysis combines both male and female participants.

Sham Control

We provided comparisons of ipsilateral and contralateral hemispheric effects across two groups: TILS to right PFC and TILS to left PFC using the same stimulation parameters in healthy adults randomized to TILS (n = 40) or sham controls (n = 41). As expected, we found no significant changes in any chromophore in the sham control (TILS off), verifying that sham values did not significantly differ from baseline values. Table 1 summarizes the sham data.

Ipsilateral TILS Effects Were Similar for Both Right and Left Stimulation Sites

Ipsilateral (TILS‐stimulated side) increases in CCO and HbO were similar regardless of right–left side stimulated, while HHb showed no significant changes. Table 2A summarizes the ipsilateral effects. The baseline change in a chromophore concentration is set to zero as part of the modified Beer–Lambert law used in spectroscopic measures such as bbNIRS. Concentration changes from zero are reported after TILS (minutes 8 and 13). For each chromophore, there were no significant differences found between the ipsilateral effects of TILS to left versus right stimulation sites. An ANOVA did not reflect any right versus left significant difference for the ipsilateral stimulation effects. However, some of the ipsilateral data was not normally distributed, which necessitated evaluating it with separate parametric and non‐parametric tests. Therefore, to compare ipsilateral TILS effects between left versus right stimulation sites, independent, two‐tailed, two‐sample tests were conducted for each chromophore (CCO, HbO, HHb) at the target dose of stimulation (minute 8) and at 5 min post‐stimulation (minute 13). Normality and homogeneity of variance were tested for each analysis. Student's t‐tests (two‐tailed) were performed when assumptions were met, and non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U tests were performed when one or both assumptions were violated. Contralateral TILS Effects Were Different Between Right and Left Stimulation Sites

The only significant contralateral difference was in CCO at post‐stimulation: stimulation of the left PFC resulted in larger CCO increases in the contralateral right hemisphere compared to stimulation of the right PFC, with a medium‐to‐large effect size (U = 122, p = 0.036, effect size: rank‐biserial r = 0.39) (Table 2B, Figure 4B). Contralateral CCO effects also developed later (post‐stimulation period) than ipsilateral effects (stimulation period) and were greater in the right hemisphere after TILS to the left side (Figure 4A). No other contralateral differences were found.

These callosal connections are reciprocal (indicted by arrowheads in both directions) and provide a neuroanatomical circuit mechanism for one hemisphere to influence the other. Therefore, TILS delivered to one hemisphere (ipsilateral hemisphere) targets cell bodies of neurons that can have a synaptic influence on the other hemisphere (contralateral hemisphere) through their crossing axons in the corpus callosum. Fewer inhibitory interneurons in the right hemisphere (Rosen et al. 1993; Rosen 1996; Schenker et al. 2007) results in greater excitation on the right side, and CCO is proportional to excitatory activity (Wong‐Riley 2012). In addition, interhemispheric connections are functionally asymmetrical, because the left hemisphere is generally dominant over the right for the coordination of complex cerebral functions, requiring greater energy demands. These asymmetries suggest that the TILS‐administered left hemisphere may have a greater contralateral influence over the right hemisphere in terms of CCO induction.

An omnibus repeated measures ANOVA demonstrated that the only significant findings were a between‐group effect of TILS lateralization [F(1,38) = 4.29, p = 0.045] and an interaction of time by TILS lateralization [F(12,456) = 3.76, p < 0.001]. These effects were due to a contralateral TILS effect on CCO in the right hemisphere, beginning with stimulation and continuing post‐stimulation (Figure 4A). The CCO ipsilateral effects showed a peak (mean = 0.109, Table 2A) at 8 min after stimulation to the left PFC, while the CCO contralateral effects showed a peak (mean = 0.406, Table 2B) at 13 min. Therefore, these peak effects in CCO happened 5 min later contralaterally as compared to ipsilaterally. No other contralateral differences were found. Follow‐up independent two‐tailed tests for each chromophore (CCO, HbO, HHb) confirmed that the only significant contralateral difference was in CCO at post‐stimulation.

Discussion

This is the first controlled study to demonstrate interhemispheric differences in the human brain metabolic responses to TILS at light energy doses used in vivo. First, we found that contralateral CCO effects in the side opposite to the stimulated side occurred later than the ipsilateral effects and were characterized by post‐stimulation CCO increases in the right hemisphere after TILS to the left. These contralateral metabolic effects cannot be accounted for by direct light stimulation because they did not happen in the left hemisphere after TILS to the right.

Second, all previous studies of TILS using bbNIRS methods have supported an in vivo mechanism by which infrared laser at 1064 nm causes CCO photo‐oxidation and Hb oxygenation in the irradiated tissue (Wang, Tian, et al. 2017; O'Donnell et al. 2023; Pruitt et al. 2020; Pruitt et al. 2022; Saucedo et al. 2021; Wang, Reddy, et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2016). Our present findings, using a larger sample than all previous TILS‐bbNIRS studies combined, also support that TILS effects on CCO and HbO are present in the irradiated ipsilateral side. In addition, we demonstrated for the first time that these ipsilateral TILS‐induced increases in CCO and HbO can be equally found at both the right and left PFC. This ipsilateral mechanism appears unique to in vivo conditions because it depends on the combined action of light reaching cortical CCO and oxygen delivered by HbO from the circulation, a combination likely not present in vitro.

Finally, the findings support previous studies utilizing other measuring methods, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and two‐wavelength functional near‐infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) that have demonstrated that TILS can induce a robust hemodynamic effect (Holmes et al. 2019; Tian et al. 2016; Vargas et al. 2017). Below, we further discuss TILS light penetration findings, ipsilateral and contralateral TILS effects, and possible interpretations for the observed CCO hemispheric difference based on known interhemispheric cortical asymmetries.

TILS Light Penetration Model Demonstrated Effective Photon Propagation Confined Unilaterally

While it is important to note that CCO primarily absorbs photons at wavelengths closer to 660 and 810 nm, and its absorption decreases as the wavelength approaches 1064 nm, shorter wavelengths are prone to greater light scattering, limiting their penetration into brain tissue (Tian et al. 2020). The photons that fail to penetrate the PFC gray matter cannot be absorbed by CCO in the target tissue. Tian et al. (2020) have previously done Monte Carlo simulations to assess photon penetration into the PFC using three distinct wavelengths (660, 810, and 1064 nm). These simulations revealed that 1064 nm shows higher local photon flux, mainly due to reduced tissue scattering. Data from the Monte Carlo simulations done by Huang et al. (2021) based on MRIs from six individual brains also showed that 1064 nm is the optimal wavelength to deliver light fluence to the human cerebral cortex. Therefore, the longer 1064‐nm wavelength allows for deeper tissue penetration with less scattering, highlighting a trade‐off between light absorption by CCO and the depth of penetration to the PFC. In addition, a recent human PFC tPBM study showed that transcranial light with 1064 nm wavelength improved visual memory task performance and visual electrophysiological brain responses, while 850 nm wavelength light did not (Zhao et al. 2022).

In the rat brain, quantitative enzyme histochemistry of CCO demonstrated that in vivo TILS at 1064 nm can significantly modify CCO activity (Wade et al. 2023). We demonstrated a biphasic dose–response with both increases and decreases in histochemical CCO activity measures, dependent on different light power levels across the entire rat brain: lower power increases CCO activity and higher power decreases CCO activity. However, light penetration power levels in the rat brain are not comparable to levels in the human brain. In rats, transcranial 1064 nm laser penetrates across the entire brain, with approximately 8.5% reaching the inferior surface of the brain (Wade et al. 2023). In the human brain, however, only low power light levels reach the brain, with about 1%–5% of 1064 nm light reaching the gray matter on the cortical surface of the brain (Figure 3). Furthermore, this level of TILS penetration was confined to the ipsilateral stimulated PFC site. The stimulated hemisphere data indicated that a contralateral light penetration of 0.1%–0.001% cannot lead to significantly different responses than the sham control without the 1064 nm wavelength light. Therefore, the contralateral TILS effects cannot be due to the 0.1%–0.001% of light reaching the contralateral hemisphere.

Ipsilateral TILS In Vivo Mechanisms: CCO Photo‐Oxidation and Hb Oxygenation

Increases in concentrations of ipsilateral oxidized CCO and HbO were not found to significantly differ between groups receiving TILS to either the right or left PFC. However, ipsilateral concentration changes of HbO were found to be an order of magnitude greater than that of oxidized CCO and persisted post‐stimulation for at least 5 min, which suggests a long‐lasting effect. Ipsilateral HbO may provide a larger physiological signal than CCO in future studies investigating TILS.

Based on the present findings and the existing scientific literature, we can gain a deeper understanding of the possible in vivo mechanisms in the TILS‐stimulated PFC, as proposed previously in detail by Gonzalez‐Lima (2021) and Wang et al. (2024). We propose that increases in oxidized CCO in the ipsilateral (light‐stimulated) gray matter are the direct result of CCO photo‐oxidation, which is why the ipsilateral TILS mechanism is the same for both hemispheres.

A hypothesis‐driven interpretation of this ipsilateral finding is that CCO photo‐oxidation by TILS involves the removal of electrons from CCO caused by the combined action of infrared photons and oxygen. After photon absorption, CCO envelops oxygen, which is then reduced to water in a process coupled with ATP and nitric oxide (NO) production in vivo (Wang, Tian, et al. 2017). Briefly, 1064 nm photons initiate a sequence of reactions associated with bioenergetic production within brain mitochondria that elicits a hemodynamic response, as previously described (Gonzalez‐Lima 2021; Wang et al. 2024). As TILS illuminates a relatively large PFC area, it affects a substantial group of mitochondria, leading to notable increases in oxidized CCO concentration and NO‐associated hemodynamic response. This results in increased HbO parameters in the irradiated PFC tissue, indicating heightened regional cerebral blood flow and hemoglobin oxygenation.

Contralateral TILS In Vivo Mechanisms: Interhemispheric CCO Induction

The results suggest that in vivo mechanisms of CCO effects are different for the ipsilateral (TILS‐stimulated) and contralateral (opposite) PFC, which therefore cannot be explained only by in vitro mechanisms found in mitochondria. The most interesting contralateral effect was the large increase in oxidized CCO within the right PFC due to stimulation of the left PFC. This result in the right PFC could not be due to direct CCO photo‐oxidation, because the laser stimulation was applied to the left PFC, and there is no significant light scattering to the other hemisphere (Figure 3). Instead, this result suggests a possible interhemispheric mechanism.

A hypothesis‐driven interpretation of this contralateral finding is that local CCO photo‐oxidation and hemodynamic upregulation of the left PFC appear to facilitate interhemispheric CCO induction in the right PFC, with no support for the inverse. The contralateral mechanisms are likely different than direct CCO photo‐oxidation mechanisms in the ipsilateral TILS‐stimulated PFC, possibly reflecting the different anatomical‐functional interconnections of the two hemispheres. Indeed, higher‐order cerebrovascular mechanisms are different and more computationally complex than molecular mechanisms found within a cell or isolated mitochondria (Hartwigsen et al. 2021). Below we briefly explain interhemispheric mechanisms that could account for TILS effects on the contralateral PFC. Well‐established right–left commissural (crossing) connections and functional hemispheric asymmetries at the cellular level may account for how one PFC side influences the other and for the contralateral response differences in the PFC for the right versus left hemispheres.

Interhemispheric Connections

The anatomical connections between the right and left PFC consist primarily of a large bundle of axons called the corpus callosum (Tzourio‐Mazoyer 2016). These connections originate from commissural axons of pyramidal neurons with cell bodies in the gray matter of the cerebral cortex and axon terminals in the corresponding site of the opposite hemisphere (Figure 4C). These commissural pyramidal neurons enable the exchange of information and coordination between the hemispheres via reciprocal connections. Neuroimaging techniques such as fMRI, fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography, and diffusion tensor imaging have revealed that while each hemisphere can perform certain functions independently, they also exhibit a high degree of interhemispheric connectivity (Hartwigsen et al. 2021).

Previous research has shown how different proportions of outgoing versus incoming connections between brain regions affect network function (McIntosh and Gonzalez‐Lima 1994; Li et al. 2018). Specifically, the right and left PFC differ in the number of outgoing connections to other brain regions, which could result in different regions without direct laser stimulation becoming activated into functional networks, as revealed by regional cerebrovascular responses found with fMRI (Li et al. 2018). Therefore, when there are reciprocal inter‐connections between two regions, such as right–left PFC, the differences in outgoing/incoming connections to these regions create a network function asymmetry.

A hypothesis‐driven interpretation of the role of interhemispheric connections is that they may allow a local irradiation of one hemisphere to lead to a CCO metabolic response in the opposite hemisphere. It has been shown that in vivo tPBM causes CCO enzymatic induction, supporting a plausible mechanism behind PFC contralateral effects. Enzymatic induction refers to the process by which the expression of an enzyme is increased in response to an increased enzymatic activity rate. CCO induction in neurons and muscle cells has been demonstrated in dozens of animal studies with our quantitative histochemical CCO mapping method over the past 30 years (Gonzalez‐Lima and Barrett 2014; Cardoso et al. 2022; Wade et al. 2023; Gonzalez‐Lima and Cada 1998; Gonzalez‐Lima and Auchter 2015). This method of enzyme histochemistry is not dependent on bbNIRS methods; therefore, it provides independent evidence from animal studies about the proposed mechanistic induction of CCO (Hayworth et al. 2010). We have also shown histochemical CCO induction in the PFC of rats exposed to in vivo tPBM (Rojas et al. 2012).

Hemispheric Asymmetries

The two hemispheres are not functionally symmetrical because they exhibit lateralization of cognitive and emotional functions (Gibson et al. 2022; Schiffer 2022). For example, language functions are more often localized in the left hemisphere, while spatial and emotional processing are more associated with the right hemisphere. In the specific case of TILS effects, TILS to the right PFC augments working memory, sustained attention, executive function, PFC‐based category learning, and mood (Barrett and Gonzalez‐Lima 2013; Disner et al. 2016; Holmes et al. 2019; Blanco, Maddox, and Gonzalez‐Lima 2017; Blanco, Saucedo, and Gonzalez‐Lima 2017; Hwang et al. 2016). However, TILS to the left PFC does not lead to these effects (Disner et al. 2016; Zhao et al. 2022). These findings suggest lateralized TILS effects based on different mechanisms of TILS applied to the right versus left PFC.

A hypothesis‐driven interpretation of the role of hemispheric asymmetries is that when the ipsilateral CCO activity is increased beyond baseline levels by direct photobiomodulation of the cell body of pyramidal neurons, this may result in enzymatic induction of CCO in their commissural excitatory axon terminals in the opposite PFC. The ipsilateral CCO photo‐oxidation is expected to increase the oxygen metabolism of irradiated neurons. The increased oxygen metabolism may then facilitate excitatory neurotransmission in the synapses on the contralateral hemisphere, where the commissural axons have their synaptic terminals. It is well‐established that increased synaptic excitatory neurotransmission will cause CCO induction in the terminal axon synaptic regions, because there is a coupling of excitatory neurotransmission genes and CCO genes at the transcriptional level (Wong‐Riley 2026, 2012). This coupling serves to meet the metabolic demand caused by excitatory synaptic transmission (Wong‐Riley 2012). This coupling is how a synaptic‐activated brain region can elevate its metabolic capacity through CCO enzymatic induction (Gonzalez‐Lima and Cada 1998).

The contralateral TILS effect on CCO may be explained by asymmetric density of inhibitory interneurons that modifies the excitatory/inhibitory circuit balance differently in the two hemispheres, because the right hemisphere has fewer parvalbumin‐positive inhibitory interneurons than the left (Rosen et al. 1993; Rosen 1996; Schenker et al. 2007). TILS delivered to one hemisphere (ipsilateral hemisphere) targets cell bodies of neurons that can have a synaptic influence on the other hemisphere (contralateral hemisphere) through their crossing axons in the corpus callosum. However, fewer inhibitory interneurons in the right hemisphere (Rosen et al. 1993; Rosen 1996; Schenker et al. 2007) may result in greater excitation on the right side, and CCO is proportional to excitatory activity (Wong‐Riley 2012). In addition, better interhemispheric communication has been linked to better cognitive performance, particularly in older adults (Piccirilli et al. 2025). This suggests that interhemispheric transfer produced by TILS may contribute to its mechanisms for enhancing cognitive performance, though this remains to be tested directly. These proposed TILS contralateral mechanisms will have to be confirmed using further in vivo studies because in vitro studies are unlikely to capture interhemispheric network interactions.

Alternative explanations for the observed contralateral asymmetry merit consideration. Differential detection sensitivity between the bbNIRS probes in the two hemispheres is unlikely given that the probes were identical in design and shared a common light source via a Y‐shaped fiber bundle, and no hemispheric differences were observed in sham controls (Table 1). Similarly, it is unlikely that the observed hemispheric asymmetry reflects differential change in CCO signals between hemispheres independent of stimulation. If one hemisphere exhibited greater signal change over the recording session regardless of TILS, such a pattern would be expected in the sham condition. However, CCO concentration changes did not differ between hemispheres at any timepoint in sham controls (Table 1), indicating that the contralateral asymmetry emerged only in response to active TILS.

Limitations

Participants were young, healthy adults from the university student population, but they were not screened for other potential confounds to reduce burden and provide for greater generalizability. Instead, subjects were randomized to control for confounding variables, factors other than the independent variable (TILS/sham group) that might influence the dependent variable (chromophore concentration). By randomly assigning participants to different conditions, these extraneous variables are likely to be evenly distributed across the groups, minimizing their potential confounding effects. Random assignment with limited inclusion and exclusion criteria lends greater generalizability, as the study sample is more representative of the greater population. However, generalizability is limited to college‐age students. Additionally, we acknowledge that the asymmetry conclusion is driven by a single outcome pattern rather than converging effects across all three chromophores. Furthermore, the mechanistic interpretation remains indirect because neither interhemispheric connectivity nor behavior was measured directly.

Future Directions

In future studies, we wish to see if the differences observed in the healthy population are also present in various clinical populations. It would be interesting to learn if these interhemispheric differences are present in those with bilateral or lateralized brain pathologies, including cognitive impairment, unipolar depression, bipolar disorder, autism spectrum disorders, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, Parkinson's disease, and stroke, to name a few. In brief, we would like to know if these interhemispheric differences to tPBM could be utilized to improve rehabilitation and outcome in patients with bilateral or unilateral brain pathologies.

Conclusions

The findings showed that: (1) 1%–5% photon penetration of TILS was confined ipsilaterally to the stimulated side of the human PFC. (2) TILS to the right or left PFC resulted in similarly increased CCO and HbO in the stimulated side (ipsilateral PFC). (3) The contralateral responses were not symmetric. TILS contralateral CCO effects in the right PFC opposite to the stimulated left side peaked 5 min later than the ipsilateral TILS effects and were characterized by larger post‐stimulation CCO increases. This is the first controlled study demonstrating an interhemispheric difference in the human brain response to photobiomodulation, specifically contralateral CCO effects.
