Health risks of carbon monoxide and mitigation strategies in Abuja municipal area council, Nigeria
Authors: Salawu Jibril Olarotimi, Appollonia A Okhimamhe, Adewuyi Taiye Oluwafemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbon monoxide (CO) pollution from rapid urbanization and increasing vehicle emissions poses a significant public health and environmental threat in the Abuja Municipal Area Council (AMAC), Nigeria. This study addresses a critical gap by conducting an integrated assessment linking CO levels to vegetation health and community impacts to inform localized policies. We analysed CO concentrations and Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) values from 2018 to 2024 using satellite data from Google Earth Engine, assessing their relationship with advanced regression models. A concurrent survey of 84 residents, health workers, and policymakers evaluated health impacts and awareness. Results revealed higher CO levels in high-traffic zones and during the dry season, with a complex, non-linear relationship to EVI. The survey showed a strong correlation between CO exposure and respiratory/cardiovascular issues, with most respondents facing financial burdens from related illnesses. Despite high awareness (80%), a significant gap existed in adopting protective measures. The findings underscore the urgent need for integrated strategies in AMAC, including stricter vehicle emission standards, low-emission zones, and expanded green spaces to reduce CO pollution, protect public health, and promote sustainable urban development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42734-2.Keywords: Health impacts, Respiratory diseases, Environmental exposure, GIS, Low-emission zones, Sub-Saharan AfricaSubject terms: Environmental impact, Atmospheric dynamics, Natural hazards

Introduction

Carbon monoxide (CO) emissions from vehicles and industrial sources represent a critical global environmental challenge, posing significant threats to both public health and ecosystem integrity. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS) sets an 8-hour CO concentration limit at 9 ppm, yet epidemiological studies reveal that even small increases can elevate mortality rates; a 1 mg/m³ rise in CO has been correlated with a 0.91% increase in daily mortality across 337 cities globally1,2. Beyond its direct health impacts, CO pollution contributes to broader atmospheric chemistry changes and imposes stress on urban vegetation, which is a vital component of ecosystem services.

In rapidly urbanizing regions of Africa, the confluence of increasing traffic, industrial growth, and often inadequate regulatory frameworks exacerbates the CO pollution problem. In Nigeria, the energy and transportation sectors are primary drivers of air quality deterioration. For instance, Lagos exhibits CO levels between 1030.7 and 3664.6 µg/m³, far exceeding acceptable limits3. Within the Abuja Municipal Area Council (AMAC), CO concentrations frequently exceed 30 ppm during peak traffic, disproportionately affecting low-income communities situated near major roadways4. This situation creates a dual threat: direct health risks to residents and potential degradation of the urban green infrastructure that helps mitigate pollution.

The interaction between air pollution and urban ecosystems is complex. Vegetation plays a crucial role in absorbing pollutants and sequestering carbon, thereby indirectly enhancing population quality of life by improving air quality and providing recreational spaces. The Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) is a key remote sensing tool for monitoring vegetation health and stress5. However, elevated CO levels can inhibit plant photosynthesis and growth, potentially creating a negative feedback loop where degraded vegetation is less capable of mitigating air pollution. Understanding this dynamic is critical for sustainable urban planning, as the vitality of urban greenery is directly linked to its capacity to provide these essential ecosystem services.

Globally, established frameworks for monitoring and mitigating CO pollution include integrating satellite with ground-based data6, updating air quality standards1, and promoting green infrastructure7. However, in West African contexts like Nigeria, the application of these strategies is hindered by a critical lack of localized, integrated data. As8 highlights, the absence of systematic monitoring and guidelines is a significant barrier. While studies in Abuja have focused on other pollutants9, there is a distinct lack of research that directly connects CO pollution trends to both environmental health (via EVI) and public health outcomes within AMAC. This gap stifles the development of evidence-based, locally relevant policies.

This study is justified by the urgent need to bridge this gap and provide an integrated evidence base for policymakers. Therefore, the present study aims to provide a comprehensive assessment of CO pollution in AMAC by pursuing three specific objectives:

To assess the dynamics of CO and EVI in AMAC.

The novelty of this research lies in its integrated, multi-method approach, which uniquely combines satellite remote sensing, advanced regression models (including polynomial and Random Forest), and primary household health data. By doing so, this study provides a replicable model for urban air quality assessment and delivers a critical evidence base for policymakers to develop targeted interventions. These interventions aim to reduce CO pollution, enhance urban ecosystem resilience, and protect public health, thereby contributing to sustainable urban development in AMAC and similar regions.

The study area

Abuja Municipal Area Council (AMAC), located within Nigeria’s Federal Capital Territory (FCT), spans latitudes 8° 37’ 49" N to 9° 8’ 44" N and longitudes 7° 6’ 33" E to 7° 33’ 45" E, covering approximately 1,475.74 km2 (See Fig. 1). As the core metropolitan area of the capital city, AMAC is characterized by intense human activity, which is directly linked to the primary sources of carbon monoxide (CO) emissions. The area experiences high traffic volumes from a growing vehicle fleet, with over 5 million registered vehicles in Nigeria as of 2023, a significant portion concentrated in capital regions like the FCT10. Major transportation corridors like the Nnamdi Azikiwe Expressway and the Inner Northern Circular Road (INCR) act as significant emission hotspots due to daily traffic congestion3. Industrial activities within and on the periphery of AMAC, including manufacturing, construction, and power generation, further contribute to the local CO burden11. The significant impact of these sources is confirmed by12, who identified these very corridors and urban wards like Garki and Kabusa as persistent CO hotspots, establishing AMAC as a critical area for CO pollution studies.

AMAC’s land use and land cover (LULC) is a rapidly changing mix of built-up areas, vegetation, and bare land. The council has experienced intense urban sprawl, with central districts evolving into high-density residential and commercial zones, government buildings, and major transportation infrastructure13. Outlying areas transition to suburban and peri-urban landscapes, where patches of agricultural land and remnant Guinea Savanna vegetation are increasingly interspersed with new development. This LULC pattern directly influences the distribution of CO sources (concentrated in built-up and high-traffic zones) and sinks (provided by vegetated areas).

AMAC experiences a tropical savanna climate with distinct wet and dry seasons. The dry season, from November to March, brings daytime temperatures between 28 °C and 35 °C. From April to October, the rainy season peaks in July and September, bringing high humidity and heavy downpours that shape hydrological cycles and ecosystem dynamics14. AMAC’s natural landscape is diverse, with Guinea Savanna vegetation characterized by tall grasses and trees such as Parkia biglobosa and Vitellaria paradoxa15. This blend of intense anthropogenic activity and specific geographic and ecological features makes AMAC a critical area for socio-economic and environmental studies within the FCT.

Methods

This study employed an integrated methodology to overcome the limitations of sparse ground-based monitoring networks. For spatial and temporal analysis, satellite-derived CO and EVI data from Google Earth Engine were selected for their comprehensive coverage across AMAC. To establish relationships between variables, a suite of statistical techniques was used, from correlation to machine learning. Finally, to assess health impacts, a community survey was chosen to capture perceptual and socio-economic data not found in clinical records.

This study was conducted as part of a postgraduate research thesis at the Federal University of Technology, Minna, Niger State, Nigeria. The research was carried out under the supervision and approval of the University’s Postgraduate Committee (Business Committee), which served as the responsible oversight body for ethical and methodological review at the time of the study.

Data collection and pre-processing

Monthly average tropospheric CO column density (mole/cm2) data were sourced from the Sentinel-5P TROPOMI Level 3 product (COPERNICUS/S5P/OFFL/L3_CO) on Google Earth Engine, utilizing the ‘CO_column_number_density’ band for the period from November 2017 to July 2024 (European Space Agency). The data has a spatial resolution of approximately 0.01° x 0.01° (roughly 1.1 km2). Monthly Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) values were derived from the Sentinel-2 MSI Level-2 A surface reflectance product (COPERNICUS/S2_SR) on Google Earth Engine (European Space Agency). Community health survey

To directly assess community-level health impacts and awareness, a structured survey was conducted. This approach was chosen over clinical records as it allowed for the targeted collection of perceptual, behavioural, and socio-economic data that are not typically captured in medical files, providing a more nuanced understanding of the human dimension of CO exposure.

Results

Dynamics of carbon monoxide and vegetation Health

Temporal trends of carbon monoxide and enhance vegetation index

Figure 2 illustrates the average EVI and CO levels in AMAC from 2018 to 2024, highlighting the dynamic relationship between vegetation health and CO emissions over time. The data shows clear seasonal fluctuations, with CO levels peaking during the dry season (November-April), coinciding with increased dust and fuel combustion in the AMAC region. The dry season peaks align with AMAC’s Harmattan period (November-February), characterized by increased dust, temperature inversions, and higher reliance on fossil-fuel generators across the municipality. For instance, a notable peak was observed in March 2019, with an EVI of 1.3696 and a CO level of 0.0569. Overall, the relationship between CO and EVI is complex and not simply inverse, as shown by the subsequent statistical analysis.

Correlation and regression analysis

Table 1 presents the Pearson correlation coefficient (0.692, p < 0.001), indicating a moderate to strong positive relationship between CO and EVI in AMAC, suggesting that increases in CO often coincide with higher EVI values, though influenced by other factors. Regression analysis results (Fig. 5; Table 2) indicate that a Multiple Linear Regression model explains 41% of EVI variance in the training data and 59.1% in the test data. Polynomial models (Degrees 2 and 3) capture variability more effectively, with lower MSE values and higher R2 values. Figure 6 illustrates the polynomial regression fits for Degrees 2 and 3, highlighting the non-linear relationship between carbon monoxide (CO) levels and Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) in AMAC. The Degree 2 model shows a quadratic relationship where EVI initially increases with CO but declines at higher concentrations, indicating potential detrimental effects on vegetation. In contrast, the Degree 3 model captures more complex interactions, revealing multiple peaks and valleys, suggesting varying impacts of CO on EVI. Overall, the Degree 3 fit provides a better representation of the data, emphasizing the intricate dynamics between air quality and vegetation health in AMAC’s unique urban environment. Table 3 shows the Random Forest regression results, optimized with a third-degree polynomial and 50 trees, further confirming non-linear relationships, with CO emerging as the strongest predictor of EVI. Table 4, which lists the feature importance scores, reveals that CO itself (0.4702) was the strongest predictor of EVI, followed by its non-linear terms (CO2 and CO3). This confirms that the relationship between air quality and vegetation health in AMAC is predominantly non-linear and best understood through these advanced modelling techniques.

Health survey findings

Demographic characteristics of respondents

The demographic profile of respondents reveals that 44.67% are aged 31–40, with a higher proportion of females (56.08%) compared to males (43.67%). Most respondents are self-employed (53.85%) or civil servants (29.78%), and household sizes predominantly range from 3 to 4 members (43.42% and 31.76%, respectively).

Awareness and perceptions of carbon monoxide pollution

Regarding awareness, 40.69% of AMAC residents were “very aware” of CO pollution. An overwhelming 68.98% correctly identified vehicle emissions as the primary source, directly implicating the congested traffic on major corridors like the Nnamdi Azikiwe Expressway as a key concern for residents. Media outlets (44.67%) were the main source of information, and 39.96% expressed strong concern about the long-term health effects of CO pollution.

Health impacts and mitigation behaviours

Health-related data indicates that 12.41% of respondents have been diagnosed with respiratory diseases, and 17.12% have experienced cardiovascular symptoms. Many respondents (67.25%) live near high-traffic or industrial areas, which increases their CO exposure. Despite this, only 3.97% have been tested for CO exposure, and 43.42% have experienced symptoms like headaches. Additionally, 40.94% report a family history of respiratory issues, while 35.24% believe green spaces are the most effective urban feature for reducing CO pollution. Although only 9.43% have noticed significant changes in local wildlife, 56.58% are willing to participate in CO reduction initiatives.

Regarding health workers, only 3.23% have a CO detector in their clinics, and most prefer using personal vehicles over public transportation. While 11.41% support CO reduction efforts, clinic mitigation measures remain limited. Few health workers (9.43%) have experienced CO-related health issues, with only 5.21% missing work or school due to respiratory or cardiovascular problems. Policymakers generally express dissatisfaction with current CO pollution policies, with many advocating for stricter vehicle emission standards. However, there is little consensus on other measures like green spaces or public transportation. Only 2.48% rate their neighbourhood’s environmental quality as good.

Field survey findings indicate that CO pollution significantly affects residents’ health and finances, with 72 out of 84 respondents facing financial strain due to healthcare costs related to respiratory and cardiovascular issues. Most policymakers view current policies as ineffective and support stricter vehicle emission standards.

Discussion

The analysis of carbon monoxide (CO) concentrations and Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) values in the AMAC area from 2018 to 2024 revealed notable correlations that must be interpreted through the lens of the region’s specific geography and climate. The positive correlation found in the Pearson analysis (r = 0.692, p < 0.001) may be coincidental rather than causal, driven by a common third factor. For instance, AMAC’s distinct dry season (with higher CO from increased fuel combustion and dust) also coincides with the peak growing season for the native Guinea Savanna vegetation after the initial rains, leading to higher EVI. This seasonal coupling, unique to the tropical savanna climate, can create a statistical association that masks the underlying negative physiological impact of CO on plants at the micro level.

These findings suggest a complex relationship between air quality and vegetation health in AMAC, highlighting that enhancing vegetation cover could potentially mitigate CO emissions. The results align with some studies, such as16, who identified variations in CO levels across different seasons and locations in Nigeria. However, the non-linear relationships captured by our polynomial and Random Forest models reveal a more nuanced dynamic than previously reported in other Nigerian contexts. The finding that CO is the strongest predictor of EVI in our Random Forest model (importance = 0.4702) underscores the significant pressure that urban emissions place on AMAC’s green infrastructure.

The findings on spatial CO hotspots in AMAC align with established methodologies that emphasize integrating satellite data with ground-based measurements for accurate pollution assessment6. Our GIS analysis specifically identified persistent hotspots in AMAC’s central business districts and along high-traffic corridors like the Nnamdi Azikiwe Expressway, reflecting the intense development pressure in these areas. This pattern mirrors the rapid urban expansion documented by Mashi et al. (2025), where settlement areas in AMAC grew from 18.5 km2 to 333.6 km2 between 1991 and 2021.

The strong public support for stricter vehicle emission standards in our AMAC survey reinforces recommendations by17 and18 regarding the importance of regulatory enforcement. Furthermore, AMAC respondents’ identification of green spaces as an effective mitigation strategy (35.24%) corroborates findings by7 and3 on the value of green infrastructure in urban air quality management, while also reflecting local appreciation for the remaining Guinea Savanna vegetation within the municipality.

Public awareness and perceptions of CO pollution in AMAC revealed high awareness levels, with 40.69% of respondents being “very aware” and 39.95% “somewhat aware.” Vehicle emissions were overwhelmingly recognized as the primary source of CO pollution (68.98%), with strong support for government initiatives aimed at reducing emissions. The high awareness levels suggest that educational efforts in AMAC have been effective, laying the foundation for public support of mitigation strategies.

These findings are consistent with existing research showing that urbanization and industrialization contribute to rising CO levels globally19. reported that air pollution in the Niger Delta, primarily from CO and particulate matter, leads to health issues. In AMAC, the health impacts are particularly concerning given the demographic profile showing many residents living near high-traffic areas (67.25%) and reporting symptoms like headaches (43.42%). The financial burden reported by 72 of 84 respondents highlights the economic dimension of CO exposure in this specific municipal context.

While the study found strong public awareness of CO pollution in AMAC, alternative explanations must be considered, including the possibility of social desirability bias, where respondents provide answers, they believe are expected. Additionally, although many respondents support government initiatives, it is important to assess whether this support leads to concrete actions or merely reflects concern without actual engagement.

A limitation of the study is its geographic focus on urban and peri-urban areas of AMAC, potentially overlooking rural perspectives on CO pollution. Expanding future research to include rural areas surrounding AMAC will offer a more comprehensive understanding of CO pollution’s impact across different community settings within the municipality. Future studies should also explore the impacts of specific anthropogenic activities unique to AMAC on both CO levels and EVI, which could lead to more targeted interventions for the region.

Conclusion

This study provides an integrated assessment of carbon monoxide pollution in Abuja Municipal Area Council, demonstrating significant spatial and temporal variations in CO concentrations linked to traffic patterns and seasonal changes. The research establishes a complex relationship between air quality and vegetation health, reveals a substantial gap between public awareness and protective actions regarding CO exposure, and documents significant health and financial burdens on residents from CO-related illnesses. By combining satellite data analysis with community health surveys, this study offers a replicable model for urban air quality assessment that can inform both environmental management and public health policy in rapidly urbanizing areas.

Ethical approval

All methods involving human participants were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki and the Nigerian National Code of Health Research Ethics. This study was conducted as part of a postgraduate research thesis at the Federal University of Technology, Minna, Niger State, Nigeria, under the supervision and approval of the University’s Postgraduate Committee (Business Committee), which served as the responsible oversight body for ethical and methodological review at the time of the study. The research involved minimal-risk, non-clinical, face-to-face interviews with 84 adult participants from the Abuja Municipal Area Council (AMAC), Nigeria. Participants were fully informed about the objectives of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, potential risks, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Written informed consent was not obtained. Instead, verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Responses were recorded digitally using the KoboCollect mobile application during face-to-face interviews. The use of verbal consent was considered appropriate for the minimal-risk nature of the study and to maintain participant anonymity and comfort in the community setting. This consent procedure was reviewed and approved by the supervising Postgraduate Committee. All participant information was anonymised and handled with strict confidentiality.
