Explaining reported generative AI engagement in higher education: an extended TAM with ethical compatibility and reliance-based trust
Authors: Zhenyu Liu, Jing Fu, Yanan Cao, Sahar Khayatnezhad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid integration of generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools into higher education has intensified conversations regarding usefulness, ethical alignment, and responsible engagement. Unlike traditional technology acceptance studies that focus on initial use, this study examines AI use intensity among active university users. Building on an extended Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the model incorporates AI-Alignment Construct, reliance-based trust in AI outputs, and normative alignment within academic contexts. Data were collected from 637 university students and analyzed using variance-based structural equation modeling. The results indicate that perceived usefulness remains the strongest predictor of AI use. Furthermore, reliance-based trust and AI-Alignment Construct demonstrate statistically significant correlations with engagement, whereas moderation hypotheses were not supported. These findings suggest that ethical and trust-related mechanisms operate primarily at the attitudinal alignment level rather than as boundary conditions within this cross-sectional framework. Moreover, the study contributes by repositioning TAM within a post-adoption engagement context and clarifying the bounded conceptualization of ethics and trust in AI-mediated learning environments. Practical implications emphasize calibrated AI integration, transparent governance, and assessment design aligned with academic integrity. Finally, the findings are associative in nature and should be interpreted within the methodological constraints of self-reported, cross-sectional data.Keywords: Extended TAM, Generative artificial intelligence, Ethics and trust, Subjective norms, PLS-SEMSubject terms: Business and management; Business and management; Cultural and media studies; Cultural and media studies; Education; Information systems and information technology; Psychology; Psychology; Science, technology and society

Introduction

The rapid diffusion of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies across digital platforms has fundamentally reshaped consumer interaction patterns and decision-making processes. Recent research suggests that AI-based platform adoption is influenced by technological attributes and by users’ psychological and cognitive mechanisms1. In particular, evidence indicates that personality traits especially the Dark Triad traits of narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy play a significant role in shaping attitudes toward AI-powered platforms and subsequent usage intentions, particularly when status-driven motivations are activated2,3.

Beyond personality factors, adoption research consistently highlights the importance of attitudinal formation and initial trust in predicting technology use behaviors4. To this end, emotional trust, perceived technology fit, and privacy risk perceptions have been identified as critical drivers of consumer engagement with algorithm-driven platforms, demonstrating that AI adoption is embedded within broader affective and evaluative processes5,6. These findings emphasise the view that adoption behavior is not purely instrumental but is shaped by intertwined psychological, emotional, and contextual influences.

At a broader digital ecosystem level, technological uncertainty has been shown to moderate relationships between knowledge mechanisms and sustainability-oriented outcomes, suggesting that user responses to emerging technologies are formed within environments characterized by perceived risk and complexity7. Moreover, machine learning–based modeling approaches demonstrate that behavioral and perceptual variables often interact in nonlinear and multidimensional ways, highlighting the need for more sophisticated analytical frameworks in studying digital adoption phenomena8.

In recent years, universities have increasingly become testing grounds for the everyday use of AI technologies, especially generative AI tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot9. Rather than being replacements for human judgment, these systems serve as adjuncts to cognitive collaboration in writing, translation, programming, and data analysis tasks10. Applications of AI in higher education have spread across the arc of teaching, assessment, and academic support, with questions about student acceptance and responsible use more salient than before11. Meanwhile, privacy, algorithmic transparency, bias, and academic misconduct concerns have signified ethics and trust from secondary concerns to central preconditions for the sustainable integration of AI into universities12.

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) has been one of the leading frameworks for describing how technology is adopted within educational contexts for an extended period of time since Davis13. The TAM argues that key roles in forming attitudes toward technology and, eventually, reported AI engagement (AU) are performed by such issues as perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEoU)14. Empirical studies recently conducted by Aarthi15, confirmed that recent empirical studies on AI showed PU to be a powerful driver of behavioral intention and actual usage whereas PEoU was much less instrumental, especially for digitally savvy students who have no problems with working out complex interfaces. Additionally, systematic reviews underlined that functional expectations and perceived usefulness were strong predictors of the adoption of AI, where persistent obstacles include distrust, lack of AI literacy, and ethical uncertainty that has yet to disappear16.

However, purely instrumental models of acceptance no longer appear to suffice for generative AI tools. Recent studies extending TAM by factors such as trust, ethical considerations, and social or subjective norms indicate that decisions about adoption are influenced by a wider socio-ethical environment17. Trust in the reliability and transparency of an AI directly encourages use18, while subjective norms (such as peer expectations, institutional signals, and disciplinary culture) have emerged as strong predictors of perceived usefulness6. Nevertheless, most of these studies consider such variables in a linear fashion, without considering nonlinear or saturation effects16.

Ethical and governance perspectives add a layer of intricacy in the context of higher education. Recent scholarship has emphasized that ethical anxiety, perceived ethical risks, and ethical awareness significantly influence the behavioral intentions of students and reported AI engagement (AU) of generative AI tools, demonstrating that ethics operates as a critical determinant within acceptance frameworks19. Institutional efforts to establish ethical standards for GenAI require comprehensive roadmaps that address policy development, curriculum integration, and continuous governance measures to ensure academic integrity and fairness20. Empirical investigations reveal that ethical implications directly influence adoption decisions among academic communities, highlighting the need for institutions to proactively address ethical challenges in GenAI deployment18. Furthermore, institutional policies must navigate complex dimensions including data privacy, academic integrity, tool vetting processes, and governance frameworks to create responsible AI ecosystems in higher education12. While governance frameworks offer pragmatic ethical guidance and strategic principles for responsible AI use, these operate at the institutional level and rarely connect with models of acceptance at the student level19.

Despite growing interest in AI, there is still little understanding of how ethics, trust, and subjective norms interact within TAM-based frameworks in predicting actual student use of AI tools. Most existing research tends to examine one variable in isolation or treats social influence as a simple predictor, without investigating interactions that are more nuanced or nonlinear21. Systematic reviews have thus called for more integrated approaches5.

Ethical compatibility and reliance-based trust are theoretically positioned as contextual boundary conditions that may influence the extent to which favorable attitudes translate into behavioral engagement. In AI-supported academic environments, student attitudes toward AI may be enacted more strongly when they perceive ethical alignment and output reliability, and potentially constrained when such alignment is weak. Accordingly, moderation effects are hypothesized as theoretically plausible contextual mechanisms rather than as primary structural drivers. However, these relationships are modeled as structural associations rather than causal mechanisms. By focusing on actual usage rather than intention alone, the current study offers a more grounded picture of how students navigate the tension between the perceived utility of AI tools and the ethical, social, and trust-based conditions under which they are willing to integrate these tools into their academic lives.

The findings of this study carry significant implications for institutional governance, pedagogical design, and assessment practices. At the governance level, the structural associations identified in this model suggest that institutional AI policies should not simply articulate ethical principles but also actively shape students’ attitudinal formation through transparent communication about AI reliability, data protection, and responsible use expectations. For pedagogy, the importance of perceived usefulness and attitudinal strength indicates that educators should clearly demonstrate the value of AI tools in authentic learning contexts while modeling ethical integration practices. In terms of assessment design, the findings demonstrate the need for evaluation frameworks that recognize AI as a cognitive partner rather than a threat to academic integrity, embedding clear guidelines for attribution, transparency, and appropriate use into assessment rubrics and feedback mechanisms. By translating these empirical insights into concrete institutional actions, universities can move beyond generic policy statements toward evidence-informed strategies that support responsible AI adoption.

Given the cross-sectional and self-reported nature of the data, this study does not claim causal inference, rather offering a variance-based explanatory model of structural associations among psychological and governance-related constructs shaping students’ reported AI engagement (AU).

Unlike traditional TAM extensions that primarily model intention or initial adoption, the present study focuses on reported use intensity among active generative AI users. To this end, ethical compatibility and reliance-based trust operate as attitudinal alignment mechanisms rather than mere additive predictors. This positioning shifts the theoretical emphasis from technology acceptance onset to engagement dynamics within AI-integrated learning environments.

In this study, the dependent construct is interpreted as AI use, referring to self-reported intensity of AI use among already active users, instead of binary adoption status or initial use only. Given the single-source, self-reported, and cross-sectional nature of the data, this study does not support causal inference. Instead, it provides an explanatory model of structural associations among attitudinal, normative, and governance-related constructs linked to AI use.

Materials and methods

Research design and methodological approach

This study employed a quantitative cross-sectional survey design to examine variations in AI use among university students who already use generative AI tools for academic purposes. Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) was employed to estimate the proposed model22. The choice of PLS-SEM was based on the specific characteristics of the model rather than generic exploratory considerations. First, the model included interaction terms to test moderating effects. Second, the analysis emphasized variance explanation and predictive relevance of AI use, as reflected in R2 and Importance-Performance Map Analysis (IPMA) results. Third, the study did not aim to test a covariance-based global fit structure, but rather to estimate a prediction-oriented explanatory model under cross-sectional conditions. Consequently, PLS-SEM was considered methodologically appropriate for the present model specification14.

Population and sample

The population of this study comprised university students who had experience using generative AI tools, such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, for academic activities. Purposive sampling was employed given the study’s focus on examining actual engagement among active users of AI technology23. The inclusion criterion required respondents to have at least one experience of using AI tools for university coursework assignments.

In the present model, the reported AI engagement (AU) had the most incoming paths (seven paths), establishing a minimum sample requirement of 70 respondents. A total of 637 complete questionnaires were collected to ensure adequate statistical power and result reliability, which far exceeds the minimum requirement and guarantees high statistical significance (> 0.95)24. The sample consisted of 384 males (60.28%) and 253 females (39.72%), with the largest concentration among fifth-semester students (327 respondents, 51.33%).

Data collection instruments and measurement scales

Data were collected through a structured, self-reported questionnaire. The questionnaire consisted of two primary sections:

Section one: demographic information

This section contained questions regarding gender, academic term, and the purpose of AI tool usage (multiple-choice format). To protect participant privacy and adhere to ethical research principles, no identifying information such as names, student identification numbers, academic disciplines, or institutional affiliation was collected. All questionnaires remained completely anonymous throughout the study.

Section two: research construct measurement

All constructs in the extended TAM model were measured using a 4-point Likert scale: 4 = Strongly Agree, 3 = Agree, 2 = Disagree, 1 = Strongly Disagree. The 4-point scale was selected to mitigate central tendency bias and encourage respondents to adopt a definitive position25. While the four-point Likert format was selected to reduce central tendency bias and encourage directional responses, it may have constrained response variance relative to broader scale formats. In general, restricted variance can reduce statistical sensitivity for detecting interaction or nonlinear effects. Therefore, moderation findings should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive theoretical absence.

Data collection procedure

The questionnaire was designed and distributed online via Google Forms. The questionnaire was reviewed by five specialists in educational technology and research methodology to confirm content validity before large-scale distribution.

The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Research and Innovation Institute of the university. An informed consent statement was presented at the beginning of the questionnaire, explaining the study’s purpose, the confidential nature of data, the voluntary nature of participation, and the right to withdraw without consequences. Only respondents who consented to participate were granted access to the questionnaire.

Data collection was conducted over a six-week period. Of the 685 questionnaires returned, 637 were complete and usable (response rate: 93.0%). No missing data occurred (missing data = 0%), indicating high-quality data collection.

Data analysis

Analytical software

All statistical analyses were conducted using SmartPLS 328, a comprehensive software platform for PLS-SEM analysis. Data from Google Forms were exported in Excel format and subsequently imported into SmartPLS for analysis.

PLS-SEM analysis procedures

The PLS-SEM analysis comprised two main stages22:

Stage 1: outer model evaluation (measurement model assessment)

The measurement model was evaluated to ensure construct validity and reliability:

Outer loading assessment

The factor loading of each item on its respective construct was calculated. The acceptance criteria were factor loadings over 0.6 for exploratory research and 0.7 for confirmatory research22,24. Two indicators were removed during item purification: SN2 (loading = 0.209) and the original E3 (loading = 0.635). Following this deletion, the remaining ethics indicators were renumbered for sequential consistency; therefore, the final reported E3 corresponds to the previously labeled E4. All remaining items demonstrated loadings ranging from 0.619 to 0.891.

Construct reliability

Composite Reliability (CR) and Cronbach’s Alpha coefficients were calculated for each construct. The acceptance threshold was CR > 0.7, with CR > 0.6 acceptable for exploratory research (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). Composite reliability values for all constructs exceeded acceptable thresholds. Cronbach’s alpha values were also overall acceptable, although the ethics construct showed a borderline alpha (0.634), which was retained given its bounded operationalization and acceptable composite reliability.

Convergent validity was assessed through Average Variance Extracted (AVE). The threshold is AVE > 0.527, indicating that more than 50% of item variance is explained by the construct. All constructs met this criterion (range: 0.511 to 0.756).

Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity was examined using the Fornell-Larcker Criterion. According to this criterion, the square root of the AVE for each construct should exceed the correlations of that construct with all other constructs27. The results demonstrated that all constructs possessed adequate discriminant validity.

Multicollinearity assessment

To ensure absence of high multicollinearity among items, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was calculated for each item. The acceptable threshold is VIF < 4.0, with VIF < 3.0 being ideal14,22. Indicator-level diagnostics and structural-model collinearity are distinguished, where for the structural model, inner VIF values are reported to assess collinearity among predictors of endogenous constructs. Extremely large VIF values are not interpreted as “acceptable”; instead, they signal redundancy and are discussed as a limitation of closely worded reflective indicators. In addition, using VIF results as stand-alone evidence against common method bias are avoided and instead are presented as one robustness diagnostic alongside procedural remedies.

Common method bias assessment

Common method bias (CMB) was addressed through both procedural and statistical approaches. Procedurally, the survey ensured full anonymity, voluntary participation, and separation of demographic and construct-related questions to reduce evaluation apprehension and response pattern bias. Harman’s single-factor test was conducted as an initial diagnostic; no single factor accounted for the majority of variance. Furthermore, collinearity-based diagnostics were examined in line with PLS-SEM practice. However, consistent with contemporary methodological guidance, these tests are interpreted as robustness checks rather than definitive proof of the absence of common method bias.

Stage 2: inner model evaluation (structural model assessment)

Following confirmation of the measurement model, the structural model was evaluated to test the hypotheses:

Path coefficients

Standardized path coefficients (β) were calculated for each structural relationship. These coefficients indicate the strength and direction of relationships, ranging from − 1 to + 1.

Significance testing (bootstrapping)

Bootstrapping with 5000 subsamples was employed to determine the statistical significance of path coefficients22. This procedure generates an empirical sampling distribution, enabling calculation of standard errors, t-statistics, and p-values. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Coefficient of determination (R2)

R2 was calculated for each endogenous variable, indicating the percentage of variance explained by its predictors. Evaluation criteria are: R2 = 0.75 (substantial), R2 = 0.50 (moderate), R2 = 0.25 (weak)22.

IPMA was conducted to identify management priorities. This analysis compares construct importance (total path coefficients) and performance (mean scores) and identifies constructs with high importance but low performance.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in strict adherence to international research ethics standards. Ethical approval was obtained from the university’s Ethics Committee. The principles of informed consent, complete confidentiality, right to withdraw, and protection of participant privacy were strictly observed. No identifying information (names, student IDs, academic disciplines, institutional affiliation) was collected or recorded, and all data were analyzed in aggregate, anonymous form.

All reported inner VIF values are below conservative thresholds (e.g., VIF < 5), suggesting that predictor redundancy does not threaten the validity of the structural inferences. Accordingly, multicollinearity is not considered a substantive concern in the structural model.

Discussion

The results of this study confirm and extend knowledge on the TAM in the context of AI. Perceived usefulness was the optimal driver for students’ attitudes in using AI; this is also consistent with previous research where students consider the value of AI tools in performing their assignments more than how easy they are to use26. Also, perceived ease of use showed a weaker effect, potentially due to the fact that contemporary students are comfortable with complex digital interfaces. This can suggest that after the level of basic digital fluency is reached, ease of use is no longer a differentiating factor, but rather a feature that can drive use30.

There is a significant positive effect of trust on actual usage, confirming recent findings in both educational and health-related studies31. In instances where students perceive AI recommendations as reliable and believe such tools indeed support their academic tasks, they are more likely to include AI into their daily learning. These findings suggest that institutional transparency regarding AI reliability may be associated with higher reported use.

Ethics were also directly and meaningfully related to Reported AI engagement (AU). Students who considered AI use to be consonant with academic integrity (through disclosure, respect for original authorship, or consultation with instructors) were more likely to utilize these tools. This echoes institutional policies emphasizing transparency, honesty, and accountability29. In other words, ethics may work as an internal motivator of responsible behavior, not just as an external constraint.

It is important to acknowledge that certain items particularly within perceived usefulness and reported AI engagement (AU) contain evaluative or normatively framed wording. Although procedural safeguards were implemented, such wording may contribute to shared method variance or inflated structural associations. Interpretations should therefore remain context-bound and cautious. More broadly, the use of single-source self-reported data introduces potential risks of common method bias and social desirability effects. Also, the cross-sectional design prevents temporal ordering and causal interpretation. The reported relationships should therefore be interpreted as associative and context-bound rather than causal or developmentally stable.

However, neither trust nor ethics significantly moderated the relationship between attitude and AI use. The absence of statistically significant interaction effects suggests that the strength of the attitude–engagement relationship does not vary meaningfully as a function of ethical compatibility or reliance-based trust within this sample of active users. Alternative explanations, including restricted variance, bounded measurement scope, or model specification choices, remain plausible. This non-significant pattern may reflect several possibilities, including restricted response variance associated with the four-point scale, bounded measurement scope of the ethics construct, relative homogeneity among active users, or the possibility that trust and ethical compatibility operate primarily as direct attitudinal alignment mechanisms instead of boundary conditions.

From this pragmatic perspective, several priorities can be highlighted. First, integrating AI into coursework particularly into feedback and assessment processes has the potential to considerably extend students’ perceived usefulness. Second, transparency in communication of the capabilities and limitations of AI is essential to developing trust. Third, university policies must move beyond prohibitive stances and articulate clear discipline-specific guidelines on ethics that support responsible use. Future research may explore alternative theoretical specifications. For example, ethical compatibility and reliance-based trust may operate indirectly through attitudinal integration mechanisms rather than as boundary conditions. Higher-order attitudinal constructs combining evaluative and normative dimensions may also provide a more theoretically clear explanation of AI engagement dynamics in higher education. Finally, because social norms will continue to play their part, instructors and institutions have a responsibility to themselves model ethical and effective uses of AI.

Conclusion

This study extends the TAM by incorporating ethics, trust, and subjective norms, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of students’ AI use intensity within higher education contexts. Empirical findings demonstrate PU as the strongest driver in AI use among university students. Students report higher engagement with AI tools when they perceive them as useful for academic tasks26. PEoU is a secondary driver that is also less decisive, including an implication that interface simplicity within digitally mature ecosystems and tech-savvy students is no longer a differentiating factor.

An important contribution of this research lies in modeling ethics and trust as integral components within an extended TAM framework and empirically examining whether they function as direct predictors or boundary conditions in the context of generative AI use. Previous research has often examined ethics and trust either independently from established acceptance frameworks or primarily as moderating variables18. Conversely, the present model integrates ethical compatibility and reliance-based trust as direct alignment mechanisms within a post-adoption engagement context. Students who perceive AI use as ethically compatible with academic norms and who consider AI outputs reliable are more likely to report higher levels of engagement17.

While ethics and reliance-based trust demonstrated direct associations with AI use, their moderating roles were not empirically supported in this model. Subjective norms exhibited a significant positive linear effect on Reported AI engagement (AU), underlining the strong role of peer expectations, institutional signals, and disciplinary cultures in shaping student behavior.

The key takeaway for policy makers and university administrators is that alignment on three levels will be required for the sustainable and responsible use of AI. To implement effectively, universities should: (1) incorporate AI into authentic and meaningful educational assignments and projects; (2) reinforce transparency, reliability, and limitations of the AI systems continuously; (3) articulate and develop ethical and professional behavioral guidelines with discipline-specific guidance in mind; (4) promote responsible modeling of AI use by instructors and institutional leaders themselves.

Privacy and Research Ethics: This research complies with the highest standard of ethics and principles that constitute the basis of scientific research; it has obtained an ethics approval from the University Ethics Committee. Participant information, data, and identifiers will be kept completely confidential and anonymous. Names, student IDs, academic programs, institutions, and personally identifiable data about students or instructors will never be revealed. Suggestions for Further Research: Due to the limitations of this study, future research should address the following: (1) longitudinal studies to assess changes in attitude and usage patterns over time, and the dynamic importance of ease of use; (2) analyzing the influence of cultural background, age, gender, and academic disciplines regarding the role of ethics, trust, and subjective norms; (3) appropriate qualitative methods, such as interviews or focus groups, for obtaining in-depth insights into the perceptions and behavior of students; (4) investigation into the effectiveness of educational treatments in strengthening ethical conceptions and trust in AI tools; (5) model comparison: testing TAM against other models like UTAUT to validate results and explore superior theoretical frameworks; (6) cross-cultural studies: extending research to various cultural and geographic contexts to further enhancing generalizability.

Overall, the current study provides contextual refinement in understanding how university students make complex decisions related to AI use, with a emphasis on the normalization of generative AI in educational contexts.
