Assessing the properties of an encounter-based “process of recovery” measure of patient benefit following rotator cuff repair
Authors: John M. Brooks, Natalie L. Myers, Sarah B. Floyd, Adam D. Lutz, Allyson L. Sandago, Allison A. Nall, Prashant Duhoon, Charles A. Thigpen, Stephan G. Pill, Michael J. Kissenberth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRotator cuff repair (RCR) is a common orthopedic procedure, with patient benefit often assessed using the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score. However, because ASES scores are not consistently collected across patient populations or care settings, they may be impractical for broad policy evaluation. The objective of this study was to assess whether the number of shoulder-related encounters a patient has during a Recovery Period after standard follow-up care (here 181-365 days post-RCR) can serve as a proxy benefit measure for 12-month ASES scores following RCR.MethodsAdult patients (18 years or older) who underwent a primary RCR between 2017 and 2022 and had complete post-operative ASES surveys at 12 months were included in this study. Health-care encounters with shoulder-related diagnoses were counted during the Recovery Period. Linear regression was used to contrast outcome models as a function of baseline covariates for Recovery Period shoulder-related encounter and 12-month ASES score benefit measures and to estimate the association between these measures.ResultsOf an initial sample of 683 patients who underwent primary RCR and provided a baseline ASES score, 513 (75%) had complete 12-month ASES scores. Outcome models showed consistent relationships between patient baseline covariates and the 12-month ASES scores and Recovery Period shoulder-related encounters. Furthermore, regression showed a strong association between the number of Recovery Period shoulder-related encounters and ASES scores at 12 months (P < .001). Additional shoulder-related encounters during the Recovery Period were associated with 4.7-point reduction 12-month ASES scores (P < .01). Patients with 3 or more shoulder-related encounters during the Recovery Period had predicted lower 12-month ASES scores within the range of the published Minimal Clinically Important Difference results for rotator cuff.ConclusionAdditional shoulder-related encounters during the Recovery Period were associated with clinically important lower 12-month ASES scores. Encounter-based measures should not replace patient-reported outcome measures for clinically focused research and individual clinical decision-making but may provide useful proxy measures of patient benefit for population-level orthopedic outcomes research when patient-reported outcome measure data are incomplete or unavailable.

Orthopedic conditions are among the most common causes of pain and disability, accounting for substantial health-care utilization and cost.15,37,51 Although many orthopedic conditions can be managed non-operatively, patients also benefit from surgical treatments which can be costly. Approximately 7 million musculoskeletal and orthopedic procedures are performed annually in the United States across inpatient and outpatient settings.7 Without widely available approaches to measure patient benefit at the population level, higher cost orthopedic treatments are ripe for rate reductions within cost-focused reimbursement approaches like Medicare's Accountable Care Organizations and through the use of prior authorization within Medicare Advantage plans.8,33,34 Accordingly, policymakers need accurate measures of the patient benefit that accrues from orthopedic treatments across populations to properly assess whether changes in existing orthopedic treatment rates are warranted.30,31,36

Patient “benefit” in orthopedic medicine can be defined as the alleviation of pain and restoration of normal joint function.22,26 Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are considered among the most important tools for evaluating patient benefit in orthopedics.20 PROMs are survey-based instruments that capture joint-specific and general health perceptions, including symptoms, function, and health-related quality of life.20 Direct collection of PROMs has been used extensively in clinical orthopedic research but has been less widely adopted in real-world practice, as PROM collection can be time consuming, cumbersome, and costly.4,46 A recent study from a large, high-volume arthroplasty center reported costs of $272 per patient to collect a complete set of pre-operative and post-operative PROM scores.47 Furthermore, despite active collection approaches patient response usually reaches only 50% leaving a subset of patient data unmeasured.47 Patient non-response introduces concerns about attrition bias, further limiting the value of PROM data to accurately capture the benefit of orthopedic treatments across broad populations.43 Accordingly, an urgent need exists to develop and validate alternative and readily quantifiable measures of patient benefit in orthopedics across broad populations to provide evidence for policy evaluations.

In this study, we develop an encounter-based “process of recovery”16,41 patient benefit measure1, 2, 3,16,24,28,29,38,40, 41, 42,52 based on models of care-seeking behavior in orthopedics.5,6,12,17,21,35 While theory suggests that patient care-seeking after standard follow-up can also be influenced by predisposing and enabling factors beyond health-care needs,48 we contend that patient benefit measures drawn from observed health-care utilization data for orthopedic patients after standard follow-up can yield valid proxy measures of patient benefit suitable for policy-based evaluations. In published research using Medicare data for patients with proximal humerus fractures it was found that early-surgery for proximal humerus fracture patients had a strong association with a Recovery Period encounter-based benefit measure, but it was not possible to validate the extent to which this measure accurately tracked patient benefit.9,11

If an encounter-based approach can provide valid proxy measures of patient benefit, this could open the door for much broader use of large observational databases like electronic health records (EHRs) and Medicare claims to assess the comparative value of alternative orthopedic treatments in real world practice. Rotator cuff repair (RCR) represents an ideal population to test and validate this encounter-based measure. RCR is among the most common orthopedic procedures, with well-defined post-operative follow-up schedules, and widely used validated PROMs such as the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score.19,25,27 Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate whether a patient benefit measure derived from shoulder-related orthopedic encounters after routine post-operative follow-up are associated with 12-month ASES scores following RCR. We sought to determine whether routinely captured utilization data may serve as a pragmatic, population-level proxy for patient benefit when PROM data are incomplete or unavailable. We hypothesized that patients with fewer orthopedic-related health-care visits during the Recovery Period would demonstrate higher 12-month ASES scores.

Materials and methods

This study included all adult (18 years and older), English speaking patients who underwent primary RCR at Steadman Hawkins Clinic of the Carolina (SHCC) within the Prisma Health System between January 2017 to December 2022. For each RCR patient, we measured all shoulder-related health-care encounters (ambulatory, emergency department, and inpatient) with a shoulder diagnosis code or surgery after-care diagnosis code during a designated Recovery Period postsurgery using data from Prisma Health System's Orthopaedic Patient Data Repository (OPDR). The OPDR, a collaboration between the Center for Effectiveness Research in Orthopaedics at the University of South Carolina and the Prisma Health System, prospectively and unobtrusively captures clinical and health-care utilization data for all patients with orthopedic conditions treated within the system. The study included patients from 6 surgeons and was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Prisma Health.

Patient-reported outcome measure

Included patients had complete baseline and a post-operative ASES score at the 12-month time point. SHCC routinely collects ASES scores for RCR patients pre-operatively and at 12-month postsurgery through an online survey platform, supplemented by in-clinic data collection and follow-up phone reminders as needed. ASES scores were collected within 30 days 12 months after the surgery date. ASES scores range from 0 to 100 with a higher ASES score indicating lower pain and better function.32

Shoulder-related health-care encounters

All shoulder-related health-care encounters (ambulatory, emergency room, inpatient) were identified from the OPDR using shoulder-specific or surgery after-care International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision diagnosis codes. The postsurgery Recovery Period for RCR patients was designated in consultation with study clinicians to distinguish between routine post-operative follow-up care and a recovery phase during which additional health-care utilization may indicate care-seeking by patients for sub-optimal recovery. At SHCC, routine post-operative follow-up for RCR patients typically includes visits approximately 1-2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3-4 months after surgery, with additional care provided beyond 6 months with patient initiation if complications arise or symptoms persist. Therefore, we designated the period 181-365 days after surgery as the Recovery Period for RCR with health-care encounters during this period signaling the patient sought care beyond routine follow-up for either pain that had not been alleviated, or that the patient had not been able to return to normal function.18

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics, ASES scores, and Recovery Period encounter counts for the patients who completed the ASES baseline survey and were stratified by patients who did and did not respond to the ASES 12-month survey. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Next, we estimated separate outcome models for each benefit measure: the ASES 12-month score and the number of shoulder encounters in the Recovery Period as a function of patient baseline covariates which were available using structured fields in the EHR. Each model included sex, age, marital status, smoking status, insurance status, distance from patient residence to provider practice, surgical procedures billed, and surgeon fixed effects. We then standardized both benefit measures and used a seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) model to evaluate convergent validity by assessing the consistency of baseline covariate relationships between benefit measures.23,45,53

Finally, we regressed the 12-month ASES score on linear and non-linear combinations of the number of shoulder encounters in the Recovery Period to directly test the relationship between the benefit measures while controlling for the patient covariates from the structured fields in the EMR. The estimated models were used to calculate the number of shoulder encounters in the Recovery Period needed to generate a minimally significant reduction in the 12-month ASES score relative to published Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) results for rotator cuff patients.14,49,50 Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Our theory stipulates that the number of shoulder-related encounters during the Recovery Period will be negatively related to the 12-month ASES score. As a result, model parameter estimates are consistent if a positive relationship between a baseline covariate and the 12-month ASES score coincides with a negative relationship between the covariate and the number of shoulder-related encounters in the Recovery Period and vice versa. Consistent relationships were found for all baseline covariates except Medicaid insurance status which had a negative influence on both the 12-month ASES score and shoulder-related encounters in the Recovery Period. Because the benefit measures are assessed with different scales, it is not possible to directly test for differences in these relationships across covariates. To enable testing for differences, we used the SUR method after first standardizing each benefit measure using its respective estimated mean and standard error. SUR combines these standardized benefit measures into a single regression with separate parameter estimates for each covariate on each standardized benefit measure. This structure enabled direct testing of differences in the effects of each covariate between standardized benefit measures to assess convergent validity. Discussion

This study demonstrated that shoulder-related encounters during the Recovery Period and 12-month ASES score have generally consistent relationships with baseline patient covariates, suggesting that these alternative benefit measures are describing similar outcome concepts. Only the influence of Medicaid eligibility was inconsistent across the outcome models. Regression models further showed that the number of shoulder-related encounters during the Recovery Period had a direct and negative association with 12-month ASES scores. Patients with encounters during the Recovery Period were more likely to report lower 12-month ASES scores. Our estimates indicate that patients with 3 shoulder-related encounters during the Recovery Period will have on average a 14.1 lower 12-month ASES score which corresponds to the lower portion of the 11–27-point MCID range reported for rotator cuff patients. Patients with 4 shoulder-related encounters during the Recovery Period had an estimated 18.8 lower 12-month ASES score which reflects the midportion of the MCID range.14,49,50 Patients would require 5 shoulder-related encounters during the Recovery Period for an estimated 23.5 lower 12-month ASES core to reflect the higher portion of the MCID range.

It is important to acknowledge that counts of shoulder-related health-care encounters during the Recovery Period will likely not capture the nuances of patient outcomes found in survey-based PROM responses which can be used to guide patient-specific clinical care and clinically focused research. However, given access to health system EHRs and population claims files like those used in prior Medicare studies,9,11 encounter-based patient benefit measures can be useful proxies in comparative effectiveness and policy studies at the population-level in which it is not feasible to use PROMS to measure patient benefit. For example, Medicare-based research showed that higher rates of early surgery for patients with proximal humerus fractures in 2011 lowered probability by 20 percentage points of patients requiring more than 3 encounters in the Recovery Period after index diagnosis.9 In addition, machine learning algorithms showed that probability of patient benefit varied significantly across patients grouped by baseline factors ranging from a reduction in benefit probability of 32 percentage points to an increase in benefit probably of nearly 60 percentage points.9

Our results fit within the existing literature on patient benefit after RCR. Prior studies describing typical ASES score trajectories after RCR show most patients demonstrating substantial improvement by 12 months and modest additional gains by 24 months.13,44 Previous work also highlighted both the central importance and the practical limitations of routine PROM collection in large health systems.10 As stated above, systems required to collect a complete set of pre-operative and post-operative PROM scores are costly with continued patient survey response compliance problems.47 Another study by Patel et al39 did report an 88.2% PROM completion rate at 2-year follow-up in a multicenter rotator cuff registry, but these high rates of long-term PROM capture were achievable only with substantial infrastructure and dedicated effort. By demonstrating a strong, consistent link between Recovery Period shoulder-related encounters and PROM-defined treatment benefit, our study provides empirical support for using encounter-based measures as proxy measures of patient benefit in empirical settings in which PROM capture is incomplete or inconsistent. We should emphasize that encounter-based measures should not be interpreted as PROM replacements for clinical-based research and individual clinical decision-making. However, our results suggest that when PROM data are inconsistently available or unavailable across broad patient populations, Recovery-Period shoulder-related utilization may provide a useful proxy measure for comparative effectiveness research, quality monitoring, and population-level evaluations of orthopedic treatment outcomes.

Limitations

Our study sample only included patients with a single condition who received the same treatment at the same clinic within a single health-care system. Future work should explore assessing the generalizability of our approach to other settings, orthopedic patients, conditions, and treatments. In addition, our results excluded patients who did not respond to the ASES 12-month survey. Table I shows that non-responders were more likely single, minorities, smokers, and to be on Medicaid or to self-pay for services. Accordingly, there is a risk that our results may not generalize to non-responders. In Appendix A, we report the postsurgery shoulder encounter counts for the non-responders. The distribution of postsurgery shoulder encounters across non-responders is like the distribution for survey responders seen in Table III. For both populations, the percentage of patients with no shoulder-related encounters in the Recovery Period was 66%. Non-responders had a larger mean number of shoulder-related encounters in the Recovery Period, but the mean number of encounters of non-responders was not statistically different than responders. Because the populations had different distributions of baseline covariates, we also computed the mean number of shoulder-related encounters in the Recovery Period for non-responders after applying propensity score weights associated with responding. This drew the estimated number of shoulder-related encounters in the Recovery Period closer to the average of the responders.

We also found that the outcome models in Table IV were inconsistent with respect to Medicaid beneficiaries. Relative to private fee-for-service patients, Medicaid beneficiaries had lower 12-month ASES scores and had fewer shoulder-related encounters during the Recovery Period. Because of the direct influence on insurance coverage characteristics on health-care utilization, it is possible that differences in the breadth of insurance coverage could yield misleading outcome inferences when using encounter-based patient benefit measures across populations with different insurance coverage. This suggests that encounter-based measures may be best-suited to describe variation in patient benefit for patients with the same insurance coverage. In addition, because we only had access to structured information within the EMR, we were unable to measure baseline tear size, number of tendons repaired, and tissue quality, so it remains unknown whether these factors consistently influence both the 12-month ASES score and the number of shoulder-related encounters in the Recovery Period.

Lastly, because we had access to patient encounters within the EHR of a single health-care system, we could count only the shoulder-related encounters sought by patients from providers within that health-care system during the Recovery Period. All shoulder-related encounters sought by patients from physicians and physical therapists during the Recovery Period who were not affiliated with the health-care system could not be identified. Because our main analysis focused on responders to the ASES 12-month survey who likely remained in contact with the health-care system during the Recovery Period, this undercounting may not be problem for this analysis. In fact, over 93% of the patients who responded to the ASES 12-month survey continued to receive health care within the health-care system beyond 12 months after surgery. Ultimately, though, the encounter-based measure used in the study should be best considered a lower bound of the actual number of shoulder-related encounters sought by patients during the Recovery Period. Given this limitation, it remains informative that a strong estimated relationship between the encounter-based measure and the ASES 12-month score remains. The use of encounter-based measures of patient benefit is likely best suited for environments in which researchers have access to an exhaustive accounting of patient encounters like a health-care insurance claims-based database.

Conclusion

These results support the use of counts of shoulder-related encounters during a post-treatment Recovery Period as complementary markers of patient benefit for orthopedic population-level analyses, particularly when PROM data are incomplete or unavailable. Future research using encounter-based measures of patient benefit in orthopedics will likely require condition- and treatment-specific definitions of Recovery Periods that reflect the unique recovery trajectory of each orthopedic condition. Accordingly, further validation across institutions, procedures, and patient populations is needed before encounter thresholds can be broadly used for comparative effectiveness or policy evaluation.
