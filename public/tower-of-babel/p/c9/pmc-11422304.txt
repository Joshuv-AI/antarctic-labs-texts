Geographical Differences in the Safety and Efficacy of Tofacitinib Versus TNFi: A Post Hoc Analysis of ORAL Surveillance
Authors: Bogdan Batko, Slawomir Jeka, Piotr Wiland, Agnieszka Zielińska, Maria Stopińska-Polaszewska, Marcin Stajszczyk, Magdalena Kosydar-Piechna, Mary Jane Cadatal, Jose L. Rivas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn ORAL Surveillance, incidence rates (IRs) of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and malignancies (excluding non-melanoma skin cancer [NMSC]) in cardiovascular (CV)-risk-enriched patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) were numerically greater with tofacitinib in North America versus the rest of the world, due to underlying risk factors. Here, we evaluated the safety and efficacy of tofacitinib versus tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) among patients with RA across geographical regions.MethodsPatients with RA in ORAL Surveillance (NCT02092467), who were aged ≥ 50 years with ≥ 1 additional CV risk factor, received tofacitinib 5 or 10 mg twice daily or TNFi; 45.9% were from either Poland or North America. This post hoc analysis stratified patients by region (Poland, North America, Other countries). Efficacy endpoints included Clinical Disease Activity Index, Disease Activity Score in 28 joints, with C-reactive protein (DAS28-4[CRP]), and Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability Index (HAQ-DI). IRs and hazard ratios for adverse events were reported.ResultsOf 4362 patients (Poland, N = 759; North America, N = 1243; Other countries, N = 2360), more patients from North America versus Poland/Other countries had CV risk factors such as body mass index ≥ 30 kg/m2 and history of diabetes/hypertension; however, more patients from Poland versus other regions were ever smokers and more patients from Poland/North America versus Other countries had history of coronary artery disease. MACE IRs were similar in North America and Poland, and numerically higher versus Other countries. IRs for malignancies (excluding NMSC) were numerically higher in North America versus Poland/Other countries with tofacitinib. Serious infections IRs were numerically higher in North America versus Poland across treatments. Venous thromboembolism/all-cause mortality IRs were generally comparable across regions. DAS28-4(CRP)/HAQ-DI improvements were generally lowest in North America.ConclusionsDifferences in safety outcomes were driven by the presence of baseline risk factors; North America and Poland demonstrated a higher proportion of patients with some baseline CV risk factors/comorbidities versus Other countries.Trial RegistrationNCT02092467 (ClinicalTrials.gov).

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic disease characterized by systemic inflammation and joint damage that affects multiple organs, leading to impairments in physical functioning and quality of life [1, 2]. Disease pathogenesis is multifaceted, with environmental, genetic, and lifestyle factors all contributing to aberrant immune activation [1]. Patients with RA have an increased risk of cardiovascular (CV) disorders versus the general population [3, 4], attributed to the systemic inflammation underlying RA [2, 5].

Early diagnosis and stringent disease control are key in preventing disability and optimizing quality of life in patients with RA; treatment guidelines recommend a treat-to-target approach, involving frequent disease activity monitoring and modifications of treatment as needed in order to achieve remission or low disease activity [6, 7]. Recommendations from the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) emphasize the importance of reducing CV disease risk as part of an RA management plan [8].

The efficacy and safety of the oral Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor tofacitinib in patients with RA have been demonstrated in phase III and phase IIIb/IV randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [9–16] and in long-term extension studies [17–19]. Analyses of registry data from > 30,000 patients with RA reported that JAK inhibitors (including tofacitinib) were less frequently discontinued due to ineffectiveness but more frequently discontinued due to adverse events (AEs) compared with tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) [20]. An improved understanding of the AE risks associated with tofacitinib versus other advanced therapies, in different RA populations, is crucial for tailored therapy.

ORAL Surveillance, a post-authorization study of tofacitinib in CV-risk-enriched patients with RA, showed that risks of the co-primary outcomes, major adverse CV events (MACE) and malignancies (excluding non-melanoma skin cancer [NMSC]), were higher with tofacitinib versus TNFi [21]. Incidence rates (IRs) of MACE and malignancies (excluding NMSC) were numerically greater with tofacitinib in North America versus the rest of the world, but 95% confidence intervals (CIs) overlapped [21], due to variations in underlying risk factors. Additional post hoc analyses have identified high- and low-risk patient populations with different relative risk of AEs with tofacitinib versus TNFi; age ≥ 65 years and long-time smoking (current or past) were identified as differential risk factors for MACE, malignancies (excluding NMSC), myocardial infarction and venous thromboembolism (VTE) [22], and history of atherosclerotic CV disease (ASCVD) was identified as a differential risk factor for MACE [23].

Here, using data from ORAL Surveillance, we expand on the initial findings from this trial by evaluating the safety and efficacy of tofacitinib and TNFi across different geographical regions, specifically Poland, North America, and Other countries. Of randomized and treated patients in ORAL Surveillance, 45.9% were from either Poland or North America. The Poland cohort provided an opportunity to assess efficacy and safety outcomes in a relatively large and generally homogeneous population, including patients with similar baseline demographics who were likely receiving a similar level of care given the public nature of healthcare in Poland.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

ORAL Surveillance was a randomized, open-label, noninferiority, safety endpoint study conducted from March 2014 to July 2020 in patients with active RA despite methotrexate treatment who were aged ≥ 50 years with ≥ 1 additional CV risk factor. Full study details, along with inclusion and exclusion criteria, are published elsewhere [21]. Patients were randomized 1:1:1 to receive oral tofacitinib 5 and 10 mg twice daily (BID), or subcutaneous TNFi (adalimumab 40 mg every 2 weeks [North America] or etanercept 50 mg once weekly [rest of world]). All patients continued their pre-study stable dose of methotrexate unless modification was clinically indicated. In February 2019, the tofacitinib 10 mg BID dose was reduced to 5 mg BID as a protocol amendment across sites in all regions after the Data Safety Monitoring Board noted an increased frequency of pulmonary embolism in patients receiving tofacitinib 10 mg BID compared with TNFi, and an increase in overall mortality with tofacitinib 10 mg BID compared with 5 mg BID and TNFi.

ORAL Surveillance was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation, and was approved by the Institutional Review Board and/or Independent Ethics Committee at each center (Supplementary Material). Patients provided written informed consent.

Baseline CV Risk Assessment

Patients were categorized by history of ASCVD (yes/no), with ASCVD defined as having history of at least one of the following: myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, coronary artery procedure, stable angina pectoris, cerebrovascular disease (stroke, carotid atherosclerosis), peripheral artery disease, or undefined ASCVD. Patients without history of ASCVD were further categorized according to their baseline 10-year risk of MACE, which was calculated using the ASCVD-Pooled Cohorts Equation calculator with a 1.5 × multiplier applied per EULAR recommendations, as previously described [23]. Ten-year risk of MACE categories were defined as high (CV risk score ≥ 20%), intermediate (CV risk score ≥ 7.5%– < 20%), borderline (CV risk score ≥ 5%– < 7.5%), and low (CV risk score < 5%).

Safety

Safety outcomes assessed included treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs; all causalities), treatment-emergent serious AEs (TESAEs; treatment-related), discontinuations due to AEs, and AEs of special interest (AESIs). The following AESIs were reported: adjudicated MACE (60-day risk period), adjudicated malignancies excluding NMSC (total time analysis), serious infections (28-day risk period), VTE (28-day risk period), and all-cause mortality (28-day risk period). Safety endpoints were analyzed and reported for each specific period (28-day, 60-day, or total time) and not by visit.

Efficacy Outcomes and Patient-Reported Outcomes

Efficacy outcomes and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) included change from baseline in Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI); Disease Activity Score in 28 joints, with C-reactive protein (DAS28-4[CRP]); and Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability Index (HAQ-DI) score. Efficacy outcomes and PROs were assessed at baseline and scheduled follow-up visits.

Statistical Analysis

The safety analysis set was analyzed; this included patients who were randomized and received at least one dose of investigational product. Analysis cohorts based on geographical region were defined as (1) Poland, (2) North America (US, Canada, Puerto Rico), and (3) Other countries (this cohort consisted of all countries included in ORAL Surveillance excluding Poland and North America).

Demographic and baseline characteristics were summarized for each geographical region, overall and by treatment arm; these data were stratified further by history of ASCVD (yes/no) and baseline 10-year risk of MACE category.

Percentages of patients with history of ASCVD (yes/no) and within each baseline 10-year risk of MACE category (in those without history of ASCVD) were presented for each geographical region by treatment arm. If patients had missing components, CV risk scores were set to missing.

For each geographical region, crude IRs (number of patients with events per 100 patient-years) and 95% exact Poisson two-sided CIs were generated for each AESI by treatment arm. First events were calculated within the risk period. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% two-sided CIs (combined tofacitinib doses versus TNFi and pairwise comparisons among treatment groups) were estimated using Cox proportional hazard regression models. Missing values were not imputed.

For efficacy outcomes and PROs, repeated measures data for continuous endpoints were analyzed as change from baseline with a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) that included fixed effects of treatment arm, visit, treatment arm by visit interaction, baseline value, and baseline value by visit interaction without explicit imputation for missing values. Least squares means and 95% CIs for each treatment arm are reported. Only visits with > 50 patients in each treatment arm were included in the MMRM.

Demographics and baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The majority of patients across all geographical regions were female and of white race. In North America, a greater proportion of patients had a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2 and were aged ≥ 65 years versus the Poland and Other countries cohorts. In Poland, a greater proportion of patients were ever smokers versus the North America and Other countries cohorts. The proportion of patients who were either ≥ 65 years of age or were ever smokers (‘high-risk’ patients) [22] was similar in the Poland and North America cohorts, and lower in the Other countries cohort. The proportion of patients with C-reactive protein (CRP) > 2.87 mg/l and > 5 mg/l tended to be lower in the North America cohort than the Poland and Other countries cohorts. History of diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, VTE, and cerebrovascular disease were more common in the North America cohort versus Poland and Other countries, whereas a greater proportion of patients from North America and Poland had a history of coronary artery disease (CAD) compared with Other countries. Baseline use of statins, aspirins, and antiplatelets was elevated in the North America versus Poland and Other countries cohorts. In contrast, corticosteroid use was higher in Poland and Other countries versus North America. For each geographical region, demographics and baseline characteristics were generally similar across treatment arms (Table 1).

Discussion

ORAL Surveillance was the first study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of tofacitinib versus TNFi in a CV-risk-enriched RA population [21]. Our post hoc analysis expands on the primary findings of ORAL Surveillance, examining potential geographical differences in safety and efficacy outcomes among randomized and treated patients in this trial. In this study, 45.9% of patients were from either Poland or North America, with Polish patients alone representing 17.4% of the trial population. This, together with Poland’s homogeneity in standards of care (due to a public healthcare system) [24], genetics, and environmental exposure [25, 26], made Poland a useful comparator for North America and Other countries and provided an opportunity to further understand the impact of risk factors on safety outcomes.

Variation in demographics and clinical characteristics, including baseline risk factors, carries implications for healthcare decision-making and predicting which patients may have AEs. The prevalence of RA is known to vary considerably according to geographical region, with differences attributed to genetic factors, environmental exposures, demographics, and socioeconomics [27]. A greater proportion of patients with baseline CV risk factors and comorbidities (such as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, history of diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, VTE, or cerebrovascular disease) noted in the North America cohort versus other geographical regions, a higher proportion of ever smokers in Poland, and a higher proportion of patients with history of CAD in North America and Poland compared with Other countries, may have contributed to geographical differences in incidence of the safety outcomes. Age ≥ 65 years and long-time smoking have previously been identified as differential risk factors for tofacitinib versus TNFi for MACE, malignancies (excluding NMSC), and other AEs (e.g., myocardial infarction and VTE), while a history of ASCVD has been reported as a differential risk factor for MACE [22, 23]. In this analysis, the incidence of MACE was similar in North America and Poland, and numerically higher than for Other countries across treatment arms. Increased incidence of MACE was associated with having a history of ASCVD. The incidence of malignancies excluding NMSC was generally numerically higher in North America compared with Poland or Other countries with tofacitinib, whereas the incidence of serious infections was numerically higher in North America versus Poland, though similar for North America and Other countries. While CDAI was generally similar across regions and treatment arms, improvements in DAS28-4(CRP) were greater for Poland and Other countries versus North America, and improvements in HAQ-DI were greater for Other countries versus North America and Poland, for all treatment arms. The proportion of patients with elevated levels of CRP at baseline tended to be lower in North America versus the other two cohorts. Therefore, baseline CRP levels do not offer an explanation for the lower DAS28-4(CRP) improvements observed in Poland and Other countries versus North America. Efficacy was generally similar between treatment arms; however, an increased risk of some safety outcomes was noted with tofacitinib 10 mg BID versus TNFi (i.e., VTE in North America, serious infections in Poland and Other countries, and all-cause mortality in Poland).

The largest safety analysis of tofacitinib to date in over 7000 patients with RA, using integrated data from phase I–IV trials over 9.5 years, reported a MACE IR (95% CI) of 0.4 (0.3, 0.5) with tofacitinib [28], which was consistent with that reported by others (combined tofacitinib doses: 0.4 [0.3, 0.5]) [29]. However, the IR (95% CI) of MACE with combined tofacitinib doses was greater in the CV-risk-enriched population of ORAL Surveillance: 0.98 (0.79, 1.19) [21]. In the current analysis, IRs (95% CI) for MACE were even greater for CV-risk-enriched patients from Poland and North America (combined tofacitinib doses: 1.25 [0.79, 1.88] and 1.27 [0.88, 1.77], respectively). As noted above, a higher rate of baseline CV risk factors observed in the North America cohort (consistent with higher treatment rates with statins, antiplatelet agents, and aspirin at baseline) accounts for the MACE IRs being greatest in this region. Patients without history of ASCVD had lower MACE IRs, and differences in MACE IRs between combined tofacitinib doses versus TNFi were of lesser magnitude, compared with patients with history of ASCVD. This observation is consistent with a large population-based study of patients with RA in the US, which found a numerically increased risk of CV outcomes among those with, but not those without, a history of CV disease [30].

Tofacitinib treatment in patients with RA has been associated with lipid alterations [31, 32], but potential protective effects against atherosclerosis with tofacitinib have also been reported [33]. A meta-analysis of RCTs including ~ 12,000 patients with RA reported no significant changes to CV risk following treatment with JAK inhibitors (including tofacitinib) [34]. A worldwide 3-year post-marketing surveillance analysis showed that rates of CV events (such as myocardial infarctions, cardiac failure, cardiac disorder, and atrial fibrillation) reduced over time in patients with RA following initiation of tofacitinib treatment [35]. Indeed, in our analysis, HRs of MACE with tofacitinib versus TNFi did not indicate an increase in risk across any of the geographical regions (95% CI included 1).

Patients with RA have an elevated risk for certain types of malignancies but further research is needed to ascertain whether this risk is related to treatments or the underlying disease [36]. Moreover, chronic inflammation plays a role in the pathogenesis of neoplasia [37], while immunosuppressive treatment may be a risk factor associated with cancer [38]. Risk factors associated with CV disease are also related to an increased incidence of cancer [39]. Further, patient demographics and other health-related factors represent an additional driving force behind exposure to cancer-related risk factors, which may vary across geographical locations. The American Cancer Society [40] reports US data demonstrating the variation in risk factors for cancer (such as smoking, obesity [BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2], and physical activity) according to factors such as socioeconomic status, region of the US, and mental illness. Similarly, access to cancer screenings varied according to factors such as education level or household income. Here, malignancy (excluding NMSC) risk with tofacitinib versus TNFi was lower in Poland versus the North America and Other countries cohorts, which appears to be primarily due to the higher incidence of malignancies (excluding NMSC) with TNFi observed in Poland versus North America and Other countries. It is noteworthy that in the general population, IRs for some cancers are reported to be lower in Poland versus other European countries [41]. Analyses of data from 5671 patients with RA with 12,664 patient-years of tofacitinib exposure showed that the incidence of malignancies with tofacitinib were not different to that of other biological disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) [42]. A 5-year post-approval US study reported a comparable rate of malignancies in patients with RA with tofacitinib versus bDMARDs [43]. Taken together, these data suggest that overall malignancy risk and malignancy risk with target therapies may vary in patients with RA depending on geographical region.

A 9.5-year, integrated safety study in patients with RA reported serious infection IRs (95% CI) of 2.8 (2.5, 3.2) and 2.3 (2.1, 2.6) with tofacitinib 5 mg and 10 mg BID, respectively [28]; these IRs were generally lower versus those reported in the current analysis, particularly when taking into account the North America and Other countries cohorts. This difference may partially be due to the ORAL Surveillance population only including patients aged ≥ 50 years. Incidence of serious infection with tofacitinib has been shown to be greater in patients with RA aged ≥ 65 years compared with younger patients [44]. A meta-analysis of data from RCTs of patients with RA revealed that serious infection risk was comparable for tofacitinib and other bDMARDs [45]. A multi-database cohort study of patients with RA reported a similar risk of serious infection for tofacitinib and the TNFi adalimumab [46]. Here, IRs for serious infections were numerically lower for Poland versus other geographical cohorts, regardless of treatment arm. Additionally, tofacitinib 10 mg BID was associated with an elevated risk of serious infections versus TNFi for the Poland and Other countries cohorts.

A strength of this study is that the Polish population provided an opportunity to evaluate safety and efficacy among a large number of patients with similar demographics and healthcare. The post hoc nature of this analysis is a noted limitation. Additionally, while ORAL Surveillance was a noninferiority trial, noninferiority criteria were not applied to this analysis. Moreover, even with randomization processes, it can be difficult to achieve adequate balance across treatment groups in RCTs in terms of demographics and baseline characteristics. Subsequently, it can be difficult to detect and pinpoint the underlying source of variation or interaction in primary analyses. Under-representation of older patients has been reported in RCTs in RA, which limits the evidence base for treatment of older patients in clinical practice [47]. Other limitations include the use of two different TNFi as the comparator treatment arm (i.e., adalimumab in North America and etanercept in the rest of the world) and that ORAL Surveillance was an open-label trial, which carries some inherent bias. Accepting their inherent bias, post hoc investigations are critical for understanding the risk associated with CV factors and how this may be related to a given demographic. The impact of small event numbers in each geographical cohort and the presence of high-risk groups within geographical regions should also be considered. In addition, due to overlapping 95% CIs for IRs and HRs, differences between regions should be interpreted with caution. Further, across geographical regions, 5.7–12.4% of tofacitinib users had a history of prior use of TNFi, so it is possible that these patients were experiencing a refractory period of RA.

Conclusions

We observed differences in demographics and baseline characteristics, including CV risk factors, across geographical regions, which contributed to differences observed in certain safety outcomes across the different regions, regardless of treatment. While CDAI was generally similar across regions and treatment arms, DAS28-4(CRP) and HAQ-DI improvements were generally lowest in North America. Our findings emphasize the importance of assessing CV risk factors when rheumatologists are considering tofacitinib as a treatment for patients with RA. These findings can guide individualized benefit/risk assessments and clinical decision-making on treatment with tofacitinib.
