Identification of in-hospital cardiac arrest aetiologies and return of spontaneous circulation
Authors: Peter Carøe Lind, Lars Wiuff Andersen, Asger Granfeldt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The rate of ROSC is higher in patients with correct identification of aetiology.•This effect is not statistically significant after adjustment for confounders.•ROSC rates differ according to IHCA aetiologies.Keywords: In-hospital cardiac arrest, Aetiology, Outcomes

Introduction

In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) occurs at an incidence rate of approximately 1–10 per 1000 hospital admissions.1, 2, 3 Prognosis is poor with approximately 50–60% of patients achieving return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and only 25–30% of patients surviving to hospital discharge.3, 4, 5 Guidelines emphasise that prevention of IHCA as well as identification and active correction of reversible causes of IHCA are crucial in reducing the incidence of IHCA and in improving outcomes after IHCA.6, 7, 8 However, it remains largely unknown if correct identification of IHCA aetiologies affect outcomes, as this has only been studied previously in a single study for IHCA in general9 as well as in one other study specifically in patients with pulseless electrical activity as the initial rhythm.10

The aim of this study was to investigate if correct identification of IHCA aetiologies affect the rate of ROSC.

Methods

Overview and study population

This study was a post-hoc analysis of the WHY-IHCA study.11 In brief, the WHY-IHCA study was a prospective clinical observational study exploring IHCA aetiologies through blood laboratory analyses, toxicological profiling, and imaging, in 150 adult IHCA patients irrespective of ROSC status. Imaging included contrast-enhanced CT and echocardiography in patients with ROSC and non-contrast enhanced whole-body post-mortem CT and MRI in patients without ROSC. Patients were excluded if the cause of IHCA was very obvious and if the corresponding duration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was very short (e.g., immediate defibrillation in patients with myocardial ischaemia). The study was approved by the regional ethics committee of the Central Denmark region (reference: 1-10-72-154-22). Informed consent was obtained from a legal research guardian, from surrogates, and from patients in whom consent capacity was regained according to Danish law.

Variables

In the WHY-IHCA study, data on presumed aetiologies were obtained by telephone interview with the team leader as soon as possible and typically within 1 h of the IHCA event. The team leader was asked to state their assessment of the presumed aetiology from a list of 7 predefined main categories with 23 associated subcategories.11 Subsequently, aetiologies were determined by an appointed four-person expert panel using the same list of categories based on the patients’ medical charts and the results of the blood laboratory analyses, toxicological analyses, and imaging studies outlined above. All experts reviewed cases independently and were blinded to presumed aetiologies and to autopsy reports.

For our analyses of whether correct identification of IHCA aetiologies affects the rate of ROSC, correct identification was defined as cases in which the presumed main category aetiology was the same as the main category aetiology for at least 3 of 4 experts. If the presumed cause and the expert panel causes were both assessed as unknown, the aetiology was assessed as incorrectly identified.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was ROSC. This was defined as sustained circulation without the need for chest compressions or defibrillations for at least 20 min. Survival at 72 h and at hospital discharge were also reported descriptively, but the effect of correct aetiology identification on these outcomes were not analysed due to sparse data.

Statistical analyses

The effect of correct identification on the rate of ROSC was assessed using logistic regression to yield odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. For these analyses, we constructed an unadjusted model (model 1) and two stepwise adjusted models. The variables for these models were based on author discussion and the construction of directed acyclic graphs. Given 72 ROSC events, we prespecified limited adjusted models and only analysed main category aetiologies to avoid overfitting. Our first adjusted model (model 2) included witnessed status, monitored status, age, and whether the initial rhythm was shockable or non-shockable. In model 3, we further adjusted for the actual aetiology. The actual aetiology was defined as cases with agreement between at least 3 of 4 experts. Hence, the aetiology was categorised as ‘unknown’ in cases where less than 3 of 4 experts agreed.

As exploratory analyses, we performed additional analyses with more liberal and more stringent definitions of correct identification. These were defined as cases where the presumed aetiology was the same as at least 2 of 4 and 4 of 4 expert aetiologies, respectively.

Results

A total of 150 patients were included in this study of which 72 (48%) achieved sustained ROSC. Demographics, cardiac arrest characteristics, and outcomes are presented in Table 1 stratified by whether the aetiology was correctly identified and according to ROSC status in supplementary Table S1. The median age was 71 years in both groups and patients were predominantly male. Overall, the IHCA aetiology was correctly identified in 80 (53%) patients. Of these, 43 (54%) achieved ROSC compared to 29 (41%) in those without correct identification. There were no patients in the primary analysis where the presumed aetiology was unknown and where at least 3 of 4 experts also assessed the aetiology as unknown.

The results of our analyses of the effect of correct identification of aetiologies on the rate of ROSC are presented in Table 3. In our primary analysis, we found a 1.88 times higher odds of ROSC (95% confidence interval: 0.77–4.58) if the IHCA aetiology was correctly identified in our fully adjusted model. In general, these findings were similar across different models as well as in our exploratory analyses of more liberal and stringent definitions of correct IHCA aetiology identification, although with wide confidence intervals.

Discussion

In this WHY-IHCA post-hoc analysis, we explored presumed and expert panel-assessed IHCA aetiologies and how identification of IHCA aetiologies affect the rate of ROSC. We found large variation in ROSC rates according to aetiology groups but consistent rates of ROSC within aetiology groups across presumed and actual aetiologies. Furthermore, we found that correct identification of IHCA aetiologies resulted in a numerically higher odds of ROSC, although this effect was only statistically significant in our exploratory analyses and not in our primary analysis.

This study was inspired by a hypothesis of a potentially higher rate of ROSC in patients with correct identification of aetiology, as this could be explained by these patients receiving early intra-cardiac arrest interventions targeting the underlying cause of IHCA. To our knowledge, only two previous studies have examined a similar research question.9, 10 One study applied a propensity score matching approach in 285 patients and found a 29% absolute increase in 1-h survival in patients in whom the aetiology was correctly identified.9 Another study in 104 patients with pulseless electrical activity (PEA) as their initial rhythm found an increased 30-day survival in patients in whom cause-specific treatment was employed during the cardiac arrest event compared to patients in whom non-specific treatment was employed.10 Both studies were limited by the fact that aetiologies were determined simply by retrospective chart review by single investigators and not through a standardised protocolised investigation in both patients with and without ROSC, as was the case in the WHY-IHCA study.11 Hence, only very limited information was available to qualify aetiologies in patients without ROSC in these previous studies. Moreover, aetiology categorisations were crude (cardiac vs. non-cardiac) and the methods of ascertainment of presumed aetiologies were unclear in one study.9, 12 Furthermore, patients with obvious causes and correspondingly short durations of CPR were excluded in our study. These patients consisted almost entirely of patients with acute coronary syndromes who were immediately defibrillated to a perfusing rhythm.11

Our study has strengths. First, patients were recruited prospectively and subjected to a prespecified protocolised investigation, limiting the risk of our results on actual aetiologies being distorted. Second, our study included detailed investigations in patients without ROSC. Third, we collected information on presumed aetiologies as soon as possible after the event in an effort to limit the risk of these aetiologies being biased by post-ROSC investigations.

This study also had limitations. First, our sample size limited us to explore main category aetiologies and prespecify limited adjusted models. Important effect differences might exist within subcategorical aetiologies. Moreover, the limited sample size resulted in wide confidence intervals for our odds ratio estimates, why potential type 2 error cannot be excluded. Second, no formal between-expert consensus approach was performed in order to obtain unequivocal data on actual aetiologies. Third, although we collected data on presumed aetiologies as soon as possible, we cannot exclude that post-ROSC investigations could have biased the results. Fourth, we cannot rule out the presence of residual or unmeasured confounding that potentially could affect our results as our limited sample size only allowed for adjustment for selected prespecified variables.

Conclusion

The rate of ROSC varies depending on IHCA aetiologies and depending on whether or not the IHCA aetiology was correctly identified. Correct identification of IHCA aetiologies result in numerically higher, albeit not uniformly statistically significant, odds of ROSC.
