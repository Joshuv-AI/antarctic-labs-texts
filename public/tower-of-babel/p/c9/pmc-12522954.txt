Impact of liver cirrhosis on health-related quality of life in Indian patients: interplay of disease severity, alcohol dependence, and malnutrition
Authors: Anurag Kumar Singh, Sachin Kumar, Sumit Rajotiya, Shivang Mishra, Prashant Nakash, Pusparghya Pal, Sourav Debnath, Mahaveer Singh, Deepak Nathiya, Balvir Singh Tomar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver cirrhosis (LC) significantly impairs health-related quality of life (HR-QoL) due to physical, psychological, and nutritional challenges. Despite its growing burden in India, limited evidence exists on how disease severity, alcohol dependence, and malnutrition influence HR-QoL in Indian LC patients.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 565 LC patients attending a tertiary care hospital over 30 months. HR-QoL was assessed using SF-36. Disease severity was graded using the Child-Turcotte-Pugh (CTP) score, while nutritional status and alcohol dependence were evaluated using the RFH-NPT and AUDIT tools. Associations were analysed using Pearson’s correlation, ANOVA, and logistic regression.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 42.5 ± 14.9 years; 81.9% were male, and 58.6% resided in rural areas. Malnutrition was present in 80.9% of patients, and 50.6% were alcohol-dependent. Patients with severe liver cirrhosis (CTP Class C) had significantly lower HR-QoL scores across all domains (p < 0.001). Malnutrition was associated with higher odds of severe liver cirrhosis (OR 1.53; 95% CI: 1.27–1.85), and lower physical function scores were also significantly associated with disease severity (OR 0.89; 95% CI: 0.89–0.95). Severe cirrhosis was additionally marked by elevated serum bilirubin and INR levels, and reduced albumin concentrations.ConclusionHR-QoL declines substantially with worsening cirrhosis, especially in malnourished and alcohol-dependent patients. These findings highlight the need for multidisciplinary interventions focusing on nutrition, lifestyle modification, and psychological support to improve outcomes in LC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-025-02399-3.

Introduction

Liver cirrhosis (LC) is a chronic and progressive liver condition characterized by irreversible scarring of liver tissue due to prolonged injury and inflammation. It is one of the leading global causes of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost and is increasingly driven by metabolic risk factors such as obesity, diabetes, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [1, 2]. In 2017, LC affected approximately 122 million people globally, with a prevalence of 1,395 cases per 100,000 population [2]. The global mortality rate from LC is estimated at 25.3 deaths per 100,000 people. The major contributing factors include alcoholic liver disease (ALD), chronic hepatitis B and C infections, and rising rates of NAFLD [3, 4]. India carries a significant share of the global burden, where LC remains a major cause of mortality and hospital admissions. The death rate due to LC in India is reported at 2.21 per 100,000 population, and this is compounded by lifestyle changes, late diagnoses, and limited access to comprehensive treatment services [5]. These factors highlight the urgent need for patient-centered research and interventions.

Despite improvements in medical management, LC continues to cause substantial deterioration in health-related quality of life (HR-QoL) [6]. HR-QoL refers to an individual’s perception of their physical, emotional, and social well-being in the context of illness [7]. Patients with LC often experience complications such as ascites, muscle wasting, and hepatic encephalopathy, which reduce their ability to perform daily tasks, maintain employment, and engage in social activities [8]. In addition, stigma related to alcohol-associated liver disease can lead to social withdrawal and worsening of mental health [9]. Depression and anxiety are also common in this population and are frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated [10].

Global literature has shown that HR-QoL is a strong predictor of disease progression, hospitalization, and survival among individuals with LC [11]. General instruments like the SF-36 and EQ-5D are widely used to measure physical and mental functioning, while disease-specific tools such as the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ) and the Liver Disease Symptom Index (LDSI) help capture liver-related symptom burden [12–14]. These tools support a comprehensive evaluation of patient experiences beyond clinical or laboratory parameters.

However, there is a paucity of population-based studies in India examining HR-QoL in patients with LC. Most Indian studies have concentrated on biochemical and clinical aspects of liver disease, often overlooking the psychosocial and functional impacts experienced by patients. For instance, a study by Janani et al. evaluated quality of life in LC patients using the CLDQ but did not explore related factors such as perceived stigma or psychological distress [11]. These examples highlight that existing research does not adequately address the lived experiences of LC patients in the Indian context. Moreover, challenges such as fragmented healthcare delivery and limited access to psychosocial support services further contribute to the problem [15].

To address this critical gap, the present study aims to evaluate health-related quality of life (HR-QoL) in Indian patients with LC using the SF-36 questionnaire and identifying psychological well-being, social support, perceived stigma, and nutritional status as key determinants influencing disease outcomes. Specifically, the research aims to evaluate how these factors collectively shape patients’ lived experiences. By offering a comprehensive understanding of how the condition impacts day-to-day functioning, the study aims to inform more patient-centered care approaches and ultimately improve treatment outcomes.

Patients and methods

Study design and study recruitment

This cross-sectional research sought to investigate the HR-QoL in Indian patients afflicted with liver cirrhosis. A total of 565 participants were recruited from the Department of Gastroenterology at NIMS Hospital, a tertiary care center, over a period of 30 months (February 2022 to August 2024).

Inclusion criteria included adults aged ≥ 18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of LC irrespective of etiology, and who were clinically stable. Exclusion criteria comprised pregnant or lactating women, individuals with serious comorbidities (including active malignancy, heart failure, HIV, hepatocellular carcinoma), hepatic encephalopathy Grade III or IV, major psychiatric disorders diagnosed prior to LC, cognitive impairment, renal or pancreatic insufficiency (Fig. 1).

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of NIMS University (Reference number: NIMSUR/IEC/2023/678), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Data collection

Sociodemographic information was collected by one of the study authors using a structured case record form. This form captured key demographic variables, including age, gender, marital status, area of residence, educational level, and substance use habits (such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and tobacco use). Anthropometric data, specifically body mass index (BMI), were also recorded. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). Classification was done according to Asia-specific criteria. Biochemical investigations were conducted in the NABL accredited Biochemistry Laboratory of NIMS Hospital, adhering to standard clinical protocols. These included complete blood count (haemoglobin, platelet count and total leukocyte count) was performed using an automated hematology analyzer (BC-6200, Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics, Shenzhen, China) based on impedance and flow cytometry principles. Liver function tests (SGOT, SGPT, total bilirubin, albumin, and INR) and random blood sugar were measured using an automated biochemistry analyzer (BS-240, Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics, Shenzhen, China), employing photometric, clot-based, and enzymatic methods as appropriate. All analyses were conducted in accordance with hospital procedures and aligned with national standards for hepatic evaluation, as recommended by the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [16]. Ascites grading was performed using abdominal ultrasound.

Instruments used in this study

Child-Turcotte-Pugh (CTP) score

The severity of LC was evaluated using CTP score. It categorizes Patients into three categories: Class A, which indicates good liver function; Class B, which shows moderately impaired liver function; and Class C, which reflects severely impaired liver function [17]. The CTP score is a well-established and widely validated tool commonly used to assess disease severity and predict prognosis in patients with liver cirrhosis.

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT)

The AUDIT is a 10-item screening tool developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) to identify individuals at risk of alcohol misuse or dependence. It has demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.94) and strong construct validity. The tool has also been validated and widely used in Indian populations, supporting its cultural and clinical applicability in this setting [18].

The liver disease symptom index 2.0 (LDSI 2.0)

The LDSI 2.0 is a self-administered tool designed to evaluate symptom burden in patients suffering with LD. All questions pertain to symptoms experienced in the last week and are rated on a Likert scale that goes from “not at all” to “very much.” The results are reported as individual items rather than combined into scales, as this approach is believed to be more useful for clinical practice and patient care [14].

Short form 36 (SF-36)

The RAND Hindi-translated version of the SF-36 tool was utilized to evaluate the mental and physical well-being of patients. It comprises 8 instruments that collectively generate 2 primary summary measures: physical health (PCS) and mental health (MCS). PCS comprises 4 scales: physiological discomfort (PD) (2 items), physical functioning (PF) (10 items), role limitations due to physical health (RL-PH) (4 items), and general health (GH) perceptions (5 items). The MCS comprises 4 scales: mental health (MH) (5 items), social functioning (SF) (2 items), role limitations due to emotional disorders (RL-ED) (3 items), and vitality (4 items). These evaluations measure the influence of emotional issues on daily activities, energy levels, social interactions, and overall mental well-being [19, 20]. In our study population, the tool demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.72 across all domains) and satisfactory construct validity, showing its reliability and relevance.

The Royal Free Hospital- Nutritional Prioritizing Tool (RFH-NPT)

The Royal Free Hospital–Nutritional Prioritizing Tool (RFH-NPT) is widely used to assess malnutrition or the risk of malnutrition in patients with liver disease. It calculates a score based on factors such as recent weight loss, body mass index (BMI), and recent food intake to determine the patient’s nutritional status [21].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 29 (Chicago, IL, USA), with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Normally distributed variables were reported as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and proportions.

A one-way ANOVA was conducted to compare mean differences among CTP classes, with Tukey’s HSD test used for post-hoc comparisons. Independent Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons, and the chi-square test was applied for categorical variables. The correlation between the severity of LC and factors such PCS, MCS, alcoholism, and malnourishment was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient Correlation strengths were interpreted based on Cohen’s conventional criteria, where|r| values of 0.1–0.29 were considered weak, 0.3–0.49 moderate, and ≥ 0.5 strong. A multivariate multinomial logistic regression analysis (reference category- CTP Class A) was conducted to assess the association between LC severity, HR-QoL, and nutritional status.

Results

Demographic details of study participants

A total of 565 enrolled participants’ data was analyzed, having a mean age 42.51 ± 14.85 years. Of them 58.6% hailed from rural areas and 81.9% of overall were male participants. The current and former smokers were 33.5% and 20.7%. Similarly, for alcohol and tobacco consumption it was 51.2%, 29.4% and 27.1%, 12.9% respectively. The mean BMI was 23.99 ± 3.91 kg/m² (Table 1).

Descriptive analysis for alcohol dependency, nutritional assessments and liver disease severity

The mean AUDIT score of participants was 14.85 ± 10.84, with 20.2% were hazardous or harmful drinkers, and 50.6% were identified as alcohol-dependent. The mean LDSI score was 47.90 ± 6.67, where 57.7% had moderate symptoms burden and 42.3% experienced a high symptoms burden. Details about the symptom burden of each domain are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and Fig. 2. The nutritional status was assessed using RFH-NPT, resulting 80.9% were at high risk with a mean score of 47.90 ± 6.67. (Supplementary Table S2).

Comparison of biochemical findings with severity of liver cirrhosis (CTP score)

Participants were classified into three classes on the basis of their CTP score: Class A (6.2%), Class B (40.5%), and Class C (53.3%). Biochemical Findings were compared across the CTP classes using one-way ANOVA, as shown in Table 2. Hemoglobin and albumin levels were significantly lower in Class C participants compared to those in Class A and Class B (p < 0.001). Conversely, total leukocyte count (TLC) (p < 0.001), SGPT, SGOT, and bilirubin levels were significantly higher in Class C, indicating a worsening biochemical profile with the progression of liver cirrhosis. Furthermore, ultrasound of the whole abdomen (USG) revealed a higher grade of ascites in CTP Class C (p < 0.001), analyzed using the Chi-squared test.

Correlation of CTP score with PCS, MCS, AUDIT, LDSI, and RFH-NPT score

Pearson’s correlation indicated a negative correlation between the CTP score and PCS score (r = -0.260, p < 0.001) as well as the LDSI score (r = -0.080, p = 0.058). Conversely, there was a weak positive correlation with the MCS score (r = 0.098, p = 0.040), AUDIT score (r = 0.166, p < 0.001), and RFH-NPT score (r = 0.252, p < 0.001). These results imply that cirrhosis severity worsens, the physical health declines (Table 4 and Fig. 3).

Association of CTP with PCS, MCS, AUDIT and RFH-NPT score

Multinomial Logistic Regression analysis (reference category: CTP class A) established a strong positive association between CTP class B and the RFH-NPT score (OR = 1.362, p = 0.001), suggesting that a unit increase in the RFH-NPT score, the odds of being classified as CTP class B increased by 1.36-fold. This result highlights that malnutrition had a detrimental effect on liver function. Additionally, the PCS (OR = 0.893, p < 0.001) and malnutrition (OR = 1.531, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with CTP class C. Specifically, for each unit increase in the RFH-NPT score, the odds of being classified as CTP class C increased by 1.53-fold. Meanwhile, the PCS score showed that for each unit decrease in physical function, there was an 11% increase in the odds of being classified as CTP class C, reflecting the severe deterioration in physical health among severe cirrhotic patients (Table 5). To ensure model robustness, diagnostic checks including assessment of multicollinearity and goodness-of-fit were conducted.

Discussion

The current cross-sectional study evaluated the HR-QoL status in LC patients using the SF-36, a generic, non-disease-specific tool that offers a comprehensive measure of both MH and PH for patients suffering with any chronic illness [20]. Regarding the severity of the disease, CTP score was used to measure the severity of LC.

The total CTP score demonstrated notable variations across all domains, with group C displaying significantly higher scores compared to groups A and B. A research by Younossi et al. showed similar outcomes, significant differences in the mean overall domain scores among the groups were reported [22].

The intensity of LC is also altered by the symptom burden, measured using the LDSI, which revealed that 42.3% of patients experienced a high symptom burden. Our results indicated that as disease severity worsened, there was a corresponding decline in HR-QoL. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient revealed that an increase in CTP score was significantly linked to reduced HR-QoL in both PH and MH domains. However, multivariate logistic analysis confirmed a significant association only between CTP class C and poor physical function. These findings align with a research by Huo et al., which indicated a strong association between the severity of cirrhosis and diminished HR-QoL, showing that more advanced liver cirrhosis leads to poorer health outcomes [22]. In line with our study’s findings, other studies also demonstrated significant differences in the PCS domain, but no notable differences in the MCS domain [23–25]. Moreover, Arguedas et al. revealed that as the PCS score increases, the MCS score decreases, and likewise, a decline in mental functioning leads to a reduction in PCS [24]. A study by Kalaitzakis et al. identified the CTP as one of the most predictive variables that independently correlated with the PCS [25].

This study also incorporated the Liver Disease Symptom Index (LDSI) to assess symptom burden, which revealed that 42.3% of patients experienced a high symptom burden. Our correlation analysis confirmed that higher CTP scores were significantly associated with lower HR-QoL in both physical and mental domains. However, multivariate logistic regression revealed a significant association only between CTP class C and reduced physical functioning, not with MH. This finding may suggest that physical limitations due to advanced disease are more directly measurable, while mental health might be influenced by other modifiable psychosocial factors.

However, the entire study cohort underwent ultrasound to assess the severity of ascites, a crucial predictor of liver cirrhosis progression and impaired physical function [26]. Grade 3 (severe) ascites were predominantly seen in CTP class C patients. Marchesini et al. identified ascites as a significant factor associated with reduced HR-QoL, particularly impacting three SF-36 domains: bodily pain, GH, and MH [27]. The presence of ascites not only contributes to physical discomfort and immobility but also affects body image and self-perception, thus influencing both physical and psychological health [28].

Furthermore, other biochemical milieu, including SGOT, total serum bilirubin, and INR levels were significantly higher in patients suffering with severe LC or those classified as CTP class C. Although serum albumin levels were notably lower in CTP class C patients. Gines et al., found that low albumin levels are associated with an impaired immune response, making cirrhotic patients more susceptible to infections, which in turn exacerbate liver injury [29].

According to AUDIT screening the present study observed a higher proportion (50.6%) of participants with alcohol dependence, which was significantly correlated with LC severity progression. Previous studies have identified alcohol use disorder as a major risk factor for developing LC [30]. Chronic alcohol intake contributes to direct hepatocellular damage, systemic inflammation, oxidative stress and malnutrition, all of which can accelerate the progression of liver disease. Moreover, alcohol dependence is commonly linked to poor adherence to medical care and diminished social functioning, both of which further impair health-related quality of life (HR-QoL) [31, 32]. In our study, patients with severe liver cirrhosis (CTP Class C) exhibited a high prevalence (80.9%) of severe malnutrition, as measured by the RFH-NPT. This finding underscores the urgent need for regular nutritional assessments and targeted interventions in the management of liver cirrhosis. Multivariate logistic regression further revealed that patients with severe cirrhosis had a 53% higher risk of malnutrition compared to those with less advanced disease. Malnutrition is strongly associated with greater disease severity and poorer HR-QoL in cirrhosis patients, primarily due to factors like anorexia, malabsorption, hypermetabolism, and early satiety, which collectively increase frailty, complications, and impair physical functioning and well-being [30]. This not only increases frailty and the likelihood of complications but also negatively affects physical performance and overall well-being. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that alcohol consumption contributes significantly to malnutrition in individuals with liver cirrhosis, thereby accelerating the progression of alcoholic liver disease [33].

While several findings demonstrated significant associations, others did not. For example, the absence of a significant relationship between the CTP score and the Mental Component Summary (MCS) may be attributed to individual variability in mental resilience [34], limited sample size, or possible underreporting due to stigma or cognitive impairment [11]. Additionally, the presence of subclinical hepatic encephalopathy may have confounded mental health assessments, affecting the accuracy of MCS scoring [35]. These non-significant results warrant cautious interpretation and could benefit from more nuanced psychological assessments in future research.

Our study is not without any limitations, the single-centered and cross-sectional study design made it challenging to assess causal relationships among LC severity and HR-QoL and were unable to reflect temporal changes or trends in outcomes. It also impacts the generalizability and validity of our findings. Furthermore, due to the cross-sectional nature of the data, we cannot infer the directionality of the observed associations. It remains unclear whether declining liver function leads to poor physical and nutritional status, as reflected by reduced HR-QoL scores, BMI, and high malnutrition prevalence or whether existing physical frailty and nutritional deficits contribute to worsening liver outcomes. These relationships are likely to be complex and bidirectional, and future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these dynamics.

We also did not include patients with overt hepatic encephalopathy to avoid selection bias, as it may have influenced cognitive function of our study participants and consequently their responses to the questionnaire. Moreover, exclusion of Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score in our study may limit our ability to accurately assess liver disease severity, potentially introducing confounding variables and reducing the generalizability of our findings compared to studies that utilize the MELD score as a standard metric. Another consideration is that PCS and MCS scores in our study were derived using factor-weighting methods based on U.S. population norms, which may not fully capture cultural or regional differences in health perception. While these scoring systems are globally accepted, their application in non-U.S. populations should be interpreted cautiously.

Future longitudinal studies should assess how HR-QoL evolves over time in response to medical and lifestyle interventions. Additionally, qualitative research may help capture the lived experiences of LC patients, particularly regarding mental health challenges.

Despite all these drawbacks, our study evaluated the HR-QoL of LC patients using the most global standard tool SF-36 tool and the severity of LC was determined by the Child-Turcotte-Pugh (CTP) score. Further research with a larger sample size and consideration of additional factors, such as comorbidities, disease duration, and healthcare access, is necessary to confirm and expand on these findings.

Conclusion

This study reveals significant insights into the profound impact of liver cirrhosis (LC) on patients’ quality of life, with severe cases (CTP Class C) leading to considerable physical and mental health impairments. Advanced cirrhosis patients face limitations in daily activities, increased pain, and diminished overall health. Alcohol dependence and malnutrition were identified as major factors exacerbating liver function decline and reducing quality of life. The findings highlight the need to address these lifestyle factors in conjunction with medical treatment. Early intervention, comprehensive care, and psychological support are essential for improving the well-being of LC patients, particularly in India, where socio-economic and healthcare challenges contribute to disease progression.
