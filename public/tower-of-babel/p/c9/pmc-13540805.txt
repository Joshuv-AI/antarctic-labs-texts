Spatiotemporal trends in food deserts, food swamps and food environments around schools in Wallonia, Belgium (2008–2024)
Authors: Nicolas Cammue, Nicolas Berger, Fanny Pandolfi, Stefanie Vandevijvere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFood environments influence dietary behaviors and nutritional health outcomes. Food deserts, areas characterized by limited spatial access to healthy foods, and food swamps, areas where unhealthy outlets predominate, present challenges to the adoption and maintenance of a healthy diet. This study analyzed trends in access to healthy and unhealthy food environments in neighborhoods and around schools in Wallonia, Belgium, over the period 2008–2024. The region remains understudied and offers a particularly insightful case due to its mix of urban and rural areas.MethodsWe combined data on geocoded food outlets (Locatus database), short chain initiatives (LogCiCa), schools, road networks, residential addresses and area socio-demographic characteristics to identify and quantify food deserts, food swamps and the food environment within 1000m around schools across the study region. Potential food deserts were defined as areas lacking a supermarket within 1000 m road distance and a bus stop within 500 m, while actual food deserts additionally required high proportions of low-income households or elderly (65 years and older). Food swamps were quantified using the modified Retail Food Environment Index (mRFEI). Food environments around 4,696 schools were assessed using 1000 m road-network buffers. Temporal trends were evaluated using linear regression.ResultsBetween 2008 and 2024, approximately 11% of households lived in potential food deserts, with fewer than 2% residing in actual food deserts. In contrast, food swamps were widespread: in 2024, 52.2% of households were located in sectors where unhealthy outlets outnumbered healthy ones. Sectors with high proportions of elderly residents or children were disproportionately affected. Around secondary schools, traditional fresh food retailers declined substantially (fruit and vegetable stores − 60%), while takeaway and delivery outlets increased threefold. By 2024, over 90% of secondary schools were located in predominantly unhealthy food environments within walking distance.ConclusionsIn Wallonia, exposure to unhealthy food environments is widespread and persistent, particularly around schools and in socio-demographically vulnerable areas, while food deserts remain relatively rare. Policy efforts addressing the density and spatial distribution of unhealthy outlets may contribute to reducing dietary inequalities and improving population health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13690-026-02012-z.Keywords: Food environment, Belgium, Wallonia, Food deserts, Food swamps, mRFEI

Background

Food environments represent the complex interactions between physical, economic, policy, and sociocultural contexts that shape individuals’ dietary choices and eating behaviors [1–3]. Food environments include the availability, accessibility, affordability, marketing and nutritional quality of food options within particular geographic regions and social contexts, ultimately determining population dietary patterns and related health outcomes [4]. Growing evidence demonstrates the significant contribution of food environments in the worldwide increase of diet-related chronic diseases, such as obesity, diabetes mellitus type 2, and cardiovascular diseases [5, 6]. Globally, 2.5 billion people suffered from overweight in 2022, and this number is expected to be even higher today [7]. Beyond their role in disease prevention, food environments also constitute a key lever for health promotion, as they shape the structural conditions that enable individuals and communities to make healthier choices, thereby supporting overall well-being and reducing health inequalities [8].

Research on diets has evolved from focusing on individual dietary behaviors to examining the structural determinants, especially food environments, that either facilitate or obstruct healthy eating practices [9, 10]. Variations in food environments across socioeconomic and geographical settings, can create significant implications for population health equity [2]. Geographic areas with restricted access to nutritionally and economically affordable food options, are commonly referred as "food deserts,". To date, studies on food deserts have been mainly conducted in the US, UK and Canada [2]. The unequal distribution of and access to healthy food options adds to dietary inequalities and poor health outcomes that mainly affect marginalized populations such as socio-economically disadvantaged communities, racial and ethnic minorities, and residents of specific geographical regions [11, 12]. Living in a food desert has been linked to adverse health outcomes, with studies in the US reporting an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases [13] and lower chances of survival for stage 2 and 3 of colorectal and breast cancer [14].

Food swamps are generally defined as geographic areas where unhealthy food retailers outnumber healthy food retailers [15, 16]. Conversely, food oases are areas where healthy food options such as fruits, vegetables, or local and fresh food options are abundant and easily accessible [17, 18]. Several studies have demonstrated the potentially harmful effects of food swamps on population health. For instance in the US, Cooksey-Stowers et al. [15] found that food swamps are better predictors of obesity rates than food deserts. Additionally, Phillips et al. [19] showed that adults with diabetes living in food swamps have a higher hospitalization rates. Also, food swamps tend to be more common in more deprived areas [20] reinforcing existing health inequalities. However, the applicability of the concepts of food deserts and swamps to continental European settings requires adaptation as the European region is characterized by denser urban fabrics, a stronger presence of small independent retailers, and different food cultures requires adaptation [21, 22]. Comparative research between European and North American cities has indeed highlighted that food shopping behaviours and their relationships with socioeconomic status differ substantially across contexts [23], and that measuring food environments in European settings requires adapted methodological frameworks [24]. In the Netherlands, for instance, Helbich et al. [25] found no clear evidence of food deserts in Amsterdam, suggesting that in dense European cities, physical access to supermarkets may be less of a constraint than in the US. Also, in the UK, scholars observed an increasing number of takeaway food and fast-food restaurants in disadvantaged communities, which seems to be structural and constant both in urban and rural environments [26]. In this context, the widespread availability and visibility of unhealthy food options raise particular concerns for younger populations. Current food environments do not consistently support healthy eating [5], and unhealthy eating habits established during childhood and adolescence are more likely to persist into adulthood [27]. Early exposure to unhealthy food environments have lasting consequences for both physical and mental health [28, 29]. In this regard, schools constitute particularly relevant settings, as the surroundings may influence dietary behaviors and shape health trajectories across the life course.

Belgium represents an interesting case study for the study of food environments due to its high population density, extensive urbanization, pronounced regional heterogeneity, and complex federal governance system composed of multiple linguistic and cultural regions with different legislation [30]. These characteristics generate unique food environment patterns. To date, community retail food environments were only studied in the Flemish region up to 2020 [16], where extensive availability and promotion of unhealthy foods has been observed, especially in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas. About 28.3% of the Flemish population lived in a potential food desert in 2020. Furthermore, 88.2% of the Flemish population resided in food swamps, where unhealthy food outlets outnumbered healthy ones in 2020. Food environments around Flemish schools became less healthy between 2008 and 2020, with a stark increase in the number of fast food outlets and convenience stores within a buffer zone of 1000 m [31].

The retail food environment in Wallonia, Belgium, has not been specifically investigated, and structural, cultural, and legislative differences with Flanders, Belgium, prevent direct extrapolation of findings.

According to the latest national food consumption surveys [32], Wallonia had the highest obesity prevalence among Belgian regions, reaching 20% compared to 14% in Flanders and 18% for Brussels and other studies have likewise indicated that Wallonia is the most socioeconomically deprived region in Belgium [33, 34]. Such disparities suggest that food environmental determinants may play a critical role in shaping dietary behaviors and health outcomes, highlighting the need for dedicated studies in Wallonia.

This study analyzed trends in access to healthy and unhealthy food environments in residential neighborhoods and around schools in Wallonia, over the period 2008–2024.

Methods

Study area

The study area covers the Walloon region of Belgium. Wallonia is divided into 262 communes and 9,876 statistical sectors (the basic territorial units) [35]. In 2024, with a population of 3,662,245, which corresponds to 31.6% of the Belgian population, living in an area of 16,844 km2, Wallonia had a population of 218 inhabitants/km2 but was still significantly urbanized, with population centers concentrated around cities such as Liège, Charleroi, and Namur [30]. The proportion of uninhabited land accounted for approximately 27.1% of Wallonia’s territory [36]. Around 19% of the population were 65 years or older, and 17% were under 15 years old. The median gross monthly salary (2022) was around 3,540€ [37]. In 2018, 74% of Walloons reported being in good or very good health [38].

Data sources

Multiple datasets were combined (Table 1). The Locatus Database [39] from 2008, 2013, 2020, 2022 and 2024 was used to derive information on all food retail locations and shopping, covering a 16-year period for which comparable data were available. This database is covering 3 countries: The Netherlands, Belgium and the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg and has previously been field validated in the Netherlands, showing high accuracy and strong agreement with field audits [40]. The data includes geographical coordinates, names of food outlets, addresses, retail types, and floor sizes for all listed food outlets.

Data sources used in the study

The Locatus database, however, contains very few short chain initiatives, which are initiatives that minimize the number of intermediaries between local producers and consumers, such as farm collection points and local markets [45]. To complete our data, we retrieved these points of sales from the LogCiCa website [41]. This website includes an inventory of short chain initiatives, such as community supported agriculture, farmers’ outlets, bio-shops and local products outlets, located in Wallonia and Brussels, Belgium. This database was available only for 2024 and therefore could not be used for other years. Some duplicates were present across the LogCiCa and Locatus datasets. To avoid any redundancies, it was decided to delete the overlapping point of sale from the LogCiCa database as it has been used to complete the Locatus data.

For the other datasets, the Belgium road network, bus stops, places of worship (POFW) where religious services are conducted as well as points of interest (POIs), Dataset containing places or destination that someone might find interesting or useful, as the police station, the fire station, library, courthouse, prison, shops, pharmacy,… were extracted from OpenStreetMap (OSM), an open-source and crowd-sourced geographic information platform providing freely accessible spatial data [43].

The geographical boundaries, statistical sectors, municipalities and socio-economic data at these levels were provided by Statbel [30]. The dataset containing the location and types of schools (primary, secondary schools and universities and university colleges) was collected from [44].

Geographical analysis on food environments

Spatial mapping and analyses were conducted at the statistical sector level across the entire region of Wallonia, Belgium for Potential Food Deserts (PFD), Actual Food Deserts (AFD), food oases, and food swamps. Additionally, the percentage of households in PFD, AFD, food oases, and food swamps was estimated using residential address data corresponding to the private home location of households. Food swamps and food oases, as well as distances to retailers and retailer density, were also assessed around schools. Conducting the analysis at the statistical sector level allows linkage with other socio-demographic data. The spatial analyses were conducted using QGIS 3.40 and PostGIS 3.5.0.

Households identification

Using residential addresses allows fine-scale measurement of spatial exposure to food environments. It provides a more precise understanding of these phenomena and allows the evolution of affected addresses to be monitored over time.

To identify the Households, we used the ICAR layer, which is based on the Walloon regional address register [42]. We then used OSM layers to exclude all non-residential addresses, such as POIs or POFW [43]. Addresses corresponding to food retailers listed in the Locatus database were also excluded. After this process, we obtained 1,753,680 residential address points or households representing 88.8% of the total addresses in the ICAR layer for Wallonia.

Potential (PFD) and actual food deserts (AFD)

A potential food desert (PFD) [2, 16] is a geographical area where inhabitants have limited or no access to some or all of the foods necessary for a nutritionally adequate diet [46–48]. PFD have been defined as areas with no supermarket within 1000 m of road distance and no bus stop within 500 m of road distance [16]. Supermarkets were defined according to the Locatus classification which distinguishes supermarkets from smaller food outlets based on a minimum sales areas of 149m2.The latter accounts for the fact that elderly may struggle to walk the full 1000 m and may instead rely on public transport for grocery shopping. Other studies conducted in Belgium adopted the same definition [16, 49]. Supermarkets were used as the reference retailer because they provide the broadest and most affordable range of food products and are commonly used as the benchmark in food desert research.

To identify PDF, a 500 m service area was created around the bus stops, based on the road network available [43]. The service areas were then used with the convex hull tool to create a geometric envelope of each service area. The convex hull was used solely to simplify the geometry of contiguous service areas and did not influence the accessibility calculations themselves. Similarly, a 1000 m service area polygon was defined around supermarkets based on the same road network. The polygons representing areas served by bus stops and supermarkets were merged to form a single zone covering areas accessible both by public transport and in close proximity to a supermarket. The Wallonia polygon was then used to define the geographical limits of the analysis. A cutting operation was then carried out, subtracting the areas served by bus stops and supermarkets from the Wallonia polygon. The end result is a polygon representing PFD in Wallonia: areas where there is neither a supermarket within 1000 m nor a bus stop within 500 m. However, this method only allows to identify the number and percentage of people living in a PFD in Wallonia, it cannot be used to identify the actual area size (m2) as it also includes nature parks and non-residential zones where it is not necessary neither expected to have supermarkets and/or bus stops available. The results in this study will therefore be expressed in terms of population rather than surface area. Based on the PFD, two types of AFD were then identified using additional demographic data (2024) on the number of elderly (65 years and over) and on the median disposable household income (2022) for each statistical sector [30]. Actual food deserts (AFD) refer to geographically and socially disadvantaged areas. First, statistical sectors falling within the lowest two deciles of median household disposable income, that is, the 20% of sectors with the lowest income levels, were intersected with PFD areas to identify low-income food-inaccessible zones. Second, statistical sectors falling within the highest two deciles for the number of elderly residents, or the 20% of sectors with the highest concentrations of older adults, were similarly intersected with PFD areas. Each intersection was performed independently, yielding two distinct AFD layers reflecting different dimensions of social vulnerability. This choice was guided by previous demographic and socioeconomic studies that use extreme-quantile thresholds to capture populations most exposed to structural vulnerabilities [34, 50].

The location of households situated in food deserts was analyzed according to the DEGURBA classification in order to determine the level of urbanization in which these households were predominantly located. The DEGURBA classification [51] applied to the Walloon territory characterize neighborhoods by degree of urbanization [36]. This classification, defines seven major territorial categories using a 1 km2 population grid: Urban centers, Dense urban clusters, Semi-dense urban clusters, Suburban cells, Rural clusters, Low-density rural cells and Very low-density rural cells.

It is based on two main criteria: the total population size of the group of cells and population density in inhabitants per square kilometer. The population size criterion distinguishes large urban agglomerations (≥ 50,000 inhabitants), medium and small towns (5,000–49,999 inhabitants), small settlements (500–4,999 inhabitants), and areas with no minimum population threshold. Population density further differentiates high-density urban areas (≥ 1,500 inhabitants/km2), semi-dense areas (300–1,499 inhabitants/km2), and rural areas (< 300 inhabitants/km2).

Food swamps and oases

The Modified Retail Food Environment Index (mRFEI) [15, 52], which describes the proportion between the number of healthy and unhealthy food retailers was estimated at the statistical sector level.

The mRFEI was selected as the primary measure of food swamps for several reasons. First, it allows direct comparability with the Flemish study [16], which relied on the same indicator and the same Locatus database. Second, because it is ratio-based, the mRFEI reflects the balance between healthy and unhealthy outlets within a given area, which corresponds closely to how food swamps are conceptually defined. We applied the same classification of healthy, unhealthy, and neutral retailers as it was done in Flanders [16]. This classification was developed by an expert committee from Flanders, composed of 15 dieticians, food scientists, and food policy advisors, who rated healthiness of outlets on a scale from 1 to 5. Retailers from Locatus database with a score of 4 or 5 are considered healthy like greengrocers, farm stores, bio stores, dried fruit stores, fish markets while those with a score of 1 or 2 are classified as unhealthy such as fast-food restaurants, night shops, convenient stores and gas stations. The convenience stores include the following Locatus categories: '11.010.132-Chocolate', '11.010.657-Candystores', '59.210.246-Ice cream parlour', '11.010.309-Minisuper', '11.010.350-Nightshop'. Retailers with a score of 3 were categorized as neutral such as supermarkets or minisupers (supermarkets but the surface is less than 149m2) and full service restaurants. These “neutral” points of sales were therefore not taken into account in the calculation of the mRFEI because their food offerings include both healthy and unhealthy products, making them difficult to classify within a binary framework. While several international studies have applied the mRFEI using similar ratio-based principles [16, 53–55], methodological differences exist that should be noted when making cross-national comparisons. Studies across the food environment literature, classify supermarkets as either healthy [53, 54, 56], unhealthy [57], or neutral [16, 55]. In our article, the classification of supermarkets as neutral for the mRFEI reflects their dual role as providers of both a wide variety of healthy and unhealthy products.

The full classification and definitions of the outlets can be found in Additional file 1.

The Points of sales from the LogCiCa database were classified as healthy (5) for the calculation of the mRFEI index. Short chain initiatives could only be included for the 2024 assessment as unlike for the Locatus database, historical data for the LogCiCa database were not available.

The mRFEI with values ranging from 0 to 1 for each statistical sector. A lower score (mRFEI < 0.5) indicates a lower proportion of healthy food retailers which means that the area is a food swamps and on the contrary, a higher score (mRFEI > 0.5) reflects a higher proportion of healthy food options and thus is a food oases. An mRFEI score of 0.5 indicates an equal number of healthy and unhealthy food outlets. The mRFEI was also crossed with data at the level of statistical sectors characterized by high concentrations of elderly and young populations, defined as sectors with the two highest deciles in the number of residents aged 65 years and over and under 15 years, respectively. Additionally mRFEI was also crossed with statistical sectors data characterized by low income (with the two lowest income deciles).

Food environment around schools

A total of 4696 schools were included: 2481 primary schools, 1985 secondary schools and 230 university campuses. We examined changes over time for several types of food outlets located around schools (fruit and vegetable stores, bakeries, butcher shops, fast-food outlets, takeaway and delivery outlets, and convenience stores). For each outlet type, the mean number within a 1000 m road-network buffer was calculated, together with the 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), as well as the proportion of schools with at least one outlet within this distance. Changes in the median distance and interquartile range (IQR) from secondary schools to the nearest outlet of each type were also assessed. Results for primary schools and universities are presented in Additional file 2 and Additional file 3. The mRFEI was calculated at the school level by counting healthy and unhealthy food outlets located within 1000 m along the road network around each school [31, 49] using both Locatus and LogCiCa datasets.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.2. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the distribution of food deserts, food swamps, and retailer types across years. Proportions of households located in potential food deserts (PFD), actual food deserts (AFD), food swamps, and food oases were calculated for each time point (2008, 2013, 2020, 2022, and 2024).

Temporal trends were assessed using linear regression models with calendar year treated as a continuous independent variable. These models were used as descriptive tools to summarise average annual changes across the observation period. Given the limited number of historical time points and the objective of capturing long-term directional trends, year was modelled as a continuous variable. For analyses at the household level, the annual proportion of households exposed to each food environment category was used as the dependent variable. At the statistical sector level, the proportion of sectors classified in each mRFEI category was modeled over time. For school-based analyses, the mean number of outlets within a 1000 m road-network buffer was modeled as a function of year. Regression coefficients (β) represent the average annual change in the outcome variable. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. Given the ecological and repeated cross-sectional nature of the data, models were not adjusted for additional covariates, and results are interpreted as population-level trends rather than causal effects.

Sensitivity analyses for 2024 were conducted by comparing estimates derived from the Locatus database alone with estimates incorporating additional short-chain initiatives from the LogCiCa dataset to assess potential influence on mRFEI classification.

Food environments around schools

In 2024, convenience stores were the most represented stores around secondary schools (6.7 stores on average), followed by fast-food outlets (4.7) and delivery services (4). These were then followed by more traditional shops such as bakeries (2.5), butchers (1.5), fruit and vegetable retailers (0.2). Even though the number of convenience stores increased less, their number were already high and they were the most represented type of outlets around schools in 2008, and still are in 2024.

Between 2008 and 2024, the food environment around secondary schools shifted from traditional fresh food retailers toward convenience stores, fast food, and delivery services. A general decline was observed in traditional retailers, including greengrocers, bakeries and butcher shops around the secondary schools. For instance the number of greengrocers outlets decreased by 60.2% (β = −0.02; 95% CI: − 0.03 to −0.01; p-value = 0.01), the bakeries and the butcher outlets also decreased by 34.8% (β = −0.09; 95% CI: − 0.13 to −0.05; p-value = 0.01) and 37.3% (β = −0.06; 95% CI: − 0.06 to −0.05; p-value < 0.01). The share of schools with at least one of these outlets also declined, particularly the proportion of schools with at least one fruit and vegetable store, which nearly halved.

Fast-food outlets and convenience stores around secondary schools increased by 22.1% and 10%, respectively during the study period, but the trends were not statistically significant (fast-food outlets: β = 0.04; 95% CI: − 0.05 to 0.13; p = 0.25; Convenience stores: β = 0.06; 95% CI: − 0.02 to 0.12; p = 0.09). Importantly, these outlet types were already highly prevalent around schools in 2008, with 81.9% of schools having at least one fast-food outlet or convenience store in their vicinity at that time. In contrast, the most pronounced increase was observed for takeaway and delivery services. Since 2008, the number of these establishments has increased by approximately 300% (β = 0.2; 95% CI: 0.16 to 0.23; p < 0.01). Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive spatiotemporal assessment of food deserts, food swamps, and school food environments in Wallonia over a 16-year period. The findings reveal a dual pattern: while food deserts remain relatively rare and stable over time, unhealthy food exposure, particularly in the form of food swamps and deteriorating school food environments, is widespread and persistent. Large segments of the population, both in neighborhoods and around schools are increasingly exposed to environments dominated by unhealthy food outlets. In European contexts, where supermarket density is generally high and public transport reduces distance constraints, limited physical access to food is rarely the main issue [25]. Instead, the predominance of convenience stores, fast-food outlets and delivery-oriented retailers represents a more structural challenge [15, 16]. The 2020 observation coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have temporarily affected food retail operations and accelerated growth in takeaway and delivery services. Because the available data represent repeated cross-sectional snapshots rather than continuous monitoring, pandemic-related effects could not be isolated.

Food deserts: limited but socially patterned

In total, more than 11% of the households were located in potential food desert (PDF). When considering actual food deserts (AFD) through low income or the oldest segment of the population, they remain a relatively marginal phenomenon in Wallonia, Belgium, affecting less than 2% of households in 2024. AFD related to low-income populations have remained relatively stable since 2013, while those related to older populations have slightly increased since 2008, highlighting the importance of considering demographic heterogeneity in the lack of access to healthy food. Wallonia is experiencing different socio-economic trends to those observed in Flanders, Belgium [30, 58]. The ageing population trend is less pronounced but still present [59] and the increasing income gap between high and low income groups is already well established. This may turn more PFD into AFD in the future, thereby affecting a larger part of the population [16].

Food swamps: the dominant feature of Walloon food environments

Food swamps represent a significant concern in Wallonia, Belgium affecting 52.2% of households (even though they have declined slightly since 2008). Indeed, frequent and continuous exposure to unhealthy food outlets encourages impulse purchases and normalizes the consumption of unhealthy foods, especially among young people [5]. Additionally, marketing strategies such as promotions, extended hours, and delivery services have been shown to further influence dietary choices in ways that traditional grocery stores do not [22, 60]. However, food swamps are less common than in Flanders, where 88.2% of dwellings were located in a food swamp in 2020 [16]. The substantial difference between Wallonia and Flanders in food swamp prevalence warrants consideration of several structural, contextual and methodological factors. First, the two regions differ markedly in terms of population density and settlement patterns: Flanders (~ 504 inhabitants/km2) is nearly twice as densely populated as Wallonia (~ 219 inhabitants/km2) and is characterized by a highly dispersed built environment [61] with a characteristic pattern of roadside linear development [62], which generates a near-ubiquitous presence of commercial activity, including unhealthy food outlets, across almost all statistical sectors [16]. Indeed, Belgium as a whole scores the highest in Europe on the Weighted Urban Proliferation index, with this phenomenon being most pronounced in Flanders [63]. In contrast, Wallonia's more rural and sparsely populated areas contain many statistical sectors where insufficient food retail activity precludes mRFEI calculation altogether, as reflected by the 41.8% of Walloon sectors for which no mRFEI score could be derived in the present study. Even though this does not necessarily imply a complete absence of food retailer. Some outlets may be present but classified as neutral, such as supermarkets. Socioeconomic and cultural differences may also play a role: Wallonia has a lower GDP per capita [64] and a distinct food culture, with a relatively stronger tradition of local markets, bakeries, and independent food retailers [65]. Methodological differences between the two studies should also be considered when interpreting inter-regional comparisons. In the Flemish study [16], food swamps were assessed by counting healthy and unhealthy food outlets within residential areas constructed by buffering a 1,000 m road-network distance around the centroid of each statistical sector. While this approach offers a standardised catchment area, it relies on a single central point to represent the entire sector, meaning that residents living beyond 1,000 m from the centroid, a situation particularly likely in large or irregularly shaped sectors such as those found in Wallonia. In the present study, the mRFEI was computed using the full administrative boundaries of statistical sectors as the unit of analysis, ensuring that all food outlets present within a sector contribute to the score regardless of their distance from any central reference point. Taken together, these structural differences might explain the differences in food swamps between the two regions and should be considered when interpreting inter-regional comparisons.

Statistical sectors characterized by disadvantaged socio-economic factors are more affected by food swamps. Geographical sectors with a high proportion of elderly people are mainly classified as food swamps (71.3%). The same applies to sectors with a higher proportion of children under 15 years (75.2%). Although the association is less pronounced for low-income sectors, exposure remains higher than the Walloon average (48.6% vs. 26.9%). This aligns with international findings, as food swamps are predominantly located in disadvantaged areas [66, 67]. These mechanisms may partly reflect the spatial clustering of residential neighbourhoods: areas with lower Socioeconomic status tend to be located in dense residential zones where convenience-oriented outlets are more likely to cluster [68, 69].

The transformation of school food environments

The analysis of secondary school food environments revealed that children and adolescents in schools are increasingly exposed to convenience stores (+ 10%), fast food outlets (+ 22.1%), takeaway and delivery retailers (+ 300%), while traditional stores such as bakeries (−34.8%), greengrocers (−60.2%), and butchers (−37.3%) continue to decline.

The increase in convenience stores and fast-food outlets appears less pronounced than that observed for delivery retailers. However, this trend must be interpreted in context, as these two types of outlets were already highly prevalent around schools in 2008, with 81.9% of schools having at least one convenience store or fast food outlet within a 1000 m road network distance. In contrast, the decline in access to fruit and vegetable stores is particularly striking. While such outlets were present around 35.2% of schools in 2008, this proportion had fallen to only 17.6% by 2024. These results are consistent with the study conducted in Flanders [31]. This is also confirmed by the fact that 83.1% of Walloon schools are located in food swamps. Moreover, the distances to reach unhealthy food outlets are considerably shorter than those to fruit and vegetable stores, with, for example, nearly twice the distance required to reach a fruit and vegetable store compared to a fast-food outlet. These differences between the school types could be driven by their geographical distribution: the higher the school level is, the more frequently they are located in dense urban centers, where both retailer density and exposure to unhealthy outlets are higher (see Additional file 5). The study conducted in Flanders on school environments found a positive association between the density of fast-food outlets around schools and overweight among pupils under 6 years old as well as those aged 6 to 12 [31]. Together, these indicate a structural transformation of food environments around schools, with an increasing presence of convenience stores, fast-food outlets and delivery-based retailers, alongside a continued decline in traditional and healthier outlets such as greengrocers and bakeries. The low mRFEI values observed around schools, combined with shorter distances to unhealthy food outlets compared to the healthy ones, suggest a high level of exposure of children and adolescents to unhealthy food environments at a key stage in the development of dietary behaviors [28]. Such spatial inequalities, while not indicative of actual purchasing behaviours, are associated with everyday food consumption and may contribute to the normalization of unhealthy food choices and, consequently, their health and future behaviors [70]. This also underlines the relevance of school-centered food environment policies, particularly in dense urban contexts where exposure to food swamps is highest.

Policy implications

This study suggests that interventions should not only focus on reducing food deserts, as they are more rarely encountered, but also on mitigating food swamps, especially around schools and in areas with a high proportion of older adults and young/adolescents.

At the policy level, improving food environments in Wallonia requires moving from fragmented and predominantly informational approaches towards more structural, preventive, and coordinated strategies. As highlighted in a recent Belgian report on multi-level policies to address food environments [71] the current dispersion of competences across federal, regional, community, and local levels governments creates gaps and inefficiencies. However, within this complex governance architecture, Wallonia retains meaningful policy levers that could be mobilized in the short and longer term. In the short term, regional and local authorities could prioritize school perimeter or density threshold policies by restricting the opening of new unhealthy food outlets such as fast-food restaurants and convenience stores within a defined distance from schools. Such zoning instruments fall within urban planning competences that municipalities in Wallonia already hold, and could be operationalized through local land-use plans. Density thresholds, limiting the number of fast-food outlets per 1,000 inhabitants in a given area, could complement distance-based restrictions as well as restricting opening hours of unhealthy food outlet during and before/after school hours.

In England, for example around 50% of local authorities have adopted planning guidance restricting new fast-food outlets near schools, most commonly through exclusion zones of 400 m around secondary schools [72]. Evidence from Gateshead, where a multi-pronged approach combining school exclusion zones, density thresholds, and obesity-based criteria was implemented in 2015, showed a 14% reduction in the proportion of fast-food outlets within four years [73]. Closer to home, the municipality of Auderghem (Brussels, Belgium) adopted in 2025 a pioneering tax-based approach: a one-time opening fee of 10,000€ and an annual levy of 12,000€ are imposed on fast-food chain outlets, with both amounts doubled for establishments located within 100 m of a school [74].

In the longer term, structural support for healthy food retail could be developed, particularly in rural and low-density areas where physical accessibility is limited and supply chains are weaker. Concrete mechanisms could include direct subsidies or reduced commercial rents for greengrocers and fresh food retailers in underserved areas, the development of mobile fresh food markets serving isolated communities, and institutional support for short-chain and community-supported agriculture (CSA) initiatives.

While supply-side measures are central, demand-side strategies also remain important to foster lasting behavioural change [75]. Nutrition education programs integrated into school curricula, improvements in school meal quality and accessibility, and public awareness campaigns about healthy eating [76] can complement structural interventions by strengthening individuals' capacity to make healthier choices within improved food environments.

To address these challenges, policy action should simultaneously support sustainable and healthy food retail to strengthen local food systems and establish legal frameworks enabling local authorities to regulate the spatial distribution of unhealthy outlets (e.g., near schools). Finally, infrastructural and logistical support for sustainable retail food systems would be especially beneficial in low density and rural zones, where physical accessibility is limited and supply chains are generally weaker. These actions reflect the importance of coordinated multi-level governance, where Wallonia can use its own levers while working closely with federal and community levels to build healthier and more equitable food environments.

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is that the mRFEI index relies on a categorization scheme originally developed by experts from Flanders, which may not fully reflect the specific retail landscape and consumer behaviors in Wallonia, even if the same database (Locatus) was used. Furthermore, minor changes in the classification of outlets within the Locatus database occurred over the study period. These changes may have led to slight reclassifications of certain points of sale over time, potentially affecting longitudinal comparisons. More broadly, the mRFEI represents only one way to represent food swamps. The classification of supermarkets as healthy, neutral or unhealthy outlets remains inconsistent across the food environment literature [16, 53–57], and this lack of consensus may influence the results of studies using ratio-based indices such as the mRFEI. Their categorization carries methodological implications that warrant consideration when interpreting and comparing findings across studies.In Wallonia, this means that a significant share of actual food exposure occurs in outlets that the mRFEI does not capture. This also explains why many statistical sectors (41.8%) could not be assigned an mRFEI score, as they contain only supermarkets and no outlets classified as healthy or unhealthy. Alternative classifications treating supermarkets as healthy or unhealthy retailers may have yielded different estimates.

Temporal comparisons across the study period should be interpreted with caution. The LogCiCa database, which captures short-chain food initiatives such as farm collection points, community-supported agriculture, and local markets, was only available for 2024 and could therefore not be integrated into the analyses for earlier time points (2008, 2013, 2020, and 2022). As a result, estimates for years prior to 2024 likely underestimate the presence of healthy food outlets, meaning that the true extent of food oases and the mRFEI scores for these years may be slightly underestimated.

A harmonized study covering Flanders, Wallonia, and Brussels, the three Belgian regions using identical methods would be required to establish a comprehensive national picture. Another limitation concerns the operational definition of accessibility. Using a 1000 m threshold may appear relatively long, especially in residential contexts: as shown by Cant [49], this distance may represent close to one hour of walking to do grocery shopping, which few people are likely to undertake. This metric may therefore overestimate the actual accessibility of food outlets. However this distance was selected to ensure comparability with previous Belgian and international studies [2, 16, 31, 49]. Although the 1000 m threshold is widely used, alternative distances (e.g., 500 m or 800 m) [24] could lead to different accessibility patterns. The absence of a formal sensitivity analysis represents a limitation of the present study. Shorter thresholds generally reduce the number of reachable outlets particularly in rural or semi-rural areas while intermediate thresholds tend to produce similar spatial patterns with lower prevalence values. To partially address this concern, a sensitivity analysis was conducted for potential food deserts by applying a 500 m threshold to supermarket accessibility. Results showed that the proportion of residential addresses classified as potentially food desert-exposed increased from 11.29% at 1,000 m to 14.12% at 500 m. Suggesting that absolute prevalence estimates are sensitive to the distance parameter, though the overall spatial pattern and regional trends remained directionally consistent across both thresholds. Future research should incorporate multi-threshold analyses to assess the robustness of accessibility metrics and to better capture the variability of food environments across diverse territorial contexts. Temporal analyses were based on five irregularly spaced time points over a 16-year period. Consequently, linear regression results should be interpreted as summaries of average directional change rather than precise estimates of temporal dynamics or non-linear processes. More sophisticated longitudinal or spatial models were not pursued because the study aimed primarily to describe regional trends rather than estimate causal relationships.

Moreover, we could not capture shopping trips made by car, which represent a considerable proportion of food acquisition in Wallonia, particularly outside urban centers. Incorporating transport modes, as well as walkability/bikeability of areas into future analyses would allow a more nuanced assessment [77]. The retail datasets used in this study could be supplemented with mobile vendors such as food trucks. These outlets are difficult to document systematically because their locations, operating days, and periods of activity are not consistently recorded in administrative sources. Their omission may therefore lead to a partial underestimation of food availability. The absence of online food delivery platforms also underestimate the food availability. These services have expanded rapidly over the study period and now represent an important channel through which many individuals, particularly young people, access predominantly unhealthy food options [78]. Because these platforms are not captured in the available datasets, the analysis might underestimates the full extent of exposure to unhealthy food environments.

Conclusions

This study shows that food environments in Wallonia, Belgium have generally deteriorated over time, both at the residential level and around schools. Food swamps are widespread, and affect a large share of the population whereas food deserts remain a possible threat, especially for the most vulnerable part of the population. Policy efforts should address the density and spatial distribution of unhealthy outlets in order to reduce dietary inequalities and improve population health in Wallonia.
