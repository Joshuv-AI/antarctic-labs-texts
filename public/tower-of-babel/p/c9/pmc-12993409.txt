Oncocytic adrenocortical carcinoma: A case report
Authors: Jiale Wang, Hongjin Shi, Jinsong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oncocytic adrenocortical carcinoma (OACC) is an exceptionally rare adrenal malignancy. We report a case of a 47-year-old male in whom preoperative CT/MRI revealed a left adrenal bed mass, with the largest lesion measuring 11.6 cm and suggestive of renal invasion. All other preoperative hormone test results were within normal limits, except for the adrenocorticotropic hormone test. The patient underwent open surgical resection with partial nephrectomy. Histopathology confirmed OACC according to the Lin-Weiss-Bisceglia criteria. Complete surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment.

Introduction

Oncocytic adrenocortical carcinoma (OACC) is an exceedingly rare histological variant of adrenal cortical carcinoma (ACC),1 characterized by the diffuse proliferation of large polygonal tumor cells with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm.2 Given its extremely low incidence, challenges in histopathological differentiation, and nonspecific radiological features, OACC poses substantial difficulties in both clinical diagnosis and therapeutic management.3 This report describes a rare case of OACC in a patient with a prior history of pheochromocytoma and ipsilateral adrenalectomy, who underwent open surgical resection with concomitant partial nephrectomy.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old male patient was identified to have a mass in the left adrenal region on follow-up imaging performed three months prior. Notably, his medical history includes a diagnosis of left-sided pheochromocytoma two years ago, for which he underwent open total left adrenalectomy. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the whole-abdomen revealed multiple space-occupying lesions in the left adrenal region, left renal area, and surrounding tissues, with the largest measuring approximately 12 cm × 8 cm and demonstrating heterogeneous enhancement on contrast imaging (Fig. 2A). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the adrenal glands, including unenhanced, contrast-enhanced, and diffusion-weighted sequences, showed multiple masses in the left adrenal region, left abdominal cavity, and retroperitoneum (Fig. 2B). The lesion margins were indistinct from the left kidney, suggesting possible local invasion. The largest mass measured 7.8 cm × 11.6 cm × 10.0 cm and exhibited intratumoral hemorrhage (Fig. 2C). These imaging features are highly suggestive of adrenal cortical carcinoma with multifocal metastatic involvement. Chest CT and bone scintigraphy revealed no evidence of metastatic disease.Fig. 2(A)Enhanced CT scan of the whole-abdomen (horizontal plane); (B)Enhanced MRI scan of the adrenal glands (horizontal plane); (C)Enhanced MRI scan of the adrenal glands (coronal plane).Fig. 2

Preoperatively, the patient was diagnosed with a large mass of unknown origin in the left adrenal bed following prior adrenalectomy, with differential considerations including primary malignancy, metastatic tumor, or other etiologies. Preoperative preparation was conducted according to standard protocols for pheochromocytoma. Two weeks before surgery, the patient was initiated on oral alpha-adrenergic blockade with phenoxybenzamine at 10 mg daily, with gradual dose titration up to 30 mg to achieve hemodynamic stability. Blood pressure and heart rate were closely monitored and maintained within normal ranges. Starting five days preoperatively, intravenous fluid expansion was implemented, with daily intake exceeding 2000 mL to prevent postoperative hypotension. Upon completion of adequate preoperative optimization, the patient underwent open surgical resection with concomitant partial nephrectomy. Intraoperatively, multiple tumor fragments and a portion of the left renal tissue were excised. The resected specimens exhibited heterogeneous consistency, ranging from soft to firm, and displayed a pale red appearance (Fig. 3). Postoperatively, the patient remained hemodynamically stable and reported no adverse symptoms.Fig. 3Surgical specimens comprising the tumour and part of the renal tissue.Fig. 3

Under the microscope, it can be seen that the cells are diffusely arranged and possess an abundance of eosinophilic granular cytoplasm (Fig. 4A). The pathological diagnosis was assessed according to the Lin-Weiss-Bisceglia (LWB) classification system4, and the findings were consistent with eosinophilic OACC. (This case fulfills the diagnostic criteria of the LWB classification system as follows: one major criterion—mitotic rate exceeding 5 per 50 high-power fields (HPF) (Fig. 4B)—and two minor criteria—presence of tumor infarction and necrosis, and a maximum tumor diameter of 10 cm.) Immunohistochemical analysis of the resected specimen revealed negative immunoreactivity for inhibin-α, INSM1, GATA3, S100, chromogranin A (CgA), and SF-1 (Fig. 4C), with a Ki-67 proliferation index of 20%. Discussion

Oncocytic adrenocortical carcinoma (OACC) is a rare and diagnostically challenging neoplasm. The clinical presentation is predominantly non-functional and nonspecific, often resulting in delayed diagnosis until patients develop symptoms of local mass effect from tumor enlargement—such as abdominal distension and pain—or until the lesion is incidentally identified on imaging studies.1 Differentiation from benign eosinophilic adenoma and other malignant adrenal neoplasms relies heavily on rigorous histopathological evaluation. Currently, the LWB classification system is widely endorsed for diagnostic categorization in international guidelines.4 The primary treatment modality is complete surgical resection, with adjuvant postoperative chemotherapy using mitotane.5 The prognosis of this disease appears more favorable than that of conventional ACC, with a higher 5-year survival rate.1 However, a study by Thway K et al. reported conflicting findings,6 suggesting that OACC as a distinct subtype of ACC, exhibits similarly high aggressiveness and is frequently associated with local invasion of adjacent tissues or distant metastasis at the time of diagnosis—a feature that aligns with the clinical presentation of the present case. Several notable aspects of this case warrant emphasis: (1) the large tumor size at initial presentation (>10 cm); (2) occurrence within the prior surgical bed of a pheochromocytoma resected two years earlier, necessitating comprehensive histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation to exclude recurrent pheochromocytoma or metastatic renal cell carcinoma; and (3) the requirement for partial nephrectomy due to direct renal parenchymal invasion. In summary, this case underscores the rarity and clinical complexity of OACC, particularly in patients with a history of open adrenal surgery, where altered anatomical planes significantly increase surgical difficulty and risk.

OACC is a rare subtype of ACC. Radiological features of OACC frequently overlap with those of other adrenal neoplasms, including pheochromocytoma and metastatic adrenal tumors, rendering preoperative differentiation highly challenging. To date, dedicated research on OACC remains limited, and its clinical management largely adheres to established guidelines for conventional ACC, with complete surgical resection serving as the cornerstone of therapy. Given the distinct pathological and biological characteristics of this entity, further studies are warranted to better characterize its behavior and to develop a more standardized diagnostic and therapeutic framework.
