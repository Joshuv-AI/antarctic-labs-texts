Anti‐Inflammatory Potential of Key Phytochemicals From Humboldtia sanjappae: In Vivo Studies and Molecular Docking With MM‐GBSA Analysis
Authors: Jameema Sidhic, Madhu Tanya Singh, Sruthi Mohan C. K., Aparna Prasad, Paromita Sarbadhikary, Praveen Thaggikuppe Krishnamurthy, Sulaiman C. T., Arunaksharan Narayanankutty, Heidi Abrahamse, Satheesh George, Blassan P. George
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Humboldtia sanjappae, an endemic medicinal plant, was examined for its anti‐inflammatory properties using both in vivo and in silico methodologies. The anti‐inflammatory activity was assessed utilizing carrageenan‐induced and formalin‐induced paw oedema models in Swiss albino mice. The ethanolic extract of H. sanjappae demonstrated significant, dose‐dependent inhibition of inflammation. Phytochemical analysis identified bioactive flavonoids, with neoeriocitrin demonstrating the highest binding affinity for TNF‐α in molecular docking studies, indicating its significant role in inflammation modulation. Subsequent docking studies targeting COX‐2, IL‐6, and IL‐1β revealed advantageous interactions, validated by MM‐GBSA free energy analyses. Although COX‐1, crucial for normal physiological function, was also inhibited, no adverse effects were noted, suggesting that the extract may function as a nonselective COX inhibitor similar to indomethacin. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of H. sanjappae as a natural source of anti‐inflammatory agents that target different pathways.

Introduction

Traditional medicine, particularly herbal medicine, has been used for thousands of years to prevent and treat various illnesses [1, 2]. For centuries, traditional medicine has been a fundamental component of healthcare systems, particularly in countries where modern pharmaceuticals are either expensive or unavailable. Numerous cultures, such as those practicing Ayurveda, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), and Indigenous healing practices, depend on medicinal plants to alleviate a variety of maladies [3, 4]. Medicinal plants have long played a transformative role in drug discovery. For example, aspirin, derived from the bark of the willow tree (Salix alba), revolutionized pain management and paved the way for NSAIDs [5]. Similarly, the Madagascar periwinkle (Catharanthus roseus) led to the development of vinblastine and vincristine, which have significantly advanced cancer treatment [6]. Another notable example is artemisinin, extracted from Artemisia annua, which has been instrumental in malaria therapy [7].

Natural substances and molecules that are biologically active have been isolated and identified throughout human history, leading to the development of novel treatments and the advancement of the medical and pharmaceutical industries [8]. When integrated with laboratory experiments and animal model studies, these approaches enable researchers to accelerate drug discovery and refine plant‐based treatments for clinical application. This synergy not only cuts development costs but also improves the precision and efficiency of bringing new therapeutics to market [9, 10].

Murine models are essential in preclinical research, offering significant insights into the pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, toxicity, and efficacy of phytochemicals [11]. These models enable researchers to evaluate physiological and biochemical responses in a regulated setting, facilitating the transition from in vitro investigations to human clinical trials, which has predictive utility [12, 13]. Researchers can explore the mechanisms of action, metabolism, and potential adverse effects of bioactive substances using mouse models, ultimately aiding in the development of innovative plant‐based medicines [14, 15].

Herbal bioactive compounds have gained considerable attention due to their multi‐targeted mechanisms of action [16, 17]. Computational molecular docking and MM‐GBSA analyses complement these studies by predicting how phytochemicals interact with key inflammatory mediators such as COX‐1, COX‐2, IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α at the molecular level. This integration of experimental and computational methodologies not only helps to identify the compounds most likely responsible for the observed effects but also provides insight into potential mechanisms of action, thereby supporting target‐based drug discovery. Moreover, it enables researchers to prioritize compounds for further validation, reducing both time and cost in drug development. Although the ethanol extract of Humboldtia sanjappae bark (HBA) has previously shown promising in vitro anti‐inflammatory effects [18]. However, its efficacy in inflammation models and underlying molecular mechanisms require further exploration. This study evaluates the anti‐inflammatory potential of HBA in both acute and chronic inflammation models using Swiss albino mice. The therapeutic potential of plant‐based formulations needs to be carefully tested in preclinical studies to confirm both their safety and effectiveness. In this study, the anti‐inflammatory activity of Humboldtia sanjappae bark extract (HBA) was evaluated using several complementary methods. In vivo experiments in Swiss albino mice were used to measure paw thickness, study tissue changes through histopathology, and analyze gene expression, providing valuable information on how the extract affects inflammation in a living system. However, these experiments alone cannot explain exactly which molecules in the extract are responsible for the effects. To gain this insight, molecular docking and MM‐GBSA analyses were used to predict how the key phytochemicals in H. sanjappae interact with important inflammatory mediators such as COX‐1, COX‐2, IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α. Combining these approaches offers a clearer understanding of the possible mechanisms behind HBA's anti‐inflammatory action, strengthens the scientific basis for its use, and helps identify the most promising compounds for future research and drug development. This kind of methodology has been previously employed and validated in earlier studies [19, 20]. Although several plant‐derived flavonoids have been reported for their anti‐inflammatory activity, the pharmacological potential of H. sanjappae remains largely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has provided in vivo validation of its anti‐inflammatory effects. Therefore, the present study aims to bridge this gap by integrating phytochemical insights with in vivo, molecular, and in silico analyses.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material and Extraction

Plant material of H. sanjappae was collected on 12 December, 2022 from Ernakulam district, Western Ghats, Kerala (10.04829°N, 76.8399°E). The species was taxonomically authenticated, and a voucher specimen was deposited in the “DEV” herbarium (Accession No. 9208).

The collected leaves and bark were thoroughly washed to remove extraneous matter, shade‐dried for 21 days, and coarsely powdered using a mechanical grinder. Approximately 10 g of each powdered sample was subjected to continuous hot extraction with ethanol using a Soxhlet apparatus. The extraction was carried out until the siphon tube solvent became colorless, indicating exhaustive extraction. The resulting extracts were filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to obtain the crude ethanol extract. This extract was used for all further studies.

Animal Study

Animal Selection and Housing

Eight‐week‐old male Swiss albino mice were randomly selected for the study. The animals were housed in a controlled laboratory environment with a temperature range of 23.4–28.8°C and relative humidity maintained between 54%–78%. A 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycle was maintained throughout the study. Mice were housed in standard polypropylene cages (L 410 × B 282 × H 150 mm) with stainless steel top grills equipped with provisions for pelleted food and drinking water in bottles. Paddy husk was used as bedding material, which was changed at least twice a week.

Acclimatization and Ethical Compliance

Before the initiation of the experiments, animals were acclimatized for 7 days to the laboratory conditions to minimize stress and ensure adaptation to the environment. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) of JSS College of Pharmacy, Ooty, JSS Academy of Higher Education & Research (Deemed to be University), Mysuru (IAEC Registration No.: 118/PO/ReBiRc/S/1999/CPCSEA), under approval number JSSCP/OT/IAEC/24/2024–2025.

Animal Model

The carrageenan‐induced paw edema model was used to evaluate acute inflammation [21]. Baseline paw thickness (0 h) was measured using a digital Vernier calliper before inflammation induction, and animals were treated with their respective treatments. Acute inflammation was induced by sub‐plantar injection of 100 µL of 1% freshly prepared carrageenan into the right hind paw. Respective treatments were administered 30 min prior to carrageenan induction. Paw thickness was recorded at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h posttreatment.

The formalin‐induced inflammation model was used to assess chronic inflammation [22]. On day 0, animals were injected with 100 µL of 5% formalin in the sub‐plantar region of the right hind paw to induce persistent inflammation. Starting from day 1, animals received their respective treatments daily. The paw thickness was measured using a digital Vernier calliper on day 0, and then daily from day 1 to day 5. The reduction in paw thickness over time was used to evaluate the chronic anti‐inflammatory effect of HBA.

The percentage change in paw thickness and the mean increase in paw volume was calculated to assess the anti‐inflammatory effect. Paw volume was measured using a plethysmometer to assess inflammation levels and evaluate the anti‐inflammatory effects of treatment.

Experimental Groups

Two separate studies were conducted to evaluate the acute and chronic anti‐inflammatory effects of HBA. For each study, animals were randomly allocated into five groups (n = 6 per group) using a simple randomization method to minimize selection bias. Normal Control (received vehicle only), Disease Control (received vehicle only along with induction of inflammation), Standard Treatment (treated with indomethacin, 5 mg/kg, i.p.), HBA 100 mg/kg (treated with HBA, 100 mg/kg, p.o.), and HBA 200 mg/kg (treated with HBA, 200 mg/kg, p.o.). The doses of 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg were selected based on prior toxicity and ADMET studies, which demonstrated that the extract was safe up to 300 mg/kg. Therefore, sub‐maximal doses were chosen to evaluate dose‐dependent pharmacological effects while ensuring safety. The experimental design is presented in Figures 1 and 2.

Experimental design for testing the effects of different treatments in a carrageenan‐induced acute inflammatory model in mice.

Experimental design for testing the effects of different treatments in a formalin‐induced chronic inflammatory model in mice.

Distilled water was used as the vehicle and was administered orally (p.o.) in an equivalent volume to the normal control, disease control, and HBA‐treated groups to ensure uniformity.

Histopathology

At the end of the experiment, paw tissues were collected from the respective groups and fixed in 10% formalin. After removing lipid debris by soaking in alcohol, the sections were dehydrated in increasing ethanol concentrations, and the tissue samples were embedded in paraffin wax. 5–10 µm‐thick coronal section of wound tissue with a thickness of 2 mm was taken using a microtome. Slides were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and the stained tissue sections were observed under a light microscope (40x). The paw region was photographed to assess the changes observed by different treatments.

Histopathological alterations were evaluated using a semi‐quantitative scoring system based on the severity of epidermal hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and examined under a light microscope. The severity of histopathological changes was graded on a scale of 0–3 as follows:

0 = Normal histological architecture/no detectable lesion, 1 = Mild alteration, 2 = Moderate alteration, 3 = Severe alteration.

Histopathological scoring was performed by an independent observer blinded to the experimental groups to minimize observational bias.

RT–PCR Analysis

Total RNA was isolated from paw tissue using TRIzol reagent according to standard protocols. Tissue samples were collected from experimental groups under sterile conditions, immediately snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until RNA extraction. RNA concentration was measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer, and RNA purity was assessed using a spectrophotometer by determining A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios. RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. DNase I (RNase‐free) treatment was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions to eliminate genomic DNA contamination.

First‐strand cDNA synthesis was carried out using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, India) with 500 ng of total RNA (volume adjusted accordingly) in a 10 µL reaction volume containing 5× PrimeScript buffer (2 µL), PrimeScript RT enzyme mix (0.5 µL), oligo(dT) primers (0.5 µL), RNA template, and RNase‐free water. Reverse transcription was performed at 37°C for 15 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 85°C for 5 s, and the reaction was held at 4°C. Synthesized cDNA was stored at −20°C until further use.

Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT–PCR) was performed using an Applied Biosystems 7300 Real‐Time PCR System with SYBR Green chemistry. Amplification reactions were carried out in a total volume of 10 µL containing SYBR Green master mix (Applied Biosystems, USA), gene‐specific forward and reverse primers, cDNA template, and nuclease‐free water. The target genes included cyclooxygenase‐1 (COX‐1), cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2), interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), with glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) used as the internal reference gene. GAPDH expression was validated to be stable across all experimental groups.

Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, annealing at 60°C for 45 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72°C for 2 min. A melt curve analysis (60–95°C, incremental increase) was performed at the end of each run to confirm amplification specificity. A single sharp peak was observed for each target gene, indicating the absence of non‐specific amplification and primer‐dimer formation.

Primer efficiency for each gene was determined using standard curve analysis and was within the acceptable range (90%–110%) with correlation coefficients (R 2) > 0.99. All reactions were performed in technical triplicates with at least three biological replicates per group. No‐template controls (NTC) and no‐reverse transcription (–RT) controls were included to check for contamination and genomic DNA carryover.

Relative gene expression was calculated using the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method with GAPDH as the endogenous control and the control group as the calibrator. The specific primer sequences used for all target and housekeeping genes are provided in Supporting Information.

Molecular Docking and MM‐GBSA Analysis of Identified Phytochemicals From HBA Against Inflammatory Targets

Ligand Preparation

The phytochemicals identified from H. sanjappae via LC–MS/MS were retrieved from the PubChem database using their respective PubChem IDs. Ligands were prepared using the LigPrep module in Schrödinger Maestro. Energy minimisation was performed using the OPLS force field, and ionisation states were generated at physiological pH (7.0 ± 2.0) with Epik.

Protein Target Preparation

Crystal structures of COX‐1, COX‐2, IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α were obtained from the Protein Data Bank. Protein preparation was performed using the Protein Preparation Wizard, including hydrogen addition, bond‐order assignment, removal of distant water molecules (>5 Å), and energy minimization with the OPLS force field, using an RMSD cutoff of 0.3 Å.

Molecular Docking

A hierarchical docking protocol was employed using the Glide module of Schrödinger. Initially, all ligands were screened using High Throughput Virtual Screening (HTVS), followed by Standard Precision (SP) docking. The top‐ranked ligands were further refined using Extra Precision (XP) docking.

The receptor grid was generated around the active site using the co‐crystallized ligand as a reference. Docking results were evaluated based on Glide Score (XP), docking score, and interaction energy components, including van der Waals (glide_evdw) and Coulombic interactions (glide_ecoul).

Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area

Binding free energy calculations were performed using the Prime MM‐GBSA module of the Schrödinger suite. The OPLS force field was applied in combination with the VSGB implicit solvent model. The binding free energy (ΔG_bind) was calculated according to the equation: ΔG_bind=G_complex−G_protein+G_ligand

Binding free energy calculations were performed using the Prime MM‐GBSA module. The VSGB implicit solvent model was applied, with default dielectric constants (solute dielectric = 1.0 and solvent dielectric = 80.0). During the MM‐GBSA analysis, the protein was treated as rigid, whereas ligand flexibility was retained. Furthermore, energy decomposition analysis was conducted to assess the contributions of van der Waals, Coulombic, and lipophilic interactions to the overall binding affinity.

Statistical Analysis

All in vitro experiments and biochemical assays were performed in triplicate. For the in vivo studies, six animals (n = 6) were used per group as biological replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25). Data obtained from carrageenan‐induced and formalin‐induced paw edema studies, involving repeated measurements collected over multiple time points from the same animals, were analyzed using two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc test. Endpoint measurements such as gene expression studies, in which samples collected at a single terminal time point were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test to compare treatment groups with the control group. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In contrast, the Normal Control group showed minimal variation due to the absence of inflammation. The standard Indomethacin (5 mg/kg, i.p.) group exhibited effective anti‐inflammatory activity, with paw thickness stabilizing after 2 h and significantly decreasing by 6 hours.

In the HBA‐treated groups, both the 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg dosages exhibited a decrease in paw thickness. The 200 mg/kg dosage significantly reduced paw thickness, reaching up to the standard treatment. Statistical analysis indicated substantial differences between the Disease Control and treatment groups. HBA 200 mg/kg demonstrating a consistent reduction in inflammation after 2 h (Table 1, Figures 3 and 4).

Histopathological Observations

The epidermal (E) and dermal (D) layers of the histopathological sections of Normal Group animals were intact, indicating a well‐preserved and normal skin architecture. However, there were no indications of irregular thickening or disruption in the epidermis, which maintained its typical stratified structure. The dermis was well‐organized, with a normal distribution of collagen and the absence of inflammatory alterations. It is important to note that the animals preserved a healthy tissue morphology, as there was no indication of vascular congestion, edema, or infiltration of inflammatory cells. While the histopathological sections of disease control group animals, exhibited substantial pathological changes. A significant amount of epidermal hyperplasia was observed, which was distinguished by an abnormal thickening of the epidermal layer. This thickening was likely the result of excessive keratinocyte proliferation. Furthermore, a significant infiltration of inflammatory cells was observed, particularly in the dermal layer, suggesting an active inflammatory response. The disease model is associated with severe pathological changes, as indicated by the structural abnormalities and signs of tissue disruption.

In comparison to the disease control group, animals administered with Indomethacin exhibited substantial enhancements in histopathological characteristics. The degree of epidermal hyperplasia was diminished, suggesting a partial restoration of the normal skin structure. Additionally, the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration was lower in the Indomethacin‐treated group than in the disease‐induced group, indicating that the treatment effectively reduced inflammation. Nevertheless, there were still some residual indications of tissue remodeling and inflammation. The histopathological sections of animals treated with a low dose of HBA (100 mg/kg) demonstrated a decrease in epidermal hyperplasia. The severity of the condition was less than that of diseased animals, even though the epidermal layer remained somewhat thicker than that of normal controls. Mild structural enhancements were also observed in the dermal layer, and a few blood vessels were present, which may suggest that vascularization or tissue remodeling was enhanced. The animals administered a high dose of HBA (200 mg/kg) exhibited the most significant histopathological recovery. The epidermal and dermal layers exhibited architecture that was similar to that of normal controls, with minimal evidence of pathological changes. The ductal structures appeared nearly normal, and the epidermal and dermal hyperplasia were mild. Furthermore, the high‐dose treatment demonstrated a robust anti‐inflammatory effect, as evidenced by the substantial reduction in inflammatory cell infiltration. This was demonstrated by the change in paw thickness. Indomethacin treatment was effective in reducing inflammation, as the paw thickness reached a maximum of 0.19 ± 0.01 mm on Day 3 and subsequently decreased to 0.14 ± 0.01 mm on Day 5, which is close to the normal range. HBA 100 mg/kg moderately reduced inflammation in the animals, with a peak of 0.21 ± 0.01 mm on Day 4 and a modest decrease to 0.18 ± 0.01 mm on Day 5. The most effective treatment was HBA 200 mg/kg, which exhibited a lower peak of 0.17 ± 0.01 mm on Day 3 and a subsequent decrease to 0.12 ± 0.01 mm on Day 5, which was consistent with the normal control group. HBA 200 mg/kg shown to be as effective as Indomethacin in reducing inflammation, according to these findings, while HBA 100 mg/kg has a moderate effect (Table 3, Figures 8 and 9).

Histopathology Observations

The histological sections of all groups in the chronic inflammation model are represented in Figure 11. The epidermal and dermal architecture of animals in the normal control was well‐preserved and normal in the histopathological sections. No evidence of thickening or irregularities was observed in the epidermis, which maintained its distinctive stratified structure. The connective tissues and blood vessels were evenly distributed throughout the dermal layer, which appeared structurally intact. It is crucial to note that the skin morphology was healthy, as there was no evidence of hemorrhage, oedema, or vascular abnormalities, and there was no infiltration of inflammatory cells.

Histopathological analysis of paw tissue sections after 5 days of treatment in chronic inflammation model stained with hematoxylin and eosin showing inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue architecture in different treatment groups. Images were captured at 40× magnification.

While in the diseased animals, which were subjected to formalin treatment to induce a chronic disease state, severe epidermal hyperplasia was observed. This condition is characterized by an excessive thickening of the epidermis as a result of sustained cellular proliferation. Furthermore, there was a significant infiltration of inflammatory cells into both the epidermal and dermal layers, suggesting a persistent inflammatory response. The number of blood vessels also increased significantly, with a significant number of them exhibiting symptoms of hemorrhage. This suggests that the vessels are fragile and prone to leakage, which is a characteristic of chronic tissue inflammation and damage.

Animals that were administered Indomethacin exhibited a decrease in epidermal hyperplasia in comparison to diseased animals. But dermal hyperplasia persisted, indicating that Indomethacin had a partial impact on suppressing epidermal proliferation. The level of inflammatory cell infiltration was diminished, even though the inflammation had not been entirely resolved. This implies that Indomethacin had a moderate anti‐inflammatory effect but was not entirely effective in restoring normal skin architecture in the chronic model. In contrast to the diseased group, animals treated 100 mg/kg HBA demonstrated a decrease in epidermal hyperplasia, despite the fact that the epidermis remained slightly thickened. Despite some structural improvements, inflammatory cell infiltration remained present, suggesting that inflammation was ongoing. These results indicate that the modest dose of HBA had a beneficial effect on reducing epidermal alterations; however, it was insufficient to completely suppress inflammation in the chronic model. The animals which were administered a high dose of HBA (200 mg/kg), exhibited the most substantial recovery observed histopathologically. The epidermal and dermal structures were strikingly similar to those of normal controls, with the exception of moderate epidermal hyperplasia. The ductal architecture appeared virtually normal, indicating a restoration of tissue integrity. Furthermore, the high‐dose treatment exerted a good anti‐inflammatory effect, as indicated by the low infiltration of inflammatory cells. The results of these studies indicate that a high dose of HBA was highly effective in restoring normal histological features and mitigating the chronic inflammatory response.

Gene Expression

The fold induction of inflammatory genes (COX‐1, COX‐2, IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α) in the chronic inflammation model following treatment is depicted in the bar graph (Figure 12). COX‐2 and TNF‐α demonstrate the most significant upregulation of all inflammatory markers in the diseased group, with an induction of approximately 2.7–3.0 times. This confirms the presence of sustained chronic inflammation. The treatment with indomethacin results in a decrease in the expression of inflammatory genes, particularly in the case of cytokines. However, COX‐1 and COX‐2 remain elevated compared to cytokines. But the expression of COX‐1 and COX‐2 is reduced compared to disease control. HBA exhibited moderate anti‐inflammatory effects at a dosage of 100 mg/kg, resulting in a decrease in inflammatory gene expression in comparison to the diseased group. However, it remains higher than the Indomethacin group. HBA at a dosage of 200 mg/kg demonstrates a more potent anti‐inflammatory effect, as it substantially reduces IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α. This suggests a dose‐dependent reduction in inflammation. These findings suggest that HBA, particularly at high dosages, has potential anti‐inflammatory properties that are comparable to Indomethacin.

Discussion

Inflammation is key symptom of many diseases, greatly affecting overall health and quality of life [23]. Chronic pain and inflammation can lead to emotional distress, fatigue, and severe health complications, including organ damage, heart disease, and cancer, ultimately affecting overall well‐being and longevity [24, 25, 26]. Treatment processes often rely on analgesics and anti‐inflammatory drugs, but their use is linked to various health concerns. As a result, there is a growing shift toward herbal remedies as safer alternatives [27, 28].

The present study evaluated the anti‐inflammatory potential of H. sanjappae extract (HBA) in both acute and chronic inflammation models, comparing its effects to the standard anti‐inflammatory drug, Indomethacin. The results indicate that HBA, particularly at a higher dosage (200 mg/kg), exhibits a significant anti‐inflammatory response, comparable to Indomethacin. Interestingly, In the present study, HBA at 200 mg/kg markedly suppressed the expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, in some cases reducing their levels below the normal baseline. This effect may be due to strong inhibition of inflammatory signaling pathways, possibly through suppression of transcription factors such as NF‐κB, which regulate the production of pro‐inflammatory mediators. Suppression of these factors can reduce the cytokine storm [29, 30]. The reduction below basal levels may also reflect the natural regulation of inflammatory responses, where an initial inflammatory reaction is followed by a compensatory decline during the resolution phase. The anti‐inflammatory responses reduce the expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines to help control excessive inflammation. This type of regulation may help interrupt the continuous cycle of inflammation‐related tissue damage and restore immune balance. The interaction between pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory cytokines plays an important role in maintaining normal immune homeostasis [31]. However, strong suppression of inflammatory pathways may also affect other interconnected signaling mechanisms in the body. Therefore, the therapeutic benefits and possible risks of such modulation should be carefully evaluated to avoid unwanted disruption of normal immune regulation [32]. Previous studies have demonstrated that inflammatory signaling itself can exhibit dynamic regulation. For instance, IL‐1 stimulation has been reported to produce a biphasic response in COX‐2 mRNA expression, characterized by an initial increase followed by a marked decline below basal levels at later time points. This supports the notion that cytokine expression can be tightly regulated and, under certain conditions, suppressed below baseline [33]. Additionally, activation of endogenous anti‐inflammatory regulatory mechanisms may contribute to this effect [34]. Moreover, individual genetic differences and variations in immune status may influence the degree of cytokine suppression observed after treatment. Therefore, further investigation is needed to determine whether these reductions reflect protective control of inflammation or may potentially impair normal immune function under certain conditions [35]. Such reductions are likely transient and reversible, with expression levels expected to return to basal conditions upon restoration of physiological homeostasis. In general, the findings suggest that higher dosages of HBA have a more significant effect on the suppression of inflammation, which is comparable to the standard anti‐inflammatory drug, Indomethacin.

The histopathological analysis of paw tissues in both acute and chronic inflammation models revealed distinct patterns of inflammation and recovery. Table 6 summarizes these observations. The differences in histopathological observations between acute and chronic inflammation might be primarily due to the duration of the immune reaction associated with each type of inflammation. Inflammation is the body's protective response to harmful stimuli, involving increased blood flow, vascular permeability, and immune cell migration, leading to pain, swelling, redness, heat, and functional impairment of the affected tissue [36, 37, 38]. These findings were further supported by semi‐quantitative histopathological scoring, which demonstrated a significant reduction in epidermal hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration in the treatment groups compared to the disease control group. Histological alterations were graded using a 0–3 scoring scale, where 0 represented normal histology and 3 represented severe pathological changes. The scoring results were further visualized using a heat map to provide a comparative representation of lesion severity across experimental groups (Figure 18). Chronic inflammation exhibited more pronounced pathological alterations than the acute model, whereas treatment groups showed dose‐dependent improvement, with HBA 200 mg/kg demonstrating near‐normal tissue architecture. These signs were persistent in the case of chronic inflammation, indicated by elevated hyperplasia, wide spread inflammatory cell infiltration, and more disrupted ductal architecture. But all these symptoms were less severe in the case of the acute inflammation model.

COX‐2 is an essential enzyme that is linked to tumorigenesis and inflammation. It regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [39]. Proinflammatory cytokines and COX‐2 can be upregulated by the inflammatory microenvironment, which can contribute to malignancy [40]. These findings are consistent with recent reports highlighting the inhibitory activity of flavonoids and phenolic compounds against COX‐2 through interactions within the enzyme's catalytic domain [41, 42]. Previous reports suggest that the COX‐2 expression has a role in the severity of the hyperplasic condition [39]. Our study supports this observation, as the elevated COX‐2 expression in the acute inflammation model contributed to hyperplasia in the inflamed paw tissues. This effect intensified in the chronic disease model, likely due to prolonged immune activation. Additionally, cytokine levels remained significantly elevated, intensified the inflammatory response.

In acute inflammation, treatment helps lower gene expression and reduce inflammation effectively. However, in chronic inflammation, inflammatory genes stay elevated despite treatment, showing ongoing immune activation. HBA (200 mg/kg) and Indomethacin reduce inflammation in both models, but the effects were more pronounced in acute inflammation. The inflammatory process begins with an external stimulus, such as injury, infection, or toxins, which triggers immune cell activation (macrophages, neutrophils, and mast cells) [37]. These activated immune cells then release pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐6, TNF‐α, and IL‐1β, which further amplify inflammation [43, 44].

COX‐2 and TNF‐α remain high in chronic inflammation, suggesting the need for longer treatment period. The link between COX‐2 and TNF‐α is evident from the study. They act in a positive feedback loop that amplifies inflammation [45, 46]. IL‐6 and IL‐1β plays a role in promoting the inflammation. The persistent activation of these cytokines in chronic inflammation has been linked to cancer development and progression. Additionally, COX‐2 overexpression and sustained TNF‐α signalling create a pro‐tumorigenic environment [47, 48].

The gene expression analysis revealed a substantial downregulation of inflammatory markers, particularly COX‐2 and pro‐inflammatory cytokines, aligning with the molecular docking studies. The strong binding energies and docking scores indicate that the phytochemicals present in HBA may effectively inhibit inflammatory enzymes and cytokines, contributing to their potential anti‐inflammatory effects. Neoeriocitrin displayed the strongest interactions with TNF‐α, suggesting its possible role in inflammation modulation. The docking results further support the use of these compounds in targeting key pathways of inflammation. In our study, COX‐1, which is important for normal body functions, was inhibited, but no negative effects were seen. This might be because the treatment acted like a non‐selective COX inhibitor, similar to Indomethacin. Most anti‐inflammatory drugs work by blocking both COX‐1 and COX‐2 rather than just COX‐2 [49]. The docking results suggest a possible molecular basis for the observed anti‐inflammatory effects; however, a direct mechanistic correlation between in silico predictions and in vivo outcomes was not established in the present study. Thus, the findings of our study align with the mechanistic pathway represented in Figure 19, further validating the anti‐inflammatory potential of compounds from the Ethanol extract of H. sanjappae bark in regulating inflammation through cytokine suppression and COX inhibition.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study provides the first in vivo evidence supporting the anti‐inflammatory activity of H. sanjappae‐derived HBA, demonstrated across both acute (carrageenan‐induced) and chronic (formalin‐induced) inflammation models. The observed pharmacological effects are supported by complementary in silico analyses, which indicate potential interactions with key inflammatory mediators, including cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, suggesting a plausible mechanism involving inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis and modulation of pro‐inflammatory signaling pathways.

Importantly, this study expands the currently limited pharmacological understanding of H. sanjappae, offering new insights that differentiate it from commonly investigated plant‐derived flavonoids. Rather than relying solely on established anti‐inflammatory pathways, the observed effects of HBA may involve alternative or complementary mechanisms, potentially linked to its distinct phytochemical composition. These findings highlight the relevance of HBA as a natural anti‐inflammatory agent and emphasize the need for further investigations to clarify its precise mode of action and therapeutic applicability.
