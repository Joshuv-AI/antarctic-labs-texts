Recruitment of healthy third-trimester pregnant women and their newborns into a prospective dental study at a primary health care clinic
Authors: Nisreen Al Jallad, Shasha Cui, Rita Cacciato, Naemah Alkhars, Marie Thomas, Colleen Fogarty, Noor Abid, Shahenda Aboelmagd, Jin Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecruiting and retaining mother-infant dyads in clinical research is often complex, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged and ethnically diverse communities. This paper reports on the recruitment and retention strategies employed within a prospective longitudinal cohort study (First Tooth Study) that investigated the oral colonization of Candida albicans and Streptococcus mutans as risk factors for Severe Early Childhood Caries (S-ECC) and the vertical transmission of these oral microorganisms. The study population was underserved mother-infant dyads, a group historically challenging to engage and retain due to factors such as socioeconomic barriers, busy schedules, and logistical challenges, which were further complicated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Few studies have systematically described recruitment and retention strategies for this population in the context of oral health research.AimThe purpose of this paper is to describe and evaluate the recruitment and retention strategies used to enroll and maintain 186 third-trimester pregnant women and their infants within a primary care setting, and to assess the relative efficiency of each approach based on enrollment conversion rates, contribution to overall recruitment, and time allocation.MethodsParticipants were recruited from two facilities of the University of Rochester: Highland Family Medicine (HFM), and the Perinatal dental clinic at Eastman Institute for Oral Health in Rochester, NY using a combination of direct recruitment, community networking, and outreach within prenatal groups. Retention efforts included scheduling study visits alongside routine pediatric appointments to reduce participant burden, offering financial incentives, and implementing a Reminder, Recall, and Outreach (RRO) system managed by a dedicated outreach worker. During the COVID-19 pandemic, additional adaptations were made, including offering home visits and curbside data collection, to maintain participant safety and adherence.Results and conclusionOver 27 months, the study successfully enrolled 186 mother-infant dyads, meeting the recruitment target despite initial challenges and COVID-19 disruptions. Retention rates remained high, with an approximate 15% annual attrition during the two-year follow-up period, attributed to strategic visit alignment, personalized outreach, and practical supports like parking vouchers and home visits. The study underscores the importance of customized recruitment and retention strategies in overcoming socioeconomic and logistical barriers in underserved populations. Findings contribute valuable insights into best practices for engaging mother-infant dyads in longitudinal oral health research.

Introduction

Early childhood caries (ECC) is the single most common chronic childhood disease, disproportionately affecting up to 72.7% of underprivileged preschool children in both developing and industrialized countries. Dental caries is a transmissible, and biofilm (plaque)-dependent infectious disease [1]. ECC occurs on the primary teeth in a child 72 months of age or younger [2]. Severe early childhood caries (S-ECC) occurs in children younger than 3 years of age and in children 4–6 years of age with elevated caries scores [3]. This disease substantially and adversely impacts children, families, and public health systems.

Contemporary evidence has shifted the understanding of dental caries from a traditional infectious disease model toward an ecological dysbiosis framework, in which dental caries results from a disruption of the homeostatic balance within the oral microbial community [4, 5]. Within this framework, cross-kingdom interactions between C. albicans and S. mutans are of particular interest, as C. albicans has been shown to synergistically amplify biofilm virulence and promote cariogenic community assembly [6, 7].

Clinical studies have observed that Candida albicans is highly abundant in the oral cavity of children with ECC, together with Streptococcus mutans, a well-known culprit for ECC [8]. However, the acquisition of oral C. albicans in children with ECC and the association between the colonization of C. albicans and S. mutans remains unclear [9]. The primary objective of this prospective observational cohort study was to enroll 186 women during the third trimester and their newborn infants (birth to 24 months of age) and investigate the colonization of C. albicans and S. mutans in infants with high risk for severe-ECC [9], as well as the vertical transmission of C. albicans [10].

Successful recruitment and retention are crucial to the success of clinical studies, but often challenging, particularly more difficult when the study subjects are pregnant women and young children both are considered vulnerable populations [11]. Barriers to participation in research among these groups include lack of interest, historical distrust in research, cultural differences, language barriers, busy personal schedule, transportation difficulties, compliance issues, and concerns from partners or young, anxious mothers. These factors often increase hesitancy to participate, even in minimally invasive observational studies [12]. Additionally, our study population was racially diverse and socio-economically disadvantaged, further complicating compliance with healthcare provider appointments. Moreover, the implementation of COVID-19 lockdown measures posed substantial barriers to infant enrollment, making new recruitment nearly impossible during this period.

Previous studies have explored various models of recruiting and retaining mother-infant dyads in health-related research, highlighting several unique challenges. Walson identified six steps in the recruitment process for clinical trials: (1) identifying eligible patient populations, (2) adequately explaining the study to potential subjects, (3) recruiting a representative sample, (4) determining recruitment approaches at each office or agency, (5) obtaining Institutional Review Board approval, and (6) arranging for staffing, materials, and/or compensating collaborating entities for their recruitment efforts [13]. Retention, which involves developing and maintaining relationships that encourage participants to remain engaged for the duration of the study, presents an equally significant challenge [14].

A distinctive aspect of this study was the enrollment of mother-infant dyads with the goal of maintaining high retention rates, with dental study enrollment and retention conducted at a primary care physician’s office rather than a dental clinic, given that adherence to medical care is generally higher than adherence to dental care. However, this approach introduces another level of recruitment complexity, as it requires the cooperation of family physicians and their office staff to facilitate recruitment. Pediatricians and family physicians typically see infants and toddlers much more frequently than dentists during the first years of life.

While past research has studied the varying success rates for numerous types of passive and active recruitment strategies, few studies have focused specifically on recruiting pregnant women. For example, a study that recruited healthy first-trimester pregnant women found that a combination of passive and active recruitment methods was successful. Word-of-mouth referral, recommendations from other studies, and direct email were identified as the most cost-effective strategies, though they tended to yield a less representative study population (i.e., less ethnically diverse, more affluent, and better educated) [15]. A 2001 study on the recruitment of lactating women noted success through use of advertisement to new mothers and recruiting immediately postpartum in hospitals [16].

The purpose of the present paper is to describe and evaluate the recruitment and retention strategies employed in this prospective cohort, conducted within a primary care setting serving an underserved and ethnically diverse population, and to assess the relative efficiency of each approach. This paper does not report on the microbiological outcomes of First Tooth Study, which are presented elsewhere.

Methods

This prospective study was designed to examine the association between Candida albicans and Streptococcus mutans oral colonization in mothers and children who are at high risk of severe early childhood caries. The study aimed to enroll third-trimester pregnant women and their infants to investigate the potential vertical transmission of these organisms. The study was approved by the University of Rochester Medical Center’s (URMC) Institutional Review Board (RSRB number #67191.)

Eligibility and study design

The eligibility criteria for the mothers participating in this study required that they provide signed and dated informed consent, demonstrate willingness to comply with all study procedures, and be available throughout the study period. Participants needed to be female, at least 18 years of age, and pregnant at the time of enrollment, specifically in their third trimester, which spans from the 28th week of gestation to delivery. Additionally, eligibility was restricted to those covered by a state-supported Medicaid type of insurance, justified by the focus on children facing socio-economic disparities who are at higher risk for severe Early Childhood Caries (ECC).

For infants, eligibility criteria required informed permission signed and dated by a legal guardian, the ability to comply with all study procedures, and availability throughout the study. Infants could be male or female and were required to be between newborn and six months old. Moreover, eligibility depended on the infant’s mother having been enrolled in the study during her third trimester.

Exclusion criteria for mothers included any decisional impairment that would render them incapable of making an informed choice regarding participation for themselves and their child. Mothers who had received oral or systemic antifungal treatment within 90 days before the baseline study visit, or those with severe systemic medical conditions (such as HIV infection) that increase susceptibility to yeast infections, were also excluded. Additionally, pregnant women carrying more than one fetus were not eligible for participation. For infants, exclusion criteria covered those born preterm (less than 37 weeks gestation), with a low birth weight (less than 2500 g), or born with orofacial deformities such as cleft lip, cleft palate, or an oral-pharyngeal mass. Infants diagnosed with Down syndrome or those who had received antifungal treatment (oral or systemic) prior to the baseline study visit were also excluded. First tooth study included eight visits (illustrated in Fig. 1). Visit 1 (V1) was conducted exclusively with the third-trimester pregnant woman and involved a comprehensive dental examination with collection of saliva and plaque specimens. Visit 2 (V2), which occurred during the infant’s first week of life, involved enrollment of the newborn and collection of infant saliva samples using a SalivaBio Infant’s Swab, which features an antichoking design safe for use with infants under six months of age. Mothers who tested positive for C. albicans were eligible to complete swab sample collection at this visit. Visits 3 through 8 (V3–V8) included swab sample collections of infant saliva and plaque samples (when applicable) at 2, 4, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months, coinciding with well-child checkups. Survey data on demographics, history of fungal infection, antibiotic or antifungal treatment, oral hygiene practices, and infant feeding methods were collected at each visit. Recruitment strategy

Study subjects were recruited from Highland Family Medicine (HFM) and University of Rochester (UR). HFM is one of the largest family medicine residency teaching practices in Monroe County, NY, and within the Greater Rochester Practice-Based Research Network (GR-PBRN). HFM provides clinical care for pregnancy care, delivery, and comprehensive prenatal care including Centering Pregnancy and the care to the baby immediately after birth. Typically, HFM schedules the newborn’s first check-up within two days of hospital discharge, confirming the appointment with the family before they leave the hospital, These characteristics make HFM an ideal site for recruiting study participants. In 2015, HFM medical records indicated that 656 pregnant women (44% African American, 39% Caucasian, 7% Asian, and 10% other ethnicities) received care at the clinic. These demographic proportions were used to establish race- and ethnicity-specific enrollment targets for the study. By applying the racial/ethnic distribution of HFM’s patient population to the overall target of 186 mother-infant dyads, the study aimed to recruit a sample representative of the community served by the clinic. This approach was intended to ensure that the study population reflected the diversity of the primary recruitment site and that findings would be generalizable to the underserved communities the study aimed to serve.

Recruitment methods

The recruitment methods for this study included several direct and indirect approaches to engage potential participants. At the HFM clinic, recruitment was actively conducted by leveraging the clinic’s existing relationship with patients, allowing for direct engagement. Additionally, active recruitment took place during Centering Pregnancy group meetings at HFM, where participants were approached during these group sessions. At the EIOH prenatal dental clinic, pregnant women receiving care were also directly contacted, further facilitating active recruitment efforts.

In addition to these direct methods, passive recruitment strategies were employed. Posters were placed in key community locations frequently visited by the target population, enabling outreach to a broader audience. Another passive approach involved listing the study in the University of Rochester’s online registry of active research studies, making information about the study accessible to individuals seeking participation in research. Finally, enrolled mothers were encouraged to refer friends and family members through word of mouth, tapping into personal networks to enhance recruitment efforts.

Retention strategy

The study’s follow-up visits were strategically scheduled to be concurrent with routine prenatal and well-baby/immunization checkups, maximizing convenience for participants. Subject recruitment and follow-up site HFM had shown a retention rate of 85–90% for pregnancy and well-baby immunization visits. To further enhance retention, a Reminder Recall and Outreach (RRO) system was implemented, specifically designed to support socio-economically disadvantaged participants.

The RRO System was managed by a designated outreach worker trained to follow a strict reminder/recall protocol. The worker maintained a tickler-file system to track the enrolled mothers and their infants, ensuring timely reminders for upcoming study visits. Depending on the mother’s preference, reminders were sent via telephone, postcard, and/or letter, with the reminder method tailored to each participant’s needs.

All study participants were compensated for their time and commitment to the study, with the potential to earn up to $40 per completed visit, for a total of $280 if all eight visits were attended (including the prenatal visit for the mother and the infant visits at 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months). If a mother was tested positive for oral C. albicans carriage at Visit 1 and completed a swab sample collection at Visit 2, she received an additional $20. A $40 gift card was also provided for attending the 12-, 18-, and 24-month visits. Subject received compensation upon finishing each sample collection visit.

To address socioeconomic and physical barriers that could prevent participants from attending follow-up visits, the study provided practical support such as parking vouchers for study visit when needed.

Study design and analytical approach

This study employed a descriptive observational design to characterize recruitment and retention outcomes across three recruitment sites and multiple retention strategies. Enrollment conversion rates (percentage of eligible patients approached who enrolled), contribution to total recruitment (percentage of all enrolled participants), and time allocated to each strategy were calculated as descriptive metrics. Formal statistical comparisons between recruitment strategies were not performed, as the sites differed substantially in patient volume, visit frequency, staffing models, and the nature of patient–provider relationships, and the sample sizes across sites were highly unequal (HFM: n = 191 approached; EIOH: n = 60; Centering groups: n = 7). Visit-specific completion rates were calculated using the total number of enrolled infants (n = 160) as the denominator. Overall study completion was defined as completing Visit 8 or having early childhood caries onset documented before Visit 8.

Recruitment and enrollment

The study initially aimed to enroll 186 mother-infant dyads within a 24-month period. However, due to challenges associated with recruiting the population and logistical issues, the recruitment period was extended over 27 months. Between November 22, 2017, and March 5, 2020, a total of 258 third-trimester pregnant women were screened across three different recruitment sites: HFM clinic, Centering prenatal groups at HFM, and EIOH prenatal dental clinic.

Out of the 258 women who were screened for eligibility, 251 met the inclusion criteria, and 186 provided consent to participate in the study. Of these, 181 mothers completed the first visit, while 93 out of 106 (87.7%) eligible for V2 continued to complete the second visit. For the infant participants, 160 babies met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled in the study between March 1, 2018, and August 20, 2020. The majority of infants (n = 134) were enrolled at Visit 2, while the remaining 26 infants were enrolled at later visits: 19 at Visit 3, 3 at Visit 4, 2 at Visit 5, and 2 at Visit 6.

The most effective recruitment strategy was direct contact at the HFM clinic, which accounted for 71.5% of total recruitment. In contrast, the Centering prenatal groups at HFM contributed only 1.6%, while the EIOH prenatal dental clinic accounted for 26.9% of the total recruitment. Passive recruitment strategies, such as posters and online study registries, did not yield any enrolled participants.

Retention rates

Retention was a significant challenge, with noticeable decline in participant numbers at each subsequent follow-up visit. The most significant drop in infant participation occurred after Visit 2, despite implementing strategies such as coordinating study visits with routine healthcare appointments, providing financial incentives, and utilizing a reminder/recall outreach system. The majority of the infants who continued participation beyond Visit 2 were those whose mothers had a strong initial commitment to the study, indicating that early engagement may be crucial to long-term retention. Recruitment strategies: efficiency and enrollment outcomes

Among the 186 enrolled pregnant women, 91% were retained through delivery. Seventeen subjects were discontinued early by the principal investigator for the following reasons: 6 mothers delivered preterm or low-birth-weight infants whose infants were ineligible for enrollment; 4 mothers delivered before completing V1; 1 mother’s husband did not allow her continued participation or infant enrollment; 1 mother voluntarily withdrew before completing V1; 1 mother voluntarily withdrew after V1 but before completing V2; 3 mothers were unreachable and did not complete V2 (2 unreachable, 1 moved out of state); and 1 mother withdrew before V2 due to COVID-19 safety concerns and declined to enroll her infant.

According to the inclusion/exclusion criteria, 16 infants were ineligible, yielding 170 eligible infants (186 − 16). Of these, 160 infants were enrolled (86% of the 186 target). Among enrolled infants, 28 were discontinued: 1 voluntary withdrawal, 8 moved out of town, and 19 lost to follow-up.

Visit-specific completion rates, calculated using the 160 enrolled infants as the denominator, were as follows: V2, 84% (134/160); V3, 79% (126/160); V4, 62% (99/160); V5, 72% (115/160); V6, 77% (123/160); V7, 66% (106/160); and V8, 71% (114/160). Based on First Tooth Study’s protocol, infants who completed V8 or had ECC onset before V8 were considered to have completed the study; 74% of infants (118/160) met this criterion. During the COVID-19 outbreak, the study was paused from March 16 to May 31, 2020, resulting in 74 missed visits (8 for V2, 11 for V3, 24 for V4, 11 for V5, 5 for V6, 5 for V7, and 10 for V8). Overall retention from enrollment to study completion was 74% (118/160), with an annualized attrition rate of approximately 15%. Table 1 presents the demographic distribution and target achievement for pregnant women and infant participants, showing strong overall enrollment performance. Pregnant women achieved 100% of the enrollment target, while infants reached 86%. Black or African American participants exceeded enrollment targets for both groups (115% for pregnant women, 103% for infants), while White or Caucasian participants fell short (80% and 49%, respectively). Asian enrollment was notably low at 36% for pregnant women and 40% for infants. Conversely, participants in the “Others” and Hispanic/Latino groups were overrepresented, with enrollment exceeding 100%, including 240% for infants in the “Others” category.

Although the study aimed to enroll more Asian participants, their enrollment fell significantly short, likely due to limited outreach, cultural or linguistic barriers, or insufficiently targeted recruitment strategies. Participants in the “Other” racial group greatly exceeded targets (180% for pregnant women, 240% for infants), likely because recruitment efforts resonated more with individuals outside predefined racial categories. These discrepancies emphasize the need for tailored recruitment strategies to align participant representation with study goals and ensure inclusivity across all demographic groups.

Discussion

Recruitment and retention strategies: lessons learned

This study demonstrates that tailored, active recruitment strategies are essential for enrolling and retaining underserved mother-infant dyads in longitudinal dental research. Among the approaches employed, direct recruitment at the HFM clinic was the most effective, accounting for 71.5% of total enrollment with a 69.6% conversion rate among eligible patients approached. Face-to-face interactions at a familiar healthcare setting fostered trust and rapport, which were critical for engaging a population with well-documented barriers to research participation, including historical distrust, cultural differences, and language barriers [7, 8]. This finding is consistent with prior work by Webster et al.15 who found that active, personal recruitment methods were more effective than passive approaches for enrolling pregnant women, though their study noted that such methods may yield less demographically representative samples. In the present study, direct recruitment at HFM achieved strong representation of Black or African American participants (115% of target), though Asian enrollment fell short (36% of target), suggesting that even effective active strategies require further cultural and linguistic tailoring to reach all subgroups.

The EIOH Perinatal Dental Clinic achieved the highest per-patient efficiency (83.3% enrollment rate) despite contributing a smaller share of total enrollment (26.9%), likely because women attending a dental clinic were already engaged with oral health care and more receptive to a dental research study. In contrast, passive strategies (posters, online registries) and recruitment through Centering Pregnancy groups (1.6% of enrollment) were ineffective, reinforcing that personal engagement is indispensable for this population.

A distinctive feature of this study was the integration of dental research visits into routine primary care appointments at a family medicine clinic rather than a dental setting. This interdisciplinary model leveraged the higher adherence rates associated with medical care compared to dental care and reduced participant burden by eliminating the need for separate study visits. HFM’s established 85–90% retention rate for well-baby immunization visits provided a strong foundation for study retention. The alignment of study visits with well-child checkups at 2, 4, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months was a key factor in achieving visit-specific completion rates ranging from 62% to 84%, with an overall study completion rate of 74% (118/160). However, this approach required close coordination with family physicians and clinic staff, adding operational complexity that should be anticipated in future studies adopting similar models.

Retention was further supported by the RRO system, which provided personalized reminders tailored to each participant’s preferred communication method, and by practical support including financial compensation (up to $280 across all visits) and parking vouchers. These strategies collectively addressed the socioeconomic and logistical barriers, transportation difficulties, busy schedules, and competing priorities that are well-recognized obstacles to research participation among underserved populations [8, 10]. The most significant attrition occurred among infants who were lost to follow-up (n = 19) or moved out of the area (n = 8), factors that are largely beyond the control of study-level retention strategies. Early maternal engagement appeared to be an important predictor of sustained participation, as infants whose mothers demonstrated strong initial commitment were more likely to complete the full study protocol.

Improving recruitment and retention in future studies

The challenges encountered in this study offer valuable insights into designing more effective recruitment and retention strategies in future research. Integrating research activities more closely with routine care processes could prove beneficial, as it allows research to become a seamless part of participants’ regular healthcare experiences. This strategy could be particularly effective in maintaining long-term engagement, especially in studies involving underserved populations.

Increasing the frequency and intensity of direct contact with potential participants could also improve recruitment outcomes. Personal engagement through cultural and linguistically appropriate communication is crucial for building trust within diverse populations. Additionally, strengthening partnerships with community organizations and healthcare providers could enhance recruitment efforts by leveraging existing relationships and networks trusted by the community.

Impact of COVID-19 on study operations: challenges and adaptation

The COVID-19 pandemic introduced significant challenges to human research, impacting ethical, logistical, and operational aspects [18]. Many studies were paused or halted due to social distancing measures, while participant withdrawal rates increased due to concerns over infection risk [19]. Researchers faced ethical dilemmas in balancing participant safety with research goals, and obtaining informed consent became more complex.

Recruitment and retention efforts faced significant challenges due to limited access to participants and logistical difficulties, particularly with the transition to virtual data collection [20]. Concerns about participant safety, exacerbated by the heightened risk of infection, necessitated the implementation of stringent safety protocols, leading to increased costs. The pandemic also impacted data integrity, introducing biases through remote methodologies and disrupting longitudinal studies [21]. Given the substantial impact of these challenges on our human research during the COVID-19 pandemic, we developed new strategies to adapt effectively: We expanded the option of home visits to conduct the study at participants’ residences. Initially, since the study’s inception in late 2017, home visits were offered selectively and less than 10% of participants received this service to prevent protocol deviations when facility visits were not possible. Following the onset of COVID-19, this service was extended to all participants, with home visits exceeding 80%.

Additionally, we introduced curbside services to enhance safety and social distancing measures. To minimize exposure, particularly for infants, we conducted data collection while babies remained in their cars, with research assistants retrieving the necessary data from the curb (Fig. 3).

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study highlights the complexity of recruiting and retaining pregnant women and young children in longitudinal research and the importance of personalized, community-engaged strategies. The most effective recruitment method in the study was direct, face-to-face interaction at a familiar and trusted healthcare facility. The interdisciplinary recruitment model, which integrated dental research into routine healthcare visits at a primary care physician’s office was also a unique and effective strategy. Future research efforts should focus on building stronger community partnerships, integrating research into routine care, and addressing the specific barriers that hinder participant engagement and retention.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study employed descriptive design, and formal statistical comparisons between recruitment strategies were not performed. The three recruitment sites differed in patient volume, clinical context, staffing allocation, and the nature of patient–provider relationships, precluding meaningful inferential comparisons without adjustment for these confounders. Second, a formal cost-effectiveness analysis was not conducted, as the study did not prospectively track all direct and indirect costs associated with each recruitment strategy, including staff salary allocation, travel expenses for home visits, materials, and opportunity costs of clinic time. The efficiency metrics reported (conversion rates, time allocation) provide a practical but incomplete picture of resource utilization. Future studies should incorporate prospective cost tracking from the design phase to enable formal cost-effectiveness analyses. Third, the study was conducted at a single academic medical center in Rochester, New York, which may limit generalizability to other clinical settings or geographic regions. Fourth, the COVID-19 pandemic introduced an unanticipated disruption that affected visit completion rates, particularly for Visit 4, and necessitated protocol adaptations (home visits, curbside data collection) that may have introduced variability in data collection conditions. Finally, the reasons for non-participation among eligible women who declined enrollment (n = 65) were not systematically recorded beyond brief notes, limiting the ability to fully characterize barriers to enrollment.
