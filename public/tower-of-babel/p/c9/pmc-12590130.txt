Research Note: The influence of storage duration and short periods of incubation during egg storage on hatching performance of indigenous chickens
Authors: Zhouying Zhang, Caiyue Ge, Aixin Ni, Hanhan Yang, Hongfeng Du, Jiangpeng Guo, Yunlei Li, Xintong Han, Jingwei Yuan, Jilan Chen, Yanyan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtended duration of fertile eggs severely compromises hatchability and chick quality in poultry. This study evaluated the effect of short periods of incubation during egg storage (SPIDES) in mitigating hatching losses associated with storage for indigenous Beijing-You chickens. A total of 1,440 eggs were used across six treatments representing durations of egg storage (7, 14, and 21 days) and SPIDES treatment. Freshly collected eggs were stored in egg storage room at 16 °C and 65 % relative humidity. The eggs were subjected to a standard SPIDES procedure, which involved 1-h incubation at 35 °C and natural cooling to 28.8 °C. Following the cooling phase, the eggs were returned to the same storage room as the control eggs. SPIDES was performed at day 5, day 5 and 10, and day 5, 10, and 15 for eggs stored for 7 days, 14 days, and 21 days during storage, respectively. Results showed that prolonged storage significantly reduced the hatchability of fertile eggs (89.77 % for 7 days to 54.96 % for 21 days) and the percentage of normal day-old chicks (99.77 % to 78.07 %), and increased the percentage of mid-late-dead embryos (9-21 embryonic day) (4.69 % to 39.04 %). SPIDES treatment dramatically reversed these effects on eggs stored for 21-day by improving hatchability of fertile eggs to 79.87 % (P < 0.05) and the percentage of normal day-old chicks to 89.50 % (P < 0.05), while reducing the percentage of mid-late-dead embryos to 17.09 % (P < 0.05). SPIDES also reduced early embryonic mortality and enhanced hatch synchrony by narrowing the hatch window. These results demonstrated that SPIDES effectively increase the hatching performance by reversing storage-induced embryonic damage. This confirms SPIDES as an essential intervention for maintaining hatching performance in eggs of indigenous chicken breeds subjected to prolonged storage.Keywords: Egg storage, SPIDES, Incubation, Embryonic mortality

Introduction

Storage duration is critical for the hatchability of eggs. Notably, freshly laid eggs exhibit lower embryonic viability and longer incubation periods compared to those stored for 1-2 days. This is attributed to high initial CO₂ concentrations hindering embryonic development and the viscous albumen impeding gas exchange (Adriaensen et al., 2022). To address these challenges, commercial hatcheries routinely refrigerate eggs for 3–7 days post-collection. However, the extended storage beyond 7 days is often necessary to accommodate large-capacity incubators, achieve uniform hatch times, and manage irregular egg collection schedules (Ansah, et al., 2023). While storage for up to 7 days at approximately 18 °C has negligible effects on hatchability, storage exceeding this period leads to reduced hatchability and chick quality. This decline is attributed to CO₂ accumulation, albumen thinning, increased pH, partial protein denaturation, degradation of the vitelline membrane and elevated levels of necrotic and apoptotic cells, causing irreversible damage to the embryo (Dai, et al., 2025; Pennings, et al., 2025). To mitigate these adverse effects, strategies like short periods of incubation during egg storage (SPIDES)—a pre-incubation technique mimicking maternal behavior that enables embryos to reach the hypoblast stage—significantly improve hatchability of eggs stored for extended duration (Ansah, et al., 2023). In the same vein, indigenous breeds, especially those kept as small live conservation populations produce fertile egg over an extended duration, making collection and storage of the eggs follow the same pattern. This is necessary to ensure that enough offspring are generated to maintain the population. Although the detrimental impact of extended storage on hatchability and the mitigating effect of SPIDES is a routine practice in the broiler and laying hens industry, the application of the SPIDES technique on eggs of indigenous breeds that are stored for a longer duration has not been optimized. Consequently, this study was designed to evaluate the effects of SPIDES on hatching performance, chick quality, and hatching window in eggs of indigenous Beijing-You chickens subjected to different storage durations.

Materials and methods

Ethical consideration

All animal procedures complied with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (IAS-CAAS). The study protocol was approved by the IAS-CAAS Ethics Committee (Protocol No. IAS2021-48).

Experimental design and treatment

This study used 1,440 eggs from 33 to 35-week-old Beijing-You chickens at the experimental farm of IAS-CAAS, collected between 9:00 AM and 11:00 AM. All eggs were visually inspected and candled to eliminate those with cracks, flaws, or abnormal shapes. There were six treatments according to storage duration (7, 14, or 21 days) and the application of SPIDES or not. Hence, the treatments were delineated as 1 (7-day storage without SPIDES), 2 (7-day storage with one SPIDES on day 5), 3 (14-day storage without SPIDES), 4 (14-day storage with SPIDES on days 5 and 10), 5 (21-day storage without SPIDES), and 6 (21-day storage with SPIDES on days 5, 10, and 15). To avoid the effect of different incubators on the focused hatch-related traits, the same incubator was used for the six treatments at the same time. To achieve this, we started the collection of the eggs of six treatments differently to ensure that they should be set for incubation on the same day. All treatments were replicated three times with 80 eggs per replicate. All eggs were stored under controlled conditions (16 °C and 65 % RH). During all SPIDES treatments, eggs were positioned vertically with the blunt end up in a single commercial incubator (Model BLF-440C, Supplier: Chengdu Beili Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China) and subjected to a thermal regimen of 35.0 °C for 1 h without turning and natural cooling to 28.8 °C before being transferred to the storage room. The highest eggshell temperature recorded during the SPIDES phase (1 hour at 35.0 °C) was approximately 34.6 °C, monitored using an infrared thermometer (Model DL333600, Supplier: Ningbo Deli Tools Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China).

Incubation and data collection

The formal single-stage incubation (embryonic day [E] 1 to E18) of all groups was performed in the same commercial incubator (Model 19200, Supplier: Beijing Haijiang Hatching Equipment Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), where temperature (37.8 °C) and RH (55 %) were automatically monitored and maintained by the equipment under stable conditions. Eggs were turned automatically once every hour. Fertility was determined by candling at E8. Eggs were classified as fertilized if distinct vascular networks or embryonic shadows were observed. Unfertilized eggs and early dead-in-shell (E1-E8) were recorded based on candling and observation of eggs without obvious embryos. Day of death for early dead-in-shell eggs were recorded following the method of Cobb-Vantress (2024).

On E18, all eggs were transferred to hatcher baskets and placed in a hatcher set at 37.5 °C until E21. Starting at 456 hours of incubation (E19), the number of hatched chicks was documented for each replicate at 12-hour intervals until the end of the hatching process (i.e., 456 h, 468 h, 480 h, 492 h, and 504 h). The hatching dynamics and embryonic survival patterns were monitored. The hatching process was terminated at 504 hours, with no further extension provided for prolonged-stored eggs. Chick quality was assessed using a scoring system adapted from Tona et al.(2003), which evaluates multiple morphological and vitality traits, including activity, down cleanliness, navel closure, yolk sac absorption, leg strength, and overall conformation. Each parameter was assigned a weighted score, with a perfect total of 100 points. Chicks with a total score ≥ 85 were classified as "normal," while those scoring < 85 were classified as "weak." Concurrently, the number of normal chicks, weak chicks, and mid-late-dead embryos (E9-E21) was counted in each replicate.

Statistical analyses

The present study was a 3 × 2 factorial design with 3 storage duration and 2 SPIDES treatment (as a binary factor: applied or not). Fertility rate, hatchability rate from the total egg set hatchability of fertile eggs, and percentages of normal day-old chicks, weak day-old chicks, mid-late-dead embryos and early-dead embryos were analyzed via a factorial ANOVA using the GLM procedure in SAS 9.4 software. Statistical significance was defined at P < 0.05. The model used for hatchability-related parameters was Yijk = μ + Ai + Tj + (AT)ij + eijk, where Yijkis the dependent variable, μ is the overall mean, Ai is the storage duration(i = 7, 14 or 21 days), Tj is the preincubation heat treatment (j = control or SPIDES), (AT)ij is the interaction between the storage duration and SPIDES treatment, and eijk is the error term. The proportion of hatched chicks at 456, 468, 480, 492, and 504 hours post-incubation were plotted using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2.

Results and discussion

Duration of egg storage and SPIDES treatment significantly impacted hatchability (Table 1). Storage time is a critical factor affecting hatchability. The optimal storage of 3-7 days benefits hatchability by improving early embryonic oxygen supply. This improvement is facilitated by liquefaction of albumen which reduces the physical barrier to oxygen diffusion towards the blastoderm (Adriaensen, et al., 2022). Conversely, storage beyond 7 days increases albumen thinning and causes death of chicken blastoderm cells, thereby reducing hatchability and lowering chick quality (Ansah, et al., 2023). Our results demonstrate that prolonged storage (7→14→21 days) significantly degraded key hatchability metrics: hatchability of fertile eggs decreases from 89.77 % to 54.96 % (P < 0.05), while the percentage of mid-late-dead embryos increased from 4.69 % to 39.04 % (P < 0.05). Storage duration also significantly impacted post-hatch chick quality. For egg storage up to 21 days, the percentage of normal day-old chicks dropped from 99.77 % to 78.07 %.

SPIDES treatment during egg storage provides a brief developmental window for the blastoderm, enhancing cell viability and developmental progression. A recent study indicated that SPIDES improved hatchability and reduced embryonic mortality by promoting endoderm formation during egg storage (Özlü, et al., 2021b), effectively mitigating the negative effects of prolonged storage. SPIDES treatment significantly improves hatching metrics by increasing hatchability of fertile egg (79.87 % vs. 54.86 %) and the percentage of normal day-old chicks (89.50 % vs. 78.07 %), and reducing percentage of mid-late-dead embryos (17.09 % vs. 39.04 %, compared to the control (P < 0.05). These findings are corroborated by a previous study that reported marked improvement in hatchability of approximately 20 % of set and fertile eggs stored for over a period of 20 days (Ansah et al., 2023). Strikingly, SPIDES treatment resulted in a higher percentage of normal day-old chicks (88.65 % vs. 85.50 %) and a lower percentage of weak day-old chicks (11.35 % vs. 14.50 %) compared to the control group for a storage of 14 days. This improvement in embryo survival and chick quality for eggs stored longer than the conventional maximum 7 days is attributable to SPIDES’s ability to initiate and promote uniform embryonic development. This finding underscores the importance of SPIDES treatment for eggs stored for an extended duration.

Beyond improving hatchability, SPIDES treatment could be associated with a reduction in early embryonic mortality among eggs under extended storage (5.82 % vs. 4.39 %), although this difference was not statistically significant (Table 1, P = 0.4178). The most pronounced numerical improvement was observed in the reduction in early mortality from 6.00 % to 3.06 % for eggs stored for a duration of 21 days. These results align with the findings of Özlü et al. (2021a) who reported significant increase in hatchability by 3.8 % (P < 0.05) and reduction in embryonic mortality by 1.55 % (P < 0.05) for eggs that received SPIDES treatment. This further highlights the potential role of SPIDES treatment in enhancing embryonic viability during incubation. As suggested by previous studies, SPIDES may achieve this by reducing apoptotic cell death in the blastoderm (Brady et al., 2022), and potentially by helping to maintain blastoderm viability during the storage period (Özlü et al., 2021b).

For eggs stored for a duration of 7 to 14 days, over 90 % of chicks hatched within 480 hours of incubation, with no significant difference between control and SPIDES-treated eggs (Fig. 1A and 1B). Beyond 14 days of egg storage, hatching rates dropped sharply to only 20 % for untreated eggs, but remained substantially higher (60 %) for eggs treated with SPIDES. Furthermore, extended period of egg storage significantly widened the hatch window. Eggs stored for 7 days had higher proportions of early and mid-term hatching, whereas those stored for 21 days exhibited delayed and less synchronized hatching—a pattern consistent with prior findings (Tona et al., 2003). Importantly, SPIDES treatment markedly improved hatch synchrony in eggs stored for a longer duration, substantially increasing the proportion of early and mid-term hatching and effectively narrowing the hatch window (Fig. 1C). This suggests that SPIDES counteracts the negative effects of prolonged storage on hatch synchrony. While a previous study reported a less pronounced effect of SPIDES (Damaziaket al., 2018), the stronger response observed in the current study may be attributable to our longer storage duration and higher SPIDES temperature.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that prolonged duration of egg storage significantly degrades hatchability, increases embryonic mortality (particularly mid-late-dead embryos), reduces chick quality, and widens the hatch window. Notably, SPIDES technique effectively mitigates these detrimental effects primarily. SPIDES treatment significantly improved hatchability of fertile eggs and healthy chick yield especially for eggs stored over a duration of 21 days. Additionally, SPIDES markedly reduced the incidence of early and mid-late embryonic mortality, while enhancing hatch synchrony by increasing early/mid-term hatching proportions and narrowing the hatch window. These findings confirmed that SPIDES is an essential intervention for maintaining hatching performance and chick quality in indigenous chicken breed hatchery when utilizing eggs stored for a long duration.
