Seasonal variation in temperature and life-space mobility among older adults: a pilot study using mobile health technology
Authors: Annabel X Tan, Sylvie Dobrota Lai, Vishnu Ravi, J David Rhodes, Suzanne E Judd, Michelle C Odden, Min-Ah Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesExtreme outdoor temperatures are known barriers to physical activity and may constrain life-space mobility, the geographic footprint of where people live, work, and recreate, and an important indicator of health and independence among older adults. We conducted a pilot study to evaluate the feasibility of a mobile health (mHealth) app designed to objectively capture life-space and to demonstrate its utility through a downstream analysis of ambient temperature and life-space mobility.Research Design and MethodsUsing data collected via an iPhone app from 82 participants in an ongoing cohort study (June 2023-January 2024), we used linear mixed-effects models to examine associations between daily average temperature and 3 objective life-space measures: ellipse area, maximum distance traveled, and total distance traveled. Models were stratified by season and adjusted for relative humidity and sociodemographic covariates.ResultsThe app successfully captured high-resolution longitudinal mobility data over 2 weeks per participant. There was a significant negative nonlinear association between higher daily average temperature and life-space in the summer. With the peak life-space ellipse area observed at 28.8 °C, for every degree Celsius greater than 28.8 °C, we found a statistically significant decrease in ellipse area by 2.97 km2 (95% CI: −5.45, −0.51; p = .02). Findings were similar for the outcomes of maximum and total distances traveled.Discussion and ImplicationsThis study demonstrates that mHealth technology is a feasible tool for assessing life-space mobility in older adults. Higher temperatures are associated with lower life-space metrics, highlighting the potential of app-derived metrics as digital biomarkers for mobility research.Keywords: Life-space mobility, Mobile health, Temperature, REGARDS

Older adults are vulnerable to high temperatures, yet the association between ambient temperature and life-space mobility, a measure of independence and well-being among older adults traditionally captured by questionnaire, remains understudied. This pilot study addresses critical gaps by replacing recall-dependent methods with a mobile health (mHealth) app for objective tracking of life-space mobility. We found a significant, negative nonlinear association between ambient temperature in the summer and life-space mobility. These insights establish life-space mobility as a mobile phone-derived metric that could be deployed at a larger scale and could inform strategies to improve mobility in older adults amidst climate change.

Introduction

Older adults are considered among the most vulnerable to temperature highs and lows, due to the decreasing ability to thermoregulate and the increased number of chronic conditions.1,2,3 Functional mobility is associated with positive health outcomes among older adults; thus, environmental factors that limit mobility could be detrimental for healthy aging.4 While high and low outdoor temperature is an established barriers to physical activity,5 its impact on life-space mobility, defined as the geographic footprint of where people live, work, and recreate, remains poorly understood. This research is especially relevant for marginalized older populations, who often live in areas that are most impacted by high and low temperature extremes due to structural inequities.6

Life-space quantifies the concept of mobility by measuring distance traveled, frequency of excursions, and independence attained as older adults go about their environment.7 Compared to mobility or mobility function, life-space is a more complex, multifactorial construct that captures the physical and social aspects of a person’s environment.8 Life-space has been shown to be associated with numerous health outcomes, such as cognitive impairment, peripheral arterial disease, and executive function.9–12,13 A study in community-dwelling older adults found that poorer life-space mobility was associated with a higher prevalence of different dimensions of depressive symptoms.14 Life-space has also been used as a potential predictor of nursing home admission15 as well as a predictor of 6-month mortality among older adults.16 Restricted life-space has been associated with a sharper decline in cognitive ability.17 In general, life-space instruments have been shown to predict mortality, morbidity, and healthcare use.18 These studies underscore the value of life-space as a potential novel and protective risk factor for health outcomes, especially among older adults. Despite the clinical significance of life-space mobility, traditional methods of measuring life-space rely on self-reported questionnaires, which are prone to recall bias.

This study addresses 2 critical gaps: first, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility of deploying a mobile health (mHealth) app to capture high-resolution, longitudinal life-space data. Second, we sought to determine the significance of ambient temperature (very high, very low) as a risk factor for winnowing life-space. Life-space was objectively measured through a mobile phone application (app)12,19 carried by participants, to increase the accuracy of life-space capture compared to traditionally self-reported life-space survey responses. The goal of the analysis was to understand the association between ambient temperature, matched to an individual’s zip code and date of data collection, and objectively measured life-space metrics. The primary hypothesis was that higher ambient temperatures would be associated with a smaller life-space. We also hypothesize that associations will vary by season. Participants were recruited from the REasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS) study. Given the intersection between place, environment, and health outcomes, the long-term goal of this research could inform urban planning efforts, especially among neighborhoods that are most socially vulnerable and most susceptible to the effects of high and low temperatures.

Methods

This pilot study collected data utilizing a mobile iPhone app to passively capture life-space from a subset of participants who were enrolled in the REGARDS study. The study enrolled 30 239 Black and White participants aged ≥45 years from the continental United States between 2003 and 2007. Participants were oversampled from the southeastern United States, commonly known as the “Stroke Belt,” which comprises Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Indiana, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, and Virginia. The primary objective of the REGARDS study is to better understand the reasons for the excess stroke mortality among Black persons and among those residing in the southeastern United States.20 Health and demographic data in REGARDS were collected at baseline on participants in 3 in-home exams that included a questionnaire, physical exam, and blood draw. Participants are followed up via telephone at 6-month intervals for identification of cardiovascular events. Participants were initially screened for eligibility for this pilot life-space study during these biannual telephone calls and invited to participate in the proposed study by mail.

Study enrollment

Recruitment and enrollment for the pilot life-space study took place over 3 waves from June 2023 through January 2024. Among 1768 participants screened, there were 389 participants who met the eligibility criteria of (1) having an iPhone model 8 or later, (2) bringing their iPhone with them usually when they left the house, (3) knowing how to download an app, and (4) who were not homebound. Further enrollment details are described in the CONSORT diagram in Supplementary Figure 1. Among the 389 eligible participants, 82 enrolled and completed the study. The demographic characteristics of our analytical sample of 82 participants are described in Table 1.

After emailing the study team to express interest in the study, participants were given a study number as an identifier and instructions to download the Cardinal LifeSpace app, described below, over email. They were informed of the study goals, risks, and benefits, and consented within the app. Participants engaged in the study for 2 weeks (14 days), after which they were instructed to delete the app from their phones. Participants received compensation of up to $100 if they were enrolled and engaged in the study for 14 days. This study was approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board (IRB #65276).

Participant measures

Demographic data on age, sex (male/female), race (Black/White), education (less than high school, high school graduate, some college, college graduate), income (<$35,000, $35,000-$75,000, >$75,000), employment status (employed/retired/other), and urbanicity (urban/rural/mixed) were self-reported at baseline in the main REGARDS study. Details of other REGARDS participant measures are described elsewhere.20

Cardinal LifeSpace App

The Cardinal LifeSpace App was developed in-house at Stanford University in 2022 and designed to ensure participant data and privacy were protected. This app was built on the open-source Spezi (formally CardinalKit) mobile development platform.21 After a participant downloaded the app, the app passively tracked the participant’s latitude and longitude every time the phone was moved 250 m. When the app was opened, a map was displayed for the participant to see their location, and they had the option of turning off location-tracking temporarily if desired. Each evening, at 7 pm in a participant’s local time zone, a notification invited participants to engage in a survey asking 3 questions: (a) “How would you rate your day?” represented as a sliding bar ranging from poor (1) to excellent (5); (b) “How would you rate the environments in which you spent your time?” represented as a sliding bar ranging from poor (1) to excellent (5); and (c) Is this map of your daily activity accurate (yes/no). If the participant answered that the map was not accurate, a text box was displayed with the question “why?.” Screenshots from the app are available in Supplementary Figure 2.

Outcome—life-space data

Life-space measures were calculated using the iPhone’s geospatial positional system (GPS) coordinates. Life-space was derived from 3 GPS-derived metrics that have been previously found to be associated with health outcomes: (1) ellipse area (square km),19,22,23 (2) total distance traveled across all GPS tracts (km),19 and (3) maximum distance from home traveled over 2 weeks (km).12,24 Ellipse area was calculated as the area of the minimum ellipse that encompasses all GPS points in 2 dimensions. Total distance provided an overall view of life-space size by summing the distance between all the GPS points recorded over 2 weeks. Maximum distance from home was conceptualized as the maximum distance from the GPS point that was most frequently recorded between midnight and 5am in the participant’s data and therefore assumed to be the participant’s primary place of residence during the study participation period. If a participant chose to disable location tracking, GPS coordinates would not be recorded, and life-space data could not be calculated during this time.

Life-space measures were winsorized due to some extreme outliers. Values larger than those in the 99% quantile and smaller than in the 1% quantile were replaced with the 99th and 1st quantiles and retained in the analytic sample.

Exposure—ambient temperature

An individual’s latitude and longitude from data collection were used to determine the zip code at any given point. Temperature data was obtained from the gridded Parameter-elevation Relationships on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM), which was matched based on zip code and date, using the PRISM temperature data.25 In our final dataset, we had zip code-level temperature matched to each individual that varied hourly.

Confounders and effect modifiers

In adjusted models, additional covariates included relative humidity, age, sex, race, education, employment status, and urban area. We tested for interaction by season in the adjusted models, and all analyses were additionally stratified by season. Seasons were classified as follows: summer (June, July, August), fall (September, October, November), and winter (December, January). Data were not available for the later part of winter (February) and spring (March–May) as enrollment and data collection did not occur during this period. The formula for deriving relative humidity is available in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analyses

We used mixed linear models to determine the relationship between ambient temperature as the exposure and life-space metrics (ellipse, maximum distance travelled, total distance travelled) as the outcomes, and used a random intercept for each individual to account for individual-specific variation. Mixed-effect models were chosen in order to capture the group effects among study participants over the course of 2 weeks. A quadratic temperature term was included in both unadjusted and adjusted models to account for any nonlinearity in the temperature and life-space association. The temperature was also centered to make for easier interpretability of results. The equations representing these associations are available in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Table 1 describes the characteristics of the pilot study sample. There were 82 participants who completed the study over the course of 14 days. The mean age of participants was 74.5 years (range: 61-89 years), 82.9% were White, 41.5% were male, and 70.7% were college graduates. A majority of the sample also lived in urban areas, and more than half made over $75,000 a year in annual income. 48% of participants were employed, while 44% were retired. Most of the study sample had 0 or 1 chronic condition. In the fall, the mean average temperature was 19.9 °C (range: 8.9-28.9 °C); in the summer, the mean average temperature was 25.5 °C (range: 12.1-37.5 °C), and in the winter, the mean average temperature was 7.2 °C (range: −8.2-21.4 °C) (results not shown in tables).

We found that associations between life-space metrics and temperature vary by season in the fully adjusted models (Figure 1). The p-values for interaction between average temperature and summer seasons are statistically significant in adjusted models for ellipse area (p < .01), total distance travelled (p = .03), and maximum distance travelled (p < .01) but were not statistically significant for fall and winter seasons.

Association between life-space metrics (ellipse area in km2, max distance travelled in km, total distance travelled over 2 weeks in km) and temperature, stratified by season.

A curvilinear association between all life-space metrics and temperature is present in the summer months (June-August). Maximum life-space ellipse area occurs when temperatures reach 28.8 °C (Figure 1). All tabulated results from both unadjusted and adjusted models are provided in Supplementary Table 1. For every degree Celsius beyond 28.8 °C, there is a statistically significant decrease in ellipse area by 2.97 km2 (95% CI: −5.45, −0.51; p-value = .02). Similarly, peak total distance travelled from home occurs when daily average temperatures reach 29.9 °C. For every degree Celsius beyond 29.9 °C, there is a statistically significant decrease in total distance from home by 0.19 km (95% CI: −0.30, −0.07; p-value = .001). Finally, the peak maximum distance travelled from home occurs when daily average temperatures reach 32.9 °C. For every degree Celsius higher beyond 32.9 °C, maximum distance from home decreases by 0.54 km (95% CI: −0.76, −0.33, p < .001).

A curvilinear association between life-space metrics and daily average temperature exists in both fall and winter seasons. However, these associations were not statistically significant in adjusted models during these seasons. In Supplementary Table 2, we report results examining the association between temperature and life-space pooled across all seasons.

Discussion

This study successfully established the feasibility of utilizing an mHealth app to objectively capture life-space mobility in a cohort of older adults. Collecting longitudinal data over 2 weeks for every participant enabled a more accurate assessment of life-space and underscored the potential to capture dimensions not reflected in traditional methods like paper questionnaires or self-report. Furthermore, to demonstrate the utility of life-space mobility, we characterized the association between ambient temperature and life-space mobility. We found that all 3 life-space metrics—life-space ellipse area, maximum distance travelled from home, and total distance travelled—were significantly and nonlinearly negatively associated with daily average temperature during the summer months of June, July, and August. The significant association between life-space metrics and daily average temperature during these months persisted after adjustment. No statistically significant association was found between any life-space metrics and daily average temperature in adjusted models in the fall and winter months.

Previous studies have highlighted a linear association between greater life-space activity and higher temperatures. Studies conducted in the Netherlands, Finland, and Canada found that older adults had greater life-space areas in the summer than in the winter.26,27,28 Another study in Germany showed a positive association between physical activity and sunshine.29 Unlike our findings, a study in Finland found that there were no significant associations between maximum daily temperature and mobility measures in Finland.30 Our results may differ slightly from the studies discussed due to the differing latitudes; many of these studies were set in areas further north, compared to our study, which takes place in areas of the United States with diverse climates. These findings from literature may demonstrate one part of the life-space and ambient temperature relationship. However, we cannot comment on the specific geographic locations, such as which states participants resided in, of our REGARDS subsample due to privacy protections. From our results, this study provides evidence that higher outdoor temperatures may deter older adults from being mobile, particularly when temperatures are higher than 30 °C in the summer. This novel finding identifies a specific modifiable factor that likely mediates the relationship between environmental conditions and diminished life-space.

Similar to our findings, a study from Finland highlights a U-shaped relationship between life-space and extreme outdoor temperature (very hot, very cold) as a barrier to outdoor physical activity.5 The highest temperature in our sample peaked at 37.5 °C in the summer, and the lowest of −8.2 °C in the winter. Our study is unique in that it was able to demonstrate an inverse U-shape relationship between life-space ellipse area and daily average temperature in the summer despite the limited sample size, highlighting that extreme temperatures deter older adults from being mobile. Our results may also differ slightly from what has been found in literature because our population is based in the United States, which has great regional differences. Furthermore, people living in the United States may have lifestyles that rely more on automobiles than walking as a primary means of transport, compared to their counterparts in Europe, where the previously discussed studies were conducted.

Limitations

This study has several potential limitations. We had a small sample size of 82 participants, with 17 in the summer, 24 in the fall, and 41 in the winter. Despite this, we still observed that life-space metrics, such as life-space ellipse area, were significantly nonlinearly associated with daily average temperatures. A key challenge of this study was the low enrollment rate, which resulted from the administrative hurdle that required participants to transition from a paper-based recruitment method to an electronic enrollment process. Another limitation of our study is the specificity of our sample, which comprises a healthy and distinct subset of the REGARDS cohort. Participants in this study were healthy: 74% of our participants reported frequent exercise, 46% reported having no chronic conditions, and generally had very good or excellent self-reported health (data not shown). Consequently, the homogeneity of this subsample limited our statistical power to robustly assess effect modification by chronic conditions. Furthermore, the generalizability of these findings is constrained by the selection criteria: eligibility was restricted to individuals owning newer iPhone models, which inherently introduced a selection bias toward a sample that was predominantly White, affluent, well-educated, and urban-dwelling, which limits the generalizability of these findings to more diverse populations.

A further limitation stems from potential participant bias and variable data quality influenced by compliance. We recognize the potential for reactivity bias, where a participant may increase their life-space to demonstrate active participation in the study. Conversely, inconsistent compliance with study protocols, such as intermittent phone usage, failure to maintain required application settings, or choosing to disable location tracking, could lead to episodes of missing data and the attenuation of life-space values. Missing data cannot be followed up on due to privacy reasons.

Our analyses focused on ambient temperature and did not explicitly account for combined temperature–humidity conditions. Although we evaluated wet bulb temperature—which incorporates humidity—in sensitivity analyses, the results were not materially different. Future work in a larger-scale dataset could include wet bulb and apparent temperature as additional analyses.

Finally, our analyses were constrained by the limited number of confounders for inclusion due to a lack of data availability. For example, we could not account for factors such as mode of transportation or use of mobility aids. We were also not able to consider effect modification by geographic region in the United States due to privacy reasons as the analytic dataset was deidentified. These limitations may have led to residual confounding.

Conclusion

Deploying a mobile iPhone app in REGARDS offers a new opportunity to study life-space mobility among an older population in areas of the United States that have higher incidence of stroke. This study highlights the feasibility of leveraging app-based mobile phone measures in cohorts of older adults. To our knowledge, this is a novel area of environmental health research in REGARDS and otherwise. Further work in environmental factors REGARDS needs to be done to establish firm conclusions about the relationship between ambient temperature and other environmental factors, and life-space mobility. This work can provide additional objective data for future studies in digital health and aging, and outline strategies to improve mobility among older adults in a warming climate. The results of this work would also be useful in the growing field of mobile phone app-based assessments, which may be used in tandem with traditional epidemiological methods for environmental health research in older adults and beyond.
