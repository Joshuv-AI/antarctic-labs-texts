Subchronic oral toxicity and in vitro anti-neuroinflammatory evaluation of Ficus erecta leaves extract for potential functional food applications
Authors: Eunjin Sohn, Yu Jin Kim, Woo-Young Jeon, Sae-Rom Yoo, Kyuhyung Jo, Ami Lee, Aejin Kim, Jin Ah Ryuk, Chan-Sik Kim, Bu-Yeo Kim, Mee-Young Lee, Hye-Sun Lim, Youn-Hwan Hwang, Soo-Jin Jeong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFicus erecta leaves, a traditional medicinal plant that is widely used in East Asia, have demonstrated promising cognitive-enhancing effects. However, the safety of its long-term administration has not been elucidated. Therefore, we evaluated the subchronic toxicity of an ethanol extract of F. erecta leaves (EEFE) by conducting 13-week repeated oral toxicity study using Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats. Male and female rats were administered EEFE at doses of 0, 500, 1000, or 2000 mg/kg/day. Clinical signs, body weight, food consumption, hematology, serum biochemistry, organ weights, and gross necropsy findings were also monitored. No treatment-related mortality or toxic effects were observed at any doses of EEFE. Accordingly, the No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) was determined to be 2000 mg/kg/day for both sexes. Additionally, we observed anti-neuroinflammatory effects of EEFE and its active compound rutin in BV-2 microglia. EEFE and rutin significantly inhibited nitrate and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) production in BV-2 cells. These results support the safety and anti-inflammatory action of EEFE for further development as a candidate herbal therapeutic or functional food for improving cognitive health and age-related conditions.Keywords: Ficus erecta leaves, Subchronic oral toxicity test, No Observed Adverse Effect Level, Anti-neuroinflammation

Introduction

Ficus erecta, a deciduous shrub belonging to the Moraceae family, is widely distributed in temperate and subtropical regions of East and Southeast Asia. In Korea, it grows predominantly along the southern coast and islands [1]. In traditional medicine, different parts of the plant are known by various names; the root is known as “Wunaejanggeun,” the leaf as “Wunaesi,” and the fruit as “Wunaejang.” They have been used to treat constipation, enhance gastrointestinal function, and alleviate conditions such as arthritis, bruises, and menstrual disorders [1], [2].

Recent studies have provided scientific evidence supporting the pharmacological efficacies of F. erecta. The antioxidant and thrombolytic effects of its fruit extract [3] and the anti-osteoporotic effects of its leaf extract [4] have been reported. We previously demonstrated the cognition-enhancing effect of the leaf extract of F. erecta using cognition-deficient animal models. Phytochemical analysis has identified rutin, kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside, and chlorogenic acid as the main marker compounds in the extract, with rutin being a key contributor to cognitive enhancement [5], [6], [7]. Herbal extracts contain various chemical components, some of which may exert toxic effects. Thus, toxicity assessments are essential, and safety must first be ensured to develop health functional foods or therapeutic drugs using herbal extracts [8].

The leaves and fruits of F. erecta are listed as ingredients permitted for food use under the Code of Health Functional Food of the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety of Korea (notification No. 2021–54) [9], suggesting their potential as candidates for the development of functional foods or pharmaceuticals. We previously confirmed the safety of ethanol extract of F. erecta leaves (EEFE) through single dose toxicity and genotoxicity assessments. In a single dose toxicity test, the Approximate Lethal Dose (ALD) of EEFE was ≥ 5000 mg/kg in Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats [7]. A battery of three genotoxicity tests, including the micronucleus using SPF ICR mice, chromosome aberration using Chinese hamster lung (CHL) cells, and bacterial reverse mutation tests using specially mutated bacterial strains, have shown that EEFE is not genotoxic [10]. Bacterial strains for reverse mutation tests included Salmonella typhimurium (TA98, TA100, TA1535, and TA1537) and a tryptophan-requiring Escherichia coli (WP2uvrA(pKM101). In addition, the potential of EEFE as a cognition-enhancing functional food or therapeutic agent requires an evaluation of its safety caused by repeated long-term intake. Even if EEFE is listed as an ingredient that can be used in food, it is necessary to increase the reliability of the raw materials and ensure long-term stability [11].

Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the subchronic toxicity of EEFE. To this end, we conducted a 13-week repeated oral toxicity study of EEFE in SDrats. The test was performed in accordance with the OECD Test Guideline 408 [12] and the guidelines of the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) for repeated-dose toxicity testing of herbal (botanical) preparations in rodents [13]. The inhibitory effects of EEFE and its marker compound rutin on neuroinflammation were assessed in BV-2 microglia stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS).

Materials and methods

Preparation of EEFE

The leaves of F. erecta were collected in August 2023 from Gasi-ri, Pyoseon-myeon, Seogwipo-si, Jeju Island, South Korea. Plant specimens were identified based on morphological characteristics and through a comparison of similar species using phylogenomic analysis by Professor Joo-Hwan Kim at the Medicinal Plant Resources Bank, Gachon University [14]. The EEFE was prepared by the standard Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) certified facility HL Science Co., Ltd. (Chungju, South Korea) using the following procedure: 70 % ethanol (20 × the volume of the dried leaves) was added and extracted once at 80 °C for 6 h. The extract was filtered, concentrated, mixed with dextrin, and freeze-dried. The final extraction yield was 11.7 %. EEFE used in this study (sample code: SCD-A-114) was deposited at the Korean Medicine Convergence Research Division, Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine (Daejeon, South Korea).

Formulation analysis of EEFE

Prior to the 13-week repeated oral toxicity study, the EEFE preparation was evaluated for stability, homogeneity, and marker compound content. Stability was assessed by measuring rutin content in samples stored at 4 °C for 0, 1, 2, 4, 7, 10, and 14 days. Homogeneity and content analyses were performed on the preparations used at the start of administration in weeks 4 and 13. Homogeneity was verified by measuring the rutin content in the top, middle, and bottom layers of the low (50 mg/mL), medium (100 mg/mL), and high (200 mg/mL) dose solutions and was confirmed by calculating the relative standard deviation (RSD).

Experimental animals and housing conditions

The animal study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine (Approval/Protocol Number: 24–056) and performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Five-week-old male and female SPF SD rats were purchased from Orient Bio Inc. (Seongnam, South Korea). Animals were provided with standard rodent diet (2918 C; ENVIGO, Blackthorn, United Kingdom) and water ad libitum, maintained in acryl cages under controlled conditions (temperature, 22 ± 2 °C; relative humidity, 55 %; 12-h light/dark cycle) and acclimated for 1 week prior to the start of the toxicity test.

Grouping and dosage setting

Based on the results of a preliminary 4-week dose-range study, the No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) was estimated to be 2000 mg/kg/day for both sexes. Eighty healthy rats were divided into four groups (n = 10 per group, male and female each): control (vehicle only), low dose (500 mg/kg/day), medium dose (1000 mg/kg/day), and high dose (2000 mg/kg/day) (Table 1). Animals were randomly assigned to each group after stratification by body weight to minimize baseline differences between groups (average body weight (g) of 210.1 ± 0.33 for male and 164.5 ± 0.17 for female). All evaluations were conducted by the experimenters in a blinded manner. EEFE was administered once daily by oral gavage for 13 consecutive weeks. The extract was freshly prepared each day by dissolving in distilled water, a negative control, which served as the vehicle. Each rat received an individualized dose adjusted according to its body weight. In compliance with OECD test guideline 408 [15], the administration volume was standardized at 10 mL/kg body weight, and this volume was recalculated regularly to reflect changes in body weight throughout the study.

Clinical signs, body weight, and food consumption

Mortality, clinical abnormal behavior, and side effects were monitored twice daily. Body weights and food consumption were recorded once a week. Body weight on the day of sacrifice was excluded from the results because it was due to fasting. All rats were under deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of freshly prepared Avertin (300 mg/kg, Tribromoethanol; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and euthanized by exsanguination via abdominal aorta, followed by necropsy.

Hematology

Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-treated tubes (BD Vacutainer®, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Hematological analysis was conducted using an automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex XN-V, Sysmex Corp., Kobe, Japan) at KPN&T Co., Ltd. (Cheongju, Korea), an institution specializing in pathology in the field of non-clinical testing.

Serum biochemistry

Blood samples were collected in BD Vacutainer Citrate Tubes (BD Bioscience), allowed to clot at room temperature, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum. Serum biochemical parameters were analyzed using a HITACHI 7180 Clinical Analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

Organ weights

Major organs were excised and weighed using a precision balance (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). For bilateral organs, both left and right sides were measured. Absolute organ weights were measured and relative organ weights (organ weights (%) / body weight) were calculated for the following organs: brain, pituitary gland, thymus, heart, lungs, thyroid (with parathyroid), liver, kidneys, adrenal glands, spleen, testes, ovaries, uterus (with cervix), epididymis, and prostate.

Cell culture and treatment

The BV-2 cell line was purchased from AcceGen Biotechnology (Fairfield, NJ, USA). The cells were cultured using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Hyclone/Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA), supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin/streptomycin in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. Cells were pretreated with EEFE following (LPS) Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) stimulation.

Cytotoxicity assay

Cell viability was measured by performing a CCK assay using Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). BV-2 cells were seeded onto 96-well microplates at a density of 3 × 104/well, treated with various concentrations of EEFE or rutin for 24 h, and incubated with CCK-8 solution for 4 h. Distilled water was used as a control vehicle. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA). The cytotoxicity of EEFE and rutin was calculated as the cell viability rate (%) of the untreated control.

Nitric oxide (NO) assay

BV-2 cells were pretreated with EEFE or rutin for 2 h followed by treatment with LPS (1 μg/mL) for an additional 22 h and collected culture supernatant. The NO synthesis was analyzed by determining the accumulation of nitrite (NO2−) in culture supernatant using the Griess Reagent System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Equal volumes of the supernatant and sulfanilamide solution were mixed and incubated for 10 min at room temperature, followed by the addition of naphthylethylenediamine dihydrochloride solution for 5 min. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using an Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer. Nitrite concentration was determined using a standard curve generated using sodium nitrite (NaNO2).

Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) assay

Production of PGE2 in the culture supernatants was measured using Prostaglandin E2 ELISA Kit from Cayman Chemical Co. (Ann Arbor, MI, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The optical density (OD) values at 450 nm were measured, and the concentration of PGE2 was calculated based on a standard curve.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Formulation analysis

Stability of EEFE was confirmed at 4 °C at the range of administration concentrations (50–200 mg/mL) for 14 days. The rutin content was maintained within an acceptable range (90–110 %) (Supplementary table 1). Homogeneity analysis showed that rutin contents of top, middle, and bottom layers of EEFE remained within the RSD ± 5 % at 50, 100, and 200 mg/mL (Supplementary table 2). The rutin contents in all analyzed batches ranged from 92.94–101.84 % of the reference concentration, supporting the suitability of EEFE for toxicological evaluation (Table 2).

Clinical observations and body weight

No EEFE-related clinical signs were observed during the entire study period. No mortality was recorded in any of the tested groups, except for one female in the group administered 500 mg/kg/day EEFE, that died following an administration error (suspected aspiration), which was deemed unrelated to EEFE toxicity (Table 3). Body weight increased steadily throughout the study period. After 13 weeks of administration, the weight gain in female rats was 171, 173, 176, and 171 % at 0, 500, 1000, and 2000 mg/kg/day, respectively. In male rats, the corresponding increases were 239, 233, 227, and 236 %, respectively. No significant EEFE treatment-related trends were observed in any of the animals (Fig. 1a and b). Food consumption revealed no treatment-related differences in both male and female groups (Fig. 2a and b), although transient changes were noted in the male control group at 4 weeks and in female rats in the low-dose group at 2 weeks, which were considered incidental.

Hematology

The hematological parameters are presented in Table 8. In three males administered 500 mg/kg/day, red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), and platelets (PLT) were observed because of clot formation during the blood collection process. These outliers were excluded from the analysis because of sampling artifacts unrelated to EEFE administration. A decrease in basophil levels was observed in females in the group administered 200 mg/kg/day. However, the values were within the range of historical data ranges [16], [17] and no basophil decrease was observed in males. Thus, these results were not considered toxicologically relevant because they occurred infrequently and with no consistency across the sexes. Overall, hematological parameters did not show significant changes following EEFE administration in any of the experimental groups (Table 6).

Serum biochemistry

Serum biochemical analysis revealed no significant changes in either male or female rats compared to the control group (Table 7).

Necropsy findings

On the day of necropsy, animals were euthanized by exsanguination under deep anesthesia. Retention of clear fluid was observed in the uterine horns of two females in the group administered 1000 mg/kg and one female in the group administered 2000 mg/kg. These findings were considered to be within the physiological variation or individual differences specific to females, and not toxicologically relevant findings [18]. No necropsy findings with significant or dose-dependent relationships were observed in either of the sexes.

Anti-neuroinflammatory effect of EEFE in LPS-stimulated BV-2 microglia

We previously investigated the role of anti-neuroinflammatory activity for the cognitive-enhancing efficacy of EEFE using an animal model [6]. In the present study, we explored in vitro anti-neuroinflammatory effects of EEFE and its marker compound rutin using microglia cell line BV-2. LPS were used to activate inflammatory reactions. To evaluate the cytotoxicity of EEFE and rutin in BV-2 cells, the CCK assay was performed. EEFE or rutin had no significant effect on cell viability up to 100 μg/mL (99.78 % ± 4.6) or 50 μM (86.78 % ± 4.63), respectively, in BV-2 cells (Fig. 3a and b). The potential anti-inflammatory activities of EEFE and rutin were explored by assessing the levels of the two major inflammatory mediators NO and PGE2 in LPS-stimulated BV-2 cells. LPS stimulation dramatically increased production of NO and PGE2. EEFE significantly reduced LPS-generated NO level in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4a). EEFE significantly inhibited LPS-stimulated PGE2 production. However, dose-dependent inhibition by EEFE was not observed for PGE2 (Fig. 4b). Discussion

As of December 2024, the elderly population aged ≥ 65 years in Korea reached 12 million, accounting for 20 % of the total population [19]. Recently, there has been an increasing interest not only in anti-aging but also in the concept of “slow aging,” which emphasizes prolonging a healthy lifespan and delaying the onset of age-related diseases. Traditional Korean Medicine (TKM) plays an important role in promoting health and immunity, aligning with the goals of slow aging. Especially, herbal medicines have a longstanding history and perceived safety in traditional medicine, and the scientific validation of their efficacy and safety remains a critical concern [20]. Compared with synthetic compounds, which typically consist of a single active ingredient with a defined chemical structure, herbal medicines are complex mixtures containing multiple bioactive compounds. These constituents may interact synergistically or antagonistically, leading to unpredictable pharmacological and toxicological effects [13], [21], [22]. Therefore, comprehensive toxicological evaluations are essential, particularly when considering chronic use or clinical applications for managing age-related conditions.

F. erecta Thunb. has been traditionally used to treat arthritis and rectal prolapse [23], [24] and is also listed in the Korea Food Code for functional food uses [8]. Several groups have reported the pharmacological effects of F. erecta, including anti-osteoporosis using MG-63 fibroblasts and RAW 264.7 macrophages [4], anti-inflammatory in RAW264.7 cells [25], and antioxidant effects in free radical screening assay [3]. F. erecta Thunb. leaves have been shown to improve neuroprotective action and enhance memory impairment in cognitively impaired models in ICR or C57/BL6 mice [5], [6], indicating its potential as a treatment candidate for Alzheimer’s disease or as a functional food resource for cognitive impairment. Although the candidate materials have a history of use in medicines or food, additional toxicity tests may be suggested depending on the intended use and duration of use. Recently, we reported the safety of EEFE in terms of single oral toxicity and genotoxicity. An acute single oral toxicity test showed no mortality or meaningful clinical symptoms during the 2-week observation period after EEFE administration in the range of 0–5000 mg/kg, suggesting that the ADL of EEFE is > 5000 mg/kg [7]. The genotoxicity of EEFE was assessed using standard genotoxicity tests, including bacterial reverse mutation (Ames), chromosomal aberration, and micronucleus tests. In the bacterial reverse mutation test, five bacterial strains (S. typhimurium TA 98, 100, 1535, and 1537, and E. coli WP2uvrA), with or without a metabolic activator (S9), were used. The results displayed that number of revertant colonies did not increase in a dose-dependent manner in the absence or presence of the S9 mix. In the chromosomal aberration test, chromosomal abnormalities associated with EEFE treatment were not observed with or without S9 mix. In the micronucleus test, EEFE had no effect on the frequency of bone marrow micronuclei at ≤ 2000 mg/kg body weight in mice, and no change in body weight or mortality rate was observed [10].

In general, it is recommended to use substances that improve cognitive impairment or treat dementia for at least 8 weeks in clinical trials [26], [27]. Thus, long-term administration toxicity test of EEFE is required. Therefore, in the present study, we extended our investigation by conducting a 13-week repeated oral toxicity test of EEFE. Prior to toxicity evaluation, the stability, content uniformity, and marker compound concentration of EEFE were analyzed for quality control according to regulatory guidelines, such as the OECD Test Guideline 408 and the Korean MFDS guideline for non-clinical testing of herbal preparations [13], [28]. EEFE met all quality criteria, with confirmed stability and homogeneity, and the content of rutin, a marker compound, was within acceptable limits.

In a 4-week dose-range finding study, NOAEL of EEFE was determined as 2000 mg/kg/day. Thus, for our 13-week repeated oral toxicity study, the doses of EEFE were determined to be high (2000 mg/kg), medium (1000 mg/kg), and low (500 mg/kg), and the control group was administered the vehicle. During the entire treatment period, no mortality or adverse clinical signs attributable to EEFE were observed across all groups except for one female rat that died due to a day-1 administration error. Changes in body weight and relative organ weight are major factors that determine toxicity following exposure to potentially toxic substances [29]. Normal weight gain was observed in the EEFE-administered groups as well as control groups. Changes in body weight following EEFE administration were not significant compared with those in the control group. In addition, there were no differences in dietary intake in EEFE-treated groups compared to the control group. No specific clinical symptoms were observed. Moreover, there were no statistically significant changes in relative organ weight in any of the experimental groups. At the end of the test, an autopsy was performed, and the organs of all experimental animals were visually examined to check for the presence of lesions. No toxicological abnormalities were observed in male or female rats after EEFE administration. Although some female rats showed fluid retention in the uterus, this phenomenon was not dose dependent, and no changes in body or organ weights were observed. This could appear as a normal estrous cycle reaction [18], [30] rather than toxicity caused by EEFE.

Changes in hematological and biochemical parameters are associated with toxicity caused by the intake of some plant extracts in rodent or nonrodent animals and humans [31], [32]. Hematological and biochemical parameters are crucial tools for evaluating health and used to monitor the pathological status of humans and animals [31], [33]. In the hematological analysis, the number of basophils decreased in the female group treated with 2000 mg/kg EEFE. However, the value was within the range of historical reference data in SD rats [16], [17] and was not consistent across the sexes. Other hematological parameters showed no significant differences between EEFE-treated groups and the vehicle control group. No biochemical parameters were significantly influenced by EEFE administration. Taken together, body weight, relative organ weight, food consumption, and hematological and biochemical parameters showed no toxicologically significant changes following EEFE administration.

Neuroinflammation has a considerable effect on cognitive function [34]. Microglia are macrophages that play a pivotal role in maintaining brain homeostasis. However, excessive or chronic microglial activation mediates inflammatory reactions [35]. In our previous in vivo study, we demonstrated that the cognitive-enhancing effects of EEFE were closely associated with its ability to suppress neuroinflammation [6]. Given the central role of microglial activation in neuroinflammation [35], we employed the BV-2 microglial cell line in the current study to further investigate the anti-neuroinflammatory potential of EEFE in vitro. BV-2 cells, as an immortalized microglia cell line derived from C57BL/6 mice [36], have been widely utilized as a convenient and reliable in vitro model to study neuroinflammation and screen anti-inflammatory agents targeting microglial activation [37]. Inclusion of this cellular model was intended to provide mechanistic support for the cognitive benefits observed previously [6], while complementing the subchronic toxicity evaluation by confirming that the extract exerts its pharmacological effects without cytotoxicity in a relevant central nervous system (CNS) immune cell model.

The cells were treated with LPS in the absence or presence of EEFE. LPS is a potent inflammatory stimulus that causes cognitive impairment in mice or macrophages [38], [39]. Our results showed that EEFE treatment significantly reduced the LPS-activated production of NO and PGE2, which are major inflammatory mediators [40], in BV-2 cells. Parallel assays were performed using rutin, the active compound present in EEFE. The anti-neuroinflammatory effects of rutin have been widely reported in microglia [41], [42], [43]. Consistent with the results of previous studies, our data showed that rutin treatment significantly decreased NO and PGE2 levels in LPS-stimulated BV-2 cells. These data demonstrated the in vitro anti-neuroinflammatory effects of EEFE.

It should be noted that a direct comparison between the in vivo dosing regimen and the in vitro concentrations of EEFE or rutin is limited, since plasma levels of the compounds were not determined in the rat toxicity study. Previous pharmacokinetic reports suggest that rutin and related flavonoids generally achieve plasma concentrations in the low micromolar or sub-micromolar range after oral administration, which may be lower than the concentrations typically tested in vitro. Therefore, our in vitro results should be interpreted primarily as providing mechanistic insights rather than direct reflections of systemic exposure. Furthermore, based on the quantified rutin content in EEFE (1.13 mg/g, 0.113 % w/w) [7], the rutin-equivalent concentrations in our BV-2 cell experiments were estimated to be only 0.046–0.185 µM at 25–100 µg/mL EEFE (Supplementary table 3). These levels are far below the 50 µM pure rutin condition, supporting the interpretation that the stronger anti-inflammatory effect of EEFE cannot be explained by rutin alone but likely involves additional bioactive constituents and/or synergistic interactions. It should also be noted that an “EEFE-only (no LPS)” group was not included in the present NO and PGE2 assays, which limits interpretation of basal effects of the extract. Future studies should therefore include an EEFE-only control group, perform batch-specific quantification of multiple marker compounds (e.g., rutin, kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside, and chlorogenic acid), and apply activity-guided fractionation to delineate the relative contributions of rutin and other bioactive compounds to the overall anti-neuroinflammatory activity of EEFE.

Conclusion

In a 13-week repeated oral toxicity test of EEFE in SD rats, no EEFE-related toxic effects were observed up to 2000 mg/kg/day administration in male and female rats. Clinical observations, body weight, food consumption, hematological and serum biochemical parameters, and organ weight assessments revealed no toxicologically significant changes. Based on these findings, the NOAEL of EEFE was determined to be 2000 mg/kg/day in both male and female rats. In addition, the in vitro anti-neuroinflammatory effects of EEFE and an active compound rutin were elucidated by assessing the significant reduction in NO and PGE2 in LPS-stimulated BV-2 microglia. These results provide important safety data that support the potential use of EEFE as a safe functional ingredient in the development of herbal medicines or nutraceuticals targeting cognitive functions.
