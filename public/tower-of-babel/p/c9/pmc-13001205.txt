Balancing safety and comfort in childbirth: a grounded theory of labor progress assessment among frontline obstetric healthcare providers
Authors: Li-Li Chen, Cherng-Chia Yang, Tzu-Ling Chen, Chien-Huei Kao, Meei-Ling Gau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring labor, both invasive and noninvasive assessments are performed to track progress. Invasive methods are more commonly employed in clinical settings but uncomfortable for women, whereas noninvasive assessments are less frequently used but more comfortable. This study aimed to understand the evolving processes of labor progress assessment conducted by frontline obstetricians, registered nurses, and practicing midwives during the labor process.MethodsThis study adopted a qualitative design based on Straussian grounded theory. Participants included obstetricians, registered nurses, and midwives from northern, central, and southern Taiwan. A total of 33 participants (21 healthcare workers and 12 midwives) were recruited through theoretical sampling until data saturation was reached. Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted, audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using constant comparative analysis.ResultsAnalysis revealed that labor progress assessment by frontline healthcare providers comprised six interrelated categories: “React,” “Rapport,” “Record,” “Readjust,” “Rudimentary,” and “Rebuild”, integrated by a core category, “Balancing Safety and Comfort to Achieve Holistic Health in Childbirth.” These categories illustrate how healthcare workers and midwives continuously adjust assessment strategies in response to women’s physiological and behavioral cues, clinical documentation requirements, and situational demands during labor.ConclusionLabor progress assessment practices are shaped by professional education, training philosophies, clinical experience, and underlying perspectives on childbirth held by both midwives and obstetric healthcare workers. The proposed model provides a conceptual framework that may support reflection on current assessment practices, contribute to reducing reliance on routine invasive examinations, and facilitate dialogue between midwives and obstetric professionals regarding the integration of non-invasive assessment approaches in clinical care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Assessment of labor progress, Grounded theory, Midwife, Noninvasive assessment, Obstetrician, Vaginal examination, Balancing safety, Comfort-assessment

Background

Normal labor is classified into three stages: first, second, and third stages. The first stage of labor begins with the onset of regular uterine contractions and continues until the cervix is fully dilated to 10 cm. During this stage, obstetric and midwifery personnel monitor women’s physiological changes through labor progress assessment to determine whether labor is progressing or experiencing delays. In this study, “balancing safety and comfort” refers to the integration of medical safety considerations and women’s comfort during labor assessment, while “frontline delivery room healthcare workers” include obstetricians, nurses, and midwives directly involved in intrapartum care.

This information is crucial for decision making and care planning during labor. Beginning in the 1950s, Friedman identified cervical dilation and fetal position changes over time as key parameters for assessing labor progress. He noted that while all normal labors exhibit similar patterns of cervical dilation and fetal descent, they consist of segments that vary in duration and slope [1]. Consequently, invasive cervical examinations have become the primary method of labor progress evaluation in medical institutions.

However, vaginal examination is a private and invasive procedure that many women find painful. During these examinations, women often feel exposed, vulnerable, and defenseless due to the visibility of their private parts [2]. While many clinical staff regard vaginal examination as the fastest, simplest, and most convenient method for assessing labor progress [3], they may overlook the significant discomfort and embarrassment that it can cause. In addition, women face not only the physical pain of uterine contractions but also the anxiety associated with being in a cold medical environment. Consequently, the combination of these factors places women under considerable physical and mental stress [4, 5]. Although the World Health Organization [6] recognizes the necessity of vaginal examinations, it emphasizes that the number of such examinations should be strictly limited. Excessive invasive examinations, particularly when performed after rupture of membranes or prolonged labor, can increase the risk of infection.

This recommendation is consistent with the 2018 World Health Organization intrapartum care guidelines, which emphasize respectful maternity care and the minimization of unnecessary medical interventions during physiological labor [7].

Previous research has shown that most women believe vaginal examinations are necessary and are willing to endure the associated discomfort and embarrassment for the health of themselves and their fetuses; however, the frequency of internal examinations is often excessive [5]. Similarly, the study by Dabagh-Fekri et al. revealed that over 50% of women held negative views about internal examinations, with negative evaluations positively correlating with both the number of examinations performed and the number of individuals undergoing these examinations [8].

In addition, the number of healthcare providers performing internal examinations on women is a critical issue that warrants discussion. During the labor process, women are often evaluated by multiple healthcare providers, typically being examined by nursing staff and then again by obstetricians in quick succession for confirmation of progress. The study by de Klerk et al. found that for each additional healthcare provider performing vaginal examinations, the likelihood of expectant women experiencing negative feelings increased by 58% (OR = 1.58, 95% CI = 1.2–2.1) [9].

Previous studies have shown that the use of non-invasive assessment methods during labor, such as language, behavioral changes, and ultrasound, can effectively reduce the number of vaginal exams [10, 11]. Additionally, assessing uterine contractions [12] and the presence of the purple line [13], as well as employing Dynamic External Pelvimetry [14], can help in understanding labor progress based on subjective data provided by the women. Although research has indicated that women’s spontaneous physical reflections, along with their verbal and non-verbal expressions, can effectively evaluate labor progress, vaginal examinations remain the primary method for assessing this process in clinical practice.

Taken together, existing evidence highlights a knowledge gap in the absence of integrated and holistic labor progress assessment models that combine objective clinical indicators with women’s subjective physiological and behavioral cues. Moreover, a clear practice gap persists, characterized by an overreliance on internal examinations and the limited incorporation of non-invasive assessment strategies in routine clinical care.

In Taiwan, obstetric care is predominantly grounded in a biomedically led model, and midwife-led care remains relatively limited. As a result, labor progress assessment practices are largely shaped by biomedical indicators, with less emphasis on experiential, woman-centered cues emphasized in international recommendations.

Because existing assessment frameworks do not adequately capture the real-world decision-making processes of frontline obstetricians, nurses, and midwives during labor, a qualitative approach based on grounded theory is particularly suitable for exploring how labor progress assessment is enacted in clinical practice. Therefore, this study aimed to understand the methods and experiences of frontline obstetricians, nurses, and midwives regarding clinical labor progress assessment, providing a reference for future research on interventions in clinical labor progress evaluation.

Methods

Design and setting

This study employed Straussian grounded theory to comprehensively explore the changes and perceptions in the process of labor progress assessment and its clinical applications. In-depth interviews were conducted with obstetricians and midwives during the labor process, allowing us to understand the meaning of the phenomenon from the interviewees’ perspectives, including their experiences, feelings, interactions, and how meaning is constructed within real-world clinical decision-making contexts [15, 16]. Straussian grounded theory was chosen because existing labor assessment models do not adequately capture the dynamic and context-dependent decision-making processes of frontline clinicians. This approach allows for a comprehensive understanding of the complexities involved in labor assessment practices. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Taiwan University (institutional review board approval number: 202203EM019).

Sample

This study recruited practicing midwives, nurses, and obstetricians working in diverse medical institutions across Northern, Central, and Southern Taiwan, including tertiary medical centers, regional hospitals, and local obstetric units. The inclusion criteria were as follows: obstetricians, nursing professionals (including registered nurses and nurse practitioners) and practicing midwives with more than five years of experience in delivery. The exclusion criterion was no experience in delivering or providing care to expectant women in the past year. Sampling and data collection continued until theoretical saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new categories, properties, or theoretical insights emerged from subsequent interviews. At this stage, further data collection was deemed unnecessary, and recruitment was discontinued.

Procedures

From January 2023 to May 2024, initial purposive sampling was employed to recruit eligible participants for in-depth interviews. Data collection and analysis occurred simultaneously, with emergent concepts guiding the analytical process. As analysis progressed, theoretical sampling was subsequently applied to refine and elaborate emerging categories [16, 17].

For example, when early analysis indicated that participants frequently referred to balancing assessment methods to support vaginal delivery, subsequent interviews deliberately explored how clinicians adjusted assessment strategies in response to labor progression and contextual constraints. Based on the evolving theoretical concepts, the content and focus of subsequent interviews were iteratively refined.

Semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted face to face using an open-ended interview outline (Table 1). The interview questions were developed by the research team in consultation with relevant experts. All interviews were conducted by the first author, who had received formal training in qualitative research methods. Prior to the interviews, basic information about participants was collected. The interviews took place in a quiet and confidential setting, lasting approximately 30 to 50 min. The interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese, which was the participants’ native language, and no translation was required. Prior to the interviews, the interviewer explained the study purpose, procedures, and participants’ rights, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. With participants’ consent, each interview was audio-recorded, and the interviewer also took notes to capture key points.

Data analysis

Data analysis was carried out independently by two researchers without the use of qualitative analysis software and began immediately after the first interview. This study adopted a Straussian grounded theory approach, following the analytic procedures proposed by Strauss and Corbin [16], and the constant comparative method was applied throughout all stages of analysis, whereby data were continuously compared across interviews, codes, and categories.

The data analysis proceeded through several stages. First, during open coding, microanalysis (line-by-line coding) was conducted to identify and label emerging concepts, which were then grouped into preliminary categories and subcategories. Next, in axial coding, relationships between categories and subcategories were examined by comparing conditions, actions/interactions, and consequences, allowing for analytic integration of the data. In the selective coding phase, categories were further refined and integrated around a central explanatory framework.

Throughout the analytic process, researchers engaged in memo writing to document analytic decisions, emerging hypotheses, and theoretical insights. Conceptual diagrams were developed iteratively to visualize relationships among categories and to support theory construction.

Reflexivity was maintained through ongoing team discussions, in which researchers critically reflected on how their professional backgrounds and clinical experiences might influence data interpretation. Continuous comparison with existing theory was used to enhance analytic rigor and theoretical depth [17].

The research team consisted of an obstetrician (C.C.Y.), nursing and midwifery experts (T.L.C. & M.L.G.), and qualitative research specialists (L.L.C. & C.H.K.). Team triangulation was achieved through regular analytic meetings, during which emerging codes, categories, and theoretical interpretations were discussed and critically examined from multiple disciplinary perspectives.

Each transcript was independently reviewed and cross-checked by at least two researchers, and discrepancies in coding were resolved through consensus discussion. To ensure high-quality data analysis, the research team remained vigilant against bias and employed both empirical and conceptual strategies to confirm the coherence and completeness of the findings [18, 19].

During analysis, a shared goal among healthcare workers and midwives across different professional backgrounds was identified: promoting vaginal delivery. Axial and selective coding were subsequently employed to refine themes and develop the theoretical model. Interviews continued until theoretical saturation was confirmed, and a total of 33 participants were interviewed to validate the categories and the final model.

Trustworthiness of findings

The interviews were conducted by the first author, who had received formal qualitative research training and had prior experience in qualitative studies. During data collection, the researcher audio-recorded interviews, wrote analytic memos, and observed participants’ facial expressions and body language, enabling methodological triangulation and enhancing dependability. Nursing experts and team members participated in the analytic process to establish credibility and ensure that findings reflected participants’ perspectives. All interviews were audio-recorded to minimize recall bias [20, 21]. The study followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) to ensure transparency and rigor in qualitative reporting.

Results

Participants

From January 2023 to May 2024, a total of 33 participants were recruited for this study. In the healthcare component, the study involved 21 obstetricians and nurses with an average of 19 years of obstetrics experience. In terms of educational attainment, the majority (12 people) had a bachelor’s degree. The gender distribution was eight males and 13 females. Regarding hospital distribution, five participants were from medical centers, 15 were from regional hospitals, and one was from a clinic. For midwives, the average tenure was 26 years, with the majority (n = 7) holding a master’s degree. Ten of them worked in midwifery clinics, and two others worked as midwives in a medical center and a clinic (Table 2).

Grounded theory model

Following data analysis, this study identified that the labor progress assessment by frontline healthcare providers encompassed six categories: “React,” “Rapport,” “Record,” “Readjust,” “Rudimentary,” and “Rebuild,” along with a core category of “Balancing Safety and Comfort to Achieve Holistic Health in Childbirth.” These six categories influenced one another throughout the adaptive process. Given that labor is inherently unpredictable, participants engaged in iterative adjustments in response to the observations and insights derived from labor assessment data. This ongoing adaptation reflects the back-and-forth adjustments necessary to accommodate the unique progression of each labor experience. The findings indicated that midwives and healthcare workers with varying educational backgrounds tended to employ different labor assessment methods. Frontline healthcare workers primarily focused on parturients’ spontaneous responses, while midwives prioritized providing care and support to help parturients adapt to the labor process. Conversely, once healthcare workers recognized the parturient’s spontaneous reactions, they required data to facilitate handovers or confirm the status of subsequent medications, which led to an emphasis on data-driven decision making in clinical practice. Through in-depth and repeated comparisons, it became evident that their shared goal was to promote vaginal delivery. A holistic model for balancing safety and comfort in childbirth. Presents the grounded theory model of labor progress assessment developed in this study. The model illustrates six interrelated categories—React, Rapport, Record, Readjust, Rudimentary, and Rebuild—that operate in a dynamic and cyclical manner rather than as a linear sequence. Frontline healthcare workers and midwives move iteratively between these categories in response to women’s physiological cues, clinical observations, and situational demands during labor. The core category, “Balancing Safety and Comfort to Achieve Holistic Health in Childbirth,” emerged as the central integrative process linking all six categories. It represents how clinical decision making during labor involves the continuous weighing of medical safety requirements against women’s comfort, experiential needs, and adaptability throughout the childbirth process

React

During the labor process, the parturient can indicate the progression of labor based on her own perceptions and bodily changes. Healthcare and midwifery personnel described in interviews that when the parturient exhibited verbal or non-verbal changes, these served as indicators of shifts in the labor process.

If an expectant mother shows signs that she is about to give birth, we can often tell from her appearance. For example, she becomes extremely quiet and may emit sounds, such as “ah” or involuntary roars, which coincide with uterine contractions. During these contractions, she vocalizes to express the intensity of the contractions and the feeling of pressure (P22).

The assessment of labor progress primarily involves internal examinations, listening to the expectant mother’s vocalizations, and observing her clinical presentation. This includes evaluating her auditory and olfactory responses, facial expressions, physical movements, and sounds to gauge labor progression (P24).

The purple line appears in the upper middle area of the buttocks, where the gluteal muscles converge. As labor progresses and the fetus descends, this line becomes increasingly triangular and darkens in color. At times, it may appear brown, while other times it can be a deep or dark purple. Additionally, the amount of bloody show is significant; as labor advances, an increase in the quantity of bloody show is evident, and the color may intensify to a brighter red (P25).

The critical factors to assess include the location of the fetal heartbeat, the appearance of the expectant mother’s vagina, and the presence of hemorrhoids, which are swollen veins in the anal region. For example, if we arrive in the ward and the cervix is dilated to 9 or 10 centimeters, and the Dynamic External Pelvimetry indicator is visible, we then evaluate the condition of the vagina (P26).

We assess the intensity of the expectant mother’s contractions and inquire about her perceptions regarding their strength. We often ask her, “Do you feel the uterine contractions more in the front or the back?” Many women report experiencing soreness in their back, which can occur if the fetal position is not optimal. In such cases, the labor process may take longer. Additionally, we observe her facial expressions and listen to her vocalizations. If her voice remains relatively high-pitched during contractions, it indicates that labor has not progressed sufficiently, and we may choose not to intervene aggressively at that stage (P28).

Rapport

The most effective method of labor assessment for midwives is to establish a familiar and trusting relationship with the expectant mother while providing continuous support throughout the entire process. This approach enables midwives to understand clearly whether changes in the labor process are reflected in the expectant mother’s language and behavior, corresponding to the progression of labor.

We continuously evaluate and observe the parturient’s performance throughout the labor process. Because we are almost always present with the expectant mother, we remain attentive to her condition. For instance, during a certain period, she may need to rest and can lie down with our assistance, possibly even sleeping briefly. During this time, I might assess that the labor process is not actively progressing toward childbirth. It tends to be a gradual change; while she is resting, her condition evolves slowly, but it has not yet progressed to the point of imminent delivery (P22).

Midwives are fully capable and responsible, accompanying expectant mothers throughout the entire process. This allows me to have a clear understanding of an expectant mother’s progress, which differs from assessments conducted in medical institutions (P31).

During this period of labor, we strive to accompany and support the expectant mother by understanding her condition and interacting with her closely. Through this engagement, we can visually estimate the level of cervical dilation based on our observations (P32).

I believe that the labor process closely follows the parturient’s uterine contractions. For example, if the parturient’s uterine contractions are initially weak, irregular, and highly variable, our assessment as midwives involves paying attention gently and gradually while remaining relaxed. However, as the contractions become stronger and more frequent, our focus also intensifies, following the rhythm of the contractions until delivery. It feels as though we are giving birth together; it truly feels like we are bringing a baby into the world alongside the mother (P25).

Record

Healthcare workers follow a shift system that emphasizes the importance of data, evidence, and facts. In their labor assessment methods, they utilize equipment, such as uterine contraction monitors and fetal monitors, to record labor progress. Additionally, healthcare workers must maintain written records to facilitate smooth shift handovers, ensuring that all three shifts have a consistent understanding of the parturient’s condition. The recorded data underscores the importance of effective handovers and the presentation of numerical information in clinical settings.

The progress of labor is generally assessed by the degree of cervical dilation. However, even in the absence of internal examinations, we can observe changes, such as increased regularity and frequency of uterine contractions. Additionally, we may integrate information from fetal heart rate monitoring. For example, if we observe a deceleration in the fetal heart rate during contractions, it can also serve as an evaluation criterion for assessing labor progression (P7).

We need precise data for our records, whether it’s for the nurse’s documentation of labor progress or our medical records. As a result, we often find it challenging to include subjective observations as part of the official records (21).

During an internal examination, we assess the cervical dilation in centimeters. Next, we determine the fetal position and station, noting whether it is positive or negative, and the relative height. We include these details in our internal examination report before handing it over to the next shift. This information ensures that others understand the current status regarding the internal examination, allowing them to monitor the case closely and decide when to reassess (11).

The numbers provide important information about the patient’s labor status and safety at that moment. During the shift handover, nursing staff must be aware of the current progress of the patient’s labor. While we wouldn’t describe the frequency of internal examinations as very intensive, there is typically one examination conducted before and one after the shift change. It would be impractical to rely on internal exam data from eight hours prior when transitioning between shifts (17).

In fact, many expectant mothers visit the hospital for check-ups, eager to understand the current changes in their cervix and the safety of their babies. They often ask, “How many centimeters is my cervical dilation?” We typically perform internal examinations to assess and explain cervical dilation, thickness, and the descent of the fetal head. This information provides reassurance to the expectant mother (14).

Internal examination is generally considered the gold standard for assessing labor progress. Although using a speculum can offer some information, it is not as accurate as digital examination techniques using fingers (15).

Readjust

Midwives indicated that when they accompany parturients throughout the entire labor process, they closely observe the parturient’s spontaneous reactions. Vaginal examinations are conducted when necessary or at the onset of labor. However, the focus of these examinations is not on numerical data but rather on interpreting the parturient’s spontaneous responses to understand the relative positions of the perineal muscles, cervix, and fetus. Such assessments may not be needed unless the labor process deviates from expected progress, allowing for an overall readjustment during labor.

When the first stage of labor begins, we initially assess the parturient’s vital signs, followed by an internal examination, although this may not always be necessary. In addition, we observe the parturient’s visual cues, such as appearance and facial expressions, and listen to her vocalizations (P26).

Non-invasive assessments can sometimes lack the practical experience needed for accurate labor evaluation. However, when necessary, internal examinations provide important information about the positions of the anterior and posterior fontanels relative to the pelvis and can also assess which side of the pelvic floor muscles is tighter or looser. During childbirth, it is essential for these muscles to be relaxed, as tension, especially when the parturient is nervous, can increase the risk of lacerations. In an internal examination, I guide the parturient to perform exercises that engage and relax the pelvic floor muscles, encouraging her to release tension. I notice that when I prompt her to relax, she noticeably becomes more relaxed as her pelvic floor muscles loosen (P24).

Unless the labor process has already prolonged, I will conduct another internal examination to assess what type of assistance may facilitate progress. The internal examination serves as an evaluative tool, acting like a ruler in my mind that helps determine the next steps and necessary adjustments based on the current fetal position (P33).

Rudimentary

Midwives utilize internal examinations alongside the parturient’s spontaneous reactions to assess the state of the labor process. Healthcare workers recognize that observing the parturient’s spontaneous reactions to labor progression is a crucial evaluation method. However, this subjective technique necessitates considerable experience, which many healthcare workers may lack. As a result, their ability to use this approach effectively can be limited.

Sometimes, less experienced staff may misjudge situations due to their limited experience. Frankly, understanding a patient’s condition often depends on interpreting indirect signs, such as facial expressions, which require a considerable amount of experience (P20).

Training and experience require time, especially when building knowledge from foundational principles based on experience. For example, using observational methods to assess non-stress tests (NST) and bloody show is crucial. Once these skills are mastered, the frequency of internal examinations can be significantly reduced. However, it is essential to recognize that achieving this level of proficiency requires substantial time and practice (P5).

When the nursing staff hands over the shift, if they only describe the status of the case without including the numeric data from internal examinations, does that make sense? For example, during a shift handover, they might state, “This patient has made no progress so far.” However, this lack of progress could mean that the cervix has not dilated at all or that it has dilated very slowly and has not yet entered the active phase. Without this numeric data, we are unable to obtain the objective information needed to make an accurate assessment (P18).

Rebuild

Previously, midwives relied on the parturient’s reactions and the frequency of uterine contractions to assist in the delivery process. With the introduction of Western medicine, internal examination data became essential for assessing labor progress, such as identifying regular contractions and full cervical dilation to 10 cm. Therefore, integrating the parturient’s spontaneous reactions with clinical evidence on labor progress is crucial for establishing a more comprehensive evaluation framework. Once this integration is rebuilt, an assessment scale can be developed that combines internal examination findings with the parturient’s spontaneous reactions, facilitating effective handovers between shifts in clinical practice. This approach can also help reduce the frequency of internal examinations.

Each parturient has a unique pain tolerance level. If the goal is to develop a tool to assist with clinical judgment, it is crucial to establish a more precise definition of pain levels. For instance, defining what specific types of pain correspond to particular pain ratings would enhance communication among healthcare professionals (P13).

Is it possible to measure some of the twisting and gripping movements of the parturient’s body using specific scales? Additionally, I believe that eye expressions are very important. When the parturient begins to feel pain, she often closes her eyes and stops looking at her surroundings. She becomes disinterested in both her environment and the people present. These observable signs and scales may help reduce the frequency of internal examinations, as they could serve as indicators of labor progress (P19).

Discussion

This study explored the labor assessment process and its implications as utilized by frontline healthcare workers. The results identified six categories: “React,” “Rapport,” “Record,” “Readjust,” “Rudimentary,” and “Rebuild”, along with a core category, ”Balancing Safety and Comfort to Achieve Holistic Health in Childbirth.”

The model primarily provides a comprehensive perspective on the roles of frontline personnel who care for parturients during the labor process, encompassing the viewpoints of both healthcare professionals and midwives. In labor progress assessment, each professional brings unique focuses and educational backgrounds. Traditionally, medical models divide the labor process into stages based on mechanical or physical changes, such as cervical dilation and fetal head descent, as depicted in Friedman’s curve or the WHO Labor Care Guidelines [22]. Abalos et al. conducted a systematic literature review highlighting that the duration of normal labor can vary significantly among women [23]. While some women’s labor may exceed expected durations, they can still result in smooth deliveries without adverse outcomes. Consequently, the time limits defined for the first stage of labor should not be strictly enforced. Furthermore, women’s subjective emotional expressions during childbirth may not fully align with mechanical descriptions of bodily changes [24].

Numerous studies have demonstrated that various labor assessment methods can aid midwives and healthcare workers in understanding the progress of labor [10, 12–14]. Women exhibit specific responses to uterine contractions during labor and delivery, which include facial expressions, verbal expressions, tone of voice, body movements, and breathing patterns [25–27].

Based on the information summarized in this study, the clinical use of women’s reactions to pain, along with fetal monitors, ultrasound, and other instruments, has shown effectiveness in supporting non-invasive labor assessment. Historically, there has been continuous advocacy for women to have autonomy in their birth experience [28]. In labor evaluation, there is a need to combine both non-invasive and invasive assessment methods. Furthermore, invasive labor assessments (such as routine vaginal examinations during labor in medical institutions) have been reported in numerous studies to increase women’s stress, pain, and negative labor experiences [4, 9].

From a medical perspective, invasive labor assessments, such as internal examinations, are considered valuable tools for gaining insight into the overall progression of labor [29]. However, these assessments require skilled physical evaluation and experience to translate effectively the information gathered into meaningful clinical decisions [30]. Traditionally, medically centered education in obstetrics has prioritized safety in childbirth, focusing on prevention, management, and risk control. Consequently, subjective data provided by pregnant women and parturients during labor have often been overlooked as important information [31]. Clinical decision making is crucial in frontline obstetric care, as it not only involves conveying accurate information about the labor course to caregivers and parturients but also necessitates considering women’s thoughts, experiences, and evidence of effectiveness in assessing labor progress [32].

During the midwife-led care process, invasive internal examinations may be performed at specific times, such as the onset of labor; during comprehensive assessments of the cervix, fetal position, and pelvis; upon the request of parturients; or in cases of abnormal labor progression (e.g., bleeding or prolonged labor stagnation). Most importantly, midwives prioritize respecting the parturient’s wishes before conducting internal examinations. Therefore, while these examinations are integral to assessing labor progress, they should be performed with a priority on the parturient’s benefits and interests [33].

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Pan et al. further indicated that, beyond parturients’ self-reports, non-invasive labor assessment methods such as the use of the purple line and ultrasound demonstrate good clinical accuracy [34, 35]. In previous research, Choe et al. conducted an in utero analysis of fetal heart sound deceleration. Early deceleration can be detected through fetal heart sound monitoring, which aids in assessing labor progression [36]. Other physiological indicators, including the purple line [37–39] and dynamic external pelvimetry [40, 41], have also been reported to enhance the accuracy of non-invasive labor assessments.

Finally, in the past decade, numerous scholars, organizations, and associations have committed to research aimed at reducing unnecessary medical interventions [7, 42]. Researchers have advocated for obstetric professionals to adopt a holistic assessment approach to evaluate labor progression, which involves monitoring women’s physiological and behavioral responses during labor. Many developed countries have acknowledged the importance and appropriateness of these assessment methods [43]. Darling et al. conducted a systematic review of literature to explore the facilitators of and barriers to the implementation of a physiological approach to care during labor and birth in obstetric settings. The research concluded that their care philosophy includes “birth as inherently physiological” and “birth as inherently risky,” thus categorizing care methods into “watch and wait” and “control and risk surveillance.” Primary research has mainly identified the barriers to implementing a physiological approach at a professional level and identified the facilitators to include midwives and obstetricians in their collaboration [44].

Taken together, this study contributes a grounded theory model that clarifies how labor progress assessment is enacted in real-world clinical practice through the continuous balancing of biomedical safety and woman-centered comfort. By conceptualizing “Balancing Safety and Comfort” as the core category, the model demonstrates how frontline healthcare workers and midwives dynamically integrate non-invasive assessments, women’s subjective cues, and selective medical evaluations, rather than privileging a single assessment approach. In the context of the global call to reduce the overmedicalization of childbirth, this model provides a pragmatic middle ground between medically oriented risk management and midwifery-led supportive care, highlighting that safety and comfort are not competing goals but are continually weighed and reconciled throughout the labor process.

Limitations

Data collection for this study was based on interviews with frontline healthcare personnel regarding their experiences in caring for low-risk cases. Consequently, the findings cannot be generalized to the experiences and contexts of labor progress assessment in high-risk pregnancies. Future research should aim to more extensively explore the methods utilized by frontline healthcare personnel to assess labor progress across various case types.

Implications

This study aimed to understand the perspectives of healthcare professionals with varying educational backgrounds regarding labor assessment while also anticipating a new clinical perspective through model development. Additionally, it is hoped that the findings from this study can contribute to the creation of an integrated labor assessment scale in the future, ultimately enhancing the quality of maternal care during labor and making the clinical assessment model more structured.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of how frontline obstetric healthcare workers and midwives assess labor progress in clinical practice and proposes a six-category grounded theory model that captures this dynamic process. The novelty of this model lies in its explicit integration of women’s subjective experiences with clinical data, highlighting how labor assessment is guided by the continuous balancing of safety and comfort rather than reliance on a single evaluative approach. By offering actionable pathways for integrating non-invasive assessments with selective medical evaluations, the model has the potential to guide reflective clinical practice, reduce unnecessary internal examinations, and support holistic childbirth care. Future research is warranted to validate this model across diverse clinical settings and to explore its incorporation into institutional guidelines and maternity care policies.
