Effect of safe water delivery plus water and sanitation hygiene behavior change communication on diarrheal disease prevalence among children under 5 years in a slum setting in Nairobi, Kenya: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Jonathan Izudi, Evans Muchiri, Dennis Musyoka, Sheillah Simiyu, Innocent K Tumwebaze, Nelson Mbaya, Estelle Sidze, Kelvin Musau, Amanuel Abajobir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiarrheal diseases are prevalent among children under five years of age in slum areas. We evaluated the effect of safe water delivery plus water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH)-Behavior Change Communication (WASH-BCC on diarrheal disease prevalence among children under 5 years in a slum setting in Nairobi, Kenya.MethodsWe designed a quasi-experimental study, with the intervention as safe water delivery plus WASH-BCC. The intervention arm consisted of 2 slum villages that received safe water plus WASH-BCC, while the comparison arm comprised 2 slum villages that did not receive the intervention. The primary outcome was diarrheal disease in a child under five years of age in the past 2 weeks in both study arms, defined as the passage of ≥ 3 watery stools in the past 24 h. Data were collected at the baseline and endline from a random sample of eligible households in the villages. Propensity score weighting was used to achieve similarity in measured covariates between both arms. Binary logistic regression analysis, adjusting for propensity-score weights, was utilized to estimate the causal effect of the intervention, reported as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI).ResultsWe analyzed data from 1,876 participants in a 1:1 ratio and found 382 (20.4%) children under five years of age had diarrheal disease at the endline. Diarrheal disease prevalence declined in the intervention villages (33.3% baseline vs. 23.5% endline) but increased in the comparison villages (15.7% baseline vs. 17.5% endline). We found a 31% decline in diarrheal disease in the intervention villages relative to the comparison villages (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.55–0.86). In a difference-in-differences analysis, the decline was 11.4%. The findings remained robust in a sensitivity analysis.ConclusionThe intervention significantly reduced diarrheal disease prevalence in children under five years of age in the slum setting. Therefore, there is a need to expand the intervention to the remaining areas to reduce diarrheal disease morbidity and mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24951-y.Keywords: Access to safe water, Behavior change communication, Diarrheal disease, Slum, Kenya

Background

Diarrheal diseases are one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in children under five years of age, with nearly 500,000 deaths globally [1]. In Kenya, approximately 41 deaths per 1000 live births are in children under five years of age [2], and the national prevalence of diarrheal disease is 14% based on the 2022 Kenya Demographic and Health Survey [3]. Most of the diarrheal disease burden is attributed to inadequate Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH), with estimates showing that diarrheal disease accounts for up to 55 million Disability Adjusted Life Years [4]. The 2022 estimates suggest that 63% of Kenyans have access to basic sources of drinking water, 37% have access to basic sanitation, and 38% have access to basic hygiene facilities [5]. While urban areas generally have slightly better access to WASH services compared to rural areas, inequalities in access to water exist, with informal settlements being the most affected [6]. Previous data show that slums in Nairobi have the highest prevalence of diarrheal diseases, ranging from 18.7% to 25.6% in children under five years of age [7, 8]. One potential reason for the high prevalence of diarrheal disease in such settlements is inadequate WASH services. To improve health outcomes among children under five years of age, evidence suggests that population-based interventions such as improved access to safe drinking water, together with sanitation and hygiene practices, are effective in reducing the prevalence of diarrheal disease [9, 10].

Kibera slum, located in Nairobi, Kenya, is the largest slum in Africa and has limited access to safe drinking water and sanitation, placing the population at risk for several waterborne diseases [11, 12]. Children under five years of age in this setting are at a higher risk for diarrheal disease due to a lack of access to safe drinking water and inadequate sanitation and hygiene services. To reverse the water crisis, prevalent diarrheal and other waterborne diseases among children under five years of age in this setting, Shining Hope for Communities (SHOFCO)—a grassroots national organization in Nairobi providing support to community advocacy platforms, leadership development, health, and education, with a focus on women and girls—aimed to improve access to safe WASH services and WASH-Behavior Change Communication (WASH-BCC) through a 2-year pilot phase intervention known as the Kibera Investment for Delivery of Safe Water (KIDS). Through the KIDS project, SHOFCO increased access to safe drinking water at affordable prices at the household, school, and healthcare facility levels in collaboration with local and national stakeholders, including scaling up WASH-BCC to improve health outcomes among children under five years of age.

Whereas improving access to WASH services combined with BCC has the potential to improve child health outcomes, the impact of the KIDS intervention on diarrheal disease prevalence has not been rigorously evaluated. We, therefore, evaluated the effect of safe water delivery plus WASH-BCC on the prevalence of diarrheal disease in children under five years of age in Kibera slum following 9 months of active intervention implementation.

Materials and methods

Study setting

This evaluation was conducted in Kibera, Nairobi, Kenya. Kibera is the largest slum (informal settlement) in Africa [13]. Population estimates for Kibera vary widely, ranging from approximately 170,000 based on the 2009 census to over 1 million according to some recent estimates by local organizations and the United Nations Human Settlements Programme (UN-Habitat). Due to the informal nature of the settlement, rapid population changes, and data collection challenges, the true population size remains contested and is likely unknown. The intervention arm included 2 villages (Kisumu Ndogo and Makina) in the Kibera slum, and the comparison arm had 2 villages (Gatope and Feed the Children) in the Mukuru slum. Both settings have similar population densities, and the residents have comparable socio-economic conditions—water shortage, unemployment, and inadequate sanitary facilities [14]. The majority of the residents in both settings collect water at public water points, costing around $0.15 or 20 Kenyan Shillings/L [6]. Spaghetti piping (a tangled, disorganized network of water pipes) that passes through muck and sewage drains is typical of the water infrastructure in these slums. This piping is vulnerable to vandalism from unauthorized/illegal connections and contamination.

Study design

We employed a quasi-experimental design, with the intervention arm as the 2 villages (Kisumu Ndogo and Makina) in the Kibera slum, and the comparison arm as the 2 villages (Gatope and Feed the Children) in the Mukuru slum. This design was appropriate as it was unethical and impractical to randomize WASH services to the villages, as their benefits are well-established. To avoid cross-contamination between the study arms, the comparison arm was distant (nearly 7 km) from the intervention arm, but the socio-demographic and epidemiological profiles were similar. No clear legal boundaries exist within the intervention arm, so the 2 villages were defined by SHOFCO—the implementing partner—for practicality reasons.

Participants

With the help of Community Health Promoters (CHPs) in the target villages, we conducted household listing in both the intervention and comparison arms. We listed 1,630 households in the intervention arm and another 1,203 households in the comparison arm. Within these arms, a simple random sampling was applied to select eligible households for inclusion in the evaluation study. First, all households were listed to form the sampling frame, followed by simple random sampling using a computer-generated list of random numbers, ensuring all eligible households had an equal chance of selection. Eligible households had ≥ 1 child under five years of age and ≥ 2 weeks of stay in the village. In households with ≥ 2 eligible children, data on the diarrheal prevalence were collected for the youngest child—each household contributed data for a single child. If the households had a visitor with a new arrival of an eligible child, the visitor/child should have stayed in the village for ≥ 2 weeks. We excluded households with a child under five years of age who had severe or chronic illnesses, necessitating immediate medical care. We deemed that interviewing such mothers or caregivers would delay treatment, hence placing the child at risk for mortality. We enrolled more households in the intervention arm at baseline to allow for a potential shift to a single-arm, pre–post study design (without a comparison group) if the same intervention were later implemented in the comparison arm.

Intervention and study arms

The intervention was access to safe drinking water combined with WASH-BCC under the KIDS project. The WASH BCC component comprised context-specific content developed and approved by the Ministry of Health, Public Health Experts, and the Ministry of Water. This included sensitization on safe water handling, hygiene, and sanitation, including sensitization delivered through community meetings, household visits, and demonstrations. The component also involved monthly WASH promotion sessions at community gathering points such as markets and meetings, and training of community members (Water Kiosk Attendants) on water management. Posters promoting handwashing with soap were displayed in public toilets, and soap was made available. The intervention arm received both safe drinking water and WASH-BCC, while the comparison arm remained as usual (did not receive the intervention).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was diarrheal disease in the past 2 weeks reported by the child’s mother or caregiver. We defined diarrheal disease as the passage of 3 or more loose stools in the past 24 h by a child under five years of age in a selected household in the intervention and comparison study arms. For this outcome, the numerator was the number of children under five years of age reported to have experienced diarrheal disease in the past 2 weeks, while the denominator was the sample size, expressed as a percentage. The child’s mother or caregiver was asked to report whether the child had experienced diarrheal disease in the past 2 weeks. The covariates included: (1) child factors—sex, age in months, and whether the child was exclusively breastfed; and (2) respondent factors—age in years, level of education, presence of income-generating activity, employment, and wealth quintile based on household asset index.

Community engagement for the evaluation

To facilitate seamless community entry, community engagement, and inception meetings were held on November 30, 2022, in the intervention arm and on December 2, 2022, in the comparison arm. During the endline data collection, similar engagements were conducted around August 22–23, 2023, in both study arms. These meetings enabled information sharing about the evaluation process and design with the residents, community leaders, senior administration officials, and government health officials. We pre-tested the data collection tools following the community engagement meetings at 2 sites (Soweto West for the intervention arm, and Simba Cool for the comparison arm). The pre-testing sites did not receive the intervention. Following the pre-testing of the tools, responses and comments were reviewed and addressed, and a final questionnaire was developed.

Baseline and endline data collection

We conducted 2 rounds of data collection using a research assistant-administered questionnaire among randomly sampled households in the intervention and comparison arms—at the baseline around November and December 2022, and endline around October and November 2023 (approximately 10–11 months later) in both the intervention and comparison arms. Therefore, the lag period was adequate to capture changes attributable to the intervention. Notably, most diarrhoeal diseases have an incubation period ranging from a few hours up to 7 days, which is substantially shorter than the interval between data collection points.

We collected data on diarrheal disease among children under five years of age in the past 2 weeks, WASH services, BCC, household demographics, residency status, housing conditions, and socio-economic status as derived from household assets. WASH services-related factors included the primary sources of drinking water, approaches to water treatment, latrine utilization, and awareness and adherence to standard WASH practices. Statistical issues

Sample size Estimation

We hypothesized that the KIDS project would reduce the prevalence of diarrheal disease among children under five years of age due to improved access to safe drinking water and WASH-BCC. We estimated a sample size that was sufficient for the evaluation to have good statistical power to detect a small difference in the outcome resulting from the intervention. We used the sample size estimation approach for two proportions. Existing data show an 18.7% prevalence of diarrheal disease among children under five years of age in the Kibera slum [7]. To detect a 5% effect size (absolute difference in the prevalence of diarrheal disease with and without intervention), we estimated that 1,876 participants in a 1:1 ratio were needed at 80% statistical power and a 5% statistical significance level for a two-sided chi-square test, adjusting for a 5% non-response rate. The sample size was, therefore, not powered to detect a difference between two samples from the same population at different time points (i.e., before and after the intervention), but rather for the post-intervention comparison between study arms.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed in R version 4.3.0 (2023-04-21 ucrt). Frequencies and percentages were computed for categorical variables. Mean and standard deviation were computed for numerical data. We applied Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to create socioeconomic indices based on items owned by households—a bicycle, refrigerators, televisions, and radios, as in a previous study [15]. Using PCA, we reduced the number of items owned by the households into a simple score that created wealth quintiles, categorized from the lowest to the highest. We stratified the covariates by the study arm (intervention vs. comparison) and assessed differences through the Chi-square test for categorical variables and Student’s t-test for numerical variables. To estimate the effect of the intervention on the outcome, propensity score weighting, a statistical technique for creating a comparison arm identical to the intervention arm, was applied [16].

First, we used a logit model to fit the intervention as a function of the baseline covariates and used the resulting coefficients to predict the propensity scores—the probability of receiving the intervention conditional on the covariates. As reported in previous studies [17, 18], we weighted the intervention arm using the inverse of the propensity score (1/propensity score) and the comparison arm as 1/1-propensity score, creating a pseudo-population where all observed characteristics were balanced, thus mimicking a randomized controlled trial [19].

We assessed covariate balance using absolute standardized mean difference (SMD), with an SMD < 0.1 considered suggestive of a covariate balance [20]. SMD was preferred over p-values because it measures the magnitude of covariate imbalance and, unlike p-values, is not influenced by sample size [21, 22]. Furthermore, we explored covariate balance in a propensity score mirror histogram, with distributional similarity in propensity scores between the intervention and comparison arms taken to imply good covariate balance. Following successful covariate balance (SMD < 0.1), we assessed the effect of the intervention on the outcome by fitting a logistic regression as a function of the intervention adjusted for propensity-score weights, expressed as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). The statistical code is provided in Additional File 2.

Sub-group analysis

We anticipated that the effect of the intervention on the prevalence of diarrheal disease would vary by age. Therefore, we performed a sub-group analysis based on the child’s age: <2 years vs. 2 years and beyond.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the causal estimates, we performed several sensitivity analyses. The Rosenbaum Wilcoxon-rank sum test was performed to assess the robustness of the estimate to unmeasured confounders [20], with distant gamma values considered as suggestive of robust findings to unmeasured confounders. We checked the robustness of the propensity score model specification via a propensity score model specification test, with the null hypothesis stating the model specification was correct [23]. We compared outcome trends using Difference-in-Differences (DiD) analysis, computing absolute percentage difference by comparing changes between the endline and baseline across the intervention and comparison arms.

Ethical considerations

We obtained ethical approval from the APHRC Internal Review Board (reference number: DOR/2022/057) and the African Medical and Research Foundation Research Ethics Committee (reference number: P1302-2022), and a research permit from the National Commission of Science, Technology and Innovation (NACOSTI reference number: NACOSTI/P/22/22045). All participants gave informed consent, either in writing or by thumbprint, after receiving information on the purpose of the evaluation study, voluntary participation, possible risks and benefits, privacy and confidentiality of information, and dissemination of the findings. All Evaluation Team members received training in research ethics to ensure the protection of the safety and welfare of participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, which emphasizes the ethical principles for human subject research, namely beneficence, nonmaleficence, autonomy, and justice.

Results

Study profile

At baseline, 2,860 households were listed (1,630 intervention, 1,230 comparison). Of these, 463 were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria (155 intervention, 308 comparison), leaving 1,475 in the intervention arm and 1,005 in the comparison arm. After loss to follow-up due to relocation (15 intervention, 10 comparison), the sampling frame comprised 1,460 intervention and 995 comparison households, from which 873 in each arm were randomly selected and analyzed. At endline, 2,160 households were listed (1,052 intervention, 1,108 comparison), and 225 were excluded for ineligibility (95 intervention, 130 comparison), leaving 922 in the intervention arm and 913 in the comparison arm. Following loss to follow-up (0 intervention, 7 comparison), the sampling frame comprised 922 intervention and 906 comparison households, from which 873 in each arm were again randomly selected and analyzed (Fig. 1).

Effect of the intervention on diarrheal disease in children under five years of age

Overall, 382 (20.4%) diarrheal disease cases were reported in the past 2 weeks at the endline: 23.5% in the intervention arm vs. 17.3% in the comparison arm (Table 3). Considering the baseline prevalence of diarrheal disease (33.3% in the intervention arm vs. 15.7% in the comparison arm), a 28.2%% drop was observed in the intervention arm while a 12.7% increase occurred in the comparison arm (risk difference, −11.4% percentage points). The intervention arm experienced a 31% reduction in the prevalence of diarrheal disease among children under five years of age relative to the comparison arm (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.55–0.86).

Sensitivity analyses findings

We found that the causal effect estimate was robust, as both DiD and propensity-score analysis results show the same direction of effect. We failed to reject the null hypothesis (Ho) that stated the propensity-score model was correctly specified based on the propensity-score model specification test (t-test = 5.16, df = 8, p = 0.740), suggesting correct model specification. We found the causal estimate was robust to unmeasured confounders as shown by a large shift in gamma value before attaining statistical significance (Gamma = 2.5, p = 0.003).

Discussion

In children under five years of age in Kibera slum, the largest slum in Africa, improving access to safe and clean water and WASH-BCC has resulted in a significant reduction in diarrheal disease prevalence. The reduction in the prevalence of diarrheal disease is consistent with previous literature [24–26]. The overall reduction in the prevalence of diarrheal disease might be attributed to the intervention, as the installation of water kiosks increased during the project implementation, enabling increased access to affordable, clean, and safe water. Based on data from a limited household survey sample, participants indicated that the water was used not only for drinking but also for various domestic purposes, including cleaning, bathing, laundry, and cooking.

Data collected during the intervention implementation phase show that 14 new water kiosks were installed in one of the intervention villages (Kisumu Ndogo) and another 7 were installed at the other intervention village (Makina), which is a scale-up from 4 operational water kiosks at the baseline in Kisumu Ndogo only. Therefore, during the intervention, the proportion of households accessing safe and clean water supplies increased. There was an increase in water consumption in the intervention arm, rising from 2% at baseline to 10% at endline. SHOFCO adopted a pricing model that ensured water was available and affordable to the residents in the intervention arm by subsidizing the cost of water at approximately $0.02 or 2.5 Kenyan Shillings per 20 L water jerrycan.

Implications of findings for WASH-BCC intervention in slum settings

Our findings have several implications for slum settings. We show that increasing access to affordable, safe, and clean water, combined with WASH-BCC, dramatically reduced the prevalence of diarrheal disease in children under five years of age. The implication is that improving water availability, addressing intermittent water supply, inflated water cost, and water quality challenges in slum settings is possible and would lead to a significant reduction in diarrheal disease and better child health outcomes. Our findings call for increased expansion of water kiosks/points in slum settings. In doing so, strategic collaboration with major water service providers and other WASH stakeholders will be needed to sustain an adequate volume of water flow through water kiosks. Interactions with water users indicated the acceptability of the SHOFCO water systems in the intervention arm. The water was considered affordable, cleaner, and more reliable. With an increasing number of households willing to pay for improved water sources, our findings provide a basis for implementing a sustainable pricing model during scale-up in the villages not covered by the intervention and similar settings in sub-Saharan Africa.

Furthermore, we learned that continuous community engagement is crucial in maintaining the acceptability and uptake of the intervention and thus should be considered in future undertakings.

To assess the sustainability of behavior change investments in the long term, data are needed on adherence to adopted practices over a longer duration, including new ways of encouraging people to maintain handwashing habits [27]. Data are equally needed on the long-term effects of WASH interventions, perhaps from large-scale, randomized trials [28]. The provision of safe water to households and water treatment products may not exhaustively negate entry points for disease-causing pathogens. Therefore, measures to ensure water remains clean and safe should be implemented throughout the entire water chain system—supply, distribution, collection, transportation, storage, and drinking points.

Strengths and limitations of the evaluation

Our evaluation has several strengths. The sample size was large and adequately powered to detect changes in the outcome. We employed a rigorous methodology to achieve a covariate balance between the intervention and comparison arms, hence emulating a true randomized trial. The propensity score model was correctly specified, and findings remain robust to unmeasured confounders. The limitations of the evaluation include the non-quantifiable intensity of the intervention and possible variations in its intensity and fidelity. This uncertainty, while a potential constraint, was transparently communicated to the implementing partner (SHOFCO) and the Children’s Investment Fund Foundation (CIFF), the funder. Additionally, while a rigorously designed and conducted randomized trial would have provided robust findings, it was not feasible for programmatic and ethical reasons. The outcome, diarrheal disease in the past 2 weeks, was measured by self-reporting, and this might have introduced social desirability bias. However, this bias was minimized by restricting the assessment period for diarrheal disease to the past 2 weeks. Another important limitation is the effect of co-interventions on the observed outcome. While there could have been co-interventions, deliberate steps were taken to document and report all of them, but none were observed. And, findings remain robust to such co-interventions even if they had existed. Our intervention combined a safe water supply and WASH-BCC, so it was impossible to isolate the effect of each intervention component. However, the combined intervention was logical considering that a single intervention component would be insufficient to achieve the desired effect.

Conclusion and recommendation

The KIDS intervention—safe water delivery and WASH-BCC—led to a significant reduction in diarrheal disease prevalence among children under five years of age in Kibera slum, Nairobi, Kenya. Therefore, the intervention should be expanded to the remaining arms and replicated in similar settings to reduce diarrheal morbidity and mortality in children under five years of age.
