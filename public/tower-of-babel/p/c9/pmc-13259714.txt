Development of an Artificial Intelligence Web Application for Predicting Chemotherapy‐Induced Neutropenia in Patients With Non‐Small Cell Lung Cancer: A Prospective Study
Authors: Jingyue Zhang, Yang Zhai, Chang Liu, Miaomiao Luo, Jiahui Liu, Qi Liu, Hanxu Zhang, Shijiao Cai, Ye Tian, Liangfu Lu, Linlin Zhang, Hengjie Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to develop an artificial intelligence (AI) web application for predicting chemotherapy‐induced neutropenia (CIN) in patients with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsWe conducted a prospective study including 310 patients who underwent 1047 chemotherapy cycles with NSCLC patients between 2019 and 2024 from Tianjin Medical University General Hospital. Detailed clinical information and laboratory data were collected. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%) at the patient level. Machine learning was employed to develop the model. After completing training and hyperparameter optimization (HPO) through cross‐validation on the training set, the performance was compared through the test set. Meanwhile, a stacking ensemble model was developed by integrating these optimized base learners described above. Standard evaluation metrics, such as area under the receiver operating curve (AUC), Accuracy, Recall, Precision, and F1 score, and Brier score, were used for discrimination of the model. To enhance the transparency of the optimal model, the shapley additive explanations (SHAP) were used together with the Local interpretable model‐agnostic explanations (LIME) and the partial dependence plot (PDP) techniques. Based on the best machine learning‐based model, an AI application was developed on the Internet.ResultsThe categorical boosting (CatBoost) model with an AUC of 0.849 (95% Cl: 0.788–0.905), an accuracy of 0.813, a recall of 0.814, a specificity of 0.813, a F1 score of 0.642, and a Brier score of 0.141 had a higher discriminatory capability than other models. The five most significant features in the model were identified as body surface area, lymphocyte count, body mass index (BMI), absolute neutrophil count, and chemotherapy regimen. Notably, body surface area emerged as the most influential factor for predicting outcomes. Specifically, higher body surface area level was associated with an increased risk of CIN. The chemotherapy regimen of Paclitaxel + Carboplatin and Paclitaxel + Cisplatin showed a positive correlation with the risk of CIN. Lower lymphocyte count, BMI, and absolute neutrophil count were indicative of a higher risk of CIN. The AI application has been deployed online at http://39.96.172.15/, based on the CatBoost model.ConclusionsThe CatBoost model exhibited strong discriminatory ability in predicting CIN risk in NSCLC patients. The developed AI model serves as a valuable tool to enhance clinical decision‐making.

Introduction

Chemotherapy remains a cornerstone in the multidisciplinary management of non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), spanning curative‐intent adjuvant/neoadjuvant settings and palliative care for advanced disease [1]. However, its efficacy is frequently compromised by dose‐limiting toxicities, among which chemotherapy‐induced neutropenia (CIN) represents a pervasive and clinically significant hematological complication [2]. Severe (grade 3/4) neutropenia, defined as an absolute neutrophil count below 1.0 × 109/L, substantially increases the risk of life‐threatening infections, sepsis, hospitalization, and chemotherapy interruptions or dose reductions [2, 3]. This toxicity is particularly common among NSCLC patients receiving myelosuppressive platinum‐based doublet regimens, such as carboplatin–paclitaxel or cisplatin–pemetrexed, with reported incidence rates of grade 3/4 CIN exceeding 20%–40% during the first cycle without prophylaxis [4]. Prophylactic granulocyte colony‐stimulating factors (G‐CSF), while effective in mitigating this risk, are inconsistently applied due to imperfect risk stratification tools, potentially compromising both patient safety and treatment efficacy.

Research on models for predicting CIN has made progress. Early studies mostly used logistic regression, Cox proportional hazards model, etc., identifying key predictive factors such as age, baseline neutrophil count, and intensity of chemotherapy regimens. Although such models provide a basic framework, their predictive efficacy is often moderate (AUC about 0.65–0.75), failing to meet the needs of precision medicine [5, 6, 7]. A few studies have attempted to integrate pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) parameters to predict neutrophil dynamics, but model construction is complex and requires intensive blood sampling to obtain drug concentrations, resulting in poor clinical universality [8, 9].

In recent years, machine learning models have shown great potential in predicting clinical outcomes and identifying risk factors in various medical fields [10]. Recent studies [9, 11] have used electronic health record (EHR) data and advanced algorithms (such as random forest, support vector machine, gradient boosting machine) to predict CIN. These methods show better discriminative ability and highlight the value of incorporating early treatment dynamics. However, high‐performance prediction models specifically for NSCLC cohorts are rare. Moreover, few of these models have been developed into easily accessible web applications for clinicians and patients. The absence of such tools hinders the efficient translation of research findings into clinical practice. Meanwhile, model interpretability is also a challenge to overcome for application promotion. To ensure accessibility and interpretability, an artificial intelligence (AI) web application was developed, and the shapley additive explanations (SHAP) [12] was incorporated with the partial dependence plot (PDP) [13, 14] and the local interpretable model‐agnostic explanations (LIME) [15] techniques into the model whose methods enable the model to discern and emphasize crucial features, offering comprehensive explanations for its predictions, which not only facilitates a deeper comprehension of the rationale behind the model's predictions for clinicians but also elevates its potential for adoption within clinical practice.

This study aims to develop an innovative AI‐driven web application. The application utilizes advanced machine learning algorithms to provide individualized risk predictions for severe (grade 3/4) CIN in NSCLC patients receiving common myelosuppressive chemotherapy regimens. By constructing this user‐friendly web tool that can be directly integrated into clinical workflows, the study seeks to achieve precise, dynamic, and clinically practical CIN risk prediction. The ultimate goal is to guide the rational prophylactic use of G‐CSF, reduce CIN‐related complications, and optimize chemotherapy management for NSCLC patients.

Methods

Study Population

The present prospective observational study included patients with NSCLC receiving chemotherapy in the Department of Oncology, Tianjin Medical University General Hospital, China, between January 2019 and December 2024. Routine blood cell count monitoring was performed on days 3, 7, 10, and 14 after chemotherapy administration as part of standardized post‐chemotherapy clinical surveillance. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) non‐cancer diagnosis; (3) pregnancy or lactation; (4) concurrent radiotherapy; (5) history of stem cell transplantation; (6) use of granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) or prophylactic antibiotics prior to chemotherapy; (7) diagnosis of acute leukemia; and (8) pre‐existing neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count < 1.0 × 109/L). This study was approved by the ethics committees of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (Approval No. IRB2024‐KY‐221) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data Collection

The patients' data was prospectively collected, including demographics, potential CIN predictors, comorbidities, and laboratory parameters before chemotherapy, including gender, age, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, rheumatoid arthritis, autoimmune disease, coronary heart disease, bone metastasis, body surface area, body mass index (BMI), chemotherapy cycle, chemotherapy regimen, chemotherapy repetition cycle, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, gamma‐glutamyl transferase, plasma bilirubin, total protein, albumin, glomerular filtration rate, leukocyte count, absolute neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, blood hemoglobin, and thrombocyte count.

Clinical Follow‐Up and Outcome Definition

The primary endpoint was ≥ grade 3 CIN (i.e., ANC below 1.0 × 109/L) after chemotherapy based on the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI‐CTCAE) version 5.0 [16]. CIN outcome data was collected prospectively. Patients were asked to have their blood checked periodically and give it to the nurse at the next admission. The nurse staff assessed the grades of CIN from the patient's periodic blood test data using NCI‐CTCAE (version 5.0).

Data Splitting and Preprocessing

Isolation forest was used for outlier detection, and variables with outliers were transformed into logarithmic space to avoid the effect of outliers. For missing data, variables with more than 30% missing values were removed to minimize possible bias due to imputation. And the Multivariate Imputation Chained Equations (MICE) [17] was performed to impute the remaining variables. Details concerning the number of missing values for each variable are listed in Supplemental Table S1. To prevent data leakage, data splitting was performed at the patient level rather than at the chemotherapy cycle level. Unique patient identifiers were used to randomly assign patients into a training cohort (80%) and a testing cohort (20%), ensuring that all chemotherapy cycles from a given patient were contained entirely within either the training or testing set.

Model Development and Evaluation

The Boruta algorithm [18] performed feature screening to identify the features most associated with the risk of CIN. Normalize the data to ensure the model is not biased by the scale of the features. Machine learning algorithms, deep forest [19], categorical boosting (CatBoost) [20], neural network [21], decision tree [22], random forest, and support vector machine [23] (SVM) were selected to construct predictive models. Each model used a 5‐fold cross‐validation process to optimize the model hyperparameters based on the open source optimization framework Optuna (version 2.10.0) [24]. Meanwhile, a stacking ensemble model [25, 26, 27] was constructed by combining the predictions of multiple optimized base learners, including deep forest, neural network, decision tree, CatBoost, random forest, and SVM. A logistic regression model was used as the meta‐learner to integrate the outputs of the base models. And as a sensitivity analysis for missing data, a complete‐case analysis was performed by excluding all observations with missing values. The same modeling pipeline was subsequently applied to the resulting complete‐case dataset to assess the robustness of the findings. In the primary analysis, each chemotherapy cycle was treated as an independent observation to capture dynamic changes in neutropenia risk across treatment courses. This approach may introduce potential pseudoreplication due to within‐patient correlation. To address this issue, an additional sensitivity analysis was conducted including only the first available chemotherapy cycle per patient.

The area under the receiver operating curve (AUC), Accuracy, Recall, Precision, and F1 score were used as a model evaluation metric. The confusion matrix was used to determine the Accuracy, Recall, Specificity, and F1 score. The confusion matrix is essentially a table that comprises four core elements: True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN). Each of these elements corresponds to a particular outcome resulting from the model's predictions. Details concerning the formulas for each evaluation metric are listed in Table S1. In addition, the calibration was also plotted to assess the consistency between the predicted and actual probabilities of the model, and the Brier score was used to assess the calibration of the model. The optimal decision threshold was determined using the Youden index derived from the ROC curve in the testing set. The threshold corresponding to the maximum Youden index (sensitivity + specificity −1) was selected as the optimal decision threshold. And decision curve analysis (DCA) [28] was employed to evaluate the clinical utility and net benefit of the model. SHAP, LIME, and PDP were used to interpret the model at the global and local levels to avoid the contingency caused by a single interpretation method. Development of an Online AI Application

The development of the AI online application was developed through the Streamlit platform. The application incorporates multiple functional modules designed to enhance operational efficiency and practical utility. A core component of the system is an interactive control panel that allows users to select model parameters from 11 clinically significant variables, thereby enabling personalized input configuration based on individual requirements. Furthermore, the platform features a dedicated computational module for predicting CIN risk. This module generates probability estimates based on user‐defined parameters, providing clinicians with quantifiable prognostic assessments to support clinical decision‐making.

To enhance interpretability, a supplementary panel was incorporated. This module utilizes the SHAP method to quantitatively illustrate the impact of individual model predictors on the final risk assessment. This functionality enables the identification of influential variables and elucidates their directional contribution (protective or detrimental) to the prediction for each specific case. Consequently, clinicians can pinpoint key risk factors warranting heightened clinical attention for individual patients. Finally, based on an optimally determined risk threshold derived from the model outputs, patients are stratified into distinct high‐risk and low‐risk cohorts. This stratification offers clinically actionable insights to support evidence‐based medical decision‐making, thereby augmenting the platform's utility.

Statistical Analysis

In this study, statistical analysis and machine learning models and hyperparameter tuning based on scikit‐learn were conducted using Python 3.8.3 (Python Software Foundation). Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequency (%) for categorical variables.

The median age was 65.00 years; 74.84% of patients were male (Table 1). Approximately 34.52% of patients had hypertension, 16.13% had diabetes. The chemotherapy was Paclitaxel (PTX) + Carboplatin (CBP) or Pemetrexed (Pem) + CBP based in 43.65% and 32.76% of cycles, respectively. Details of the clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Based on the optimal hyperparameters, 6 models were developed using data from the training set. Each of the models was evaluated on the test set. Among the single models, the CatBoost model achieved the best overall performance, with an AUC of 0.849 (95% Cl: 0.788–0.905), an accuracy of 0.813, a recall of 0.814, a specificity of 0.813, a F1 score of 0.642, and a Brier score of 0.141 (Table 2, Figure 4A,B). However, the improvement in AUC compared with the CatBoost model was marginal (0.854 vs. 0.849), and the stacking model showed lower accuracy and specificity (Table 2, Figure 4A). Considering both discrimination and overall model stability, the CatBoost model was selected as the final prediction model. Meanwhile, the results of the sensitivity analysis were largely consistent with those of the main analysis. The CatBoost model is stable and robust across MICE missing‐data handling strategies. In addition, to address potential within‐patient correlation, a sensitivity analysis restricted to the first available chemotherapy cycle per patient was conducted, which yielded comparable model performance. To further enhance the interpretability of feature effects, PDP and Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) plots were additionally utilized as supplementary analytical tools. ICE plots delineate case‐specific feature dependencies by illustrating prediction variations across feature value ranges, while PDPs aggregate these effects by displaying average prediction changes across the entire dataset. This dual visualization approach enables a comprehensive examination of both individual and population‐level feature impacts on model outputs. The analysis revealed that the probability of experiencing CIN increases with body surface area (Figure 6A,B). Notably, the SHAP value of body surface area crossed zero at approximately 1.624 m2, suggesting increased CIN risk above this level (Figure 6A). It can be seen from Figure 6C that when lymphocyte count is less than 1.070 × 109/L, the risk of CIN increases, and a similar trend can be seen in PDP (Figure 6D). A threshold effect was observed, whereby the predicted risk of CIN increased when the absolute neutrophil count decreased below approximately 4.410 × 109/L. Online AI Prediction

The CatBoost model has been deployed for online access at http://39.96.172.15/, available to users free of charge. As illustrated in Figure 8, clicking on the provided URL enables entry into the online AI application. After inputting and submitting the relevant model parameters, the application will present an assessment of the risk for CIN. Patients with predicted CIN risk greater than 0.348 were defined as high‐risk individuals, who may benefit from prophylactic strategies such as G‐CSF administration (Table 2). Moreover, the AI application generates a detailed risk report that identifies and evaluates key risk and protective factors, as well as the relative importance of each feature.

Discussion

Accurate prediction of CIN holds significant clinical importance. On the one hand, for patients predicted to be at high risk, clinicians can implement preventive measures in advance, such as prophylactic use of G‐CSF, adjustment of chemotherapy doses, or modification of treatment regimens. This reduces the risk of CIN and its severe complications, such as infections, thereby improving patients' tolerance to chemotherapy, ensuring the smooth progression of treatment, enhancing quality of life, and reducing medical costs. On the other hand, for low‐risk patients, unnecessary preventive interventions can be avoided, minimizing waste of medical resources and reducing adverse reactions associated with prophylactic medications. This study successfully developed and validated an artificial intelligence‐based web application for predicting CIN in patients with NSCLC. The results demonstrate the potential value and application prospects of this model in clinical practice.

Currently, there is a lack of unified standards for feature selection in predictive models, making it difficult to determine the optimal number of features. Although increasing the number of features helps improve the information coverage of the model, an excessive number of features not only impairs the convenience of clinical operations but may also reduce prediction accuracy due to the introduction of non‐causally associated features [29, 30]. To address this issue, this study employed the Boruta algorithm for feature screening and ultimately constructed an efficient and streamlined machine learning model, which can be used for the clinical assessment of CIN risk in NSCLC patients. The final predictive factors identified included age, chemotherapy regimen, number of chemotherapy repetition cycles, lymphocyte count, glomerular filtration rate, leukocyte count, body surface area, BMI, absolute neutrophil count, plasma bilirubin concentration, and alanine aminotransferase activity.

Among the key features analyzed in this study, the top five most influential factors were determined to be body surface area, lymphocyte count, BMI, absolute neutrophil count, and chemotherapy regimen. Notably, body surface area is the most influential predictor. The relationship between body surface area and CIN risk is unclear, and a number of studies focusing on breast and renal cancer cell carcinoma have shown an association between low body surface area and increased CIN risk [31, 32, 33]. Contrary to previous studies, this study found that body surface area has a positive correlation with the risk of CIN in NSCLC patients, and the risk of CIN increased for patients whose body surface area exceeds 1.624 m2. Body surface area is an important parameter in the calculation of chemotherapeutic drug dosages and may indirectly affect the risk of neutropenia by influencing pharmacokinetics [34]. A study of mitomycin C (MMC) drug metabolic concentrations and CIN found a positive correlation between MMC‐AUC and neutropenia risk [35]. Mosteller's formula shows that height and weight are the key variables in determining body surface area. In taller patients, body surface area is significantly higher, resulting in a higher chemotherapy dose according to the formula, making it likely that the actual concentration of the drug acting on the bone marrow will be higher than expected, aggravating myelosuppression. Lymphocyte is a key factor contributing to the body's immune response [36]. Baseline lymphopenia has been shown to be a poor prognostic factor for hematological adverse event [37]. The difference in patients' lymphocyte count in this study was considered to affect CIN significantly. And when lymphocyte count is less than 1.070 × 109/L, the risk of CIN increases.

And this study found that BMI is a negative correlation with the risk of CIN in NSCLC patients. A study of patients with diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) in U.S. veterans treated with R‐CHOP found that obese patients receiving actual weight‐based chemotherapy doses did not have an increased risk of FN or treatment‐related death compared to normal‐weight patients [38]. In addition, a study has shown that for patients with NSCLC, a higher BMI (e.g., BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) is associated with significantly improved overall survival (OS) and progression‐free survival (PFS) after treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [39]. Another study also found that for every 1 standard deviation (SD) increase in BMI, the risk of death was reduced by 20% (p < 0.001) [40]. This finding indirectly suggests that a higher BMI may be associated with a reduced risk of CIN among patients with NSCLC. Chemotherapy regimens have consistently emerged as established risk factors for CIN [41, 42]. Consistent with these findings, this study revealed that chemotherapy regimens are associated with CIN risk, with paclitaxel + carboplatin and paclitaxel + cisplatin showing a higher risk of CIN. In addition, a consistent relationship was observed between baseline absolute neutrophil count and the risk of CIN. A threshold effect appeared, with the predicted risk of CIN increasing when absolute neutrophil count decreased below approximately 4.410 × 109/L. This association is biologically plausible, as baseline neutrophil levels reflect bone marrow reserve and the capacity to maintain granulopoiesis under physiological stress. Lower baseline absolute neutrophil count may indicate a reduced hematopoietic buffer, increasing susceptibility to neutrophil depletion in response to cytotoxic therapies or systemic inflammatory stimuli [43, 44]. Previous studies have identified baseline hematologic parameters, including absolute neutrophil count, as important predictors of neutropenic complications during chemotherapy. Crawford et al. reported that lower pretreatment neutrophil counts were associated with a higher incidence of severe neutropenia and related infectious complications [45].

This study developed and compared the performance of 6 machine learning models, including deep forest, CatBoost, neural network, decision tree, random forest, and SVM. Among these, CatBoost demonstrated the best overall discriminative power with an AUC of 0.849 (95% Cl: 0.788–0.905), an accuracy of 0.813, and a F1 score of 0.642, making it the optimal choice for this study. In addition, the potential benefit of ensemble learning was explored by constructing a stacking model [46, 47, 48] that integrated multiple base learners. Although the stacking model achieved the highest AUC, the improvement compared with the CatBoost model was marginal. Furthermore, the stacking model showed lower accuracy and specificity than CatBoost, suggesting that the overall performance gain was limited. One possible explanation is that CatBoost itself is a powerful gradient boosting algorithm capable of effectively capturing nonlinear relationships and complex feature interactions, particularly in structured clinical data [20]. Previous studies have shown that gradient boosting models often achieve performance comparable to, or even exceeding, that of ensemble combinations of heterogeneous models [49]. In the present dataset, the predictive information contained in the clinical variables may have already been sufficiently captured by the CatBoost model, leaving limited room for further improvement through stacking. Moreover, model interpretability and stability are important considerations in clinical prediction research. Compared with ensemble frameworks involving multiple models, a single well‐performing model such as CatBoost is easier to interpret and implement in clinical practice. Therefore, considering the comparable discriminative ability, a better balance of performance metrics, and greater practical applicability, the CatBoost model was selected as the final prediction model in this study.

This study utilized CatBoost techniques to construct a prediction model capable of accurately forecasting the risk of CIN in patients with NSCLC. While the model provided precise predictions, its inherent “black box” nature hindered its practical generalization [50, 51]. The burgeoning field of interpretability in machine learning has emerged as a crucial area of research, aiming to enhance transparency in predictive processes [14, 52, 53]. It is essential to acknowledge that interpretations of model results based on different theoretical foundations may diverge significantly, potentially leading to inconclusive interpretations. To mitigate this risk, a diverse array of interpretability techniques was employed to elucidate the model's inner workings. This encompassed the integration of multiple interpretability approaches at each level of the model (global, feature, and individual) to ensure steadfastness and objectivity in result interpretation. By adopting this comprehensive interpretability strategy, the study aimed to elevate the model's credibility. In addition, by using the convenient tools of the Streamlit platform, the CatBoost model and SHAP method are integrated into a user‐friendly online AI application for doctors. Its web‐based deployment lowers adoption barriers, as clinicians require no local installation or computational expertise.

While the prediction model in this study offers potential benefits, it is essential to acknowledge its limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single center, and variations in patient characteristics, treatment protocols, and medical environments across different centers may exist. The applicability of the model in other centers requires further validation. Second, although the inclusion of a large volume of patient data, the model was developed based on data from Chinese patients. There is still no basis for the generalizability of the model for use in patients from other populations. Research indicates black cancer patients treated with taxanes for any cancer had a higher risk of neutropenia compared with their White counterparts, especially those who received paclitaxel [54], emphasizing the importance of validating the model in different demographic groups. Additionally, the model primarily relies on routine clinical indicators, and emerging biomarkers not yet widely used in clinical practice were not fully incorporated. With advances in research and technology, integrating more valuable biomarkers into the model could further improve prediction accuracy.

In conclusion, the artificial intelligence web application developed in this study provides a new and effective tool for predicting CIN in NSCLC patients. Despite its limitations, it lays a foundation for future clinical practice and research. With ongoing technological progress and in‐depth research, this model is expected to play a greater role in clinical settings, offering stronger support for chemotherapy management in NSCLC patients.
