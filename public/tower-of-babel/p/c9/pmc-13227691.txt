Dengue virus serotype 4-leading serotype epidemic and its clinical and molecular characteristics in Vung Tau, South Vietnam, 2020–2021
Authors: Ngan Thi Thanh Nguyen, Taichiro Takemura, Motoki Ihara, Kei Yamasato, Tomomi Kurashige, Haruka Abe, Hien Thi Tran, Hieu Vu, Anh Hong Quynh Pham, Hau Thi Bich Vu, Linh Ngoc Nguyen, Hang Thi Thu Pham, Quyen Do Pham, Thu Thi Hien Le, Thuy Thi Thu Nguyen, Phuong Vu Mai Hoang, Hang Le Khanh Nguyen, Tu Tuan Lam, Truong Thien Tran, Lam Thi Le, Kha Hong Vu, Trang Huyen Chu, Mya Myat Ngwe Tun, Kouichi Morita, Yuki Takamatsu, Futoshi Hasebe, Mai Thi Quynh Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDengue cases are reported annually in Vung Tau province, southern Vietnam; however, data on viral and host factors associated with disease severity are limited, particularly during epidemics driven by specific serotypes. We investigated the prevalence of dengue virus (DENV), its molecular epidemiology, and clinical–laboratory correlates of severity in patients with suspected dengue at Vung Tau General Hospital between September 2020 and May 2021. Among 1435 suspected cases, 639 (44.5%) were classified as DENV infection-negative, 60 (4.2%) presented with primary infection and 736 (51.3%) with secondary infection. DENV serotyping indicated that DENV-4 was the most prevalent serotype (50 patients, 37.6%), followed by DENV-2 (41, 30.8%), DENV-1 (39, 29.3%), and DENV-3 (3, 2.3%). Phylogenetic analysis revealed DENV-1 genotype I, DENV-2 genotype II (cosmopolitan) and genotype V (Asian I), and DENV-4 genotype I. Notably, DENV-4 exhibited two coexisting clades, suggesting that variations in clade distribution may have contributed to the epidemic. Although parameters commonly associated with dengue severity, such as white blood cell counts, platelet counts, and liver enzyme levels (AST and ALT), were comparable across serotypes, hemoglobin and hematocrit levels were elevated in DENV-4 infections compared with other serotypes. This elevation may indicate a higher risk of plasma leakage, a key feature of severe dengue, underscoring the potential clinical significance of DENV-4 during this epidemic. These findings highlight the epidemiological and clinical importance of DENV-4 and provide evidence for refining dengue control strategies in endemic areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-00962-2.Keywords: Dengue, Vietnam, Dengue virus 4, Severity, Secondary infection

Introduction

Dengue is a mosquito-borne disease found in tropical and subtropical regions, with more than 390 million infections and 25,000 deaths each year, and remains a global public health threat [1]. The causative agent of dengue fever is the dengue virus (DENV), an enveloped virus belonging to the genus Flavivirus of the family Flaviviridae. It contains a positive-sense single-stranded RNA genome of approximately 10.6 kb. DENV genomic RNA encodes three structural proteins: capsid (C), pre-membrane protein (prM), and envelope protein (E), and seven nonstructural (NS) proteins. Based on antigenic differences, DENV is classified into four serotypes: DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4 [2–4]. Each serotype is further divided into multiple genotypes depending on geographic distribution and prevalence potential [5], which is important for understanding the viral evolution and epidemiology. Currently, DENV-1 consists of five genotypes (I-V): genotype I is of Asian origin; genotype II includes strains from Thailand; genotype III comprises sylvatic strains; genotype IV viruses originate from the South Pacific; and genotype V viruses originate from the Americas and Africa [6, 7]. DENV-2 comprises six genotypes with distinct geographical distributions. The Asian I and II genotypes circulate primarily in Asia, whereas the cosmopolitan genotype is widely distributed globally. The American genotype was once predominant in Southeast Asia but is now widespread in Central and South America. Asian I and Asian II genotypes circulate in Asia, whereas the cosmopolitan genotype is widely distributed. The American genotype is prevalent in Central and South America, whereas the Asian-American genotype, originally predominant in Southeast Asia, is now predominant in the Americas. The sylvatic genotype is found in non-human primates living in forests in Africa and South Asia [8]. DENV-2 is associated with severe dengue cases [8, 9]. DENV-3 consists of five genotypes: genotype I is found in Southeast Asia and the South Pacific; genotype II originated in Thailand; genotype III was originally isolated in the Indian subcontinent; genotype IV was originally isolated in the Americas; and genotype V is found in both Asia and the Americas [10]. DENV-4 comprises five genotypes. Genotype I (prototype) was first reported in the Philippines in 1956; genotype IIA is common in Southeast Asia and China; genotype IIB has been isolated in Southeast Asia and the Pacific Islands, genotype III has been reported only in Thailand since 1997. Sylvatic DENV-4 is present only in nonhuman primates [5].

Laboratory tests play a crucial role in confirming dengue, as the infection produces a wide variety of often nonspecific symptoms. In the early stages of the disease, diagnosis can be made through viral isolation, nucleic acid detection, and antigen detection. At the end of the acute infectious phase, serology is the method of choice for diagnosis [11–14]. In addition to specific diagnostic tests for demonstrating DENV infection, a full blood count, platelet count, and hematocrit are important for aligning clinical features to diagnose and assess disease severity [11]. Early dengue detection is challenging because the initial symptoms are nonspecific, and viremia can be variable [15].

Dengue virus (DENV) infection induces short-term cross-protection against other serotypes. However, subsequent secondary infections with different serotypes may lead to antibody-dependent enhancement, increasing the risk of severe dengue [16]. Clinically, dengue infection ranges from mild dengue fever to severe disease, characterized by plasma leakage and hemorrhagic manifestations. Several factors have been suggested to contribute to severe dengue, including secondary infection, age, viral load, infecting serotype, and genotype [17, 18]. Previous reports on dengue fever in children indicate that secondary DENV-2 infection is more likely to cause severe disease than infection with other serotypes, whereas primary DENV-1 infection is often more clinically apparent. In contrast, primary DENV-2 and DENV-3 infections tend to be asymptomatic [19, 20]. Infection with any dengue serotype provides long-term immunity to that serotype but not to other serotypes. Asian genotypes of DENV-2 and DENV-3 are frequently associated with severe manifestations such as dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS) [21].

In Southeast Asia, dengue fever outbreaks have been reported as early as the late 1940s [22], and the disease has presented a significant public health burden for decades [23]. In Vietnam, the first case of dengue fever was recorded in Hanoi in 1958, and the first outbreak was reported in 1963; the number of cases has steadily increased in recent years [24]. Vietnam has a humid tropical climate with seasonal and regional variations. Dengue occurs year-round, with approximately 72% of cases reported between June and November, although seasonal patterns vary by region [25].

All four DENV serotypes have been detected in Vietnam and are associated with annual outbreaks; however, DENV-1 and -2 are the most common serotypes [26, 27]. A previous study indicated that the emergence of the Asian 1 genotype of DENV-2 represents a significant development in the regional epidemiology [27]. Other studies have reported DENV surveillance in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Quang Nam, and Dong Thap [28–30]; however, limited data are available on the characteristics of the DENV responsible for the recent outbreak in Ba Ria-Vung Tau Province, Vietnam. Although DENV-4 is often underreported or considered less clinically severe, it remains an important pathogen. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate dengue cases among suspected patients presenting to the Vung Tau General Hospital between September 2020 and May 2021. We performed serological and molecular characterization of circulating DENV strains and analyzed their associated clinical features, with a particular focus on the predominance and clinical relevance of DENV-4 infection.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

This study was based on a project to promote research on emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases between Nagasaki University, Japan and the National Institute of Hygiene and Epidemiology (NIHE), Vietnam. Informed consent to participate in this study was obtained from all participants. The Institute Review Board of NIHE, Vietnam (Ethical Approval No. IRB-VND1057/IORG008555) and the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Tropical Medicine, Nagasaki University, Japan (Ethical Approval No.230209288).

Data collection and study design

All patients with suspected dengue admitted to Vung Tau General Hospital, Vietnam, between September 2020 and May 2021 were recruited for the study. With the record and written consent of the selected patients, an additional 3–5 ml of whole blood was collected during routine investigations. Serum was separated and sent to the NIHE for DENV testing and serotype identification. Samples were primarily collected during the early stages of the illness. Most samples were obtained on days 0–3 of fever onset, with the highest numbers on day 1 (n = 416), followed by days 2 (n = 371), 0 (n = 324), and 3 (n = 212). The number of samples collected declined sharply after day 3, with only a few samples collected on the later days. Twelve samples had missing information regarding the day of fever.

Case definition

Primary analyses were performed on laboratory-confirmed dengue cases defined by positive real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) results (n = 133). Serological testing (IgM/IgG ELISA) was conducted for population-level description and infection staging but was not used as the primary inclusion criterion for molecular analyses. For analytical clarity, the immune status was simplified into three categories: DENV infection-negative (IgM−/IgG−, primary infection (IgM+/IgG−), and secondary infection (IgG+, regardless of IgM status). In cases where both IgM and IgG were positive, the IgM/IgG ratio was calculated to further support classification, with a ratio >1.2 considered indicative of primary infection and ≤1.2 suggestive of secondary infection. Patients who were positive for DENV serotype by PCR but had no detectable IgM or IgG were considered to have primary infection. Because samples were collected early during the acute phase, a substantial proportion of patients lacked detectable antibodies and were classified as DENV infection–negative, reflecting the absence of confirmed acute dengue infection.

Serology test

A total of 1435 dengue-suspected samples were screened using ELISA to detect anti-DENV IgM and IgG. We used anti-DENV IgG and DENV IgM kits (Dengue Elisa IgM capture, Dengue Elisa IgG capture, Virell, Spain) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Viral RNA extraction

Dengue viral RNA was extracted from serum samples using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [31]. The extracted RNA was used for real-time RT-PCR and cDNA synthesis.

Serotyping by Taqman real-time RT-PCR

We detected DENV serotypes using real-time RT-PCR (TaqMan) assays [32, 33]. Briefly, dengue virus serotyping was performed using a fourplex Taqman Real-Time RT-PCR detection platform, as described. PCR reactions were prepared in a cocktail of 12.5 μl of 2× RT (reverse transcriptase)-PCR Mix (i-Script One Step RT-PCR kit, Biorad, USA), 0.5 μl of each primers (DENV 1 and DENV 3 primers: 50 μM; DENV 2 and DENV 4 primers: 25 μM), 0.45 μl of each probes (10 μM), 0.5 μl of RT Enzyme Mix, and 1.2 μl of nuclease-free water. Nuclease-free water was used as the negative control. Real-Time RT-PCR amplification was performed on the CFX 96 (Biorad, USA) platform at 50 °C for 30 min, 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, the end stage at 72 °C for 1 min, and keep heating at 10 °C.

PCR and Sanger sequencing

RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using ReverTra Ace® (Toyobo, Cat. No. TRT-101). Sequences of E protein-coding regions were amplified using KOD One PCR master mix and a set of serotype-specific amplification primers, as described elsewhere [34]. These primers were designed to detect the DENV genotypes currently circulating globally. PCRs were assembled by mixing 25 µl of KOD One PCR master mix, 30 µM concentrations of each DENV forward and reverse primer, and 2.0 µl template cDNA to a final reaction volume of 50.0 µl. PCR analysis was performed in a conventional thermal cycler programmed for 30 cycles as follows: denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 40 °C for 10 s, and elongation at 68 °C for 10 s, and a final extension at 4 °C. PCR products were detected and visualized using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. A 1-kb ladder DNA marker was used to determine the amplicon size. Appropriate controls were included in each PCR run to ensure the reliability of the assay. The positive control consisted of RNA extracted from a previously confirmed DENV-positive sample, while the negative controls included nuclease-free water (no-template control) and RNA extracted from DENV-negative samples to monitor contamination and non-specific amplification. The PCR amplicon length varies by serotype: approximately 1740 bp for DENV-1, 1859 bp for DENV-2, and 1747 bp for DENV-4. Sequencing reactions were assembled using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 cycle sequencing kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 4337455). The optimal purified product input was experimentally determined to range between 200 and 300 ng/reaction. Six sequencing reactions were performed for each sample using serotype-specific primer sets [34]. Sequencing reactions were assembled according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol: 1 µl of BigDye Terminator 3.1, 2 µl of sequencing buffer, a 3.2 µM concentration of the corresponding single sequencing primer, 300 ng of purified DNA product, and nuclease-free PCR-grade water to complete a final reaction volume of 10 µl. Sequencing reactions were performed on a conventional thermocycler programmed with the following protocol: denaturation at 96 °C for 1 min, followed by 25 cycles at 96 °C for 10 s, 50 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 1 min. Sequencing was performed using a 3500 Series Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Genotypeing by phylogenic analysis

Consensus sequences were generated from six sequencing trace files per sample using DNA Dynamo software. The resulting sequences were grouped by serotype and aligned with representative dengue virus genotype reference sequences from the GenBank database. Genotype assignment for the newly assembled DENV-1, DENV-2, and DENV-4 sequences was performed using the Genome Detective Dengue Typing Tool (https://www.genomedetective.com/) (accessed November 15, 2023). All sequences generated in this study were deposited in GenBank, and the accession numbers are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Complete coding sequences of the envelope (E) gene were retrieved from GenBank in February 2026. A total of DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4 sequences were selected as representative reference strains for phylogenetic and evolutionary analyses using CD-HIT [35]. The reference sequences and all Vietnamese DENV E-gene sequences were aligned using MAFFT [36]. Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using the maximum likelihood method in IQ-TREE software. The best-fit nucleotide substitution model was selected using ModelFinder within IQ-TREE, and branch support was assessed using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized and annotated using FigTree v1.4.4 [37] to highlight the clustering of Vietnamese sequences within the established DENV genotype clades. Amino acid substitutions in the E protein were identified by comparison with representative reference strains and analyzed to characterize genotype- and lineage-specific mutations.

Statistical analysis

Stata version 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) was used for data analysis. Categorical variables, including sex, serotype, genotype, and clinical manifestations, were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square and Pearson’s chi-square tests were performed to analyze the associations between categorical variables. Non-parametric analysis using the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to data with non-normal distributions, and the results are presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR). All analyses were conducted at a 95% confidence level, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Data are presented as n (%) unless otherwise specified. Fisher’s exact test was used to assess the association between immune status and warning signs, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to compare platelet counts between patients with and without warning signs.

Data availability

The sequence data were deposited in GenBank under the accession numbers listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Results

Characterization of dengue cases

A total of 1435 individuals were enrolled in this study. The median age was 8 years (IQR, 3–30 years). The largest age group was 0–9 years (751/1435, 52.3%), followed by 10–19 years (182/1435, 12.7%), 20–29 years (133/1435, 9.3%), 30–39 years (133/1435, 9.3%), 40–49 years (80/1435, 5.6%), 50–59 years (71/1435, 4.9%), 60–69 years (50/1435, 3.5%), 70–79 years (21/1435, 1.5%), and ≥ 80 years (11/1435, 0.8%). Age data were missing for three participants (0.2%). Of the participants, 642 (44.7%) were female and 793 (55.3%) were male. Regarding serological characteristics, 77 participants (5.4%) were IgM positive and 743 participants (51.8%) were IgG positive. Based on serological profiles, 639 individuals (44.5%) were classified as naïve, 60 (4.2%) as having a primary infection, and 736 (51.3%) as having a secondary infection. The DENV serotype distribution showed that DENV-1 was detected in 39 participants (2.7%), DENV-2 in 41 participants (2.9%), DENV-3 in three participants (0.2%), and DENV-4 in 50 participants (3.5%) (Table 1).

Prevalence of dengue serotypes and genotypes

Phylogenetic analysis of the DENV-1 strains detected in this study revealed that all strains belonged to genotype I, consistent with previous reports of DENV-1 from Vietnam. DENV-2 was classified as the Asian I genotype, consistent with previous Vietnamese isolates. Notably, the Cosmopolitan genotype strain that emerged during the 2022 outbreak was absent in 2021, suggesting that local strains of DENV-1 and DENV-2 continued to circulate. In contrast, DENV-4 was classified into genotype I and included strains from clades that have been frequently recognized in Vietnam (D 4-143, D 4-197), as well as numerous strains from minor clades that are rarely reported. It is intriguing to consider whether the differences in clade distribution contributed to the epidemic.

The clinical manifestation in relation to dengue serotypes

Clinical manifestations and laboratory parameters were compared among the three serotype groups (Table 2). No significant differences were observed in the clinical manifestations of patients infected with different dengue virus serotypes. The frequencies of nausea, positive tourniquet test, petechiae, muscle pain, abdominal pain, eye pain, and sleeping difficulty were comparable across the DENV-1, DENV-2, and DENV-4 groups (all p > 0.05). Similarly, the median respiratory rates did not differ significantly among serotypes. Regarding laboratory findings, white blood cell counts tended to be higher in patients infected with DENV-2 than in those infected with DENV-1 and DENV-4; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.057). Red blood cell counts, platelet counts, liver enzyme levels (AST and ALT), creatinine concentrations, and pulse pressure were comparable among the three serotypes (all p > 0.05). In contrast, hemoglobin and hematocrit levels differed significantly between the serotypes. Patients infected with DENV-4 exhibited higher median hemoglobin and hematocrit values than those infected with DENV-1 and DENV-2 (p = 0.004 and p = 0.002, respectively).

The clinical manifestation in relation to dengue patients by immune status

Clinical and laboratory characteristics stratified by immune status (naïve, primary, and secondary infection) are presented in Table 3. Significant differences were observed between the three groups. The median body temperature differed modestly but significantly across groups (p = 0.035), with the highest median temperature recorded in the primary infection group. Both systolic and diastolic blood pressure showed a progressive increase from naïve to secondary infection (both, p < 0.001). In contrast, the respiratory rate was significantly higher in the naïve group than in the primary and secondary groups (p < 0.001). Significant variations were also observed in the hematological parameters. White blood cell counts were lowest in primary infection (median 6.1 × 103/mm3) and differed significantly among groups (p < 0.001). Hemoglobin and hematocrit levels were higher in primary and secondary infections than in naïve individuals (both p < 0.001). Platelet counts demonstrated a graded pattern, being lowest in primary infection (median 168 × 103/mm3), intermediate in secondary infection (223 × 103/mm3), and highest in the naïve group (261 × 103/mm3) (p < 0.001). The red blood cell counts did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.243). The biochemical parameters also varied significantly. AST and ALT levels were elevated in both primary and secondary infections compared to naïve individuals (both p < 0.001). Creatinine levels were significantly higher in patients with secondary infections (p < 0.001). With respect to clinical manifestations, the frequencies of nausea, muscle pain, retro-orbital pain, petechiae, and positive tourniquet test differed significantly among the groups. Muscle pain was most frequently reported in primary infections (37%) (p < 0.001). Petechiae and positive tourniquet tests were more common in the immune groups than in naïve individuals (both p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in abdominal pain or sleeping difficulty (Table 3). The distribution of immune status varied across the serotypes. Among the DENV-1 cases, 22 (56.4%) were classified as primary infections and 17 (43.6%) as secondary infections. In DENV-2, secondary infection predominated (24/41, 58.5%), whereas 17 cases (41.5%) were primary infections. In contrast, DENV-4 was predominantly associated with secondary infection (44/50, 88%), with only six cases (12%) classified as primary infection, indicating that most patients had pre-existing dengue immunity. When assessing the association between immune status and the presence of warning signs within each serotype, no statistically significant associations were observed for DENV-1 (p = 0.228) or DENV-2 (p = 0.46) infection. In DENV-4, the association approached statistical significance (p = 0.052), suggesting a borderline trend that may be influenced by the high proportion of secondary infections in this group of patients. Similarly, platelet count was not significantly associated with warning signs of DENV-1 (p = 0.238) or DENV-2 (p = 0.859). However, in DENV-4 infection, the platelet count was significantly associated with warning signs (p = 0.033) (Table 4). This finding should be interpreted with caution, particularly given the predominance of secondary infections in DENV-4 cases.

Phylogenetic characteristics of DENV identified in Vung Tau, 2020–2010

Complete coding sequences of the envelope (E) gene were retrieved from GenBank, and DENV-1, DENV-2, and DENV-4 sequences were selected as representative reference strains for phylogenetic and evolutionary analyses. Phylogenetic analyses were performed using the maximum likelihood method, and the best-fit nucleotide substitution model was selected and visualized to highlight the clustering of Vietnamese sequences within the established DENV genotype clades.

A total of 2301 registered sequences of Vietnamese DENV-1 strains in GenBank were collected and reduced to 89 strains using CD-HIT software. The 10 strains with complete sequences were registered in the NCBI database. However, by including strains with partially unreadable sections in the 5′ UTR or 3′ UTR gene sequences, the total increased to 20 strains, all of which were classified as genotype 1. Furthermore, the DENV-1 strains detected in this study clustered with viruses previously reported in southern Vietnam, suggesting local circulation rather than a recent introduction (Fig. 1).

Characteristic amino acid substitutions in DENV-4 in this study

Amino acid substitutions in the E protein were identified by comparison with representative reference strains and analyzed to characterize genotype- and lineage-specific mutations. Comparative analysis of amino acid substitutions between DENV-4 strains from the VT 2020–2021 outbreak and reference strains revealed multiple residues with markedly different mutation frequencies. In particular, substitutions at position 384 (D → N and D → E) displayed exceptionally strong statistical support, highlighting this site as a potential hotspot for viral variation in the identified DENV-4 strains. Pronounced differences were also detected at positions 227 (S → L), 494 (H → R), and 160 (E → V) in the protein sequence. In contrast, substitutions at several other positions, including 17, 22, 38, 140, and related sites, showed no statistically significant differences between groups, suggesting that these changes likely represent background genetic variation rather than outbreak-specific signatures (Table 5).

Discussion

This study provides seroepidemiological insights into dengue virus exposure in a large cohort of 1435 individuals from an endemic setting. The predominance of children aged 0–9 years, with a median age of 8 years, indicates that dengue infection occurs early in life, which is consistent with the sustained viral transmission in tropical regions. Such early exposure has been widely reported in dengue-endemic countries and reflects the continuous circulation of the virus and the high vector activity [38]. More than half of the participants were IgG positive, indicating substantial prior exposure to DENV in the population, whereas IgM positivity was comparatively low, suggesting that only a limited proportion of individuals had recent infections at the time of sampling. The high proportion of individuals classified as having secondary infections further supports the hyperendemic nature of dengue transmission, in which repeated exposure to different serotypes is common. The small number of primary infections may be attributable to the timing of sample collection or rapid antibody maturation in endemic populations. All four DENV serotypes were detected, with DENV-1, DENV-2, and DENV-4 co-circulating, and DENV-3 was detected at low frequency. The simultaneous circulation of multiple serotypes is epidemiologically important because it increases the likelihood of sequential heterologous infections and may contribute to disease severity through antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) [39, 40]. These findings highlight the complexity of dengue transmission dynamics in the study area and emphasize the need for continued surveillance of serotype distribution.

In this study, we compared the clinical manifestations and laboratory parameters of dengue patients infected with DENV-1, DENV-2, and DENV-4. Overall, the clinical features were largely similar across serotypes, with no statistically significant differences in the frequency of common dengue symptoms or warning signs. These findings suggest that within this cohort, the dengue virus serotype alone was not strongly associated with distinct clinical presentations [41]. Most vital signs, such as blood pressure and pulse, and laboratory parameters, including platelet counts, liver enzyme levels, and renal function markers, were comparable among the serotypes. Although white blood cell counts tended to be higher in DENV-2-infected patients, this difference did not reach statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted with caution. The absence of marked laboratory differences may reflect the relatively homogeneous clinical severity of the patients enrolled in this study and the timing of sample collection during the acute phase of the infection. Notably, hemoglobin and hematocrit levels were significantly higher in patients infected with DENV-4. Elevated hemoglobin and hematocrit values may indicate hemoconcentration, which is commonly associated with plasma leakage during dengue infections [42, 43]. Although other markers of disease severity did not differ significantly among serotypes, this finding may suggest subtle differences in vascular permeability or fluid balance among patients infected with different dengue virus serotypes. Further studies incorporating longitudinal measurements and larger sample sizes are required to clarify the clinical significance of these findings.

DENV strains information originating from Vietnam registered in the International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration (INSDC), DENV-2 was predominant from 1998 to 2005. DENV-1 infections increased from 2006 and remained the predominant strain until 2017. Since then, an increased circulation of DENV-2 has been observed [44]. It is important to note that these registered strains are predominantly from the southern region, with fewer registrations from the north, and that the number of tests conducted has decreased because of the prevalence of other infectious diseases. Nevertheless, dengue fever outbreaks in the southern region, centered around HCMC, significantly influenced the number of infected individuals nationwide [44]. In our study, DENV-4 was the most prominent in Vung Tau (2020–2021), followed by DENV-2, DENV-1, and DENV-3. A study conducted in HCMC from 2010 to 2013 showed that DENV-1 (36%) was the most prevalent, followed by DENV-4 (27%), DENV-2 (26%), and DENV-3 (11%) [30]. Another study conducted in Hanoi from 2019 to 2020 showed different results: DENV-2 had the highest prevalence (73%), followed by DENV-1 (25%) [45]. These data suggest that the predominant distribution of DENV serotypes varies by region. Therefore, longitudinal studies on the distribution of DENV serotypes are necessary to predict dengue fever outbreaks in specific regions and countries. Consequently, the comprehensive survey currently being conducted in HCMC and Vung Tau is of significant importance for exploring dengue fever trends in Vietnam.

In the newly identified DENV-4 strain, amino acid substitutions not previously reported in Vietnamese strains were detected at positions 160, 227, 384, and 494. To the best of our knowledge, no prior reports have described these mutations. These findings highlight the need for future functional studies to determine whether these substitutions affect viral fitness or contribute to immune evasion. Previously, the T46I mutation, located in the DI-DII hinge region, was significantly detected in severe patients [46]. The K203T mutation, located in the E protein hinge region, has also been detected in severe cases [47]. The F461L mutation is a known neutralizing epitope site that has been previously reported [48, 49] to be strongly associated with severe disease. In this study, no amino acid variations were observed at these previously reported positions, suggesting that the viral genetic determinants of disease severity during this outbreak remain unclear.

Phylogenetic analysis generally demonstrated that previously circulating Vietnamese epidemic strains continue to circulate. However, DENV-4 exhibited two coexisting clades, indicating that differences in clade distribution may have contributed to the subsequent epidemics. Notably, the emergence of a new serotype was linked to a widespread epidemic in 1998 [24], and a new genotype was detected in severe dengue cases in 2013 [50]. In recent years, dengue fever cases associated with the DENV-2 cosmopolitan genotype have increased, as reported in 2022 [51]. However, only the Asian I genotype was detected during 2020 and 2021 in our study. This suggests that the study period may represent a pre-epidemic phase, characterized by relatively limited viral genetic diversity and the absence of newly introduced genotypes. The subsequent introduction and establishment of the Cosmopolitan genotype in 2022 may have contributed to the large-scale outbreak, potentially by increasing viral diversity and transmission dynamics in a population with varying levels of immunity. Despite the availability of extensive global data on dengue virus phylogenetics [51, 52], the relationship between the magnitude of epidemics and the diversity of circulating viruses or the introduction of new viral lineages remains incompletely understood. Therefore, broader and continuous sampling and consideration of region-specific factors are required. Although no specific amino acid changes unique to this outbreak have been identified, future studies are needed to investigate the viral fitness, cellular virulence, and adaptation to mosquito vectors. Our findings highlight the importance of continuous genomic surveillance to better understand the role of viral population dynamics in shaping the outbreak patterns.

Our investigation found that DENV-4 was the leading serotype during the dengue outbreak in Vung Tau Province in 2020–2021, and that secondary infections were associated with more severe clinical manifestations. In contrast, DENV-2 was predominant in 2022. These findings underscore the importance of continued epidemiological surveillance in outbreak settings and further research to elucidate the mechanisms underlying severe disease. A key limitation of this study is that the phylogenetic analyses were based solely on the envelope (E) gene rather than whole-genome sequences. Although the E gene is commonly used for dengue genotyping, it does not provide sufficient resolution for accurate assignment to the recently proposed lineage classifications. Therefore, future studies incorporating whole-genome sequencing are needed to enable more comprehensive genomic characterization and alignment with the updated lineage nomenclature system.
