Juvenile Hyaline Fibromatosis Presenting as Progressive Flexion Contracture of the Middle Finger in a Child: A Case Report
Authors: Simeon Mulugeta Mengistu, Abraham Gebreegziabher Negussie, Yohannis Elias, Harom Gari Wakjira, Tariku Mulatu Bore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Juvenile fibromatosis is a rare benign fibroproliferative condition that may cause functional impairment when affecting the upper extremity. Deep involvement of flexor tendons in the hand is particularly unusual. An 8-year-old girl presented with a 6-year history of progressive flexion contracture of the left middle finger, causing inability to extend or grasp objects. Examination showed severe proximal interphalangeal and distal interphalangeal contractures of the middle finger and mild proximal interphalangeal contracture of the index finger. Radiographs revealed deformity without bony abnormality. Intraoperatively, extensive fibrosis and thickening of the flexor tendons of the middle and ring fingers were found (zones III–V), with adhesions and tendon shortening. Adhesiolysis, excision of fibrotic tissue, and tendon lengthening were performed, achieving full passive extension. Histopathology confirmed juvenile fibromatosis. Postoperative splinting and physiotherapy resulted in functional improvement. Juvenile fibromatosis is a rare myofibroblastic proliferation that, although histologically benign, is characterized by locally aggressive growth and a high propensity for recurrence. Its presentation within the deep flexor tendons of the hand is exceedingly uncommon and can easily be misdiagnosed as more frequent pediatric conditions, such as camptodactyly or congenital trigger finger. In this case, the six-year progression of the contracture led to significant adaptive shortening of the tendons, necessitating adhesiolysis and tendon lengthening. Juvenile fibromatosis of the flexor tendons is an exceedingly rare cause of progressive digital contracture in children. Early recognition and surgical intervention with rehabilitation can preserve hand function.

Juvenile fibromatosis is a rare fibroproliferative disorder affecting children, with varied clinical presentations depending on the depth and anatomical location of involvement. Superficial forms, such as juvenile palmar or plantar fibromatosis, are relatively well recognized, whereas deep fibromatosis involving the flexor tendons of the hand is distinctly uncommon. The disease is characterized by locally infiltrative but nonmetastatic proliferation of fibroblasts with collagen deposition, often resulting in progressive functional limitation if left untreated.1,2 This report described a rare case of flexor tendon fibromatosis in a child presenting with progressive flexion contracture.

Juvenile hyaline fibromatosis rarely involves the hand with direct infiltration of flexor or extensor tendons, leading to functional compromise. Owing to the extreme rarity of this presentation, there is currently no standardized surgical or reconstructive algorithm described in the literature. This case is presented to highlight the reconstructive challenges associated with tendon-involved juvenile hyaline fibromatosis and to propose a practical surgical strategy for functional preservation in pediatric patients.

CASE PRESENTATION

An 8-year-old girl was brought by her mother with progressive deformity of the left middle finger, first noticed at 2 years of age. Over time, she developed increasing difficulty extending her finger and grasping objects. There was no history of trauma, systemic illness, or family history of similar problems.

On examination, there was severe flexion contracture at the proximal interphalangeal and distal interphalangeal joints of the middle finger, both joints at 45 degrees of flexion, with no active or passive extension possible. The index finger showed a mild proximal interphalangeal joint contracture with 90 degrees of flexion; there were no scars, nodularity, or palpable masses. The rest of the digits of the right hand were normal (Fig. 1). Physical examination of the contralateral hand and the rest of the systemic examination was unremarkable.

Left-hand anterior view showing severe flexion contracture of the middle finger at both the proximal interphalangeal and distal interphalangeal joints and flexion contracture of the index finger at the proximal interphalangeal joint.

Laboratory results of complete blood count and serum electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride, and calcium) were within normal ranges. Radiographs of the hand showed flexion deformity of the middle finger without underlying bony changes (Fig. 2).

Left-hand x-ray. Lateral view showing flexion deformity of the middle finger and index finger, with other digits extended.

The patient underwent surgical exploration of the flexor tendons for severe middle finger flexion contracture. A proximal-to-distal zigzag incision was used to achieve adequate exposure. Intraoperatively, extensive fibrosis, thickening, dense adhesions, and shortening of the flexor tendons of the middle and ring fingers were identified, extending from zone III to zone V (Fig. 3). Adhesiolysis and excision of fibrotic tissue were performed, followed by tendon lengthening, resulting in restoration of full passive extension. No significant skin contracture or tethering was noted; therefore, adjunctive skin release procedures were not required. The wound was closed, and a volar flexion-blocking splint was applied.

Intraoperative image of the left hand with thickened and fibrosed flexor tendons of the middle finger and index finger.

Histopathology of the excised tissue confirmed juvenile fibromatosis, demonstrating proliferation of fibroblasts within a collagenous stroma without malignant features (Fig. 4).

Histopathology image showing a well-circumscribed but locally infiltrative fibrous lesion composed of spindle-shaped cells arranged in fascicles. The stroma is variably collagenized with alternating cellular areas; the findings are consistent with fibromatosis.

The wound healed uneventfully, and splinting was maintained for three weeks, followed by a structured physiotherapy program. Early follow-up demonstrated improved function with gradual recovery of hand use. At 7-month follow-up, the wound had healed completely, with no significant recurrence or contracture. (Supplemental Digital Content 1, which describes 7 mo postoperative follow-up images showing significant improvement in hand function, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F21.) The patient was able to write and perform activities of daily living without significant difficulty, with restoration of a functional grip. (Supplemental Digital Content 2, which describes 7 mo postoperative follow-up images showing significant improvement in hand function, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F22.) The patient and her family expressed satisfaction with both the aesthetic and functional outcomes. The patient continues physiotherapy and remains under regular follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Juvenile hyaline fibromatosis is a rare fibroproliferative disorder with limited reports involving the hand, and even fewer describing direct tendon involvement leading to functional impairment. The present case highlights both the reconstructive challenge and a practical surgical approach to restoring hand function in this rare scenario.

Juvenile fibromatosis encompasses a spectrum of benign fibroblastic proliferations in children and is commonly classified into superficial and deep types.2,3 Palmar fibromatosis in adults is well described; however, pediatric cases are considerably rarer and typically present as nodular lesions rather than tendon-associated contractures.1,4

Our patient demonstrated progressive contracture of the middle finger, initially mimicking a congenital deformity or sequelae of trauma. However, the insidious onset, absence of preceding injury, and confirmatory histopathologic findings supported the diagnosis of juvenile fibromatosis. Direct flexor tendon involvement is exceptionally rare in children, with most reported cases limited to isolated nodular or fascial disease without functional tendon compromise.1,4,5

Preoperative imaging modalities such as ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging may assist in delineating soft tissue extent, tendon involvement, and depth of infiltration in cases of unexplained digital contracture. Although imaging may not alter the need for surgical intervention in cases with established functional impairment, it may aid in surgical planning and patient counseling.

The mainstay of management for symptomatic juvenile fibromatosis is surgical excision of fibrous tissue with release of adhesions and tendon reconstruction or lengthening when required. Incomplete excision is associated with a higher risk of recurrence.3,5 Mild joint contractures may respond to intralesional steroid injections and physiotherapy in selected cases.6 Postoperative splinting and structured hand therapy remain essential to maintain tendon glide, prevent stiffness, and optimize functional recovery.4

Although adjuvant therapies such as radiotherapy have been described in selected cases of aggressive fibromatosis, their use in pediatric hand fibromatosis is limited by insufficient evidence and concerns regarding long-term adverse effects. Currently, there is no established adjuvant therapy proven to prevent recurrence in localized juvenile fibromatosis of the hand, and management remains primarily surgical with close follow-up.

This case underscores the importance of including juvenile fibromatosis in the differential diagnosis of progressive finger contractures in children, as timely recognition and appropriate surgical intervention may restore function and prevent long-term disability.

This case adds to current knowledge by highlighting an exceptionally rare presentation of juvenile hyaline fibromatosis with direct tendon involvement in the hand and by demonstrating a feasible tendon-preserving reconstructive strategy with functional recovery. To our knowledge, detailed descriptions of surgical management for tendon-infiltrating juvenile hyaline fibromatosis of the hand are lacking in the existing literature. This report provides a practical reference for reconstructive surgeons facing similar rare presentations and underscores the importance of early functional reconstruction and structured follow-up.

CONCLUSIONS

Juvenile fibromatosis involving the flexor tendons of the hand is extremely rare in children. It should be suspected in cases of progressive digital contracture without trauma or bony abnormalities. Complete excision, tendon release, and structured rehabilitation can yield excellent outcomes; nevertheless, recurrence is the rule, not an exception.
