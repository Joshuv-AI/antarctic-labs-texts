Lnk Governs chemokine-directed trafficking of CD8+ T cells in melanoma
Authors: Yael Derdikman Ofir, Miran Aswad, Michal Hayun, Antonina Pechkovsky, Ghazal Kheshaiboun, Narmeen Ghanayiem, Yasmine Khier, Yaniv Zohar, Yishai Ofran, Igal Louria-Hayon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lnk is an adaptor protein that attenuates cytokine receptor signaling in hematopoietic and immune cells, but its role in the behavior of CD8+ T cells within solid tumors is not well defined. Using a murine melanoma model, we compared tumor growth and immune infiltration in Lnk-deficient mice and in wild-type controls and evaluated CD8+ T cell trafficking toward chemokine signals produced by melanoma cells. Lnk-deficient mice developed smaller tumors containing greater numbers of intratumoral CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. Tumor chemokine profiles, including high levels of interleukin-8 family signals such as CXCL2, were comparable between groups, yet CD8+ T cells lacking Lnk showed enhanced migration toward melanoma-derived cues ex vivo and accumulated more effectively within tumors in vivo. Pharmacologic inhibition of CXCR1/2 abolished this migratory advantage. Upon stimulation with interleukin-8 family chemokines, Lnk-deficient CD8+ T cells exhibited increased activation of STAT3 and ERK signaling pathways. Moreover, antisense-mediated downregulation of Lnk in wild-type T cells resulted in enhanced accumulation within melanoma tumors following adoptive transfer into wild-type hosts. These findings identify Lnk as a negative regulator of CXCR1/2-dependent trafficking of CD8+ T cells in melanoma and suggest that modulating this intracellular checkpoint may improve T cell trafficking into solid tumors and enhance the effectiveness of adoptive cellular immunotherapies.

Introduction

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) play a central role in antitumor immunity by directly killing malignant cells and orchestrating broader immune responses within the tumor microenvironment (TME).1 Among them, CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) are key effectors whose presence within tumors strongly correlate with improved patient survival and response to immunotherapy.2 However, their therapeutic potential is often constrained by barriers that limit trafficking, retention, and spatial distribution within solid tumors.

While T-cell-based immunotherapies have shown remarkable success in hematologic cancers, their efficacy in solid tumors remains limited.3,4,5 This limitation stems from structural and microenvironmental barriers that restrict T cell trafficking and infiltration, as well as from limited persistence and progressive functional exhaustion of CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment.6 Effector CD8+ T cells often accumulate at the tumor periphery but fail to penetrate the core due to abnormal vasculature, dense extracellular matrix (ECM), and immunosuppressive stroma.7,8,9 In addition to acting as a physical barrier, ECM components can also bind and sequester cytokines and chemokines, thereby shaping immune cell trafficking and further limiting effective antitumor responses.10 Clinical studies in colorectal carcinoma and preclinical tumor models demonstrate that high CTL densities within both the invasive margin and tumor core strongly correlate with improved survival.2,11 Thus, overcoming spatial barriers to T cell migration is essential for enhancing immunotherapy efficacy.12,13

Chemokines and their receptors play pivotal roles in directing lymphocyte migration into and within tumors.14,15 Members of the interleukin (IL)-8 (CXCL8) chemokine family, including MIP-2 (CXCL2) and LIX (CXCL5), signal through the CXCR1 and CXCR2 chemokine receptors to regulate leukocyte trafficking and activation.16,17,18,19 While the IL-8-CXCR1/2 axis has been extensively studied in cancer cells and myeloid populations,20,21 its role in modulating CD8+ T cell migration within the TME remains poorly understood.22,23,24 Given that IL-8 family chemokines are often abundantly expressed in melanoma and other tumors,19,25 elucidating their impact on CD8+ T cell positioning and function may inform strategies for improving immune infiltration into tumor cores.

Lnk (SH2B3) is an intracellular adaptor protein best known as a negative regulator of cytokine receptor signaling, including the JAK-STAT and MAPK pathways26,27,28,29, as well as γc cytokine signaling in T cells.30 Structurally, Lnk contains an N-terminal dimerization (DD) domain, a central Pleckstrin homology (PH) domain that enables membrane targeting via phospholipid binding, and a C-terminal Src homology 2 (SH2) domain that binds phosphorylated tyrosine motifs on signaling partners to inhibit downstream signaling (Figure 1A). Loss of Lnk function has been implicated in altered immune cell development,31,32 enhanced hematopoietic stem cell proliferation,33 and dysregulated inflammatory responses.34,35 Recent studies have further highlighted the broad immunoregulatory role of Lnk across distinct immune and cancerous compartments: its deficiency attenuates the suppressive function of tumor-associated myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) via ferroptosis,36 enhances cytokine responsiveness in T cells, accelerates autoimmune diabetes,30 and suppresses interferon signaling within melanoma cells to promote immune evasion.29 Previous work has shown that Lnk constrains T cell activation in certain contexts,29,37 but its role in controlling chemokine-receptor-driven migration in CD8+ T cells has not been defined.Figure 1Lnk deficiency suppresses melanoma tumor growth and enhances immune cell infiltration in the TME(A) Structure of Lnk (SH2B3), a 66 kDa adaptor protein comprising three domains: DD (dimerization domain), PH (Pleckstrin homology domain), and SH2 (Src homology 2 domain). Based on its known regulatory functions and the critical importance of T cell trafficking into solid tumors, we hypothesized that Lnk negatively regulates chemokine-mediated signaling in CD8+ T cells, thereby limiting their migration and infiltration into the tumor. Here, using the B16-F10 melanoma model in Lnk-deficient (Lnk−/−) and wild-type (WT) mice, we examined how the loss of Lnk affects CD8+ T cell trafficking, accumulation, chemokine responsiveness, and downstream signaling in solid tumors. Our findings support a role for Lnk in restraining IL-8-CXCR1/2-driven migration and infiltration of CD8+ T cells in the TME, with implications for strategies aimed at improving immune trafficking into poorly accessible solid tumors.

Results

Lnk deficiency enhances tumor immune infiltration and suppresses melanoma tumor growth

To investigate the role of Lnk in antitumor immunity, we subcutaneously implanted B16-F10 melanoma cells into Lnk−/− and WT C57BL/6 mice and assessed their capacity to sustain tumor growth. After 14 days, the tumors were excised and analyzed (Figure 1B). Notably, tumors harvested from Lnk−/− mice appeared significantly smaller than those from WT controls (Figure 1C). Quantitative analysis confirmed a marked reduction in tumor mass and volume in Lnk−/− mice compared with their WT counterparts (Figures 1D and 1E). Given that the implanted tumor cells were identical in both groups, we hypothesized that alterations in the TME drive these differences. To explore this hypothesis, we examined tumor morphology via H&E staining. Tumors from Lnk−/− mice showed pronounced infiltration of inflammatory cells, predominantly lymphocytes, which were particularly concentrated at the tumor interphase region (Figure 1F). In contrast, tumors from WT mice displayed minimal inflammatory infiltration at the interphase zone. To characterize this infiltrating lymphocyte population further, we conducted flow cytometric analysis via size and granularity parameters (forward scatter [FSC] and side scatter [SSC], respectively). As depicted in Figures 1G and 1H, tumors from Lnk−/− mice had a significantly greater frequency of TILs than did those from WT controls. These findings suggest that the absence of Lnk enhances immune cell infiltration within the TME, thereby contributing to the impaired tumor growth observed in Lnk-deficient hosts.

Tumor-specific immune modulation in Lnk−/− mice is associated with enhanced T cell accumulation

To identify which immune cell populations account for the increased lymphocyte infiltration in Lnk−/− hosts, we performed phenotypic analysis via flow cytometry (Figure 2A; Figure S1A). Within the lymphocyte compartment, we observed a marked enrichment of CD3+ T cells in the tumors of Lnk−/− hosts compared with those in the WT controls. Among T cells, CD8+ CTLs showed the greatest increase, while more modest but consistent increases were observed in CD4+ helper T cells, B220+ B cells, and NK1.1+ NK cells. In contrast, analysis of tumor-infiltrating myeloid populations (Figure 2B; Figure S1B) revealed only a selective increase in CD11b+Gr-1− neutrophils, whereas the frequencies of CD11b+Gr-1+ MDSCs, F4/80+ macrophages, and CD11c+ dendritic cells remained comparable between the two genotypes. To determine whether this selective accumulation of lymphocytes was tumor specific, we examined lymphoid and myeloid populations in the spleens of tumor-bearing mice (Figures 2C and 2D; Figures S1A and S1B). As previously reported, Lnk−/− mice display a mild elevation in splenic B cell frequencies due to hematopoietic dysregulation31; however, the proportions of splenic CD3+ T cells and NK1.1+ natural killer (NK) cells (Figure 2C), as well as the proportions of MDSCs, macrophages, and dendritic cells (Figure 2D), were not significantly different between the genotypes. To determine whether the observed enrichment of T cells in tumors of Lnk−/− hosts reflects a T-cell-intrinsic-tumor-specific effect rather than systemic immune dysregulation, we performed adoptive transfer experiments into WT hosts. Antisense-mediated downregulation of Lnk in WT T cells (Figure S2) demonstrated that ex vivo Lnk knockdown alone is sufficient to enhance T cell accumulation within B16-F10 tumors (Figure S3). Lnk deficiency enhances tumor-specific accumulation of CD8+ T cells

To further characterize the enhanced T cell infiltration observed in the tumors of Lnk−/− mice, we first compared the ratio of CD8+ to CD4+ T cells within the TIL population. Compared with those from WT controls, tumors from Lnk−/− hosts presented a significant 3-fold increase in the CD8+/CD4+ T cell ratio (Figure 3A). Since Lnk deficiency has previously been associated with increased cellular proliferation,31,37 we examined whether increased proliferative activity could account for the accumulation of CD8+ T cells in the tumors of Lnk−/− mice. To test this hypothesis, we performed an in vivo BrdU incorporation assay and assessed BrdU uptake in CD8+ T cells from tumors, spleens, tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs), and bone marrow. As shown in Figure 3C (and Figure S1C), no significant differences were detected in the proliferative status of CD8+ T cells between genotypes at any of these sites.

Together, these findings indicate that the increased abundance of CD8+ T cells in tumors of Lnk−/− hosts is not driven by enhanced proliferation, supporting a role for Lnk in regulating T cell accumulation within the tumor microenvironment, likely through effects on tumor-directed trafficking.

Lnk deficiency enhances CD8+ T cell migration through the IL-8-CXCR1/2 axis

To assess whether differences in secreted mediators underline the enhanced immune infiltration observed in the tumors of Lnk−/− mice, we profiled tumor supernatants using a cytokine array (Figure S4) and validated selected analytes by ELISA (Figure 4A). The cytokine array served as a semi-quantitative discovery screen and revealed broadly similar profiles between genotypes, including robust detection of IL-8 superfamily chemokines, particularly MIP-2/CXCL2 and LIX/CXCL5. ELISA confirmed that MIP-2 concentrations were significantly greater than those of IL-6 and IL-10 in both genotypes but did not differ significantly between WT and Lnk−/−-derived tumors (Figure 4A). Given the established role of IL-8-like chemokines and their receptors CXCR1 and CXCR2 in melanoma progression18,19,20,21,38 and the limited understanding of how tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells respond to this axis,18,22,23,24,39 we first assessed CXCR1 and CXCR2 surface expression on CD8+ T cells (Figure S1D). We found comparable expression levels between genotypes, indicating that differential receptor abundance is unlikely to account for the enhanced T cell accumulation observed (Figures 4B and 4C). We next tested whether Lnk deficiency affects T cell migration toward melanoma cues through the IL-8-CXCR1/2 axis. For this purpose, a chemotaxis Boyden-chamber-based assay combined with CXCR1/2 inhibitor was utilized (Figures 4D–4G). To better simulate the physical and chemical complexity of the TME, we modified the Boyden chamber assay by coating the bottom-outside surface of the inserts with a thin layer of Matrigel—a matrix rich in basement membrane proteins that provides physical guidance to migrating cells.12,40,41 Chemotactic signals were introduced by coculturing TDLN cells in the upper chamber with live B16-F10 melanoma cells in the lower chamber (Figure 4D).

After 16 h, flow cytometric analysis of the cells that migrated through the Matrigel-coated membrane (Figure S1E) revealed significantly greater invasion of Lnk−/− T cells in response to the melanoma-derived cues than under basal conditions and in WT control T cells. This response was specific to CD8+ T cells, with no difference observed in CD4+ T cell migration. Importantly, pretreatment with the CXCR1/2 inhibitor SCH563705 abrogated the enhanced invasion of Lnk−/− CD8+ T cells but had no significant effect on WT T cells or on CD4+ subsets of either genotype (Figures 4E–4G). These findings suggest that the Lnk adaptor protein restrains the CXCR1/2-dependent migratory responsiveness of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells to melanoma-secreted chemokines.

Lnk−/− CD8+ T cells exhibit enhanced in vivo invasion in response to Mip-2

Following our ex vivo findings, we next evaluated whether Lnk-deficient CD8+ T cells exhibit enhanced migratory responses to IL-8-like signals in vivo. To this end, we developed an adoptive transfer model combined with subcutaneous implantation of Matrigel plugs42 preloaded with the murine IL-8 homolog Mip-2 (CXCL2), as depicted in Figure 5A. Fluorescently labeled CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of tumor-bearing WT or Lnk−/− donors and transferred into WT recipient mice. Recipients were simultaneously implanted with Matrigel plugs supplemented with either Mip-2, SDF-1α (a well-established T cell chemoattractant,43 serving as a positive control), or ultrapure water (negative control). Notably, Lnk−/− CD8+ T cells accumulated at significantly greater numbers in Mip-2-containing plugs than in both the SDF-1α and vehicle control plugs, as did WT CD8+ T cells exposed to Mip-2; furthermore, Lnk−/− cells infiltrated to the Mip-2-containing plugs at significantly higher levels than their WT counterparts (Figure 5B).

Lnk−/− CD8+ T cells display enhanced activation of STAT3 and ERK1/2 upon IL-8 stimulation, downstream of CXCR1/2

IL-8 signaling through CXCR1 and CXCR2 activates multiple intracellular cascades, including the JAK-STAT and MAPK pathways, that orchestrate migration, proliferation, survival, and other context-dependent cellular programs.41,44,45 Among these, STAT3 and ERK1/2 are key mediators, and both have been identified as targets of negative regulation by Lnk in several cytokine receptor systems.26,31,46 Our findings indicate that Lnk suppresses T cell migration through a CXCR1/2-dependent mechanism (Figures 4E–4G). We therefore hypothesized that Lnk constrains CXCR1/2-driven signaling in CD8+ T cells by limiting STAT3 and ERK1/2 activation.

To test this hypothesis, we purified CD8+ T cells from the spleens of tumor-bearing WT and Lnk−/− mice, serum-starved them for 5 h, and stimulated them with recombinant IL-8 (100 ng/mL, 5 min). Data supporting the proposed pathway were obtained from isolated CD8+ T cells derived from the spleens of tumor-bearing mice. ∗This schematic model (C) was created in https://BioRender.com.

The convergence of the following findings—the comparable cytokine composition of the TME (Figure S4), the comparable CXCR1/2 levels between genotypes (Figures 4B and 4C), the complete loss of the migratory advantage of Lnk−/− CD8+ T cells upon CXCR1/2 blockade (Figure 4F), and the increased phosphorylation of STAT3 and ERK1/2 following IL-8 stimulation (Figures 6A and 6B)—support the interpretation that Lnk regulates IL-8-CXCR1/2-driven responses primarily at the level of downstream signal transduction.

Taken together, these results position Lnk as a negative regulator of CXCR1/2 signaling in CD8+ T cells through attenuation of STAT3 and ERK1/2 activation, thereby modulating their migratory and functional responses in the tumor context. This mechanistic link is summarized in the proposed signaling model (Figure 6C), which integrates our biochemical and functional findings.

Discussion

CD8+ CTLs are central mediators of antitumor immunity through direct tumor cell killing and cytokine secretion. Effective antitumor responses require not only adequate CTL numbers but also efficient trafficking and persistence within the TME. Impaired T cell trafficking into tumors remains a major barrier to successful immunotherapy.2,11,15

In this study, we identify the adaptor protein Lnk as a negative regulator of CD8+ T cell accumulation within melanoma tumors. Using complementary in vivo and ex vivo assays, we demonstrate that Lnk deficiency enhances CD8+ T cell migration toward tumor-derived IL-8 family chemokines, particularly Mip-2 (CXCL2), through a CXCR1/2-dependent mechanism. This phenotype was tumor specific, as systemic lymphoid and myeloid compartments remained largely unaltered, indicating localized regulation within the TME rather than global immune skewing.

Mechanistically, Lnk−/− CD8+ T cells exhibit increased STAT3 and ERK1/2 activation downstream of CXCR1/2, while Mip-2 and CXCR1/2 levels within tumors were comparable between genotypes. Pharmacologic CXCR1/2 blockade completely abolished the enhanced migration of Lnk−/− CD8+ T cells, supporting a model in which Lnk restrains signaling downstream of these receptors. This aligns with previous studies showing that Lnk negatively regulates JAK-STAT and MAPK pathways across diverse cytokine receptor contexts.26,31,46 While Lnk deficiency has been associated with heightened T cell responsiveness in inflammatory settings,30 in the tumor context this phenotype translates into enhanced tumor-directed migration and accumulation.

Importantly, the increased intratumoral abundance of CD8+ T cells in Lnk−/− mice was not associated with increased proliferation, indicating that enhanced accumulation is driven by migratory mechanisms rather than local expansion. This interpretation is supported by our adoptive transfer experiments, in which ex vivo Lnk knockdown in T cells was sufficient to enhance their accumulation within tumors in a fully WT microenvironment (Figure S3). Together, these findings support a direct, cell-intrinsic role for Lnk in regulating tumor-directed T cell trafficking.

We also observed increased B cell frequencies in tumors from Lnk−/− mice, consistent with the known hematopoietic effects of global Lnk deficiency. Because B cells can influence CD8+ T cell responses through cytokine production, antigen presentation, and regulatory interactions, we cannot fully exclude a contributory role for B cell-T cell crosstalk in the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, because the Lnk−/− mice used in this study are germline global knockouts, we cannot fully exclude contributions from other Lnk-deficient host compartments to the observed phenotype. However, this possibility is mitigated by our adoptive transfer experiment performed entirely in WT hosts, in which ex vivo Lnk knockdown in T cells was sufficient to enhance their accumulation within B16-F10 tumors (Figure S3). Because both the host and the tumor microenvironment were WT in this setting, these data support a predominantly T-cell-intrinsic role for Lnk in regulating tumor-directed T cell trafficking.

Additionally, previous reports have shown that Lnk deficiency in MDSCs induces ferroptosis and reduces their immunosuppressive activity,36 further enhancing CD8+ T cell function. Collectively, our findings establish Lnk as an intracellular checkpoint of CXCR1/2-mediated migration in CD8+ T cells. Targeting this regulatory axis could improve the efficacy of T-cell-based immunotherapies, particularly in IL-8-secreting tumors. Combining CXCR1/2 receptor engineering18,23,39 with intracellular modulation of Lnk may further augment T cell trafficking and tumor infiltration.

Beyond immune cells, Lnk also exerts tumor-intrinsic effects by promoting immune evasion and growth. In melanoma, Lnk suppresses interferon-STAT1 signaling and antigen presentation,29 while in ovarian and triple-negative breast cancers it enhances AKT/MAPK and JAK/STAT3 signaling, proliferation, and metastasis.47,48 Collectively, these findings position Lnk as a regulator operating across T cell, myeloid, and tumor compartments, with its loss consistently shifting the balance toward enhanced antitumor immunity.

Although the cytokine array identified several additional soluble factors of potential interest, this analysis was used primarily as an exploratory screen to guide mechanistic follow-up. Our subsequent validation and functional experiments converged specifically on the CXCR1/2 axis: IL-8-family chemokines were robustly detected in tumor supernatants, their abundance did not differ significantly between genotypes by ELISA, receptor expression on CD8+ T cells was comparable, and the enhanced migration of Lnk-deficient CD8+ T cells was abrogated by CXCR1/2 inhibition and reproduced in vivo in response to Mip-2. Together, these findings support a model in which Lnk primarily restrains downstream chemokine responsiveness in CD8+ T cells. We cannot exclude the possibility that other soluble factors also contribute to the overall phenotype and should be explored in future studies. In conclusion, our study identifies Lnk as a regulator of the CXCR1/2-IL-8 axis in T cells and highlights it as a potential target to enhance T cell accumulation in solid tumors for immunotherapeutic applications.

Materials and methods

Mice

WT C57BL/6JOlaHSD mice (RRID:MGI:2164189) were purchased from Harlan Laboratories Israel. Lnk−/− mice (a gift from the laboratory of Dr. Tony Pawson, Mount Sinai Hospital, Toronto, Canada) were backcrossed for 10 generations onto a C57BL/6J-Ly5.2-Gpi1b/b background26,31; no RRID is available for this strain.

Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups based on genotype. Both male and female mice aged 6–8 weeks were used, in approximately equal proportions unless otherwise specified. Inclusion criteria included normal appearance, expected baseline weight, and regular activity. Exclusion criteria included ulcerated tumors involving >10% of the tumor surface area and any signs of unrelated illness. No animals were excluded unless otherwise stated.

Mice were housed under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with controlled temperature, humidity, and a 12-h light/dark cycle, with unrestricted access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Technion – Rappaport Faculty of Medicine and are reported in accordance with ARRIVE v.2.0 guidelines.

Cell lines and culture

B16-F10 murine melanoma cells (RRID:CVCL_0159), originally obtained as a gift from Dr. Michal Lotem (Sharett Institute of Oncology, Hadassah Medical Organization, Jerusalem), were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Biological Industries, Israel) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS), 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cultures were kept at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. All cell cultures tested negative for mycoplasma contamination and were used within 6–8 passages from thawing.

Tumor implantation and measurements

For subcutaneous tumor implantation, 1.4 × 106 B16-F10 cells were used based on pilot calibration experiments, which established this inoculum as suitable for reproducible tumor formation by the planned day-14 endpoint in both genotypes. Cells were resuspended in 0.2 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Biological Industries) and injected at the lower back. This tumor model was designed as a single-endpoint experiment to enable synchronized tissue harvest for downstream flow cytometry, histology, and mechanistic assays. Fourteen days later, mice were euthanized, and tumors, spleens, and inguinal TDLNs were collected and processed into single-cell suspensions as described. Tumor length and width were measured with a manual caliper at endpoint, and tumor volume was calculated as (L × W2)/2, with L and W denoting length and width in centimeters. Tumors were weighed before further processing.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions

Spleens and inguinal TDLNs were mechanically dissociated and passed through 40-μm cell strainers. Tumors and Matrigel plugs were minced and digested in HBSS containing collagenase IV (Sigma, Cat# C5138), DNase I (Sigma, Cat# D5025), and hyaluronidase V (Sigma, Cat# H6254) for 60 min at 37°C with continuous magnetic stirring.42 After digestion, samples were centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were stored at −20°C for cytokine analysis. Cell pellets were washed and used for flow cytometry or downstream functional assays. Red blood cells were lysed using ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) buffer when necessary.

For experiments requiring purified CD8+ T cells, splenic lymphocytes were isolated by negative magnetic selection using the EasySep Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat# 19853), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions (1–5 × 106 cells) were washed and resuspended in cold PBS containing 2% FCS and 1 mM EDTA. To minimize nonspecific binding, all staining steps were performed at 4°C in the presence of anti-mouse CD16/32 (Fc Block; BioLegend, Cat# 101302, RRID:AB_312801). Cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD3, CD4, CD8α, CD19, B220, NK1.1, CXCR1, and CXCR2 from BioLegend, BD Biosciences, and Miltenyi Biotec. Full antibody information, including clones, fluorophores, catalog numbers, RRIDs, and working dilutions, is provided in Table S1.

Samples were acquired on an LSRFortessa II (BD Biosciences) or on an Attune NxT cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed in FlowJo v.10.0.7 (BD Biosciences, RRID:SCR_008520). Compensation was performed using single-stained controls, and gating strategies were defined using fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls when appropriate.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were initially identified based on FSC/SSC scatter characteristics and singlet gating, followed by sequential lineage gating to define specific immune subsets (Figure S1). A dedicated CD45 marker and viability dye were not included in the original tumor immune phenotyping panel and are acknowledged as limitations of that dataset.

Histology

Formalin-fixed tumors were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for routine morphological assessment.

BrdU proliferation assay (in vivo)

To assess the proliferation of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, melanoma-bearing WT and Lnk−/− mice were injected intraperitoneally with 2 mg BrdU on day 14 of tumor growth. Two hours after injection, mice were euthanized, and tumors, spleens, inguinal TDLNs, and bone marrow were collected and processed into single-cell suspensions. BrdU incorporation in CD8+ T cells was measured using the APC BrdU Flow Kit (BD Pharmingen, Cat# 557892, RRID:AB_2861367) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were acquired on an LSRFortessa II cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo v.10.0.7 (BD Biosciences, RRID:SCR_008520).

Cytokine analysis

Tumor-derived supernatants were analyzed using the Proteome Profiler Mouse XL Cytokine Array (R&D Systems, Cat# ARY028. See supplemental methods). Fourteen days after tumor implantation, B16-F10 tumors were harvested and processed into single-cell suspensions as described above. Following centrifugation, the supernatants were collected, stored at −20°C, and later pooled from three mice per genotype. Before use, pooled samples were clarified by an additional centrifugation step and passed through a filter to remove residual debris.

The same tumor supernatants tested in the array were used to quantify Mip-2, IL-6, and IL-10 concentrations by ELISA using RayBiotech and BioLegend assay kits, with full reagent information provided in Table S1. Absorbance values were read on a BioTek microplate reader and analyzed with Gen5 software (BioTek, RRID:SCR_017317).

Chemotaxis assays

Modified chemotaxis assay (Matrigel-coated Transwell system)

To assess CXCR1/2-dependent migration, we used a Matrigel-modified chemotaxis assay. B16-F10 melanoma cells (0.4 × 106 cells per well) were seeded into 24-well plates (Sigma, Cat# M8812) and allowed to adhere. Single-cell suspensions of inguinal TDLNs from tumor-bearing WT and Lnk−/− mice were prepared and either pretreated or left untreated with the CXCR1/2 inhibitor SCH563705 (500 nM; AdooQ).49

For insert preparation (3.0 μm pore size; Millipore, Cat# MCSP24H48), Matrigel (Corning, Cat# 354234) was diluted 1:1 with cold PBS and kept on ice to prevent premature solidification.41 Inserts were placed upside down on the lid of a 24-well plate, and 100 μL of the diluted Matrigel was gently applied to the outer membrane surface. The plate was covered and incubated at 37°C for 4 h. After solidification, inserts were briefly rinsed and transferred into the Transwell system.

TDLN cells (0.25 × 106 per insert) were added to the coated inserts positioned above the B16-F10 cultures. Cocultures were incubated for 16 h. Cells that migrated to the lower chamber were collected, stained for CD3, CD4, and CD8α, and quantified using an Attune NxT acoustic focusing cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Full reagent information is provided in Table S1.

In vivo Matrigel invasion assay

To evaluate in vivo recruitment of CD8+ T cells, we combined adoptive cell transfer with the Matrigel plug assay.42 B16-F10 melanoma cells were injected subcutaneously into WT and Lnk−/− donor mice, and on day 14 of tumor growth the spleens were collected and processed into single-cell suspensions. CD8+ T cells were purified by negative magnetic selection (EasySep Mouse CD8+ T cell Isolation Kit; STEMCELL Technologies, Cat# 19853), labeled with CellTrace Violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# C34557) for 30 min at 37°C, resuspended in 1× PBS at a concentration of 8 × 106 cells/mL, and transferred intravenously via the tail vein into WT recipient mice. Each recipient mouse received 200 μL of cell suspension containing 1.6 × 106 CellTrace Violet-labeled CD8+ T cells. Equal numbers of labeled cells were administered to all recipient mice. Each recipient mouse received CD8+ T cells from a single donor genotype (WT-to-WT and Lnk−/−-to-WT).

For the Matrigel plug assay, 0.3 mL of Matrigel (Corning, Cat# 354234) was mixed with 3 μg of Mip-2, SDF-1α, or ultra-pure water and injected subcutaneously into the flanks of recipient mice. Each mouse received a pair of plugs containing predefined chemokine combinations (left flank : right flank): water : water, Mip-2 : water, or SDF-1α : Mip-2. Twenty-four hours after plug implantation and cell transfer, each plug received a chemokine boost consisting of 1.6 μg of the corresponding chemokine in 50 μL PBS. Plugs were harvested the following day, digested enzymatically as described for tumor processing, and analyzed by flow cytometry (LSR Fortessa II, BD Biosciences) to quantify infiltration of CellTrace-labeled donor CD8+ T cells. Full reagent information is provided in Table S1.

IL-8-induced signaling assays

Splenic CD8+ T cells were isolated from tumor-bearing WT and Lnk−/− mice using negative magnetic selection (EasySep Mouse CD8+ T cell Isolation Kit; STEMCELL Technologies, Cat# 19853). Purified cells were serum-starved for 5 h in RPMI without supplements at 37°C, then placed at 4°C for 5 h to minimize basal signaling activity. Cells were stimulated with recombinant human IL-8 (CXCL8, 77 aa; PeproTech, Cat# 200-08-250) at 100 ng/mL for 5 min at 37°C and lysed immediately in sample buffer.

Lysates were analyzed by western blot to assess phosphorylation of STAT3 and ERK1/2. Phospho-specific antibodies against p-STAT3 (Abcam, Cat# ab76315, RRID:AB_1658549) and p-ERK1/2 (Cell Signaling Technologies, Cat# 9101, RRID:AB_331646) were used, with total STAT3 (CST, Cat# 4904, RRID:AB_331269) and total ERK1/2 (CST, Cat# 4696, RRID: AB_390780) serving as loading controls. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and full reagent details are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests with Welch’s correction. Multiple t tests were applied where appropriate, including analyses of splenic immune phenotyping, BrdU incorporation, and ELISA measurements. Ordinary two-way ANOVA with post hoc testing for multiple comparisons was used for the chemotaxis and in vivo Matrigel infiltration assays. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with the following thresholds: ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Sample sizes were based on prior experience with this tumor model. Outcome assessment was not performed under blinded conditions. All analyses were performed using Prism v.8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, RRID:SCR_002798).
