Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Replacement in Torrential Tricuspid Regurgitation With Severe Right Ventricular Dysfunction
Authors: Jocelyn Chai, Brian Chiang, Gnalini Sathananthan, Robert Boone, Anson Cheung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTorrential tricuspid regurgitation (TR) with right heart failure poses management challenges, particularly in patients ineligible for surgery or transcatheter edge-to-edge repair.Case SummaryA 61-year-old man with torrential TR, severe right ventricular (RV) dilation (6.2 cm), and severe RV dysfunction presented with NYHA functional class IV symptoms. He was refractory to diuretics and inotropes and was declined for surgery, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, and transplant. He underwent transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement with the LuX-Valve Plus. Postprocedure echocardiography showed improved RV end-diastolic area, RV fractional area change, and RV strain, with RV function improving to mild-moderate. He improved symptomatically but later died from unrelated complications.DiscussionThis case illustrates the feasibility of the LuX-Valve Plus in torrential TR with severe RV dysfunction and dilation. Procedural success and early signs of RV remodeling were achieved.Take-Home MessagesTranscatheter tricuspid valve replacement with the LuX-Valve Plus is viable in high-risk TR patients. Multiparametric RV assessment is essential for guiding intervention.

Past Medical History

The patient's past medical history included tricuspid regurgitation (TR) diagnosed 8 years ago, with longstanding isolated right heart failure secondary to severe TR (now progressed to torrential) from a combination of annular dilation, severe right atrial dilation, leaflet malcoaptation, and valvular disruption from his ventricular lead. A prior right ventricular (RV) biopsy was negative for infiltrative disorders. The working diagnosis was progressive right heart failure.

His history was also notable for a mechanical aortic valve (On-X valve) implanted 11 years before the current presentation for bicuspid aortic valve, complicated by infective endocarditis of this valve 4 years ago that was treated with a course of antibiotics. He had paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and a dual-chamber pacemaker inserted for symptomatic bradycardia, upgraded to cardiac resynchronization therapy-defibrillator after a ventricular fibrillation arrest 3 years ago. Previous coronary angiograms showed only nonobstructive coronary artery disease.

Differential Diagnosis

The differentials included mechanical aortic valve dysfunction (stenosis or regurgitation), thrombus, pannus formation, or infective endocarditis. This was assessed with inpatient transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showing torrential TR with no development of any other valvular pathologies compared with outpatient transesophageal echocardiography performed 2 months prior. Tachyarrhythmia-related cardiomyopathy was considered given the patient's history of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and ventricular fibrillation, but no events were demonstrated on interrogation of his cardiac resynchronization therapy-defibrillator, and his TTE continued to show preserved left ventricular function. Other noncardiac etiologies of anasarca, including liver and renal dysfunction (nephrotic syndrome), were excluded based on laboratory investigations.

Investigations

On hospital admission, the patient's laboratory investigations demonstrated acute on chronic kidney injury with a creatinine of 200 μmol/L (baseline: 150 μmol/L). His lactate was normal, but his N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide was elevated at 4,061 pg/mL (increased from previous level of 1,775 pg/mL). Right heart catheterization demonstrated a Fick cardiac output of 8.26 L/min, with a cardiac index of 3.67 mL/min/m2. His right atrial pressure was 22 mm Hg, pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) was 33 mm Hg (mean: 20 mm Hg), and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure was 19 mm Hg. Pulmonary artery pulsatility index was calculated at 1.0. His RV basal diameter was dilated even up to 6 months before his admission at 6.2 cm, with severe RV dysfunction (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion [TAPSE] of 14 mm; RV fractional area change of 26.1%; RV free wall strain of −8.0%) (Video 1). Two months prior, his RV basal diameter was unchanged at 6.3 cm. On admission day, his RV basal diameter measured 6.1 cm with severe RV dysfunction supported by inotropes (RV end-diastolic area: 56.8 cm2, RV fractional area change: 25.0%, TAPSE: 15 mm, and RV strain: −9.6%).

Management

The patient remained volume overloaded despite diuretics, milrinone, and intermittent hemodialysis. His weight continued to increase by up to 6 kg. He was declined for tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) owing to a large coaptation gap of 3.4 cm (Videos 2A to 2C). Given his severely dilated RV, he was declined for radial force–dependent transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) options. The heart transplant team declined cardiac transplantation on the basis of medical comorbidities (deconditioning, cachexia, renal dysfunction), and psychosocial factors. After multidisciplinary discussions, a 70-mm radial-independent LuX-Valve Plus (Jenscare Biotechnology) was implanted via the right internal jugular vein 3 weeks into his hospital course.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After the procedure, the patient developed transient vasoplegic shock. He received continuous renal replacement therapy for 2 days. Milrinone and epinephrine infusions were weaned off within 48 hours. His weight decreased 10 kg to his baseline dry weight, and he reported symptomatic improvement. TTE on postoperative day 11 demonstrated a well-seated valve with only mild paravalvular leak (Videos 3A and 3B). RV diameter and TAPSE were unchanged at 6.2 cm and 15 mm, respectively. His other RV parameters improved: RV end-diastolic area, 49.8 cm2; RV fractional area change, 31.7%; RV free wall strain, −14.0%; and RV S′, 6.1 cm/s. His RV function was reclassified as mild-moderate dysfunction by the echocardiographer.

Later during hospitalization, the patient developed new-onset seizures suspected to be embolic from his mechanical aortic valve. Six weeks in, he developed a spontaneous right-sided retroperitoneal bleed while on anticoagulation. Despite interventional radiology coil embolization, he remained in hemorrhagic shock. He was deemed unsuitable for surgery. The patient was transitioned to comfort care, and he died surrounded by his family members.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the potential of the LuX-Valve Plus in patients with torrential TR, severe RV dilation (6.2 cm), and severe RV dysfunction—all features that were exclusion criteria in early trials. The LuX-Valve Plus is a second-generation self-expanding, radial force–independent TTVR device. It has demonstrated long-term sustained reduction in TR, reverse remodeling of RV and hepatic structures, and improved survival rates when compared with medical therapy alone.1,2 Unlike T-TEER, which often results in residual TR, TTVR can offer complete elimination of TR, improving systemic decongestion.

The first-in-human study3 of this valve included 10 patients in China with a mean RV basal diameter of 38.3 mm, which is significantly smaller than in our patient. The etiologies of TR were quite similar to our patient, with left-sided valvular surgery, atrial fibrillation, and pacemaker implantation among the most common causes. Subsequent reports included RV diameter of 4.0 to 4.7 cm,4 and excluded those with left ventricular ejection fraction <50%, PASP ≥60 mm Hg, TAPSE <10 mm, and RV fractional area change <20%.4 In contrast, our patient had an RV diameter of 6.2 cm and a fractional area change of 25%, highlighting the feasibility of TTVR in more complex anatomies.

One retrospective study1 included 31 TTVR patients with RV basal diameter of 5.24 ± 0.91 cm and showed a significant decrease to 4.42 ± 0.50 cm at 6 months, suggestive of RV reverse remodeling. Though the RV basal diameter and TAPSE did not change within the hospitalization, improvements in RV end-diastolic area, RV fractional area change, and RV free wall strain suggest early remodeling. Many of these parameters are not routinely included in echocardiography reports, which emphasizes the limitations of TAPSE alone and supports the use of multiparametric RV assessment when evaluating patients for intervention.

Several studies have shown that RV-PA coupling, calculated as a ratio of TAPSE to PASP, is a prognostic marker reflecting the relationship between RV contractility and afterload after tricuspid intervention. RV-PA uncoupling of <0.41 to 0.55 mm/mm Hg is associated with impaired adaptation to pulmonary pressures and poorer postprocedural outcomes, including reduced 1-year survival outcomes.5,6 Our patient's baseline TAPSE/PASP ratio was approximately 0.42, consistent with uncoupling and high-risk physiology. In addition, he had other markers of elevated procedural risk, including a EuroSCORE II of 8.34% and a TRI-SCORE of 8. Despite this, he achieved procedural success, as defined1 as successful device implantation, patient survival, and postprocedural TR grade of moderate or better. His RV function improved from severe (6 months preadmission) to mild-moderate dysfunction postprocedure.

Determining when to intervene in torrential TR remains challenging, particularly in high-risk populations. In our case, the patient was refractory to high-dose diuretic treatment, a finding that has been reported in up to 30% of patients and associated with increased mortality.7,8 This often leads to progressive TR and further RV dysfunction. Although a TRI-SCORE of ≤6 has been associated with more favorable prognosis, the optimal threshold for intervention remains unclear.1 Our case suggests that TTVR with LuX-Valve Plus may be an option even in anatomically and functionally advanced cases.

Although our findings may apply to TTVR broadly, the LuX-Valve Plus is particularly suited to advanced TR with massive annular dilation because it offers a wider size range (50, 60, 70 mm) than other platforms such as the Evoque system (44, 48, 52 mm). This allows treatment of anatomies often deemed unsuitable for intervention, including those with severe RV remodeling and large annuli. Expanding valve size options across TTVR devices could extend feasibility to a greater proportion of high-risk TR patients.

Conclusions

This case highlights the challenges in selecting appropriate candidates for TTVR and underscores the need for standardized, multiparametric RV function assessment. Despite torrential TR, severe RV dilation and dysfunction, and high procedural risk, our patient achieved procedural success and early signs of RV remodeling with the LuX-Valve Plus. This device appears to be a feasible and safe option for high-risk patients who are not candidates for surgery or T-TEER. Further research is needed to clarify the optimal timing and criteria for patient selection.
