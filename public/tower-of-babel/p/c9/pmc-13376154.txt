AI-based performance feedback and coaching effectiveness: a moderated mediation model in football
Authors: Xiaohui Jiang, Yitong Zhang, Hao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative tool in professional sports coaching, yet its impact on psychological and strategic coaching outcomes remains insufficiently understood. This study investigates the influence of AI-Based Performance Feedback (AIPF) on Coaching Effectiveness (CE), incorporating Tactical Awareness (TA) and Coaching Self-Efficacy (CSE) as mediators and Coaching Tenure Perception (CTP) as a moderator. Using data from 512 professional football coaches in Henan, China, Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling was employed to test a moderated mediation model. The results reveal that AIPF significantly predicts CE both directly (β = 0.74, p < .001) and indirectly through TA (β = 0.61, p < .001) and CSE (β = 0.55, p < .001), indicating partial mediation. Technology Acceptance and Coach Digital Literacy were identified as significant antecedents of AIPF, indicating that coaches’ acceptance of AI tools and digital competence support engagement with AI-based feedback rather than directly predicting CE. Additionally, CTP significantly moderated the AIPF–TA relationship (β = 0.11, p = .018), suggesting that coaches with higher perceived tenure derive greater tactical insights from AI feedback. These findings provide theoretical contributions to the fields of educational technology and coaching psychology and offer practical implications for designing AI-driven coaching interventions that are both personalized and psychologically grounded.

Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into sports analytics and coaching processes has redefined traditional paradigms of athletic development, strategy, and performance optimization. In recent years, AI-driven feedback systems have gained prominence in professional football for their ability to deliver real-time insights, analyze complex patterns, and inform data-supported decision-making1; Huang and Yongquan2. These systems provide coaches with access to player statistics, tactical analysis, and predictive models that were not easily available through manual methods. As a result, coaching practices are becoming increasingly augmented by technology, which necessitates a deeper understanding of how AI-based performance feedback (AIPF) influences coaching effectiveness (CE).

While prior research has extensively explored the use of AI in sports performance prediction and injury prevention3,4, limited attention has been paid to how AI tools affect the coach’s psychological and strategic capabilities. In this context, coaching refers to a structured process through which coaches guide athletes’ technical, tactical, and psychological development to achieve individual and team performance goals. Coaching effectiveness (CE) refers to a coach’s ability to facilitate athletes’ development and performance through effective decision-making, communication, leadership, and the integration of technical, tactical, and psychological skills. Côté et al.,5. However, the mechanisms through which AI feedback influences this effectiveness remain understudied. Specifically, little is known about the cognitive and behavioral mediators that explain this relationship and how individual or contextual factors, such as experience, shape this process6.

One such mechanism is tactical awareness (TA), which refers to a coach’s ability to interpret in-game dynamics, adapt strategies in real time, and exploit spatial-temporal opportunities. AI feedback systems, by providing tactical heatmaps, spatial statistics, and dynamic visual cues, may enhance a coach’s situational awareness7. Thus, tactical awareness can be viewed as a cognitive mechanism that may mediate the relationship between AI-based performance feedback and coaching effectiveness. In addition to cognitive mechanisms, coaching self-efficacy (CSE) represents a key psychological pathway through which AI-based performance feedback may influence coaching effectiveness. Coaching self-efficacy refers to a coach’s belief in their ability to effectively plan, execute, and adapt coaching strategies. AI-generated insights can enhance this confidence by providing objective and data-driven support for decision-making. Therefore, coaching self-efficacy may also function as an important mediator linking AI-based performance feedback to coaching effectiveness. At the same time, coaching tenure, a reflection of a coach’s accumulated experience and strategic maturitymay shape how AI is interpreted and applied8. More experienced coaches may integrate AI insights differently compared to less-tenured coaches, potentially moderating the relationship between AI feedback and tactical awareness or effectiveness9.

To provide a clearer theoretical foundation, this study adopts an integrative cognitive–affective framework of technology-enabled coaching performance. The framework integrates the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and social cognitive theory to explain how AI-based coaching technologies influence coaching effectiveness. TAM explains the technology-readiness stage of the model, where coaches’ perceived usefulness, ease of use, trust, and intention to use AI systems shape their willingness to engage with AI-based feedback. However, technology acceptance alone is insufficient unless coaches possess the digital capability to interpret and apply AI-generated information. Therefore, coach digital literacy is included as a complementary capability condition that enables coaches to transform AI outputs into meaningful coaching insights.

Within this framework, AI-based performance feedback functions as an external informational resource that supports coaching decision-making. Drawing on social cognitive theory, this external resource affects coaching effectiveness through two internal mechanisms. The first is a cognitive mechanism, represented by tactical awareness, through which coaches interpret AI-generated feedback, identify tactical patterns, understand spatial–temporal game dynamics, and adjust strategies. The second is an affective and motivational mechanism, represented by coaching self-efficacy, through which AI-supported feedback reduces uncertainty and strengthens coaches’ confidence in making and implementing coaching decisions. Coaching effectiveness is therefore conceptualized as the behavioral outcome produced by technology-enabled cognitive understanding and technology-supported self-belief. Coaching tenure perception is included as a boundary condition because coaches with stronger perceived experiential maturity may be better able to contextualize and apply AI-generated tactical information.

To address these gaps, the present study develops and tests a comprehensive conceptual model that examines the impact of AI-based performance feedback (AIPF) on coaching effectiveness (CE). The model introduces tactical awareness (TA) as a mediator and coaching tenure perception (CTP) as a moderator, while also accounting for the roles of technology acceptance (TAM), coach digital literacy (CDL), and coaching self-efficacy (CSE). Using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) and a dataset of 512 football coaches from professional and semi-professional clubs in China, this study empirically investigates how AI systems interact with human factors in shaping coaching outcomes.This research is significant for three key reasons. First, it offers a novel empirical examination of how AI-driven feedback systems influence coaching performance through cognitive and experiential pathways. Second, it introduces tactical awareness as a central mediator, linking technical innovation with human decision-making in sports. Third, it recognizes the heterogeneity of coaches’ experiences by testing coaching tenure as a moderator, thus aligning with calls for more nuanced, person-centered models in coaching science.

Literature review

AI-based performance feedback and coaching in football

In the contemporary landscape of football coaching, the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) has revolutionized how performance is measured, interpreted, and acted upon. AI-based performance feedback systems harness real-time data analytics, video analysis, motion tracking, and predictive modeling to assist coaches in evaluating player performance, identifying tactical patterns, and making informed decisions during training and competition10,3. These systems can offer feedback on variables such as player fatigue, movement efficiency, and tactical positioning—dimensions traditionally dependent on a coach’s intuition and observation.

The shift from manual to AI-supported decision-making is particularly significant in elite-level football, where marginal gains can impact match outcomes. By providing customized and precise insights, AI tools are designed to enhance the coach’s cognitive load management and decision-making accuracy1. Although these tools are technically advanced, their effectiveness depends on how coaches interpret and apply the feedback in tactical and psychological contexts11. This shift highlights the need to examine how AI feedback translates into effective coaching behavior, including the role of awareness, experience, and digital competencies.

Tactical awareness and coaching effectiveness

Tactical awareness refers to a coach’s ability to perceive, interpret, and respond to in-game situations through strategic planning and real-time adaptation. It encompasses the recognition of spatial dynamics, opponent behavior, and opportunities for adjustment within a fluid match environment12. In football, where rapid transitions and evolving play structures are frequent, tactical awareness becomes a critical skill for both players and coaches. Coaches with heightened tactical awareness are more capable of adjusting formations, instructing on-the-fly strategies, and guiding team cohesion based on evolving match conditions Sampaio and Maçãs13.

From a theoretical standpoint, tactical awareness functions as a cognitive mediator that connects technological tools with behavioral execution. AI-based feedback enhances tactical awareness by providing visual analytics that reveal in-game details often missed in real time14. Such tools enable coaches to not only analyze past performance but also to develop scenario-based plans for future games. Research suggests that strategic cognition, such as that enhanced through tactical awarenessis positively associated with higher coaching effectiveness, particularly in areas such as decision-making, communication, and leadership15.

Technology acceptance and coach digital literacy

The successful implementation of artificial intelligence systems in coaching environments depends heavily on coaches’ attitudes, perceptions, and competence in using digital technologies. The Technology Acceptance Model explains how perceived usefulness, ease of use, trust in technology, and behavioral intention influence individuals’ willingness to adopt technological innovations16. In sports contexts, coaches who perceive AI tools as reliable, beneficial, and intuitive are more likely to integrate them into their training routines and performance evaluation practices (Yilmaz et al., 2021).

Technology acceptance is not solely a psychological construct. It is closely linked with digital literacy, which refers to a coach’s capacity to navigate digital tools, interpret data visualizations, troubleshoot systems, and operate analytical platforms effectively. Coaches with higher digital literacy are better positioned to extract meaningful insights from AI-based systems, thereby reinforcing both the perceived value and actual utility of these technologies17. This interaction suggests that digital literacy serves as an enabler of both technology use and subsequent performance enhancement.

In the context of football coaching, the acceptance of AI tools and the digital competence of the coach may play a foundational role in determining how AI-based feedback systems are used, how accurately they are interpreted, and how effectively they are applied to real-world coaching decisions. Therefore, this study includes both technology acceptance and coach digital literacy as antecedent variables that may indirectly influence coaching outcomes through their effects on AI feedback utilization.

Coaching self-efficacy and performance outcomes

In this study, coaching self-efficacy (CSE) is conceptualized as a task-specific belief reflecting a coach’s confidence in their ability to perform coaching-related tasks effectively. Unlike general self-efficacy, which represents a relatively stable personality trait, task-specific self-efficacy is context-dependent and can be influenced by external factors such as feedback, experience, and performance-related information. In the context of AI-based performance feedback, coaching self-efficacy is expected to evolve as coaches interact with data-driven insights that support decision-making and strategy development.

It is important to distinguish coaching self-efficacy from experience-related perceptions. Coaching self-efficacy refers to a coach’s current confidence in performing specific coaching tasks, such as making decisions under pressure, communicating with players, adapting training plans, and applying performance feedback. Therefore, CSE is primarily a psychological capability belief related to task execution. It does not directly capture how coaches evaluate the value of their accumulated coaching experience or how strategically mature they perceive themselves to be.

Coaching self-efficacy refers to a coach’s belief in their own capability to influence athlete development, lead strategic decisions, maintain composure under pressure, and foster motivation within a team environment. Grounded in Bandura’s social cognitive theory, self-efficacy plays a central role in shaping goal-setting behavior, persistence in challenging situations, and overall performance quality18. In football, where coaches operate under high-pressure conditions and must deliver consistent results, self-efficacy becomes a foundational trait that influences coaching style, resilience, and leadership19.

Previous studies have shown that coaches with strong self-efficacy are more confident in implementing tactical strategies, communicating effectively with athletes, and adapting to changing match situations20. These coaches tend to create more empowering training environments, leading to better individual and team outcomes. Additionally, self-efficacy contributes to decision-making autonomy and motivation, which are key dimensions of effective coaching behavior.

Artificial intelligence-based performance feedback may contribute to coaching self-efficacy by offering objective validation of coaching decisions, supporting analytical thinking, and reducing uncertainty21. When coaches observe that their strategic decisions are supported by AI-generated data, their confidence in their own abilities may be reinforced. This study therefore positions coaching self-efficacy as both a direct contributor to coaching effectiveness and a possible mediator in the relationship between AI feedback and coaching outcomes.

The mediating role of tactical awareness and self-efficacy

Understanding the mechanisms through which artificial intelligence-based performance feedback influences coaching outcomes requires examining not only direct effects but also potential mediating pathways. Two critical mediators in this context are tactical awareness and coaching self-efficacy. Both constructs represent internal cognitive and motivational processes that shape how external information, such as AI feedback, is interpreted and translated into effective coaching actions.

Tactical awareness is inherently linked to situational decision-making, which can be significantly enhanced through access to real-time data and performance analytics. Artificial intelligence systems support coaches in identifying spatial dynamics, opposition weaknesses, and game flow patterns. These insights can deepen a coach’s tactical understanding and lead to more informed decisions during training and competition. As such, tactical awareness may function as a cognitive bridge between AI feedback and coaching effectiveness, especially in domains such as match preparation, player positioning, and in-game adjustments22.

Similarly, coaching self-efficacy explains the motivational pathway through which AI-based performance feedback may influence coaching effectiveness. Rather than redefining the construct, this section focuses on its role within the proposed model. When coaches receive clear, objective, and data-supported feedback, they may feel more confident in interpreting tactical information, making decisions under pressure, and applying feedback during training or competition. This enhanced confidence can encourage more proactive, adaptive, and effective coaching behavior. Empirical research supports the view that self-efficacy mediates the relationship between external support systems and individual performance in both sports and educational contexts20,23.

In this study, both tactical awareness and coaching self-efficacy are proposed as mediators that help explain the indirect effects of AI-based performance feedback on coaching effectiveness. Their inclusion enables a deeper understanding of how technological tools interact with human cognitive and motivational systems to shape coaching behavior. This dual-path mechanism aligns with an integrated cognitive–affective perspective, where technological inputs influence both cognitive evaluation and psychological motivation processes.

The moderating role of coaching tenure perception

While artificial intelligence systems and cognitive factors such as tactical awareness and self-efficacy contribute significantly to coaching effectiveness, the extent to which these effects manifest may vary across individuals24. One factor that may influence this relationship is the coach’s perception of their own accumulated experience, referred to in this study as coaching tenure perception. This construct goes beyond the simple number of years spent in coaching and instead reflects how coaches evaluate the value, adaptability, contextual judgment, and strategic maturity they have gained through experience.

It is important to distinguish coaching tenure perception from coaching self-efficacy. Coaching self-efficacy reflects a coach’s task-specific confidence in performing coaching-related actions, such as making decisions under pressure, communicating with players, adapting training plans, and applying feedback. By contrast, coaching tenure perception reflects perceived experiential maturity, accumulated contextual knowledge, and the strategic value coaches attach to their prior coaching experience. Thus, CTP represents an experiential and contextual appraisal, whereas CSE represents a psychological belief about task execution capability. A coach may perceive their accumulated experience as strategically valuable even when their confidence in using specific AI-supported coaching tasks is moderate. Conversely, a coach may feel confident in performing coaching tasks but may not necessarily perceive their tenure as providing strong strategic maturity or contextual judgment. This distinction justifies the positioning of CTP as a moderator and CSE as a mediator in the proposed model.

Coaches with a high perception of tenure-related maturity may be better equipped to integrate AI-based feedback into their decision-making process. These individuals often possess a more nuanced understanding of game dynamics, have encountered a wider variety of match situations, and may be more skilled at evaluating the relevance of data within different contexts. As a result, the positive effects of AI feedback on tactical awareness may be amplified when coaching tenure perception is high. Conversely, coaches with low tenure perception may underutilize or misinterpret AI feedback due to limited perceived experiential knowledge and strategic familiarity. This variability in how coaches engage with AI tools based on their experiential self-assessment suggests a potential moderation effect. In this regard, coaching tenure perception is not conceptualized as confidence in performing coaching tasks; rather, it shapes how effectively coaches interpret and apply AI-based feedback in practice. Theoretical models of technology use in performance environments have supported the notion that individual differences in expertise and experience perception shape the adoption and application of digital systems25.

Accordingly, this study hypothesizes that coaching tenure perception moderates the relationship between AI-based performance feedback and tactical awareness, such that the relationship becomes stronger among coaches who perceive their experience as more valuable and strategically mature.

Theoretical framework: integrating TAM and social cognitive theory

The proposed model is grounded in an integrative cognitive–affective framework that explains coaching effectiveness as the result of technology-enabled cognition and technology-supported motivation. The framework combines the Technology Acceptance Model and social cognitive theory. TAM provides the basis for explaining why coaches are willing to engage with AI-based systems, whereas social cognitive theory explains how external informational resources influence internal cognitive and motivational processes that guide behavior and performance.

In the first stage of the framework, technology acceptance and coach digital literacy operate as antecedent conditions of AI-based performance feedback utilization. Technology acceptance reflects coaches’ perceptions that AI tools are useful, reliable, easy to use, and relevant to coaching practice. Coach digital literacy reflects the capability to operate AI-supported platforms, interpret dashboards, understand performance indicators, and apply digital information to coaching tasks. Therefore, TAM and CDL are positioned as enabling factors that determine whether coaches can meaningfully access and use AI-based performance feedback.

In the second stage, AI-based performance feedback is conceptualized as an external informational resource. It provides coaches with structured, real-time, and data-driven information about player movement, tactical positioning, team performance, and decision outcomes. This feedback does not automatically improve coaching effectiveness; rather, its influence depends on how coaches cognitively interpret and psychologically internalize the information.

The cognitive pathway is represented by tactical awareness. AI-generated feedback can improve coaches’ ability to recognize tactical patterns, interpret in-game dynamics, evaluate opponents’ behavior, and adjust strategies in response to changing match situations. Thus, tactical awareness explains how AI feedback is transformed into strategic understanding and tactical decision-making.

The affective and motivational pathway is represented by coaching self-efficacy. According to social cognitive theory, self-efficacy reflects individuals’ beliefs in their ability to organize and execute actions required to achieve desired outcomes. In football coaching, AI-based feedback may strengthen coaching self-efficacy by validating coaching decisions, reducing uncertainty, and increasing confidence in data-supported planning and communication. Coaches who feel more capable of using AI-driven insights are more likely to demonstrate effective leadership, decision-making, and adaptive coaching behavior.

Coaching tenure perception is incorporated as a boundary condition within this framework. Coaches who perceive their accumulated experience as valuable and strategically mature may be better able to contextualize AI-generated tactical information. Therefore, the relationship between AI-based performance feedback and tactical awareness is expected to be stronger when coaching tenure perception is high. Overall, the model follows a theory-driven sequence in which technology acceptance and digital literacy enable AI-based feedback utilization, AI feedback activates cognitive and affective mechanisms, and these mechanisms enhance coaching effectiveness.

Hypotheses development

Based on the integrative cognitive–affective framework, the hypotheses are derived from a sequential theoretical logic. First, technology acceptance and coach digital literacy are expected to predict AI-based performance feedback because coaches who perceive AI systems as useful, reliable, and easy to use, and who possess sufficient digital competence, are more likely to engage with AI-generated feedback meaningfully. Second, AI-based performance feedback is expected to influence tactical awareness and coaching self-efficacy because AI feedback provides external performance information that supports both cognitive interpretation and motivational confidence. Third, tactical awareness and coaching self-efficacy are expected to improve coaching effectiveness because strategic understanding and coaching confidence are central to effective coaching behavior. Finally, coaching tenure perception is expected to moderate the relationship between AI-based performance feedback and tactical awareness because perceived experiential maturity may strengthen coaches’ ability to transform AI-generated feedback into tactical understanding. Accordingly, the hypotheses are organized into four categories: antecedent effects, direct effects, mediating effects, and moderating effects.

Methodology

Research design

This study uses a quantitative, cross-sectional design to examine the structural relationships among key latent constructs relevant to football coaching effectiveness. The primary focus was to investigate the direct, mediating, and moderating effects among Artificial Intelligence-Based Performance Feedback, Tactical Awareness, Coaching Self-Efficacy, Coach Digital Literacy, Technology Acceptance, Coaching Tenure Perception, and Coaching Effectiveness.

Grounded in the positivist paradigm, the study emphasized objectivity, measurability, and statistical rigor. We collected data using a structured survey instrument and analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) via SmartPLS 4.0. This approach was chosen for its robustness in handling complex models with mediation and moderation paths, non-normal data distributions, and medium-sized samples.

In addition, PLS-SEM is particularly suitable for prediction-oriented research and exploratory model development, as it focuses on maximizing explained variance and identifying key driver relationships among constructs. This aligns well with the present study’s objective of examining the predictive relationships between AI-based performance feedback and coaching effectiveness.

The conceptual model comprised seven latent constructs, each measured reflectively using multiple indicators. Mediation effects were tested through Tactical Awareness and Coaching Self-Efficacy, while Coaching Tenure Perception served as a moderator. The use of PLS-SEM enabled simultaneous estimation of both the measurement and structural models, allowing for an integrated evaluation of reliability, validity, and hypothesis testing.The conceptual model illustrating the hypothesized relationships is presented in Fig. 1.

Population and sampling technique

The target population for this study included professional and semi-professional football coaches actively involved in organized coaching across various competitive tiers in Henan Province, China. Participants were drawn from youth academies, amateur leagues, semi-professional clubs, and professional teams to ensure a diverse representation across coaching contexts.

A stratified random sampling technique was employed to achieve proportional representation across coaching levels, geographic regions, years of experience, and certification categories. A total of 512 valid responses were obtained, exceeding the minimum recommended sample size for PLS-SEM. According to the ten-times rule, the most complex construct in the model (with six indicators) would require a minimum of 60 cases. However, a more conservative power analysis suggested a minimum of 350 participants. Thus, the final sample of 512 provided sufficient statistical power for structural modeling.

Coaches were recruited through formal communication with sports federations, coaching academies, regional sports councils, and club management associations in Henan. Additional participants were approached directly at coaching seminars and football development workshops. Inclusion criteria required participants to (a) be currently employed as football coaches, (b) have at least one year of coaching experience, and (c) have knowledge of or exposure to AI-assisted training technologies or data-supported performance feedback systems (Table 1).

Research instrument and measurement model

The primary data collection tool for this study was a structured, self-administered questionnaire specifically developed to measure seven latent constructs: Technology Acceptance, AI-Based Performance Feedback, Coach Digital Literacy, Tactical Awareness, Coaching Self-Efficacy, Coaching Tenure Perception, and Coaching Effectiveness. These constructs were selected based on an extensive review of literature across sport psychology, technology adoption, educational leadership, and AI-assisted coaching systems. Each construct was operationalized using reflective measurement modeling, where the observed indicators were assumed to be caused by the underlying latent variable.

To capture respondents’ agreement with various attitudinal and behavioral statements, each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). The Technology Acceptance construct was adapted from the original Technology Acceptance Model and included items reflecting perceived usefulness, ease of use, behavioral intention, trust in technology, and confidence in using AI tools. AI-Based Performance Feedback, which served as a key independent variable in the model, was measured through six indicators newly developed for this study. These items evaluated coaches’ perceptions regarding the accuracy, relevance, real-time delivery, decision support, interpretability, and customizability of AI-generated performance insights.

Coach Digital Literacy was operationalized using four indicators adapted from widely used digital competency frameworks. These items assessed the coach’s ability to navigate performance software, interpret dashboards and statistics, troubleshoot basic system issues, and operate training-related technology tools. Tactical Awareness, which functioned as one of the two mediating constructs, was measured using five indicators adapted from tactical cognition research. These included assessments of the coach’s ability to read the game, adapt strategies, analyze opponent behavior, recognize spatial and temporal dynamics, and adjust tactics in real time.

Coaching Self-Efficacy was measured using five items derived from prior work in educational and sports psychology. These items focused on the coach’s perceived confidence in influencing players, making effective decisions under pressure, leading a team, and communicating with clarity. Coaching Tenure Perception, the moderating construct, was newly developed and measured using three items designed to reflect the coach’s self-evaluation of how their experience contributes to adaptability, strategic maturity, and receptivity to AI tools. Coaching Effectiveness, the primary outcome construct, was measured using seven items adapted from validated coaching assessment scales. These items encompassed multiple dimensions of effective coaching such as athlete motivation, skill development, planning, leadership, character building, and team success.

The instrument underwent a rigorous two-stage validation process. In the first stage, content validity and face validity were ensured through expert reviews conducted by two sport psychology researchers and three elite football coaches. These experts evaluated each item for clarity, contextual appropriateness, and theoretical alignment. Based on their feedback, minor modifications were made to item phrasing and sequence. In the second stage, a pilot study involving 40 football coaches (not included in the main dataset) was conducted. Feedback from the pilot confirmed item clarity, and reliability estimates showed satisfactory internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding the 0.80 threshold across all constructs. The finalized questionnaire was then distributed both online and offline depending on respondent preference and technological access. Participants were assured of anonymity and confidentiality throughout the administration process.

Data collection procedure

The data collection process for this study was conducted over a three-month period in Henan Province, China, using both online and offline modalities to maximize accessibility, response diversity, and representativeness. The final version of the survey instrument was deployed via a secure, password-protected online platform, while printed copies were disseminated at selected coaching development workshops, regional conferences, and national symposiums involving certified football coaches. This dual-distribution approach allowed the researchers to reach coaches with varying levels of digital access and technological familiarity.

Prior to survey distribution, ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the lead research institution. All research activities were designed and conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for the ethical treatment of human participants. A brief cover page on both digital and printed versions of the questionnaire explained the research objectives, highlighted the voluntary nature of participation, and confirmed that responses would remain confidential and anonymized. Informed consent was obtained electronically for online submissions and in written form for participants completing paper-based surveys.

Each respondent was instructed to complete the questionnaire based on their current coaching role and personal experience with AI-assisted feedback tools or data-driven performance platforms. To minimize common method variance, the instrument incorporated a balanced mix of positively and negatively worded items and ensured variability in question sequencing. Furthermore, participants were encouraged to respond independently, without external consultation or time constraints, to ensure authentic and unbiased input.

To promote response accuracy and data integrity, the online platform included automated range checks and real-time validation rules for incomplete or inconsistent entries. Additionally, reminder emails were issued to affiliated coaching organizations and individuals who had initially expressed interest but had not finalized their responses. Responses were screened manually to eliminate duplicates or incomplete records. The final dataset consisted of 512 complete and validated responses, which met the sample size requirements for structural equation modeling using SmartPLS.

All collected data were securely archived using anonymized identifiers and stored in encrypted repositories to facilitate future audit, transparency, and replication. This comprehensive and methodologically rigorous data collection strategy provided a solid empirical foundation for evaluating the hypothesized relationships among the key constructs in the theoretical model.

Data analysis procedure

We conducted data analysis using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) in SmartPLS version 4.0. This technique was chosen due to its suitability for estimating complex structural models that include mediating and moderating effects, multiple latent constructs, and reflective indicators. Prior to structural model testing, data were examined for completeness, accuracy, and outliers. No missing values were found, and all cases passed multivariate screening criteria. Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the demographic profile and verify data integrity.

The measurement model was evaluated based on indicator reliability, internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. Indicator reliability was assessed by examining the factor loadings of individual items on their respective constructs, where a value of 0.70 or higher was considered acceptable. Internal consistency reliability was measured using both Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR). After validation of the measurement model, the structural model was assessed to test the hypothesized relationships among the constructs. Model explanatory power was examined using R-squared (R²) for each endogenous construct. We tested mediation effects through indirect path coefficients using bootstrapped confidence intervals. The significance of both direct and indirect effects was assessed to determine full or partial mediation. We conducted moderation analysis by constructing an interaction term between the independent and moderating variables. A significant interaction effect confirmed the presence of moderation, and post-hoc slope analysis was used to interpret the strength of the moderating effect at different levels of Coaching Tenure Perception. Because this study used PLS-SEM, model evaluation focused primarily on measurement quality, explanatory power, predictive relevance, and the significance of structural relationships rather than absolute global model fit. Therefore, the assessment emphasized indicator reliability, internal consistency, convergent validity, discriminant validity, multicollinearity, path coefficients, R², f², Q², and bootstrapped confidence intervals. In addition, theoretically plausible alternative models were compared using R², Q², and SRMR to evaluate whether the hypothesized moderated mediation model provided stronger explanatory and predictive performance than competing specifications. Data preprocessing and screening

Prior to conducting structural equation modeling, the dataset underwent a comprehensive preprocessing and screening process to ensure its suitability for multivariate analysis. The raw data, exported from the online survey platform and merged with entries from manually collected paper-based responses, were first reviewed using SPSS version 28. Initial checks were conducted to identify missing values, outliers, and data entry inconsistencies. The questionnaire’s design enforced required responses through both digital validation and manual oversight, resulting in a complete dataset with no missing values. Thus, no imputation techniques were applied.

Descriptive statistics were computed for all observed variables to evaluate central tendency, dispersion, and distributional properties. Skewness and kurtosis values were examined to assess univariate normality, and all values fell within the acceptable range of ± 1.0, indicating no significant deviations from normality. Although multivariate normality is not a requirement for PLS-SEM, this preliminary assessment supported the robustness of the dataset. To detect multivariate outliers, Mahalanobis distance was calculated across all observed indicators, and no extreme values were found based on the conservative chi-square threshold at the 0.001 significance level.

Common method variance (CMV), a potential threat in cross-sectional self-reported survey research, was assessed using multiple diagnostic procedures rather than relying only on Harman’s single-factor test. First, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted as an initial assessment, and the first unrotated factor accounted for 34.7% of the total variance, which was below the recommended 50% threshold. However, given the limitations of Harman’s test, additional checks were performed. Full collinearity VIF values were examined for all latent constructs, and all values were below the conservative threshold of 3.3, suggesting that common method bias was unlikely to substantially inflate the structural relationships. In addition, an unmeasured latent method factor was introduced into the measurement model by allowing the observed indicators to load on both their theoretical constructs and a common method factor. The substantive factor loadings remained significant, and the key structural path coefficients showed only minimal changes after controlling for the method factor. These results indicate that CMV was not a serious threat to the validity of the findings.

Additionally, a subset of paper-based responses was randomly cross-checked against digital entries to validate transcription accuracy. No inconsistencies were detected, confirming the integrity of the data integration process. All measurement items were reviewed for coding consistency, scale direction, and construct alignment prior to analysis. The finalized dataset was then imported into SmartPLS version 4.0, where each indicator was mapped to its corresponding latent construct according to the reflective measurement model specification. Indicator coding, construct alignment, and loading direction were verified as part of the model preparation process. This screening procedure ensured that the dataset was clean, consistent, and ready for confirmatory analysis of the measurement and structural models. Statistical tools and analysis plan

Data analysis for this study was performed using a combination of SPSS version 28 and SmartPLS version 4.0. SPSS was used in the initial stages to facilitate descriptive statistics, data screening, and reliability checks, while SmartPLS was employed to conduct advanced multivariate modeling through Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM). The analysis plan was executed in two major phases. In the first phase, the measurement model was evaluated to verify the psychometric properties of all latent constructs. This involved assessing indicator reliability via outer loadings, where values of 0.70 or higher were retained. Internal consistency was verified through both Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability, using acceptable thresholds of 0.70 and 0.80 respectively. Convergent validity was determined through the Average Variance Extracted (AVE), where values exceeding 0.50 indicated sufficient shared variance among items. These criteria are summarized in Table 2 and applied consistently throughout the model evaluation.

In the second phase, the structural model was assessed to examine the hypothesized relationships among latent variables. This involved estimation of path coefficients, coefficient of determination (R²), effect sizes (f²), and predictive relevance (Q²). R² represents the proportion of variance explained by the model and was interpreted using the classification by Chin (1998), where 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 reflect weak, moderate, and substantial explanatory power respectively. The formula for R² is expressed as:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}=1-\:\frac{Sum\:of\:Squares\:of\:Residuals}{Total\:Sum\:of\:Squares}$$\end{document}

Effect size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}^{2}$$\end{document}was calculated to assess the relative impact of exogenous constructs on endogenous variables using:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}^{2}=\:\frac{{R}_{included}^{2}-{R}_{excluded}^{2}}{1-{R}_{included}^{2}}$$\end{document}

Values of 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 represent small, medium, and large effect sizes respectively. Predictive relevance (Q²) was evaluated using the Stone–Geisser test via blindfolding, where Q² values greater than zero indicated meaningful predictive capability. Path significance was assessed using the bootstrapping procedure with 5000 resamples, which generated bias-corrected confidence intervals and p-values for each path. Both direct effects and indirect effects were tested. Mediation effects were analyzed using the indirect effect estimation framework, and mediation was supported if the indirect path was statistically significant while considering the presence or absence of a direct effect.

To test for moderation, an interaction term was created between AI-Based Performance Feedback and Coaching Tenure Perception. A significant interaction path indicated the presence of moderation, and slope difference analysis was used to interpret the strength and direction of moderation at high and low levels of tenure perception. To further support the moderation interpretation, conditional effect analysis was conducted. Simple slope effects were estimated at low and high levels of Coaching Tenure Perception (CTP), defined as one standard deviation below and above the mean. In addition, the conditional indirect effects of AIPF on CE through TA were estimated at low and high levels of CTP using bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals. The index of moderated mediation was also calculated to determine whether the indirect effect of AIPF on CE through TA significantly varied across different levels of CTP. Multicollinearity was checked using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values, where values below 3.0 were deemed acceptable. Table 8 reports the results of the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio analysis, which is a modern and statistically rigorous approach for assessing discriminant validity in structural equation modeling. Discriminant validity ensures that each construct in the model is empirically distinct from the others—that is, the indicators of one construct do not overlap significantly with those of another. The HTMT ratio is calculated by comparing the average correlations across indicators measuring different constructs (heterotrait-heteromethod) with the average correlations within the same construct (monotrait-heteromethod). A commonly accepted threshold for HTMT is 0.85, below which discriminant validity is considered adequate (Henseler et al., 2015). As presented in Table 8, all HTMT values for pairwise construct comparisons fall below the 0.85 threshold, with values ranging from 0.57 (CDL–CTP) to 0.79 (CSE–CE). These results provide strong evidence that the constructs in this study are empirically distinct from one another and that their measurement items are not excessively correlated with items from different constructs. In particular, lower HTMT values between technology-related constructs (e.g., TAM, CDL) and psychological constructs (e.g., CSE, TA) suggest that the instrument successfully differentiates between coaches’ technological perceptions and their cognitive or experiential characteristics. Overall, the HTMT analysis confirms that each latent variable exhibits unique discriminant properties, supporting the structural integrity of the model. Importantly, the HTMT value between Coaching Self-Efficacy (CSE) and Coaching Tenure Perception (CTP) was 0.62, which is well below the recommended threshold of 0.85. This result provides empirical support that CSE and CTP are distinct constructs despite their theoretical relationship within the coaching context.

Table 9 presents the results of the Fornell–Larcker criterion analysis, which serves as a classical approach to evaluating discriminant validity in reflective measurement models. According to this criterion, a latent construct should share more variance with its own indicators than with those of any other construct. This is established when the square root of the AVE for each construct (shown on the diagonal in bold) is greater than the correlation coefficients with all other constructs (the off-diagonal values). As shown in Table 9, each construct’s diagonal value is consistently higher than its respective correlations with other constructs. For instance, the square root of AVE for Coaching Effectiveness (0.840) is greater than its correlations with Tactical Awareness (0.802), Self-Efficacy (0.790), and all other variables. Similarly, Tactical Awareness (0.829) shows stronger association with its own indicators than with any other constructs, despite having moderate correlations with AIPF (0.755) and CSE (0.760). These findings affirm that each construct in the model possesses adequate discriminant validity, meaning they are statistically distinguishable from one another despite theoretical overlaps. The consistent satisfaction of the Fornell–Larcker criterion further strengthens the validity of the measurement model and supports the conceptual distinctiveness of constructs such as AI-Based Performance Feedback, Coaching Self-Efficacy, and Tactical Awareness. When interpreted alongside the HTMT analysis in Table 8, the Fornell–Larcker results provide converging evidence that discriminant validity is well established across all latent constructs used in the study.

Mediation analysis

The mediating roles of tactical awareness (TA) and coaching self-efficacy (CSE) were examined using a bootstrapping procedure with 5000 resamples in SmartPLS. The significance of the indirect effects was evaluated using bootstrapped t-values, p-values, and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals. Mediation was considered statistically significant when the 95% confidence interval did not include zero.

The results indicate that both indirect paths are statistically significant, providing strong support for the mediating roles of TA and CSE. Specifically, AIPF had a significant indirect effect on CE through TA (β = 0.61, t = 13.88, p < .001, 95% CI [0.52, 0.70]) and through CSE (β = 0.55, t = 12.01, p < .001, 95% CI [0.46, 0.64]). Since neither bootstrapped confidence interval included zero, both mediation effects were statistically supported. These findings confirm that TA and CSE function as parallel mediators, each transmitting the effect of AIPF on coaching effectiveness.

Furthermore, the results suggest a partial mediation effect, indicating that AIPF enhances coaching effectiveness both directly and indirectly through cognitive and psychological mechanisms. This highlights the importance of internal processes, such as tactical understanding and self-confidence, in translating AI-driven feedback into effective coaching outcomes. Discussion and conclusion

Summary of key findings

These findings extend prior literature in several important ways. First, the significant effect of AIPF on TA is consistent with previous studies showing that AI-supported analytics, video analysis, and data-driven feedback can improve the interpretation of player movement, tactical positioning, and match dynamics14,1,3. However, the present study extends this literature by showing that AI feedback does not only support technical performance analysis but also strengthens coaches’ tactical awareness. This suggests that AI-based feedback systems can function as cognitive support tools that help coaches interpret complex game situations and make more informed tactical decisions.

Second, the positive relationship between AIPF and CSE aligns with Bandura’s social cognitive theory, which emphasizes that feedback, performance information, and mastery-related cues can strengthen individuals’ confidence in performing domain-specific tasks 18. The finding is also consistent with coaching self-efficacy literature, which shows that coaches’ confidence is closely related to decision-making, communication, leadership, and adaptive coaching behavior20. In the present study, AI-generated feedback may have enhanced coaching self-efficacy by reducing uncertainty and providing objective support for coaching decisions.

Third, the significant effects of TAM and CDL on AIPF are consistent with technology adoption research. The Technology Acceptance Model argues that perceived usefulness, ease of use, and trust influence individuals’ willingness to adopt new technologies16. Similarly, technology-use research emphasizes that users’ digital competence and readiness shape their ability to benefit from technological systems25. The present findings support these arguments in the football coaching context by showing that coaches are more likely to engage meaningfully with AI-based feedback when they accept AI tools and possess sufficient digital literacy.

Fourth, the mediation results clarify the mechanisms through which AI feedback influences coaching effectiveness. Prior research on AI in sport has often focused on performance monitoring, prediction, injury prevention, or tactical analytics. In contrast, this study shows that AI-based feedback improves coaching effectiveness through both a cognitive pathway, represented by tactical awareness, and an affective-motivational pathway, represented by coaching self-efficacy. This supports the integrated cognitive–affective framework proposed in this study and demonstrates that AI feedback affects coaching outcomes by shaping how coaches understand tactical information and how confident they feel in applying it.

Finally, the moderation result involving CTP contributes to literature on experience, expertise, and technology use. Prior studies suggest that experience and contextual judgment influence how individuals interpret and apply digital technologies in performance environments 25,11. The present study extends this perspective by showing that coaches with stronger perceived experiential maturity derive greater tactical benefit from AI-based performance feedback. This finding indicates that the effectiveness of AI feedback is not uniform across all coaches; rather, it depends partly on how coaches perceive and use their accumulated coaching experience.

Theoretical contributions

This study makes several important theoretical contributions by providing an integrated and mechanism-based understanding of AI-enabled coaching effectiveness. First, the study combines the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) with social cognitive theory within a unified framework of technology-enabled coaching performance. TAM explains why coaches are willing and able to engage with AI-based performance feedback, while social cognitive theory explains how such feedback influences internal cognitive and psychological processes. This integration clarifies how technological inputs are transformed into coaching-related outcomes.

Second, the mediation results clarify the mechanisms through which AI-based performance feedback influences coaching effectiveness. Tactical awareness represents the cognitive pathway because AI-generated feedback must be interpreted and converted into tactical understanding before it can support coaching decisions, match preparation, and in-game adaptation. Coaching self-efficacy represents the affective and motivational pathway because AI feedback can reduce uncertainty, validate coaching decisions, and strengthen coaches’ confidence in applying data-supported strategies. Together, these two mediators show that AI-based feedback improves coaching effectiveness through both cognitive interpretation and motivational confidence.

Third, the moderation and moderated mediation findings provide a conditional process explanation of AI-enabled coaching effectiveness. Coaching tenure perception strengthened the relationship between AI-based performance feedback and tactical awareness, indicating that coaches with stronger perceived experiential maturity are better able to contextualize AI-generated information. The moderated mediation result further suggests that the indirect effect of AI-based feedback on coaching effectiveness through tactical awareness is stronger when coaching tenure perception is high. This finding demonstrates that AI-supported coaching effectiveness depends not only on technological input, but also on coaches’ cognitive interpretation, motivational confidence, and experiential judgment.

Although the proposed moderated mediation model was theoretically supported, alternative explanations should also be considered. Coaches with higher digital literacy or prior experience with analytical tools may be more capable of interpreting AI-based feedback. Organizational factors such as club-level investment in analytics, availability of performance analysts, technical support, and coaching culture may also influence how effectively AI feedback is used. In addition, coaches who already possess stronger tactical awareness or higher self-efficacy may be more likely to engage with AI tools, suggesting possible reciprocal or reverse effects. Because the study used cross-sectional self-reported data, the findings should be interpreted with caution as theoretically meaningful associations rather than definitive causal effects. Although the statistical results support the proposed moderated mediation model, they do not establish causality. Future longitudinal, experimental, and multi-source studies are needed to confirm the causal pathways and rule out competing explanations more robustly.

Practical implications

The findings of this study offer concrete implications for football clubs, coach education programs, and developers of AI-based coaching systems. Because AI-based performance feedback significantly improved tactical awareness, coaching self-efficacy, and coaching effectiveness, the practical value of AI systems should be understood not only in terms of data generation but also in terms of how coaches interpret, trust, and apply feedback in real coaching situations.

Implications for coaches

Football clubs should integrate AI-based performance feedback into routine coaching workflows rather than treating it as an optional analytical tool. Since AIPF showed a strong relationship with tactical awareness, clubs can use AI-generated feedback in pre-match planning, post-match review, opponent analysis, and training-session adjustment. For example, tactical heatmaps, player-positioning data, pressing patterns, transition indicators, and spatial–temporal movement reports should be translated into actionable coaching notes that coaches can use during team meetings and training design.

The findings also suggest that clubs should not simply provide AI systems without structured interpretation support. Coaches may receive large amounts of performance data, but the benefit depends on their ability to convert this information into tactical decisions. Therefore, clubs should create analyst–coach collaboration routines in which performance analysts help coaches interpret AI outputs and connect them with formation changes, player roles, substitution decisions, and training priorities. This is especially important for coaches with lower coaching tenure perception, who may require more guidance in contextualizing AI-generated tactical information.

Implications for sports organizations and training institutions

Coach education programs should include AI literacy and data-informed tactical decision-making as core components of professional training. The significant effects of Technology Acceptance and Coach Digital Literacy on AIPF indicate that coaches are more likely to benefit from AI feedback when they understand the usefulness, reliability, and practical meaning of AI-generated information. Therefore, coaching certification programs should include modules on interpreting AI dashboards, reading tactical visualizations, evaluating performance indicators, and applying feedback to training and match decisions.

The mediating role of coaching self-efficacy also suggests that coach education should not focus only on technical skills. Training should help coaches build confidence in using AI-supported evidence while maintaining professional judgment. Practical exercises such as AI-assisted match analysis, scenario-based tactical planning, and reflective feedback sessions can help coaches develop both competence and confidence. Since coaching tenure perception strengthened the AIPF–TA relationship, training should also be differentiated by experience level. Less-experienced coaches may need more structured guidance and examples, whereas experienced coaches may benefit from advanced tactical analytics and customized feedback interpretation.

Implications for technology developers and managers

Developers of AI-based coaching systems should design tools that support tactical interpretation, not only statistical reporting. The strong link between AIPF and tactical awareness indicates that coaches need AI systems that convert complex data into clear tactical insights. Therefore, dashboards should include explainable visualizations, tactical pattern summaries, player-role comparisons, and context-sensitive recommendations. For example, instead of only showing distance covered or possession statistics, AI systems should indicate how these patterns relate to pressing efficiency, defensive compactness, transition speed, or space creation.

Because Technology Acceptance and Coach Digital Literacy were significant antecedents of AIPF, system usability is also critical. Developers should design interfaces that are easy to navigate, minimize unnecessary technical complexity, and allow coaches to customize feedback according to coaching level, tactical objective, and match context. The moderation finding further suggests that AI systems should include adaptive features for different experience levels. Less-experienced coaches may benefit from guided explanations and basic tactical summaries, while more experienced coaches may prefer advanced analytics, comparative reports, and customizable scenario simulations.

Overall, the findings indicate that AI-driven coaching systems should be implemented as decision-support tools that enhance tactical understanding and coaching confidence, rather than as replacements for human judgment. Effective implementation requires coordinated support from football clubs, coach education providers, analysts, and technology developers.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal conclusions. Future studies should use longitudinal or experimental designs to examine changes over time. Second, the use of self-reported data may introduce bias, including social desirability effects. Future research should incorporate objective performance measures or multi-source data. Third, although Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method variance (CMV), this approach has recognized limitations and may not fully detect method bias. More advanced techniques, such as the use of marker variables, were not employed in this study. Therefore, the potential influence of CMV cannot be entirely ruled out, and future research is encouraged to adopt more robust methods to address CMV. Fourth, the sample is limited to football coaches in a specific region, which may affect generalizability. Studies across different sports and cultural contexts are needed. Finally, variations in the type and quality of AI tools were not examined. Future research should compare different systems to better understand their effectiveness.

Recommendations for future research

Future research should explore the long-term impact of AI-based performance feedback on coaching behavior and team performance. Additional moderators, such as organizational support or digital anxiety, could be examined to better understand contextual influences. The integration of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) into coaching systems is another promising area. This would allow researchers to examine how transparency affects trust and adoption. Comparative studies across different sports and the use of mixed-method approaches may provide deeper insights into how coaches interact with AI systems. These directions will help develop more effective and human-centered coaching technologies.

Conclusion

This study set out to examine the impact of AI-Based Performance Feedback (AIPF) on Coaching Effectiveness (CE) within a theoretically grounded framework that integrates both psychological and technological constructs. By employing Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), the analysis revealed that AIPF significantly enhances coaching outcomes, not only through direct influence but also via the mediating roles of Tactical Awareness (TA) and Coaching Self-Efficacy (CSE). The model further demonstrated that Technology Acceptance (TAM) and Coach Digital Literacy (CDL) are foundational antecedents to AIPF, reinforcing the importance of digital readiness in successful AI adoption. Notably, Coaching Tenure Perception (CTP) was found to significantly moderate the AIPF–TA path, highlighting that perceived coaching experience strengthens the effectiveness of AI-driven feedback mechanisms.

Theoretically, the study contributes to the growing intersection of educational technology, sports coaching, and cognitive behavioral theory by embedding both mediating and moderating variables into a unified framework. It moves beyond simple input-output models by capturing the nuanced psychological and experiential factors that influence how AI tools translate into meaningful coaching outcomes. Practically, the findings underscore the need for personalized and experience-sensitive implementation strategies when integrating AI systems into coaching environments. Clubs, institutions, and developers must prioritize not only system design but also digital literacy training, cognitive feedback interpretation, and trust-building through transparent AI features.

While the findings are robust and empirically grounded, they are not without limitations. The cross-sectional nature of the data restricts causal inference, and the focus on football coaches within a specific regional context may limit broader applicability. Future research should aim to validate these findings through longitudinal studies, experimental designs, and cross-sport comparisons. Moreover, the inclusion of additional moderators such as organizational culture, digital anxiety, or AI explainability features could enrich understanding and practical relevance. Collectively, this study provides a solid foundation for building more adaptive, human-centered AI interventions in the domain of sports coaching and performance optimization.
