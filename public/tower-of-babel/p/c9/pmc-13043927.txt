Sulfur vacancy-confined Co-Mo sites in MoS2 for high-efficiency CO2 hydrogenation to formate
Authors: Zifeng Wang, Yiran Kang, Guancheng Chen, Qinqin Ji, Yunlong Zhang, Yutai Qi, Juntong Zhu, Wu Zhou, Rui Huang, Jingting Hu, Liang Yu, Zhong-qun Tian, Dehui Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCO2 hydrogenation to formate is a significant process for converting greenhouse gas to high-value chemicals. MoS2 catalysts are emerging as low-cost candidates for this reaction, but their performance is constrained by the challenge of generating sufficient and high-activity sulfur vacancy-confined Mo sites as active sites. Here we report that confining Co atoms into MoS2 lattice to substitute Mo atoms (Co-MoS2) remarkably enhances the efficiency of CO2 hydrogenation to formate via enriching sulfur vacancies under practical reaction conditions. The Co-MoS2 achieves a high formate production rate of 17.0 mmol gcat.−1 h−1 with a selectivity exceeding 99% at 200 °C, in which the reaction rate is nearly three times that of the pristine MoS2 catalyst and surpasses previously reported non-precious metal-based catalysts. Experimental characterizations combined with theoretical calculations demonstrate that the weakened bonding between the Co-Mo pair and its adjacent sulfur or oxygen facilitates the removal of sulfur or oxygen for the generation of sulfur vacancies during hydrogenation. The exposed sulfur vacancy-confined Co-Mo sites both at the edge and in the basal plane exhibit moderate CO2 adsorption strength, thus suppressing C-O bond cleavage and promoting selective CO2 hydrogenation to formate.Subject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Catalyst synthesis, Two-dimensional materials

Introduction

Formate is an important chemical building-block that has been widely used in the fields of leather, textile, and dyeing industries, etc. Hydrogenation of CO2 to formate provides a promising route for converting this greenhouse gas to high-value chemicals, which is typically achieved over precious metal catalyst1–5. Over the past few decades, various precious metal catalysts based on Pd6,7, Au8, Ir9, and Ru10,11, were developed for this reaction with high activity, but are limited by their scarcity and high cost. In contrast, earth-abundant non-precious metal-based catalysts are more attractive for practical applications. To date, great efforts have been made to develop non-precious metal-based heterogeneous catalysts for this process, yet these catalysts usually suffer from insufficient reactivity12–20. Coordinatively unsaturated Mo sites at the sulfur vacancies (SVs) of MoS2, i.e., SV-confined Mo sites, were found possessing superior catalytic activity for CO2 hydrogenation. These sites enabled highly efficient hydrogenation of CO2 to methanol in a gas-solid phase reaction and to formate in aqueous reaction system20,21. However, the practical utilization of MoS2 is limited by the difficulty in generating highly active SV-confined Mo sites via removing sulfur atoms as well as their susceptibility to oxygen blockage upon air exposure22. The oxygen-blocked SVs are exceedingly difficult to regenerate due to the strong bonding between oxygen species with the SV-confined Mo sites20, which severely limits the amount of accessible active sites and thus the overall catalytic performance.

Herein, we report that by confining Co atoms into the MoS2 lattice to facilitate the formation of SVs, highly efficient CO2 hydrogenation to formate is achieved over this Co-MoS2 catalyst. It delivers a high formate production rate of 17.0 mmol gcat.−1 h−1 with a selectivity of over 99% at 200 °C, in which the reaction rate is nearly three times that of the pristine MoS2 catalyst and surpasses previously reported non-precious metal-based catalysts. In addition, the activity of Co-MoS2 can be well-maintained for over 80 h in 8 reaction cycles, demonstrating the high durability of the catalyst. Multiple experimental characterizations combined with theoretical calculations reveal that the lower coordination number of Co than that of Mo leads to a weaker bonding interaction between the lattice-confined Co-Mo pair and surface sulfur or oxygen, which promotes the hydrogenation-driven removal of sulfur or oxygen species to generate SVs. The exposed SV-confined Co-Mo sites with moderate CO2 adsorption both at the edge and in the basal plane suppress the cleavage of C-O bond and promote the formation of formate, leading to the superior activity and selectivity of Co-MoS2 for the CO2 hydrogenation to formate.

Results

Characterizations and catalytic performance of Co-MoS2

Co-MoS2 catalysts with different Co loadings were prepared using a one-pot hydrothermal method. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) results show that these catalysts are all featured by flower-like thin-layer nanosheets with large lateral sizes, exposing abundant basal plane sites (Fig. 1a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 1). The Co loading has no obvious influence on the overall structure and morphology of the catalyst (Fig. 1a–c, and Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). To determine the chemical state of Co in the Co-MoS2, various spectroscopic and microscopic techniques were employed to analyze its geometric and electronic structures. No signs of Co-related nanoparticles or clusters were observed in HRTEM images and XRD patterns, indicating the well dispersion of Co in MoS2 (Fig. 1a–c, and Supplementary Figs. 1, 2), which was further demonstrated by energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping characterization (Fig. 1d). In addition, as indicated by the X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra, the Co coordination structure of Co-MoS2 differs from those of Co foil, CoS, and Co3O4 standard samples and its absorption edge falls between those of CoS and Co3O4, indicating an intermediate oxidation state of Co in Co-MoS2 between CoS and Co3O4 (Fig. 1e). The extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra of Co-MoS2 and its fitting result further reveal that Co-S coordination is dominant in Co-MoS2 with a coordination number of ca. 4.42 and no Co-Co coordination observed (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Fig. 3, and Supplementary Table 1). These findings suggest that Co atoms are atomically dispersed and covalently confined within the MoS2 lattice, rather than forming other phases like Co, CoS, or Co3O4 nanoparticles. The existence and location of Co single atom was further confirmed by the atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) combined with electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS). Intensity profile analyses of the HAADF-STEM image along the corresponding dashed lines in Fig. 1g show the replacement of bright spot (Mo atom) by relatively darker spot in the atomic lattice (Fig. 1h). The relatively darker spot was identified as Co atom based on the Co L2,3 feature peak in the EELS spectra (Fig. 1i). These results reveal that the Co atoms are confined into the MoS2 lattice by replacing Mo atoms.

Catalytic performances were evaluated by using stainless-steel autoclave. To enhance the solubility of H2 in the aqueous solution, a CO2/H2 ratio of 1/3 with a higher H2 partial pressure compared to the stoichiometric ratio of 1/1 was used for CO2 hydrogenation, enabling a significantly increased formate production rate (rformate, Supplementary Fig. 4). Compared to the pristine MoS2 catalyst, the introduction of Co significantly enhances the activity, achieving a maximum rformate of 12.1 mmol gcat.−1 h−1 at a Co loading of 1.2 wt%, which is nearly three times that over the pristine MoS2 catalyst (Fig. 2a, b). The rformate was not increased at higher Co loadings, which may be due to the limitation of equilibrium. The superiority of the Co-MoS2 catalyst in rformate was further verified at different reaction temperatures and pressures (Fig. 2c, d). Notably, the Co-MoS2 catalyst delivers a high rformate of 17.0 mmol gcat.−1 h−1 with a selectivity of over 99% at 200 °C and 3.5 MPa, in which the rformate markedly surpasses those of previously reported non-precious metal-based catalysts (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Figs. 5, 6, and Supplementary Table 2)12–20. In addition, the Co-MoS2 catalyst exhibits high durability in the reaction without obvious activity decay in 8 reaction cycles for 80 h (Fig. 2f). HRTEM and X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterizations of the used Co-MoS2 catalyst reveal the well-maintained morphology and 2H-MoS2 crystal phase after 8 reaction cycles (Supplementary Fig. 7), further demonstrating the structural stability of the catalyst.

Characterizations of sulfur vacancies on Co-MoS2

Coordinatively unsaturated Mo sites at the SVs of pristine MoS2 were found possessing unique activity and selectivity for CO2 hydrogenation20,21. To investigate the effect of Co substituting Mo on the formation of SVs, in-situ NO pulse adsorption measurements were conducted to quantify the SVs23,24. NO adsorption was not detected on the as-prepared fresh 1.2% Co-MoS2 and pristine MoS2 catalysts, which could be due to the occupation of SVs by oxygen species after their exposure to air (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 8), as revealed by the presence of Mo-O vibrational peaks in the diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 9)21,25,26. After in-situ treatment of the samples with H2, the NO adsorption capacities are significantly increased, indicating the regeneration of SVs due to the removal of oxygen species (Fig. 3a, and Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). It should be noted that when the H2-reduced catalysts are exposed again to the O2 atmosphere even at room temperature, the SVs on MoS2 could be blocked again due to the formation of Mo-O species (Fig. 3b). Therefore, the in-situ removal of O* species from the SVs by H2 to regenerate the active sites during the reaction plays an essential role in boosting the catalytic activity of MoS2 catalysts for CO2 hydrogenation to formate.

To understand the role of lattice-confined Co single atoms in promoting the reaction, H2 temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) characterizations were conducted to study the in-situ reduction process. With the consumption of H2 in the H2-TPR test, only signals of H2O were detected without H2S signal (Supplementary Fig. 10), indicating that the formation of SVs in the reduction atmosphere is mainly due to the removal of O* species occupying the SVs. Figure 3c shows that the reduction of the Co-MoS2 catalyst is accompanied with H2 consumption peaks at 146 and 196 °C, which are notably lower than the peaks at 171 and 218 °C for the pristine MoS2 catalyst. This suggests that the O* species on the Co-MoS2 catalyst are less stable and more readily reduced by H2 compared to those on the pristine MoS2 catalyst. NO adsorption DRIFTS spectra show that, in addition to the characteristic vibrational bands around 1789 and 1700 cm−1 arising from NO adsorption on Mo sites, an additional vibrational bond at 1871 cm−1 was observed on Co-MoS2 (Supplementary Fig. 11), which could be assigned to the weaker NO adsorption on Co sites27. It suggests Co site possesses a lower activity than the Mo site, which accounts for the weakened adsorption of O* species at the Co sites of Co‑MoS2 catalyst. In addition, the NO adsorption capacity on the reduced Co-MoS2 catalyst is more than two times that on the reduced MoS2 catalyst (130 vs. 63 μmol gcat.−1 at 200 °C, 47 vs. 23 μmol gcat.−1 at 160 °C, Fig. 3d). According to the density functional theory (DFT)-optimized NO adsorption structures on double SVs of pristine MoS2 and Co-MoS2 (Supplementary Fig. 12)20, the SVs density on Co-MoS2 is estimated to be 2.8–4.1 times that of pristine MoS2. These results indicate that the lattice-confined Co single atoms are beneficial for the formation of more SVs on MoS2. The Arrhenius plots, derived from the formate production rate at various reaction temperatures (Fig. 2c), indicate that the apparent activation energies (Ea) for CO2 hydrogenation on both the Co-MoS2 and pristine MoS2 catalysts are nearly identical (Fig. 3e). However, the Co-MoS2 catalyst shows markedly higher CO2 hydrogenation activity than the pristine MoS2 catalyst, which could be attributed to the increased number of SVs on Co-MoS2 induced by the lattice-confined Co single atoms (Fig. 3f)28.

DFT calculations on active site and reaction pathway

To elucidate the role of the lattice-confined Co single atoms in enhancing the catalytic activity of MoS2, we have carried out DFT calculations to simulate the evolution of SVs during hydrogenation and the reaction mechanism of CO2 hydrogenation to formate. Double-SV configurations were employed for studying the reaction process (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 13). The results show that both in-plane and edge SV-confined metal sites on Co-MoS2 or pristine MoS2 are highly active for the adsorption of O2 with dissociative adsorption energies (∆Gads) ranging from −1.90 eV to −7.97 eV (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 14), thus are susceptible to blockage by oxygen20. This aligns with the immediate formation of Mo-O species when O2 passes through the reduced catalysts, as mentioned above (Fig. 3b). The regeneration of in-plane SVs of pristine MoS2 requires a high activation energy of 2.71 eV, which is notably higher than that of 1.68 eV for the regeneration of edge SVs (Fig. 4b), which could be the reason for the low activity of the basal plane-dominated pristine MoS2 catalyst20. In comparison, for the Co-MoS2, the energy barriers for the regeneration of Co-decorated in-plane SVs and edge SVs are markedly decreased to 1.45 eV and 0.51 eV, respectively (Fig. 4b, and Supplementary Figs. 15, 16), indicating that Co substituting Mo can significantly promote the formation of SVs. This is resulted by the weakened interaction between the lattice-confined Co-Mo pair and oxygen at the SVs, as revealed by the decreased overlapping between the Co 3d (or Mo 4d) and O 2p orbitals compared with that between the Mo 4d and O 2p orbitals in the case of pristine MoS2 (Fig. 4c). In addition, the higher electron density of states near the Fermi level for the oxygen at the SV-confined Co-Mo sites suggests the increased activity of the adsorbed oxygen atom on Co-MoS2, which is favorable for the removal of oxygen by H2 to generate the SVs (Fig. 4c).

The reaction pathways for the CO2 hydrogenation were then studied on the SV-confined metal sites. At the in-plane SV-confined Co-Mo sites, the CO2 hydrogenation to COOH* intermediate is energetically more favorable with a lower energy barrier of 0.92 eV than that of 1.19 eV for the dissociation to CO* and O* (Fig. 4d). This is different from the facile dissociation of CO2* to CO* and O* at the SV-confined Mo sites of pristine MoS221, due to the relatively lower reactivity of the SV-confined Co-Mo sites than the pristine Mo sites. The COOH* pathway is also more favorable than the HCOO* pathway which has a notably high energy barrier of 1.83 eV (Fig. 4d). In addition, the H2O-mediated mechanism was also investigated considering the water environment20. On the in-plane SV-confined Co-Mo sites, although H2O can be readily dissociated to OH* and H* with a low energy barrier of 0.43 eV, the subsequent hydrogenation of CO2 to HCOO* is very difficult with a high energy barrier of 2.71 eV (Supplementary Fig. 17a). In the case of edge SV-confined Co-Mo sites, the direct adsorption and hydrogenation of CO2 is also more favorable than the adsorption and dissociation of H2O* into OH* and H* with a high energy barrier of 1.51 eV (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 17b). These results suggest that the H2O-mediated mechanism is not likely at the SV-confined Co-Mo sites and the reaction proceeds via the direct hydrogenation of CO2* by adsorbed H2* through the COOH* pathway.

Discussion

The highly active SV-confined Mo sites of MoS2 are susceptible to oxygen blockage when exposed to air, which can hardly be removed under moderate reaction conditions. In this study, we demonstrate that the in-situ regeneration of oxygen-blocked SVs during the CO2 hydrogenation reaction can be enabled by confining Co atoms into MoS2 lattice to substitute Mo atoms, resulting in a remarkable increase in the production rate of formate. Notably, the Co-MoS2 catalyst achieves a high formate production rate of 17.0 mmol gcat.−1 h−1 at 200 °C, which is nearly three times that of the pristine MoS2 catalyst and surpasses previously reported non-precious metal catalysts. This work establishes a strategy for fabricating oxygen-tolerant sites through heteroatom confinement within the lattice, providing a valuable reference for designing robust catalysts via precise structural manipulation at atomic level.

Methods

Preparation of catalysts

The MoS2 catalyst was synthesized using the method reported previously20,21,29. The Co-MoS2 catalyst was synthesized through the similar method to MoS2 with the addition of Co(NO3)2·6H2O as Co source. Typically, for the synthesis of Co-MoS2, 900 mg of (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O and a specified amount of Co(NO3)2·6H2O were dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water. Then, the mixture and 10 mL CS2 were transferred into a 40 mL stainless steel autoclave in the glovebox and followed by maintaining at 400 °C for 4 h. The product was treated with 6 M KOH at 60 °C for 3 h and then washed with deionized water and dried.

Catalytic tests of catalysts in CO2 hydrogenation

The catalytic reactions were performed in a stainless-steel autoclave with a capacity of 78 mL. Typically, 20 mg of catalyst was added into 10 mL of 0.5 M KHCO3 aqueous solution. After flushing with reaction gas (23% CO2, 69% H2, and 8% Ar as internal standard, or 24% CO2, 72% H2, and 4% N2 as internal standard) for three times, the autoclave was pressurized to 2.5 MPa. Then the reaction proceeded under stirring at 200 °C for 10 h. After the reaction, the possible gas-phase products were detected via gas chromatography (GC-2014, Shimadzu or GC-8860, Angilent) with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and flame ionization detector (FID). The formate product was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) equipped with refractive index and UV detectors using a Shodex SUGARSH-1011 (8 × 300 mm) column. Materials characterization

PXRD measurements were conducted on a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation at 40 kV and 30 mA. ICP-OES tests were conducted on Thermo Fisher Scientific iCAP 7400. X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra, including XANES and EXAFS, were measured at the BL14W1 beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. TEM was conducted on FEI Tecnai F20 Transmission Electron Microscope and the accelerating voltage was 200 kV. Atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM images and EELS mapping were performed on a spherical aberration-corrected Nion U-HERMES100. NO chemisorption measurements were performed on a Micromeritics Auto Chem II 2920 instrument. Typically, catalysts were in-situ pretreated with 10% H2/He at the specific temperature for 2 h and subsequently purged with He for 1 h. NO chemisorption experiments were then performed at 0 °C by pulsing 500 μL of 2% NO/He through the catalyst every 30 min. For the NO chemisorption of the untreated catalysts, experiments followed the same processes except for the H2 pretreatment. The double SVs of pristine MoS2 can adsorb four NO molecules20, whereas the Co-decorated double SVs at Mo-edge and in-plane of Co-MoS2 can only adsorb three and two NO molecules, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 12). Using the measured NO adsorption capacities (Fig. 3d), the SVs densities are estimated as 15.75 μmol gcat.−1 for pristine MoS2 (63/4 μmol gcat.−1) and 43.3–65 μmol gcat.−1 for Co-MoS2 (130/3–130/2 μmol gcat.−1). On these bases, although Co-MoS2 exhibits a twofold higher total NO adsorption capacity, its density of SVs is estimated to be 2.8–4.1 times that of pristine MoS2. In situ DRIFTS and ex situ FT-IR measurements were performed using a Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Nicolet 6700 for O2 adsorption tests and ex situ FT-IR tests; Vertex 70 v for NO adsorption tests) equipped with a mercury cadmium telluride detector. For the O2 adsorption tests, the catalysts were in-situ pretreated with H2 at 200 °C for 2 h. Then, the catalysts were exposed to 2% O2/Ar at 25 °C after purging with He. For the NO adsorption test, the catalysts were in-situ pretreated with H2 at 200 °C for 2 h. Then, the catalysts were exposed to 2% NO/Ar at 25 °C after purging with He. Finally, the unabsorbed NO was swept with He. The H2 temperature-programmed reduction tests were performed on a Micromeritics Auto Chem II 2920 instrument. Typically, catalysts were in-situ pretreated with He at 80 °C for 1 h and subsequently switched feeding gas to 10% H2/He. The temperature was elevated to 240 °C with a rate of 4 °C min−1 and maintained for 2 h.

Computational details

The structure optimization and the pathways for CO2 hydrogenation to HCOOH on various catalysts were calculated using DFT with spin polarization, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP 5.4.4) code30–33. The projector augmented wave (PAW) method was used to describe the interactions between ion cores and valence electrons34,35. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was used to describe the exchange-correlation interactions36,37. Dispersion interactions were considered by applying D3 corrections38,39. For the plane-wave expansion, a 400 eV energy off was used. The convergence criterion for electronic structure iteration was set to be 10−5 eV and that for geometry optimization 0.02 eV/Å. To calculate the transition states (TS), the fixed bond length (FBL) method implemented in the Atomic Simulation Environment was used40. The free energies of adsorbed reaction intermediates were calculated as G = Etotal + ZPE + Hvib − TSvib, where Etotal is the total energy calculated by DFT, ZPE is the zero point energy, Hvib and Svib represent the enthalpy and entropy contributions of non-imaginary vibrations, which are derived from the harmonic approximation to the potential at the temperature T. The free energies of gas molecules were calculated as Etotal + ZPE + ∫0TCpdT − TS, where Etotal is DFT-calculated total energy, ZPE is the zero-point energy, T is temperature, ∫0TCpdT presents the integrated heat capacity from 0 K to T, and S is the entropy under ideal gas approximation. A tri-layer model of MoS2 with confined Co single atoms was built in a 6 × 6 supercell to simulate the in-plane structure of Co-MoS2 (Supplementary Fig. 13). A nanoribbon model with lattice-confined Co single atoms was constructed in an 8 × 8 supercell to simulate the edges with the Mo edge saturated with S monomers and the S edge saturated with a combination of S monomers and dimers (Supplementary Fig. 13)41,42. Different confining sites were discussed, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 13. The most stable confining structures were adopted to simulate the in-plane (s2) and Mo-edge (s2) structure of Co-MoS2. The vacuum spaces were set larger than 20 Å. For the Brillouin zones sampling, a Monkhorst-Pack 3 × 3 × 1 k-point mesh was selected for the in-plane structure of Co-MoS2 and a Monkhorst-Pack 1 × 1 1 k-point mesh was used for the Mo-edge structure of Co-MoS2, respectively43.
