Associations between fatigue, burnout, and risk behaviors in mental health occupational therapists: a cross-sectional study of coping and substance use
Authors: Miguel-Ángel Talavera-Valverde, Luis-Javier Márquez-Álvarez, Ana-Isabel Souto-Gómez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOccupational therapists working in mental health services face sustained emotional demands, high workload, and clinical complexity, which may increase burnout risk. While Maslach’s three-dimensional model remains the gold standard for conceptualization, a gap persists in integrating physiological factors such as fatigue and behavioral risk outcomes within a theoretically derived framework in occupational therapy.ObjectiveTo analyze the associations between fatigue and burnout among mental health occupational therapists in Spain, and to examine the relationships among emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and substance use within a theoretically derived model.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted with a nationwide sample of 663 occupational therapists. Burnout was assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory, fatigue with the Chalder Fatigue Scale, and coping strategies with the COPE-28. Correlational analyses, hierarchical regressions, and structural equation modeling were performed.ResultsFatigue was significantly associated with higher emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, and lower personal accomplishment. Sleep and concentration problems were strongly associated with emotional exhaustion. The structural model revealed associations consistent with a theoretically derived sequence linking fatigue, emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and substance use. Problem-focused coping was associated with lower depersonalization without reducing baseline fatigue.ConclusionsBurnout in mental health occupational therapists may be understood within a theoretically derived framework in which fatigue is associated with emotional exhaustion and, in turn, with depersonalization and substance use. These findings provide preliminary evidence consistent with a theoretically derived sequence of associations, although causal inferences cannot be established due to the cross-sectional design.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Occupational therapy, Mental Health, Burnout, Fatigue, Emotional exhaustion, Coping strategies, Substance use, Structural equation modeling

Introduction

Mental health services constitute one of the healthcare contexts with the highest emotional, cognitive, and ethical demands for healthcare professionals. Prolonged exposure to psychological suffering, the clinical complexity of cases, and sustained workload create work environments characterized by high demands and frequently insufficient resources, which may increase the risk of professional well-being deterioration and may compromise the sustainability of clinical practice [1, 2]. In this context, burnout has emerged as a priority occupational health problem, with direct implications for care quality, service continuity, and professional retention. In Spain, studies have documented high burnout prevalence among occupational therapists, indicating that over 60% exhibit significant emotional exhaustion symptoms and highlighting the impact of sociodemographic and work-related factors on this professional reality [3].

Within this framework, occupational therapy occupies a particularly vulnerable position. Work in mental health requires intense relational engagement, constant management of the professional role, and continuous adaptation of tasks and activities in contexts of high clinical uncertainty. In Spain, where the estimated population of registered occupational therapists is 8,450 [4], these professionals face not only the emotional burden of their work but also high rates of intention to leave the profession and occupational performance deterioration linked to burnout [3, 5]. Previous studies have documented high burnout prevalence associated with intention to leave the profession [6], occupational performance decline, loss of occupational balance [5, 6], and difficulties sustaining interventions focused on participation and meaningful activity [3, 7–9].

These findings have raised growing concern about the occupational sustainability of the profession in mental health services. Although meta-analyses support the association between fatigue and burnout [10], no study has examined the associations consistent with a sequential pathway from fatigue through emotional exhaustion and depersonalization to substance use in Spanish occupational therapists.

The three-dimensional model proposed by Maslach—emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment—remains the gold standard for conceptualizing burnout [11]. However, its explanatory power is limited in professions that require sustained functional emotional distancing, complex occupational role management, and long-term maintenance of occupational balance, as occurs in occupational therapy in mental health [5]. In these contexts, burnout may be conceptualized as a process involving broader physiological, cognitive, and organizational determinants [12].

Within this framework, emotional exhaustion is commonly understood as being associated with higher levels of depersonalization as a coping or defensive response to sustained emotional overload.

In this regard, occupational fatigue emerges as a key factor associated with professional wear of professional wear. Beyond the subjective perception of tiredness, fatigue is characterized by sustained depletion of energy resources associated with sleep disturbances, cognitive overload, and concentration difficulties, which may compromise emotional self-regulation and daily occupational performance [13]. According to effort–recovery and demand–resources models, the accumulation of demands without adequate recovery opportunities may represent a physiological basis associated with the development of emotional exhaustion [1, 9, 14]. At the international level, recent meta-analyses have identified multiple factors associated with burnout in occupational therapists, including workload, job satisfaction, and organizational resources, reinforcing the need to better integrate fatigue within theoretical models of burnout in occupational therapists [10]. However, in the occupational therapy literature, fatigue has been addressed fragmentarily and rarely integrated as a relevant factor associated with burnout within theoretically grounded models.

From the perspective of the effort–recovery and Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) models, sustained job demands without adequate recovery are associated with progressive depletion of energetic resources, which may manifest as fatigue and may contribute to emotional exhaustion. In this sense, fatigue may be conceptualized as a factor associated with increased vulnerability to burnout-related processes.

Simultaneously, coping strategies [15] deployed by occupational therapists in high-demand care settings may show complex, and sometimes paradoxical, patterns [17]. While problem-focused and support-focused coping styles [18] are consistently associated with lower depersonalization and higher personal accomplishment, recent research suggests that strategies traditionally considered adaptive, such as positive reinterpretation, may generate cognitive dissonance and contribute to wear in chronically precarious organizational contexts [17, 19]. Similarly, forms of functional self-blame or partial behavioral disengagement may operate as transient occupational resources that sustain perceptions of efficacy and professional competence [20], allowing the therapeutic role to be maintained in the short term, though potentially at the cost of greater vulnerability to burnout when these strategies become chronic [17, 21, 22].

A further underexplored aspect in occupational therapy is the emergence of risk behaviors as an outcome of the wear process. In other healthcare groups, depersonalization has been described as a proximal defensive mechanism that may be associated with avoidance responses to distress, including substance use [23]. However, this sequence has rarely been examined in an integrated manner in occupational therapists, despite its clinical, ethical, and organizational implications.

Overall, the literature reveals a critical gap: the absence of integrative models that articulate occupational fatigue, burnout dimensions, coping strategies [15–17], and risk behaviors within a coherent explanatory sequence. To date, no study has examined these variables within an integrated framework in occupational therapists, nor tested whether their associations are consistent with a theoretically derived sequential model. This gap is particularly relevant for occupational therapists working in mental health services in Spain, where high workload, continuous exposure to psychological suffering, and limited availability of recovery resources may intensify the risk of wear and complicate the design of multicomponent preventive interventions.

Therefore, the general objective of this study was to analyze the relationships between fatigue and burnout in occupational therapists working in mental health services in Spain, as well as their associations with coping strategies and risk behaviors, within a theoretically derived occupational distress model. Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, this model is approached as a theoretically informed framework rather than a definitive temporal or causal sequence. Specifically, the study aimed to: (a) examine the sequential mediation of fatigue on substance use through emotional exhaustion and depersonalization; (b) identify paradoxical occupational patterns in coping strategy use and their relationship with burnout dimensions; and (c) evaluate the protective role of problem-focused coping on depersonalization and personal accomplishment.

Based on the theoretical frameworks described above, the following hypotheses were proposed: H1: Fatigue will be positively associated with the core dimensions of burnout; H2: Burnout dimensions, will show a positive association with risk behaviors, specifically substance use; H3: Fatigue will demonstrate a significant indirect association with substance use, sequentially mediated through the levels of burnout.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a quantitative, observational, cross-sectional design. This design was chosen for its suitability in examining associations between variables relevant to the study question. The STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies were used as a reference [24].

Study setting

The study was conducted across Spain, involving professionals working in public and private institutions (hospital or community centers) with mental health services from September to December 2025. Given that working conditions in these settings are often characterized by high demand, stress, and exposure to emotional crises [7, 8], this setting was selected due to the need to examine factors affecting the well-being of occupational therapists working in mental health in Spain.

Participants

Non-probabilistic convenience sampling was employed through professional associations and occupational therapy colleges. The target population included occupational therapists practicing in Spain with at least six months of professional experience and currently working in a mental health care facility of any type (hospitals, community centers, specialized clinics, or family associations, among others). Professionals with less than six months of mental health experience or those not actively employed at the time of completing the questionnaire were excluded, as the study aimed to explore patterns associated with sustained work over time.

Data from the Spanish National Institute of Statistics (INE) report a total of 8,450 registered occupational therapists in Spain [4]. Although the number of occupational therapists working in mental health settings is much lower, no official data are available. Given the non-probabilistic convenience sampling method employed, strict population representativeness cannot be assumed. Therefore, the required sample size was not determined based on population probability, but rather on the need to ensure adequate statistical power for the planned multivariate analyses. Specifically, a large sample size (n > 500) was targeted to robustly support Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) and meet the recommended case-to-parameter ratios for maximum likelihood estimation, following Kline’s guidelines [25]. The final sample of N = 663 sufficiently met these statistical power requirements.

A total of n = 689 responses were collected, of which 26 were excluded for not meeting the established eligibility criteria or for containing incomplete data. The final sample for analysis consisted of n = 663 occupational therapists.

The mean age of participants was 38.69 years, with a median of 39 years (SD = 8.10; interquartile range: 14). A marked predominance of female participants was observed (n = 543; 81.9%) compared to 18.1% male (n = 120).

Regarding occupational characteristics, the public administration sector was the most represented (n = 324; 48.9%), followed by private companies (25.9%) and the third sector (21.0%). Participants reported an average of 14.50 years (SD = 8.52) of professional experience specifically as occupational therapists. The average weekly working hours were 34.06 (SD = 8.29).

Variables

Independent variables included sociodemographic aspects such as age, sex, years of experience as an occupational therapist, type of workplace, and work schedule. Dependent variables included burnout as the primary outcome, and fatigue and coping strategies as secondary outcomes.

Burnout was measured using the Spanish version of the Maslach Burnout Inventory for Human Services (MBI-HSS) [26], one of the most recognized tools that ensures comparability with previous international literature. This version consists of 22 items exploring three key dimensions of burnout: depersonalization (DP), personal accomplishment (PA), and emotional exhaustion (EE). The scale has been validated in diverse professional contexts and is considered essential for assessing the impact of work-related stress on mental and emotional health, particularly for healthcare professionals such as occupational therapists. Historically, in the Spanish normative validation [26], the PA and EE subscales have shown acceptable values (Cronbach’s alpha of 0.72 and 0.87 respectively); in contrast, the DP subscale has shown a low Cronbach’s alpha (0.57), largely due to its short 5-item structure. In our sample, reliability measured by Cronbach’s alpha (⍺)and McDonald’s omega (⍵) was ⍺= 0.81, ⍵= 0.82 for EE; ⍺= 0.66, ⍵= 0.68 for PA; and ⍺= 0.60, ⍵= 0.62 for DP. Although the internal consistency for Depersonalization remains modest, the omega coefficient supports its retention for analytical purposes.

Fatigue was assessed using the Chalder Fatigue Scale [27, 28], which measures perceived fatigue as the set of subjective feelings of being tired, requiring greater effort, or finding it harder to start and maintain physical and cognitive activities. We administered the Spanish version (Sp-CFS) [26], which has historically shown high reliability (⍺=0.92, ω = 0.92). This 13-item tool is divided into four factors, which in previous validations presented the following ordinal alphas: Low Energy (0.91), Sleep Problems (0.91), Concentration Problems (0.88), and Cognitive Dysfunction (0.81), providing information on how fatigue affects daily functioning. In our sample, the global reliability coefficients were ⍺=0.82, ω = 0.83. For the specific subscales, internal consistency was: Low Energy (⍺=0.69, ω = 0.73); Sleep Problems (⍺=0.55, ω = 0.55); Concentration Problems (⍺=0.70, ω = 0.73); and Cognitive Dysfunction (⍺=0.60, ω = 0.63).

Finally, the COPE-28 Inventory [29] was used to assess coping strategies employed by professionals in the workplace. This 28-item questionnaire identifies how workers manage stress and challenges associated with their professional performance. The COPE-28 evaluates a broad range of coping strategies, from action- and problem-focused approaches to more emotional responses. In the Spanish version [30], the 28 items are grouped into 14 factors, further combined into four higher-order factors, different from the original scale. Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients were 0.74 for Cognitive Coping, 0.74 for Social Coping, 0.80 for Spiritual Coping, and 0.71 for Coping Blockage.

Variables were categorized according to their role in the theoretically derived model: burnout dimensions were treated as outcome variables, fatigue as the main exposure variable, emotional exhaustion and depersonalization as mediating variables, and sociodemographic and occupational characteristics as potential confounders.

Procedures

Data collection took place from September to December 2025 using online questionnaires via Microsoft Forms. The survey was distributed through occupational therapy colleges, national professional organizations, and professional associations across Spain. Because the survey utilized open distribution channels rather than direct, individualized mailing lists, an exact response rate could not be calculated. Given the inherent challenges in accessing this specific target population, this open-link design was considered the most viable approach for examining the prevalence and associations of fatigue, burnout, and coping strategies among these professionals.

The questionnaire was first administered to a small pilot group of n > 20 occupational therapists who met inclusion criteria, after which necessary adjustments were made based on their feedback, and responses from this pilot phase were excluded.

Subsequently, contact was made with the main field-entry agents. After an initially agreed period of three months and reaching the minimum sample size, the questionnaire was closed to comply with current legislation.

Data processing, including database cleaning and complete case analysis, was conducted immediately following the closure of data collection in late December 2025. All subsequent statistical analyses and structural equation modeling were executed sequentially during January 2026.

Data analysis

Prior to analysis, missing data were handled using listwise deletion (complete case analysis). Specifically, 26 questionnaires were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria or for containing incomplete data, resulting in a robust final sample of N = 663 complete records. Total and mean scores for each scale were calculated and treated as continuous variables for statistical analysis using R software (version 4.5.1).

An initial descriptive analysis of all variables was performed. Quantitative variables were described using measures of central tendency and dispersion (mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range) due to non-normal distribution of all variables, confirmed by Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p < 0.01). Qualitative variables were summarized as absolute frequencies and percentages to characterize the sample. Spearman correlation coefficients were used to compare burnout, fatigue, and coping strategy scores.

Following data-driven guidance, sequential analysis was conducted using multiple linear regressions, followed by a mediation analysis. This procedure was framed within a structural equation modeling (SEM) approach guided by a theoretically derived model of occupational distress and complemented with a data-driven perspective. SEM was evaluated using recommended fit indices: CFI > 0.95, TLI > 0.95, and RMSEA < 0.08 [31], employing robust maximum likelihood estimation due to data non-normality. A significance level of p < 0.05 was applied for all analyses, and results were presented with 95% confidence intervals. The significance of indirect effects within the structural equation model was evaluated using a bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 resamples to compute 95% confidence intervals, implemented via the lavaan package.

Ethical and legal considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association: Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects (revised October 2013) [32]. Research was carried out with institutional approval, following the “Norms and Operational Guidelines for Ethical Review of Health-Related Research Involving Human Participants” of the World Health Organization [33]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the University of A Coruña (Approval Code: 2025-022). The manuscript was prepared following STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies. All participants received written information about the study and provided informed consent prior to participation. Data were collected anonymously and managed in accordance with applicable Spanish data protection regulations.

A strong positive association was confirmed between fatigue symptoms and burnout dimensions. Although all subscales of the Chalder Fatigue Scale correlated in some way with burnout, Cognitive Dysfunction showed the weakest correlations with burnout dimensions. Total fatigue scores were robustly correlated with Emotional Exhaustion (φ= 0.45, p < 0.01) and Depersonalization (φ = 0.25, p < 0.01), while showing an inverse relationship with Personal Accomplishment (φ = − 0.32, p < 0.01). Among specific factors, Sleep Problems (Factor 2 of the Chalder Scale) showed the strongest association with Emotional Exhaustion (φ = 0.39, p < 0.01).

The analysis revealed complex patterns in coping strategy use. Social Support coping appeared to have the greatest appeared to be associated with lower levels of depersonalization against depersonalization, though the difference compared to cognitive coping was minimal (Cognitive Coping: φ = − 0.107, p < 0.01; Social Support Coping: φ = − 0.112, p <0.01). Both promoted Personal Accomplishment, with cognitive coping showing a stronger effect (Cognitive Coping: φ = 0.20, p < 0.01; Social Support Coping: φ = 0.10, p < 0.01).

Spiritual coping was the only strategy significantly protective against emotional exhaustion, though the correlation was low (φ = 0.10, p < 0.01). Data also suggest an inverse relationship, indicating it may decrease levels of Personal Accomplishment. The Coping Blockage factor was positively associated with Personal Accomplishment, though subfactor analysis revealed more explanatory detail.

The Substance Use strategy displayed a high-risk profile, with the highest positive correlation with depersonalization among all strategies evaluated (φ = 0.31, p < 0.01), and also correlated with Emotional Exhaustion (φ = 0.17, p < 0.01).

Counterintuitive relationships were identified for certain strategies. For example, Self-Blame correlated negatively with Emotional Exhaustion (φ = − 0.13, p < 0.01) and positively with Personal Accomplishment (φ = − 0.19, p < 0.01), suggesting a functional role in this sample. Similarly, avoidant strategies such as Behavioral Disengagement (φ= 0.18, p <0.01) and Denial (φ= 0.13, p < 0.01) were positively associated with Personal Accomplishment. Positive Reinterpretation showed an unexpected positive correlation with Emotional Exhaustion (φ = 0.18, p < 0.01). Finally, Emotional Support and Religion appeared to were negatively associated with Personal Accomplishment (φ = − 0.157, p < 0.01; φ = − 0.86, p <0.05, respectively).

Regression analysis

Given the correlations observed, a series of planned linear regression analyses were conducted to test the hypothesis of a theoretically derived sequence of associations linking fatigue, burnout dimensions, and maladaptive coping. This stepwise approach allows examining the specific contribution of each variable in the wear chain, before examining their joint behavior through a structural equation model (SEM).

The first analysis confirmed that the fatigue profile explained 24.6% of the variance in Emotional Exhaustion (F(2, 660) φ = 107.72, p < 0.001). Specifically, Concentration Problems (φ= 0.29, p < 0.001) and Sleep Problems (φ= 0.29, p < 0.001) were strongly associated with Emotional Exhaustion is may be associated with sleep deprivation and cognitive effort.

In the second step, we examined whether depersonalization is more strongly associated with fatigue or with emotional exhaustion. The model (R² = 0.150) showed that Emotional Exhaustion showed the strongest association (φ = 0.33, t = 8.37, p < 0.001). Although Sleep Problems subscale maintained a smaller statistical significance (φ= 0.10, p = 0.009), its explanatory contribution was three times lower than that of exhaustion. This supports the idea that depersonalization may be more closely associated with emotional overload, potentially functioning as a defensive mechanism rather than being linked to mere physical tiredness.

The third model identified the critical pathway toward risk behaviors. Analysis showed that higher levels of depersonalization was significantly associated with Substance Use (β = 0.31, t = 8.33, p < 0.001), accounting for 9.5% of the variance in this behavior (R² = 0.095). Clinically, these findings may be consistent with a pattern in which substance use is associated with higher levels of emotional disconnection, with depersonalization closely linked to risk behaviors.

Depersonalization was significantly associated with substance use (β = 0.08, z = 8.34, p < 0.001). Although the unstandardized coefficient may appear small, the z-value (8.34) indicates a robust statistical association. This apparent discrepancy is attributable to the metric properties of the substance use scale, which uses a restricted range, and the naturally low variance of these behaviors in an active professional population, mathematically compressing regression coefficients without diminishing the validity of the association. Additionally, significant indirect effects were confirmed. Specifically, sleep problems demonstrated a significant indirect association with substance use through the sequential mediation of Emotional Exhaustion and Depersonalization (Indirect Effect = 0.031, 95% CI [0.019, 0.044], p < 0.001). Similarly, concentration problems also showed a significant indirect effect on substance use via this same pathway (Indirect Effect = 0.026, 95% CI [0.016, 0.038], p < 0.001).

To further explore the cross-sectional nature of the data and test for potential bidirectional associations, an additional linear regression was conducted outside the primary SEM framework. This analysis examined the inverse relationship between substance use and emotional exhaustion to assess whether these variables might interact in a vicious cycle rather than a strict temporal sequence. Results indicated that substance use was positively associated with levels of emotional exhaustion (β = 0.19, t = 4.91, p < 0.001), explaining 3.5% of the variance (R² = 0.035). This suggests that mechanisms deployed to cope with distress are closely linked to higher baseline exhaustion, highlighting a potential vicious cycle rather than a unidirectional sequence.

Taken together, the overall associative network and the supplementary regression provide preliminary support for a theoretically derived model of occupational distress. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the data, these relationships preclude any definitive confirmation of causality or temporal progression.

Discussion

This study examined the interaction between fatigue, burnout dimensions, and coping strategies in occupational therapists working in mental health services, providing findings consistent with a theoretically derived model of occupational distress. Importantly, given the cross-sectional design of this study, the findings should be interpreted as associations consistent with a theoretically derived model rather than as evidence of causal or temporal relationships.

The results extend the classical three-dimensional burnout model by suggesting that fatigue may act as a physiological factor associated with burnout dimensions, with substance use potentially emerging as a related high-risk behavior. This pattern is consistent with the classical three-dimensional burnout model, in which motional exhaustion is generally considered to constitute the core of the syndrome, followed by depersonalization and reduced personal accomplishment [9, 11, 14, 9], but may also reflect the identification of fatigue as a prior physiological substrate and substance use as a high-risk behavioral outcome. These findings are consistent with recent evidence documenting elevated burnout levels in occupational therapists and their relationship with poorer health, increased intention to leave [6], and difficulties in sustaining practice centered on occupational participation and balance [3, 5, 10, 34]. These findings can be interpreted within the framework of occupational therapy practice, considering occupational performance, occupational balance, occupational participation, and occupational justice as relevant variables for understanding professional burnout [5]. In this sense, the results may reinforce concerns expressed by professional associations and recent studies regarding the impact of burnout on continuity of care, retention of occupational therapists, and quality of care [8, 10, 34, 35]. Four main findings can be identified:

The central finding: the “firewall” of burnout and the mediating role of emotional exhaustion in the relationship between fatigue and risk behaviors

Specific findings are consistent with a theoretically derived model of work-related burnout, in which physiological and cognitive fatigue is associated with emotional exhaustion [9, 15], and is further associated with maladaptive coping behaviors through depersonalization [16]. One of the central findings of the study can be described as a pattern consistent with a mediating role of emotional exhaustion in the relationship between fatigue and substance use. The results of the structural equation model suggest that fatigue, in its different manifestations, is not directly associated with risk behaviors, but its role may operate through emotional exhaustion and depersonalization [9, 14]. This finding suggests that physiological tiredness alone may be insufficient to explain the emergence of risk behaviors, challenging simplistic models of direct self-medication.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that burnout may be interpreted as a mediating process: while fatigue remains at a functional level, it is not necessarily associated with maladaptive behaviors; however, when fatigue is associated with sustained emotional exhaustion [14, 15], defensive responses may be more likely. This pattern is consistent with the Conservation of Resources (COR) model [36], which suggests that the progressive loss of emotional resources may be linked to behaviors oriented toward avoidance or compensation of distress. In this context, depersonalization may be understood as a defensive mechanism of emotional disconnection [20], which, when chronic, may be associated with less appropriate clinical and personal decisions, including recourse to high-risk relief strategies [15, 17]. This central role of emotional exhaustion appears to align with research reviews and meta-analyses that identify this dimension as the one most strongly associated with health indicators, intention to leave the profession, and deterioration of professional performance in occupational therapists [3, 9, 10, 34, 37].

In terms of prevention, the full mediation model suggests that interventions aimed exclusively at reducing fatigue or controlling substance use may be limited if they do not specifically address emotional exhaustion as a “firewall” of the deterioration process. Early intervention targeting emotional exhaustion [9, 14, 15], given its association with higher levels of depersonalization and risk behaviors, may be particularly relevant. This may involve combining individual interventions (e.g., sleep hygiene, cognitive load management, early recognition of emotional exhaustion, critical review of costly coping strategies [15, 16, 17] such as positive reinterpretation in toxic contexts) with organizational interventions (e.g., regular clinical supervision, limits on accumulation of complex cases, shift redesign) [36, 38], which may contribute to the development of a comprehensive preventive approach that considers both personal and structural dimensions of work.

Furthermore, it may be beneficial to integrate strategies promoting occupational autonomy, occupational balance [5, 15], role adaptation, task modification, and workplace reorganization to maximize intervention effectiveness [17].

Finally, the identification of a pattern consistent with a theoretically derived model may support the potential implementation of periodic screening protocols for fatigue and burnout using standardized instruments (e.g., MBI and fatigue scales) in mental health services, potentially facilitating early detection and emotional exhaustion [9, 14, 15] in relation to depersonalization and risk behaviors.

This preventive approach may be particularly relevant in mental health contexts, where previous studies have documented elevated burnout and psychological distress among psychiatrists, psychologists, and nursing staff, suggesting that the identified mechanisms could be extrapolated to other healthcare groups [2, 23]. Specifically, studies in occupational therapists have shown that burnout is associated with intention to leave the position [36], decreased job satisfaction [5, 17], and difficulties in sustaining participation-centered interventions [3, 10, 34], reinforcing the need for systematic screening protocols in this group. Incorporating functional assessments, analysis of occupational tasks, and regular clinical supervision strategies may help strengthen prevention from the occupational therapy perspective [15, 38].

“Fatigue” as the great predator: physiological substrate of burnout in occupational therapy

The results suggest that fatigue is strongly associated with variance in emotional exhaustion [14, 15], with sleep and concentration problems being the most robust predictors. This finding is consistent with the conceptual distinction between fatigue, as a predominantly physiological-cognitive construct, and emotional exhaustion, as the psychological manifestation of burnout, providing clarity to a distinction that often appears blurred in the literature.

However, the interpretation of the Sleep Problems factor (F2) should be approached with caution. This subscale consists of a limited number of items, which may reduce its internal consistency and variability. In the present sample, the relatively low dispersion of scores suggests a high degree of homogeneity in responses, with most participants reporting similar levels of sleep-related difficulties. This restricted variability may attenuate the discriminative capacity of this factor and should be considered when interpreting its relative contribution within the fatigue construct.

In this sense, the data suggest that, in the context of occupational therapy in mental health, burnout may be understood as being associated with a prior base of energy depletion. This approach is consistent with effort–recovery models, which suggest that the absence of adequate rest periods may be associated with sustained psychophysiological activation and emotional wear [13]. It also connects with the self-regulation depletion theory, which suggests that fatigue may reduce the capacity to modulate complex emotional responses [39].

From the perspective of the Job Demands–Resources model, these results are consistent with the idea that sustained demands associated with high patient load and elevated cognitive workload, when not accompanied by sufficient resources (rest periods, support, supervision) [36], may be linked to energy depletion processes associated with emotional exhaustion [1, 9, 14]. Evidence in occupational therapists suggests that case overload, time pressure, and complexity of care demands are among the main risk factors for burnout in this group [3, 10, 35]. This may highlight the relevance of monitoring occupational performance, occupational balance, occupational participation, and functional roles, as well as implementing task adaptation strategies and modification of the occupational environment [5, 17].

These findings suggest the importance of integrating physiological and organizational determinants in burnout prevention. For example, it may be important to review workload and ensure adequate recovery resources, potentially reducing the likelihood of basal fatigue becoming chronic and generating downstream associations on relational and ethical dimensions of professional work.

Moreover, the results suggest reconsidering burnout approaches focused exclusively on motivational or vocational variables, highlighting the need to shift the explanatory focus from the “person–vocation fit” toward material and temporal working conditions, especially in high-demand contexts. Promoting occupational autonomy, task planning, ergonomic adaptation, and role supervision may contribute to improving the sustainability of professional performance [38].

This preventive approach may be particularly relevant in mental health services, where previous studies have documented elevated burnout and psychological distress among psychiatrists, psychologists, and nursing staff, suggesting that the identified mechanisms could be extrapolated to other healthcare groups [2, 23]. However, the literature specific to occupational therapy points out that the content of the work—focused on occupation, roles, and participation—introduces particularities that justify the need for specific burnout prevention models in this group [3, 10, 34].

The paradoxes of coping: context-dependent coping strategies

One of the most relevant contributions of the study can be found in the identification of nonlinear and, in some cases, counterintuitive patterns in the use of coping strategies [16, 17]. Far from a rigid dichotomy between adaptive and maladaptive coping [16], the results suggest that the functionality of strategies may depend on the organizational context and the stage of the burnout process, highlighting three main patterns: (a) Positive reinterpretation, usually considered a protective strategy, was associated with higher levels of emotional exhaustion [9, 14, 15], suggesting that attempts to reframe situations positively may be linked to greater exhaustion. This finding can be interpreted in light of the concept of toxic positivity or cognitive dissonance: in an overloaded healthcare system, forcing oneself to positively reinterpret a precarious reality may entail additional metabolic and cognitive demands, potentially contributing to occupational fatigue and imbalance [5]. In contexts of chronic overload, this strategy may inadvertently contribute to wear [19]; (b) Functional self-blame showed associations with lower exhaustion and higher personal accomplishment [14]. A possible explanation is that this strategy may function as an internal locus of control mechanism: in occupational therapy, assuming responsibility (“I failed, not the system”) may provide a (potentially illusory) sense of control that temporarily protects against learned occupational helplessness, acting as a double-edged mechanism [18]. This pattern may help buffer, at least temporarily, learned helplessness described in chronic stress contexts [22], although its long-term effects require further research; (c) Disconnection to survive [20]: avoidant strategies such as behavioral disconnection or denial were positively associated with personal accomplishment, suggesting that these strategies may be linked to a greater sense of efficacy [17]. Clinically, this finding may reflect the need to establish functional occupational distance, differentiating therapeutic empathy from excessive emotional identification. To feel effective as an occupational therapist, it may sometimes be necessary to “switch off” intense emotional connection; partial disconnection may allow continued functioning and sense of usefulness, especially in environments with high exposure to psychological suffering and heavy occupational load [20].

Overall, these findings suggest that certain forms of “paradoxical coping” (e.g., functional self-blame, partial disconnection) may operate as temporary occupational resources to sustain perceived efficacy and professional competence, although potentially at the cost of an increased risk of chronic burnout if not accompanied by structural changes in working conditions [16]. Recent studies on coping and burnout in occupational therapists describe similar patterns of using distancing strategies, role redefinition, and meaning-making to sustain practice in high-demand contexts, with ambivalent results regarding emotional distress [17, 35, 40].

These paradoxes invite reconsideration of traditional coping taxonomies in terms of “adaptive” vs. “maladaptive,” proposing dynamic and contextual frameworks that consider the stage of the burnout process, occupational participation, occupational balance, and the organizational structure in which strategies are deployed [5, 17]. Furthermore, recent reviews on burnout in occupational therapy students and professionals highlight the need for qualitative research exploring the subjective experience of coping and burnout, incorporating the experience of professional role and occupational balance [5, 16, 41].

The “problem-focused” style: a relational protector rather than an energy-restorer, dignifies but does not cure

Problem-focused and social support–oriented coping styles [18] were associated with lower levels of depersonalization and higher levels of personal accomplishment, although, as previously shown, they do not appear to be directly associated with reductions in fatigue or emotional exhaustion [9, 14, 16]. This finding suggests that, while this style is not associated with lower levels of fatigue, it is associated with lower levels of depersonalization. This pattern suggests that these strategies do not restore energy resources, but they may protect the ethical, relational, and occupational dimensions of professional practice, allowing therapists to maintain humanity in patient care and reduce dehumanization in the therapeutic relationship [17].

From this perspective, problem-focused coping [16] may be understood as an occupational resource that may support professional meaning, enabling occupational therapy practice quality to be maintained even in high-demand care contexts. Solving technical problems may not be directly associated with recovery, but it may contribute to maintaining ethics, occupational competence, and humanity in patient care. Recent literature suggests that resources such as social support [18], clinical supervision [38], teamwork, and “job crafting” strategies are associated with lower burnout and higher professional engagement in occupational therapists, suggesting the protective role of these relational and organizational elements [3, 17, 35].

In terms of prevention, this finding suggests the importance of combining individual and organizational interventions to protect relational, ethical, and occupational resources, suggesting that problem-focused strategies may function as a “buffer” against depersonalization and deterioration of the patient–therapist relationship [17].

From a Job Demands–Resources model perspective, this type of coping could operate as a work and occupational resource, buffering the impact of demands on depersonalization and loss of meaning, without repeating the previously discussed ideas on fatigue or exhaustion, which may help explain why professionals maintain the quality of the therapeutic relationship even under high occupational load [12, 14, 15, 16]. This approach appears to align with recent proposals for occupational therapy burnout prevention programs advocating integrated interventions, combining workload adjustments, organizational support strengthening, and development of problem-focused coping skills [8, 10, 16, 18, 34, 35, 40].

Limitations

This study presents several limitations. First and foremost, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing definitive temporal or causal relationships. While the mediation analysis and SEM provide a robust framework for exploring associations consistent with occupational distress theories, the pathways identified represent indirect statistical links rather than confirmed mechanisms. Consequently, alternative directional models cannot be definitively ruled out [42].

Second, the use of non-probabilistic convenience sampling and online self-selection may have introduced selection bias, potentially over-representing professionals with higher levels of distress or a specific interest in burnout [3, 43]. Furthermore, reliance on self-report measures may introduce common method bias [44], which, along with the sampling method, may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader occupational therapy workforce.

Third, specific psychometric limitations must be acknowledged. The MBI-HSS depersonalization subscale, consisting of only 5 items, yielded a weak Cronbach’s alpha (0.60). Similarly, the Sleep Problems factor of the Chalder Fatigue Scale showed low reliability (⍺ = 0.55), likely due to its short length and the high homogeneity of the sample. Although the supplementary use of McDonald’s Omega provided slightly more acceptable values (0.62 and 0.55, respectively), and both constructs were retained to ensure theoretical coherence with the broader burnout literature, the specific statistical weight of these subscales within the associative network should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, the sample was predominantly female (81.9%). While this accurately reflects the gender distribution of the occupational therapy workforce, it introduces potential unmeasured confounders. Female professionals often face a “double burden” due to the interaction between occupational demands and unpaid caregiving or domestic responsibilities. Because specific non-occupational variables (e.g., marital status, caregiving roles, or dependent children) were not assessed, our ability to disentangle workplace-related fatigue from personal baseline exhaustion is limited.

Future research directions

These findings open opportunities for longitudinal studies to explore potential temporal relationships and possible reciprocal associations, such as the relationship between substance use, fatigue, and emotional exhaustion. Furthermore, it is recommended to incorporate objective indicators of performance, occupational participation, balance, and workload, as well as indirect methods to minimize self-report and social desirability biases. Finally, qualitative or mixed-method studies could explore the evolution of coping strategies, identifying when they cease to be functional and become risk factors for burnout in occupational therapists.

Conclusion

Overall, the results suggest that burnout in mental health occupational therapists may be understood within a theoretically derived framework in which occupational and physiological fatigue is associated with emotional exhaustion, which in turn is linked to defensive responses and risk behaviors, such as substance use. Coping strategies exhibit contextual and dynamic functionality, far from dichotomous classifications of “adaptive” versus “maladaptive.” Reducing basal fatigue and occupational overload emerges as an absolute priority, as effective psychological coping and the maintenance of occupational participation and balance are impossible without sustained physical energy.

Protecting the professional’s emotional, ethical, and occupational resources allows for preservation of both therapist well-being and the quality of care and occupational performance in mental health services. This integrative approach, which articulates physiological, emotional, behavioral, and organizational factors, provides a useful clinical perspective for understanding and addressing burnout from an occupational therapy viewpoint and constitutes a solid basis for designing preventive interventions and specific occupational resource preservation strategies in these services.

Overall, these findings contribute to the understanding of burnout processes in occupational therapists by providing preliminary evidence consistent with a theoretically derived sequence of associations, although causal inferences cannot be established due to the cross-sectional design.
