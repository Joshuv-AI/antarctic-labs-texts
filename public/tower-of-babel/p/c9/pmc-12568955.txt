Interaction effects of outdoor thermal comfort and air pollution
Authors: Tianyi Sun, Xiangzi Liu, Xunlei Liu, Kai Nan, Guangmeng Bian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban haze pollution is a major problem affecting outdoor environmental quality, thereby hindering the construction of ecological civilization and healthy urban development. The urban physical environment also affects the urban outdoor thermal environment. Both the thermal and air environment have important effects on the subjective comfort evaluations of people. In this study, four typical University Campus Green Spaces (UCGSs) in Xi’an, China, were selected to investigate the thermal and air environments in outdoor spaces. The adaptive thermal comfort due to the interaction between the outdoor thermal environment and air pollution was studied based on questionnaires and field measurements, and a strategy was developed for optimizing the thermal comfort of urban campus green space environments. The results showed that the influence of the thermal air quality environment on subjective comfort varied among different spaces. The influence of haze pollution on subjective comfort was greater in high and low-temperature environments, but less obvious in a moderate temperature environment. When the PM2.5 particle concentration was greater than 100 µg/m3, the evaluation of respiratory comfort was low in the high-temperature environment. The perception of air pollution was less sensitive in the thermal environment in covered road sections. Heat pollution and air pollution will affect human health to varying degrees. The outdoor thermal environment and patients with respiratory diseases are affected most by haze pollution.Keywords: Comprehensive comfort, Interaction effect, Subjective evaluation, Outdoor thermal comfort, University Campus Green Space (UCGS)Subject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Research background and significance

China’s rapid urbanization, with a rate of 66% in 2023 expected to reach 79% by 2040, has led to higher urban temperatures due to the urban heat island effect, affecting thermal comfort and quality of life1. Improved living standards haven’t mitigated issues like thermal discomfort, which affects mood, efficiency, and health2. A comfortable outdoor thermal environment encourages outdoor activities, benefiting physical and mental health7. Thus, outdoor thermal comfort has become a research focus point in urban environments8.

In this regard, with the acceleration of urbanization, air pollution, especially the increase in PM2.5 concentration, has become another major challenge in the urban environment9. Studies have shown that increased PM2.5 concentrations pose a serious threat to human health. Nowak et al. found that there is a potential relationship between PM2.5 and the incidence of lung cancer10, while Deng et al. showed that PM2.5 may be closely related to the incidence of cardiovascular and respiratory diseases11. More importantly, there is a complex interaction between PM2.5 and thermal comfort. Lin et al. pointed out that the interaction between the thermal environment and PM2.5 in urban outdoor spaces affects people’s thermal sensation votes12, while An et al. found that reducing PM2.5 concentrations can significantly improve people’s thermal comfort13. Therefore, studying the relationship between thermal comfort and air pollution, especially PM2.5, has become an important direction in urban environmental research.

Urban green spaces, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), include parks, gardens, and university campuses, providing various benefits14–16. Comfortable university campus green spaces enhance student life and health, promoting outdoor activities17. Previous studies in Guangzhou and Sichuan have highlighted the importance of accurate comfort evaluation indicators for optimizing campus environments18–20.

Research status and development trends

Thermal comfort research began indoors in the 1920s, focusing on factors like temperature, humidity, and wind speeds21,22. Outdoor thermal comfort studies started in the 1930s, gaining traction in the 1990s with advancements in data availability23–28. Recent studies have used software like ENVI-met and RAYMAN to predict bioclimatic parameters based on the outdoor physical environment29–31. However, the interaction between thermal comfort and air pollution, especially PM2.5, has become a critical concern due to its health impacts, including respiratory and cardiovascular issues32–34. In some large cities, PM2.5 concentrations far exceed national standards35, especially under extreme weather conditions, where air pollution interacts with extreme weather, further exacerbating people’s discomfort36,37. Taking Xi’an as an example, according to the results of PM2.5 pollution source analysis from 2020 to 2021, PM2.5 is the primary pollutant in the city, and its secondary pollution sources are the main source of PM2.5. In addition, the average concentration of PM2.5 in 2022 was 52 µg/m3, significantly higher than the national standard. This data highlights the urgency and necessity of effectively controlling air pollution38.

Main study objectives

Although previous studies have explored the effects of thermal comfort and air pollution on human health, few studies have considered the joint impact of both on comfort. This study investigates these interactions at Xi’an Jiaotong University’s Xingqing Campus. Using questionnaires and field measurements, we evaluated thermal and air quality comfort in campus green spaces. Our goals were to assess the impact of physical parameters on comfort and understand respondents’ perceptions of heat and air quality, providing a reference for future research on outdoor thermal comfort in university settings.

Materials and methods

Study site

Xi’an City, located in the Guanzhong Basin of the Weihe River Basin, China, has a warm temperate semi-humid continental monsoon climate, experiencing four distinct seasons: warm spring, hot summer, cool autumn, and cold winter. The annual average temperature ranges from 11 to 21 °C, and annual precipitation is 508–720 mm. This study was conducted at the Xingqing Campus of Xi’an Jiaotong University (108° 98′ 70.41″ E, 34° 24′ 65.03″ N) in Beilin District, Xi’an. The campus covers 2.6 km2 with a construction area of 1.8 km2. University Campus Green Spaces (UCGSs) are crucial for student activities such as socializing, leisure, entertainment, and physical exercise, directly impacting students’ quality of life39.

Four test spaces were selected within the Xingqing Campus: the Wutong tree-shaded section (SR), the empty area (CS), the green area (GA), and the residential area (RA). A questionnaire survey on thermal comfort and air quality monitoring was conducted in these areas (Fig. 1).

The questionnaire mainly involves residents’ perception of thermal sensation, thermal comfort, air quality, breathing sensation, etc. The questions in the questionnaire are all from previous relevant studies12,19. Investigators explained the study’s purpose to respondents, who completed the questionnaires voluntarily. Field investigations and measurements were carried out in four areas of Xingqing Campus, Xi’an Jiaotong University.

In addition, before conducting the research in this study, the researchers were familiar with the relevant ethical standards and ensured that all participants were fully informed about the purpose of the study, the subjects were fully aware of the purpose of our research, participated in the research voluntarily, and were informed that all personal information would be deidentified to remain anonymous. Experimental design

The most commonly used index for assessing thermal comfort is the Physiological Equivalent Temperature (PET)40. An et al. (2021) found that outdoor thermal comfort had little effect on SET in Harbin’s winter population41. Manoj et al. used PET and UTCI for outdoor thermal comfort measurements42. Mohamed demonstrated PMV’s use in outdoor environments and proposed PET as an alternative43. A formula was developed to estimate outdoor thermal sensation based on air temperature, wind speed, solar radiation, and humidity.

PET evaluates the human thermal environment by equating the heat budget of the human body under specific conditions. However, evaluations can be unstable due to varying factors. Therefore, we used the modified PET (mPET), which correlates better with TSV (Thermal Sensation Vote) and has greater sensitivity and fewer limitations44,45. Thermal Sensation Vote (TSV) refers to the subjective response of individuals regarding their perception of thermal comfort, typically expressed on a scale ranging from “cold” to “hot”. mPET considers meteorological parameters, clothing thermal resistance, and activity levels. We used RayMan Pro simulation software for mPET calculations, inputting parameters like date, time, temperature (Ta), wind speed (V), solar radiation (G), relative humidity, age, and clothing thermal resistance (Clo)46.

Environmental pollution, particularly PM2.5, affects air quality and outdoor activities on campuses. In Xi’an, PM2.5 concentrations measured in different seasons and locations did not exceed 150 µg/m3. Based on national standards, PM2.5 concentrations were categorized as: 0–50 µg/m3 (excellent), 50–100 µg/m3 (good), and 100–150 µg/m3 (slight pollution).

Data analysis

In this study, we employed a combination of Excel, SPSS, and GraphPad Prism for data analysis and result presentation. Initially, Excel was utilized to organize meteorological data, with a focus on calculating fundamental statistical metrics closely related to thermal comfort and air quality. These calculations provided a solid foundation for subsequent analyses. Subsequently, SPSS was applied for in—depth statistical exploration. Finally, GraphPad Prism was used to visualize the analytical results, presenting them in the form of figures and tables for clear and concise communication of the findings47.

Results

Combined impact on thermal assessment

We used linear regression analysis to analyze the effects of mPET and air pollution on thermal sensation (TSV) and thermal comfort (TCV) in the four spaces. The results showed that the slopes of the linear regression equations between TSV and mPET in the four spaces were 0.179 (RA), 0.068 (CS), 0.135 (SR) and 0.091 (GA), respectively. The slopes of the linear regression equations between TCV and mPET in the four spaces were 0.127 (RA), − 0.070 (CS), − 0.076 (SR) and − 0.036 (GA), respectively. In general, the temperature change in the residential area (RA) had the most significant effect on thermal sensation and thermal comfort, while the open space (CS) had the least effect, and the shaded roads (SR) and green spaces (GA) had a moderate effect (Fig. 3).

Figure 3 presents TSV and TCV evaluations from respondents in four spaces. In the residential area (RA) (Fig. 3b), the mPET range was 0.7–25.8 °C, with an average TSV of − 1.29. TSV values were categorized as 'Cool (−2)' below 12 °C and 'Warm (2)' above 20 °C, generally increasing with mPET. The average TCV was − 0.31, indicating moderate comfort. Lower PM2.5 concentrations (< 50 µg/m3) at low mPET resulted in lower TSV and TCV, indicating colder and less comfortable perceptions.

In the green area (GA) (Fig. 3c), TSV tended to be warm, increasing with mPET, while TCV decreased with mPET. The average TCV was − 0.04, suggesting moderate comfort.

At PM2.5 concentrations (100 µg/m3 > PM2.5 > 50 µg/m3), medium mPET temperatures resulted in TCV values of 0–1, indicating more comfortable.

For the empty area (CS) (Fig. 3d), the mPET range was 7.6–42.9 °C, with an average TSV of 1.10, indicating a slightly warm perception. TSV values were cooler below 15 °C and warmer above 30 °C, generally increasing with mPET. The average TCV was − 0.38, showing decreased comfort with increasing mPET.

In the shaded road (SR) area (Fig. 3e), the mPET range was 16.6–42.5 °C, with an average TSV of 1.05 and TCV of − 0.07, indicating slightly warm and moderately comfortable perceptions. PM2.5 concentrations had no significant effects on thermal comfort in these spaces.

TSV and TCV values reflect respondents’ feelings about the thermal–air quality environment. Extreme mPET values caused discomfort. PM2.5 concentration effects on thermal comfort were more evident in SR areas, with high PM2.5 causing discomfort in sheltered areas and low PM2.5 causing discomfort in open areas.

Thermal adaptation behavior

In summer, most respondents preferred walking to sheltered places and water bodies (34%), drinking ice water (30%), or returning to air-conditioned rooms (28%). Only 8% reduced clothing to improve thermal comfort, likely due to already minimal clothing (average insulation 0.16 clo) (Fig. 4a).

In winter, the primary adaptation was wearing more clothing (51%). Other strategies included moving indoors (24%), drinking hot water (17%), and reducing outdoor activities (8%) (Fig. 4b).

Preference votes

In the spring (Fig. 4c), 18% of respondents preferred lower temperatures, 59% preferred the temperature to remain unchanged, and 23% preferred higher temperatures. In regards to solar radiation, 27% of respondents preferred a decrease, 51% preferred no change, and 22% preferred an increase. For relative humidity, 30% of respondents preferred a decrease, 35% preferred no change, and 35% preferred an increase. In terms of wind speed, 25% preferred a decrease, 60% preferred no change, and 15% preferred an increase.

In the summer (Fig. 4d), 83% of respondents preferred lower temperatures, 16% preferred the temperature to remain unchanged, and 1% preferred higher temperatures. Regarding solar radiation, 65% of respondents preferred a decrease, 34% preferred no change, and 1% preferred an increase. For relative humidity, 31% preferred a decrease, 56% preferred no change, and 13% preferred an increase. In terms of wind speed, 2% preferred a decrease, 32% preferred no change, and 66% preferred an increase.

In the autumn (Fig. 4e), 8% of respondents preferred lower temperatures, 48% preferred the temperature to remain unchanged, and 44% preferred higher temperatures. Regarding solar radiation, 2% of respondents preferred a decrease, 33% preferred no change, and 65% preferred an increase. For relative humidity, 27% preferred a decrease, 30% preferred no change, and 43% preferred an increase. In terms of wind speed, 11% preferred a decrease, 81% preferred no change, and 8% preferred an increase.

In the winter (Fig. 4f), 18% of respondents preferred lower temperatures, 62% preferred the temperature to remain unchanged, and 20% preferred higher temperatures. Regarding solar radiation, 21% of respondents preferred a decrease, 62% preferred no change, and 17% preferred an increase. For relative humidity, 4% preferred a decrease, 51% preferred no change, and 45% preferred an increase. In terms of wind speed, 16% preferred a decrease, 64% preferred no change, and 20% preferred an increase.

Overall, respondents’ preferences for wind speed varied significantly across the four seasons. In spring and autumn, most people preferred a stable temperature, with a smaller proportion wanting a decrease or increase. In contrast, summer showed a clear preference, with the majority of respondents wanting lower temperatures, as well as a decrease in solar radiation and humidity. In winter, there was a stronger preference for keeping the temperature and humidity unchanged, with only a few respondents desiring higher temperatures.

Factors that affected thermal comfort

Effects of age on thermal comfort

Different age groups provided varying TSV scores in the same area. Figure 4g,h shows TSV and TCV scores for different age groups at four survey sites.

Different age groups showed varying preferences for TSV across the four survey sites. At the RA site, 60% of respondents aged 21–25 and 41–50 felt "cold," while the highest proportion of respondents aged 18–20 felt "a little cold" (59%). At the GA site, the highest rates of “moderate” responses were from those aged 18–20 (23%), 21–25 (23%), 26–30 (47%), 31–40 (35%), and over 61 years (40%). Respondents aged 41–50 and 51–60 preferred "a little warmer" (40% and 33%, respectively). At the CS site, the highest rates of “moderate” responses were from those aged 18–20 (32%), 21–25 (36%), 26–30 (26%), 31–40 (27%), 41–50 (43%), 51–60 (40%), and over 61 years (50%). Only 1.32% of those aged 21–25 years felt "cold." Finally, at the SR site, 39% of respondents aged 31–40 years felt "hot," while those aged 18–30 generally gave more moderate TSV ratings, and respondents aged 31–50 showed more sensitivity to site conditions.

Effects of different seasons on thermal comfort

Particulate matter concentrations varied by season, affecting respondents’ subjective evaluations (Fig. 4i). Regarding PM2.5 concentrations up to 50 µg/m3, the highest proportion of respondents in summer felt "a little warm" (34%), while in winter, respondents most frequently felt "a little cool" (56%). In spring, 7% of respondents felt “hot” when PM2.5 levels exceeded 100 µg/m3, while 1% felt “hot” when PM2.5 was ≤ 50 µg/m3. The highest percentage of “moderate” responses were 34% (PM2.5 ≤ 50 µg/m3), 28% (50 < PM2.5 ≤ 100 µg/m3), and 41% (PM2.5 > 100 µg/m3). In autumn, 38% felt "a little warm" with PM2.5 > 100 µg/m3, and the highest "a little cool" responses occurred with PM2.5 ≤ 50 µg/m3 (33%) and 50 < PM2.5 ≤ 100 µg/m3 (44%), indicating a hotter TSV perception in areas with severe haze.

When considering PM2.5 concentrations greater than 100 µg/m3, the highest proportion of respondents in summer felt "a little warm," while in winter, the majority felt “cooler”. In the spring, 7% of respondents felt “hot” with PM2.5 > 100, and in autumn, the highest percentage of “moderate” responses were 42% (PM2.5 ≤ 50 µg/m3) and 38% (50 < PM2.5 ≤ 100 µg/m3).

In Fig. 4j, TCV distributions were analyzed, and the highest “moderate” responses in spring and autumn were 42% (PM2.5 ≤ 50 µg/m3) and 38% (50 < PM2.5 ≤ 100 µg/m3), respectively. "A bit uncomfortable" responses were more frequent in summer (39%) and winter (33%) with PM2.5 ≤ 50 µg/m3.

Relationship between urban outdoor environment and human health

Influence of urban thermal environment on human health

Thermal pollution refers to abnormal ambient temperatures caused by natural factors and human activities48. With increasing urbanization, thermal pollution is particularly severe in big cities, disrupting the original temperature equilibrium and potentially affecting humans, the environment, and climate. The most comfortable temperature range for humans is 25–29 °C. Temperatures above 29 °C cause discomfort, and above 35 °C can lead to palpitations, heat stroke, mental disorders, and serious health issues like heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, and respiratory diseases49.

Figure 8a shows that eye diseases were most common (78%) in the thermal environment, while cerebrovascular diseases were least common (2%). Figure 8b indicates that respondents with mental illness were most sensitive to the thermal environment, and those with eye diseases were least sensitive. Additionally, respiratory diseases showed a positive association with NPET, suggesting that individuals with respiratory diseases are more sensitive to thermal environments. In contrast, all other health impacts surveyed, including skin diseases, immune system diseases, and mental illness, also exhibited positive associations with NPET, indicating heightened sensitivity to temperature fluctuations. The highest NPET was 29.66 °C in patients with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.

Effects of urban air quality on human health

PM2.5 refers to fine particles smaller than 2.5 nm, which can enter the respiratory system and adversely affect human health by damaging the respiratory system and potentially causing lung diseases and eye irritation50. PM2.5 also affects the cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems, leading to arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and coronary heart disease. Clinical trials indicate that short-term exposure to high PM2.5 levels increases morbidity and mortality from cardiopulmonary diseases. Additionally, haze environments can cause skin discomfort and exacerbate skin diseases10.

Figure 8d shows that respiratory diseases, including rhinitis, pharyngitis, bronchitis, asthma, and pneumonia, account for the highest proportion of haze-related illnesses (71%). Immune system diseases like leukemia account for the lowest proportion (2%).

Figure 8e shows the correlation coefficients between different diseases and NPET and NPETR values. Respondents with immune system diseases were most sensitive to outdoor air quality, while those with mental diseases were the least sensitive. Notably, the highest NPET was found in subjects with mental illness at 25.26 °C, warranting further investigation.

Figure 8f shows the linear regressions between mPET and AQV for respondents with different symptoms or diseases caused by haze. At outdoor temperature ranges of 6–15 °C, 18–24 °C, and 27–33 °C, respondents with respiratory diseases gave the lowest AQV scores, perceiving severe haze pollution. Those with skin disorders were most affected, followed by individuals with mental disorders, differing significantly from other conditions.

Discussion

Thermal environment and air quality

This study investigated thermal comfort in relation to the outdoor thermal environment and air pollution at four sites at Xi’an Jiaotong University. Subjective thermal comfort evaluations varied due to geographical locations, research methods, and physical environments. We aimed to provide a reference for future research by examining human thermal comfort under different heat–air quality conditions.

Table 4 compares our results with similar studies based on location, time, season, and evaluation indicators. For example, Geng et al. studied the effects of background sound on thermal comfort at five university sites and found that music presence affected TCV scores51. Their study highlighted the influence of the acoustic environment on thermal comfort, suggesting that future studies could include visual and auditory comfort.

Another study on children’s outdoor thermal comfort in Xi’an parks found acceptable heat ranges of 6.5–26.4 °C for boys and 5.7–27.3 °C for girls, with pavement materials affecting thermal safety52. Yin et al. explored thermal–acoustic comfort on campuses, showing that thermal comfort and haze pollution influence each other, similar to our findings53.

Guan et al. assessed human comfort in multi-factor environments using EEG (electroencephalogram) data to correlate physiological and psychological indicators with comfort levels54. Their method suggests future research could incorporate physiological parameters for a more accurate analysis of environmental effects on human comfort.

Qianlan measured phthalate concentrations to assess PM2.5 health risks in different indoor environments, finding seasonal variations in PM2.5 levels55. Yuyang et al. studied spatiotemporal variations in PM2.5 in Wuhan parks, finding lower concentrations in green spaces during winter and summer56.

Overall, studies confirm that thermal environment and air quality significantly affect human comfort and health. Thermal and air pollution can cause discomfort and health issues, with effects varying by location, season, and age. Extreme temperatures and particle concentrations have the greatest impact on comfort.

This study had several limitations. First, the sample size of 927 valid questionnaires was relatively small and limited to the university campus, potentially affecting the generalizability of the results. A larger and more diverse sample would strengthen the conclusions. Second, a longer study duration would provide more comprehensive data. Third, only four typical outdoor thermal environments were examined, whereas many other outdoor spaces on campus were not considered. Future research should include a broader range of environments. Finally, while interactions between thermal and air quality comfort were identified, future studies should also consider the effects of thermal, auditory, and visual comfort on overall environmental assessments.

Conclusions

This study explored the interactions between the thermal environment, air quality, and human health in urban settings, focusing on four typical sites across a university campus in Xi’an. By analyzing data from 927 valid questionnaires, we gained insights into how factors such as age, gender, season, and specific site conditions affect both thermal comfort and the perception of air quality.

Our findings revealed that younger individuals (aged 18–25) preferred moderate or slightly cool temperatures, often feeling "a little uncomfortable" due to heat, while older individuals (aged 41–60) favored slightly warmer conditions. Those in the 31–50 age range were more affected by thermal comfort changes at specific sites, reflecting a heightened sensitivity to varying thermal conditions. Females exhibited greater sensitivity to thermal discomfort compared to males, though males reported higher levels of thermal comfort despite experiencing less sensitivity.

Regarding thermal adaptation behaviors, the study found that in the summer, participants favored sheltered areas or water bodies for cooling, and few adjusted their clothing to improve thermal comfort. During spring and summer, most respondents preferred lower temperatures, less solar radiation, and greater wind speed, while opposite preferences were observed in autumn and winter. These findings underscore the seasonal variations in thermal comfort preferences.

In terms of air quality, the study revealed that PM2.5 concentrations did not exceed 150 µg/m3 during the study period. mPET significantly influenced the perception of air quality and breathing comfort (AQV and BSV), with the strongest effects observed in residential areas and the least in green areas. The presence of light pollution contributed to discomfort, while higher temperatures reduced breathing comfort, even when haze pollution was alleviated.

Seasonal variations in PM2.5 levels were evident, with concentrations below 50 µg/m3 in summer and exceeding 100 µg/m3 in winter. The study found that while higher temperatures in spring and summer alleviated haze, they also led to decreased breathing comfort. Conversely, higher temperatures in autumn and winter exacerbated haze but improved breathing comfort. Gender differences were also noted, with females generally more sensitive to air quality than males, although males reported higher levels of breathing comfort.

The health effects of thermal and air pollution were significant. Eye diseases were most commonly reported in thermal environments, while individuals with mental health conditions showed the highest sensitivity to thermal changes. Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular patients had the highest neutral physiologically equivalent temperatures (NPET), whereas respiratory disease patients provided the lowest subjective evaluations at temperatures above 21 °C. Respiratory diseases were most prevalent due to haze pollution, and immune system disease patients were particularly sensitive to air quality. Skin disease patients were most affected by air pollution, with respiratory disease patients giving the lowest evaluations across various mPET ranges.

These findings emphasize the importance of addressing the complex relationships between thermal comfort, air quality, and public health. Tailored environmental interventions are necessary to improve urban living conditions and enhance the well-being of residents, particularly for vulnerable groups such as the elderly, individuals with respiratory and skin conditions, and those with mental health challenges. Effective strategies that account for seasonal variations and gender differences will be essential for creating healthier and more comfortable urban environments.
