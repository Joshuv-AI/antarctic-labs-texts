Silent suppuration: an afebrile infected left anterior descending artery pseudoaneurysm with contiguous myocardial necrosis after drug-eluting stent implantation — a case report
Authors: Sanketh Edem, Mallikarjuna Pavan Aggarapu, Sathaiah Dadi, Sachin Mahajan, Basant Kumar, Rajesh Vijayvergiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfected coronary artery pseudoaneurysm is an uncommon but lethal complication of percutaneous coronary intervention, with a reported mortality approaching half of all cases. Fever and raised inflammatory markers are the dominant diagnostic clues, and their absence almost invariably delays diagnosis. Contiguous myocardial involvement by the suppurative process has, to our knowledge, not previously been described.Case presentationA 46-year-old hypertensive Indian man presented with acute exertional chest pain four weeks after drug-eluting stent implantation in the left anterior descending (LAD) artery. He was afebrile and haemodynamically stable, with normal serial total leucocyte counts, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin; multiple blood cultures remained sterile. Coronary angiography showed a saccular pseudoaneurysm at the proximal edge of the LAD stent, and ECG-gated CT aortography confirmed a 19 × 12 mm peri-stent contrast leak. 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–computed tomography (FDG PET-CT) demonstrated intense uptake (SUVmax 18.0) in a paracardiac soft-tissue collection abutting the stent, raising the unsuspected diagnosis of infection. At urgent operation, frank pus was evacuated from the sac and had tracked into the adjacent epicardium and superficial myocardium, producing focal myocardial necrosis that required debridement. The stent was retrieved, the proximal LAD ligated, coronary endarterectomy performed, and revascularisation achieved with an in situ left internal mammary artery (LIMA) to LAD anastomosis. Cultures grew Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Recovery was uneventful and the patient remained well at three-month follow-up.ConclusionsInfected coronary pseudoaneurysm can present with a silent inflammatory profile and may extend beyond the vessel wall into the surrounding myocardium. FDG PET-CT can be a decisive, and at times the principal, diagnostic test when conventional clinical and laboratory clues are absent. Background

Coronary artery pseudoaneurysm following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is an established but rare entity, reported in roughly 0.2% to 2.3% of interventions in the drug-eluting stent (DES) era [1, 2]. The small subset that become infected carry a high mortality, reported to approach 40–60% in published series, with sudden rupture, septic embolisation, fistulisation, and refractory sepsis accounting for most deaths [2, 3]. Pathogenesis is conceptualised as a two-hit process: a breach of vessel-wall integrity by mechanical injury or atheromatous disease, followed by microbial seeding through direct contamination at intervention, haematogenous spread, or impaired local host defences attributable to the antiproliferative agent eluted from the stent [2, 4].

Diagnosis traditionally rests on a triad: recent PCI, fever or other signs of systemic inflammation, and imaging evidence of a peri-stent contrast-filled cavity. The widely cited diagnostic criteria proposed by Dieter similarly emphasise recent coronary stenting, repeated interventions through the same arterial access, fever, documented bacteraemia, leucocytosis of no other identifiable cause, acute coronary syndrome, and positive cardiac imaging [5]. Fever is the most commonly reported presenting feature [2, 5] and is so consistently the herald symptom that its absence has been considered nearly exonerating. In the largest cohort of Pseudomonas aeruginosa–associated coronary stent infections, seven of eight patients were febrile; the single afebrile patient nonetheless showed angiographically progressive aneurysmal expansion that prompted suspicion [3]. The diagnostic challenge is far greater when fever, leucocytosis, and acute-phase reactants are all silent, a scenario, to our knowledge, not previously documented for an infected coronary pseudoaneurysm.

We describe such a case: an infected LAD pseudoaneurysm presenting four weeks after DES implantation with chest pain alone, normal inflammatory indices, sterile blood cultures, and a non-infective initial clinical impression. The case is doubly unusual in that, at operation, suppurative material had breached the pseudoaneurysmal wall and was tracking into the adjacent myocardium, producing focal necrosis that required debridement in addition to stent retrieval, sac ligation, and bypass grafting. We use the case to argue that FDG PET-CT should be considered the decisive test in clinically equivocal peri-stent lesions, and to outline a surgical strategy that anticipates rather than merely reacts to extravascular extension.

Case presentation

Clinical presentation and prior interventional history

A 46-year-old Indian (South Asian) man presented with three hours of retrosternal chest pain radiating to the left arm, with palpitations and diaphoresis, graded Canadian Cardiovascular Society class III. He was a non-smoker and non-alcoholic, hypertensive on regular therapy, and a survivor of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. On arrival he was afebrile (37.0 °C), with a heart rate of 94 beats per minute, blood pressure 116/82 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable. There were no constitutional symptoms, no fever, chills, rigors, sweats, weight loss, or malaise at presentation or, by his account, in the preceding weeks. He was not diabetic and had no chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, or known immunodeficiency. He was not receiving corticosteroids, chemotherapy, biologic agents, or any other immunosuppressive therapy, had no history of malignancy or solid-organ transplantation, and was not known to be HIV-positive. He was therefore immunocompetent, with no identifiable condition or medication that might be expected to blunt a febrile or laboratory inflammatory response, which makes the absence of such a response in the presence of active suppuration all the more notable.

His coronary history was dense and recent. About three months earlier he underwent plain-old balloon angioplasty (POBA) to a chronic total occlusion of the mid-LAD; the procedure was successful but without stent deployment. Six weeks later he was readmitted for definitive PCI and received a drug-eluting stent across the previously dilated mid-LAD segment, and was discharged on dual antiplatelet therapy. The current admission was thus the third coronary procedure in the same vessel within approximately four months.

Investigations and the diagnostic narrative

Laboratory evaluation on admission was, in retrospect, the most striking feature of the case: it was entirely normal. The total leucocyte count and differential were within the reference range; C-reactive protein and procalcitonin were not elevated; renal and hepatic profiles were unremarkable; troponin was not raised. Three sets of blood cultures over the first 48 h remained sterile after extended incubation. The electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm without acute ischaemic change. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60% (modified Simpson), no regional wall motion abnormality, mild mitral regurgitation, and no pericardial collection or signs of endocarditis. Importantly, all three sets of blood cultures were drawn before any antimicrobial agent was administered, the first empirical antibiotics being started only at the time of surgery; prior antibiotic exposure therefore cannot account for their sterility. Coronary angiography, undertaken on the basis of the symptom profile and interventional history, gave the first hint that this was not simply post-PCI angina. A focal saccular outpouching arose from the proximal end of the LAD stent, with the remainder of the coronary tree unremarkable (Fig. 1). To delineate the lesion and exclude aortic pathology — a penetrating ulcer or intramural haematoma had been considered given the radiating pain — ECG-gated CT aortography was performed on a 384-detector scanner. The thoracic aorta was plaque-free, without intramural haematoma or penetrating ulcer. A 19 × 12 mm peri-stent contrast leak was confirmed at the proximal stent edge, with faint intraluminal and distal opacification of the stent and a non-calcified plaque immediately proximal causing severe focal luminal reduction over a 5 mm segment (Fig. 2c).

At this point the picture remained ambiguous. A peri-stent pseudoaneurysm in a repeatedly instrumented vessel was unsurprising on mechanical grounds, and nothing in the history, examination, or laboratory profile pointed to infection; the leak could plausibly have been managed by observation or elective surgery on structural grounds. It was the decision to obtain an FDG PET-CT study, prompted by residual unease at the recent and repeated instrumentation of the vessel, that reframed the case.

PET-CT demonstrated intense focal FDG uptake (SUVmax 18.0) in a soft-tissue collection in the paracardiac space immediately adjacent to the LAD stent (Fig. 2a, b). Notably, the contrast-filled sac itself was not FDG-avid; avidity was concentrated in the perivascular soft tissue, suggesting that the active infective process was extravascular rather than confined to the sac lumen. Faintly avid mediastinal and cervical nodes were considered reactive, and there was no evidence of distant septic foci. An SUVmax of 18.0 localised to a perivascular collection in this clinical context was considered strongly suggestive of active suppuration, and on the basis of the imaging a working diagnosis of infected pseudoaneurysm was made.

Operative findings and management

After multidisciplinary discussion the patient was scheduled for urgent surgery. Empirical intravenous ceftriaxone and vancomycin were commenced to cover the Gram-positive organisms most commonly implicated while culture data were awaited. The mediastinum was entered through a median sternotomy, aorto-bicaval cannulation established, and cardiopulmonary bypass instituted with moderate systemic hypothermia. The heart was arrested with antegrade cold-blood cardioplegia.

Mobilisation was unusually difficult. Dense, vascular, inflammatory adhesions bound the visceral pericardium to the LAD region and to an indurated mass overlying the proximal LAD (Fig. 3a). Meticulous adhesiolysis was performed to avoid premature entry into what was correctly anticipated to be a contained suppurative cavity. On opening the sac, frank purulent material — yellow, viscid, malodorous was released together with multiple organising thrombi, and was sent for aerobic, anaerobic, and fungal culture and histopathology.

The intraoperative finding that distinguished this case was made at this point: the pus was not contained within the sac alone. It had eroded through the adventitial and epicardial layers and was tracking into the superficial myocardium overlying the proximal LAD, producing a discrete area of softened, dusky, friable tissue with an adherent fibrinous exudate (Fig. 3b). There was no acute transmural infarction; this was a localised inflammatory–necrotic process contiguous with the sac, and clearly the substrate of the high preoperative FDG avidity in the paracardiac soft tissue rather than in the sac lumen. The necrotic myocardium was debrided sharply until healthy, bleeding tissue was reached, and the cavity was lavaged with warm saline.

The proximal LAD was completely thrombosed, with no antegrade flow through the stent. The drug-eluting stent was retrieved en bloc (Fig. 3d); it bore adherent organised thrombus and a thin layer of inflammatory material proximally, though the scaffold was intact. The proximal LAD was ligated immediately proximal to the pseudoaneurysmal origin. Coronary endarterectomy was performed distal to the ligation point, removing residual thrombotic and intimal material to produce a clean lumen verified by passage of a coronary probe (Fig. 3c), onto which the distal anastomosis could be safely placed. Revascularisation was completed by anastomosing the in situ LIMA to the LAD distal to the endarterectomised segment with continuous 7 − 0 polypropylene. The right and circumflex territories were disease-free and did not require grafting.

The patient was rewarmed and weaned from bypass without difficulty, in sinus rhythm and on minimal inotropic support (noradrenaline 0.04 µg/kg/min, weaned over 24 h). No mechanical circulatory support was required. Haemostasis was secured, drains placed, and the chest closed in standard fashion.

Microbiology and postoperative course

Intraoperative pus culture grew Pseudomonas aeruginosa sensitive to amikacin, ceftazidime, cefepime, meropenem, ciprofloxacin, cefoperazone-sulbactam, levofloxacin, aztreonam, and piperacillin-tazobactam. Anaerobic and fungal cultures were sterile at 48 h. Vancomycin was discontinued and ceftriaxone replaced with piperacillin-tazobactam, subsequently rationalised to a course of intravenous meropenem on the advice of the antimicrobial stewardship team. Histopathology of the debrided myocardium showed coagulative necrosis with a dense neutrophilic infiltrate and Gram-negative bacilli on tissue Gram stain, corroborating the microbiological diagnosis.

The patient was extubated within 24 h, inotropes were weaned by the second postoperative day, drains removed by the fourth, and he was transferred to the ward on the fifth. Inflammatory markers remained normal throughout mirroring his preoperative profile, and a reminder that the absence of a systemic inflammatory response can be the host’s default state rather than a counter-argument to active infection. Pre-discharge echocardiography showed preserved biventricular function with no new wall motion abnormality and no pericardial collection. He was discharged on the fourteenth postoperative day. Patient remained well at three-month review with a healed sternotomy, no recurrent chest pain, and a normal echocardiogram.

Discussion

Two features of this case warrant reflection: the diagnostic narrative, in which a virulent infection was unmasked despite a uniformly normal systemic inflammatory profile; and the operative finding, in which the suppurative process had crossed the vessel wall to become a disease of the myocardium as much as of the artery.

The afebrile infected pseudoaneurysm: a diagnostic blind spot

The contemporary diagnostic pathway assumes usually safely that the host will signal the infection through fever, leucocytosis, raised C-reactive protein and procalcitonin, and positive blood cultures in roughly half of cases [2, 3]. When these clues are absent the diagnosis is rarely entertained, and the literature contains only a few afebrile presentations. The case reported by Reddy and colleagues, in which an infected right coronary artery pseudoaneurysm enlarged rapidly on serial angiography in a normothermic patient, is widely cited as the archetype [3]; even there, however, the patient was not laboratory-silent, and the angiographic progression itself functioned as a surrogate inflammatory marker.

Our patient represents, to our knowledge, one of very few reported infected coronary pseudoaneurysms with a complete absence of any systemic inflammatory signature afebrile, with serial leucocyte counts, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and blood cultures all repeatedly normal before and after surgery. The diagnosis was made in spite of the laboratory profile, not because of it, which raises the question of how many such patients are missed. To support this assertion we performed a literature search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Google Scholar from database inception to May 2026, combining the terms “infected” or “mycotic”, “coronary”, “pseudoaneurysm” or “aneurysm”, “stent” and “afebrile”, supplemented by hand-searching the reference lists of retrieved articles. We did not identify a previously reported case combining a completely silent inflammatory profile (afebrile, with normal leucocyte count, C-reactive protein and procalcitonin and persistently sterile blood cultures) with contiguous myocardial extension; accordingly, all claims of novelty in this report are qualified as being to the best of our knowledge.

Several mechanisms might explain a silent systemic response to an active, Pseudomonas-laden suppurative process. The first is anatomical containment: the pus was sequestered within a dense fibrous capsule formed by the sac and the adherent visceral pericardium, with no luminal communication to permit bloodstream seeding consistent with the persistently sterile cultures. The second is the immunomodulatory environment of the DES, whose antiproliferative agents blunt local cellular immunity, delay endothelialisation, and create a niche both permissive of infection and partially insulated from systemic recognition [4, 6]. The third, more speculative, is host-specific attenuation of the cytokine response to a contained Gram-negative focus. Whichever combination operated, the practical implication is the same: a normal laboratory panel cannot exclude infection in a patient with chest pain and a peri-stent contrast leak.

The likely source of infection and the significance of the negative blood cultures also merit comment. The patient underwent three coronary procedures in the same vessel within approximately four months, and repeated instrumentation of a single arterial segment is the most plausible portal of entry, with direct procedural contamination of the vessel wall the favoured mechanism over haematogenous seeding. Pseudomonas aeruginosa, the organism recovered here, is a recognised nosocomial and procedure-associated pathogen and has been the dominant isolate in the small published series of coronary stent infection [3, 7]. We could not identify a remote primary focus, and there was no clinical evidence of bacteraemia, endocarditis, or distant septic embolisation. The persistently sterile blood cultures, despite extensive local suppuration, are best explained by the same anatomical containment outlined above: a contained extravascular abscess walled off from the circulating blood pool seeds the bloodstream poorly or intermittently, and the absence of luminal communication limits continuous shedding of organisms. Because all blood cultures were obtained before any antimicrobial therapy, prior antibiotic exposure is excluded as a cause of their sterility. This sequence underscores that negative blood cultures cannot be taken to exclude an infected pseudoaneurysm, and that microbiological confirmation may depend, as it did here, on direct sampling of the lesion at operation.

The role of FDG PET-CT deserves emphasis. The technique is now a sensitive and specific tool for vascular graft and prosthetic infection, with a growing literature supporting its use in suspected mycotic coronary aneurysms [8]. Here the SUVmax of 18.0 was disproportionately high well above the threshold of 4 to 5 usually considered consistent with active infection [9] and importantly, localised to the perivascular soft tissue rather than the contrast-filled sac. That distinction was, in retrospect, the imaging signature of extravascular extension of pus into the myocardium, and could have prospectively predicted the operative finding. Any peri-stent FDG-avid soft-tissue collection particularly one whose avidity exceeds that of the sac itself should be assumed to harbour contiguous tissue invasion until proven otherwise, and the surgical plan constructed accordingly.

The decision to proceed to FDG PET-CT despite uniformly normal inflammatory markers warrants explanation. It was prompted by the combination of recent and repeated instrumentation of a single coronary segment, the morphology of the peri-stent lesion, and the recognition that conventional inflammatory markers have imperfect sensitivity for a contained, walled-off focus of infection; multimodality imaging was therefore pursued to characterise tissue activity rather than merely vascular anatomy. The interpretation of FDG PET-CT in this setting nonetheless requires caution. Recent PCI, stent implantation, and the associated post-procedural healing or inflammatory response can all generate non-specific FDG uptake, and physiologic myocardial glucose metabolism — which is frequently intense and non-uniform — is a well-recognised source of false-positive interpretation unless it is adequately suppressed by appropriate patient preparation, typically prolonged fasting combined with a high-fat, low-carbohydrate, protein-permitted diet, with or without heparin loading [9, 10]. In the present case several features argued against a physiologic or purely post-procedural explanation: the uptake was focal rather than diffuse, very high in intensity (SUVmax 18.0, far exceeding values attributable to physiologic or post-procedural activity), and localised to an extravascular paracardiac soft-tissue collection rather than to the myocardial blood pool. The evidence base specific to coronary stent and coronary aneurysm infection remains limited to small series and isolated case reports [8, 11], and important limitations apply: FDG PET-CT cannot reliably distinguish low-grade infection from sterile post-procedural inflammation, has limited spatial resolution for small and motion-prone coronary structures, is degraded by inadequate myocardial suppression, and is susceptible to both false-positive and false-negative results. Its findings must therefore be integrated with the clinical, microbiological, and operative picture rather than interpreted in isolation, as was done here.

Pus across the vessel wall: a rarely reported pattern of injury

The published literature on infected coronary pseudoaneurysms is essentially a literature of vessel-wall pathology: the pseudoaneurysm is described, the stent retrieved, the sac excluded or excised, the distal vessel bypassed [12–14]. The surrounding myocardium is mentioned, if at all, only in the context of secondary infarction from upstream occlusion. We are not aware of a previous surgical description in which the suppurative process had extended beyond the adventitia into the epicardium and superficial myocardium, producing focal necrosis requiring debridement.

The mechanism appears to be contained extravasation. The pseudoaneurysmal wall, formed by organising thrombus and fibrous capsule rather than original arterial media, is intrinsically thin and permeable to inflammatory mediators and bacterial constituents. When the sac is densely adherent to the visceral pericardium, as here, there is no pericardial space into which the process can vent, and the path of least resistance becomes the underlying myocardium. The result is a localised, contiguous myocardial infection without acute coronary occlusion, transmural infarction, or perforation a pattern more reminiscent of the contiguous extension seen with paravalvular abscesses in endocarditis than of any classical PCI complication.

The operative implication is that the standard algorithm of sac exclusion plus bypass is insufficient. Debridement of the contiguously involved myocardium is required, both to remove an infected necrotic reservoir for recurrent infection and to expose viable tissue onto which durable healing can mount. The depth of debridement should be guided by macroscopic appearance; here healthy bleeding myocardium was reached within 4 to 5 mm of the epicardial surface and the defect required no patch closure. In a more advanced case, deeper involvement might mandate bovine pericardial patch reconstruction or, in the extreme, regional myocardial resection. These observations derive from a single case, and the recommendation to inspect for and debride contiguously involved myocardium should be regarded as a reasonable principle suggested by our experience rather than an established standard; it requires corroboration in further cases before it can be generalised.

Surgical strategy: principles and choices

Several surgical choices depart, in subtle but important ways, from the standard published approach [15–17]. The following are presented as the rationale for the decisions taken in this individual case rather than as prescriptive recommendations; given that they rest on a single case, they should be weighed alongside the existing literature and individualised to the patient and the operative findings.

First, conduit selection. The literature is dominated by saphenous vein grafting, partly because these operations are often emergent and vein is the fastest conduit to harvest. We used the in situ LIMA despite the inflamed field on three grounds: the documented biological resistance of arterial conduits to infection; the mid-LAD as the prototypical LIMA target; and the superior long-term patency of the LIMA in a relatively young patient with a heavy interventional history. The pedicle was carefully shielded from the residually contaminated cavity during anastomosis. We do not suggest that arterial grafting is mandatory in this setting; saphenous vein remains an entirely acceptable conduit, particularly when operative time is critical, the field is heavily contaminated, or the target is unsuitable for the LIMA, and conduit choice should be individualised.

Second, endarterectomy. The proximal LAD was completely thrombosed and the stent en bloc retrievable, but the segment between the ligation point and the planned anastomosis contained adherent organised thrombus and inflammatory material that would have rendered the anastomosis unsafe. Coronary endarterectomy is uncommon in the modern era it carries a higher perioperative infarction rate and is largely reserved for diffusely diseased vessels with no other reachable target but it is the correct technique when the alternative is anastomosis onto a contaminated luminal surface. The clean lumen obtained permitted a confident distal anastomosis.

Third, covered stenting. Percutaneous exclusion of the pseudoaneurysm with a covered stent has been described in selected patients at prohibitive operative risk [18]. We considered this approach inappropriate in the presence of established infection: the covered stent leaves the infected tissue in situ and superimposes a second foreign body, and recurrent infection with continued aneurysmal enlargement has been reported [19]. With contiguous myocardial involvement of the kind described here, a covered stent would have left the infected, necrotic myocardium entirely unaddressed and, in our judgement, would have been very likely to fail. We therefore regard surgery as the preferred definitive treatment once infection is established, including in many patients of substantial operative risk, while recognising that covered stenting may retain a role as a temporising or palliative measure when open operation is genuinely not feasible.

Antimicrobial therapy and follow-up

Empirical cover should include both methicillin-resistant Gram-positive and antipseudomonal agents, given that Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa together account for most culture-positive cases [3, 7]. We commenced ceftriaxone and vancomycin and narrowed to meropenem once cultures returned. The optimal duration of parenteral therapy is undefined; expert opinion drawn from prosthetic vascular graft infection favours four to eight weeks [2, 20]. We chose six weeks, with a further two weeks of oral step-down. Recurrence has been reported months after apparent surgical cure [21], and long-term clinical and imaging follow-up is mandatory; we plan repeat coronary CT and FDG PET-CT at six months and one year.

Conclusions

Infected coronary pseudoaneurysm can present in complete clinical silence no fever, no leucocytosis, no rise in acute-phase reactants, and sterile blood cultures even when the underlying suppurative process is virulent and Pseudomonas-laden. Multimodality imaging, specifically FDG PET-CT, provides the diagnostic foothold when conventional clues are absent, and the pattern of uptake can predict the anatomical extent of disease. Contiguous extension into the surrounding myocardium is a rarely reported manifestation that, to our knowledge, has not previously been described in this form and that should be sought at operation, debrided, and reported. A combined strategy of stent retrieval, proximal vessel ligation, distal endarterectomy where required, debridement of contiguously infected myocardium, and arterial revascularisation with the in situ LIMA offers an excellent outcome in what remains one of the more lethal complications of contemporary coronary intervention.
