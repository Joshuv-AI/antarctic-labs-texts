Immunization attributable burden of stunting among under-five children in India, 2005–2021
Authors: Junaid Khan, Sanjay K Mohanty, Parul Puri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examined the stunting differences among Indian children of heterogenous socio-economic characteristics subject to their full immunization (FI) status. It also describes evidence of a declining stunting burden over time among fully immunized children across different socio-economic strata compared to their counter parts.MethodsChildren aged 12–59 months from NFHS, 2005-06 and 2015-16 and 2019-21 constitute the study sample. We applied bivariate cross-tabulation, NFHS round specific and a pooled binary logistic regression analysis to examine the unadjusted and adjusted odds for full immunization. We estimated the population attributable risk (PAR) associated with full immunization, applied a non-linear Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition analysis to examine the immunization-attributable gap in stunting, and finally applied the Fairlie decomposition analysis to assess the contribution of background characteristics to the fully immunized (FI)-not fully immunized (NFI) gap in stunting burden within the study population.ResultsNationally, stunting prevalence declined from 60% among those children not fully immunized during 2005-06 to 38% in 2019-21 among those who received all the doses of full immunization. Mother’s education and household’s economic wellbeing show a clear gradient in the stunting prevalence over time and by immunization status of the children. Decomposition analysis shows that immunization attributable burden of child stunting in India reduced substantially across different socio-economic strata.ConclusionsThough several factors have contributed to the overall reduction of stunting, the FI-attributable burden of stunting has shown a reduction among the heterogeneous child population in India during 2005–2021. Thus, it is important to monitor the progress of immunization coverage among children from different socio-economic strata to further lower the stunting prevalence in India.Keywords: Nutrition, Stunting, Immunization, National family health survey, Population attributable risk (PAR), Fairlie decomposition analysis (FDA), India

Introduction

India accounts 62 million of stunted children accounting to 40% in the world [1] and with the current trends it is projected that 127 million children under age five will suffer from stunting by 2025 globally [2]. Although stunting among under five children has shown a reduction during the last decade, still 38% of the total children are stunted in India [3]. About 50% of infant deaths are due to malnutrition and it is the single largest factor contributing to the global burden of disease [4]. At the same time, childhood malnutrition is associated with morbidity in later life with the susceptibility to different diseases particularly obesity, cardio vascular diseases and type-2 diabetes [5].

In many developing countries, malnourishment among children is still a major public health challenge and India is not an exception. Recent estimates from the fifth round of National Family Health Survey, 2019-21 on child nutrition showed that India is still carrying a substantial burden of stunting (36%) among under-five children.

The Mosley and Chen framework on child survival in developing countries provides the social science and medical science approach to study the demographic as well as the clinical phenomenon of child mortality and its causes [6]. Within the medical science approach, the framework discussed the proximate determinants of child mortality where disease infection plays a crucial role to determine child health. Parallel to Mosley and Chen framework, UNICEF’s conceptual framework on child malnutrition highlights on infectious diseases and child’s nutritional health [7]. And malnourished children from the developing countries show increased rate of mortality mostly due to infections [8].

There are several risk factors that determine child’s nutritional health and factors like inadequate feeding practices (suboptimal breastfeeding and complimentary feeding) and infections play a crucial role [2]. Timely vaccination essentially protects the children from the vaccine preventable diseases (VPDs) while promoting the overall immune system and the heterologous effects of a vaccine also prevents the child from other diseases [9–13]. A study evaluated the efficacy of the vaccination program on child anthropometry and showed that the universal immunization program substantially contributed to improved child growth among Indian children [14]. According to a previous study, early vaccination (before reaching one month of age) with BCG, DTP1, and measles vaccines was linked to lower stunting rates, while later vaccination increased the risk [15]. The study of 368,450 children in Sub-Saharan Africa highlights the importance of timing in vaccination effects [15]. Another study assessed the impact of vaccination status on child growth among 110 children aged 12–18 months and showed that fully vaccinated children had significantly lower rates of stunting (19.5% vs. 47.8%), wasting (10.3% vs. 30.4%), and underweight status (8% vs. 47.8%) compared to incompletely vaccinated children [16]. To examine the relationship between vaccine uptake and undernutrition among Nigerian children aged 0–36 months using data from the 2018 Nigeria DHS a study indicates that vaccination, particularly BCG, DPT/Pentavalent, and measles, is associated with a lower likelihood of stunting but not wasting. This suggests that vaccines may contribute to long-term nutritional benefits, emphasizing the need for targeted immunization programs for at-risk children [17].

Though malnutrition among under-five children is said to be the most common immunodeficiency yet vaccination can protect them from vaccine preventable diseases [18]. Previous studies find that children with illness due to infections show reduced intake of food and thus children are vulnerable to low dietary intake [19]. In a different longitudinal study conducted among Peruvian new-borns, it was observed that infections like diarrhoea, respiratory and other illnesses are substantially associated with lower physical growth [20]. Thus, full immunization against the VPDs can save the children from the possible infections and help those children attain improved physical growth.

Notably, there is no population-based study available for India exploring the temporal pattern of child stunting with respect to the immunization status of the children. In the above context, this study examined the full immunization attributable burden of stunting among Indian children of age 12–59 months during 2005–2021.

Research gaps and rationale of the study

Despite substantial progress in reducing child stunting in India, there remains a significant research gap in understanding the direct contribution of immunization coverage to the reduction of stunting prevalence, particularly in the Indian socio-economic context. Existing literature has widely examined the role of nutrition, sanitation, and maternal factors in childhood stunting, but few studies have rigorously quantified the immunization-attributable burden of stunting across diverse socio-economic groups in India. The available evidence is largely descriptive and does not adequately explore the differential impact of full immunization on stunting prevalence among children from marginalized or economically disadvantaged households compared to their privileged counterparts. Moreover, there is limited understanding of how this relationship has evolved over time amidst changing public health interventions and economic growth. Hence, this study addresses a critical gap by using robust statistical analyses and decomposition methods-non-linear Blinder-Oaxaca and Fairlie decomposition analysis to understand the specific contribution of immunization to child stunting reduction and explores whether immunization coverage has equitably benefited children across socio-economic strata in India.

Research questions

This study seeks to address several critical research questions related to child stunting and immunization in India over the period 2005 to 2021. Firstly, it aims to assess the difference in stunting prevalence between fully immunized and not fully immunized children across the study period. Secondly, it investigates the extent to which full immunization contributes to the reduction of stunting burden among children from diverse socio-economic backgrounds, highlighting whether immunization acts as an equalizing factor across social and economic strata. Thirdly, the study explores how the immunization-attributable difference in child stunting prevalence has evolved over time, reflecting the changing landscape of public health interventions and socio-economic development in India. Finally, it examines the relative contributions of socio-economic characteristics-such as maternal education, household economic status and other covariates in explaining the disparities observed in child stunting, thus providing a nuanced understanding of the drivers behind nutritional inequalities among under-five children in India.

Research objectives

The primary objectives of this study are to examine the trends in stunting prevalence among fully immunized and not fully immunized children aged 12–59 months in India from 2005 to 2021, and to assess the immunization-attributable burden of stunting across different socio-economic groups. The study also aims to decompose the stunting gap between fully immunized and not fully immunized children using non-linear decomposition techniques, thereby identifying the key contributors to this disparity. Additionally, it seeks to evaluate how socio-economic characteristics, maternal education, household economic status and other covariates interact with immunization status to influence child stunting outcomes over time using the Fairlie decomposition technique. Ultimately, the study intends to generate robust evidence to inform policymakers on the critical role of ensuring equitable immunization coverage in further reducing child stunting and addressing health inequalities in India.

Methods

Data

Three rounds of the National Family Health Surveys (NFHS), 2005-06, 2015-16 and 2019-21 have been used in this study. The data is publicly available in DHS repository through https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm and could be accessed upon a data request. Thus, no further ethical approval was required to utilize the datasets for this particular study. The details of the survey design, instruments and sampling procedure are available from the respective NFHS, 2005-06, 2015-16 and 2019-21 reports through DHS repository [3, 21]. The study sample consists of children aged 12–59 months at the time of survey except the third round for which the children aged 12–48 months constitute the study sample. The study sample is restricted to children who completed 12 months of age because a child is not supposed to complete all the doses of full immunization before first year of his/her birth. Outcome variable

National Family Health Survey provides the height-for-age z-score which is age and sex standardized measure of child’s anthropometry. And the stunting prevalence is defined as the percentage of children whose height-for-age z score falls below − 2SD (standard deviation) from the median of the standard child population [22]. System missing cases, cases with implausible height and weight values, and flagged cases were not included in the study sample. The stunting variable is defined as a dichotomous variable where ‘1’ denotes the child is stunted, i.e. child’s height-for-age z score is less than − 2SD and ‘0’ otherwise.

Key exposure variable

As we intend to examine the immunization attributable difference in stunting prevalence, the immunization status of children has been categorized into two disjoint categories namely, fully immunized and no or partially immunized. A child is said to be fully immunized if the child received one dose of Bacille Calmette Guerin (BCG), three doses of Diphtheria Pertussis and Tetanus (DPT), three doses of oral polio virus (OPV) and one dose of measles vaccine. Hence, the key exposure variable in this study is a binary variable and defined as ‘1’ if the child received all the doses of full immunization and ‘0’ otherwise.

Covariates

The selection of covariates is based on previous literatures and we considered child’s age [23], birth order [24], sex of the child [25, 26], mother’s education and height [27, 28], caste [29–31], religion [32, 33], wealth status [34], place of residence [35] and region, facility of improved drinking water source [36] and sanitation facility [37] in this study to identify the child population from different background and adjust the effects. Following is the description of the covariates.

Child’s age in months: children are grouped in four categories, 12–23, 24–35, 36–47 and 48–59. Birth Order: this variable signifies the order of birth for a particular child which is categorised as 1, 2–3, 4–5 and 6+. Sex of the child: sex is the bio-demographic characteristic of a child which is an important variable to examine the gender pattern/bias on any demographic event among the children. Sex of the child is categorised as male and female. Mother’s educational attainment: Educational qualification wise mothers are grouped into four categories- no education, completed primary education, completed secondary education and higher educated. Mother's height: this variable defines the stature status of the mothers. As per previous studies, mothers with a height of less than 145 cm are considered to have short stature, whereas those with a height of ≥145 cm are considered to have normal stature.Social group: this variable defines the social group to which a particular child belongs, categorised as –scheduled caste (SC), scheduled tribe (ST) and others (Others include general and other backward class). Social exclusion in India manifests strongly along caste and tribal lines, where Dalits (Scheduled Castes) and Adivasis (Scheduled Tribes) face structural disadvantages rooted in historical discrimination and isolation [38]. Religion: in India, Hindu religion is followed by majority. Muslim and Christianity are other two major religious groups in India. Other than these religions there are few minor religious groups like- Sikhism, Buddhism, Jainism and others. So, the religion variable is categorised as –Hindu, Muslim, Christian and Others. Wealth quintile: this variable defines economic wellbeing of a household where the child/children belong/(s) to. DHS provides this wealth based economic status of a household with the help of asset based information from the household [39]. There are five wealth quintiles (poorest, poorer, middle, richer and richest) considered in this study to identify the economic status of a household. Place of residence: place of residence of a child is identified as rural and urban. Region: whole India is sub divided into six regions in this study following the classification of NFHS and the regions are- North, Central, East, North-East, West and South. Drinking Water: The Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), including India’s National Family Health Survey (NFHS), classify drinking water sources based on their accessibility, safety, and likelihood of contamination. Thus, sources are categorized as “improved” (e.g., piped water, borewells, protected wells, public tap, protected dug well or spring, etc.) or “unimproved” (e.g., unprotected wells, surface water and others) according to WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme standards. The NFHS collects detailed information on the main source of drinking water for each household and the time taken to fetch it, which helps assess access to safe water. Sanitation facility: The National Family Health Survey, conducted under the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) framework, categorizes household sanitation facilities into improved, unimproved, and open defecation based on their design and usage. Improved sanitation facilities are defined as those that hygienically separate human excreta from human contact and are not shared with other households; these include flush toilets connected to piped sewer systems, septic tanks, or pit latrines, ventilated improved pit latrines, pit latrines with slabs, and composting toilets.

Statistical analyses

The prevalence of child stunting was computed by immunization status of the children over time by their background characteristics using bivariate cross tabulation. Bar plots were shown for the study indicators. Bivariate cross-tabulation was used to provide the prevalence estimates of stunting. Logistic regression analysis was executed to examine the likelihood of stunting by immunization status adjusting for other covariates. After the regression analysis, we estimated the population attributable risk (PAR) to quantify the proportion of stunting in the study population which can be attributed to a specific risk factor like no full immunization against full immunization. It estimated the potential reduction in disease burden if the exposure were eliminated, assuming a causal relationship. PAR is widely used in epidemiology and public health to assess intervention priorities and inform policy decisions. . PAR represents the absolute change in the predicted probability of an outcome (here, stunting) that would occur if the entire population were exposed to a favorable level of an exposure variable-in this case, full immunization-compared to an alternative scenario with no exposure to full immunization.

We fitted a multivariable logistic regression model with stunting (yes/no) as the binary outcome variable and full immunization status (not fully immunized vs. fully immunized) as the primary exposure of interest. The model controlled for a comprehensive set of covariates, including child's age, birth order, sex, maternal education, caste, religion, household wealth, place of residence, geographic region, access to safe drinking water, and improved toilet facility. Following the model estimation, we applied the ‘regpar’ post-estimation command to simulate and compare two counterfactual scenarios:

Scenario 0: No child is fully immunized (FI = 0).

Scenario 1: All children are fully immunized (FI = 1).

This command computes the difference in the average predicted probability of stunting between the two hypothetical scenarios, yielding the adjusted PAR. Robust standard errors and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed to assess the precision of the estimates, and p-values were used to test the statistical significance of the PARs. This analysis was conducted separately for each round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-3, NFHS-4, and NFHS-5) as well as for the pooled sample.

Once we estimated the population attributable risk of stunting based on full immunization, we applied a non-linear Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition analysis to quantify the extent to which differences in stunting prevalence between fully immunized and not-fully immunized children were attributable to observed characteristics, we employed a nonlinear Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition method using the ‘nldecompose’ command in Stata. This technique extends the traditional decomposition approach to binary outcomes estimated via logistic regression, accommodating the nonlinear functional form.

Following the nonlinear Blinder-Oaxaca Decomposition analysis, we applied a Fairlie decomposition analysis to evaluate the immunization attributable differences in child stunting over the three-survey time period of NFHS assessing the contribution of each of the factors.

Fairlie Decomposition, introduced by Robert W. Fairlie in 1999, is a non-parametric method used to decompose differences between groups in binary outcomes, such as employment status or educational attainment. The method is an extension of the Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition, which traditionally applies to linear models. Fairlie’s approach allows to understand the contribution of various explanatory variables to the differences observed between two groups in the context of logistic regression models, where the outcome variable is binary.

Fairlie Decomposition operates by first estimating separate logistic regression models for each group (not fully immunized vs. fully immunized). It then computes the predicted probabilities of the outcome for both groups, based on the observed characteristics. The differences in the average predicted probabilities between the groups are decomposed into two components:

Endowment Effect (Characteristics Effect): This component measures the part of the difference that is attributable to the different distributions of characteristics (e.g., education, age, wealth) between the groups.

Coefficient Effect: This component measures the part of the difference that is due to the different effects of characteristics (coefficients) between the groups. It reflects the idea that even if two groups had identical characteristics, they might still experience different outcomes due to differing returns on these characteristics.

Fairlie Decomposition is particularly valuable in bio-demographic and socioeconomic research where disparities between groups (e.g., gender, race, socioeconomic status) in outcomes like employment, health status, or educational attainment are common. Understanding the sources of these disparities is crucial for designing effective policies and interventions. The relevance of Fairlie Decomposition lies in its flexibility and ability to be applied to non-linear models, making it a powerful tool for researchers interested in understanding disparities in binary outcomes. It is particularly suited to bio-demographic and socioeconomic research, where outcomes are often dichotomous (e.g., employed vs. unemployed, healthy vs. unhealthy) and where understanding the underlying causes of disparities is essential for policy development. The analysis was done with the Stata Statistical Software version 17.0 SE (STATA Corp LP, College Station, TX).

Results

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics presents the demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of the children surveyed in NFHS-3 (2005–06), NFHS-4 (2015–16), and NFHS-5 (2019–21) in india (Table 1). The data reveal a shift toward younger children (12–35 months) in NFHS-5, accounting for nearly 98% of the study sample, alongside an increasing proportion of children from smaller families. Maternal education levels have improved substantially, with the percentage of mothers with no formal education decreasing from 40.41% in NFHS-3 to 20.76% in NFHS-5. Across all three NFHS rounds (2005-06, 2015-16, and 2019-21), the proportion of mothers with height < 145 cm has remained relatively stable, ranging from 11.56–12.09%. The distribution of caste representation has shifted, with increased representation of scheduled castes and scheduled tribes and a decline in the “other” caste category. Hindu children consistently form the majority (~ 74%), with minor fluctuations among other religious groups. Wealth distribution highlights a greater concentration of children in poorer quintiles in NFHS-4 and NFHS-5, coupled with a notable rise in rural representation in these rounds. Regionally, the central region’s share of children has grown over time, while the Northeast and South regions have experienced a relative decline. Access to improved drinking water increased steadily from 78.6% in 2005–06 to 88.7% in 2019–21. Similarly, improved toilet facility coverage rose significantly from 43.2–70.9% over the same period, indicating notable progress in basic household amenities. These findings indicate significant demographic and socioeconomic shifts over time, reflecting broader societal changes in india.

Differential in the burden of child stunting by immunization status and background characteristics

Table 2 depicts the prevalence of stunting among under-five children by immunization status and background characteristics. The data from the National Family Health Surveys (NFHS) reveals a consistent decline in stunting prevalence over time, particularly as children age and become fully immunized (FI). In the age group 12–23 months, stunting rate among fully immunized children dropped from 45.4% in NFHS-3 to 41.1% in NFHS-4, and further to 39.1% in NFHS-5. A similar trend is observed in the 24–35 months age group, where stunting prevalence decreased from 49.0% in NFHS-3 to 37.4% in NFHS-5. Gender differences also emerge, with male children showing slightly higher stunting rates than females. In NFHS-5, 39.6% of fully immunized males were stunted compared to 36.7% of females. Birth order is another significant factor; stunting increases with higher birth orders. For instance, among fully immunized children of birth order 1, the stunting rate was 34.0% in NFHS-5, while it was much higher at 50.0% among children of birth order 6 and above.

Children of mothers with no education showed a stunting rate of 59.7% in NFHS-3, which decreased to 49.6% in NFHS-5 for fully immunized children. In contrast, children of mothers with higher education had significantly lower stunting rates, with 25.3% in NFHS-5, highlighting the protective effect of maternal education on child nutrition. Across all NFHS rounds, children of mothers shorter than 145 cm consistently exhibited higher rates of stunting compared to those born to taller mothers. Caste-related disparities are also evident, with Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST) consistently reporting higher stunting rates. Among fully immunized children, the stunting rate for SCs dropped from 53.3% in NFHS-3 to 42.5% in NFHS-5, while for STs, it decreased from 55.7 to 44.6%. Religious differences further show that Hindu children had a stunting rate of 38.4% in NFHS-5, whereas children from Christian and Muslim communities had stunting rates of 30.6% and 39.6%, respectively, indicating variability in nutritional outcomes across religious groups.

In NFHS-5, fully immunized children from the poorest quintile had a stunting rate of 49.6%, while those from the richest quintile had a significantly lower rate of 25.3%. This disparity underscores the impact of economic resources on child health. The place of residence also influences stunting, with urban areas consistently showing lower rates compared to rural areas. For example, in NFHS-5, the stunting rate among fully immunized children in urban areas was 32.3%, compared to 40.3% in rural areas. Regional disparities are pronounced, with the Central region reporting the highest stunting rates among fully immunized children at 41.0% in NFHS-5, whereas the North East region reported a lower rate of 37.5%. Stunting prevalence was consistently higher among children with unimproved drinking water and toilet facilities across all NFHS rounds. Fully immunized children had lower stunting rates than not fully immunized ones, regardless of water and sanitation conditions.

Effect of full immunization on stunting

Table 3 presents the odds ratios (ORs) examining the association between full immunization and stunting among Indian children across NFHS-3 (2005-06), NFHS-4 (2015-16), and NFHS-5 (2019-21). In NFHS-3, fully immunized children exhibited significantly lower odds of stunting, with an unadjusted odds ration (UOR) of 0.56 (95% CI: 0.54–0.59, p < 0.01) and an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.80–0.89, p < 0.01), indicating a 44% and 16% reduction in stunting risk, respectively, before and after adjustment for confounders. For NFHS-4, the UOR was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.81–0.84, p < 0.01), showing 18% lower odds of stunting, but the adjusted association became non-significant (AOR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.97–1.01, p = 0.287). During NFHS-5, the unadjusted analysis revealed no significant association (UOR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.97–1.04, p = 0.904), whereas the adjusted analysis showed an 8% reduction in stunting risk (AOR: 0.92, 95% CI: 0.89–0.95, p < 0.01). The pooled analysis confirmed a consistent association between full immunization and reduced odds of stunting, with a UOR of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.77–0.79, p < 0.01) and an AOR of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.92–0.96, p < 0.01), reflecting 22% and 8% reductions, respectively. These findings underscore the beneficial role of full immunization in mitigating stunting risk, particularly when adjusting for potential confounders, across NFHS rounds.

Population attributable risk (PAR) of stunting

The Population Attributable Risk (PAR) is estimated by comparing the scenario where no children are fully immunized with the baseline where all the children are fully immunized. A negative estimate suggests that full immunization is protective, meaning that the risk of stunting is lower when a child is fully immunized.

In NFHS 2005–06, the PAR estimate is −0.0401 (p-value < 0.001, 95% CI: −0.0503 to −0.0275), indicating that universal full immunization would have reduced stunting prevalence by 4% points, and this effect is statistically significant (Table 4). However, in NFHS 2015–16, the PAR estimate is much smaller at −0.0023, with a p-value of 0.322 and a 95% confidence interval ranging from − 0.0070 to 0.0023, suggesting that the impact of full immunization on stunting during this period was minimal and not statistically significant. For NFHS 2019–21, the estimate is slightly positive (0.0015), with a confidence interval (−0.0065 to 0.0095) and a very high p-value (0.718), indicating no significant association between full immunization and stunting in this round. The pooled sample shows a statistically significant and a modest PAR of −0.0139 (p-value = 0.000, 95% CI: −0.0176 to −0.0101), suggesting that across all survey years combined, full immunization could have reduced stunting by 1.39% points. This pooled result implies a protective effect of full immunization on stunting over time, though the effect appears to have weakened and become non-significant in more recent years.

Immunization attributable difference in stunting

The decomposition analysis of stunting differentials by full immunization status across NFHS rounds highlights a clear decline in the stunting gap over time, alongside a shift in its underlying drivers (Table 5). In NFHS 2005–06, the stunting gap between fully immunized and not fully -immunized children was 14.2%, of which 67.90–76.01% was explained by differences in characteristics such as maternal education, wealth, and sanitation, while 23.99–32.10% was due to unexplained factors—suggesting significant structural or behavioral disparities. By NFHS 2015–16, the gap had narrowed to 4.7% points, with an even higher share, 92.41-97.79%-attributable to background characteristics, and a minimal unexplained share (1.11–7.51%), indicating that socio-economic and demographic conditions were largely responsible for reducing the disparity. In NFHS 2019–21, the gap further reduced to just 2% points, with 90.66–108.07% explained by characteristics, and an insignificant or even slightly negative unexplained portion (−8.07–9.34%), suggesting almost complete compositional explanations and very little evidence of structural inequality at that point. The pooled analysis confirms these trends, showing a 6% overall stunting gap, with 81.26–83.86% explained and 16.14–18.74% unexplained. Collectively, these findings underscore that while socioeconomic improvements have significantly reduced the stunting gap associated with immunization status, a small but meaningful portion remains unexplained-highlighting the continued need for equity-focused health and nutrition interventions.

Pooled sample

The pooled sample analysis shows that the total difference in stunting prevalence between the two groups is 0.0602, indicating that children who are not fully immunized are 6.02% points more likely to be stunted than their fully immunized counterparts (Table 6). Out of this difference, 89.58% (0.0539) is explained by differences in background characteristics, meaning that a large portion of the disparity is attributable to measurable socioeconomic and demographic factors.

The most striking contribution comes from the temporal dimension, captured through the NFHS survey rounds. The NFHS-5 round alone explains 35.17% of the total disparity. This suggests that in the most recent period (2019–21), the benefits of full immunization on stunting outcomes are more pronounced, and the distribution of immunized children is skewed toward households with more favorable nutritional environments. Conversely, NFHS-4 contributes negatively (–16.64%), indicating that during the period of NFHS-3 & 4, the gap in stunting between fully and not fully immunized children was less pronounced, possibly due to more uniform access to health services or slower progress in immunization-linked health gains. These findings highlight that recent improvements in child nutrition are largely concentrated among immunized children, further reinforcing the protective role of immunization in preventing chronic undernutrition- stunting.

Economic status emerges as one of the most powerful contributors. Children from the richest households explain 29.14% of the disparity, while those from richer and middle class households contribute 9.48% and 2.04%, respectively in the pooled sample. This indicates that fully immunized children are disproportionately from wealthier households, also enjoy better nutrition, sanitation, and health care-leading to lower stunting rates. Similarly, maternal education explains a substantial portion of the disparity: secondary education and higher education contribute 15.02% and 12.99%, respectively. These findings imply that fully immunized children tend to have more educated mothers, who are more likely to practice better child feeding, hygiene, and health-seeking behaviors-further protecting against stunting.

Maternal height, a proxy for intergenerational nutrition, explains 4.11% of the disparity, indicating that stunting is more prevalent among children of shorter mothers, who are also less likely to be fully immunized. Improved sanitation (toilet facilities) contributes positively (4.94%), and access to improved drinking water contributes negatively (–2.17%) in the pooled sample. These findings suggest that fully immunized children are more likely to live in better household environments, which collectively reduce their risk of being stunted. In contrast, unimmunized children often reside in more deprived settings, exacerbating their nutritional disadvantages.

Caste and religion also contribute to the disparity. Children from Scheduled Tribes (STs) contribute − 1.78%. Muslim and Christian households explain 2.12% and 1.84% of the gap in the pooled sample, indicating that religious differences in immunization uptake and related health behaviors also play a role in stunting outcomes. Regionally, the Central region explains 5.31%, while the North-East contributes negatively (–7.89%), highlighting significant geographic inequalities in both immunization coverage and child nutritional outcomes.

Higher birth order (4–5 and 6+) explains a combined 8.6% of the disparity, suggesting that fully immunized children are more likely to come from smaller families. Child age also shows significance: children aged 36–47 months contribute positively (0.92%), while those aged 48–59 months contribute negatively (–2.84%), reflecting differences in stunting risk and immunization timing across age groups.

Discussion

Using the three rounds of National Family Health Surveys (NFHS) for India conducted during 2005-06, 2015-16 and 2019-21, this study examined the pattern in child stunting and its differences attributable to the immunization status of the children under age five (12–59 months). From this study, it is evident that there has been a significant overall decline in stunting rates for both NFI and FI children over the years. The immunization-related gap in stunting prevalence reduced from 14% points in 2005-06 to 2.6% points in 2019-21, suggesting improvements in overall child nutrition. While FI children consistently had lower stunting rates across all survey years, the difference between NFI and FI children has become less pronounced over time.

Though India has shown a reduction in child stunting, still two-fifth of the children under age-five are still stunted [3, 21]. Within country also, there remain large differences across states and districts in stunting burden [40] over time. Meanwhile, under the universal immunization program (UIP), rate of full immunization coverage among children under age five has shown an improvement over time.

Like many developing countries, a range set of proximal, distal and environmental factors determine the nutritional status of the children under age five in India. Among other factors, environmental factors play a significant role on the causes of subclinical infections and other infectious diseases resulting in low appetite and malabsorption of the nutrients [19, 41]. And vaccination not only protects the children from the vaccine preventable diseases (VPDs) but also promotes healthy growth reducing the chances of other co morbidities among the children under age-five [10, 12, 42, 43]. Especially timely immunization against diphtheria, pertussis and tetanus (DPT) and measles substantially protects the children from growth faltering [44]. In this context, this study brings evidences in the stunting differential among the children of age 12–59 months who are supposed to receive all the doses of full immunization failing to which a particular sect of children of different socio-economic and demographic characteristics carries substantially higher burden of stunting- a chronic and severe form of undernourishment.

It is evident that Indian children who are fully immunized carry lower burden in stunting compared to the counter parts but over a decade this observed gap has gone down significantly which eventually reflects a good public health progress in terms of reducing the burden of child stunting due to immunization gap. Though the overall burden in child stunting due to immunization has gone down still a large portion of the children suffer from stunting in India. Previous studies indicate a strong association between incomplete immunization and the incidence of stunting. For instance, a study highlights that children with incomplete immunization are at a significantly higher risk of stunting [45]. Similarly, Halim et al. report that toddlers lacking complete immunization are more likely to experience stunting due to their increased vulnerability to infections that compromise nutritional status [46]. This relationship is further supported by Bourke et al., who elucidate that immune dysfunction associated with malnutrition can increase morbidity and mortality in malnourished children, thereby indirectly linking immunization status to stunting [47]. The immune dysfunction associated with malnutrition exacerbates the burden of stunting. This immune impairment can create a vicious cycle where malnutrition leads to infections, which in turn worsen nutritional status, leading to stunting [48]. Additionally, the presence of chronic inflammation, as noted by Rytter et al., can further hinder growth by affecting hormone signaling and metabolism, thereby contributing to stunting [49]. Immunization also has non-specific effects that can enhance the immune response to infections, thereby reducing the incidence of stunting. Studies have shown that vaccines, such as BCG and DTP, can have beneficial effects on child growth beyond their intended protective effects against specific diseases [50]. This is particularly relevant in LMICs, where the burden of infectious diseases is high, and the health system’s capacity to manage these diseases is often limited. Anekwe and Kumar found that immunization programs significantly reduce the prevalence of both stunting and wasting, underscoring the importance of vaccination in child health strategies [14].

As per the study findings, the prevalence of stunting is higher among children of higher birth order and in this context, a previous study [23] identifies a negative correlation between birth order and children’s height-for-age z-scores. Specifically, later-born children tend to have lower height-for-age scores compared to their earlier-born siblings, indicating higher susceptibility to stunting. As evident from this study, children from the rural areas carry significantly higher prevalence of child stunting among both- FI (fully immunized) and NFI (not fully immunized) children and decomposition results also show that place of residence contributes to the FI-NFI gap in stunting prevalence among the children. According to a previous study, severe acute malnutrition (SAM) among under-five children in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is generally more prevalent in rural areas than in urban areas and the rural–urban gap in SAM is largely explained by factors like lower neighbour-hood socioeconomic status, poorer household wealth, unimproved toilet facilities, unsafe drinking water, lower maternal education, higher birth order, and short birth intervals in rural areas [35]. While explaining the caste pattern of child nutritional health, another study found that child undernutrition, measured by the Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure (CIAF), was highest among Scheduled Tribe (ST) children, followed by Scheduled Caste (SC), Other Backward Classes (OBC), and the General category [31]. As per the study findings, maternal education significantly contributes to the FI-NFI gap in child stunting which could be supported by a previous study which highlights that children of mothers with little or no education faced a significantly higher risk of undernutrition across all social groups, as maternal education plays a crucial role in child care, health practices, and nutrition knowledge [31]. Across all rounds, maternal height < 145 cm is found to be a statistically significant and positively contributing factor to the disparity in stunting among the under-five children. The impact peaked in NFHS-5 (2019–21), with a notable increase in explanatory power, highlighting the increasing role of maternal nutrition and stature in child health disparities over time. This upward trend in the explanatory power of maternal height signals a deep-rooted intergenerational nutritional deficit that is not only persistent but potentially worsening in some subpopulations. Short maternal height reflects chronic undernutrition during a woman’s own childhood and adolescence, which, in turn, constrains uterine growth capacity and contributes to adverse birth outcomes such as intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) and low birth weight—both known precursors of childhood stunting. Studies have consistently identified a strong and independent association between maternal height and child stunting. For instance, previous studies [51, 52] reported that maternal height is one of the most significant non-modifiable predictors of child stunting globally, and particularly in South Asia. Another study, using NFHS data, also highlighted that children born to mothers shorter than 145 cm had a significantly higher risk of being stunted, even after adjusting for socioeconomic and demographic factors [53]. The findings from this study also clearly indicates that economic status also plays a crucial role contributing to the FI-NFI gap of child stunting in India and arguably we find ample evidences that children from poorer households are more likely to be undernourished compared to those from richer households, reflecting the impacts of food insecurity [54], poor living conditions [55], and limited healthcare access [56]. While exploring the regional context of the stunting prevalence, a study highlights that children from the central, western, and eastern regions of India exhibit higher undernutrition rates compared to those from other regions [31]. Although the overall prevalence of undernutrition declined between 2005-2006 and 2019–2021, significant intergroup disparities persisted, indicating that social, educational, economic, and regional inequalities continue to strongly influence child nutrition outcome in India.

This study consists of large datasets of three time periods which potentially produces the temporal phenomenon of the stunting burden by immunization status subject to heterogeneous child population. In terms of geography, culture, socio-economy and demographics, India being diversified a country so as the population of the children; there remains a large inequality in the child health care utilization as well as huge disparity in terms of child health status [57–59]. Launched in 1978, Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) aimed to make the immunization service available by 1990 and immunization services are made available through hospitals and dispensaries in urban areas whereas primary health centre/sub-centre plays a crucial role to provide immunization services in India as a part of primary health care. Still there lies a large disparity in the utilization of immunization services among the rural children and poor health services and absence of immunization programs cause a larger disparity in the nutritional status of the children [60]. For any country, the measures of child health indicator reflects the overall development in terms of economy and health care primarily and any deprivation in terms of food, water, sanitation and health care services may lead to poor nutritional status among the children in the country [60]. Thus, to reduce the heterogeneous burden of child stunting it is necessary to reduce the inequality of health care service utilization across the sub population and to make available the essential child health care services especially immunization and treatment facility to subclinical infections.

The WHO’s Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030) outlines a strategic framework for strengthening immunization within primary health care systems to advance universal health coverage, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Targeted efforts to reach zero-dose and under-immunized children, especially those in marginalized and underserved populations, are critical for addressing persistent equity gaps [61]. Strengthening vaccine supply chains, ensuring sustained political commitment, and increasing domestic resource allocation are essential for the long-term sustainability of immunization programs [61]. Immunization and nutrition are interrelated; vaccines reduce the burden of infectious diseases that can exacerbate malnutrition, thereby supporting optimal child growth and development [61]. Community engagement and the mitigation of vaccine hesitancy are vital for improving immunization uptake and public trust. The adoption of digital innovations and context-specific delivery models can further enhance immunization coverage and performance monitoring [61]. Collectively, these interventions contribute to the reduction of child mortality, promote health equity, and strengthen resilient health systems capable of responding to emerging public health challenges [61].

India’s strategic approach to reducing child stunting and improving full immunization coverage necessitates an integrated, multi-sectoral framework leveraging existing platforms such as POSHAN Abhiyaan and the National Health Mission (NHM) [62]. Evidence indicates that scaling up complementary feeding could prevent up to 60% of stunting cases among children under five years of age [62]. Addressing key social determinants—specifically, improving maternal education, delaying early marriage, and enhancing maternal health—remains critical for sustainable reductions in undernutrition [62]. Technological innovations, including the ICDS-Common Application Software (CAS), alongside strengthened capacity building for frontline workers, are essential to improve the efficiency and reach of service delivery [62]. Targeted interventions are required to address persistent coverage gaps in marginalized and urban poor populations [62]. Furthermore, behaviour change communication strategies and community engagement efforts play a pivotal role in increasing the demand for and uptake of immunization and nutrition services [62]. Collectively, these interventions are essential to accelerate progress toward India’s child health and nutrition targets within the Sustainable Development Goals.

This study makes a significant contribution by quantifying, for the first time in India’s context, the immunization-attributable burden of stunting among under-five children over a period of two decades, providing robust empirical evidence from nationally representative data. By applying pertinent statistical analysis and decomposition techniques, it dissects the immunization-stunting relationship across socio-economic strata, highlighting the heterogeneity in child health outcome-child stunting. This work advances the literature by establishing immunization not only as a preventive tool against infectious diseases but also as a critical determinant of child nutrition outcomes, an area where evidence remains limited in Indian context. Additionally, the study spans multiple NFHS rounds, offering a robust understanding on the intersection of immunization and child growth trends during a period of major health policy shifts in India. Its findings underscore the importance of equitable immunization coverage in reducing socio-economic disparities in child undernutrition, thus informing integrated child health and nutrition policies. This adds a new dimension to the global immunization and nutrition discourse by emphasizing immunization’s indirect benefits on growth outcomes, encouraging a more holistic approach to child health interventions.

There are few limitations to the study. First, immunization status of the children is defined based on vaccination card and mother’s recall in the absence of a vaccination card and this has been done following the DHS's framework and guidelines to define immunization status of the children. Thus, a part of the data is limited to mother’s recall bias. Another important aspect of the demographic nexus in between child stunting and immunization is that parent’s awareness to health care utilization for vaccination depends upon their choice to get their children vaccinated [63]. In this case also, parent’s socio-economic characteristics do play a critical role. To minimize the bias, we considered mother’s educational attainment as one of the covariates to control the underlying trait and we don’t deny about the unobserved traits for a choice of vaccination among parents. Previous studies confirm that mother’s educational attainment intrinsically play a crucial role on child’s nutritional choice with the knowledge of breastfeeding and dietary pattern. Thus, among children of parents from certain groups underprivileged in terms of different socio-economic characteristics can show concurrent occurrence of stunting and no/partial immunization. Second, the analysis is based on repeated cross-sectional data from the NFHS rounds, which restricts our ability to establish causal relationships between immunization status and child stunting. Finally, unobserved confounders such as disease infections have not been fully accounted for, which could influence the observed associations.

Implications for Low-and Middle-Income countries (LMICs)

The findings of this study have significant implications for LMICs striving to combat child stunting and improve child health outcomes. First, the observed reduction in stunting attributable to full immunization in India underscores the critical role of universal immunization programs in mitigating undernutrition. Vaccination not only protects against vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) but also indirectly promotes healthy growth by reducing the risk of infections that impair nutrient absorption and utilization. LMICs with high burdens of infectious diseases and limited healthcare infrastructure can similarly benefit from strengthening their immunization programs to address both direct and indirect causes of stunting.

Second, the temporal improvements in stunting prevalence among fully immunized children in India highlight the importance of sustained investments in public health programs. The Universal Immunization Program (UIP) in India serves as an exemplar for LMICs, demonstrating how expanding immunization coverage through targeted primary healthcare interventions can yield substantial health benefits. However, the persistent disparities in stunting across socioeconomic and geographic strata highlight the need for equitable health service delivery, particularly among rural and urban-poor populations. Third, this study emphasizes the interconnectedness of stunting with environmental, socioeconomic, and healthcare access factors. LMICs should integrate immunization programs with broader child health strategies, including efforts to improve maternal education, sanitation, access to clean water, and nutritional support. Addressing these distal factors will amplify the benefits of immunization in reducing stunting.

Finally, the evidence that immunization has non-specific protective effects beyond its intended disease prevention suggests that vaccines, such as DPT and measles, should be prioritized to reduce malnutrition-related immune dysfunction. LMICs can leverage such findings to advocate for global support and funding for comprehensive immunization strategies, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Overall, this study highlights the potential for LMICs to achieve significant reductions in child stunting by scaling up immunization coverage and addressing the broader determinants of malnutrition within their unique socio-economic and health system contexts. This trend may reflect improvements in overall healthcare, reduced disease burden, or other confounding factors influencing the relationship between immunization and health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study adds to the existing knowledge base of stunting differential due to immunization status among the heterogeneous child population of India. From the study findings it is evident that the gap in stunting burden has reduced substantially and children who received all the doses of full immunization showed a lower burden of stunting than those who did not or were partially immunized. It is interesting to know that India has shown a significant reduction in the stunting burden attributable to child immunization still a large proportion of the children are severely undernourished lately. Notably, India has made progress in terms of child health and health care utilization in terms of vaccination but there lies a lot of variations within the intricacies of socio-economic background of the population. It is encouraging that under the Universal Immunization Program and with the support of primary health care services, immunization coverage among children in India has been uplifted and children with their immune health protected against the VPDs made a difference to the reduced gap in child stunting over time. At the same time, it is of immense importance to be careful about the underlying drivers responsible for child stunting within the manifestation of low-economy setting of Indian households- rural and the urban poor households. Because clinically, completion of all the basic vaccination doses promotes the overall as well as the nutritional health of a child subject to the other proximate and distal risk factors of malnourishment. But within the study framework, this study strongly propagates that immunization attributable burden of child stunting has shown a remarkable reduction in India.
