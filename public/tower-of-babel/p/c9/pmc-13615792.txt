Herbivore defense syndromes differ between native and invasive plant species
Authors: Yu-Mei Sun, Feng He, Yi-Wen Zhao, Curtis C Daehler, Ragan M Callaway, Xiao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn their non-native ranges, invasive plant species may escape specialist herbivores and thus experience exclusive pressure from generalists. This is predicted to shape the defense strategies of successful non-native species, as proposed by the Shifting Defense Hypothesis. However, most research to date has quantified only limited sets of defense traits of non-native invasive plant species, and thus our understanding of their defense syndromes, which encompass suites of both physical and chemical traits, remains poorly understood. We evaluated a comprehensive array of leaf physicochemical traits, including quantitative structural defenses, qualitative chemical defenses, and nutritional properties, for 15 phylogenetically matched pairs of non-native species and native congeners co-occurring in abandoned farmland in China. We coupled this trait screening with assessments of herbivore damage in a common garden and laboratory larval growth assays using a generalist herbivore (Spodoptera littoralis; Lepidoptera). Non-native species experienced 50.8% less herbivory and reduced larval growth rate by 25.7%, relative to native congeners. This increase in resistance corresponded with a distinct defense syndrome: non-native species allocated more to broad spectrum, apparently qualitative, chemical defenses, exhibiting 43.1% higher phenolic concentrations, but less to quantitative structural defenses than natives. Hierarchical partitioning revealed that phenolics were the primary predictor of lower Spodoptera growth on non-native species, whereas leaf C content was the best predictor for lower Spodoptera growth on natives. We found differences in defense syndromes and strategies between non-native species and natives, and our findings were consistent with predictions of the Shifting Defense Hypothesis.Keywords: Invasive species, Generalist herbivore, Plant defense, Qualitative defenses, Quantitative defenses, Trichomes

Introduction

Upon arriving in a new geographic region, non-native invasive plant species may escape from specialist herbivores (Liu and Stiling, 2006) and exhibit rapid growth, particularly in disturbed environments characterized by fluctuating resources (Davis et al., 2000; Blumenthal, 2006). Under these circumstances, the success of non-native species may be jointly determined by defenses against generalist natural enemies and ability to grow rapidly. This combination of selection pressures could favor a particular defense syndrome, defined as an association of co-varying defensive traits (Agrawal and Fishbein, 2006). The defense syndromes of non-native species may be influenced by selection pressures that promote reduced investment in specialist defenses and increased allocation to growth and competitive ability; the Evolution of Increased Competitive Ability (EICA) (Blossey and Notzold, 1995; Agrawal et al., 2015). Simultaneously, the Shifting Defense Hypothesis (SDH) predicts increased investment in defenses against generalists after a biogeographical shift from native ranges containing both generalists and specialists to non-native ranges dominated by generalists (Joshi and Vrieling, 2005; Ridenour et al., 2008; Doorduin and Vrieling, 2011; Callaway et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023a).

Specialist herbivore defenses are generally considered quantitative (dose dependent), although quantitative defenses may also affect generalists. Examples include leaf toughness, leaf thickness, and cellulose and lignin concentrations (Liao et al., 2013; Eichenberg et al., 2015; Lin et al., 2015; Müller, 2018). In contrast, specialists are often undeterred or are even attracted to the unique secondary biochemicals that are toxic to generalists even in low doses (Müller-Schärer et al., 2004; Zvereva and Kozlov, 2016) and commonly referred to as qualitative defenses. In non-native ranges, qualitative defense strategies against generalist herbivores are thought to be most important and are predominantly plant taxon-specific toxic biochemical compounds (Müller-Schärer et al., 2004; Sun et al., 2023a). For example, non-native plant species often produce higher concentrations of alkaloids and phenolics than native plants (Sun et al., 2020). There is some evidence for non-native species escaping generalists as well (Schaffner et al., 2011), suggesting that eco-evolutionary histories among plants and generalist consumers may affect invasions. These broad patterns are important, but binary approaches to defenses and specialist vs. generalist herbivores are heuristic, as neither defense syndromes nor herbivores fall neatly into these groups (Ali and Agrawal, 2012).

In addition to chemical and broad structural defenses, microscopic leaf structures like trichomes also play a crucial, though complex, defensive role. Trichomes serve as an essential defense against herbivores (Guan et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024), but also do not fall neatly into qualitative or quantitative components of defense syndromes. Trichomes generally serve as physical barriers and quantitative deterrents to feeding, but some trichomes are glandular and may secrete qualitative defense chemicals. Some studies have reported lower trichome density on conspecifics sourced from non-native ranges (Blair and Wolfe, 2004; Valverde et al., 2015), while other studies reported the opposite (Fortuna et al., 2014; Liao et al., 2014; Kalske et al., 2022), or no difference between conspecifics sourced from native vs. non-native ranges (Alba et al., 2011; Sotes et al., 2015). Nevertheless, because trichome development requires substantial investment in physical structure, we expect successful non-native species to invest less in trichomes, on average. Funk and Throop (2010) compared trichome density on native and non-native species and found higher variation among natives, but we know of no study that has compared trichome-based defenses among large groups of native and non-native congeners. Also, most evaluations of trichomes have focused on counts and morphology, and rarely have integrated these metrics with herbivore performance, other structural defenses, leaf biochemistry, and leaf nutritional value.

The effects of plant defense syndromes on herbivores interact with the nutritional value of different species (Strauss et al., 2002); for example, high nutritional value may reduce effectiveness of defenses (Huang et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2024). Thus, comprehensive evaluation of diverse defenses and nutritional quality of plants may shed light on the role of herbivory in invasions. Here, we use 15 pairs of non-native species and native congeners to compare herbivore damage, a suite of quantitative defenses including trichomes, an integrated set of secondary biochemicals relevant to qualitative defenses, leaf nutritional chemistry, and herbivore performance to explore whole leaf syndromes that correspond with invasion. We also explored the relative contributions of various leaf defense traits to insect resistance for non-native species. We tested the following hypotheses: compared to native species, non-native congeners will (1) experience less herbivore damage and reduce the larval weight gain of a target generalist due to greater relative investment in generalist defenses in non-native ranges, (2) produce greater concentrations of broad spectrum metabolites such as phenolics, alkaloids, and terpenoids in response to disproportionate generalist herbivory, and (3) produce weaker structural defenses, such as reduced leaf thickness, lower lignin content, and lower leaf trichome density. Integrating these components of defense syndromes should provide insight into plant–herbivore interactions and the defense strategies employed by non-native invasive plant species.

Materials and methods

Study system and plant growth

This study was conducted in the Henan Province, central China (34°49′N, 114°18′E). The mean annual temperature is 14°C and the mean annual precipitation is 670 mm (http://www.weather.com.cn/cityintro/101180801.shtml).

Drawing on prior field surveys, we identified the 15 most common non-native plant species, and native congeners, to serve in our experiment (Sun et al., 2023a). According to Ma and Li (2018), all non-native species in our study qualify as non-native invasive plant species. The 15 pairs of non-native plant species and their native congeners were in seven plant families: Asteraceae, Amaranthaceae, Convolvulaceae, Malvaceae, Phytolaccaceae, Solanaceae, Anacardiaceae (Table S1). In late April 2020, we sowed seeds into individual pots (19 cm diameter, 16 cm height) filled with local topsoil. Pots were placed in a greenhouse at 60% humidity, a 16/8 h light/dark cycle, and a 20–26°C day–night temperature cycle. Pots were watered once per two days to capacity. To minimize variation due to initial plant size, we selected uniform seedlings at the four-leaf stage seedlings for the following common garden survey and pot bioassays.

Herbivore damage in the common garden experiment

To assess herbivore damage on non-native plant species under natural conditions, we carried out a common garden experiment. We constructed 150 1 × 1 m plots divided into five blocks with plots 1 m apart and blocks 1.5 m apart. One plot was randomly assigned to one of the 30 plant species in each block. Two similar seedlings were planted, 50 cm apart, in each plot. Plants were exposed to herbivorous insects under natural conditions, allowing natural colonization of herbivores. We then irrigated the plots as needed using a sprinkler system, applying an equal volume of water to each plot, and weeded manually each week. Three months later, we evaluated leaf damage by identifying affected leaves exhibiting irregular holes, notches, or skeletonization.

For each individual plant, pictures were taken of one mature leaf selected randomly. The damaged leaf area was measured using ImageJ 1.54 g. There were 10 replicates for each species.

Herbivore growth

To compare the effects of non-native species and native congeners on the generalist herbivore, we measured the weight gain of larvae feeding on excised leaves (Hettenhausen et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2019). Lepidoptera species were the predominant generalist taxa associated with non-native plant species at our field sites (Sun et al., 2023a). Therefore, we selected Spodoptera littoralis (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) as a model generalist herbivore. S. littoralis is widespread and is thought to be native to northern Africa and the greater Middle East (Shonouda and Osman, 2000; Ghoneim et al., 2020) and feeds on leaves from at least 40 plant families and now has a broad global geographical distribution (Saldamando and Marquez, 2012). We purchased S. littoralis larvae from Keyun Biological Control Co., LTD., Jiyuan, Henan Province, and fed them with soy-based artificial feed to minimize habituation to any of the plant species in our study.

We transplanted one seedling into one pot, with 75 pots for each of the 30 species. Seedlings that died were replaced within two days. All plants were cultivated in a naturally ventilated, rainproof, and semi-open shelter. Each week, we randomly relocated the pots. After plants had developed twenty leaves, herbivore bioassays were initiated. We randomly selected and harvested mature leaves and placed them in a Petri dish (9 cm diameter) on top of moist filter paper, with the adaxial surface of the leaf facing upward. A second instar caterpillar of Spodoptera littoralis was weighed (weight1) and placed in the Petri dish and the lid was sealed to prevent caterpillars from escaping. Leaves were replaced daily to maintain freshness. After four days, caterpillars were weighed again (weight2). Weight gain was calculated as caterpillar weight gain = weight2 – weight1. There were 20 replicates for each plant for a total of 600 plants used for the herbivory experiment (two nativities × 15 plant species × 20 replicates).

Leaf physicochemical traits

To correlate leaf traits with caterpillar growth, we measured a combination of leaf physicochemical traits of 15 pairs of non-native species and their native congeners.

Elements

When the plants had 20 leaves, we collected and dried two leaves from each of three sample plants per species at 45°C for 72 h. Dried plant leaves were pulverized to a powder. An elemental analyzer (Model 4010; Costech Analytical Valencia, CA, USA) was used to determine leaf total carbon (C) and total nitrogen (N) concentrations, and we calculated C/N ratios. In total, there were 180 plants used for the elemental measurements (C: two nativities × 15 plant species × three replicates; N: two nativities × 15 species × three replicates).

Primary metabolites

As above, we collected two leaves from each of three individuals per species, dried and pulverized them into powder. Soluble sugar was measured based on the anthrone colorimetric method (Wang, 2006), and soluble protein was measured with the Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 method (Deng et al., 2012). In total, there were 180 plants used for the leaf primary metabolites measurements (soluble sugar: two nativities × 15 plant species × three replicates; soluble protein: two nativities × 15 species × three replicates).

Secondary metabolites

As above, we collected two leaves from each of three individuals per species. First, freeze-dried samples were pulverized using a mixer mill at a frequency of 60 Hz for 1 min. Next, 10 mg of each sample was weighed and transferred into a 2 mL Eppendorf tube containing 1.0 mL of extraction solution (methanol/water in a 3:1 ratio, pre-chilled to −40°C and containing an internal standard). Subsequently, the samples were homogenized at 35 Hz for 4 min and then sonicated for 15 min in an ice-water bath after a 30-s vortex. After that, the samples were extracted overnight at 4°C on a shaker and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min, also at 4°C. The supernatant was meticulously filtered through a 0.22-micropore membrane. The obtained supernatants were diluted 10-fold with a methanol/water mixture (volume ratio 3:1, containing an internal standard), vortexed for 30 s, and transferred to 2 mL glass vials. Finally, 20 μL of each sample was mixed to form QC samples, which were stored at −80°C until analyzed by ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS).

The UHPL separation was implemented utilizing an EXIONLC System (manufactured by Sciex). In this process, the mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid dissolved in water, while the mobile phase B was pure acetonitrile. The column temperature was maintained at 40°C, and the auto-sampler temperature was set to 4°C. A 2 μL injection volume was adopted. SCIEX Analyst Work Station Software (v.1.6.3) was utilized for the acquisition and processing of multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) data. Metabolomic determination and a series of preparations and organization protocols with the raw data preprocessing were performed by Biotree Biotech (Shanghai, China). Finally, we classified the measured substances into total phenolics, total alkaloids, and total terpenoids based on their groups (Dong et al., 2009).

Quantitative defenses

As above, we collected two leaves from each of three individuals per species. Leaf cellulose content was determined using the anthrone colorimetric method, and leaf lignin content was determined by sulfuric acid hydrolysis-titration method (Xiong et al., 2005; Wang, 2006). We randomly chose one mature leaf from each of 10 individuals per species for measurements of leaf thickness using a vernier caliper. We measured each leaf three times avoiding the veins and calculated the average value. We collected one leaf from each of 12 individuals per species to assess leaf toughness. We used a dynamometer Chatillon (AMETEK, San Diego, California) to measure leaf toughness avoiding the leaf veins. The force of the dynamometer required to puncture the leaves represented leaf toughness. In total, there were 840 plants used for measurements of leaf quantitative defenses (leaf cellulose: two nativities × 15 species × three replicates; leaf lignin: two nativities × 15 species × three replicates; leaf thickness: two nativities × 15 species × 10 replicates; leaf toughness: two nativities × 15 species × 12 replicates).

Leaf trichome density

Since trichomes can contribute to both quantitative and qualitative defense, we present their analysis in a separate section. For each species, we collected the third mature leaf from the top from each of three similar-sized plants. Leaf trichome densities were assessed on both the adaxial and abaxial surfaces of each leaf. Three 1-cm2 pieces were cut from each leaf on each side of the midrib at 2/3 of the distance from the attachment of the petiole to the leaf tip. The leaf midrib was avoided. The sample was fixed onto a microscope stage using double-sided adhesive tape, with both surfaces of the leaf exposed and then placed under a Miniscope TM 3000 (Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan). Under a low-vacuum mode, scanning electron micrographs were acquired using an accelerating voltage of 5 kV and a magnification range of 100–500×, with an image resolution of 1280 × 960 pixels; five microscopic fields were randomly selected for each piece. Finally, we calculated the average leaf trichome density for each individual.

Statistical analysis

Herbivore damage in the common garden experiment

To assess differences in damaged leaf area between non-native and native species, we applied a Wald Chi-square test to a zero-inflated beta generalized linear mixed model (GLMM), with origin (non-natives vs. natives) as a fixed effect in both the conditional and zero-inflation components. Species identity nested within native-non-native pairs and experimental block were included as random effects to account for non-independence with paired comparisons and heterogeneity among blocks. The zero-inflation component accommodated the excess of zeros (i.e., individuals that experienced no herbivory).

Herbivore growth in laboratory experiments

To test the differences in herbivore (Spodoptera littoralis caterpillars) growth when consuming non-native vs. native species, we used a Wald Chi-square test applied to a linear mixed model (LMM) that included origin as a fixed effect, species and native-non-native pairs as random effects. To explore the relationship between damaged leaf area and caterpillar growth on non-native species vs. native congeners, we used a linear regression model to examine the relationship between the damaged leaf area and the mass of caterpillars using mean values per species. Data were natural-log-transformed to improve homoscedasticity and normality of residuals.

Leaf physicochemical traits

To test differences in leaf physicochemical traits of non-native species and native congeners, we conducted LMMs to test leaf C, N, total phenolics, total terpenoids, leaf trichome densities, soluble sugars, cellulose and thickness with origin as a fixed effect, and species and native-non-native pair as random effects. Data were natural-log-transformed, or inv-transformed to improve homoscedasticity and normality of residuals. To evaluate differences in leaf C/N ratio, total alkaloids, lignin, toughness and soluble protein between 15 pairs of non-native species and native congeners, we fitted generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMM) for each trait according to its distributional characteristics. Origin (non-native vs. native) was included as a fixed effect, and species identity and native-non-native pairs were included as random effects.

Relationships between leaf traits and herbivore growth

To further explore the relationships between leaf physicochemical traits and the growth of S. littoralis, we employed a generalized linear model (GLM) with a gaussian distribution to evaluate the effects of leaf physicochemical properties on the caterpillar growth of non-native or native species, using mean values per species. In regression analyses, leaf chemical and structural traits—including leaf total phenolics, total alkaloids, C, N, lignin content, and abaxial trichome density—were included as fixed-effect predictors. Model diagnostics were performed using variance inflation factors (VIF) to assess multicollinearity, and residual diagnostics were evaluated using the “simulateResiduals” function to ensure model assumptions were met. Furthermore, hierarchical partitioning analysis using the “glmm. hp” package was used to quantify the independent contribution of each predictor variable to variation in herbivore performance metrics. Data were natural-log-transformed to improve homoscedasticity and normality of residuals.

The DHARMa package was used for diagnostic checks of all models to evaluate residual behavior and detect any violations of model assumptions. We performed all analyses using the software R 4.5.0 (R Development Core Team 2017) with the “lme4” (Bates et al., 2014), “car” (Fox and Weisberg, 2011), “DHARMa” (Hartig, 2023), “glmmTMB” (Brooks et al., 2017), “glmm.hp” (Lai et al., 2022) and “MuMIn” (Bartoń, 2023) packages.

For primary metabolites, there was no difference in soluble protein (χ2 = 0.42, p = 0.515) or soluble sugars (χ2 = 1.26, p = 0.262) between non-natives and natives (Fig. 3a).

For secondary biochemistry, total phenolic concentrations were 43.1% higher (χ2 = 8.53, p = 0.003) and total alkaloid concentrations were 8.6% lower (χ2 = 4.04, p = 0.044) in leaves of non-native species than native congeners. Total terpenoid concentrations did not differ between natives and non-natives (χ2 = 1.70, p = 0.193) (Fig. 3a).

For quantitative structural defenses, lignin content in the leaves of non-native species was 14.9% lower than that of natives (χ2 = 4.87, p = 0.027). Non-native species showed no significant differences from natives in leaf toughness (χ2 = 1.38, p = 0.240), cellulose content (χ2 = 0.84, p = 0.360), or thickness (χ2 = 0.70, p = 0.401) (Fig. 3b).

There were no differences in total trichomes (χ2 = 3.32, p = 0.068) or trichome densities (χ2 = 1.35, p = 0.246) on the adaxial leaf surfaces. However, trichome densities on the abaxial leaf surfaces of non-native species were 43.4% lower than those of native congeners (χ2 = 3.98, p = 0.046) (Fig. 3b).

Relationships between leaf traits and herbivore growth

Multiple linear regression and the associated variance partitioning analysis indicated that the combined effects of total phenolics, total alkaloids, C, N, lignin, and abaxial trichomes of leaves accounted for 33.1% of the variation in Spodoptera littoralis caterpillar mass among non-native plant species. Furthermore, the regression model identified total phenolics as a significant negative predictor of caterpillar mass for non-native species (parameter estimates = −0.26, 95% CI = [−0.5136, −0.0070], p = 0.044) (Fig. 4a).

For native congeners, leaf traits explained 58.2% of the variation in caterpillar mass, with leaf C emerging as a significant negative predictor of caterpillar mass for the native species (parameter estimates = −0.24, 95% CI = [−0.3872, −0.0848], p = 0.002) (Fig. 4b).

Discussion

Our findings are consistent with the idea that non-native invasive plant species have defense syndromes that are different than native congeners. By integrating generalist herbivore performance with a comprehensive suite of leaf traits, we show a functional trade-off: the enhancement of phenolic-based chemical defense associated with a reduction of investment in structural components including lignin and trichomes (Fig. 5). These results suggest a shift away from defenses targeting specialists to those targeting generalists and corresponding with rapid growth. However, since the species in our study were from highly disturbed habitats, the full spectrum of potential non-native species life history strategies may not be well represented. Thus, non-native species from less disturbed systems might not exhibit similar defense syndromes. That said, our results are consistent with the SDH (Doorduin and Vrieling, 2011), although we cannot exclude the possibility that the success of non-native species reflects pre-adaptation or plasticity in the expression of a defensive traits. Non-native species defense syndromes enhanced protection from generalists through greater broad-spectrum chemical defenses, while promoting faster growth by reducing investment in more costly (in the short term) physical defenses that may better deter specialists.

The blue irregular polygon represents the contribution of the leaf traits of non-native species to experimental herbivore growth, and the red irregular polygon indicates the contribution of leaf traits of native species to herbivore growth.

Non-native species had lower values for traits generally associated with quantitative defense primarily against specialists – leaf lignin and trichome density, and one trait often associated with qualitative defense against generalists – phenolic concentrations – was higher for non-native species. However, some phenolics appear to defend against specialists and the roles of phenolics are not always clear (Müller-Schärer et al., 2004; Massad et al., 2011). The lower weight gain of the generalist herbivore on non-native species observed in our experiments is consistent with the observed differences in defense traits between native and non-native species. This pattern observed in non-native species may reflect the combined effects of constitutive and induced defense mechanisms.

Not all defensive traits differed among non-native and native species in ways that were consistent with the SDH. Alkaloids, a key group of qualitative defense chemicals (Wei et al., 2015; Fridley et al., 2025), were actually higher in natives, although the percent change was less than 10%, so it is not clear whether this difference is ecologically meaningful. Terpenoids, another group of qualitative defenses (Eilers and Heger, 2019), did not differ among native and non-native species. These findings might be explained by the multifunctionality of alkaloids and terpenoids. Besides deterring herbivores, they certain alkaloids can function in nitrogen storage and turnover (Bhambhani et al., 2021), and both chemicals may also be allelopathic, affecting plant–plant and plant–soil interactions (Wink et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2024; Bo et al., 2025). In new biogeographical ranges —where competitors and soil biota may be novel —selection may favor metabolic investment in these compounds for functions beyond or alongside herbivore defense (Ridenour et al., 2008). This functional flexibility may decouple their expression from predictions based solely on herbivore pressure, helping explain the observed patterns. Leaf toughness and thickness, two traits associated with quantitative defenses (Hanley et al., 2007), also did not differ among native and non-native species. Even though not all measured defenses fit predictions, when they were considered as defense complexes or syndromes (Agrawal and Fishbein, 2006; Agrawal, 2007), the overall native-non-native differences aligned with the SDH.

C and N contents in plant tissues influence insect growth and development (Kurokawa et al., 2010). Our results showed non-native species had higher C concentrations, lower N concentrations, and higher C/N ratios than native congeners, which independently of other factors should make non-native plant species less nutritious (Cronin et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2020). High C/N ratios in plant tissues are thought to reduce the fitness of herbivores (Awmack and Leather, 2002). Interestingly, our results indicated that leaf C content was the key predictor of herbivory in natives. Mechanistically, the lower C content in native species might reflect reduced need for structural and/or defensive carbon compounds, such as lignin and terpenes which have higher energetic costs (Coley et al., 1985; Osunkoya et al., 2008), leading to tissues that are more palatable and digestible for generalist herbivores (Awmack and Leather, 2002). This aligns with the observed pattern of higher herbivore consumption on natives. Notably, our results contrast with some previous reports of lower C/N ratios in non-native species (Kurokawa et al., 2010) or in invasive genotypes (Cronin et al., 2015), but these studies do not involve paired native and non-native congeners, which might account for the differing pattern. Our results for leaf C and N content should be interpreted in the context of the entire array of defense traits we measured. Non-native species that escape specialist herbivores may produce qualitative carbon-based chemical defenses to resist generalists in new habitats, as observed in our study. Thus, a high C/N ratio may be an indication of a strong defense syndrome tuned to generalist herbivores.

Plant trichomes are an important line of defense against herbivorous insects (Andama et al., 2020; Kaur and Kariyat, 2023). However, their roles are complex as they both produce chemicals and provide physical protection. Therefore, different selection pressures work on their number, form, and function (Goldberg et al., 2020; Johnson and Baucom, 2023). In the absence of competition and herbivores, Sun et al. (2023b) found that the invasive plant Alternanthera philoxeroides had more leaf trichomes than native plants. In our case, we found a decreased leaf trichome density on the abaxial leaf surface of the non-native species, indicating structural defense is likely decreased in non-native species. These results differ from those reported by Ridenour et al. (2008) but are consistent with the EICA hypothesis.

An important limitation of our results for defense differences between non-native and native species is that they are not biogeographical (Hierro et al., 2005); we did not compare conspecifics from the native and non-native ranges in common conditions. Thus, we did not measure changes in conspecifics in non-native ranges. Instead, we sought evidence of defense syndromes for non-native invasive plants, i.e., different patterns of defenses between non-native species and native congeners. While the observed cross-species differences align with selection on defense syndromes, they are also consistent with pre-adaptation or plasticity. Therefore, our results reveal systematic differences in defense profiles between non-native and native species, but do not provide direct evidence for post-introduction evolutionary changes. These patterns may alternatively reflect inherent phylogenetic differences, pre-adaptation, or plastic trait expression under common environmental conditions. A second limitation is that we focused on a single generalist herbivore species, Spodoptera littoralis. Although we found statistical relationships between success of the generalist and specific defense traits in our non-native and native plant species, we cannot be sure that identical relationships would be found for other generalist herbivores. We acknowledge that such relationships may be herbivore-specific. Therefore, future studies should incorporate native generalists to evaluate the broader applicability and generality of our findings. Regarding trichome quantification, we did not classify the very wide range of trichome types in our native-to-non-native comparisons. Different trichome types can have very different effects on herbivores. Thus, in future multi-species studies, categorizing different trichome types might provide insightful relationships between trichomes and herbivore damage. Last, an important methodological consideration concerns the initial size of the study plants. Although initial seedling sizes in our experiment were estimated to be very small and relatively uniform across seedlings, we acknowledge that variability in initial biomass could contribute to differences in subsequent traits. Including initial plant size as a covariate in statistical models would enhance the robustness of future analyses.

In conclusion, our multi-trait, syndrome-level analysis demonstrates that the successful defense of disturbance-adapted non-native invasive plant species against herbivores does not appear to be merely a matter of strengthening any single defense, but instead is characterized by a complex resource allocation syndrome. The observed native–non–native contrasts are characterized by a functional rebalancing in which investment appears to shift from structural components toward phenolic-based chemical defenses. This pattern advances the SDH by illustrating a quantifiable rebalancing among an integrated syndrome. Thus, our findings underscore the importance of suites of defense traits among disturbance-adapted non-native species.
