Examining the association of sociodemographic characteristics on self-reported changes in health-related behaviors, mental health, and quality of life during the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil
Authors: Alexandre Augusto de Paula da Silva, Adriano Akira Ferreira Hino, Guillermo Rosas, Rodrigo Siqueira Reis, Deborah Salvo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe challenges faced during COVID-19 for the public health were unprecedent in the last 100 years. The necessity to adapt lifestyles during this period highlighted inequalities in health-related behaviors, quality of life and mental health of the population, especially in unequal countries like Brazil.AimTo analyze the associations of sociodemographic characteristics and recalled changes in health-related behavior, mental health, and quality of life during the COVID-19 pandemic Brazil.MethodsQuantitative, web-based representative survey study of Brazilian adults (≥ 18 years). Sampling procedures proportionally considered state population and characteristics to complete quotas based on gender, age group, and socioeconomic status. Health-related outcomes included changes, from before the COVID-19 pandemic to during the COVID-19 pandemic, in habitual physical activity (no change, decrease and, increase), diet behaviors (no change, growth), quality of life (no change, decrease and, increase), and mental health tertile (4–6, 7–9 and 10–16). Variables used to characterize inequalities included gender (male/female), race (white, black, pardo and, other), and educational attainment (less than high school, high school and, higher education). Frequency distribution and multinomial regression were performed with SPSS 23.0 with a significance level 0.05.ResultsMost participants changed physical activity behaviors (77.5%), diet (56.5%), and perceived quality of life (37.9%). Women were less likely to decrease their physical activity, quality of life, and mental health. Among ethnic groups, blacks were less likely to increase unhealthy food consumption. Finally, groups with less than a high school education were more likely to have an unhealthy diet and lower odds of negative mental health scores.ConclusionBeing a woman, black, or having lower educational attainment was found to be associated with changes in health-related behaviors during the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil, underscoring the contribution of COVID-19 to widening health inequities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24707-8.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic caused one of the major public health crises in a century [1] and Brazil became one of the most severely impacted countries in the world, with over 35 million cases and 700,000 deaths reported through the end of 2023 [2]. During the pandemic, evidence shows lower odds of morbidity and mortality by COVID-19 among individuals with healthy lifestyles [3, 4]. These behaviors are known predictors of morbidity and mortality [3, 5], including overall quality of life, mental health, and premature death [6, 7]. To mitigate COVID-19 morbidity and mortality, preventive measures, such as mobility restrictions and physical distancing [8], were adopted across the globe. It has been documented that these measures had effects on health-related behaviors. During the pandemic, global physical activity levels declined from 64.7–57.0% [3, 4, 9, 10], while unhealthy food consumption (fat, sugar, unhealthy processed foods) increased globally from an estimated 15.2–18.5% [4, 9]. Mental health and quality of life were adversely impacted during the pandemic too, driven by pronounced increases in depressive, anxiety, and stress symptoms, and mental distress [11–13].

The worsening of these health-related behaviors and other health outcomes is intertwined with social inequalities, which drive susceptibility toward healthy lifestyles and exposure to social harm when major disruptive events occur, like a global infectious disease pandemic [14]. The simultaneous and combined inequalities in gender, race, and income, are defined as the “Trifecta of Inequalities” [15]. During the pandemic low-income, on-site workers, peripheral residents, and pardo and black ethnic groups experienced increased exposure and vulnerability to COVID-19 threats [16, 17].

Nonetheless, still need to explore how inequalities impacted the population’s health could enhance our understanding of how the COVID-19 pandemic affected the most deprived individuals. Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the associations of sociodemographic characteristics and recalled changes in health-related behavior, mental health, and quality of life during the COVID-19 pandemic Brazil. to determine the differential impact of socioeconomic, gender, and racial inequities on changes in health-related behavior, mental health, and quality of life during the COVID-19 pandemic Brazil.

Methods

Study design

This study builds on a quantitative, cross-sectional, and web-based panel data of over 2000 respondents representing urban adult populations in Brazil. Data was collected in January 2021, during the second wave of the COVID-19 Pandemic, and coinciding with the beginning of vaccination in the country. At this point of the pandemic in Brazil, mask use, physical distancing, and mobility control were still mandatory. A company was hired to collect data (Netquest® - www.netquest.com) through online surveys based on an existing proprietary register of individuals of 18 years or more to achieve a representative sample of internet users proportional to the five country regions. Informed consent to participate in the study was presented before the survey response. Gender, age, and socioeconomic level sample quotas approximate the marginal distributions of these demographics in the national population (S1 Table 1).

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This project was approved by the Brazilian National Commission on Ethics in Research (CONEP) CAAE 37595620.8.0000.0020, in according to Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed and consent to participate for study before the survey.

Measures

Outcomes

Changes in physical activity

Days of physical activity were recorded with two questions: “Before the COVID-19 pandemic, in a usual week, how many days you used to do physical activity at least 30 minutes (continuous or accumulated)?” and “In the last week, how many days you used to do physical activity at least 30 minutes (continuous or accumulated)?”. We calculated the difference between variables [PA last week – PA before COVID-19] and classified participants’ PA changes as “no change” (−2 to + 2 days), “decrease” (−7 to −3 days), and “increase.” (+ 3 to + 7 days.).

Changes in diet

Diet variables used were: “Since the pandemic of COVID-19, I’ve been eating more unhealthy food with excess fat and sugar” and “Since the pandemic COVID-19 started, I’m drinking more soda and beverages with sugar”. These variables were initially coded on a Likert 4-point scale (Strongly agree; agree; disagree and; strongly disagree), but we dichotomized them into “disagree and strongly disagree” into “no change” and; “strongly agree and agree” into “increased.” Afterwards, these two questions were summed and classified into “no change” for those who maintained their prior usual diet and “increase” for raise in consumption of unhealthy foods.

Changes in quality of life

Quality of life was measured with two questions: “How was your quality of life before COVID-19?” and “How is your quality of life in the last two weeks?” The responses were obtained on a 4-point Likert scale (bad, regular, good, and; excellent). The difference between questions [Last 2 weeks – Before COVID-19] was calculated, resulting in an overall score from − 3 to + 3. Significant changes in quality of life were considered on a variation higher than ± 1 point into: −3 to −2 as “decrease”; −1 to + 1 as “no change”; +2 to + 3 as “increase.”

Mental health during the pandemic

Mental health was assessed from the original UCLA Loneliness Scale [18], using four questions adapted to the context: “Feel lack of company;” “Left aside?;” “Isolated from other people?” and “Negative emotions?.” Responses were obtained with a 4-point Likert scale: “Almost never (score = 1), not often, often, and always (score = 4).” All variables were summed up and received a score ranging from 4 (never/less negative emotions) to 16 (more negative emotions). The score was classified in tertiles: “4–6”; “7–9” and; “10–16” with the highest category indicating worse mental health.

Exposures

Using the framework of the “trifecta of inequalities,” [15] we assessed self-reported gender (male or female), race/ethnicity (White, Black, Pardo [mixed race from white, black, and Indigenous], Indigenous, or Asian [19]), and socioeconomic status. Ethnicity was categorized into White, Black, Pardo and Others. Self-reported educational attainment was used as a proxy for socioeconomic status: No instruction, incomplete elementary school, complete elementary school, incomplete high school, complete high school, incomplete higher education, and complete higher education. These categories were grouped into less than high school, high school, and higher education.

Covariates

To characterize the sample, age in years was classified into five categories “18–29; 30–39; 40–49; 50–59, and ≥ 60 years” and country regions “Center-West; North; Northeast, South and, Southwest.”

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with SPSS 23.0. Descriptive frequencies were used to descriptively characterize the sample. Multinomial regression models were run to test the association between the “Trifecta of Inequalities” variables (gender, ethnicity, and educational level) and self-reported changes in health-related variables (physical activity change, diet, quality of life, and mental health). Models were adjusted for age group and country region, and a p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

The survey had a response rate of 87.3% (n = 2,413), of which 63.3% (n = 1,527) had complete data and were included in this analysis. We conducted sensitivity analyses comparing the analytical sample with full data and did not find significant differences in results, considering sociodemographic variables (Table 1). Participants were predominantly female (50.5%), identified as pardo skin color/ethnicity (43.1%), high school educated (58.9%), aged between 18 and 29 years (30.7%), and resided in the southeast region of Brazil (53.1%). Most respondents showed no changes in behaviors related to physical activity (77.5%), diet (56.5%), and quality of life (57.1%) compared to the pre-pandemic period. Discussion

This study examined the associations of sociodemographic characteristics and recalled changes in health-related behavior, mental health, and quality of life during the COVID-19 pandemic Brazil., to examine the potential impact of the pandemic in widening already existing, stark health inequities. Overall results showed no changes in behaviors related to physical activity, diet, and quality of life before pandemic, whereas one third of respondents reported 7–9 points of mental health. Women were less likely to decrease their physical activity, quality of life, and mental health. Black respondents had a lower likelihood of increasing their diet consumption of fat and sugar compared to whites. Finally, when compared to those with higher education, respondents with less than high school education had lower odds of having negative mental health scores. Our findings support the notion that the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated health inequities, consistent with those of other investigators from across the globe, who have reported that the mobility and social restrictions implemented during the pandemic led to an overall reduction in physical activity, quality of life, sleep, and mental health [4, 10, 12, 20, 21], and an increase in sedentary behavior, unhealthy diet, alcohol, smoking, and stress [11, 22].

While most of our group had no overall changes in physical activity, women were less likely to decrease their active behavior when compared to men with no changes. Despite evidence indicating a reduction in physical activity during pandemic [22], gender plays a significant role in this behavior, where necessity and choice-based physical activity were directly affected [21]. Conversely, this may highlight how men remained their usual activities, based on the necessity, and became more exposed to COVD-19. Although women were somewhat protected from losing their activity baseline levels, this group are historically less active than men [23, 24].

When considered ethnic group, black respondents had a lower chance of adopting an unhealthy diet during the pandemic. Conversely, individuals with lower educational levels had a higher likelihood of increasing the consumption of sugar and fat. A systematic review showed no changes in consumption of snacks and fast-food consumption in the general population [25]. However, when considered disadvantaged racial/ethnic and lower socioeconomic groups, the most vulnerable respondents are most likely to suffer from economic or food insecurity. Prior to the pandemic, food insecurity risen in 50% of Brazilian households [26]. Although our results showed an unexpected protective factor for ethnic group, black and pardo in Brazil are a historically marginalized, with lower educational levels, income, and access to services. Nevertheless, the large economic impact of COVID-19 around the world affected the cost of food, and high unemployment possibly led to elevated consumption of ultra-processed foods, sugar, and fat limited even further access to healthy food options [27].

Regarding quality of life, our findings demonstrated no changes whereas mental health scores ranged between 7 and 9. When compared to men, women were less likely to report decreases in their quality of life or worse mental health. Likewise, those with lower educational attainment are less likely to report more mental health problems during the pandemic. Overall, most countries reported a reduction in quality of life and mental health [13]. It is important to highlight that most of the studies used multiple instruments, with no measurement of change, big sampling bias and conducted in high income countries [13, 28]. In a study, when analyzed changes in health, almost half of the sample reported no variations (48.0%), while this perception worsened for one-third (35.0%) [29]. In Brazil, results showed that younger women, without partner, brown and black ethnicity, with lower education, unemployed, with some levels of mobility restriction showed lower scores of quality of life [30]. Regard to mental health, an increase of symptoms were observed like anxiety, depression and distress [31], since many individuals were unable to maintain their usual activities [28]. Mental health care is most discussed by women. Although the access and stigma related to mental health in Brazil, in low income and men, respectively, the pandemic scenario highlighted its necessity and underscored its importance [32]. Despite the inconsistencies in the results, it is important to note that the most vulnerable individuals in adverse situations may not perceive the worsening by the negative conditions, since the necessity of being resilient during these periods.

Some strengths, including in this study should be highlighted, including once on a nationally representative sample with a proportional sampling method considering gender, age group, and socioeconomic status. However, some limitations should be considered by the sample, including only individuals with access to the internet, could lead to selection bias of marginalized groups, although internet penetration in Brazil hovered around 82% in 2021 [33]. Also, the survey included many responses with missing data on some variables (36.7%); though Multiple Imputation of Chain Equations (MICE) techniques were considered [34], by five random imputed datasets, consistent with an assumption of data missing at random (MAR) by the main predictors (gender, ethnic group and educational level) and outcomes (physical activity, diet, quality of life and mental health) of this study. However, results were virtually the same as those of a complete case analysis (data provided in supplementary table S2, table S3). Although behavioral, quality of life, and mental health changes were computed, data were obtained at the exact moment, which was limited by the memory recall and imprecise estimations of their behaviors in past conditions before the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil (officially identified the first case in February 2020). Other contextual variables were not considered as confounders (e.g., local and regional policies related to COVID-19). Still, the selected mental health variables may limit the accuracy of results, being recommended in further studies. Despite this, scoring variables obtained with likert scale responses is a common approach to summarize and analyze these variables. Finally, the study is based on observational data, which prevents the possibility of arriving at solid causal inferences.

Conclusion

Women, black, and people with lower education were associated with changes in health-related behaviors during the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil. Supporting the most vulnerable groups reducing existent and persistent inequalities, with physical activity programs, mental health services and access to healthy food options could help reduce health inequalities and improve quality of life, while protect against public health emergencies. The main priority was to tackle the COVID-19 pandemic to control a highly transmissible disease and protect people’s health. Alongside this, actions should be taken in the subsequent public health emergencies to manage and preserve the collateral harms caused by populational health especially where vulnerable groups interact with a reinforcing system of privilege and oppression at different levels [35]. Providing feasible and accessible interventions, especially to support women, black and groups with lower education, to increase their physical activity levels, food with quality and affordable and, mental health assistance programs could improve quality of life to help reduce health inequalities [14]. Future studies should explore the Trifecta of Inequalities and the Intersectionality framework, to understand how structural inequalities generate multiple discriminations in vulnerable communities in their health-related outcomes.
