Transcriptomic and hormonic analyses reveal the effects of xenia on anthocyanin biosynthesis in blueberry
Authors: Qin Yang, Zhichao Xu, Zhuang Wen, Xingyu Lu, Xianjun Chen, Shu Peng, Yanlan Liu, Zhihong Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractXenia is the phenomenon whereby the pollen genotype directly affects seed and fruit development from fertilization to seed germination. Although anthocyanin accumulation in blueberry is influenced by xenia, the mechanism remains unknown. Considering the central role hormones in modulating fruit quality, we hypothesize that xenia alters endogenous hormone levels, affecting structural gene expression and thus anthocyanin accumulation. To validate this, ‘Premier’ blueberries were pollinated with different pollen genotypes; and parallel phenotypes assessed included anthocyanin content, hormone content and ratio, and integrated transcriptomic analyses. Results showed that anthocyanin rapidly accumulated 60 days after pollination (DAP), but significant differences between self- and cross-pollination emerged after 40 DAP. Self-pollination in ‘Premier’ resulted in significantly lower anthocyanin content than when cross-pollinated with ‘Brightwell’ and ‘Powderblue’ (P = 0.05). The hormone content and ratio significantly differed between self- and cross-pollination treatments (P = 0.05) during fruit development. Cross-pollination upregulated key structural genes, such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, chalcone synthase, flavanone 3-hydroxylase, and dihydroflavonol 4-reductase, but downregulated anthocyanidin reductase (FC > 2.00 and P < 0.01). The anthocyanin content was significantly related to the hormone content, hormone ratio, and expression levels of hub differentially expressed genes (‘*’, P = 0.05; ‘**’, P = 0.01). These results confirm that xenia affects the differential expression of structural genes via hormones, influencing anthocyanin accumulation. These findings provide new insights into the mechanism by which xenia affects fruit quality and indicate that selecting appropriate pollen genotypes is a practical approach to improving the nutritional quality of fruit.Keywords: Pollen genotypes, Nutritional enhancement, Phytohormones, Transcriptome, Biosynthetic pathway, Structural genes

Introduction

Xenia refers to the direct influence of the pollen genotype on the development of maternal seed and fruit tissues following fertilization, which is classified into two types: xenia and metaxenia. In the former, the direct effects of the pollen genotype are exhibited in the syngamous parts of the ovules; that is, the embryo and endosperm only. In the latter, the effects of the pollen genotype are demonstrated in structures other than the embryo and endosperm; that is, in tissues derived wholly from the mother plant material, including seed parts such as the nucellus and testa, as well as in the carpels and accessory tissues (Yang, Fu, et al., 2020). This phenomenon modifies key phenotypic traits—including fruit size, color, flavor, and nutrient composition—via metabolic pathways modulated by hormones, polyamines, and pollen-derived miRNAs (Yang, Liu, et al., 2020). Studies have shown that pollen source–dependent changes in endogenous hormones are a major mechanism underlying xenia in fruit crops during post-pollination fruit development (Nasr et al., 2019; Xu, 2013; Zhang et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2015).

In general, variations in the contents and relative ratios of auxins (IAAs), gibberellins (GAs), cytokinins (CTKs), abscisic acid (ABA), and ethylene (ETH) are closely associated with differences in cell division, cell expansion, specific morphological outcomes, and quality attributes induced by different pollen genotypes. Representative examples illustrate these patterns. In ‘Shatianyou’ pomelo, pollination elevates GA1+3, CTK, ABA, and IAA in the ovary and produces two hormone peaks during rapid fruit growth, which correlates with accelerated ovary enlargement (Nie & Liu, 2002). In pear, cross-pollination often increases IAA and zeatin riboside (ZR) contents during the vigorous cell-division phase, promoting cell proliferation and fruit expansion (Pan et al., 2012). Specific pollen sources can alter traits, such as duckbill incidence in ‘Yali’ by increasing GA3 and ZR and decreasing ABA (Zhang et al., 2020) and aroma enhancement by increasing the ABA, IAA, and ETH contents (Liu, Zhang, et al., 2023). In apple, pollination with ‘Mei-hong’ results in higher IAA and GA3 contents but a lower ABA content in later development, extending the growth period and yielding a greater fruit size at harvest (Xu, 2013). Comparable pollen-dependent hormonal effects have been reported in other cultivars and species (Nasr et al., 2019; Sha et al., 2006; Zou et al., 2015).

Hormones act in concert rather than isolation; their regulatory effects depend on concentration ranges and synergistic/antagonistic interactions. Thus, hormone ratios (e.g., IAA/ABA, GA3/ABA, and ZR/ABA) often predict developmental outcomes better than single-hormone levels (Yang, Fu, et al., 2020). For example, higher IAA/ABA and GA3/ABA ratios have been associated with larger fruit in sweet orange (Pang, 2017), and altered GA3/ABA, ZR/ABA, and IAA/ABA ratios at the young-fruit base have been linked to increased protruding-duck formation in cross-pollinated ‘Yali’ (Zhang et al., 2020). More recently, variations in IAA/ABA and GA3/ABA ratios have been reported among different pollinizer combinations in ‘Xiangmizao’ at 75 h after pollination (Huang et al., 2024). Collectively, these studies demonstrate that pollen genotype-dependent modulation of hormone levels and their ratios is a consistent and physiological basis for xenia-related differences in fruit appearance and internal quality (Yang, Fu, et al., 2020).

Xenia is widespread in fruit trees (Yang, Liu, et al., 2020); pollen of different genotypes affects the anthocyanin content in blueberry (Liu et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2017; Yang, Chen, et al., 2020; Yang, Li, Tian, Lv, Tang, & Zhang, 2015; Yang, Li, Tian, Lv, Tang, Zhang, Yue, & Wang, 2015). As prominent constituents of the flavonoid metabolic pathway, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins are important secondary metabolites that are beneficial to human health (Gao et al., 2021). Therefore, conducting research on the accumulation and regulatory mechanisms of anthocyanins is of great significance for increasing the anthocyanin content in fruits and improving fruit quality, and has been well studied in horticultural crops (Wang & Zhang, 2022). The biosynthesis of anthocyanins involves the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, the flavonoid metabolic pathway, and the anthocyanin modification pathway, which are coordinated and controlled by multiple structural genes (Zheng et al., 2023). In the phenylalanine pathway, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) catalyzes the formation of flavonoid precursors, phenylalanine, into cinnamic acid, which then undergoes two steps to form 4-coumaroyl-CoA. Chalcone synthase (CHS) is the first enzyme in the flavonoid metabolic pathway, catalyzing the formation of 4-coumaroyl-CoA and propionyl-CoA into tetrahydrochalcone. After several reactions, dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR) catalyzes the formation of colorless anthocyanins from dihydroflavonols, dihydrorosmanol, and dihydroquercetin. Anthocyanins are then modified by enzymes such as flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT) to form anthocyanins (Qiao et al., 2024; Zong et al., 2025).

Previous studies have shown that plant hormones, such as ABA, play a positive or negative role in regulating anthocyanin biosynthetic structural genes, influencing anthocyanin accumulation (Li and Ahammed, 2023; Shi et al., 2023). For instance, MdARF13 binds directly to the promoter of the anthocyanin pathway structural gene MdDFR. MdARF2 plays an important role in the regulation of anthocyanins in apple which negatively regulated anthocyanin accumulation in apple by decreasing the expression level of anthocyanin synthesis-related genes (Wang et al., 2021). Similarly, Cytokinin can upregulate the expression of genes such as MdCHS, MdCHI, MdMYB10, MdbHLH13 in red flesh apples, inhibit the expression of MdMYBL2, and thereby promote the accumulation of anthocyanins in red flesh apples (Wang et al., 2018). Moreover, exogenous ABA treatment increased the expression of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes VvPAL, VvC4H, VvCHS2, VvF3H1, VvF3’5′H, VvUFGT, as well as key transcription factors VvMYBA1 or VvbZIP026 in grape pericarp, thereby promoting the accumulation of anthocyanin content in grape pericarp (Li et al., 2025). In addition, the ethylene signal transduction gene MdEIL1 is a key gene in the ethylene-induced pathway for anthocyanin synthesis. It can work together with MdERF1B to upregulate the expression of regulatory genes MdMYC2, MdMYB9, and MdMYB11 in the anthocyanin synthesis pathway, thereby promoting the accumulation of anthocyanins in apples (Wang et al., 2022).

Our previous research showed that xenia significantly affects the enzyme activities of ANS, ANR, and LAR in the later stage of blueberry fruit development, as well as the anthocyanin content (Yang, Xu, & Gao, 2024). Meanwhile, a transcriptomics study on highbush blueberry showed that at 10 d after pollination (DAP), IAA signaling pathways were enhanced in cross-pollinated fruit compared TO self-pollinated fruit (Nagasaka et al., 2022). Furthermore, our previous transcriptome analysis of blueberry fruit pollinated by pollen of different genotypes identified 174 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) related to plant hormone signal transduction (Yang, Xu, & Gao, 2024). However, it remains unclear whether xenia has influenced the changes in the content and ratios of endogenous hormones during the fruit development process? Do the differences in endogenous hormones affect the expression of structural genes in the anthocyanin synthesis pathway? Is there a correlation between anthocyanin content and various endogenous hormones? How does xenia affect the pattern of anthocyanin accumulation in blueberry fruits?

Based on the aforementioned research progress, we hypothesize that xenia alters endogenous hormone levels, thereby affecting structural gene expression and, thus, anthocyanin accumulation. To test this, we conducted a comparative study to evaluate the effects of xenia on anthocyanin accumulation. We integrated transcriptomic and hormonal analyses to elucidate the physiological and molecular mechanism by which xenia affects blueberry fruit development through the regulation of hormones and anthocyanins. The results of this study provide a theoretical explanation for the physiological and molecular mechanism related to xenia in blueberry and increases our understanding of the effect of pollen genotypes on secondary metabolite regulation. Additionally, these findings provide new insights into the mechanism by which xenia affects fruit quality and indicate that selecting appropriate pollen genotypes is a practical approach to improving the nutritional quality of fruit.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

One hundred rabbiteye blueberry plants (Vaccinium virgatum Reade cv. ‘Premier’ (PR)), each seven years old, were selected as maternal parents for this study. Selection was based on uniform, robust growth and the absence of observable pests or diseases. The specimens were cultivated at the training base of Excellent Agriculture and Forestry Talents of Kaili University, Guizhou, China (26°31′N, 107°53′E). At the initial flowering stage of PR, nine medium-to-long fruiting branches were selected from the upper outer canopy of each tested plant. On each selected branch, 10 flowers at the large bud stage were retained and emasculated, while all remaining flowers were removed. To prevent unintended pollination, the treated branches were isolated using 100-mesh nylon nets, which were removed on the 7 DAP (Yang, Li, Qin, Zhou, Tang, Zhang, Yue, & Wang, 2015). Three days after emasculation, controlled pollinations were performed using pollen collected from seven-year-old PR, ‘Brightwell’ (BR), and ‘Powderblue’ (PO) donors, all cultivated under identical environmental conditions. This resulted in three distinct pollination treatments: self-pollination (PR × PR; designated as PPR) and two cross-pollinations (PR × BR (PBR), and PR × PO (PPO). To ensure a balanced experimental design, each pollination combination was applied to three medium-to-long branches per maternal plant. This resulted in approximately 29 flowers per treatment for each individual plant, yielding a total sample size of at least 2700 flowers per pollination combination across all tested plants. To ensure sufficient pollen load, each stigma was manually pollinated three times using a brush saturated with the respective pollen genotype, ensuring that the stigmas were fully covered with pollen. The viability of the pollen sources—assessed via the 0.5% TTC method—was 42.33% for PR, 86.67% for BR, and 84.33% for PO (Yang et al., 2021).

Sampling and sample preparation

Three days after emasculation and immediately prior to pollination, 300 unpollinated flowers were harvested to serve as a control group. To ensure a representative sample, ovaries were collected following a randomized block design: one ovary was selected from each designated medium-to-long branch, totaling three ovaries per maternal plant. Fruit development was monitored longitudinally, with samples collected at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 DAP. At each time point, 300 young fruits were harvested per pollination treatment. To maintain experimental consistency and ensure equal representation of all maternal branches, one fruit was randomly selected from each of the three medium-to-long branches per plant. Furthermore, samples were processed in triplicate for each treatment at every sampling interval, with each replicate comprising a pool of 100 young fruits. Upon transport to the laboratory, collected fruits were processed immediately by removing the stems and slicing the tissue into 1-mm sections. These sections were flash-frozen and ground in liquid nitrogen before being transferred to 50-mL centrifuge tubes. All samples were labeled (Table 1) and stored at −80 °C freezer until analysis.

Anthocyanin content determination

The anthocyanin content was determined using the pH differential method as described by Yuan et al. (2022), with slight modifications. Two blueberry pulp (0.6 mg) was mixed with 5.4 mL of potassium chloride buffer (pH 1.0) and sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0), respectively. The mixture was incubated in a dark room for 30 min, then centrifuged to obtain the supernatant, which was diluted 10-fold respectively. Then, the absorbance of the reaction mixtures was determined using a UV-600 UV–visible spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with 1-cm path length cuvettes. Measurements were taken at 510 nm (λmax) for anthocyanin content and 700 nm for haze correction. Total anthocyanin content was calculated and expressed as mg/g fresh weight. All measurements were performed in triplicate at room temperature using distilled water as a blank to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Endogenous hormone extraction and determination

Endogenous hormones were extracted following previously described methods (Deng et al., 2019; Ning et al., 2024) with minor modifications. Briefly, frozen fruit samples (100 mg) from each pollination treatment were ground to a fine powder using zirconia beads under liquid nitrogen. Then, 100 mg of the resulting fine powder was transferred to a 2 mL centrifuge tube and suspended in 950 μL of a methanol:water mixture (8:2, v:v) and 50 μL of an internal standard stock solution. The mixture was agitated for 20 min at 20 °C and 214 g, followed by 5 min of ultrasonication (40 kHz). The resulting suspension was then centrifuged for 10 min at 4 °C and 13,800 g. The supernatants were collected, and the remaining residues were re-extracted by adding 500 μL of methanol and repeating the shaking (20 min, 214 g, 20 °C), ultrasonication (5 min, 40 kHz), and centrifugation (10 min, 13,800 g, 4 °C) steps. Finally, both supernatants were combined and centrifuged at 13,800 g (4 °C) for 10 min. Then, a 500 μL aliquot of the combined extract was collected for hormone quantification. All procedures were performed in triplicate using three independent biological replicates.

The contents of IAA, GA3, CTK, ABA, and ACC (ethylene precursor 1-aminocyclopropanecarboxylic acid) were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS/MS; Shimadzu LC20AD–API3200MD TRAP, Kyoto, Japan) following established protocols (Chen et al., 2022), with slight modifications. Chromatographic separation was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (1.8 μm, 50 mm × 4.6 mm). The mobile phase consisted of water containing 0.02% acetic acid (A) and acetonitrile containing 0.02% acetic (B). The gradient elution program was as follows: 95:5 V(A)/V(B) at 0.5 min, 5:95 V(A)/V(B) at 5 min, 95:5 V(A)/V(B) at 5.1 min, and 95:5 V(A)/V(B) at 8 min. The flow rate was 0.80 mL/min, the column temperature was 30 °C, and the injection volume was 10 μL. Mass spectrometry was performed using an electrospray ionization source (ESI) operating in negative ion mode. Data acquisition was conducted in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode (Supplementary Table S1). The curtain gas pressure was 206,843 Pa, and the ionization voltage was 5500 V and − 4500 V for positive and negative ion modes, respectively. The ion source temperature was 600 °C, the spray gas pressure was 275,790 Pa, and the auxiliary gas pressure was 344,738 Pa. Data analysis was performed using Analyst 1.7 software.

Phytohormone content was determined by the internal standard (13C6-IAA, 13C6-GA3, 13C5-Zea, 13C6-ABA, 13C2-ACC) method using stable isotope-labeled compound; hormone identification and quantification were performed based on the retention times and mass transitions of authentic standards. External calibration curves were constructed for each hormone using a series of standard solutions with appropriate concentration ranges, and all calibration curves showed good linearity (R2 > 0.99). For each developmental stage, hormone levels were determined in triplicate using three independent biological replicates. The limits of detection (LODs) and limits of quantitation (LOQs) of five plant hormones were 0.02 to 0.05 pg/g and 0.07 to 0.13 pg/g respectively, and the recovery rates and matrix effects were within the range of 94.62 to 108.38% and − 16.62% to 18.24%, respectively.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

RNA-seq data for PR fruits were obtained from a previous study conducted by our research group (Yang, Xu, & Gao, 2024). Samples were collected at six developmental stages (at 10-day intervals from 40 to 90 days after self-pollination or cross-pollination with BR and PO). The datasets have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (Chen et al., 2021), the National Genomics Data Center (CNCB-NGDC Members and Partners, 2024), and the China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (BioProject: PRJCA027317, GSA: CRA017214), and are publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa.

RNA-seq libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, CA, USA) at Biomarker Technologies. Each sample was represented by three independent biological replicates. The raw paired-end reads of 150 bp obtained from sequencing were processed by removing reads containing adapters and eliminating low-quality reads (including reads with an N ratio greater than 10%, and reads where the number of bases with quality score Q ≤ 20 accounted for more than 50% of the entire read) to obtain high-quality clean data, ensuring decent quality and reliability for data analysis. The high-quality sequence data exhibited Q30 scores ranging from 91.67% to 95.26%, with GC contents between 46.23% and 48.20%. HISAT2 software was employed to accurately align the clean reads to a reference genome (Colle et al. 2019) and obtain the positional information of the reads on the reference genome. Subsequently, StringTie (Pertea et al. 2015) was used to assemble the aligned reads to reconstruct the transcriptome for further analysis.

The mapped reads were used to perform an alignment search in the Unigene database using Bowtie (Langmead et al., 2009), and the alignment results were then analyzed using RSEM (Li & Dewey, 2011) to determine the fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM) expression values. DEGs were identified across three biological replicates using the DESeq2 package (Varet et al., 2016). The conditions for screening DEGs were |log2Fold Change| ≥2 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01. Subsequently, the DEGs were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using KOBAS software (Xie et al., 2011).

WGCNA was performed to correlate RNA-seq datasets with traits (Langfelder & Horvath, 2008). The contents of IAA, CTK, GA, ABA, ACC, and anthocyanin served as the trait data. The analysis followed previously established protocols (Cao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025; He et al., 2021) with minor modifications. In detail, an adjacency matrix was created using TPM values for all the unigenes obtained by RNA-seq with a threshold of TPM > 1. The co-expression modules were generated using the automatic network construction function with the following default parameters: soft power of β = 6, mergeCutHeight of 0.25, powerEstimate of 18, minModuleSize of 50, and sensitivity of 3.0. The correlations between the genes in the modules and the traits were determined based on the Pearson correlation coefficient; a correlation was considered significant if the Pearson correlation coefficient was (r) > 0.8 and a p value was <0.05 (Chen et al., 2025). The correlations of the endogenous hormone content, endogenous hormone ratios, hub DEG expression, and anthocyanin content were visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.3).

Screening and expression pattern analysis of hub DEGs related to anthocyanin biosynthesis

Integrating WGCNA with DEG annotation, we selected hub DEGs related to anthocyanin biosynthesis using a module membership (MM) > 0.8, gene significance, (GS) > 0.8, and at least one sample with an TPM value >5 (Cao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025). Functional analysis mapped these DEGs to three primary KEGG pathways: phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941), and anthocyanin biosynthesis (ko00942) (Zong et al., 2025). The heat map of the differential expression of hub DEGs was drawn using TBtools-II software with log2, and a zero-one normalization function was applied between each row of data (Chen et al., 2023). The schematic representation of the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway and expression heatmap of related hub DEGs in the fruit of blueberry after pollination with pollen of different genotypes were determined based on the KEGG pathway of hub DEGs related to anthocyanin.

Validation of hub genes by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

To further validate the accuracy of hub DEGs obtained by the RNA-seq data, 12 key structural genes with significantly different and high expression levels within the candidate hub modules were selected for analysis, all of which are key nodes in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway. Specific primers were designed using Primer 5.0 (Premier Biosoft International, Palo Alto, CA, USA) (Table 2) and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Primer specificity was assessed through the Primer-BLAST program (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and further confirmed by the corresponding a single, sharp melting curves. Reaction specificity and accuracy of the RT-qPCR products for all samples were confirmed by melting curve analysis. The amplification efficiencies of all primer pairs was within the range of 95% to 108%, and the difference in amplification efficiencies among all primer pairs was ≤5%.

Total RNA was extracted from fruit using an RNAprep pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. PCR amplification was conducted according to the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit protocol (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). Data analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate using at least three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3). Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical differences were evaluated using two-way ANOVA via SPSS (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the FDR correction. Statistical comparisons were performed using Waller-Duncan's test, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Correlation analysis was performed using the endogenous hormone contents, endogenous hormone ratios, hub DEG expression levels, and anthocyanin content, calculating Pearson's correlation coefficient and assessing significance via two-tailed tests using SPSS 22.0 (‘*’, P = 0.05; ‘**’, P = 0.01). Prior to correlation analysis, FPKM values of the hub DEGs were log2-transformed to achieve normalization, and data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test in SPSS 22.0.

Results

Effect of pollination with pollen of different genotypes on the anthocyanin content in fruit

Anthocyanin accumulation differed among PR fruit resulting from self-pollination (PPR) and cross-pollination with BR and PO (Fig. 1). Significant treatment differences first appeared at 40 DAP. From 40 DAP onward, cross-pollinated fruit (PBR and PPO) exhibited a significantly higher anthocyanin content than self-pollinated fruit (PPR) (P = 0.05). Although the anthocyanin content increased under all treatments, the increase accelerated after 60 DAP and was consistently higher in cross-pollinated fruit. At maturity (80–90 DAP), fruit pollinated by PPO showed the highest anthocyanin content, which was significantly greater than that in PBR and PPR (P = 0.05).

Effect of pollination with pollen of different genotypes on the endogenous hormone content of fruit

The endogenous hormone profiles during fruit development showed clear differences between self- and cross-pollination treatments (P = 0.05) (Fig. 2). The IAA content in all treatments peaked at 10 DAP and then declined gradually. Cross-pollinated fruit consistently exhibited a higher IAA content than self-pollinated fruit from 10 to 60 DAP. However, at 70–90 DAP, the IAA content rose in self-pollinated fruit and became significantly higher than that in cross-pollinated fruit (P = 0.05) (Fig. 2A). CTK accumulation remained relatively stable in self-pollinated fruit from 30 to 80 DAP, whereas cross-pollinated fruit showed a sustained and more pronounced increase throughout development. From 50 DAP onward, both cross-pollination treatments resulted in a significantly higher CTK content than the self-pollination treatment, with PPO producing the highest concentrations at maturity (P = 0.05) (Fig. 2B). The GA3 content increased after pollination and remained high from 30 to 60 DAP under all treatments. Although all fruit showed a gradual decline after 70 DAP, PPO-pollinated fruit maintained a significantly higher GA3 content than both PBR and PPR during the later developmental stages (P = 0.05) (Fig. 2C). The ABA content increased significantly after pollination and remained higher in cross-pollinated than in self-pollinated fruit throughout development (P = 0.05). From 40 to 90 DAP, both PPO and PBR maintained consistently elevated ABA concentrations compared to PPR, indicating that exogenous pollen promoted stronger ABA accumulation (Fig. 2D). The ACC content rose progressively from 10 to 70 DAP under all treatments. Cross-pollinated fruit exhibited a significantly higher ACC content than self-pollinated fruit throughout fruit development, with PPO having the highest ACC content at maturity (90 DAP) (P = 0.05) (Fig. 2E). Overall, the five hormones displayed distinct patterns under different pollen sources, with cross-pollination enhancing CTK, GA3, ABA, and ACC accumulation, particularly during the critical period of 70–90 DAP.

Effect of pollination with pollen of different genotypes on the endogenous hormone ratios in fruit

During fruit development, the hormone ratios exhibited distinct patterns between self- and cross-pollinated PR blueberry, and these differences were most pronounced during the critical coloring stage (70–90 DAP) (Fig. 3). Overall, cross-pollinated fruit, particularly that pollinated by ‘Powderblue,’ showed hormonal balances more favorable for anthocyanin biosynthesis. The IAA/ACC ratio showed an early peak at 10 DAP under all treatments, but from 20 to 50 DAP, it was significantly lower in cross-pollinated fruit than in self-pollinated fruit. After 70 DAP, the ratio increased in self-pollinated fruit and remained significantly higher than in cross-pollinated fruit until maturity (P = 0.05) (Fig. 3A). Conversely, the CTK/IAA and CTK/ACC ratios gradually increased from mid-development onward and were consistently higher in cross-pollinated fruit from 70 to 90 DAP. The highest values were observed after PO cross-pollination, suggesting a stronger CTK advantage compared to IAA and ACC in promoting late-stage fruit coloration (Fig. 3B, C). The GA3/IAA and GA3/ACC ratios showed similar trends; after relatively stable values from 20 to 60 DAP, cross-pollinated fruit—especially PO—exhibited significantly higher ratios at 70–90 DAP, indicating enhanced GA-associated signaling during the ripening phase (P = 0.05) (Fig. 3D, E). The ABA/IAA and ABA/GA3 ratios increased steadily throughout development and were significantly higher in cross-pollinated fruit from 70 DAP onward (P = 0.05) (Fig. 3F, H). In contrast, the ABA/CTK ratio showed the opposite pattern, remaining significantly higher in self-pollinated fruit from 70 to 90 DAP (P = 0.05) (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that cross-pollination shifts the hormonal balance toward higher CTK/IAA, CTK/ACC, GA3/IAA, GA3/ACC, ABA/IAA, and ABA/GA3 ratios during the critical coloration stage, whereas self-pollinated fruit exhibits relatively higher ABA/CTK and IAA/ACC ratios. These hormonal ratio differences likely contribute to the enhanced anthocyanin accumulation observed under cross-pollination.

Weighted correlation network analysis reveals gene modules associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis

WGCNA was applied to further refine the selection of candidate genes from an initial set of 64,999 genes filtered from the transcriptome data. According to the criteria of mixed dynamic shuffling, 11 modules were obtained (Fig. 4 A). Among the 11 modules, the MEturquoise module was the largest, comprising 4733 genes, of which 3389 were DEGs. This was followed by the black module, which contained 1751 genes and 423 DEGs. The remaining 9 modules each had no more than 200 DEGs. Among them, the MEmidnightblue module had the fewest, with only 74 genes. Correlation analysis among the module, five plant hormones, and anthocyanin (Fig. 4 B) showed that the MEturquoise module had an extremely significant positive correlation with IAA (R = 0.96, P < 0.00003) and an extremely significant negative correlation with CTK, ABA, ACC, and anthocyanin (R = −0.92, −0.92, −0.89, and − 0.91, respectively; P < 4e−04, 5e−04, 0.001, and 6e−04, respectively). The MEred and MEsalmon modules were significantly positively correlated with anthocyanin (R = 0.69 and 0.78, respectively; P = 0.04 and 0.01, respectively). WGCNA correlation analysis suggests that the MEturquoise module can serve as a candidate module for screening different pollen genotypes to identify hub DEGs that regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis in blueberry fruit through plant hormones.

Screening and analysis of hub DEGs involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis

Integrating WGCNA with KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, we identified 48 hub DEGs related to anthocyanin biosynthesis from the candidate hub module of MEturquoise. These candidates primarily consist of structural genes mapping to key nodes in the phenylpropanoid, flavonoid, and anthocyanin biosynthesis pathways. The expression heatmaps of the 48 hub DEGs in fruits of PR at six developmental stages (at 10-day intervals from 40 to 90 days after self-pollination or cross-pollination with BR and PO) are shown in Fig. 5. Notably, at 80 DAP, the expression levels of most of the 48 identified hub DEGs were significantly lower in self-pollinated PR fruits than in their cross-pollinated counterparts (PBR and PPO). These findings suggest that pollen genotype may modulate the accumulation of anthocyanins by regulating the expression levels of key structural genes in the phenylpropanoid, flavonoid, and anthocyanin biosynthesis pathways.

Regulatory network of hub DEGs by endogenous hormone content and ratio

Correlation analysis indicated that multiple endogenous hormones (IAA, CTK, ABA, and ACC) and their ratios (IAA/ACC, CTK/IAA, CTK/ACC, GA3/ACC, ABA/IAA, ABA/CTK, and ABA/GA3) were closely associated with numerous hub DEGs, suggesting that anthocyanin biosynthesis is regulated by a complex hormone network (Fig. 8). The total anthocyanin content was extremely positively associated with the transcriptome expression levels of 39 hub DEGs, extremely negatively associated with 8 hub DEGs, showed a positive association with 1 additional hub DEGs (‘*’, P = 0.05; ‘**’, P = 0.01) (Supplementary Table S2). These results indicate that the effects of pollen genotype on anthocyanin biosynthesis are mediated by multiple genes rather than a single gene. Further analysis revealed that the anthocyanin content was significantly correlated with the IAA, CTK, ABA, and ACC contents in PR fruit following self- and cross-pollination (Supplementary Table S2). Specifically, the anthocyanin content was positively correlated with CTK, ABA (R = 0.948 and 0.961, respectively, P = 0.01), and ACC (R = 0.727, P = 0.05) but negatively correlated with IAA (R = −0.933, P = 0.01). The correlation with the GA3 content was not significant. Regarding hormone ratios, the anthocyanin content was positively correlated with CTK/IAA, CTK/ACC, ABA/IAA, and ABA/GA3 ratios (R = 0.954, 0.910, 0.964, and 0.911, respectively, P = 0.01) and showed a moderate positive correlation with GA3/IAA (R = 0.669, P = 0.05). Negative correlations were observed with IAA/ACC, GA3/ACC, and ABA/CTK ratios (R = −0.888, −0.835, and − 0.919, respectively, P = 0.01).

Discussion

Effects of pollen genotypes on anthocyanin accumulation

Blueberry is a perennial evergreen or deciduous shrub fruit tree belonging to the Vaccinium genus of Ericaceae (Yang et al., 2019). Currently, the commercial cultivation types of blueberry mainly include tetraploid northern and southern highbush blueberry, diploid and tetraploid dwarf blueberry, and hexaploid rabbiteye blueberry (Rowland et al., 2012). Most of these varieties are self-incompatible, and it is necessary to combine multiple varieties in cultivation to ensure fruit set and thus yield (Yang et al., 2018; Yang, Fu, et al., 2020). Many studies have also shown that pollen from different varieties affects the fruit setting rate in blueberry, fruit size, seed number, fruit development period (Ehlenfeldt & Kramer, 2012; Iwasaki et al., 2006; Miller et al., 2011), and fruit appearance qualities, such as calyx, pedicle, sepal, fruit shape, and stem separation (Yang et al., 2017). The soluble solids content, total acid content, flavonoid content, total phenol content, fructose content, sucrose content, glucose content, anthocyanin content, and other intrinsic qualities (Yang, Li, Tian, Lv, Tang, & Zhang, 2015; Yang, Li, Tian, Lv, Tang, Zhang, Yue, & Wang, 2015) had significant effects on them, showing typical phenotypic characteristics of xenia. Consistent with the aforementioned research results, this study showed that the anthocyanin content of the fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO was highest at maturity and significantly higher than that cross-pollinated with BR. The anthocyanin content of the self-pollinated fruit was lowest and significantly lower than that of fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO and ‘Brightwell’ (P = 0.05).

Anthocyanin is a water-soluble flavonoid pigment commonly distributed in plant petals, fruit, stems, leaves, and other organs (Wu et al., 2023), and its biosynthesis is often specific to tissues and organs and closely related to organ development. Hormones are important factors affecting anthocyanin synthesis (Zhao, 2016). This study confirmed that pollen of different genotypes had significant effects on the anthocyanin content in blueberry fruit. At 40 DAP, fruit pollinated with pollen of different genotypes began to show differences in anthocyanin content, and the anthocyanin content increased significantly from 70 to 90 DAP, with the differences significantly increasing between blueberry fruits pollinated with pollen of different genotypes (P = 0.05). These results confirm that 70 to 90 DAP is the key period in which the synthesis of anthocyanins, such as ANS, ANR, and LAR, is affected by the pollen genotype (Yang, Fu, et al., 2020).

Effects of IAA on anthocyanin accumulation

This study found that during the critical period of anthocyanin synthesis, namely 70 to 90 DAP, the IAA content in fruit of self-pollinated PR was significantly higher than that in fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO and BR. Previous transcriptome analysis has shown that after pollination with pollen of different genotypes, 54 DEGs play a role in regulating AUX1, TIR1, AUX/IAA, ARF, GH3, and SAUR in the IAA signal transduction pathway and may co-regulate IAA signal transduction at 80 DAP (Yang, Xu, & Gao, 2024), indicating that the differences in the IAA content in fruit induced by pollination with pollen of different genotypes may regulate anthocyanin synthesis through the Aux/IAA-ARF signaling pathway (Wang et al., 2020). In fruit of self-pollinated PR, high concentrations of IAA downregulated the expression of structural genes, such as PAL, C4H, and CHS, and upregulated the expression of structural genes C12RT1 and ANR, which are related to anthocyanin biosynthesis (Table 3, Fig. 8). These differential expression of these structural genes was not conducive to anthocyanin accumulation and may inhibit the expression of transcription factors MdMYB10 and MdbHLH3, thus inhibiting anthocyanin synthesis (Li & Ahammed, 2023; Shi et al., 2023). Conversely, the lower IAA content in the fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO and BR promoted anthocyanin synthesis. Correlation analysis in this study confirmed the negative regulatory relationship between anthocyanin synthesis and the IAA content.

Effects of CTK on anthocyanin accumulation

From 70 to 90 DAP, the CTK content in fruit of self-pollinated PR was significantly lower than that in fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO and BR. This may be because after pollination with pollen of different genotypes, there are differences in the expression of CTK signal transduction-related 21 genes of CRE1, B-ARR and A-ARR in the fruits 70 to 90 DAP (Yang, Xu, & Gao, 2024). In fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO and BR, high concentrations of CTK upregulated the expression of structural genes, such as PAL, C4H, and CHS, and downregulated the expression of structural genes C12RT1 and ANR, which are related to anthocyanin biosynthesis (Table 3, Fig. 8). The differential expression of these structural genes was conducive to anthocyanin accumulation. Moreover, high CTK concentrations may activate the MBW complex through B-type ARR and inhibit negative regulatory factor MYBL2, thus activating downstream structural gene expression and promoting sucrose-induced anthocyanin synthesis (Das, Shin, Choi, Yoo, et al., 2012; Li et al., 2017). In single mutants arr1, arr10, and arr12, double mutants arr1/10, arr1/12, and arr10/12, and triple mutant arr1/10/12, sucrose-induced accumulation of anthocyanin was decreased, indicating that CTK transactivates positive regulatory factors Production of anthocyanin Pigment1 (PAP1, MYB activator of anthocyanin), GLABRA3 (GL3), and TT8 through a photoelectron signaling pathway and inhibits the transcript level of negative regulatory factor MYBL2, thus participating in sucrose-induced anthocyanin accumulation (Das, Shin, Choi, & Park, 2012; Das, Shin, Choi, Yoo, et al., 2012). High CTK concentrations upregulate the expression of anthocyanin structural genes MdDFR and MdUFGT and transcription factor genes MdMYB10 and MdbHLH3, which may affect the expression of genes related to anthocyanin biosynthesis (Wang et al., 2019). CTK can increase MYBD expression, enhancing anthocyanin biosynthesis by inhibiting MYBL2, which encodes a transcription factor that negatively affects this process (Nguyen et al., 2015). Moreover, CTK can induce OsRR6 overexpression, enhancing anthocyanin accumulation (Bhaskar et al., 2021). Correlation analysis confirmed the positive regulatory association between anthocyanin synthesis and the CTK content.

Effects of ABA on anthocyanin accumulation

Moreover, this study found that from 70 to 90 DAP, the ABA content in fruit of self-pollinated PR was significantly lower than that in fruit of ‘Premier cross-pollinated with PO and BR, which may be attributed to the fact that after pollination with pollen of different genotypes, there are differences in the expression of ABA signal transduction-related 8 genes of pyrabactin resistant/pyrlike (PYR/PYL), PP2C, and SnRK2 in fruit at 70–90 DAP (Yang, Xu, & Gao, 2024); specifically, PYR/PYL improves the sensitivity of ABA signal transduction (Zhang et al., 2013). In fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO and BR, high ABA concentrations upregulated the expression of structural genes, such as PAL, C4H, CHS, and CCoAOMT, and downregulated the expression of structural genes C12RT1 and ANR, which are related to anthocyanin biosynthesis (Table 3, Fig. 8), promoting anthocyanin accumulation (Qu et al., 2021). In this process, the Basic region/leucine zipper (bZIP) plays a key and diverse role (Fujita et al., 2011), and ABA-induced NAC1 can bind to the BZip-type transcription factor bZIP23 to activate the transcription of MYB10 and UFGT, thus increasing anthocyanin accumulation (Liu, Mei, et al., 2023). In addition, PYR/PYL/RCAR (regulatory component of ABA receptor) is important for improving ABA sensitivity (Zhang et al., 2013). VlPYL1 plays an important role in fruit development, and upregulation of VlPYL1 promotes anthocyanin accumulation (Gao et al., 2018; Li & Ahammed, 2023). Correlation analysis confirmed the positive regulatory relationship between anthocyanin synthesis and the ABA content.

Effects of ETH on anthocyanin accumulation

Furthermore, in this study, the results showed that from 70 to 90 DAP, the ACC content in fruit of self-pollinated PR was significantly lower than that in fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO and BR, which may be attributed to the fact that after pollination with pollen of different genotypes, there are differences in the expression of ETH signal transduction-related 17 genes of ETR, CTR1, SIMKK, and EBF1/2 in the fruits 70 to 90 DAP (Yang, Xu, & Gao, 2024). In fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO and BR, high concentrations of ACC upregulated the expression of structural genes, such as C4H, CHS, and F3H, and downregulated the expression of structural genes C12RT1 and ANR, which are related to anthocyanin biosynthesis (Fig. 8). The differential expression of these structural genes was conducive to anthocyanin accumulation (Cheng et al., 2016). In this process, ETR promotes anthocyanin accumulation by regulating MdMYB9 and MdMYB11 (Zhang et al., 2018). In addition, the promotion of anthocyanin accumulation by the high ETH content may also be related to the activity of anthocyanin biosynthesis-related enzymes, such as PAL, CHS, ANS, ANR, and LAR (El-Kereamy et al., 2003; Villarreal et al., 2010; Yang, Deng, et al., 2024). Correlation analysis confirmed the positive regulatory relationship between anthocyanin synthesis and the ACC content.

Effects of hormone ratios and crosstalk on anthocyanin accumulation

The regulation of various endogenous hormones prevalent in plant tissues has an appropriate concentration range, but each hormone does not exist in isolation. Plant growth and development are not only affected by the hormone content but also by the mutual cooperation or antagonism of hormones (Yang et al., 2021). This study found that the IAA/ACC, GA3/ACC, and ABA/CTK ratios in fruit of self-pollinated PR were significantly higher than those in fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO and BR at 90 DAP when the fruit matured. There was an extremely significant negative correlation between the anthocyanin content and the IAA/ACC, GA3/ACC and ABA/CTK ratios. The CTK/IAA, CTK/ACC, GA3/IAA, ABA/IAA and ABA/GA3 ratios in fruit of self-pollinated PR were significantly lower than those in fruit after cross-pollination with PO and BR, and the anthocyanin content was positively correlated with the CTK/IAA, CTK/ACC, ABA/IAA, and ABA/GA3 ratios and positively correlated with the GA3/IAA ratio. In fruit of PR cross-pollinated with PO and BR, the high CTK/IAA, CTK/ACC, GA3/IAA, ABA/IAA, and ABA/GA3 ratios and the low IAA/ACC, GA3/ACC, and ABA/CTK ratios upregulated the expression of structural genes, such as PAL, C4H, and CHS and downregulated the expression of structural genes C12RT1 and ANR, which are related to anthocyanin biosynthesis (Fig. 8). The differential expression of these structural genes was conducive to anthocyanin accumulation. These results indicate that IAA may crosstalk with CTK, GA3, ABA, and ETH signals to regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis.

Future directions

Comprehensive analysis showed that xenia in PR rabbiteye blueberry was closely related to the contents of four endogenous hormones, namely IAA, CTK, ABA, and ACC, in pollinated fruit. The low IAA content, high CTK, ABA, and ACC contents, the relatively high CTK/IAA, CTK/ACC, GA3/IAA, ABA/IAA, and ABA/GA3 ratios and the relatively low IAA/ACC, GA3/ACC, and ABA/CTK ratios promoted anthocyanin synthesis and accumulation. These findings provide a theoretical basis for clarifying the relationship between anthocyanin synthesis and endogenous hormone content changes in rabbiteye blueberry fruit after pollination with pollen of different genotypes, thereby elucidating the physiological mechanism of xenia in this species. However, the relationship between the IAA, GA3, CTK, ABA, and ACC contents and anthocyanin synthesis is complex, and further studies should delve into the intricate mechanisms by which IAA interacts with CTK, ABA, and ETH signals to control anthocyanin homeostasis in blueberry fruit.

To strengthen the conclusion and provide more actionable insights, future research directions should be refined with specific suggestions. First, functional studies on miRNAs are warranted to explore their potential roles in mediating hormonal crosstalk during fruit development. Given that differences in hormone levels between self- and cross-pollinated fruit may stem from miRNA-mediated regulation of pollen vitality, identifying key miRNAs that influence IAA, GA3, CTK, ABA, or ETH signaling pathways could unveil novel regulatory nodes in xenia. For instance, high-throughput sequencing combined with miRNA target prediction and validation could pinpoint miRNAs that modulate hormone biosynthesis or perception genes, clarifying how pollen-derived miRNAs contribute to fruit quality traits. Second, hormone crosstalk studies should prioritize dissecting the molecular interactions between IAA and other hormones. Specifically, experiments could involve the exogenous application of hormone inhibitors or mimics to observe their effects on anthocyanin accumulation, followed by transcriptomic or proteomic analysis to identify downstream targets. Additionally, yeast two-hybrid assays or co-immunoprecipitation techniques could be employed to investigate physical interactions between hormone receptors or signaling components, providing mechanistic insights into how IAA-CTK-ABA-ETH networks orchestrate anthocyanin synthesis. Third, breeding strategies should integrate these findings to enhance xenia-related traits in rabbiteye blueberry. Marker-assisted selection could be developed based on identified hormone ratio thresholds or miRNA markers linked to favorable pollen vigor.

Furthermore, cross-pollination experiments with diverse pollen donors should be expanded to validate the consistency of the effects of xenia across genetic backgrounds, enabling the design of optimal pollination protocols for commercial orchards. Finally, future research must focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying the hormonal regulation of the anthocyanin pathway, particularly the role of specific transcription factors, such as MYB10 and bHLH3. Functional validation of these factors through gene knockout or overexpression in blueberry fruit could reveal their direct involvement in mediating hormone-responsive structural gene expression. Combining these approaches with epigenetic analysis (e.g., DNA methylation profiling) may uncover how environmental or genetic factors modulate hormone signaling during fruit development. By addressing these research gaps, the field can transition from correlative findings to a mechanistic understanding, advancing both theoretical knowledge and practical applications in rabbiteye blueberry cultivation.

Conclusion

This study employed an integrated hormonic and transcriptomic approach to elucidate the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying anthocyanin accumulation in blueberry fruit pollinated by three different pollen genotypes. The CTK, ABA, and ACC contents were significantly higher and the IAA content was significantly lower during the critical period of anthocyanin accumulation in PR fruit cross-pollinated with BR and PO. WGCNA and correlation analysis suggested that the differences in hormone contents upregulated key structural genes, such as PAL, CHS, F3H, and DFR and downregulated others, including LAR (FC > 2.00 and P < 0.01) in the fruit of cross-pollinated PR, thereby promoting normal anthocyanin accumulation at the mature stage and resulting in a significantly higher anthocyanin content in PR fruit cross-pollinated with BR and PO (P = 0.05). These findings provide new insights into the mechanism by which xenia affects fruit quality and indicate that the selection of pollen genotypes for pollination is a practical strategy for improving the nutritional quality of fruit.
