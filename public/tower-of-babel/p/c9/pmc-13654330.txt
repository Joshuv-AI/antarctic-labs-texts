Optical Coherence Tomography Findings Following Intravascular Lithotripsy in Calcified Coronary Artery Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Giacomo Maria Cioffi, Stéphane Cook, Mario Togni, Julius Jonas Jelisejevas, Pascal Meier, Peter Wenaweser, Dorian Garin, Mehdi Madanchi, Natalia Pinilla-Echeverri, Karim M. Al-Azizi, Grigorios Tsigkas, Nicolas Amabile, Ioannis Skalidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntravascular lithotripsy (IVL) has emerged as a novel technology for modifying calcified coronary lesions, whereas optical coherence tomography (OCT) enables high-resolution assessment of calcium modification and procedural outcomes. However, the mechanistic and procedural effects of IVL assessed by OCT across studies remain incompletely characterized.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261332946). PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, and Epistemonikos were searched from inception through February 2026. Randomized controlled trials and observational studies with extractable quantitative OCT outcomes were included. The primary endpoint was OCT-detected calcium fracture. Secondary endpoints included multiple calcium fractures, minimum stent area (MSA), stent expansion, acute lumen gain, procedural success, and in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). Random-effects meta-analyses were performed to estimate pooled proportions or mean values with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsThirty studies encompassing a total of 3030 patients were included. OCT-detected calcium fracture occurred in 68% (95% CI 56%-79%) of lesions, whereas multiple fractures were observed in 34% (95% CI 26%-41%). IVL-treated lesions achieved favorable procedural metrics, including a pooled final MSA of 6.45 (95% CI 6.07-6.82) mm2, stent expansion of 85.9% (95% CI 82.3%-89.5%), and acute lumen gain of 3.50 (95% CI 3.14-3.87) mm2. Procedural success was high (95%, 95% CI 93%-98%) with low in-hospital MACEs (2%, 95% CI 1%-3%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed across endpoints.ConclusionsCoronary IVL assessed by OCT is frequently associated with calcium fracture and favorable procedural outcomes with high procedural success and low early adverse events.

Severe coronary artery calcification is a major determinant of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) complexity and may lead to inadequate lesion preparation, impaired stent delivery, underexpansion, and higher procedural risk.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Traditional calcium modification techniques, including high-pressure balloon dilation, cutting/scoring balloons, rotational atherectomy (RA), and orbital atherectomy (OA), can facilitate stent delivery, but each has limitations (eg, wire bias in eccentric lesions, risk of no-reflow or vessel injury).1,7, 8, 9, 10, 11

Intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) is a relatively novel balloon-based technology that delivers pulsatile sonic pressure waves to fracture calcified plaque, both superficial and deep, at relatively low balloon pressures (4-6 atm). The energy creates circumferential microfractures in rigid calcium, increasing vessel compliance and enabling optimal stent expansion with subsequent dilations. The Disrupt Coronary Artery Disease With the Coronary Shockwave IVL System (Disrupt CAD) trials (I-IV) demonstrated that IVL is safe and effective for preparing severely calcified de novo coronary lesions for stenting. IVL’s user-friendly, guidewire-and-balloon platform also confers a short learning curve for operators.1,12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20

Intracoronary imaging, particularly optical coherence tomography (OCT), has emerged as a valuable adjunctive tool for the management of calcified lesions. OCT provides high-resolution---near-histologic---cross-sectional imaging, permitting precise plaque assessment and stent results, which are key predictors of stent underexpansion. OCT-based calcium scoring uses parameters such as calcium thickness > 0.5 mm, arc > 180°, and length > 5 mm to identify lesions at high risk for incomplete stent expansion.21 This helps guide the need for aggressive plaque modification. Moreover, OCT can directly visualize the effects of IVL---namely the calcium fractures created---and quantify luminal gain and stent expansion posttreatment.20 The combination of IVL with OCT guidance thus offers an attractive strategy for tackling complex calcified coronary disease.

Despite increasing adoption, the literature on IVL with OCT is fragmented across small observational studies, registries, and a limited number of randomized trials, often including heterogeneous lesion subsets (de novo, in-stent restenosis [ISR], chronic total occlusion [CTO], left main/bifurcations), variable OCT acquisition timing (pre-IVL, post-IVL, final), and inconsistent endpoint definitions. A comprehensive synthesis is needed to quantify OCT-defined plaque modification after IVL and contextualize downstream procedural and early clinical outcomes.

Accordingly, we performed a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of randomized and observational studies reporting coronary IVL with OCT assessment. The objective of the present study was descriptive and mechanistic rather than comparative, as the available OCT literature on IVL remains predominantly single arm and does not support robust quantitative comparison with alternative calcium-modification strategies.

Methods

Eligibility criteria and study selection

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines22 and registered in the PROSPERO database (No. CRD420261332946). No ethics board approval was required given that this was a meta-analysis of previously published data.

Systematic searches were conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, and Epistemonikos by one investigator and independently verified by a second investigator from inception up to February 16, 2026. Any disagreements or discrepancies were resolved by consensus or third-party adjudication. Titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened according to predefined eligibility criteria. The search algorithm combined terms related to intravascular lithotripsy, coronary disease, and optical coherence tomography across predefined population subsets. We included peer-reviewed English-language human studies---randomized controlled trials (RCTs), comparative observational studies, and single-arm observational studies with extractable quantitative outcome data---of adult patients undergoing coronary PCI for calcified coronary artery disease treated with IVL and assessed using OCT at any procedural stage (pre-IVL, post-IVL, poststent, and/or final). A comparator was not required for inclusion. Comparative studies and RCTs were included. Outcomes were synthesized primarily as pooled estimates across IVL-treated cohorts or comparative pooling.

The complete search strategy is provided in Supplemental Appendix S1. Additional sources included manual screening of references from included articles and relevant reviews. We excluded preclinical/animal studies, noncoronary interventions, case reports, reviews/editorials/letters without original data, and studies without extractable quantitative outcome data.

Definition of endpoints

The primary outcome was OCT-detected calcium fracture after IVL, defined as the presence of ≥ 1 calcium fracture and analyzed as a pooled proportion. Secondary outcomes included multiple calcium fractures, minimal stent area (MSA), stent expansion, final acute lumen gain, procedural success, and in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs).

Procedural success and MACEs were extracted as defined in each included study. Where explicitly stated, procedural success typically reflected successful IVL delivery, successful deployment of the stent, and completion of the intended PCI strategy with residual stenosis below a study-defined threshold (usually < 50% residual diameter stenosis). An in-hospital MACE was defined as a composite of death, myocardial infarction (MI, including periprocedural MI per study criteria), and target vessel/lesion revascularization; some studies also included stent thrombosis or stroke or target vessel MI (creatine kinase-muscle/brain > 3× the upper limit of normal).

Statistical analysis

Random-effects meta-analyses were performed. For dichotomous endpoints (calcium fracture, multiple calcium fractures, procedural success, and in-hospital MACEs), pooled proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. For continuous endpoints (MSA, stent expansion, and acute lumen gain), pooled mean estimates with 95% CIs were calculated using inverse-variance random-effects models. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, and between-study variance was estimated using τ2. Prediction intervals were calculated to reflect the expected variability of effects in future clinical settings All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the meta and metafor packages and Review Manager version 5.4 (RevMan, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark).

Quality and risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by 2 investigators using tools appropriate to study design. RCTs were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, which assesses potential bias across 5 domains: bias arising from the randomization process; bias due to deviations from intended interventions; bias due to missing outcome data; bias in outcome measurement; and bias in selection of the reported result.

Observational studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies. The NOS assesses methodologic quality across 3 domains: selection of the study population (maximum 4 points); comparability of cohorts (maximum 2 points); and outcome assessment (maximum 3 points), yielding a maximum score of 9 points. Studies scoring 7-9 points were considered high methodologic quality, 5-6 points moderate quality, and ≤ 4 points low quality. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by consensus.

Publication bias

Publication bias was explored only for outcomes including at least 10 studies. Funnel plots were visually inspected for asymmetry, and the Egger regression test was performed as an exploratory assessment of small-study effects. Given the predominance of observational studies, the use of mostly single-arm pooled estimates, and the presence of clinical and methodologic heterogeneity, these analyses were interpreted cautiously and were not considered definitive evidence of publication bias.

Detailed publication bias assessments and funnel plots are presented in Supplemental Appendix S2.

The dominant technology across included studies was the Shockwave coronary IVL (Shockwave C2 and C2+), used in 26 of the 30 studies. Four studies reported use of other coronary IVL systems (including MicroPort/TomaHawk and other non-Shockwave platforms) (Table 1). This distribution indicates that pooled results predominantly reflect contemporary Shockwave IVL practice while also incorporating a small number of alternative IVL systems.

Use of adjunctive calcium modification or optimization devices (eg, atherectomy, cutting or scoring balloons, ultra–high-pressure balloons) was variably permitted and inconsistently reported across studies. Several pivotal IVL cohorts excluded planned atherectomy or specialty balloons by protocol, limiting the ability to derive a pooled proportion of combined strategies.

Clinical presentation was variably reported; when specified, most studies included mixed acute coronary syndrome/chronic coronary syndrome (ACS/CCS) cohorts (17 of 30), whereas 9 of 30 focused on CCS-only populations (Table 1).

Lesion subsets were primarily de novo calcified lesions (23 of 30 studies). A smaller number explicitly included mixed lesion subsets (de novo, ISR, and CTO) or CTO-focused populations (Table 1). Two studies specifically focused on stent underexpansion as the IVL indication rather than de novo lesion preparation.

OCT was used to quantify both mechanistic endpoints (calcium fracture) and procedural endpoints (MSA, stent expansion, or lumen gain). Timing of OCT acquisition relative to IVL and stent implantation varied across studies (post-IVL assessment, final poststent assessment, or both), which likely contributed to the consistently high between-study heterogeneity observed across pooled outcomes.

Outcome reporting completeness

Reporting frequency varied by endpoint (Table 1), with the most consistently reported outcomes being MSA and dissection/perforation outcomes, whereas mechanistic endpoints, such as presence of calcium fracture and acute lumen gain, were less consistently reported. In the quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis), the number of contributing studies varied by outcome, consistent with the forest plots: calcium fractures reported in 20 studies; multiple calcium fractures in 14 studies; MSA in 28 studies; stent expansion in 23 studies; final acute lumen gain in 15 studies; procedural success in 19 studies; and periprocedural or in-hospital MACEs in 21 studies.

This uneven reporting is important for interpretation: pooled estimates represent the subset of studies reporting each endpoint, and comparisons across endpoints should acknowledge different contributing study sets.

Risk of bias

Among the included studies, 6 randomized controlled trials were assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. Overall, these trials were judged to have some concerns regarding risk of bias, mainly related to the open-label nature of interventional studies and potential variability in imaging outcome assessment. The remaining observational studies were evaluated using the NOS. Most studies were rated as moderate to high methodologic quality, although several smaller single-center cohorts had a higher risk of bias due to limited sample size, potential selection bias, and absence of adjusted comparative analyses. Detailed risk-of-bias assessments are presented in Supplemental Tables S1 and S2.

Discussion

In this systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis integrating randomized and observational evidence, IVL evaluated by OCT demonstrated 3 consistent findings. First, IVL frequently produced OCT-detected calcium fracture, occurring in approximately two thirds of treated lesions (0.68, 95% CI 0.56-0.79). Second, IVL-treated lesions achieved favorable OCT-derived procedural metrics, including a pooled final MSA of 6.45 (95% CI 6.07-6.82) mm2, stent expansion of 85.87% (95% CI 82.25%-89.50%), and final acute lumen gain of 3.50 (95% CI 3.14-3.87) mm2. Third, procedural feasibility was high and early safety appeared reassuring, with procedural success of 0.95 (95% CI 0.93-0.98) and in-hospital MACE of 0.02 (95% CI 0.01-0.03). Importantly, all outcomes were characterized by substantial heterogeneity (I2 ∼ 79%-95%) and broad prediction intervals, indicating considerable variability across clinical contexts and procedural strategies.

Given the predominance of single-arm observational evidence and heterogeneous comparators in a minority of studies, this synthesis should be interpreted primarily as a descriptive summary of OCT-defined plaque modification and procedural endpoints after IVL rather than as a comparative effectiveness analysis.

OCT-defined mechanism: calcium fracture as signature effect of IVL

OCT provides a mechanistic perspective that closely aligns with the biologic action of IVL, which modifies calcified plaque through acoustic pressure waves. The pooled calcium fracture rate of 68% indicates that IVL frequently produces OCT-visible disruption of coronary calcium. However, the wide prediction interval (0.13-1.00) highlights substantial variability across studies. This variability likely reflects differences in baseline calcium morphology---including arc, thickness, and depth---as well as procedural factors such as balloon sizing, pulse delivery, and adjunctive lesion preparation.

Importantly, absence of OCT-visible fracture does not necessarily imply absence of calcium modification. OCT has intrinsic limitations related to attenuation and spatial resolution, and microdisruptions may occur below the imaging detection threshold. In clinical practice, operators often perform additional lesion optimization after IVL using high-pressure noncompliant balloons, which likely contributes to the favorable final stent geometry observed in many series. Nevertheless, when present, calcium fracture remains the most direct OCT marker of IVL-induced plaque modification and represents a promising mechanistic endpoint for future standardized imaging studies.

Although OCT-defined calcium fracture is biologically plausible as a marker of improved lesion compliance and may facilitate subsequent stent expansion, the currently available evidence linking this imaging finding to procedural and especially clinical outcomes remains limited and largely observational. Accordingly, calcium fracture should be interpreted primarily as a mechanistic imaging endpoint rather than as a validated surrogate for clinical benefit.

Linking mechanism to efficacy: procedural metrics and stent optimization

Final stent geometry is a recognized determinant of longer term stent performance, and OCT-derived endpoints such as MSA and stent expansion are high-fidelity measures of procedural optimization. The pooled mean MSA of 6.45 mm2 and stent expansion of ∼ 86% suggest that IVL is frequently integrated into strategies that achieve favorable final stent results in calcified disease. However, the pronounced heterogeneity observed for both metrics likely reflects: 1) differences in vessel size and lesion location; and 2) lesion subsets (de novo calcification vs ISR/stent underexpansion and CTO). These factors limit the interpretation of any single pooled value as a universally expected target and reinforce the need for harmonized reporting standards.

Feasibility and early outcomes: high procedural success and low in-hospital MACE rates

Procedural success was high across studies (95%), supporting the feasibility of IVL in contemporary PCI workflows for calcified coronary lesions. Nevertheless, heterogeneity remained substantial (I2 = 79.8%), with a prediction interval extending to 0.81, indicating that success rates may vary across clinical scenarios depending on lesion complexity and adjunctive strategies.

Similarly, the proportion of pooled in-hospital MACEs was low at 2%, which is reassuring given the complex calcified anatomy represented in many included studies. However, the broad prediction interval (up to 11%) and heterogeneity across cohorts suggest that early event rates may vary according to clinical acuity, endpoint definitions, and surveillance strategies. Sparse-event behavior and variability in MACE definitions further limit the interpretation of a single pooled estimate as a uniform clinical risk.

Heterogeneity as a key message and a roadmap

A key observation of this analysis is that IVL outcomes assessed by OCT represent a distribution across clinical scenarios rather than a single uniform treatment effect. High heterogeneity is expected given the inclusion of both randomized and observational studies, variation in lesion substrates, differences in OCT acquisition timing (post-IVL vs final imaging), and heterogeneity in procedural strategies. These findings emphasize the need for future studies incorporating standardized imaging protocols, harmonized endpoint definitions, and careful stratification of lesion phenotypes. The substantial between-study heterogeneity across primary and secondary endpoints likely reflects important differences in lesion subsets, OCT acquisition timing, adjunctive plaque-modification strategies, and endpoint definitions, and therefore the pooled estimates should be interpreted primarily as descriptive summaries of the available literature rather than precise universal treatment effects.

Comparison with other calcium modification techniques

Compared with traditional atherectomy techniques, IVL has several distinct practical and mechanistic features that may be relevant in selected clinical scenarios. Rotational and orbital atherectomy physically ablate calcified plaque but require specialized equipment and may be associated with distal embolization or slow-flow phenomena. IVL, in contrast, uses a balloon-based platform compatible with conventional guidewires and allows preservation of side-branch protection wires, which can simplify bifurcation interventions. Furthermore, IVL delivers circumferential acoustic energy that is less dependent on guidewire position, potentially reducing the wire-bias effect that may limit atherectomy performance in eccentric calcification. OCT studies have shown that IVL can induce fractures even in deep and circumferential calcium, including lesion morphologies that may be challenging to modify with atherectomy alone. IVL may also have a potential role in stent underexpansion, where acoustic energy can penetrate through stent struts and disrupt the underlying calcified plaque. However, these considerations should be interpreted as mechanistic and procedural observations rather than as evidence of comparative clinical superiority over other calcium-modification strategies.

Clinical and research implications

Clinically, these findings support IVL as a strategy that frequently achieves OCT-visible calcium modification and is associated with favorable final stent geometry and high procedural success in calcified coronary interventions. The incremental contribution of the present study lies in the dedicated synthesis of OCT-defined mechanistic and procedural findings after coronary IVL, an area for which the existing literature remains fragmented and largely study level. From a research perspective, future studies should aim to standardize OCT definitions of calcium fracture characteristics and to link mechanistic imaging findings with patient-centered clinical outcomes. Prospective studies evaluating lesion phenotype–based strategies and fracture-guided procedural optimization may help translate these mechanistic insights into practical treatment algorithms.

Limitations

This meta-analysis should be interpreted in the context of several important limitations. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across all endpoints. Between-study heterogeneity was consistently high for mechanistic, procedural, and clinical outcomes (eg, calcium fracture I2 = 95.0%, MSA I2 = 93.2%, stent expansion I2 = 94.7%, acute lumen gain I2 = 90.1%, procedural success I2 = 79.8%, and in-hospital MACEs I2 = 78.0%). Corresponding prediction intervals were broad (eg, calcium fracture 0.13-1.00), indicating that results may vary meaningfully across clinical contexts depending on lesion phenotype, procedural strategy, imaging timing, and endpoint definitions. Therefore, pooled estimates should be interpreted as average effects across heterogeneous settings rather than as universally expected outcomes. Second, the evidence base included both randomized trials and observational studies. Observational studies are inherently susceptible to selection bias and confounding by indication, as IVL may be preferentially used in particularly complex calcified lesions or as bailout therapy. Although inclusion of observational evidence reflects real-world practice and increases the available data, it limits causal inference, particularly for clinical outcomes and comparisons with alternative lesion preparation strategies. Third, definitions of procedural success varied across studies, including device success, angiographic success, and composite definitions incorporating complications. Such variability introduces measurement heterogeneity and contributes to between-study variance. Fourth, differences in the unit of analysis may influence interpretation. OCT-derived endpoints are frequently reported at the lesion level, whereas clinical outcomes are typically reported at the patient level. Mixing lesion-level and patient-level denominators across studies may introduce additional variability and limits the interpretability of pooled estimates. Fifth, potential small-study effects and selective outcome reporting cannot be excluded. Many included cohorts were modest in size, and outcomes such as calcium fracture detection, stent expansion, and clinical event definitions may be selectively reported. Sixth, publication bias could not be robustly assessed. Although funnel plots and Egger regression were explored for outcomes in a sufficient number of studies, the interpretability of these methods is limited in meta-analyses predominantly based on observational, single-arm studies, particularly in the presence of substantial clinical and methodologic heterogeneity. In addition, sparse-event outcomes may further reduce the reliability of formal asymmetry testing. Seventh, meta-regression was considered but was not performed because of the marked clinical and methodologic heterogeneity across studies, inconsistent reporting of potentially relevant study-level covariates, and the predominance of small observational data sets. Under these conditions, metaregression would have been unlikely to provide robust or clinically meaningful inferences. Eighth, because most included studies were observational, the pooled results are susceptible to selection bias and confounding by indication. Specifically, IVL was often used at operator discretion in highly selected complex calcified lesions, sometimes in combination with other lesion-preparation devices, thereby limiting causal interpretation of the observed OCT and procedural findings.

Conclusions

In this systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis integrating randomized and observational evidence, coronary intravascular lithotripsy evaluated by OCT was frequently associated with calcium fracture and favorable procedural metrics, including adequate stent expansion and lumen gain, alongside high procedural success and low in-hospital adverse events. However, substantial between-study heterogeneity and broad prediction intervals highlight meaningful variability related to lesion phenotype, IVL technique, imaging timing, and endpoint definitions. These findings support the mechanistic plausibility and procedural effectiveness of IVL in calcified coronary disease while underscoring the need for standardized OCT endpoint definitions and adequately powered comparative studies linking imaging-defined plaque modification to clinical outcomes.
