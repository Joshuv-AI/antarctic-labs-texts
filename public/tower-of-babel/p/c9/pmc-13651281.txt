Multi-parent mapping reveals epistatic architecture and elite haplotypes for multi-trait improvement in cotton
Authors: Peng Han, Chunyuan You, Xinyuan Chen, Yuanlong Wu, Ruipeng Wang, Lu Lu, Jianying Li, Zhongping Xu, Wenyu Gong, Xianlong Zhang, Maojun Wang, Shuangxia Jin, Xinhui Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCotton domestication has fixed favorable alleles, often masking cryptic loci and reinforcing genetic trade-offs, particularly between yield and fiber quality, thereby constraining simultaneous trait improvement.ResultsWe develop an eight-parent MAGIC population comprising 319 recombinant inbred lines through a 12-year breeding program and integrate it with a panel of 318 Xinjiang cotton cultivars released between 1978 and 2024. High-resolution genomic analyses combining SNP-based and identity-by-descent mapping identify 111 sQTLs and 15 hQTLs associated with 19 agronomic traits across multiple environments. Genome-wide epistasis analysis further identifies over 3,000 cryptic loci, highlighting complex genetic interactions underlying trait variation. Parallel population genomic analyses of Xinjiang cultivars reveal 520 genomic regions consistently shaped by modern breeding selection. Integrating these datasets enabled the construction of a multi-trait optimization framework to prioritize elite genotypes with favorable allele combinations. We validate this framework using cultivars derived from MAGIC parental lines, including Zhuangjiahan902 and Huaxin103, demonstrating coordinated improvement in yield and fiber quality.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the dual utility of MAGIC populations for high-resolution genetic dissection and breeding application. The integrative framework presented here provides a scalable strategy for resolving genetic trade-offs and achieving coordinated multi-trait improvement in cotton and other major crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-026-04202-y.Keywords: Cotton, MAGIC population, Identity-by-descent mapping, Yield-quality trade-offs, Genomic breeding

Background

Genetic diversity and natural selection are key drivers of crop adaptation and the formation of complex traits, which are typically controlled by quantitative trait loci (QTLs) [1], epistatic interactions [2, 3], and environmental factors [4, 5]. Genetic diversity provides the variation necessary for ecological adaptation, while natural selection shapes allele frequencies over generations, promoting the fixation of adaptive variants [6, 7]. Comparative genomics and whole–genome resequencing have shown that natural and artificial selection jointly remodel genomic architecture, influencing functional gene variation, linkage disequilibrium, and population differentiation [8–10]. Population genomic analyses, including differentiation indices, nucleotide diversity, selective sweeps, and environmental associations, enable the identification of candidate regions underlying adaptation and agronomic traits [11, 12]. Comparative genomics between upland and sea island cotton has highlighted selection enrichment in genes related to fiber development [13]. In maize, studies across ecotypes at different altitudes have demonstrated that the ZCN8 gene plays a pivotal role in cold stress adaptation and ecological differentiation [14]. Whole–genome analyses in soybean have different selection on the oil metabolism gene in northern and southern populations [15]. Together, these findings provide a robust framework for dissecting the molecular basis of complex traits and guiding targeted crop improvement.

Among major cash crops, upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.), holds a unique global strategic position as the primary natural fiber source for the textile industry. Xinjiang has become the most important cotton-producing region worldwide, contributing more than 20% of global production and serving as the core base for China’s cotton security and industrial supply chain stability [16]. However, cotton improvement in this region faces a long-standing and unresolved biological constraint: a pronounced trade-off between yield-related traits and fiber quality [17]. Selection for higher boll number, boll weight, and lint yield is often accompanied by compromised fiber length, strength, and fineness, reflecting antagonistic genetic architectures and complex epistatic regulation [18]. Dissecting the genetic basis of this trade-off is therefore essential for breaking current breeding bottlenecks and achieving simultaneous improvement of yield and quality in modern cotton breeding programs.

Although natural populations provide valuable insights into historical selection and adaptive evolution, the complex genetic background and structural biases within natural populations often limit the precise dissection of gene effects, prompting researchers to employ controlled or experimental populations to more accurately elucidate the genetic basis of complex traits [19]. Among these, multiparent advanced generation inter–cross (MAGIC) populations offer unique advantages for dissecting complex trait genetics [20]. Through recurrent intercrossing among multiple founders followed by successive selfing, MAGIC populations generate high levels of recombination, broad allelic coverage, and balanced population structure [21]. This not only increases the resolution of recombination blocks but also expands allelic diversity, making MAGIC populations ideal for detecting epistatic interactions and dissecting the genetic architecture of quantitative traits [22, 23]. MAGIC populations have been widely applied in crops such as wheat [24, 25], rice [26, 27], and cotton [28–31] for fine–scale QTL mapping, identification of interaction effects, and gene network analysis, revealing the genetic mechanisms underlying numerous complex traits. In cotton, a 550–line MAGIC population from 11 parental lines and, by combining SNP– based and IBD–haplotype–based GWAS approaches, systematically dissected the genetic basis of cotton fiber quality, identifying 25 sQTLs and 14 hQTLs [28]. Similarly, a 920–line MAGIC population from 16 representative upland cotton varieties and, through genome–wide association analysis, identified 54 loci associated with yield and fiber quality, nearly half of which exhibited pleiotropic effects, revealing genetic trade–offs among traits [29].

While natural and MAGIC populations each offer distinct advantages for dissecting complex traits, previous studies have often used them independently, lacking an integrated approach. Combining environment–driven selection signals from natural populations with epistatic effects detected in MAGIC populations provides a dual perspective on complex trait genetics, overcoming the limitations of traditional single–population studies. Within this integrated framework, superior SNPs identified from natural population analyses can be combined with MAGIC–derived loci and interaction effects to construct dynamic and precise models of complex trait inheritance, enabling comprehensive dissection of gene interaction networks, additive effects, and environment–responsive effects. This integrative approach not only enhances our understanding of the genetic basis of complex traits but also provides more accurate guidance for crop improvement, reducing breeding cycles and costs.

Here, we propose an integrative population genomics framework that combines natural population selection signatures with high-resolution genetic dissection from an eight-parent upland cotton MAGIC population (Fig. 1). By leveraging whole-genome resequencing data from a diverse panel of 318 Xinjiang-approved upland cotton accessions (since 1978) and a structured eight-parent MAGIC population, we systematically dissected the genetic architecture of complex traits. By integrating genetic information across these populations, we elucidated epistatic effects and their evolutionary mechanisms, and constructed a superior SNP model to provide a novel framework for trait dissection and favorable gene discovery. This study not only establishes a systematic strategy for molecular analysis of complex traits but also lays a solid theoretical and empirical foundation for cotton breeding and precision improvement.

Results

Genetic diversity mapping in a MAGIC population enabled by deep intercross recombination

Eight elite lines (Additional file 2: Table S1), designated HC01 through HC08, were chosen as founder parents due to their pronounced phenotypic diversity, substantial genetic divergence, and complementary agronomic traits, in addition to their proven breeding value and strong regional adaptation to Xinjiang. These lines display marked variation in key characteristics, notably fiber length (FL), fiber strength (FS), seed cotton weight (SCW), and lint cotton weight (LCW).

The selected founders were intercrossed in a half–diallel design to generate 319 MAGIC recombinant inbred lines (RILs). To elucidate the genomic structure of the MAGIC population, whole–genome resequencing was performed on founders and RILs, yielding 1,838,490 high–quality SNPs (Fig. 2a and Additional file 2: Table S2). The genome exhibited an average coverage of 0.80, with SNP densities comparable between the At (0.77) and Dt (0.85) subgenomes (Additional file 2: Table S2), and higher variant frequencies observed at chromosomal ends (Fig. 2a). Among all SNPs, approximately 88.9% (1,635,257) were located in intergenic regions and 2.48% (45,570) in coding regions; within coding regions, 29,097 were nonsynonymous, 15,545 were synonymous, 746 introduced premature stop codons, and 182 caused stop codon loss (Additional file 2: Table S3). Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis showed that LD decay in the MAGIC progeny occurred much more rapidly than in the founders, with r2 values halving within ~ 1500 Kb (higher than that observed in natural upland cotton populations [12, 32, 33], Fig. 2b). A neighbor–joining phylogenetic tree revealed that different RIL families clustered into several distinct groups, while the founders were distributed among these clusters (Fig. 2c). These results suggest that the RILs originated from diverse founder backgrounds and indicate that extensive recombination among founder genomes enhanced genetic diversity and improved mapping resolution.

Phenotypic diversity and transgressive performance in MAGIC RILs

In 2018–2019, nineteen traits associated with yield, fiber quality, plant architecture, and disease resistance were evaluated in the MAGIC population grown at Shihezi or Manas (northern Xinjiang) and Korla (southern Xinjiang) (Additional file 2: Table S4 and S5). Most traits showed approximately normal distributions with rich phenotypic variation (Fig. 2e, Additional file 2: Table S6, Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Although the RILs did not exhibit overall heterosis, some lines outperformed their parents, and about 10% showed longer fiber than seven of the eight founders except HC04. Broad–sense heritability exceeded 50% for about 65% of the traits, with high values observed for fiber elongation, maturity, seed–cotton weight, and the height of the first fruiting branch, whereas flowering time and whole growth duration showed low heritability (~ 20%), indicating strong environmental influence (Additional file 2: Table S6). Correlation analysis revealed significant associations among fiber quality traits; seed weight was strongly correlated with lint weight (r > 0.8, Fig. 2d) and showed higher correlations with fiber–quality traits than lint weight. Plant height and the height of the first fruiting branch were also highly correlated (r = 0.71, Fig. 2d). These results highlight that balancing multiple trait relationships is essential for comprehensive cotton improvement and precision breeding.

IBD–based mosaic architecture of MAGIC RILs

To identify identical–by–descent (IBD) segments, we inferred parental origins of MAGIC RILs using a hidden Markov model (HMM) based on high–quality SNPs, resulting in 107,553 IBD segments. After merging adjacent segments from the same parent, a total of 97,679 IBD segments were obtained, which covered approximately 99.4% of the genome (Fig. 3a, Additional file 2: Table S7). The length of the IBD segments per line varied considerably, ranging from 0.4 Kb to 126 Mb, with an average of 7.41 Mb. Among them, 4.0% of the segments were shorter than 50 Kb, 40.3% ranged from 50 Kb to 1 Mb, and more than half were longer than 1 Mb (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). The average length of IBD segments in the At subgenome was 157 Kb, compared with 104 Kb in the Dt subgenome (Additional file 1: Fig. S3a), consistent with the generally longer segments in At [28, 34]. The average number of IBD segments in the At and Dt subgenomes was 9,208 and 8,056, respectively, indicating a slightly higher recombination frequency in At. However, no significant difference was observed in the overall distribution of IBD segments between the At and Dt subgenomes (Additional file 1: Fig. S3b). The distribution of IBD bins across chromosomes exhibited marked heterogeneity; while chromosomal arms harbored the vast majority of IBD segments, only a minimal fraction was identified in centromeric and pericentromeric regions (Fig. 3a, Additional file 1: Fig. S3c). The genomic distribution of IBD segment lengths was highly heterogeneous, with expansive segments clustered in centromeric regions due to suppressed recombination, contrasting with the shorter, more fragmented tracts on chromosomal arms where recombination is most active (Additional file 1: Fig. S3d). The eight founders contributed unequally to the genomic composition of the MAGIC RILs (Additional file 1: Fig. S3e).

Dual GWAS mapping of multiple cotton traits

To dissect the genetic basis of plant architecture, yield, Verticillium wilt resistance, and fiber–quality traits, we applied two model–based genome–wide association approaches: SNP–based GWAS (sGWAS) and IBD–based GWAS (hGWAS). The sGWAS analysis identified a total of 111 sQTLs, of which 103 were unreported in previous studies [32, 35, 36], indicating the enhanced resolution achieved with this approach (Additional file 2: Table S8). Functional annotation showed that sQTL-associated genes were mainly enriched in auxin/hormone-responsive pathways, signal transduction, and metabolic activities, indicating their potential roles in regulating cotton growth, plant architecture, and fiber development (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). In parallel, we reconstructed the founder haplotypes in the MAGIC RIL population and conducted a multi–trait hGWAS to capture haplotype–level associations. The 95th percentile of the likelihood ratio test (LRT) distribution from 500 permutation tests was adopted as the significance threshold for detecting hQTLs. Across the 19 traits evaluated, 15 significant hQTLs were identified [32, 37, 38], all representing novel genetic loci (Additional file 2: Table S9). No significant hQTLs were detected for FU, SFC, MV, MAT, SCW, SCWPB, LP, FP, WGD, or FBA, suggesting that these traits are likely influenced by polygenic effects or minor loci below the detection threshold (Additional file 2: Table S9). Interestingly, both sGWAS and hGWAS detected a major–effect QTL on chromosome A10 associated with FL and spinning consistency index (SCI) (Fig. 3c, d). Examination of the peak bin region (bin7093, LRT = 19.18) revealed three IBD–defined parental groups (n > 3) (Fig. 3e, f). Significant phenotypic differences were observed among these parental IBD groups for both FL and SCI (ANOVA, P(FL) = 0.0025, Fig. 3e; P(SCI) = 0.007, Fig. 3f), highlighting the genetic differentiation of founder haplotypes underlying this major locus.

Epistatic discovery of cryptic QTL underlying trait variation

Epistasis, defined as the interaction between alleles at different loci, plays an important role in the genetic architecture of complex traits. Following the identification of QTLs through GWAS, we conducted a genome-wide epistasis analysis using a filtered set of 108,566 high-quality SNPs (MAF < 0.05 and r2 < 0.5), and detected 3,000 significant (P_adjusted < 0.05) epistatic interactions across 18 traits. To further evaluate robustness, we compared BLUP-derived results with single-environment analyses. Approximately 17.9% of the epistatic interactions identified using BLUP-derived phenotypes were consistently detected in at least one single-environment analysis. These interactions reveal additional layers of genetic variation that are not captured by single-locus models and highlight the complexity of trait regulation in cotton (Fig. 4a, Additional file 2: Table S10). Specifically, the number of significant epistatic interactions varied among traits, with the highest observed in SFC (n = 519) and the lowest in FE (n = 4) (Fig. 4b), indicating considerable variation in the extent of epistatic regulation across traits. Notably, a locus (D05_65078536) on chromosome D05 may be a hotspot region for SFC, suggesting its potential importance in regulating this trait (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). By analyzing the distribution of epistatic interaction loci across the At and Dt subgenomes, we observed multiple key hotspot regions in both subgenomes, which are involved in exchanging interaction signals across different traits. Most traits exhibited a mixed pattern characterized by highly coordinated effects between the At and Dt subgenomes (Fig. 4b). Notably, for MV, interactions between the At and Dt subgenomes accounted for the highest proportion, reaching 80%. Within this trait, a locus at chromosome D12 (D12_6039345) was identified as a hotspot for MV, exhibiting interactions with a locus on chromosome A13 (Additional file 2: Table S10). We compared the cumulative contributions of three types of epistatic interactions (AA, DD, and AD) to complex traits (Fig. 4c, Additional file 1: Fig. S6). AD interactions contributed significantly more than AA or DD interactions (Permutation P = 0.0014, Additional file 1: Fig. S7), underscoring the central role of At–Dt inter–subgenome interactions in cotton quantitative trait inheritance. To quantify the phenotypic variance explained by epistatic SNPs, we separately estimated the proportion of phenotypic variance jointly explained by the identified SNPs for each trait. For SCWPB, approximately 13.68% of the phenotypic variance was discovered, highlighting the substantial contribution of epistatic SNPs to trait variation (Fig. 4d). Overall, these results demonstrate that epistatic interactions, particularly between the At and Dt subgenomes, play a central and complementary role in shaping complex trait variation in cotton.

Breeding-relevant adaptive loci pinpointed by selection signatures in natural populations

As the China’s primary region for upland cotton cultivation, Xinjiang has experienced shifts in breeding objectives over time, and the resulting long–term selection provides a valuable framework for dissecting the origin, distribution, and genetic architecture of elite alleles across breeding stages. To do so, we analyzed 318 accessions (comprising both introduced lines and independently developed Xinjiang cultivars) resequenced at ~ 10 × genome coverage and categorized into four breeding stages (pop0: early–1990, pop1: 1991–2000, pop2: 2001–2010, and pop3: 2011–2023) (Additional file 2: Table S11). Population structure analyses indicated broadly overlapping genetic backgrounds among breeding-stage groups, together with detectable genetic heterogeneity and mixed ancestry among accessions (Additional file 1: Fig. S8). These groups exhibit pronounced differences in yield and fiber–quality traits, reflecting the influence of their distinct breeding histories (Additional file 1: Fig. S9). Through a joint analysis of Fst and log2(π ratio), we identified selection–driven genomic regions in the three later breeding stages (pop1, pop2, and pop3) relative to the early stage (pop0) (Fig. 5a–c). The number of selective windows varied across breeding-stage comparisons, with the highest number detected in pop2–pop0 (pop1–pop0: 2464; pop2–pop0: 4340; pop3–pop0: 3864), suggesting a dynamic and non-linear pattern of selection across breeding stages (Fig. 5d). Notably, 1931 segments were shared across at least two stages, with 520 core regions consistently showing the same direction of π ratio change and significant Fst differentiation, representing key genomic hotspots repeatedly utilized in Xinjiang upland cotton improvement (Fig. 5d), and these 520 core regions were subsequently merged into 38 QTLs (Additional file 2: Table S12). Functional annotation revealed that these loci are predominantly associated with developmental processes, particularly root system development and plant organ formation, as well as regulatory and metabolic functions including carbohydrate binding, phosphatase activity, and transcriptional regulation (Additional file 1: Fig. S10). These findings suggest that long-term selection in Xinjiang cotton breeding has not acted on isolated loci, but rather on coordinated regulatory networks integrating hormone signaling, developmental programming, and metabolic modulation. Overall, these patterns indicate stronger and more concentrated selection pressures in later stages, reflecting the ongoing convergence of breeding objectives toward improved yield and fiber quality.

Building on these findings, we further integrated the selection regions detected in Xinjiang natural populations with GWAS–derived QTLs from the MAGIC population. This comparative framework uncovered multiple co–localized intervals (Fig. 5e, Additional file 2: Table S13). Notably, 53.16% of the GWAS-identified loci overlapped with the selected regions, a proportion significantly higher than expected by chance (Permutation P = 0.0015, Additional file 1: Fig. S11). This substantial overlap suggests that a large proportion of trait-associated loci have been shaped by artificial selection during cotton improvement, pinpointing functional loci that drive both phenotypic variation and breeding-driven adaptation. This convergence is exemplified by a QTL on chromosome A05 (A05: 37,469,256–39,519,256 bp), where GWAS signals from the MAGIC population coincide with selective sweeps detected in Xinjiang natural populations (Fig. 5e). In the MAGIC population, this QTL was consistently identified as a candidate interval in the sGWAS analysis and exhibited a pronounced LD pattern (Fig. 5f). Further analysis of parental allele composition revealed that the peak SNP within bin2982 showed significant differences in mean FL among founder–derived IBD groups (Fig. 5g). While in the Xinjiang natural population, superior haplotypes at these loci, excluding a few sites, underwent cumulative selection (Additional file 2: Table S14), with a locus (A05_39511918) within this QTL exhibiting a consistent increase in favorable haplotype frequency across breeding stages (Fig. 5h–j). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that this approach provides a robust framework for identifying loci repeatedly targeted during breeding and contributing to fiber–related phenotypic variation.

Translating favorable SNPs into optimal crosses via multi-trait optimization

Based on GWAS (sGWAS and hGWAS) and epistatic interaction mapping in the MAGIC population, combined with loci that underwent continuous selection during Xinjiang cotton breeding, we defined a set of favorable SNPs associated with key agronomic and fiber–quality traits. To evaluate their cumulative genetic effects, haplotype–based genotype coding was applied across MAGIC RILs, assigning the superior homozygote as 1, the heterozygote as 0, and the inferior homozygote as –1. The resulting favorable–allele accumulation values quantified the overall enrichment of advantageous alleles within each RILs. The analysis revealed substantial variation in favorable–allele accumulation among MAGIC RILs. Several lines exhibited pronounced enrichment of advantageous alleles across multiple traits, implying that these genotypes may have experienced sustained positive selection during breeding and retained considerable genetic potential (Fig. 6a, Additional file 2: Table S15). Further examination of the founder lines revealed pronounced differences in favorable–allele accumulation among the eight founders. HC04 exhibited the highest accumulation of favorable alleles across multiple traits, including FL, FS, and MAT. In contrast, HC02 showed the lowest accumulation, performing poorly for most traits (Fig. 6b). Accordingly, the MAGIC population provides a robust theoretical and data–driven framework for parental selection, multi–trait modeling, integrative selection, and elite parent identification using favorable SNPs.

To evaluate genetic potential, trait-specific prediction models were developed via partial least squares regression (PLSR) using favorable-allele accumulation as the explanatory variable. To maintain data distribution consistency, phenotypic data were split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets using stratified random sampling. Model parameters were optimized via tenfold cross-validation on the training set (Fig. 6c). By extracting the optimal principal components for each trait, we generated predicted values and assessed model performance (R2) for both the training and test sets (Fig. 6d–f, Additional file 1: Fig. S12). The prediction accuracy was satisfactory for most traits. Specifically, SCI, SCW, HFFBN, and FL demonstrated robust performance with test R2 values ranging from 0.410 to 0.458, accompanied by training R2 values between 0.352 and 0.503 (Fig. 6d–f). Conversely, VWDI and FBA exhibited comparatively lower predictive power (test R2 < 0.1) (Additional file 1: Fig. S12o, S12l). These findings demonstrate that favorable-allele accumulation constitutes a reliable predictor for the genetic potential of multiple agronomic and fiber-quality traits.

To translate PLSR–based multi–trait predictions into breeding applications, the framework was integrated with Pareto–front selection to guide parental choice and hybrid design. Reflecting their relative economic importance in Xinjiang breeding programs, weightings of 0.3 for FL and SCW, 0.25 for FS and LCW, and − 0.1 for HFFBN were strategically assigned to prioritize yield components and fiber quality in the identification of elite parental candidates from the MAGIC population. Standardized, weighted composite scores of PLSR–predicted values highlighted superior lines such as M280, M83, M31, and M88 (Fig. 6h, Additional file 2: Table S16). In terms of field performance, M31 exhibits higher FL, LCW and LP with lower MV, whereas M88 shows superior SCW and FS (Fig. 6i, j, Additional file 1: Fig. S13). Pareto–front analysis further revealed parental pairs achieving balanced multi–trait optimization (Additional file 2: Table S17). Notably, the cross between M88 and M31 exhibited pronounced phenotypic complementarity (Fig. 6k), underscoring the potential of this framework in selecting parents for simultaneous improvement of conflicting breeding objectives.

Translating MAGIC RILs diversity into elite cultivars through conventional breeding

MAGIC RILs serve both as a foundation for future data–driven multi–trait selection and as parental lines in conventional breeding programs. Derived from the parental lines M1 and M101, cultivars Zhuangjiahan902 (ZJH902, National Cotton Variety Approval No. 20190015) and Huaxin103 (HX103, National Cotton Variety Approval No. 20231001) were approved by the National Crop Variety Approval Committee of China and subsequently promoted for commercial production (Fig. 7a–b, d–e, Additional file 2: Table S18). To examine the genetic relationships between these cultivars and their documented progenitor lines, we further evaluated their genome-wide molecular profiles. Principal component analysis (PCA) mapped both ZJH902 and HX103 within the overall genetic architecture of the MAGIC population, though they did not exhibit tight clustering with their documented progenitor lines, M1 and M101 (Additional file 1: Fig. S14a). Genetic kinship and correlation analyses revealed detectable but moderate relatedness between the cultivars and their respective progenitors, yielding correlation coefficients of 0.350 and 0.222 (Additional file 1: Fig. S14b). Thus, ZJH902 and HX103 retain a certain degree of genome-wide relatedness to their recorded progenitor lines. Both cultivars inherit favorable alleles from their MAGIC RIL parents that underpin improvements in agronomic performance and fiber quality, with each line carrying an enriched complement of advantageous loci associated with fiber traits and plant architecture, consistent with the targeted selection priorities of the breeding program (Fig. 7c, f). An integrated analysis of haplotype structures and core functional genes demonstrates that both cultivars have assimilated superior alleles from their MAGIC RIL parental lines during the improvement of fiber quality and plant architecture.

These shared haplotypes integrate coordinated improvements in hormonal regulation, metabolic capacity, and cellular structural stability (Additional file 2: Table S19). Haplotype analyses and functional annotation suggest that candidate genes, including YUC6, TINY2, and NCED1, are associated with hormonal and metabolic pathways potentially involved in fiber maturation. Meanwhile, variants of MPH2, PP2C, RING/U–box, and TOR may contribute to fiber cell elongation through their putative roles in photosynthesis and proteostasis. Similarly, advantageous haplotypes of RRTF1, ERF B–3, PMP22/Mpv17, and 2OG–Fe (II) oxygenase were identified as potential regulators of internode elongation. These results provide a putative genetic framework, although the specific molecular interactions and causal variations remain to be further validated. These elite haplotypes constitute a synergistic regulatory network that tightly coordinates fiber development with plant structural traits. In parallel, we selected two widely cultivated commercial varieties (Xinluzao84, and H33–1–4), each grown on more than 333,333 hm2, as reference materials representing the production end. Although these varieties do not share a direct pedigree relationship with the MAGIC RIL population (Additional file 2: Table S18), their convergence reflects a directional aggregation of homologous superior alleles during breeding (Additional file 1: Fig. S15, Additional file 2: Table S20) and provides a robust molecular basis for the simultaneous improvement of fiber quality and plant architecture.

Discussion

Genetic diversity and recombination architecture empower MAGIC populations

Since the development of the first MAGIC population in Arabidopsis thaliana [39], MAGIC populations have been widely and successfully applied in various crop species as a customizable genetic resource platform [40–42]. These multiparent populations enhance genetic resolution and overcome the structural limitations of conventional population designs [43–45]. In breeding programs, MAGIC populations are generated by systematically intercrossing multiple parents that exhibit superior performance across diverse traits [45–47]. This design promotes the accumulation of favorable alleles and enhances overall selection efficiency [26, 47, 48]. The MAGIC population developed in this study shows clear structural advantages in its genetic architecture. Multiple rounds of intermating among eight founders followed by selfing effectively disrupted long-range linkage across the cotton genome. Notably, HMM-based inference using high-quality SNPs identified IBD segments, of which 44.3% were shorter than 1 Mb, indicating frequent and uniform recombination and a substantial compression of linkage blocks. This fragmented structure is reflected in rapid LD decay, with r2 halving within ~ 1,500 kb, much faster than in natural Upland cotton populations [12, 32, 33]. Dispersed clustering of RILs and interspersed founder positions in the phylogeny further confirm extensive genome reshuffling. Phenotypically, this population exhibits clear transgressive segregation rather than simple parental trait addition. Approximately 10% of the MAGIC lines showed fiber lengths exceeding those of seven superior parents, and the broad-sense heritability of several core traits exceeded 50%, indicating substantial phenotypic potential under a stable genetic background. The stable correlations among multiple traits provide a framework for coordinated improvement. Moreover, the allelic richness derived from eight founders, together with minimal population stratification, enhances the accuracy of genomic prediction, making this MAGIC population an effective genetic platform for high-resolution dissection and precision improvement of complex traits.

Although the MAGIC population constructed in this study exhibits skewed parental contributions that may induce uneven allele frequency distributions, this asymmetry does not inherently attenuate the statistical power for identifying QTLs underlying key agronomic traits. Given that the founders harbor superior genetic backgrounds, the resulting population achieved a targeted enrichment of functional loci associated with cotton yield and fiber quality, thereby optimizing both detection sensitivity and mapping precision for major-effect QTLs. The consistency of our findings with recognized breeding goals confirms that this population effectively represents core genetic variation. While a more balanced founder representation could further expand allelic diversity, the current design reflects a deliberate trade-off between maximizing diversity and enriching breeding-relevant alleles, thereby ensuring both biological relevance and analytical robustness. To further enhance mapping accuracy, we employed the HC04 founder genome as a reference, leveraging its high collinearity with the population. While we acknowledge that a single-reference approach may miss certain founder-specific structural variants, the high recombination frequency within the MAGIC framework significantly dampens the impact of reference-dependent bias, ensuring the overall reliability of our SNP and IBD inferences.

Complex genetic control of fiber quality revealed by major QTL, epistasis, and selection signatures

Patterns involving major–effect loci, polygenic minor effects, and epistatic interactions are pervasive in crop genetics [49–51], as exemplified by yield–related traits in rice [52, 53], ear row number in maize [54, 55], grain protein content in wheat [56, 57], and disease resistance in soybean [58, 59]. However, compared with diploid crops such as rice [27] and maize [60], epistatic interactions in cotton are more pronounced and are often driven by interactions between the At and Dt subgenomes, thereby intensifying genetic coupling and increasing the complexity of phenotypic variation. The genetic dissection framework established here provides a unified theoretical and analytical paradigm for resolving complex traits across crops.

Our results indicate that stable major–effect loci constitute a first–tier genetic backbone, contributing robustly across genetic backgrounds and environments but explaining only a fraction of heritability. Beyond single–locus effects, extensive epistatic interactions form a second–tier regulatory layer, capturing substantial cryptic heritability overlooked by conventional analyses. Integration with selection signature analyses from natural populations further revealed that several key QTLs identified in the MAGIC population reside in regions under strong selection, indicating their roles in domestication, improvement, and adaptive evolution; these loci represent a third–tier layer that enhances the reliability of breeding targets. Together, major loci define the structural basis of trait variation [37, 61, 62], epistasis constructs higher–order regulatory networks [28, 63–65], and selection shapes favorable allelic combinations [13, 66–68], enabling a shift from locus–centric to systems–level dissection of complex traits.

In this study, two GWAS approaches implemented in the MAGIC population enabled the identification of 111 sQTLs and 15 hQTLs, and epistasis analysis revealed more than 3,000 significant interacting locus pairs. Meanwhile, evolutionary analysis of a Xinjiang natural population detected 520 genomic regions subjected to strong artificial selection during breeding.

Although only a limited number of epistatic interactions were detected within individual environments, this likely reflects the strong environmental dependency of epistasis, whereby many interactions are expressed only under specific conditions and are therefore difficult to conistently detect [69]. In this context, BLUP-based analyses preferentially capture stable, integrated genetic signals across environments, whereas single-environment analyses tend to reveal condition-specific interactions [70]. This complementary pattern indicates that the epistatic architecture of cotton traits consists of both a stable core component and a dynamic, environment-responsive layer, underscoring the complex and hierarchical genetic regulation underlying yield and fiber quality.

Notably, the limited overlap between loci identified in the MAGIC population and breeding selection signals is not unexpected. Rather, the presence of shared genomic regions provides key evidence that genetic mapping signals can, at least in part, coincide with footprints of breeding-driven selection. Together, these results support the feasibility of an integrative framework that links high-resolution genetic dissection with the evolutionary processes shaped by modern breeding.

Breeding–oriented multi–trait optimization highlights the practical value of MAGIC RILs

Modern breeding has shifted from optimizing single traits to achieving coordinated improvement across multiple traits [71–73]. However, pervasive antagonistic relationships among traits pose a major challenge [74]. In cotton, the simultaneous improvement of lint yield and fiber quality is severely hindered by their persistent negative genetic correlations, often attributed to linkage drag or pleiotropy [75, 76] Similarly, breeding for early maturity to accommodate mechanized harvesting frequently necessitates compromises in boll weight and overall yield potential [77]. Here, we present a breeding–oriented multi–trait optimization framework based on favorable SNPs identified in a cotton MAGIC population. By integrating allelic effects, epistatic interactions, and QTL stability, this framework quantitatively evaluates the breeding potential of alternative allelic combinations and enables genotype–driven, data–informed parent selection. Analysis of allelic architectures across MAGIC RILs identified lines with superior multi–trait performance, either as elite combinatorial genotypes or as complementary parents for synergistic improvement. Notably, several RILs exhibit phenotypic performance comparable to or exceeding current cultivars, underscoring the dual value of MAGIC populations as both genetic discovery platforms and breeding–ready resources.

Beyond cotton, this framework is broadly applicable to other self- and cross-pollinated crops where complex trait trade-offs constrain breeding progress, such as Oryza sativa, Zea mays, and Triticum aestivum. Importantly, the integration of epistasis and multi-trait allelic architecture highlights the need to move beyond single-locus selection toward systems-level breeding strategies that explicitly account for genetic interactions.

Nevertheless, the predictive power of such frameworks may still be influenced by population-specific linkage structure, environmental dependency of trait expression, and incomplete capture of rare alleles. Future work integrating multi-environment trials, functional validation, and genome editing approaches will further refine the identification of truly causal and stable favorable alleles. Collectively, the favorable-allele-based multi-trait integration and intelligent crossing strategy outlined here provides a scalable blueprint for precision breeding and germplasm innovation in cotton and other major crops.

Conclusions

By integrating high–resolution resequencing with the genetic dissection of both MAGIC and natural populations, we identified significant QTLs for 19 key agronomic traits, revealed cryptic loci driven by epistatic interactions, and delineated genomic segments fixed through the modern breeding process. By integrating these findings into a multi–trait optimization framework, this study effectively bridges the gap between theoretical genetic insights and practical breeding outcomes. Ultimately, this research provides a translatable and interpretable methodological framework and elite germplasm resources, laying the foundation for multi–trait synergetic optimization and precision–designed cotton improvement.

Methods

MAGIC population construction

The eight–parent MAGIC population was developed using cotton germplasm resources maintained by the Cotton Research Institute, Shihezi Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Shihezi, China). Eight parental lines were designated HC01 to HC08. Prior to aggregation crosses, typical single plants were selected and selfed for five consecutive generations to ensure homozygosity. In the summer of 2008, four biparental crosses (HC01 × HC02, HC03 × HC04, HC05 × HC06, and HC07 × HC08) were conducted, following the design described by Huang et.al. [40], followed by two four–way F1 hybrids (HC01–04 and HC05–08) produced in Hainan during the winter. In the summer of 2009, random intercrossing between F1 (HC01–04) and F1 (HC05–08) was performed to generate the eight–way hybrid F1 (HC01–08). From the F2 generation onward, the single–seed descent method was employed for continuous selfing from F3 to F7 generations, and by the F8 generation, 319 stabilized lines were obtained. The population construction spanned six years and was completed in 2013, as illustrated in Additional file 1: Fig. S16.

Population sampling and classification in Xinjiang cotton breeding

We collected a total of 318 accessions representing Xinjiang upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) populations (Additional file 2: Table S11). These accessions were systematically sampled from major cotton–growing regions of Xinjiang, encompassing landraces, historically important cultivars, and modern breeding lines. Based on their breeding period and pedigree information, the accessions were classified into four temporal subpopulations (pop0–pop3) to capture the genetic changes during the breeding history: pop0 represents early landraces developed between 1978 and the 1990, pop1 includes cultivars released during the 1991–2000, pop2 consists of mid-stage improved cultivars from the 2001 to 2010, and pop3 represents recently released modern cultivars (2011–2023). The four subpopulations differed markedly in their breeding histories, and genetic backgrounds, thereby providing a valuable framework for tracing the evolutionary and selective processes underlying cotton improvement in Xinjiang.

Field experiments and phenotyping

From 2018 to 2019, eight parental lines and 319 RILs of the MAGIC population were cultivated at three locations in Shihezi or Manas (the northern of Xinjiang) and Korla (the southern of Xinjiang). Each line was grown in two 5–m rows with a spacing pattern of (66 + 10) + 12.5 cm under a randomized design with two replications. Standard agronomic practices were applied to ensure uniform growth across environments. A total of 19 agronomic traits were evaluated, encompassing fiber quality, yield performance, growth period, plant architecture, and disease resistance. The fiber quality traits included upper–half mean length (FL), fiber uniformity (FU), short fiber content (SFC), fiber strength (FS), fiber elongation (FE), micronaire (MV), fiber maturity (MAT), and spinning consistency index (SCI). Yield–related traits comprised lint cotton weight of 10 bolls (LCW), seed cotton weight of 10 bolls (SCW), lint cotton weight per boll (LCWPB), seed cotton weight per boll (SCWPB), and lint percentage (LP). Growth period traits consisted of flowering period (FP) and whole growth duration (WGD), while plant architecture traits included plant height (PH), height of first fruiting branch node (HFFBN), and fruit branch angle (FBA). Verticillium wilt disease index (VWDI) was recorded as the indicator of disease resistance.

Estimation of breeding value and calculation of heritability

Phenotypic data from multi–environment trials conducted in 2018–2019 were initially analyzed in Excel to calculate descriptive statistics for each trait, including mean, standard deviation, range, coefficient of variation, and variance. BLUP for each trait were then estimated using linear mixed models implemented in the lme4 package in R (V4.5.1), following the approach described by Li et al. [29], which also allowed calculation of trait heritability and correlations across environments. The BLUP values were used as breeding values to represent the genetic potential of each line for subsequent analyses.

Genome sequencing

In the spring of 2020, eight founders and 319 RILs of the MAGIC population were cultivated under greenhouse conditions. Young leaves from individual seedlings were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80℃ for subsequent analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method, treated with RNase, and assessed for quality using agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometric analysis. High–quality DNA from the 8 founders and 319 RILs was used to construct sequencing libraries, which were subsequently sequenced on the MGISEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) after passing quality control. A total of resequencing data was obtained for eight founders and 319 RILs, with an average sequencing depth of approximately 50 × for founders and 7 × for RILs, yielding about 6 Tb of raw sequencing data (Additional file 2: Table S21).

In the spring of 2024, 128 Xinjiang cotton cultivars released after 2018 were cultivated in Shihezi City, Xinjiang, China. Young leaf tissues were collected using the same sampling procedure as described above and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), with an average sequencing depth of approximately 12 ×, generating ~ 3.7 Tb of data (Additional file 2: Table S22).

Mapping and sequence variant calling

The resequencing data of seven parental lines, 319 RILs, and 318 Xinjiang accessions were aligned to the upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) reference genome of HC04 [78] using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA–MEM, v2.2.1) software. The HC04 genome was selected as the reference for read alignment and variant calling because it represents one of the founders of the MAGIC population, thereby maximizing genomic compatibility and reducing alignment bias. SNP calling was performed on the uniquely mapped reads using Sentieon software. The identified SNPs were subsequently filtered using the following criteria: “QD < 2.0 || MQ < 40.0 || FS > 60.0 || SOR > 3.0 || MQRankSum < − 12.5 || ReadPosRankSum < − 8.0”, with a minimum distance of 10 bp between adjacent SNPs.

Annotation of genetic variants

Genome–wide variants were annotated and classified based on the annotation file of the Xinluzao 31 reference genome using ANNOVAR (v2017) software [79]. According to genomic structure and variant positions, variants were categorized into intergenic, upstream, downstream, intronic, splicing, and exonic regions. Variants located within exonic regions were further classified according to their functional consequences as stop gain, stop loss, synonymous, or nonsynonymous mutations.

Phylogenetic tree, population structure and linkage–disequilibrium analysis

Based on the SNP data of the MAGIC population, a neighbor–joining tree was constructed using the PHYLIP software package (v3.695, http://evolution.genetics.washington.edu/phylip), and the phylogenetic tree was visualized using the online tool iTOL (v7, https://itol.embl.de). The linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay of the MAGIC population was calculated using PopLDdecay (v3.42) [80] with the parameters “–MaxDist 5000 –MAF 0.05 –Miss 0.2”, and the results were visualized accordingly.

Mosaic map tracing IBD origins

For reconstructing the genome–wide IBD map, the modified hidden Markov mode was applied to each MAGIC line [81]. The HMM uses information from adjacent SNPs to estimate the posterior probability that a locus in the offspring originates from a specific parent. A locus was assigned to a parent only when its posterior probability exceeded twice the random expectation (1/8); otherwise, it was considered unknown. Adjacent IBD segments from the same parent were merged, and segments containing fewer than 30 SNPs were filtered out, followed by additional merging to obtain the final IBD map. The procedure involved projecting the TM–1 genetic map onto the HC04 genome via collinear regions, calculating genetic distances of SNPs on each chromosome, computing parental prior probabilities and offspring probability matrices, merging adjacent IBD SNPs into segments, and selecting high–confidence segments for the final IBD representation.

SNP–based GWAS

Based on 1,838,490 high–quality SNPs filtered from the MAGIC population, genome–wide association analysis was performed using Fast–LMM software (v2.32) [82], with the kinship matrix calculated from the same SNP dataset. The significance threshold was set at 5.53 × 10–7(1/n, where n is the total number of SNPs). To integrate the SNP–based genome–wide association study results, adjacent significant SNPs within 1 Mb were merged into a single quantitative trait locus (QTL), referred to as an sQTL. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) between significant SNPs of neighboring sQTLs was then calculated, and those with r2 ≥ 0.2 were further merged until all adjacent sQTLs became relatively independent (r2 < 0.2). To reduce the likelihood of missing causal genes due to overly narrow intervals, the upstream and downstream boundaries of each sQTL (defined by the peak SNP surpassing the significance threshold, P < 5.53 × 10–9) were defined by identifying LD-supported flanking SNPs. The most significant SNP within each region was designated as the peak SNP, and the physical range of all significant SNPs within that region was defined as the confidence interval of the locus.

IBD–based GWAS

Using all recombination breakpoints identified in the MAGIC population, the genome was divided into 17,264 bins based on IBD segments, with each RIL carrying a single IBD state per bin. Because these IBD segments directly represent founder-derived ancestral haplotypes, association mapping based on these segments inherently functions as a haplotype-based approach. Genomic bin genotypes were converted into dummy variables following the method of VanRaden [83]. An IBD–based GWAS was performed using a mixed linear model (MLM), where both the bin and kinship matrices were treated as random effects, and the top 10 principal components were included as fixed effects. Likelihood ratio test (LRT) statistics for each bin were estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. Trait–specific LRT significance thresholds were determined via 500 permutations [84], with thresholds ranging from 13.9 to 39.2 at α = 0.05. For each trait, adjacent significant bins (≤ 1 Mb apart or separated by ≤ 5 bins) were merged into a single locus, designated as an hQTL. The most significant bin within each locus was defined as the peak bin, and the physical positions of the significant bins constituted the confidence interval of the hQTL.

Epistasis analysis

SNPs were first filtered using PLINK (v1.9, www.cog–genomics.org/plink/1.9/) to obtain high–quality variants for subsequent analyses. SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05 were excluded, and LD pruning was performed with parameters r2 < 0.5, a window size of 50 kb, and a step size of 5 SNPs. Epistatic interactions among the remaining SNPs were then analyzed using the epistasis module in PLINK, based on both BLUP–adjusted single–environment and multi–environment phenotypic data, with a significance threshold set at P < 1 × 10–4. The resulting P were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, and interactions with adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significant epistatic effects. The joint heritability of these loci was subsequently estimated using GCTA (V1.95, http://www.complextraitgenomics.com/software/gcta/).

Population genetic statistics

To investigate the genetic diversity and differentiation of the Xinjiang Upland cotton populations, we calculated nucleotide diversity (π) and population differentiation (Fst) across the genome. Nucleotide diversity (π) for each subpopulation (pop0–pop3) was computed using a sliding window approach with 100 kb windows and 10 kb steps. Pairwise population differentiation (Fst) between subpopulations was estimated using Weir and Cockerham’s method.

Windows with high Fst (top 5%) and low π ratio (top 5%) were considered candidate regions under positive selection in the target population. Log2 transformation of π ratios and z–score normalization of Fst values were applied to facilitate visualization and comparative analysis. Only regions meeting these criteria were retained as selected regions for further analysis. All calculations were performed using VCFtools (v0.1.16, https://github.com/vcftools/vcftools) and custom R scripts.

Allele frequency dynamics across populations

To investigate temporal changes in allele frequencies among sequential Xinjiang Upland cotton populations, per–population allele frequency files were generated using VCFtools and merged in R (v4.3.1). For each SNP, allele frequency differences between consecutive populations (pop0–pop3) were calculated, and changes exceeding 10% were considered biologically significant. Alleles showing a consistent directional change across populations were inferred to be potentially under long–term artificial or natural selection.

Genomic prediction using partial least squares regression

Partial least squares regression (PLSR) was employed to model each trait individually, addressing multicollinearity among high-dimensional predictors and improving model robustness. To maintain data distribution consistency and avoid circularity, phenotypic data were split into an independent training set (80%) and a test set (20%) using stratified random sampling based on phenotypic distribution. Genome-wide favorable SNP accumulation values were used as predictors. According to the definition in Table S23 (Additional file 2), the predictors were encoded as follows: homozygous favorable alleles were coded as 1, heterozygous alleles as 0, and homozygous unfavorable alleles as −1. Model parameters were optimized via tenfold cross-validation solely within the training set. Model performance was evaluated on the unseen test set, quantified using the coefficient of determination (R2) between predicted and observed phenotypic values. PLSR models were implemented using the plsr() function in the R package pls [85] and evaluated by tenfold cross-validation. The optimal number of latent components was determined by minimizing the cross-validated prediction residual error sum of squares.

Multi-trait integration and pareto-front analysis

To facilitate multi-trait decision-making in breeding applications, PLSR-predicted values were standardized across traits and integrated into a weighted composite score according to the favorable direction and relative importance of each trait. Trait-specific weights were assigned to reflect breeding objectives, allowing an overall evaluation of multi-trait performance for each line. In parallel, Pareto-front analysis was applied to identify lines or parental combinations that achieved optimal trade-offs among multiple traits without being dominated across all objectives. This combined framework enabled the prioritization of elite lines with balanced multi-trait performance and the identification of complementary parental pairs for hybrid design [86].

Population structure and pedigree confirmation

To verify the pedigree relationships and trace the genetic origin of the released cultivars (ZJH902 and HX103), we performed population structure and kinship analyses using high-quality genomic SNPs. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using PLINK (v1.9) to resolve the genetic clustering of the cultivars within the MAGIC population. To further confirm pedigree consistency, an identity-by-state kinship matrix was calculated to estimate the genome-wide genetic relatedness among the founders, RILs, and the target cultivars.
