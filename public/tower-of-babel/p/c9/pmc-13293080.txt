Multiple Origins of a Sex Ratio Supergene in Formica Ants
Authors: German Lagunas-Robles, Jessica Purcell, Evan Shimota, Alan Brelsford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolymorphisms in complex traits often map to regions of the genome with two or more functional mutations with little or no recombination between them. These “supergenes” are usually found in a single species or a few closely related species. However, when supergenes persist through a large number of speciation events, they may take on additional functions in different lineages. Here, we investigate how an ancient supergene has evolved to gain novel function. In Formica ants, most species have a “social” supergene with M and P haplotypes that determine whether a colony is headed by a single queen or multiple queens, respectively, and which diverged from each other at least 20 million years ago. At least three Formica species have an additional haplotype on the same chromosome, termed MD, associated with female-biased offspring sex ratio at the colony level. Do the MD haplotypes share a common origin or did they evolve convergently? With whole-genome resequencing, we identify MD haplotypes in three additional species and use variation across six Formica species to examine the evolutionary history of the MD haplotype. We identify two putative origins of the MD haplotype, one in the ancestor of five Nearctic species, and a second in the Palearctic species, Formica cinerea. We present evidence that the MD haplotypes originated from recombination between M and P haplotypes. The discovery of two MD haplotypes convergently evolving from distinct recombination events between two ancestral supergene haplotypes illustrates how supergenes can diversify and gain additional phenotypes.

Introduction

Clusters of linked loci that determine discrete alternative phenotypes are often referred to as “supergenes” (Darlington and Mather 1950; Thompson and Jiggins 2014). Supergenes frequently involve reduced or suppressed recombination, often through structural rearrangements (Schwander et al. 2014). Inversions, where a segment of DNA is reversed in orientation, can preserve associations between alleles at multiple loci on alternative haplotypes. Theoretical work shows that reduced recombination can maintain beneficial allele combinations (Turner 1968; Jay et al. 2024), but the evolutionary circumstances by which supergenes arise vary from system to system (Schwander et al. 2014; Faria et al. 2019; Gutiérrez-Valencia et al. 2021; Villoutreix et al. 2021; Purcell and Brelsford 2025).

Most described supergenes are found in a single species (e.g. Tuttle et al. 2016; Kim et al. 2017; Funk et al. 2021; Hendrickx et al. 2022; Baird et al. 2023; Jin et al. 2024; Lesturgie et al. 2025). When supergenes are present in multiple congeneric species, they either share a common origin (Brelsford et al. 2020; Yan et al. 2020), or they evolved convergently (Duhamel et al. 2022). Sex chromosomes are a classic supergene example with alternative sexes being determined by alternative haplotypes (Charlesworth 2016). The ancient sex chromosome pairs found in mammals, birds, and Lepidoptera have persisted through many speciation events (Zhu et al. 2025), but sex chromosome turnover is common in other lineages including some frogs (Ma and Veltsos 2021) and fishes (Kitano and Peichel 2012).

In animal clades with frequent turnover of sex chromosomes, many of the same genes are involved in conserved regulatory networks that result in ovary or testis determination, but the gene that controls which network is switched on and which is switched off can differ between species (Capel 2017). Different genes from within the gonad-determining regulatory networks can take on the role of the switch, but there is a limited pool of such genes. This means that sex chromosomes can evolve independently on homologous genomic regions in multiple species (Jeffries et al. 2018; El Taher et al. 2021).

Along the same lines, multiple plant lineages have a supergene that controls flower style length. Emerging consensus suggests that this “S locus” evolved convergently in multiple lineages through distinct gene duplication events (Shang et al. 2025). While similar genes can lead to the evolution of the same phenotype, convergent supergene phenotypes do not necessarily require the same genes to be present. In some ants, queen number (Purcell et al. 2014; Mona et al. 2025) and queen size (Sigeman et al. 2025b) are determined by convergent supergenes that do not share many genes. In contrast, rearrangements of similar genomic content led to convergent supergenes in Microbotryum mating-type loci (Branco et al. 2018; Duhamel et al. 2022). Remarkably, independent rearrangements occurred at least nine times in this genus (Duhamel et al. 2022), illustrating that the evolution of suppressed recombination can lead to phenotypic convergence. But how do supergenes diversify when they are already established? Does a three-haplotype supergene polymorphism originate through a novel inversion on an established haplotype, or through a recombination event between two divergent haplotypes?

In the ant genus Formica, most species have a supergene on chromosome 3 that is associated with colony queen number (Table 1). The “social” supergene typically spans most of the chromosome (∼10Mbp) with alternative haplotypes determining whether a colony is headed by a single queen (= monogyne) or multiple queens (= polygyne). Monogyne colonies typically have exclusively M/M homozygous workers while the P haplotype is typically only found in polygyne colonies. A phylogenetic study of over 90 Formica species showed that SNPs associated with variation in colony queen number likely predate the ancestor of all extant Formica (Purcell et al. 2021), suggesting that the P haplotype is an ancient haplotype that arose once.

Recent studies described additional trait variation that maps to chromosome 3 in a subset of Formica species. Among the species-specific variation is a haplotype associated with colony sex ratio in three species, Formica glacialis, Formica podzolica, and Formica cinerea (Lagunas-Robles et al. 2021; Scarparo et al. 2023). The MD haplotype (associated with gyne production) is only differentiated from the MA haplotype between ∼2-7.5 Mbp in F. glacialis and F. podzolica, but both haplotypes remain associated with monogynous colony structure (Lagunas-Robles et al. 2021). In F. cinerea, monogyne colonies with the MD haplotype produced gynes more often than monogyne colonies lacking the MD haplotype (Scarparo et al. 2023). The M haplotype of Formica selysi is collinear with the M haplotype of Formica exsecta (Brelsford et al. 2020) and the MA haplotype of F. glacialis (Purcell et al. 2025), suggesting that the F. glacialis MA is homologous to the M found in species with a single monogyne-associated haplotype. However, we do not yet know whether the MD originated once in a common ancestor of these three species, or convergently in different lineages.

A potential source of genetic variation that may have resulted in the MD haplotype forming could be the P haplotype. The supergene region on chromosome 3 appears to be dynamic and harbors more than two alternative haplotypes in some species, often with distinct variants on one side of the centromere or the other (hereafter “left arm” and “right arm”). A “short P haplotype” (PS), which spans about half of the extent of the putative ancestral P haplotype, was found in F. glacialis (Lagunas-Robles et al. 2021) and F. podzolica (Purcell et al. 2025). Though it is reduced in size compared to the described P haplotypes in other Formica species, the PS haplotype is still associated with polygyny. F. podzolica also has a “long P haplotype” (PL; Purcell et al. 2025), which spans the same region of chromosome 3 as the P haplotype described in other Formica species. In F. cinerea, there are two divergent P haplotypes, the P1 and the P2, both of which span the full supergene region (Scarparo et al. 2023). The two distinct P haplotypes are associated with polygyny; the P2 is in linkage disequilibrium with a queen-size supergene on chromosome 9 (Scarparo et al. 2023). The recent discovery of an M haplotype that contains two P-derived genes and is found in supercolonial Formica paralugubris and Formica aquilonia suggests that novel combinations of two supergene haplotypes can result in the evolution of new phenotypes (Sigeman et al. 2025a).

To establish when and how MD haplotypes evolved, we compared MD haplotypes in five Nearctic species and one Palearctic species. First, we asked whether there is a single MD origin or multiple origins. Our first approach was to compare the supergene boundaries of MD haplotypes among the species. If there was one origin of the MD haplotype (Fig. 1; Q1H1), then all MD haplotypes would share haplotype boundaries. If there were multiple origins (Fig. 1; Q1H2), then the species with homologous MD haplotypes could have distinct haplotype boundaries from their nonhomologous counterparts. Our second approach was to use windowed trees to identify chromosome regions where the MD haplotypes of all species formed a clade. If MD arose once, then this topology would be common; if MD haplotypes originated multiple times, then this topology would be rare or absent. Second, we asked how MD haplotypes originate. Specifically, did they originate from a rearrangement on an ancestral M haplotype or form through recombination between the M and P haplotypes? We used a windowed phylogenetic approach to scan chromosome 3 for regions where P and MD haplotypes of the same species group form a clade. If the MD haplotype arose from a rearrangement on the M haplotype (Fig. 1; Q2H1), then we would expect this topology to be rare or absent. If MD haplotypes arose from recombination between M and P haplotypes (Fig. 1; Q2H2), then we would expect this topology to be more common. Regions that freely recombine within each species are expected to have topologies matching the species tree and to be concentrated outside the supergene region, a previously documented pattern in species with alternative social supergene haplotypes (Brelsford et al. 2020).

Results

The Nearctic MD haplotypes have distinct boundaries from the F. cinerea MD haplotype

The five Nearctic species, including two previously studied (F. glacialis and F. podzolica, Lagunas-Robles et al. 2021; Purcell et al. 2025) and three with previously undescribed supergene form and function (see Supplementary Materials text and Figures S1–S3), exhibit shared boundaries of their MD haplotypes. In all five species, both windowed PCAs and windowed FST plots showed that MA and MD are differentiated from 2 to 7.5 Mbp along chromosome 3 (Fig. 2A–2E), although the abundance of repetitive sequences in the region from 5.5 to 7.5 Mbp resulted in low-mapping quality (Figure S4), making it difficult to pinpoint the boundary. The F. cinerea MD haplotype, in contrast, had a reduced length, and spanned from 2.67 to 7.5 Mbp (Fig. 2F). This result is consistent with two independent origins of an MD associated with gyne production, one in the Nearctic lineage and one in the F. cinerea lineage (Fig. 1).

The left-hand boundary of the MD haplotype is estimated at 2 Mbp in pactrans (A–C) and glacpodz (D, E) and at 2.67 Mbp in F. cinerea (F). The right-hand boundary occurs at approximately 7.5 Mbp based on FST. For each species, we plotted PC1 from a windowed PCA along chromosome 3, with MA/MA individuals in green and MD/MA in purple. Percent variance explained (PVE) by PC1 is represented on a heatmap (blue = 0%, red = 75%) for each window. Windows with fewer than 25 SNPs were excluded from the PCA. For each species, genetic differentiation (FST) was estimated between MA/MA and MD/MA individuals. All plots used nonoverlapping 10-kbp windows.

Nearctic MD haplotypes rarely cluster with the F. cinerea MD haplotype in windowed tree analysis

To examine relationships across species among haplotypes, one of which (MD) almost always occurs in a heterozygous state, we inferred neighbor-joining trees based on population allele frequencies in 50-SNP windows along chromosome 3. Each tip in the phylogeny represented one supergene haplotype in one species (see Methods). We searched the resulting windowed trees for four patterns, which are not mutually exclusive: (topology #5) MD haplotypes of all six species cluster together, (topology #4) MD haplotypes of all five Nearctic species cluster together, (topology #3) MD haplotypes of the three pactrans species cluster together, and (topology #2) MD haplotypes of the two glacpodz species cluster together. We also searched for a fifth pattern (topology #1), where sequences cluster by species rather than by supergene haplotype, as expected in nonsupergene regions of the chromosome. The MD haplotypes of all species clustered together in very few windows (Fig. 3, topology #5, Table S4). In contrast, the MD haplotypes of the five Nearctic species clustered together in many windows between 2 and 7.5 Mbp, and even more windows showed clustering of the MD haplotypes within the two Nearctic clades. This pattern is consistent with two origins of the MD haplotype, one in the ancestor of the Nearctic species and one in the F. cinerea lineage (Fig. 1).

Windowed tree analysis suggests that MD haplotypes formed by recombination between M and P haplotypes

To assess whether the Nearctic MD is derived from a Nearctic P haplotype, we searched the windowed trees for patterns where the Nearctic MD haplotypes of all species clustered with the PL haplotype of F. podzolica (Fig. 4, topology #9), which is the only Nearctic P haplotype in our sample that spans both the left and right arms of chromosome 3. We also did a second comparison searching for patterns where the Nearctic MD haplotypes clustered with the F. cinerea P1 and P2 haplotypes (Fig. 4, topology #8), which are more distantly related but also span both arms of chromosome 3. Strikingly, the patterns matching these topologies were only found in the 2 to 7.5 Mbp region on chromosome 3, consistent with the hypothesis that the Nearctic MD is derived from recombination between the M and P haplotypes.

To assess whether the F. cinerea MD was derived from recombination between a M and a P haplotype as well, we searched for windows where the F. cinerea MD clustered with the F. cinerea P2 (Fig. 4, topology #7), or where the F. cinerea MD clustered with the F. cinerea P1 (Fig. 4, topology #6). We observed a higher density of windows where the MD clustered with the P1 within the 2.67–7.5 Mbp region, relative to the MD clustering with P2 in the same region, and relative to the MD and P1 clustering together outside of this chromosome region. This pattern suggests that the F. cinerea MD formed from recombination between a P1-like haplotype and an M haplotype after the divergence between the two F. cinerea P haplotypes, but it could also result from higher levels of gene flux between MD and P1 than between MD and P2. Because the focal clade in each of these searches involved two haplotypes within a single species, we expected these patterns to occur more often than any cross-species clustering, and to be distributed across the entire chromosome including outside of the supergene region.

To further check our alternate hypothesis that MD arose from rearrangements on an ancestral M haplotype, we also searched for windowed trees where MD and MA haplotypes from all six species form a clade, where the F. cinerea MD and MA haplotypes form a clade, and where all Nearctic MD and MA haplotypes form a clade (Figure S5). As expected, these topologies were most common in the 7.5 to 12.4 Mbp region of chromosome 3, where we observe no differentiation between MA and MD haplotypes within species (Fig. 2).

Discussion

On the origins of the MD haplotype and its link to an ancient queen number supergene

We find evidence for two origins of the MD haplotype—one origin shared across the Nearctic Formica species in this study and another in F. cinerea or an ancestor of this Palearctic species, hereafter called “the F. cinerea lineage” (Figs 2 and 3). All Nearctic species share boundaries of the MD haplotype on chromosome 3 at approximately 2 and 7.5 Mbp (Fig. 2A–E). In contrast, F. cinerea has MD haplotype boundaries at approximately 2.67 and 7.5 Mbp (Fig. 2F). This difference in the extent of parallel supergenes with shared function suggests that the functional genetic basis of gyne production lies within the shorter supergene region of F. cinerea. Additionally, the MD haplotypes of all five Nearctic species cluster together in a substantial number of windows (n = 233, Fig. 3, Table S4) within the MD haplotype boundaries. Within the boundaries of the F. cinerea MD haplotype, however, only two windows grouped the MD of all six species in the study (Fig. 3, Table S4).

Both origins of the MD haplotypes were apparently derived from a P haplotype. The Nearctic MD haplotypes group with the F. podzolica P haplotype over much of their length (Fig. 4, Table S5). Similarly, the F. cinerea MD haplotype grouped more often with one of the two F. cinerea P haplotypes than with the other, with a concentration of shared topology windows in the 2.67 to 7.5 Mbp region of chromosome 3 (Fig. 4, Table S5). This suggests that the F. cinerea MD haplotype formed more recently than the split between the F. cinerea P1 and P2 haplotypes.

One limitation of our study is having only one Palearctic species in our analyses. It is possible that the version of the MD haplotype found in the F. cinerea lineage is present in other Palearctic relatives. While a close Palearctic relative, F. selysi, exhibits split sex ratios (Rosset and Chapuisat 2006), no MD or other haplotype associated with gyne production has been described to date. Sampling additional Palearctic species will be necessary to unravel the history of the Palearctic MD haplotype.

A convergent recombinant origin of the MD haplotype

Recombination events between preexisting supergene haplotypes can generate additional haplotypes (De-Kayne et al. 2025). In the African monarch butterfly, Danaus chrysippus, color pattern is determined by six divergent haplotypes (De-Kayne et al. 2025). Ancestry and admixture patterns near or at the promoter region of two genes suggest that one of the haplotypes is a recombinant product of two preexisting haplotypes (De-Kayne et al. 2025). The recombinant origin of some African monarch butterfly supergene haplotypes could parallel the Formica MD haplotype, where a portion of a P haplotype likely recombined onto an M haplotype background (Figs 1 and 4). This led to two diverging M haplotypes, the proto-MD haplotype and the MA haplotype.

In ants, rare recombination events between supergene haplotypes likely contribute to phenotypic novelty in multiple systems. Interestingly, recombination between the M and the P haplotypes led to the movement of two P-specific genes onto an M haplotype background in F. aquilonia and F. paralugubris (Sigeman et al. 2025a). Sigeman et al. suggested that strong selection against the P haplotype could have favored the fixation of this recombinant M haplotype. One potential cause of selection against P haplotypes is genetic load, which is expected to accumulate on haplotypes experiencing little recombination. Purcell et al. (2025) found that the PL always occurred in a heterozygous state in F. podzolica, a pattern that would enable accumulation of recessive genetic load and could also be a consequence of preexisting load. Across the species investigated here, we note a near-absence of P/MD individuals as well as a paucity of MD/MD homozygotes. We speculate that the recombinant origin of the MD haplotype resulted in some of the P haplotype genetic load being moved onto the MD haplotype, resulting in deleterious P/MD and MD/MD combinations. Intriguingly, genetic load would not explain the rarity of P/MD genotypes in F. cinerea, since P/P homozygotes are common in that species (Scarparo et al. 2023). Likewise, it would not explain the rarity of PS/MD heterozygotes in F. glacialis, since the portions of the chromosome with putative genetic load are nonoverlapping (Lagunas-Robles et al. 2021; this study). Our understanding of the distribution of genotypes would be enhanced by additional research into the inner workings of sex ratio in polygyne colonies.

Conclusion

The evolutionary processes that lead to the diversification of autosomal supergenes can be difficult to disentangle as supergene haplotypes are often limited to a single species. Examining convergent polymorphisms can elucidate the evolutionary processes that lead to supergenes. For example, in anther-smut fungi, a “young” (0.5 to 3.2 million years) mating-type locus supergene expanded through convergent evolution of suppressed recombination at least nine times (Branco et al. 2018; Duhamel et al. 2022). Additionally, the presence of many haplotypes can help uncover how supergene systems diversify. Sometimes, existing haplotypes can recombine to form new haplotypes associated with new phenotypes (Hill et al. 2023; De-Kayne et al. 2025). Here, we find additional support for the formation of new haplotypes resulting from recombination between preexisting supergene haplotypes. We also show that a recombinant haplotype can shape a distinct function compared to its preexisting counterparts. In two separate lineages of Formica ants, recombination between the M and P haplotypes resulted in a new haplotype, the MD, that is associated with colony-level gyne production.

Materials and methods

A comprehensive version of the Materials and Methods can be found in the Electronic Supplemental Results, Materials, and Methods.

Sample collection, sequencing, and variant calling

In the present study, we investigated five Nearctic species, F. glacialis, F. podzolica, F. pacifica, F. transmontanis and F. cf. transmontanis, and a Palearctic species, F. cinerea (see Table S1 for overview of sampling per species, Table S2 for individual metadata, and Figure S6 for sample map). The MD haplotype was previously identified, and associated with monogyne gyne-producing colonies, in F. glacialis (Lagunas-Robles et al. 2021), F. podzolica (Lagunas-Robles et al. 2021), and F. cinerea (Scarparo et al. 2023). For F. glacialis, we combined new samples with a small panel of reference samples originally sequenced by Lagunas-Robles et al. (2021). For F. podzolica, we selected samples with MA, MD, and PL haplotypes from the broader sample set sequenced by Purcell et al. (2025). For F. cinerea, we carried out whole-genome sequencing on a targeted set of samples with MA, MD, P1, and P2 haplotypes that were previously genotyped with ddRADseq by Scarparo et al. (2023). We collected and sequenced new samples broadly distributed across the ranges of Formica pacifica, Formica transmontanis, and F. cf. transmontanis, and investigated the association between supergene haplotype and colony sex ratio in F. pacifica and F. transmontanis (Table S3).

We aligned reads for all 372 samples to the chromosome-level F. selysi genome assembly (Brelsford et al. 2020; NCBI accession GCA_009859135.1), called variants with bcftools version 1.16 (Li 2011), and filtered to retain high-quality SNPs using VCFtools version 0.1.17 (Danecek et al. 2011).

Species tree inference

To establish the species tree topology, we generated a maximum-likelihood tree from concatenated genome-wide SNP data using IQ-TREE v2.2.2.6 (Minh et al. 2020). We excluded chromosome 3, as trans-species variation on this chromosome would confound inference of the species tree (Brelsford et al. 2020). We selected a subset of 10 individuals with highest sequence depth to represent each of the six focal species (Table S2, Figure S7).

Identification of haplotypes on chromosome 3

To determine the supergene variation in each species, we used two principal component analyses per species implemented in PCAngsd version 1.10 (Meisner and Albrechtsen 2018), one on the left arm of chromosome 3 (2 to 7.5 Mbp) and one on the right arm of chromosome 3 (7.5 to 12.4 Mbp). Previous studies established that supergene variation can differ between the two arms of the supergene region, with MD restricted to the left arm and P haplotypes either restricted to the right arm or spanning the entire supergene region (Lagunas-Robles et al. 2021; Scarparo et al. 2023).

We confirmed the established genotypes for previously analyzed F. cinerea (Brelsford et al. 2020; Scarparo et al. 2023), F. podzolica (Lagunas-Robles et al. 2021; Purcell et al. 2025), and used previously analyzed reference samples of F. glacialis (Lagunas-Robles et al. 2021) to assign genotypes in newly sequenced individuals. In the three species of the pactrans clade, we identified two distinct genetic clusters on the left arm of chromosome 3. Estimates of inbreeding coefficient FIS for that region of the chromosome suggested that one of these clusters was made up of supergene heterozygotes, while the other cluster contained supergene homozygotes. Heterozygous individuals at this region were provisionally determined to be MD/MA individuals, pending confirmation with multi-species genomic analyses (see “Examining the evolutionary history of the MD haplotype using windowed phylogenies”) and assessment of colony sex ratios.

Estimating the MD haplotype boundaries within species

To estimate the MD haplotype boundaries, we used two parallel approaches. First, we visualized PC1 from windowed PCAs along chromosome 3 using WinPCA (Blumer et al. 2025). Second, we calculated windowed FST between the MD/MA and MA/MA individuals for each species using the site frequency spectrum in ANGSD version 0.937 (Korneliussen et al. 2014).

Establishing MD haplotype function in the pactrans clade

We assessed the association between the MD haplotype and gyne production, once for colonies where we collected ≥5 alates and again for a subset of colonies where we collected ≥10 alates. We genotyped 5 workers from each of these colonies using a SNP assay we designed to distinguish the MD haplotype from the MA haplotype in the pactrans clade. To test for an association between gyne production and the MD haplotype, we performed Fisher's exact tests for colonies where we collected ≥5 alates and for the more conservative subset of colonies.

Examining the evolutionary history of the MD haplotype using windowed phylogenies

We examined the evolutionary history of supergene haplotypes across species using windowed phylogenies on chromosome 3. Windowed phylogenies allow us to explore the relationships between the different haplotypes across the different parts of the chromosome and identify regions that are conserved between various groups of supergene haplotypes within or across species. We were inspired by TWISST (Martin and Van Belleghem 2017) and TWISST2 (Martin 2026) to use a windowed phylogenetic approach. However, we were limited in the approaches we could use because the MD haplotype and PL haplotype are only found in the heterozygous state in diploid females. While TWISST2 does not need phased data, it assumes interchangeability between 2 copies of the chromosome within each single diploid individual (Martin 2026), and is therefore not suitable for analysis of supergene heterozygotes.

To investigate the relationships among haplotypes, some of which only occur in a heterozygous state, we developed a custom approach. We aimed to infer a neighbor-joining tree based on population-level allele frequencies with each tip representing one supergene haplotype in one species (i.e. MA and MD haplotypes in all six species, the PL haplotype in F. podzolica, and the P1 and P2 haplotypes in F. cinerea, for a total of 15 tips per tree; see Figure S8). We estimated per-SNP allele frequencies in each species for each haplotype that occurred in a homozygous state using VCFtools. We then used the same approach to estimate the PL-specific allele frequency of each SNP in F. podzolica. We used these allele frequencies to produce a distance matrix for each nonoverlapping window of 50 SNPs along chromosome 3 using the “dist” function with the “method = euclidean” in R version 4.2.3 (R Core Team 2023), and used each distance matrix to produce a neighbor-joining tree in the R package APE version 5.7.1 (Paradis et al. 2004).

After retaining only SNPs that were sequenced in at least one individual for every combination of species × genotype, and excluding SNP windows in low-mapping-quality regions (Figure S4), we retained 6,605 out of 7,472 50-SNP windows (88.40%) for the analysis. Based on our research questions, we identified 12 distinct tree topologies of interest. The first was a baseline species tree, where the haplotypes of each species cluster together. This topology is expected to be common outside of the supergene region. Each of the other topologies of interest was defined by the presence of a single clade clustering haplotypes of interest together, to the exclusion of all other haplotypes in the analysis (the latter are labeled as “other haplotypes” in the tree topologies shown in Figs 3 and 4). Topologies 2 through 5 (Fig. 3) were used to investigate homology between MD haplotypes across species. We identified clustering of MD haplotypes in the glacpodz clade (topology #2), the pactrans clade (topology #3), all five Nearctic species (topology #4), and across all six investigated species (topology #5). Topologies 6 through 9 were used to investigate our hypothesis that the MD haplotype was derived from recombination between ancestral M and P haplotypes. We scanned the windowed trees for topologies where the F. cinerea MD clustered with the F. cinerea P1 (topology #6) or F. cinerea P2 (topology #7), where the Nearctic MD haplotypes clustered with the F. cinerea P1 and P2 haplotypes (topology 8), and where the Nearctic MD haplotypes clustered with the F. podzolica PL haplotype (topology #9). In addition, we identified windows where all MD and MA haplotypes cluster within species (topology #10), where the F. cinerea MD and MA haplotypes cluster (topology #11), and where the Nearctic MD and MA haplotypes cluster (topology #12). For each windowed tree, we checked for matches against each of the topologies of interest using the function “is.monophyletic” in APE, and plotted locations of windows matching each topology (Figs 3 and 4, Figures S5 and S8).
