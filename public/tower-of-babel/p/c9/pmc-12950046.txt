Fusarium enrichment alters the soil microbial community and network structure of Brassica crops
Authors: Zhi-Feng Zhang, Jun-Jie Hou, Jing-Wei Lin, Meng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil-borne microbial diseases constitute a significant constraint on crop production, causing substantial annual economic losses estimated at 40%–60% in agricultural sectors. This study aimed to identify the principal plant pathogens causing diseases in Brassica crops in Ningxia, China, and the factors and mechanisms of infection. Employing ITS and 16S rRNA gene high-throughput sequencing, we investigated the soil microbial community associated with both healthy and diseased Brassica crops, as well as other main crops. We detected significant abundances of the soil-borne pathogenic fungi Fusarium and Olpidium, with Fusarium markedly enriched in diseased soils, suggesting its potential role in pathogenesis. Furthermore, the disease presence induced substantial disturbance in the soil fungal community, whereas the prokaryotic community structure remained relatively unaffected. Spatial and environmental factors exerted a more pronounced influence on fungal communities than on prokaryotic communities, with disease incidence attenuating the impact of environmental variables on fungal assemblages. Further analysis revealed that soil properties positively drove Fusarium abundance, and this proliferation synergized with soil factors to reduce fungal evenness. This evenness loss, coupled with the pathogen’s direct effect, ultimately led to network simplification and a vulnerable “low evenness–high complexity” state. Network analyses confirmed this vulnerability, showing heightened complexity but diminished stability in fungal networks within diseased soils. Collectively, our findings provide a theoretical and technical framework for the rapid identification and effective management of pathogens affecting Brassica crops.Key points Fusarium is significantly and highly enriched in diseased Brassica soil.Fungal community exhibits distinct patterns in diseased Brassica soil.Diseased soil exhibits more complex, less stable fungal networks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13742-z.

Introduction

The relationship between microorganisms and crops is complex and nuanced, exerting a crucial influence on crop growth. For instance, plant growth-promoting microorganisms (PGPMs) can facilitate plant growth through diverse mechanisms, such as phytohormone secretion, phytopathogen inhibition, and improved soil nutrient conditions. Conversely, pathogenic microorganisms can induce plant diseases, adversely affecting crop development. Furthermore, certain soil microorganisms contribute to enhanced soil fertility and structural integrity, thereby influencing plant root growth and nutrient uptake (Berg 2009; Rodrigo et al. 2013). Microbial pathogens are a key factor impacting the healthy growth of crops, leading to yield reductions and diminished product quality (Dodds and Rathjen 2010).

Soil-borne transmission represents a pivotal route for the dissemination of plant pathogenic microorganisms, resulting in an estimated 40%–60% direct economic loss in agricultural production annually, with losses exceeding 80% in severely affected regions (Chen et al. 2022). Direct infection by soil-borne plant pathogens can significantly impede crop growth. For instance, the soil-borne pathogenic fungus Fusarium oxysporum initiates infection at the root tip, subsequently progressing to the immature xylem, leading to diseases such as root rot, wilt, and damping-off (Ekwomadu and Mwanza 2023). Concurrently, pathogen infection may suppress plant growth by diminishing the relative abundance of beneficial rhizosphere microorganisms or fostering the proliferation of detrimental rhizosphere bacteria (Yang et al. 2019). Notably, F. oxysporum infection disrupts rhizosphere microbial community structure and assembly, thereby adversely affecting host plant growth and health. However, the intricate interplay between rhizosphere microbial communities and pathogenic microorganisms remains largely ambiguous due to the complexity of soil biotic and abiotic factors (Wen et al. 2022a). Moreover, continuous cropping significantly exacerbates pathogen infection and crop disease incidence (Li et al. 2015; Yang et al. 2019).

Cruciferous vegetables, particularly those within the Brassica genus, constitute a substantial portion of China’s vegetable production, with an annual cultivation area of approximately 3.33 million hectares, representing roughly 20% of the nation’s total vegetable acreage. These crops face severe threats from microbial pathogens, including viral diseases, downy mildew, soft rot, black rot, and black spot, all of which are pathogen-induced. These diseases lead to substantial yield reductions in Brassica vegetables, with losses exceeding 50% and even resulting in complete crop failure in some regions (Ji 2021; Li 2017). During our field survey in Zhaolou Village, Ningxia Province, China, in the autumn of 2024, we observed a severe disease incidence in locally cultivated Brassica vegetables: Chinese cabbage, flat cabbage, and round cabbage (Fig. 1). However, local farmers were unfamiliar with the specific disease types and causative pathogens, resulting in a lack of targeted control measures. Furthermore, it was noted that within the same cultivation area, some plants remained healthy while others were severely infected, raising questions regarding the underlying factors contributing to such variability.Fig. 1Pictures for sampling. Given the established association between plant diseases caused by microbial pathogens and rhizosphere microbial communities, it was hypothesized that these vegetable diseases might be related to rhizosphere microbial dynamics. To investigate this, rhizosphere and non-rhizosphere soil samples were collected from healthy and diseased Chinese cabbage, flat cabbage, and round cabbage within the study area, as well as from rhizosphere and non-rhizosphere soil samples of adjacent healthy amaranth (Portulaca oleracea), maize, and potato. Utilize amplicon sequencing and analysis techniques, the objectives of this study are to (1) identify the pathogenic microorganisms responsible for vegetable diseases, providing effective control recommendations to farmers and (2) elucidate the factors influencing cabbage disease incidence from a microbial community perspective, thereby contributing to the theoretical foundation for high-quality green agriculture development.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and geochemical measurement

The samples in this study were collected from a vegetable cultivation area in Zhaolou Village, Liancai Town, Longde County, Guyuan City, Ningxia, China (altitude 1743.8–1756.6 m, 105.8872°–105.8933°E, 35.5692°–35.5656°N). Within the same area, healthy and diseased Chinese cabbage, flat cabbage, and round cabbage (collectively referred to as Brassica spp. when not specified separately) were selected, and rhizosphere and non-rhizosphere soil samples were collected from depths of 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm, respectively. Additionally, rhizosphere and non-rhizosphere topsoil samples were collected from adjacent areas of healthy amaranth (Portulaca oleracea), maize, and potato for comparative analysis. Rhizosphere and non-rhizosphere soil collection was conducted according to the methods described Edwards et al. (2015) and He et al. (2021) with slight modifications: For non-rhizosphere soil, after removing the 1–2 cm of topsoil, triplicate soil samples were collected and homogenized from depths of 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm at a distance of 10–15 cm from selected plants using a metal sampling shovel. For rhizosphere soil, selected healthy and diseased plants were excavated, and the entire root system was extracted; large pieces of soil loosely attached to the roots were gently removed and discarded; the roots were subsequently shaken within a self-sealing bag to dislodge tightly adhered soil, which constituted the rhizosphere soil sample; the remaining root systems were collected for root tissue analysis. With the exception of healthy and diseased vegetable root samples, for which only single replicates were collected, all other sample types were collected in duplicate. A total of 40 samples were collected, comprising 38 soil samples and 2 root samples. After collection, samples were sealed in sterile self-sealing bags, stored in refrigerated ice boxes, and immediately transported to the laboratory, where they were stored at −40 °C until further analysis.

Physicochemical parameter measurements, including pH, moisture content, total carbon (TC), total organic carbon (TOC), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), available nitrogen (AN), available phosphorus (AP), ammonium nitrogen (NH4+), and nitrate nitrogen (NO3−), were conducted by Nanjing Carvins Testing Technology (Nanjing, China). In brief, the soil pH was determined by using dried and ground samples with distilled water at a ratio of 1:2.5 (w/v) using a pH meter (FE20, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) (Sparks et al. 2020). TC, TOC, and TN contents were determined by a TOC analyzer (TOC-L, Shimadzu, Japan), potassium dichromate oxidation method, and Kjeldahl nitrogen determination method, respectively (Zhang et al. 2019). Further, N/NO3−, N/NH4+, AN, and TP levels were measured using a spectrophotometric method according to the National Environmental Protection Standards of the People’s Republic of China (http://www.mee.gov.cn/ywgz/fgbz/bz/bzwb/jcffbz/).

DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

Total DNA was extracted using the PowerMax soil kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The fungal ITS2 region and the prokaryotic 16S rRNA gene V4 region were amplified by PCR using the primer pairs gITS7ngs/ITS4ngs (Nilsson et al. 2019) and 515F/806R (Parada et al. 2016), respectively. The purified PCR products were subjected to PE250 paired-end high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina Novaseq sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The ITS2 and 16S rRNA gene amplicon generation and high-throughput sequencing were performed by MagiGene (Guangzhou, China).

Data processing

Due to the distinct taxonomic classification standards for fungi and prokaryotic microorganisms, different software and analytical strategies were employed to process the raw data of the fungal ITS2 and prokaryotic 16S rRNA gene fragments.

The raw data for fungal community was processed using the analysis pipeline described by Zhang et al. (2021). Initially, paired-end reads were merged and barcodes were removed using VSEARCH (Rognes et al. 2016). Quality control was performed using Trimmomatic (SLIDINGWINDOW: 5:30 MINLEN:200) (Bolger et al. 2014), and filtered sequences were then counted, dereplicated, sorted, clustered, and chimera removal using USEARCH and VSEARCH (Edgar 2010, 2013; Edgar et al. 2011), resulting in an operational taxonomic unit (OTU) table at 97% similarity. Subsequently, representative sequences were annotated using blastn (Camacho et al. 2009) search against the UNITE eukaryotes ITS database (Abarenkov et al. 2024), and the annotation results were calibrated according to the strategy proposed by Nilsson et al. (2019). Non-fungal sequences and singleton OTUs were removed from the taxonomic annotation and the OTU table, respectively, yielding the final fungal OTU abundance table and taxonomic information. Furthermore, the nutritional modes of the fungal OTUs were predicted using the FUNGuild software (Nguyen et al. 2016).

The raw data for 16S rRNA gene was processed using the QIIME2 software (Bolyen et al. 2019). Barcodes and primers were removed using cutadapt, and paired-end sequences were then merged. Data denoising was performed using the dada2 function to obtain amplicon sequence variant (ASV) representative sequences and generate the corresponding abundance table (Feature table). Representative sequences were annotated using the feature-classifier function against the SILVA database (V138.1) (Quast et al. 2013). Eukaryotic representative sequences and abundance data were removed, resulting in the final prokaryotic ASV (equivalent to fungal OTUs) abundance table and taxonomic information.

Bioinformatic analyses

Alpha and beta diversity

Prior to statistical analyses, the OTU/ASV table was rarefied to the lowest sequence count among samples, which were 17,357 for fungi and 18,971 for prokaryotic microorganisms, respectively. α-diversity indices, including OTU richness, Shannon index, and Simpson index, were calculated using the vegan package (Oksanen et al. 2019). A distance matrix of fungal and prokaryotic communities (Hellinger-transformed OTU abundance) was constructed using the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix, and principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was employed to compare compositional differences of microbial communities. The analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) was used to assess community differences between groups (p < 0.05).

Factors shaping microbial community structure

Variation partition analysis (VPA) using distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) was employed to determine the variation in fungal and prokaryotic communities explained by spatial (latitude, longitude, altitude, and depth, etc.) and environmental factors (pH, moisture content, TC, TOC, TN, TP, AN, AP, NH4+, and NO3−), respectively (Oksanen et al. 2019). To assess the influence of each factor on fungal and prokaryotic community structure separately, Mantel’s test was used to calculate the Spearman’s correlation coefficient between the Bray–Curtis distance of the microbial communities and the Euclidean distances of spatial and environmental factors (9,999 permutations). The results were visualized using the ggcor R package (Huang et al. 2020).

Differential microbial analysis

LEfSe analysis was utilized to identify fungal and prokaryotic microorganisms (i.e., biomarkers) exhibiting significant differences between healthy and diseased vegetable groups. The analysis involved the following steps: First, the Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test was employed to detect all feature species, identifying significantly different species by examining species abundance variations across groups. Subsequently, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was applied to determine whether all subspecies of the previously identified significantly different species converged within the same taxonomic level. Finally, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was conducted to identify the ultimate differential species (Segata et al. 2011).

Co-occurrence network analysis

Microorganisms always form complex interaction networks that are influenced by, and in turn, influence the growth status of host plants (Banerjee et al. 2018; Wen et al. 2022a). In this study, the 500 most abundant OTUs/ASVs from the fungal and prokaryotic communities were selected. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated using the ggClusterNet R package to determine co-occurrence relationships among these microorganisms (Wen et al. 2022b). Only correlation coefficients with an absolute value > 0.6 and p < 0.05 were considered reliable and retained for constructing the co-occurrence network. To understand the topological structure of the co-occurrence network, network properties such as average node connectivity, average path length, diameter, clustering coefficient, and modularity were calculated (Wen et al. 2022a). Network stability (resistance to perturbation) and robustness were assessed by randomly removing network nodes and critical nodes, respectively (Ning et al. 2020; Wen et al. 2022b).

Partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM)

To dissect the hierarchical relationships among environmental factors, Fusarium abundance, microbial community structure, and network topology, a PLS-SEM analysis was performed. Initially, correlation analysis and Mantel’s tests were employed to identify the key soil physicochemical parameters and microbial diversity indices significantly associated with Fusarium abundance and composition. Subsequently, a robust PLS-SEM model was constructed using the plspm command in R package plspm (Sanchez 2013). The model incorporated the identified significant physicochemical parameters, Fusarium abundance, microbial diversity, and network metrics. The analysis specified a bootstrap resampling number of 5000 and an inner weighting scheme of “path”. The overall model fit was then assessed using the Goodness-of-Fit (GoF) index.

Results

Physicochemical properties

The results of the soil physicochemical property measurements revealed variations among the soil physicochemical properties of different crop groups. However, most of these differences were not statistically significant, and the variations between different crops and different indicators were inconsistent (Table 1). The overall soil pH was alkaline (pH > 7), with potato exhibiting the lowest pH and Brassica vegetables exhibiting the highest. Amaranth showed the highest contents of TC, TOC, TN, TP, and AN. Chinese cabbage soil exhibited the highest AP content, potato soil exhibited the highest NO3− content, and flat cabbage soil exhibited the highest NH4+ content. Flat cabbage soil also showed relatively high levels of TC and TOC. In contrast, the soil in the maize cultivation area had the lowest nutritional component levels, except for TN and AN, which were slightly higher than those in the round cabbage cultivation area. No significant differences were observed in the other soil physicochemical properties between healthy and diseased Brassica vegetables, except for AP and NH4+. Taxonomic composition and survival strategies of microbial communities

In total, data analyses yielded 10,653 eukaryotic OTUs and 46,021 prokaryotic ASVs. After removing non-target annotation results, 4749 fungal OTUs and 45,996 prokaryotic ASVs were obtained. Prior to subsequent statistical analyses, the microbial OTU tables were rarefied to the lowest sequence count across all samples. The fungal samples were rarefied to 17,357 sequences, resulting in 3644 OTUs, and the prokaryotic community was rarefied to 18,971 sequences, resulting in 43,014 ASVs.

The 4749 fungal OTUs identified belonged to 15 phyla and 680 genera. The phyla with the highest diversity and relative abundance were Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, and Mortierellomycota (Fig. 2A). Notably, Mortierellomycota and Chytridiomycota exhibited a significantly higher relative abundance (23.92% and 10.47%, respectively) compared to their OTU proportions (3.81% and 5.69%, respectively). At the genus level, 12 genera had a relative abundance exceeding 1%, and 7 genera exceeded 2.5% (Fig. 2B), including Mortierella, Fusarium, Olpidium (formerly Olpidiaster), Solicoccozyma, Linnemannia, Aspergillus, and Chaetomium. Among these, Fusarium and Olpidium are widely known as soil-borne crop pathogens. Fusarium showed a significantly higher relative abundance in diseased Brassica soils (12.26%) compared to healthy Brassica soils (5.69%), maize cultivation areas (7.95%), and potato cultivation areas (4.09%) (p < 0.05). Conversely, Olpidium exhibited a higher relative abundance in healthy Brassica soils (14.51%) compared to diseased Brassica soils (1.00%). The prokaryotic ASVs obtained in this study comprised 487 archaeal OTUs (6.29% relative abundance) and 45,509 bacterial OTUs (93.71% relative abundance). The most abundant phyla were Proteobacteria (20.19%), Acidobacteriota (19.05%), Planctomycetota (15.07%), Chloroflexi (7.44%), Bacteroidota (6.96%), Gemmatimonadota (6.67%), and Crenarchaeota (5.91%) (Fig. 2C). Specifically, the relative abundance of Crenarchaeota (5.91%) was 12.4 times higher than its OTU proportion (218 OTUs, 0.47%). At the genus level, 12 genera had a relative abundance exceeding 1% (Fig. 2D), with Nitrososphaera (family Nitrososphaeraceae) being the most abundant (116 OTUs, 5.02% relative abundance).

Microbial community structure and influencing factors

Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity revealed that, although the percentages explained by the X and Y axes were relatively low, both fungal and prokaryotic communities generally clustered according to crop type (Fig. 3). This observation was further supported by PERMANOVA analysis (fungi: R = 0.404, p = 0.001; prokaryotes: R = 0.127, p = 0.009). In the PCoA plots, the percentages explained by the X and Y axes and the R value from PERMANOVA for the fungal community were higher than that for the prokaryotic community, indicating that the differences in fungal community structure among different crops were greater than those in prokaryotic community structure. PERMANOVA analysis showed significant differences in fungal community structure between diseased and healthy Chinese cabbage, round cabbage, and flat cabbage (p < 0.05). However, significant differences in prokaryotic community structure were observed only between healthy and diseased round cabbage, with no significant differences between healthy and diseased Chinese cabbage or flat cabbage. This suggests that disease incidence in Brassica vegetables significantly alters the fungal community structure of crop soils but has no significant impact on the prokaryotic community structure.Fig. 3PCoA plots of microbial communities. Variation partition analysis (VPA) revealed that spatial and environmental variables explained 28% of the variation in the fungal community across all crop soils and 18% of the variation in the fungal community of Brassica soils (Fig. 4A). However, spatial and environmental variations only explained 3% of the variation in the prokaryotic community across all crop soils and all Brassica soils (Fig. 4D). Consistent with the VPA results, Mantel’s test showed significant correlations between the overall soil fungal community structure and oxygen content, TC, TOC, TN, and AN. The Brassica fungal community exhibited significant correlations with TOC and AN (Fig. 4B and C). In contrast, the overall soil prokaryotic community showed a significant correlation only with TN content, and the Brassica soil prokaryotic community showed significant correlations only with AP and NH4+ (Fig. 4E and F). Co-occurrence networks and keystone species in microbial communities

Co-occurrence networks for fungal and prokaryotic communities in healthy and diseased Brassica soils were constructed using Spearman’s correlation-based analysis (Figure S1). The results revealed that the co-occurrence network of fungal communities in diseased Brassica soils exhibited a more complex topological structure than that of healthy Brassica soils, as evidenced by various network topological features, including the number of edges, connectivity, average node degree, average path length, and network diameter (Table 2). However, there was no consistent trend in the complexity of co-occurrence networks for prokaryotic communities between diseased and healthy Brassica soils. Furthermore, the stability of fungal and prokaryotic networks in diseased and healthy Brassica soils displayed completely opposite trends (Fig. 5A–H). Both weighted and unweighted network changes following random removal of network species and network module hubs indicated that the fungal network in healthy Brassica soils retained more network nodes, suggesting higher network stability in healthy Brassica soils compared to diseased Brassica soils. Conversely, after the same operations, the prokaryotic network in diseased Brassica soils retained more network nodes than the healthy network, indicating higher network stability in diseased Brassica soils. Multivariate associations among soil properties, Fusarium abundance, and fungal community structure

Informed by the linear relationships and Mantel’s correlations linking Fusarium abundance and composition with environmental factors and fungal diversity (Fig. 6A and S2), we utilized PLS-SEM to establish a hierarchical structural model. This analysis disentangled the multivariate associations among soil physicochemical properties, Fusarium abundance, fungal diversity, and network topology (Fig. 6B). Path analysis revealed that soil physicochemical properties (primarily driven by pH, TC, TN, and AN) were significant positive drivers of Fusarium abundance (β = 0.428, t = 2.27, p < 0.05), accounting for 18.3% of its variation. Regarding community diversity, the soil properties exhibited a marginally significant direct negative trend on fungal community evenness (assessed via the Shannon and Evenness indices) (β = −0.376, p = 0.059). Simultaneously, the Fusarium abundance exerted a marginally significant negative impact on fungal community evenness (β = −0.345, p = 0.073). Collectively, these results suggest that soil nutrient surplus and pathogen invasion constitute combined environmental pressures that reduce fungal community evenness, jointly accounting for 38.3% of the variation in fungal diversity.

In terms of network topology, the model uncovered a highly significant negative correlation between community evenness and network structure (β = −0.731, t = 3.40, p < 0.01). This strong relationship indicates that the loss of community evenness leads to significant and drastic alterations in the fungal network structure. Furthermore, increased Fusarium abundance directly resulted in significant simplification of the fungal network structure (β = −0.446, p < 0.05). Ultimately, the model explained 40.0% of the variation in network structure (R2 = 0.40), with community evenness serving as the primary predictor. Consequently, pathogen invasion not only directly simplifies the network structure via disruption but also indirectly forces the community toward a potentially vulnerable state of “low evenness–high complexity” by rapidly reducing diversity evenness.

Discussion

In this study, the soil microbial community, including taxonomic composition, structural characteristics, and network organization, was analyzed in healthy and diseased Brassica vegetables and other crops in Ningxia, China. Consequently, the pathogenic microbial agents associated with Brassica vegetable diseases were identified, and the contributing factors to disease infection were elucidated, thereby providing practical control recommendations for farmers to enhance crop yield and increase farmer’s income.

Fusarium emerges as the potential pathogen causing Brassica disease

Soil-borne diseases pose a severe threat to cruciferous vegetables. In this study, extremely high abundances of Fusarium fungi were found in all samples, particularly in the soil samples of diseased vegetables, where the average relative abundance of Fusarium reached 12.26%. Fusarium, widely distributed in nature, is recognized as one of the most significant plant pathogenic fungi, capable of causing various plant diseases and inflicting devastating damage on most agricultural crops (Jelínek et al. 2019). It has been reported that Fusarium fungi are important pathogens of Brassica vegetables, with species such as Fusarium oxysporum, F. equisetti, and F. solani known to cause diseases like brown spot in cabbage vegetables (Garibaldi et al. 2006; Ji 2021; Li 2017; Liu et al. 2017). In this study, the relative abundances of unknown Fusarium spp., F. solani, and F. oxysporum in the soil of diseased Brassica vegetables were 10.46%, 0.59%, and 0.71%, respectively. The extremely high Fusarium content in diseased Brassica vegetable soil and the vegetable disease symptoms, which closely resemble wilt, strongly suggest that these vegetable diseases are likely caused by Fusarium wilt.

Furthermore, a high abundance of Olpidium brassicae (formerly Olpidiaster brassicae) was found in all Brassica soils, with relative abundances significantly higher than those in maize and potato cultivation areas (<0.05%). O. brassicae, a fungus belonging to the Olpidium in Chytridiomycota, produces flagellated zoospores and is an obligate plant parasite, typically parasitizing the root epidermal cells of cruciferous plants (Lay et al. 2018). However, O. brassicae is generally not considered to directly cause cruciferous crop diseases but rather acts as a vector for various plant viruses, such as Lettuce big vein virus (LBVV) (Campbell 1996; Rochon et al. 2004). The disease symptoms caused by LBVV and other viruses do not match those observed in the vegetables in this study. Additionally, the relative abundance of O. brassicae was found to be significantly higher in healthy Brassica soils than in diseased Brassica soils, indicating that O. brassicae is not a pathogen of diseased Brassica in this study area. This contrasting distribution pattern with Fusarium may be explained by two factors: First, O. brassicae typically parasitizes the root epidermal cells and root hairs of cruciferous plants, while pathogenic Fusarium species usually infect other plant parts through the root tip, causing root curling, wilting, and the appearance of lesions and necrosis on the roots (Ekwomadu and Mwanza 2023). This may lead to the failure of O. brassicae parasitism, thereby reducing its abundance. Second, the infection of one pathogen may inhibit the infection of other pathogens through the secretion of antimicrobial substances or other metabolites (Abdullah et al. 2017; Ferreira et al. 2007).

Network analysis indicated that the fungal network in diseased Brassica soils was more complex but less stable than that in healthy Brassica soils. While more complex networks are generally considered more stable, enhanced interactions between species can lead to instability in microbial interactions. Conversely, weakening these interactions often promotes community stability, suggesting that weak interactions can form more stable ecological networks than strong interactions (Coyte et al. 2015; Su et al. 2023). In this study, the increased abundance of Fusarium in diseased Brassica soils may have enhanced the interaction strength between Fusarium and other fungi, increasing network complexity but reducing network stability. In the prokaryotic network of healthy Brassica soils, ASV_671 and ASV_772, belonging to Pyrinomonadaceae, had the highest hub scores and were identified as keystone species. Pyrinomonadaceae is an important plant probiotic with a high frequency of genes involved in plant growth-promoting traits (PGPT), such as nitrogen fixation, phosphate solubilization, exopolysaccharide production, siderophore production, and plant hormone production (Gonçalves et al. 2024). Additionally, Pyrinomonadaceae (e.g., RB41) may play a crucial role in soil carbon cycling and nutrient uptake (Stone et al. 2021; Wilhelm et al. 2022). The relative abundance of Pyrinomonadaceae was slightly higher in healthy Brassica soils than in diseased Brassica soils. Therefore, it is speculated that the abundance or metabolism of Pyrinomonadaceae may play some roles in the infection of Brassica pathogens.

Soil properties-driven pathogen invasion and loss of community diversity

Our PLS-SEM analysis highlights the pivotal role of the soil chemical environment in driving microbial community restructuring. It demonstrated that nutrient enrichment, particularly in carbon and nitrogen, established a permissive environment for Fusarium proliferation (Yang et al. 2024). This observation strongly supports the copiotrophic life strategy hypothesis for Fusarium, where resource-rich conditions destabilize microbial competition and facilitate the rapid expansion of pathogenic taxa (Bonanomi et al. 2010; Ho et al. 2017; Orr and Nelson 2018; Wei et al. 2015).

More importantly, the model uncovered a distinct “soil-pathogen-diversity” cascading effect. The inverse relationship between Fusarium abundance and fungal evenness suggests that pathogen invasion triggers competitive exclusion, whereby Fusarium monopolizes ecological niches in nutrient-replete soils and inhibits subordinate fungi, effectively eroding community evenness (Orr and Nelson 2018; van Elsas et al. 2012; Wei et al. 2015; Xiong et al. 2017). This diversity loss, resulting from combined nutrient and pathogen pressures, poses a potential threat to the functional redundancy of the soil ecosystem (Delgado-Baquerizo et al. 2016; Wagg et al. 2014).

Another significant finding is the strong negative correlation between fungal community evenness and network complexity (Fig. 6B) (Faust et al. 2015). This microbial community shift is frequently observed following the invasion of plant pathogens (Hu et al. 2020; Li et al. 2022). Specifically, pathogen infection often leads to a decrease in community evenness while simultaneously increasing the complexity of the microbial network, compared to the microbiota in a healthy soil environment. The underlying ecological mechanisms for this seemingly paradoxical outcome may involve two competing dynamics: enhanced interactions with the pathogen that facilitate its colonization, or the recruitment of beneficial microbes that work to bolster the community’s resistance against the pathogen (Hu et al. 2020; Li et al. 2022).

Conclusion

This study investigated soil microbial communities in a vegetable cultivation area in Ningxia, China, identifying high abundances of the soil-borne pathogenic fungi, Fusarium spp. and Olpidium brassicae. We observed that Fusarium spp. abundance was significantly higher in diseased Brassica soils compared to healthy soils, while O. brassicae exhibited an opposite distribution. It strongly suggests that Fusarium species are primarily associated with the observed Brassica diseases. Disease incidence significantly altered the soil fungal community structure but had a minimal impact on the prokaryotic community structure. Consistently, fungal communities were strongly affected by both spatial and environmental factors, whereas prokaryotic communities were less affected. Notably, pathogen infection reduced the overall influence of environmental factors on the fungal community and altered the complexity and stability of both fungal and prokaryotic networks. The PLS-SEM analysis revealed that soil properties positively drove Fusarium abundance, and this proliferation synergized with soil factors to reduce fungal evenness, which ultimately led to network simplification and a vulnerable “low evenness–high complexity” state.

In summary, Fusarium pathogens are likely key targets for disease control in Brassica vegetables within the Guyuan region of Ningxia. Accordingly, an integrated management strategy is recommended for Brassica cultivation: (1) Physical control measures, such as reduced crop rotation, removal of plant debris, and reduced flood irrigation; (2) Biological and chemical controls, including disease-resistant varieties, biocontrol agents (e.g., Trichoderma harzianum), and chemical fungicides (e.g., prochloraz and tebuconazole). Furthermore, to achieve the accurate pathogens and provide robust support for disease control, future high-throughput sequencing researches should integrate with traditional strain isolation and subsequent pathological validation.
