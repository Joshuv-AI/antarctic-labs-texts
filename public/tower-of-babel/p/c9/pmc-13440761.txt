Two-state resonance deep-red narrowband OLED emitters
Authors: Xinyu Wang, Hanlin Gan, Yahuan Lai, Jinpu Lei, Ling Lin, Jiangbo Liu, Donghao Wen, Wenle Tan, Bohan Wang, Yue Yu, Yuguang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wide color gamut Organic Light Emitting Diode (OLED) materials require a small full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) to achieve superior display performance, and a high molar extinction coefficient (ε) is beneficial to improving energy transfer efficiency and further reducing power consumption. Merocyanine derivatives can regulate the two-state resonance between neutral and zwitterionic states through donor–acceptor modulation, and the resonance intensity can be quantified by the combination coefficient c2 parameter. A c2 = 0.5 corresponds to nearly degenerate two-state energy levels, where strong resonance suppresses ground-state energy dissipation, promotes strong emission, and simultaneously realizes high ε and small FWHM. Herein, relying on the two-state resonance theory, we select xanthene as the core chromophore and phenylamine as the donor, and further construct three novel narrowband-emission molecules (2EtN-O-2CN, 2EtN-O-S, and 2EtN-O-OCN) with different electron-withdrawing acceptors, which are different from boron-nitrogen materials. With the gradual increase of acceptor strength, the calculated c2 values of the three molecules are 0.40, 0.45, and 0.46, respectively. Photophysical results demonstrate that 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN with strong resonance exhibit a high ε of approximately 105 L mol−1 cm−1 at the absorption peak and a small FWHM of 0.12 eV, much better than 2EtN-O-2CN (FWHM = 0.19 eV, ε = 5 × 104 L mol−1 cm−1). Impressively, this type of non-multiple-resonance material can achieve efficient deep-red emission at relatively low molecular weights below 465. The 2EtN-O-S-based OLED achieves deep-red emission at 712 nm with a small FWHM of 0.16 eV. This work reveals the great potential of two-state resonance emitters for high-efficiency narrowband deep-red OLEDs.

Introduction

Fluorescent materials featuring small full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) have emerged as promising candidates for organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), photovoltaic devices,1 optical sensors,2 and other optoelectronic applications.3 In OLED systems, narrowband emission is closely associated with superior color purity, which is indispensable for the development of ultra-high-definition displays.4,5 Nevertheless, conventional organic luminophores are predominantly constructed with fused aromatic skeletons, which inherently suffer from intensive high-frequency vibration of C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> C double bonds. These molecular vibrational modes lead to energy dissipation, thereby inducing spectral broadening and diminishing molar extinction coefficients (ε).6–8 When used as a guest material, the low light absorption capacity not only impairs efficient energy transfer, but also the high doping concentration adversely affects the charge transport properties of the host material.

As a typical class of polyacetylene-based dyes, cyanine dyes are structurally composed of a central conjugated methine bridge with an odd number of carbon atoms and two terminal nitrogen-containing heterocycles, in which a positive charge is delocalized over the entire conjugated backbone.9 Hush et al. demonstrated that cyanine dyes possess two energetically degenerate resonance configurations owing to their unique molecular structures. This strongly resonant electronic structure enables uniform bond length distribution and alternating charge polarization along the conjugated skeleton, endowing the materials with soliton excitation characteristics.10,11 Spectroscopically, cyanine dyes exhibit a high ε and small FWHM. This ideal resonant form is defined as the cyanine limit.12–14

Within the framework of organic resonance theory, when a molecule, ion, or radical can be depicted by two or more classical valence-bond structures differing solely in electron distribution, its actual structure exists as a resonance hybrid of these canonical forms. In contrast to symmetric conventional cyanine dyes, merocyanines adopt a characteristic planar three-component skeleton containing electron-donating moieties (D), polymethine bridges (π-bridge), and electron-accepting groups (A), as depicted in Fig. 1.15 Brooker et al. demonstrated that the resonance characteristics of merocyanines can be described by two limiting resonance configurations: a neutral aromatic form (N) and a charge-separated zwitterionic form (Z). This theoretical paradigm, which elucidates the photophysical properties of molecules via two predominant electronic states, is defined as the two-state model.16–18 In the electroluminescence field, donor–acceptor (D–A) structured molecules typically exhibit obvious spectral red-shift and broadening, originating from the introduction of the intramolecular charge-transfer (CT) state.19–21 By contrast, merocyanine dyes are capable of tuning molecular resonance structures via rational D–A modulation. For molecules with strong resonance characteristics, effective spectral narrowing can be achieved in D–π–A systems together with a high ε, which is conducive to boosting the energy transfer efficiency in electroluminescent devices. The molecular resonance strength can be qualitatively evaluated by the two-state combination coefficient c2, which corresponds to the contribution ratio of the Z structure.22 When the N (c2 = 0) and Z (c2 = 1) resonance structures undergo energetic degeneracy, a strong resonance effect emerges, and the molecule reaches the cyanine limit. In this case, the c2 value is equal to 0.5. In rational molecular design, favorable energy matching between D and A enables strong intramolecular resonance and drives the molecular system toward the cyanine limit.24–26 Due to the bond length homogenization of the skeleton, the electron-vibration coupling of high-frequency vibrations is significantly suppressed, which is conducive to inhibiting the shoulder peaks and narrowing the emission spectrum. As shown in Fig. 1, molecular vibrational transitions can be analogized to the simple harmonic vibration of a spring oscillator. Molecules endowed with robust resonance features exhibit high ε, analogous to harmonic oscillators possessing elevated force constants. Upon electronic excitation, the molecules with strong resonance structure exhibit a slight structural difference between S0 and S1. In accordance with the Franck–Condon principle, a smaller structural difference between two electronic states leads to a shorter equilibrium internuclear distance. This obviously suppresses the high-order 0–v′ vibronic transitions and greatly increases the relative intensity of the 0–0 electronic transition, which enables narrow spectra.27–29 On the contrary, molecules with weak resonance exhibit obvious structural differences between the S0 and S1 states, along with a low ε. Structural rearrangement during excited-state relaxation will cause additional energy dissipation, which results in significant spectral broadening and reduces the light absorption performance.

Based on merocyanine, its narrowband emission characteristic is strongly correlated with the two-state resonance feature. Similarly, for multiple-resonance (MR) materials, the two-state resonance of B–N bonds can be regarded as the core origin of spectral narrowing.30–32 Extensive peripheral group substitution serves to further modulate the resonance configuration. Accordingly, the introduction of excessively strong D and A substituents weakens the molecular resonance strength, resulting in obvious spectral broadening.33,34 Furthermore, D–A resonance interactions are not confined to B–N pairs. Other feasible resonance modes, such as the electronic coupling between N-based groups and carbonyl moieties, can also be adopted. Herein, three novel narrow-emission materials with molecular architectures distinct from conventional MR systems were rationally designed and synthesized, namely 2EtN-O-2CN, 2EtN-O-S, and 2EtN-O-OCN. All three molecules adopt a xanthene moiety as the central core, with diethylamine serving as the electron-donating group. The electron-accepting units are malononitrile, thiobarbituric acid, and 3-(dicyanomethylene)indanone, respectively. Benefiting from the varied acceptor structures, these three emitters exhibit distinguishable resonance configurations and divergent photophysical properties. The c2 of 2EtN-O-2CN is 0.40, while those of 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN rise to 0.45 and 0.46, respectively. This result confirms that the latter two molecules exhibit significantly enhanced intramolecular resonance strength. Accordingly, 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN deliver the narrower FWHMem of 50 nm/0.12 eV and 60 nm/0.12 eV, respectively, and higher ε on the order of 105 L mol−1 cm−1 at 679 nm and 757 nm, respectively, better than 2EtN-O-2CN (FWHMem = 65 nm/0.19 eV, ε = 5 × 104 L mol−1 cm−1 at 608 nm), which is beneficial to improving the energy conversion efficiency. Furthermore, the emitter with enhanced intramolecular resonance exhibits better OLED device performance. The FWHM of OLEDs based on 2EtN-O-S (66 nm/0.16 eV at 712 nm) is smaller than that of 2EtN-O-2CN (64 nm/0.18 eV at 656 nm), which further verifies that strong resonance states enable narrowband emission in OLEDs.

Results and discussion

Guided by the two-state resonance theory, a series of novel D–π–A narrowband emission materials, which are structurally distinct from conventional MR systems, were rationally designed and synthesized. The xanthene bearing heteroatomic rings were selected as the π-bridge, primarily for two reasons. On the one hand, the incorporation of heteroatoms reduces the overall aromaticity of the heteroanthracene skeleton compared with conventional fused aromatic rings, facilitating the formation of quinoid-like configurations. On the other hand, the relatively planar conformation of heteroanthracene enhances electron delocalization. Both factors help diminish the energy difference between neutral and ionic resonance structures.

To identify appropriate energy levels that can achieve optimal D–A energy matching and thus strong intramolecular resonance, diethylamine was employed as the D moiety in view of the excellent electron-donating ability of nitrogen atoms, and three distinct A units were selected, namely malononitrile, thiobarbituric acid, and 3-(dicyanomethylene) indanone. In accordance with this molecular design strategy, three target molecules, designated as 2EtN-O-2CN, 2EtN-O-S, and 2EtN-O-OCN, were successfully synthesized. Differences in the electron-withdrawing capability of the A result in varying intramolecular resonance strengths among the three molecules. The synthetic routes and molecular structures of the target compounds are illustrated in Fig. 2a. All three compounds were prepared through the Knoevenagel condensation reaction between the precursor 2EtN-O and the corresponding acceptors in ethanol as the solvent. The final products were thoroughly characterized by 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectroscopy. The 1H NMR spectra are provided in Fig. S1–S3, and the 13C NMR spectra are shown in Fig. S4–S6, confirming the high purity of the as-prepared materials. Further details regarding theoretical calculations and experimental instrumentation are available in SI S1. Their single-crystal structures are depicted in Fig. 2b. The CCDC numbers of 2EtN-O-2CN, 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN are 2551828, 2551829, and 2551830, respectively. The torsion angles of the D and A units relative to the central conjugated plane for 2EtN-O-2CN are measured to be 4.13° and 7.49°, respectively. For 2EtN-O-S, the corresponding torsion angles are 5.64° and 5.10°, while those of 2EtN-O-OCN are only 0.36° and 1.01°. Collectively, all three molecules exhibit small torsion angles and favorable planarity. Satisfactory molecular planarity endows the system with efficient electron delocalization, which is conducive to establishing strong intramolecular resonance structures.

The photophysical, thermal, and electrochemical properties of the three emitters are summarized in Table 1. The thermal decomposition temperatures (Td) of 2EtN-O-2CN, 2EtN-O-S, and 2EtN-O-OCN are 242 °C, 227 °C, and 229 °C, in Fig. S7, respectively. The electrochemical energy levels of the emitters were determined via cyclic voltammetry (CV), and the corresponding CV curves are provided in Fig. S8. Since all three molecules share the same electron-donating moiety, their highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy levels are comparable, with values of −5.01 eV, −5.02 eV, and −4.95 eV, respectively. With the variation of electron-accepting units, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of 2EtN-O-2CN, 2EtN-O-S, and 2EtN-O-OCN decrease sequentially, being −3.38 eV, −3.67 eV, and −3.78 eV. This trend is attributed to the gradual enhancement of the electron-withdrawing capability of the acceptor units. Consequently, the electron-accepting strength follows the descending order: 3-(dicyanomethylene)indanone > thiobarbituric acid > malononitrile. Frontier molecular orbitals and electron–hole distributions were theoretically calculated based on density functional theory (DFT) at the BMK-D3(BJ)/6-311G(d,p) level of theory. Frontier orbital analysis was conducted using the Multiwfn program, while isosurface plots were generated with the VMD software.35–37 As illustrated in Fig. 3b, the three emitters exhibit analogous frontier molecular orbital distributions: HOMO and LUMO electron densities are predominantly localized on the central xanthene scaffold, displaying an alternating distribution pattern. In the HOMO, the electron density is mainly distributed over the diethylamine D unit, whereas in the LUMO, the electron clouds are mainly distributed on the xanthene core and the acceptor unit. As the electron-withdrawing ability of the acceptor increases, the electron density further migrates from the diethylamine group to the acceptor moiety, gradually exhibiting more pronounced CT characteristics. The electron–hole distributions of the three emitters are highly consistent with their respective HOMO and LUMO distributions, as shown in Fig. S9.

Variations in acceptor moieties induce significant differences in the intramolecular resonance strength of the three emitters. These discrepancies can be quantitatively verified by the c2 parameter, which further facilitates the rational prediction of photophysical properties. Detailed parameters and theoretical calculation methods for c2 are summarized in Table S1. 2EtN-O-2CN exhibits c2 of 0.40 in Fig. 3a, relatively far from 0.5, implying a higher energy of the Z structure and a correspondingly weaker resonance intensity. Conversely, 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN exhibit c2 of 0.45 and 0.46, respectively, which are closer to 0.5 than that of 2EtN-O-2CN. As the electron-withdrawing capability of the A increases, the energy of the Z structure decreases, narrowing the energy gap between the N and Z structures. This drives the molecular resonance to gradually approach the cyanine limit, thereby forming a robust intramolecular resonance structure. In addition, to experimentally verify that 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN possess enhanced resonance character, we calculated the bond length alternation (BLA) values of the three molecules, with detailed data provided in Fig. S10. 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN exhibit QBLA values closer to 0 compared with 2EtN-O-2CN (3.2 pm), which are 3.1 pm and 2.5 pm, respectively. This experimental evidence further verifies that 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN possess stronger resonance structures, which are directly reflected in the corresponding absorption and emission characteristics. The absorption and emission spectra of 2EtN-O-2CN, 2EtN-O-S, and 2EtN-O-OCN in DCM are shown in Fig. 3c and d. Their maximum absorption wavelengths sequentially redshift to 608 nm, 679 nm, and 757 nm, and their corresponding ε are 49 900 L mol−1 cm−1, 115 500 L mol−1 cm−1, and 103 200 L mol−1 cm−1, respectively. As the enhancement of resonance strength, their absorption spectra are gradually narrowing. Meanwhile, 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN both possess ε on the order of 105 L mol−1 cm−1, which is beneficial to improving the energy conversion efficiency. Correspondingly, the FWHMs of the emission spectra of 2EtN-O-2CN, 2EtN-O-S, and 2EtN-O-OCN narrow progressively, decreasing from 65 nm/0.19 eV for 2EtN-O-2CN to 0.12 eV (50 nm and 60 nm) for 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN. The reorganization energy and Huang–Rhys factor of the three emitters were also theoretically calculated at the BMK-D3(BJ)/6-311G(d,p) level of theory. As presented in Fig. S11, compared with 2EtN-O-2CN, the vibrational contributions of 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN in the wavenumber range of 1000–1500 cm−1 are remarkably weakened. These vibrational modes are assigned to the CC stretching and scissoring bending vibrations of aromatic rings and conjugated backbones. Benefiting from the more robust resonance structures of 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN, their bond lengths become more homogenized, which suppresses the aforementioned vibrational motions and ultimately leads to narrowband emission. Besides, the reorganization energy of 2EtN-O-OCN is 443.5 cm−1, which is lower than those of 2EtN-O-2CN (943.5 cm−1) and 2EtN-O-S (564.5 cm−1). This result indicates that high-frequency vibrations are suppressed with increasing resonance strength. Accordingly, 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN exhibit the smaller structural discrepancy between the ground and excited states, which is more favorable for the occurrence of the 0–0 electronic transition. These theoretical findings are in good agreement with the experimental spectral data.

Besides D–A strength, molecular resonance intensity is also affected by the environment, like solvent polarity. In comparison with the N structure, the Z structure can be greatly stabilized in polar solvents. To further evaluate the effect of environmental polarity on molecular resonance structures, four solvents with different polarities (HEX, EA, DCM, and ACN) were selected. Their absorption spectra were systematically measured and analyzed, as illustrated in Fig. 4a–c. For all three emitters, the absorption maxima exhibit a distinct redshift trend with increasing solvent polarity, accompanied by noticeable changes in spectral shape. With increasing solvent polarity, the absorption maximum of 2EtN-O-2CN redshifts from 574 nm to 603 nm, accompanied by a diminished vibrational shoulder and intensified 0–0 vibronic peak. Consequently, 2EtN-O-2CN shows broad absorption (FWHM ≈ 110 nm/0.45 eV) and a low ε of merely 5 × 104 L mol−1 cm−1 in Fig. 4d. Based on resonance theory, a schematic diagram depicting the two-state resonance configurations of 2EtN-O-2CN is presented in Fig. 4g. High solvent polarity lowers the energy of the Z structure, reducing the N/Z energy gap. Nevertheless, a distinct energy difference remains even in ACN, leading to weak overall intramolecular resonance.

For 2EtN-O-S, the absorption maximum shifts from 637 nm to 672 nm with increasing solvent polarity. In contrast to 2EtN-O-2CN, the vibrational shoulder peaks in the absorption spectrum of 2EtN-O-S are distinctly attenuated, accompanied by significant spectral narrowing. During this process, the energy levels of the neutral N and Z structures gradually converge, leading to a reduction in the FWHM from 118 nm (0.40 eV) in HEX to 46 nm (0.12 eV) in DCM. This trend is also manifested in the ε. As illustrated in Fig. 4e, 2EtN-O-S achieves its maximum ε in DCM, reaching 1.15 × 105 L mol−1 cm−1, which is substantially higher than that of 2EtN-O-2CN (5 × 104 L mol−1 cm−1). This finding confirms that 2EtN-O-S possesses a stronger intramolecular resonance structure. As seen in Fig. 4h, with a further increase in solvent polarity, the Z structure becomes energetically favorable over the N structure, causing the molecule to deviate from the cyanine limit and thus resulting in spectral broadening in ACN.

2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S exhibit relatively high fluorescence quantum yields in the deep-red region, which are 11.15% and 6.59%, respectively. Due to its deep-red emission, valid quantum yield data for 2EtN-O-OCN is 1.18%. Transient fluorescence measurements reveal no long-lived emission component for all three molecules (Fig. S12). In addition, no phosphorescence signal is observed in the low-temperature spectra (Fig. S13). The singlet and triplet energy levels of the three target molecules were obtained via theoretical calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) at the BMK-D3(BJ)/6-311G(d,p) level of theory. As summarized in Table S2, all of them possess large ΔEST (>0.2 eV). These results collectively confirm that the three molecules do not exhibit typical thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) characteristics.

Benefiting from their superior solubility properties, the corresponding devices were fabricated via a solution-processing method to evaluate the electroluminescent properties of these materials. 2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S were employed as guest emitters to fabricate ternary sensitized OLEDs. The device architecture and molecular structures are illustrated in Fig. 5a, with the complete structure as follows: ITO/PEDOT: PSS (30 nm)/PF8Cz (20 nm)/PVK (10 nm)/EML (20 nm)/SPPO13 (30 nm)/LiF (1 nm)/Al (100 nm). PEDOT: PSS served as the hole-injection layer, while PF8Cz and PVK functioned as hole-transporting layers.

(a) The device architecture and molecular structures; (b) the normalized electroluminescence spectra and the CIE coordinates of 2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S; the current density–voltage–luminance (J–V–L) and the current efficiency-luminance-power efficiency (CE-L-PE) curves of 2EtN-O-2CN (c) and 2EtN-O-S (d); (e) the efficiency-luminance (EQE-L) curve of 2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S.

In accordance with the spectral overlap criterion, 4CzIPN and Ir(piq)2(acac) were selected as the host and sensitizer for device fabrication, respectively (Fig. S14). By integrating thin-film characterization data of 2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S measured at a range of doping concentrations alongside a full evaluation of device electroluminescence performance, we identified the optimal doping fractions: 1 wt% for 2EtN-O-2CN and 3 wt% for 2EtN-O-S (Fig. S15 and Table S3). The two emitters were separately co-doped into the corresponding sensitizer to fabricate the emissive layers (EMLs). Thus, the EML of 2EtN-O-2CN was composed of 4CzIPN and 2EtN-O-2CN, doped at weight ratios of 99% and 1%, respectively. For 2EtN-O-S, the EML consisted of Ir(piq)2(acac) and 2EtN-O-S doped at weight ratios of 97% and 3%, respectively. LiF and Al were used as the electron-injection layer and cathode, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5b, devices based on 2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S both exhibited deep-red narrowband emission, with emission peaks at 656 nm and 712 nm, FWHM of 64 nm (0.18 eV) and 66 nm (0.16 eV), and CIE coordinates of (0.61, 0.37) and (0.67, 0.31), respectively. Such a narrow emission spectrum is competitive with those of deep-red OLEDs emitting beyond 680 nm (Table S4). As shown in Fig. 5c and d, the maximum luminance of OLEDs based on 2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S are 293.80 cd m−2 and 62.36 cd m−2, respectively. The current efficiency-luminance-power efficiency (CE-L-PE) curves of 2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S show that the CEmax are 9.83 cd A−1 and 8.86 cd A−1, respectively. The PEmax of 2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S are 0.48 lm W−1 and 0.01 lm W−1, respectively. Besides, the devices based on 2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S achieved EQEmax of 2.47% and 0.46% in Fig. 5e. These results confirm that the new narrowband-emissive materials designed on the basis of the two-state model and resonance theory are highly promising for OLED applications. In addition, in contrast to conventional fluorescent emitters, 2EtN-O-2CN and 2EtN-O-S exhibit deep-red narrowband emission while possessing relatively low molecular weights of 331 and 465, respectively. This distinctive property originates from their strong resonance framework and endows them with prominent structural advantages.

Conclusions

In summary, conventional D–A molecules typically exhibit broad emission spectra, by introducing appropriate resonance modes and energy-matched D and A units, we designed and synthesized three novel D–π–A narrowband emitters, namely 2EtN-O-2CN, 2EtN-O-S, and 2EtN-O-OCN, which are distinct from conventional MR systems. Their resonance structures were evaluated using the two-state combination coefficient c2. With the increasing strength of the acceptor units, the emission colors of 2EtN-O-2CN, 2EtN-O-S, and 2EtN-O-OCN can be tuned to deep red. Among these materials, the c2 values of 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN are 0.45 and 0.46, respectively, which are closer to the ideal value of 0.5 than that of 2EtN-O-2CN (0.40). This phenomenon arises from the formation of stronger resonance configurations by D–A modulation. Consequently, 2EtN-O-S and 2EtN-O-OCN exhibit the smaller FWHM of 0.12 eV and higher ε (105 L mol−1 cm−1). Consistent with the photophysical results, OLEDs fabricated with 2EtN-O-S achieve narrowband deep-red electroluminescence, with an emission peak at 706 nm with a FWHM of 62 nm (0.15 eV). Based on the two-state model of dyes, this study provides a novel molecular design strategy for the future development of materials with strong absorption and high color purity.
