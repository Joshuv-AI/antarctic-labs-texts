Unlocking IL-33: New Insights into Tumor Immunity
Authors: Byeong Hoon Kang, Heung Kyu Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IL-33, a member of the IL-1 cytokine family, is constitutively expressed in the nucleus of various cell types. As an alarmin, IL-33 is released upon tissue damage and activates immune cell populations through its receptor, suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (ST2). Given that ST2 is expressed on diverse immune cells involved in both type 1 and type 2 immunity, IL-33 exerts pleiotropic effects on immune responses. In the tumor microenvironment (TME), IL-33 plays a dual role, promoting both protumor and antitumor TME. In this review, we summarize general characteristics of IL-33 and its immunological functions, with particular emphasis on its role in tumor immunity. Furthermore, we discuss recent advances in utilizing IL-33 in cancer immunotherapies and propose IL-33 as a potential candidate for the development of new cancer therapies.

INTRODUCTION

IL-33, a member of the IL-1 cytokine family, was originally identified as a nuclear factor selectively expressed in high endothelial venules (1). However, in 2005, Schmitz et al. (2) first demonstrated that IL-33 functioned as a cytokine, by binding to its receptor, suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (ST2; encoded by Il1rl1) (2). As an alarmin, nuclear IL-33 can be released from cells by tissue injury or damage (3) and activates various immune cell types involved in type 2 immunity, including group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), Th2 cells, mast cells, basophils, and eosinophils (4). Furthermore, IL-33 has also been shown to enhance type 1 immune responses by activating NK cells, CD8+ T cells, NKT cells and Th1 cells (35) in viral infection. Since IL-33 is expressed by various cell types, including tissue-resident cells and immune cells, and many immune cell types express its receptor ST2, IL-33 has a context- and niche-specific effects on immune responses.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) comprises tumor cells, tissue-resident cells and infiltrating immune cells, and it represents the immunotype of tumors (6). Given its pleiotropic effects, numerous studies have identified the role of IL-33 in tumor immunity; however, its impact on tumor immune responses remains controversial (7). Although IL-33 has been shown to promote tumorigenesis in various cancer types and to suppress antitumor immune responses, several studies have also demonstrated that IL-33 can enhance the activity of cytotoxic lymphocytes (7). To address the gap in understanding these contradictory roles of IL-33 in tumor immunity, it is important to focus on the function of IL-33 within the TME, particularly its effects on various immune cell types. Here, we will review recent studies on the protumor or antitumor roles of IL-33, with a particular focus on its effects on various immune cell subsets. We also discuss the potential of IL-33 as an adjuvant in cancer immunotherapy and propose its possible application in the development of novel cancer therapies.

IL-33: MOLECULAR BIOLOGY

The gene encoding IL-33 is located on chromosome 9 in both human and mouse and comprises eight exons (12). Human IL-33 protein consists of 270 amino acids (266 amino acids in mouse) and comprises 2 distinct domains separated by central linker region: an N-terminal nuclear domain (amino acids 1–65) and a C-terminal cytokine domain (amino acids 112–270) (Fig. 1) (12). The N-terminal nuclear domain is necessary for nuclear localization (8) and contains a chromatin-binding motif (amino acids 40–58) (9) and the C-terminal cytokine domain adopts a β-trefoil fold composed of 12 β-strands, structurally similar to other IL-1 family members such as IL-1α, IL-1β, and IL-18 (10). Through C-terminal cytokine domain, IL-33 mediates signaling cascade with its receptor, ST2 and co-receptor IL-1 receptor accessory protein (IL-1RAcP) (Fig. 1) (1011). IL-33 is constitutively expressed in the nuclei of various tissues under homeostatic conditions (212). However, as an alarmin, IL-33 can be released following cell death or tissue injury to alert the immune system (Fig. 1) (3). In addition, IL-33 release can be triggered by various stimuli, including allergen exposure (13), fungal mycobiome (14), mechanical stress (1516), oxidative stress (17) and cellular senescence (18). Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying IL-33 release remain unclear. Following its release, IL-33 is further regulated through proteolytic processing by inflammatory proteases (1920), environmental allergens (21) and ripoptosome activation (Fig. 1) (22). Although full-length IL-33 can activate ST2 (23), its processed bioactive forms are 10- to 30-fold more active than full length IL-33 (1920), and excessive IL-33 release leads to lethal inflammation (24). Therefore, the bioactivity of IL-33 is limited by caspases (2325) and oxidation (26). Furthermore, soluble ST2 (sST2), a decoy receptor for IL-33, functions as a negative regulator by neutralizing IL-33 bioactivity (Fig. 1) (27).

THE ROLE OF IL-33 IN IMMUNITY

IL-33 is predominantly associated with type 2 immune responses (28), and extensive research has highlighted its indispensable role in promoting type 2 immunity (Fig. 2). ILC2s have distinct ST2 expression and are the primary innate immune cells activated by IL-33. Their responsiveness to IL-33 is regulated by transcription factors (2930) and IL-33 stimulation leads to the production of type 2 cytokines such as IL-5 and IL-13 against helminth infection (3132), asthma and allergy (333435). Granulocytes, including, eosinophils (3637), mast cells (3839) and basophils (4041) also contribute to establish type 2 immune responses upon IL-33 stimulation. In the adaptive immunity, IL-33 directly stimulates ST2 signaling in CD4+ T cells, leading to upregulation of type 2 cytokines such as IL-5 and IL-13 (4243). IL-33 can also indirectly promote Th2 cell responses by activating dendritic cells (DCs), which subsequently induce Th2 differentiation (4445). In addition, IL-33, in concert with TSLP and IL-25, acts as a tissue checkpoint to regulate type 2 immune responses (46). On the other hand, IL-33 can also modulate type 1 immune responses (Fig. 2). T-bet, the master regulator of Th1 cells, regulates the expression of ST2 in CD4+ T cells (47) and IL-33–ST2 axis amplifies IFN-γ production (4748). Additionally, IL-33 also potentiates the effector functions of NK cells (4950) and NKT cells (51). Furthermore, IL-33 synergizes with IL-12 to enhance IFN-γ production (52) of CD8+ T cells. Augmented CD8+ T cell effector functions not only drive protective antiviral responses (5354), but also regulate self-reactive autoimmune responses (5556). Specifically, IL-33 promotes effector differentiation from T cell factor 1 (TCF-1)high stem-like precursors of self-reactive CD8+ T cells (56). Conversely, recent study demonstrated that IL-33 also preserves the stemness of TCF-1+ CD8+ T cells during chronic viral infection (57), suggesting that a context-dependent role in balancing effector differentiation and stemness (Fig. 2). To summarize, IL-33 can promote type 1 and type 2 immunity and local environmental cues—including cytokines and specific immune cell subsets (e.g., ILC2s and Th2 cells for type 2 immunity; NK cells and CD8+ T cells for type 1 immunity)—are essential determinants of the pleiotropic effects of IL-33.

The role of IL-33 in type 1 and type 2 immunity.

(A) During viral infection, IL-33 promotes type 1 immune responses. T-bet induces the expression of ST2 in Th1 cell. IL-33–ST2 signaling in Th1 cell, NK cell, NKT cell and CD8+ T cell enhances the production of proinflammatory cytokines, thereby augmenting type 1 immune responses. In addition, IL-33 modulates CD8+ T cell differentiation by regulating TCF-1 expression. (B) Tissue damage caused by allergens or helminth infection leads to the release of IL-33 into the extracellular space. Extracellular IL-33 activates ILC2 and Th2 cell, promoting the production of type 2 cytokines such as IL-5, IL-13, and IL-4. Additionally, granulocytes—including eosinophil, basophil, and mast cell—are activated by IL-33 and also amplify type 2 immune responses through cytokine secretion.

IL-33 IN TUMOR IMMUNITY: PROTUMOR EFFECTS

IL-33 has been demonstrated to promote a protumoral microenvironment by modulating both tumor cells and immune cells (Fig. 3). Overexpression of nuclear IL-33 in tumor cells promotes glioma progression by altering the cytokine and chemokine milieu of the TME (58). In addition, nuclear IL-33 reduces the suppressive function of Tregs by interacting with NF-κB (59). In addition to nuclear factor, IL-33 has a protumoral effects as a cytokine (Fig. 3). First, IL-33 promotes immunosuppressive TME. IL-33–ST2 signaling axis in tumor cells initiated signaling cascade involving IFN-γ-IFNγR-JAK2-STAT3 that resulted in PD-L1 induction for immune evasion (60). Exogenous IL-33 treatment promoted the accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and Tregs (6162). In addition, endogenous IL-33 derived from cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) modulated the protumoral TME by establishing a type 2 immune mileu (63) and activating ILC2s and Tregs (64). Second, IL-33 promotes tumor growth by directly activating innate immune cells comprising the TME. ILC2s express high levels of ST2 and recent studies have demonstrated the protumoral effects of IL-33 activated ILC2s. IL-33-activated ILC2s were indispensable for tumor growth (14) and enhanced the immunosuppressive functions of MDSCs through IL-13 signaling (65). Especially, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ)+ ILC2s activated by tumor-derived IL-33 promoted tumor invasiveness through IL-13 (66). Activated ILC2s also secreted IL-9 and induced metaplasia of Paneth cell (67). In addition, IL-33 activated ILC2s promoted IL-5 induced eosinophilia, and constrained metabolic fitness of NK cells, ultimately suppressing their cytotoxic capacity (68). These protumoral functions of IL-33 and ILC2s were associated with resistance to fluorouracil chemotherapy against bone marrow metastasis. Specifically, IL-33 promoted tumor cell polyploidy and activated ILC2s, resulting in suppression of CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity (69). IL-33 also regulates protumoral effects of granulocytes. The secretory proteins released by IL-33 activated mast cells reprogrammed fibroblasts toward a protumoral phenotype (70) and facilitated the differentiation of inducible T cell costimulator (ICOS)+ Tregs in an IL-2-dependent manner (71). Apart from granulocytes, MDSCs and macrophages are also crucial for tumor progression by suppressing antitumor immunity. Exogenous IL-33 treatment facilitated the recruitment of MDSCs into the TME (72) and ST2 signaling in MDSCs upregulated arginase-1, leading to the T cell inactivation (73). ST2+ macrophages promoted tumor progression by suppressing CD8+ T cell proliferation (74). In macrophage differentiation, IL-33, IL-33 with IL-4 and IL-13 induced differentiation of immunosuppressive macrophages (757677) and tumor-initiating cells and cancer stem cells derived secreted IL-33 promoted differentiation of ST2+ Fc epsilon receptor I alpha+ macrophages and formyl peptide receptor 2+ macrophage which established tumor-favorable niches (7879). IL-33 also upregulated the expression of matrix metallopeptidase 9 in macrophages which mediated the tumor immune evasion (80). In addition to differentiation, macrophages secreted IL-33 to activate Tregs in cancer chemotherapy (81). Finally, IL-33 activates Tregs, the representative immunosuppressive subset in adaptive immunity. IL-33 signaling in esophageal cancer cells promoted the recruitment of Tregs by CCL2 (82). Recent studies also showed that tumor cells derived IL-33 activated Tregs and contributed to immunosuppression (8384). Especially, intratumoral ST2+ Tregs exhibited increased immunosuppressive features than ST2− Tregs and peripheral Tregs. ST2+ Tregs increased PD-1, ICOS, and CD39 (8586) and both tumor progression and resistance to therapy were dependent on ST2+ Tregs (1887). Furthermore, ST2+ Tregs activated the amphiregulin (AREG)-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling axis of CAFs (88) and IL-33+ stromal cells colocalized with ST2+ Tregs were correlated with poor prognosis in cancer patients (89). In summary, IL-33 promotes tumor growth by regulating the activity of immune cell subsets in the TME.

IL-33 IN TUMOR IMMUNITY: ANTITUMOR EFFECTS

Although IL-33 activates various immune cell types associated with tumor progression, it can also enhance antitumor immunity during tumor progression (Fig. 4). Nuclear IL-33 interacted with TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) to promote IFN regulatory factor 1 (IRF1) ubiquitination, thereby significantly reducing PD-L1 levels in tumor cells (90). IL-33 can also reprogram the TME to promote antitumor immunity. Compared to wild-type mice, ST2-deficient mice resulted in suppressed immune response against glioma (91) and endogenous IL-33 derived from tumor cells directly activated both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells under chemotherapy-sensitive conditions (92). Furthermore, overexpression of IL-33 in melanoma cells significantly suppressed tumor growth in vivo via T cell activation (93) and exogenous IL-33 treatment also increased T cells infiltrating and their effector functions (94). IL-33 directly promotes cytotoxic cells-mediated tumor elimination (Fig. 4). TNF-α upregulated the expression of ST2 on NK cells and IL-33-activated NK cells were recruited to the TME in a CCL5-dependent manner to suppress tumor growth (95). Intratumoral administration of a lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus-based viral vector induced increased IL-33 production in fibroblastic stromal cells, thereby sustaining the antitumor activity of CD8+ T cells (96). In addition to promoting cytotoxic functions, ST2 signaling enhanced CD8+ T cells residency by upregulating CD103 expression of CD8+ T cells (97). IL-33 could also indirectly augment the activation of cytotoxic lymphocytes by inducing accumulation of Th1-polarized cells within the TME (Fig. 4) (98). In addition to Th1 cells, tumor-derived IL-33 resulted in increase of ILC2s and conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1s), followed by CD8+ T cells activation (99). In the context of DCs functions, IL-33 increased MHC class II expression and co-stimulatory molecules on DCs (100101) and promoted the semaphorin 4A-PlexinB2 interaction between DCs and CD8+ T cells (102). IL-33 also indirectly affects DC activation and recruitment. IL-33-primed ST2+ basophils promoted Fc receptor, IgG, low affinity III (Fc gamma receptor III, FCGR3)+ CD103+ cDC1s differentiation (103). IL-33 expanded tumor ILC2s (TILC2s) to produce CCL5, potentially recruited CD103+ DCs within the TME (104) in pancreatic cancer. In addition to DCs, ILC2s and eosinophils can function as antigen-presenting cells to activate T cells. Feng et al. (105) showed that ILC2s upregulated MHC class II expression by IL-33 activation and IL-33-activated ILC2s promoted CD8+ T cells proliferation and cytokine production in an antigen-specific manner (106). IL-33 activated eosinophils also facilitated CD8+ T cells activations in breast cancer and therapeutic effects of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) with cisplatin were dependent on eosinophils (107). While cytotoxic lymphocytes constitute a central axis of antitumor immunity, recent studies have highlighted lymphocyte-independent mechanisms (Fig. 4). Dipeptidyl peptidase-4/CD26–mediated tumor control was dependent on IL-33–ST2 signaling and eosinophils, rather than lymphocytes, through a mechanism involving CCL11-mediated recruitment to the TME (108). In colorectal cancer, IL-33–activated eosinophils increased expression of degranulation markers, consistent with a cytotoxic function (109) and they also produced higher quantities of extracellular vesicles (EVs) which resulted in cell cycle arrest of tumor cells (110). Apart from regulating cellular antitumor immunity, recent study suggested that IL-33 contributes to the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs)– de novo ectopic lymphoid aggregates associated with antitumor immunity (Fig. 4). Amisaki et al. (111) identified a correlation between IL-33 and TLSs in cancer. They demonstrated that IL-33 promoted lymphoneogenic features in ILC2s, ultimately potentiates antitumor immunity for tumor clearance. Collectively, IL-33 enhances the cytotoxic activity of immune cells both directly and indirectly, and orchestrates the formation of inflammatory niches such as TLSs to sustain localized immune responses against tumors.

IL-33 IN CANCER IMMUNOTHERAPY

Given the critical role of IL-33 in tumor immunity, recent studies have focused on targeting IL-33 to improve cancer immunotherapy. In colorectal cancer, disruption of IL-33–ST2 signaling using an IL-33trap fusion protein, which consists of the extracellular parts of ST2 and its IL-1RAcP, enhanced antitumor immunity (74). In addition, targeting IL-33–ST2 signaling synergizes with ICBs. Dual blockade of PD-1 and IL-33 significantly delayed tumor progression and enhanced NK cell cytotoxicity by decreasing immunosuppressive cells in the TME (112). Given the synergistic effects of IL-33–ST2 and PD-L1 blockade, bifunctional fusion protein targeting PD-L1 and IL-33 (anti-PD-L1-sST2) also suppressed tumor growth (113). PLX4720—a high affinity inhibitor of B-RAFV600E (114)—treatment was limited by resistance mediated by IL-33+ medullary sinus macrophages. However, combined therapy with anti-ST2 and anti-PD-1 antibodies markedly suppressed tumor burden (81). On the other hand, several studies have leveraged the antitumor properties of IL-33 to enhance cancer immunotherapy. Tumor-derived IL-33 inhibited melanoma growth, and co-administration of anti-PD-1 antibody further amplified antitumor immune responses (93). Exogenous recombinant IL-33 promoted expansion of ILC2s with increased PD-1 expression. Concurrent administration of IL-33 and anti-PD-1 antibody significantly enhanced immunotherapeutic efficacy (115). Moral et al. (104) also demonstrated that IL-33 activated PD-1+ TILC2s and antitumor efficacy of anti-PD-1 treatment was synergized with IL-33. In addition to ICB, the chemotherapeutic agent cisplatin (107) and the DNA methylation inhibitor decitabine (94) have been shown to cooperate with IL-33 to enhance the response to ICB. Furthermore, recent studies have shown the antitumor efficacy of IL-33 as an adjuvant in cell therapies (Fig. 4). Orthogonal cytokine engineering of T cells to express IL-2 variant (IL-2v) binding the IL-2Rβγ receptor, the IL-33 and secreted PD-1 decoy acquired synthetic effector state, which suppressed canonical exhaustion and displayed superior effector functions. Adoptive cell transfer of these engineered T cells resulted in improved antitumor activity (116). Chimeric-antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell armored with IL-33 and superkine IL-2, an engineered variant of IL-2 binding the IL-2Rβγ receptor promoted tumor regression and reshaped immune cell proportions in the TME (117118). In summary, the dual roles of IL-33 in the TME—both protumor and antitumor effects—have been harnessed to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of cancer therapies. Given the effects of IL-33 within the TME are highly dynamic and context-dependent, a deeper understanding of the specific immunological and cellular contexts is essential for optimizing therapeutic potential of IL-33.

CONCLUDING REMARKS

IL-33 orchestrates both protumor and antitumor immune responses by regulating diverse immune cell subsets in the TME, functioning either as a nuclear factor or a cytokine. Within the TME, IL-33 can exert beneficial effects by engaging to cytotoxic immune cells and activating them in concert with local environmental cues. Conversely, IL-33 may also facilitate tumor progression when it interacts with immunosuppressive populations such as MDSCs, TAMs and Tregs. In this review, we discussed recent studies elucidating the role of IL-33 in tumor immune responses. Although several mechanisms have been proposed regarding the cellular sources and targets of IL-33, and how it modulates immune populations to promote or inhibit antitumor immune responses, significant gaps remain in understanding its functional roles within the TME. To address these gaps, it is essential to consider the its dual function as a nuclear factor and a cytokine, the heterogeneity of its cellular sources, the expression of its receptor ST2 across various immune cell subsets and the immune contexture surrounding the TME. Meanwhile, modulation of IL-33–ST2 signaling—either through blockade or enhancement—has shown synergistic effects when combined with cancer therapies. Furthermore, as an adjuvant, IL-33 can enhance the efficacy of engineered T cells. These findings suggest that IL-33 represents a promising target for cancer immunotherapy. However, given its context-dependent immunomodulatory roles, the therapeutic targeting IL-33 requires a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding of its role in the TME. In conclusion, for new cancer immunotherapies, further investigations are warranted not only into the fundamental biology of IL-33 and its role in tumor immunity, but also into its potential application in cancer immunotherapy.
