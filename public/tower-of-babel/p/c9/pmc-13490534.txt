A global feasibility gap in resilient island energy transitions
Authors: Churui Huang, Haoran Zhang, Jinyue Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIsland nations are central to achieving United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 7 on affordable and clean energy, yet the feasibility of their energy transitions remains poorly quantified. Here we show, using a high-resolution assessment of over 1800 inhabited islands and 50 million hourly records of demand and renewable resources, that resilient low-carbon power systems remain out of reach for many islands. More than half of the islands assessed face transition costs above income-based affordability thresholds, with the greatest burdens in Southeast Asia, coastal Africa and the Western Pacific. Adding climate-resilience requirements moves 7% of islands into higher-cost categories. Although technological progress could lower average levelized cost of electricity by about 4% by 2050, this reduction is too small to close the feasibility gap for low-income islands. These findings show that market-driven cost declines alone are unlikely to deliver equitable island energy transitions, and that targeted support is needed.Subject terms: Energy justice, Energy economics, Energy policy

Introduction

The feasibility of energy transitions represents one of the core challenges confronting global climate governance and sustainable development1,2. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 7 explicitly calls for ensuring access to affordable, reliable, sustainable, and modern energy for all by 2030. Yet, for the world’s most vulnerable islands, achieving this target remains particularly daunting. Island energy transitions differ fundamentally from those in mainland systems, such as small cities or rural regions. Island energy systems are typically characterized by a strong dependence on imported fossil fuels3,4, which leads to energy expenditures accounting for a disproportionately high share of gross domestic product than the global average5,6, as well as limited interconnection with larger power grids. In the absence of interregional balancing and economies of scale, island systems operate under inherently constrained conditions, where supply security, system costs, and external dependencies are tightly coupled. These structural constraints are further exacerbated by high exposure to climate hazards, including tropical cyclones, storm surges, and sea-level rise, which further increase system design requirements and costs7–10. Together, these characteristics create a distinct techno-economic context in which the feasibility of energy transitions is particularly sensitive to both cost and risk. As such, island systems provide a revealing context for assessing whether global energy transitions can be both equitable and universally attainable.

Despite the rapid advancement and cost reduction of renewable energy technologies in recent years11, the fundamental barrier to island energy transitions lies not in technological availability but in economic feasibility12–16. Building a resilient, low-carbon energy system entails substantial upfront capital investment and long-term operational costs17, while achieving climate resilience to future hazards requires additional expenditure for redundancy and protective design18–20. These financial pressures often exceed the fiscal capacity and market scale of many islands, such that even where technological solutions exist, transitions may remain economically challenging21. On the supply side, costs are shaped by resource endowment, demand characteristics, system scale, and climate exposure; on the demand side, affordability is constrained by local economic conditions. The mismatch between these dimensions determines whether energy transitions can be realized under existing market conditions, giving rise to a persistent feasibility gap in energy transitions.

Previous studies have largely focused on single technologies or regional case analyses. While these studies provide valuable insights into local system design and optimization, they typically assess feasibility from a techno-economic perspective and therefore provide only a partial view of transition feasibility22–26, giving limited explicit attention to how affordability constraints shape the realization of these systems. At the same time, research on energy affordability and energy poverty highlights the role of income constraints in shaping energy access27,28, but rarely connects these constraints explicitly to system-level costs and transition pathways. As a result, existing studies provide limited insight into how system costs and affordability jointly determine the feasibility of energy transitions, leaving the feasibility gap described above insufficiently understood in heterogeneous real-world contexts. This gap is particularly critical in island systems, where enabling conditions and structural disadvantages coexist. Their small scale and clearly defined boundaries can facilitate integrated system design and rapid renewable deployment, yet they also face limited economies of scale, high infrastructure and logistics costs, and greater redundancy requirements under isolated operation. These opposing characteristics primarily shape system costs and system design requirements, generating substantial uncertainty and heterogeneity in transition pathways across islands. Consequently, it remains unclear which islands can achieve resilient energy transitions under current economic conditions and which face structural constraints that may require external support. This knowledge gap not only limits theoretical understanding but also constrains the effectiveness of international policy design and climate finance29. In the absence of a systematic diagnostic framework, external support may become inefficiently allocated or misdirected away from the regions most in need.

To address this gap, we develop a global feasibility-assessment framework that evaluates energy transition feasibility as a function of the interaction between system costs and local affordability constraints. Using a multi-source spatiotemporal database encompassing 1830 islands worldwide, the framework integrates nearly 50 million hourly data points on energy demand and renewable energy potential to quantify baseline transition costs and feasibility under current technological and financial conditions. Within this framework, the feasibility gap is defined as an operational diagnostic indicator of the mismatch between project cost and local affordability. Building upon this baseline, high-resolution climate projections from global climate models are incorporated to examine how climate-resilient design influences the overall feasibility dynamics of energy transition projects. Furthermore, the analysis is extended to future scenarios of technological progress, assessing how projected cost reductions may reshape the global feasibility landscape. Together, these analyses establish a globally harmonized basis for understanding the conditions under which resilient energy transitions can be achieved across the world’s islands.

Results

Cost differentials across island energy transitions

Substantial geographic disparities are evident in the costs of island energy transition projects worldwide (Fig. 1a), based on simulations under the Ideal scenario (see “Methods,” scenario settings). On average, the per capita system cost is USD 624.8, with a median of USD 518.9 and a standard deviation of USD 365.7, indicating substantial heterogeneity. Half of the islands fall within the range of USD 500–624 per capita, whereas fewer than 1% exceed USD 2050 per capita, forming a long-tailed distribution. A spatial assessment further reveals a marked geographic pattern. For this assessment, islands were grouped according to the reference regions of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change Sixth Assessment Report (IPCC AR6; see Supplementary Method 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Along latitude, costs follow a U-shaped distribution. High-latitude islands in Northern Europe (NEU), Eastern North America (ENA), and Greenland/Iceland (GIC) show a mean cost of USD 919 ± 362 per capita, which is about 1.6 times that of mid- and low-latitude islands (USD 562 ± 195 per capita). Some mid- and low-latitude regions, including Southeast Asia (SEA) and the Arabian Peninsula (ARP), also exhibit comparatively high costs, averaging USD 834 ± 292 per capita. Along longitude, islands in the Western Hemisphere have higher average costs (USD 762 ± 658 per capita) than those in the Eastern Hemisphere (USD 584 ± 192 per capita). In addition, about 78% of projects with costs below the median are located in the Eastern Hemisphere.

Cost decomposition indicates that three investment categories account for much of the spatial contrast in total system costs—renewable generation, energy storage, and conventional generation (Fig. 1b). Renewable generation is the largest factor behind the cost escalation in high-latitude regions. In the northern North Atlantic, including GIC, ENA and NEU, and in the North Pacific, including Northwestern North America (NWN), Western North America (WNA) and Russian Far East (RFE), renewable capacity investments average USD 550 ± 302 per capita and account for 58% ± 24% of total system costs. Storage costs are more prominent in the Mediterranean (MED) and NEU, averaging USD 199 ± 119 per capita (33% ± 18%). Conventional fossil generation investments are highest in the ARP, GIC, Southern South America (SSA), and NWN, averaging USD 226 ± 228 per capita (18% ± 16%). In SEA, a small number of islands show fossil generation investments that are well above the regional average. In contrast, other cost components contribute less to the observed spatial disparities. Sector-coupling costs are closely associated with renewable and conventional generation capacity, while curtailment penalties reflect the management of renewable electricity output rather than a separate infrastructure requirement. Unserved energy costs account for less than 1% of total costs in 96% of the islands and therefore have a limited influence on regional cost differences.

The factors associated with regional cost disparities were further examined using multiple regression analyses (Supplementary Table 1; multicollinearity diagnostics in Supplementary Fig. 2). The results indicate that total system costs are mainly associated with energy-demand structure and seasonal variability in renewable resources (Fig. 1c). On the demand side, heating demand (HD) shows the strongest positive association with total system cost (β = 0.517, R2 = 0.702; see Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). In high-latitude regions, higher heating loads are associated with greater renewable generation capacity (β = 0.327), conventional generation investment (β = 0.187), and sector-coupling system investment (β = 0.316), suggesting that heating demand is an important contributor to elevated costs in these regions. Heating demand variability is also positively associated with storage requirements (β = 0.262). Cooling demand has a positive but smaller association with total system cost (β = 0.110), consistent with the lower contribution of cooling loads to overall system capacity requirements in most islands. On the supply side, seasonal variability in wind and wave energy (Supplementary Fig. 5) is closely associated with regional cost differences. Higher seasonal variability in wind turbine output is associated with lower renewable generation investment (β = −0.573) but higher conventional generation investment (β = 0.843) and sector-coupling system investment (β = 0.791). This pattern suggests that regions with prolonged periods of low wind availability require more dispatchable backup capacity. Under the modeled cost assumptions, conventional generation can therefore become a lower-cost backup option than large-scale hydrogen-based balancing when renewable electricity input is insufficient. Seasonal variability in wave energy converter output (WECS) is positively associated with storage costs (β = 0.254), reflecting the need to smooth fluctuations in wave power supply. This relationship is particularly evident in NEU and MED, in contrast to wind-scarce regions where cost increases are more closely linked to dispatchable backup requirements. Daily fluctuations in renewable output have a more moderate influence, as they can generally be managed through short-duration storage. These results indicate that the largest cost pressures arise from seasonal deficits that are difficult to offset through short-term balancing. Photovoltaic seasonality has only a small association with curtailment penalties (β = 0.061), consistent with the relatively small solar share in most island systems (Supplementary Fig. 6). Together, these demand- and supply-side patterns help explain why system cost peaks are concentrated in high-latitude islands and, to a lesser extent, in some tropical and subtropical regions.

Feasibility gaps under energy-expenditure limits

To evaluate the economic feasibility of island energy transition projects under local expenditure constraints, the feasibility gap is defined as the affordable electricity price minus the project’s levelized cost of electricity (LCOE). A negative gap indicates that project costs exceed local payment capacity in the absence of external financial support (see “Methods” for details). Across the global island sample, negative feasibility gaps are widespread, showing that affordability constraints are a major barrier to island energy transitions (Fig. 2a). The largest negative gaps occur in SEA, especially across the Indonesian and Philippine archipelagos, where most projects fall below the feasibility boundary. Negative gaps are also common in the Caribbean (CAR), Equatorial Pacific Ocean (EPO), South Pacific Ocean (SPO), Western Africa (WAF), West Southern Africa (WSAF), and East Southern Africa (ESAF). By contrast, small negative or positive gaps are more frequent in higher-income regions, including East Asia (EAS), ENA, NWN, MED, NEU, Northern Australia (NAU), and New Zealand (NZ).

To separate the roles of project cost and local affordability, each island is positioned according to its LCOE and affordable electricity price (Fig. 2b). The median LCOE, the feasibility boundary, where the affordable electricity price equals LCOE, and the corresponding affordability reference line implied by their intersection divide the space into six regions. Feasible projects are mainly located in high-affordability regions, with 826 islands in quadrants 1 and 2. Infeasible projects are concentrated in low-affordability regions, with 979 islands in quadrants 5 and 6. This distribution identifies three modes of economic infeasibility (Fig. 2c, d).

The largest group is the low-affordability and low-cost mode, which includes 583 islands. In this group, LCOE values are below the global median, but affordable electricity prices remain lower than project costs. Infeasibility is therefore driven primarily by limited local payment capacity rather than by high absolute system costs. This mode is distributed across much of EPO, SPO, CAR, WAF, ESAF, and parts of SEA.

The second group is the low-affordability and high-cost mode, which includes 396 islands. These islands face a combined constraint because low payment capacity coincides with above-median project costs. This mode is geographically concentrated in SEA, particularly across Indonesia and the Philippines.

The third group is the high-affordability and high-cost mode, which includes 17 islands. In these cases, local payment capacity is relatively high, but project costs remain high enough to exceed the affordability threshold. These islands are mainly located in high-latitude regions, including GIC, NEU, SSA, and RFE.

Climate-resilience design widens feasibility gaps

To meet escalating climate risks, future island energy systems need to incorporate stronger resilience requirements. Incorporating forward-looking climate-resilient design, which accounts for the intensification of future climate hazards, increases project costs across most islands. In the quadrant analysis, this change shifts island LCOE values leftward relative to the affordability threshold, indicating a higher cost burden under the same affordability constraint (Fig. 3a). The spatial distribution of cost increases is uneven (Fig. 3b). The largest increases occur in SSA, Southwest South America (SWS) and NEU, while the most spatially widespread increases extend across NEU, EAS, SEA, NWN, and SWS. Building on these patterns, this widespread escalation leads to a broad-based but heterogeneous deterioration in global economic feasibility, pushing many marginally feasible islands into infeasibility and worsening the condition for a subset of already infeasible islands. The number of islands in quadrant 1 decreases from 324 to 278, whereas those in quadrant 6 increase from 396 to 445 (Fig. 3c), indicating that resilience requirements substantially reshape the global feasibility landscape.

Category transitions across the three scenarios further clarify how islands lose feasibility. Among the 131 islands that change cost classification, equivalent to 7.2% of the global sample, transitions are concentrated in EAS and SEA (Fig. 3d). These two regions account for 96 islands, or 73.3% of all changed islands, but show different transition pathways. EAS follows a pattern of initial cost escalation followed by relative stabilization. In this region, 29 islands, or 64.4% of the EAS group, shift from low-cost feasible conditions in quadrant 1 to high-cost feasible conditions in quadrant 2. Only 3 islands, or 6.7%, move into infeasible categories, suggesting moderate buffering capacity against climate-stress costs. SEA shows a more progressive decline. In this region, 46 islands, or 90.2% of the SEA group, experience downgrades under baseline or climate-stress conditions. Of these islands, all move from quadrant 5 to quadrant 6, with 17 doing so already under baseline conditions and the remaining 29 only under climate stress. This pattern indicates a continued erosion of feasibility under climate stress. Other regions show fewer transitions. For example, the five MED islands remain feasible across scenarios, and only a small number of South Asian (SAS) islands change classification. Overall, under the Climate Stress scenario, 64 of the changed islands (48.9%) remain feasible in quadrants 1 and 2, whereas 52 (39.7%) end in the most infeasible category, quadrant 6. SEA accounts for 46 of these 52 islands (88.5%). These results indicate that climate stress accelerates feasibility loss most strongly in regions with low electricity affordability.

Multiple regression analysis identifies the cost components most associated with climate-resilience cost increases (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Hazard intensity is associated with higher expenditures on energy-storage systems (β = 8.49, p < 0.001) and conventional fossil-fuel facilities (β = 5.41, p < 0.01), indicating that climate hazards amplify costs mainly through storage and backup-capacity requirements. This pattern is consistent with the need for additional redundancy to maintain energy supply under severe disasters. However, the economic consequences of hazard-driven cost escalation vary with socioeconomic context. The ability of islands to absorb the resilience cost premium is constrained by their economic affordability (Supplementary Fig. 7). Among islands with LCOE increases greater than 0.05 USD kW h−1 (n = 201, 11.0% of the global sample), affordability differences shape distinct regional vulnerability patterns. High-affordability islands, such as those in NWN, Southern Australia (SAU), NZ, and North-Eastern North America (NEN), bear relative cost burdens of only 4.5–5.6%, measured as the ratio of the LCOE increase to the affordable electricity price, and none lose economic feasibility. In contrast, the burden is magnified in low-affordability regions. Islands in SWS (0.088 USD kW h−1) and SEA (0.056 USD kW h−1) experience comparable or lower absolute cost growth than high-affordability islands (0.099 USD kW h−1) but face much higher relative burdens. Their relative burdens reach 18.2% and 44.1%, respectively, because of their very low baseline affordable electricity prices. These results indicate that comparable resilience cost premiums can impose much greater economic stress in lower-affordability regions, thereby increasing the risk of declining feasibility (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Technological progress alone cannot close the gap

Technological progress can provide modest cost-reduction benefits for resilient renewable-energy projects on many islands. The projections suggest that continued technology learning will systematically lower project-level LCOE by 2050, reducing the global average by about 4%, and shift the overall economic landscape toward greater feasibility, as indicated by the widespread rightward movement of island LCOE values in the quadrant space (Supplementary Fig. 9). The number of projects in high-cost categories, including quadrants 2 and 6, decreases, whereas the feasible, low-cost and high-affordability category increases from 278 to 304 islands. The infeasible, high-cost, and low-affordability category decreases from 445 to 426 islands (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Despite the overall decline in LCOE, regional disparities remain pronounced. By the 2050 technology-progress scenario (TP2050), median cost reductions exceed 15% in regions, such as MED, whereas SEA and EAS show less than 10% improvement (Supplementary Fig. 11). Comparing each island’s LCOE under the climate stress and TP2050 scenarios with the Ideal scenario shows that, in several regions, learning-driven cost reductions remain insufficient to restore costs to ideal levels (Fig. 4a). The effect is most pronounced in SWS, where median island LCOE remains 31.7% higher than under the Ideal scenario. Similarly, islands in EAS and RFE exhibit median increases of 10–16%. By contrast, MED, the Sahara (SAH), and Western and Central Europe (WCE) achieve both substantial cost reductions and final LCOE values below the Ideal benchmark, with median declines of 12–23%. These regional contrasts indicate that technology learning reduces costs unevenly, leaving substantial residual gaps in regions where cost reductions are limited or affordability constraints remain strong.

Regression analysis identifies the structural mechanisms behind these system-wide cost reductions (Supplementary Fig. 12, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). First, declining storage costs are the strongest driver, substantially reducing total system costs in high-storage-penetration systems (β = 5.27). Second, in regions with high costs of conventional generation, storage expansion replaces expensive conventional peaking units, yielding substantial operational savings (β = 2.44). However, for systems already dominated by renewables, the marginal cost-reduction effect of further increasing renewable penetration is limited (β = −0.033, p > 0.05). Regional decomposition supports these mechanisms (Supplementary Fig. 13). In regions, such as MED, characterized by low reliance on conventional generation and high storage penetration, total cost reductions are mainly associated with declines in renewable-capital expenditures. Renewable costs decrease (−USD 12.04 per capita) enough to offset rising storage investments (+USD 9.21 per capita). Conversely, in SSA, ARP, and some SEA islands, where systems remain dependent on conventional generation, storage expansion achieves major cost savings by replacing costly peaking operations, reducing expenditures by USD 36.79 and USD 42.91 per capita in SSA and ARP, respectively. In contrast, in regions such as EPO and WNA, renewable cost savings (−USD 9.32 and −USD 21.92 per capita) are nearly offset by rising storage costs (+USD 7.86 and +USD 23.76 per capita), reflecting diminishing cost-reduction effects. These contrasting regional patterns explain why the benefits of technology learning are uneven across the island sample.

As a result, these uneven benefits have only a limited effect on the global feasibility landscape. Among the 50 islands that change category between scenarios (2.7% of the global sample), the direction of transition depends strongly on the magnitude of cost reduction and local affordability (Fig. 4b). In regions with larger reductions and stronger affordability, such as MED, category changes are concentrated in favorable transitions. In this region, 92.3% of changing islands move from high-cost feasible to low-cost feasible by the 2030 technology-progress scenario (TP2030). By contrast, in regions with smaller reductions and weaker affordability, such as SAS and SEA, transitions occur mainly within the infeasible domain. All changing islands in SAS and 87.5% of those in SEA remain in infeasible categories after technology learning. Globally, only three islands (one each in EAS, SEA, and SSA) achieve a genuine transition from infeasible to feasible status. Overall, technology learning systematically reduces project costs, but these reductions are generally insufficient to move most infeasible islands across the economic-feasibility threshold. For regions with limited affordability and modest learning-driven cost reductions, technology progress alone is therefore unlikely to close the feasibility gap.

Discussion

This study provides a global-scale quantitative assessment of the economic feasibility of island energy transitions and documents spatial inequality in resilient energy systems. By integrating high-resolution modeling across 1830 islands and over 50 million hourly data records, the analysis reveals interactions between energy costs, climate vulnerability, and local economic capacity. The results highlight the pivotal role of island nations in the global clean energy transition, while revealing a persistent feasibility gap that constrains progress toward both decarbonization and energy equity. Beyond empirical quantification, this study advances the conceptual understanding of energy transitions by reframing feasibility as a coupled socio-economic constraint arising from the interaction between system costs and affordability, rather than a purely techno-economic outcome.

This analysis reveals pronounced spatial heterogeneity in the economics of island energy transitions. The average per-capita system cost is USD 624.8, with a standard deviation of USD 365.7, and costs in high-latitude islands are roughly 1.6 times those in lower latitudes—driven primarily by heating demand intensity and seasonal variability of renewable resources. Affordability assessment further shows that over half of all islands are economically infeasible at current income levels. The burden is especially severe in Southeast Asia and coastal Africa, where even relatively low-cost projects remain unaffordable due to low household income. Incorporating climate-resilient design further widens the feasibility gap, causing feasibility classification changes on approximately 7% of global islands and increasing average system costs by 20–30% among the affected islands, particularly across East and Southeast Asia. Importantly, the results show that the economic impact of resilience investments is highly uneven across income levels. While high-income regions can absorb additional costs with relatively small proportional burdens, similar cost increases in low-income regions translate into substantially higher relative economic pressure, thereby accelerating the loss of feasibility. Although technological progress offers partial mitigation, the projected global decline in LCOE by 2050 is only ~4%, and benefits remain unevenly distributed. While several mid- to high-income regions (e.g., the Mediterranean and North Atlantic) achieve substantial net cost reductions, most low-income regions (e.g., South and Southeast Asia) remain economically infeasible. Overall, this pattern of persistent infeasibility is primarily driven by structural affordability constraints. In low-income regions, feasibility is fundamentally constrained by income-dependent affordability limits rather than high system costs, resulting in a structural pattern of affordability-constrained infeasibility, rather than cost-driven infeasibility. Moreover, the relative burden of both baseline system costs and additional resilience investments is disproportionately higher under constrained income conditions, further widening the feasibility gap. Crucially, the modest cost reductions achieved through technological progress are insufficient to offset these affordability constraints and the additional burden introduced by climate-resilient design, leaving most low-income regions economically infeasible despite declining system costs. These results underscore that global island energy transitions are constrained by the combined effects of technology, economics, and climate, and that neither market forces nor technological innovation alone is likely to close this structural feasibility gap.

Energy transition is therefore not only a technological challenge but also a question of economic and social justice30. Island regions face a dual pressure of high system costs and limited affordability, making them a critical litmus test for global energy equity. Those contributing the least to historical carbon emissions are now required to bear some of the highest transition costs. This asymmetry poses challenges for achieving Sustainable Development Goal 7. In the absence of additional support, many low-income islands may continue to face barriers to market-driven transitions, with a risk of further marginalization in the global clean energy landscape. Achieving energy equity may therefore depend not only on technological progress but also on addressing structural inequalities through broader institutional and financial mechanisms, ensuring that energy accessibility is treated as a shared global responsibility31.

The framework developed here links system-level transition costs with local affordability constraints and uses a six-category typology to diagnose heterogeneous feasibility conditions across islands. Rather than implying a single policy pathway, this typology helps distinguish cases in which infeasibility is driven primarily by low affordability, by high system cost, or by the interaction of both. In this sense, the framework provides a structured basis for interpreting why transition barriers differ across island groups and why uniform responses may be insufficient, suggesting that policy and finance discussions may need to be differentiated across island feasibility conditions.

Feasible islands span quadrant 1 (low-cost, high-affordability), quadrant 2 (high-cost, high-affordability), and quadrant 3 (low-cost, low-affordability). Concentrated in Europe, North America, and parts of the developed Pacific, these islands account for about 45% of the global sample. Many of them, especially those in quadrants 1 and 2, possess sufficient local affordability to absorb transition costs and serve as major hosts for clean-energy investment. Instead of direct subsidies, these patterns suggest that approaches aimed at de-risking private investment may be particularly relevant, for example, through regulatory stability, transparent pricing mechanisms, sovereign guarantees, and streamlined permitting32,33. A small subset of quadrant 3 islands have technically feasible and relatively low-cost systems, but remain constrained by limited local affordability. For these islands, tariff reform or community-based energy cooperatives could help unlock implementation with relatively limited structural intervention. However, about 10–15% of quadrant 2 islands already show signs of feasibility decline under climate stress, indicating limited resilience reserves. Integrating climate-risk considerations into energy planning through enhanced storage capacity and system redundancy will be essential to prevent further downgrades.

High-cost infeasible islands fall in quadrant 4 (infeasible, high-cost, high-affordability). Found mainly at high latitudes, such as Greenland and parts of Northern Europe, these islands have strong affordability but very high costs, driven by heavy heating loads, pronounced renewable seasonality, and long-duration storage requirements. Addressing this challenge may require technology-oriented cost-reduction pathways34,35, such as (i) prioritizing international R&D collaboration on long-duration storage, resilient grids, and efficient heat-pump technologies; (ii) establishing multilateral financing mechanisms to share high upfront capital costs; and (iii) leveraging economies of scale and knowledge transfer through large-scale demonstration projects. Together, these measures may help reduce per-capita system costs from current high levels, potentially unlocking latent affordability potential.

Low-affordability infeasible islands span quadrant 5 (infeasible, low-cost, and low-affordability) and quadrant 6 (infeasible, high-cost, and low-affordability). Representing roughly 53% of global islands, this group, concentrated in Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, and coastal Africa, constitutes the core of the global feasibility crisis. In these regions, household energy expenditure often exceeds the 10% income poverty line, while climate resilience requirements further amplify costs, especially in SEA. These findings suggest that concessional, long-term, low-interest financing mechanisms may play an important role in bridging the feasibility gap36. Such mechanisms could include support from multilateral development banks, climate funds, or other risk-sharing instruments designed to alleviate the mismatch between system costs and local economic capacity. In such contexts, technological progress alone is unlikely to be sufficient; targeted capital support and social compensation may be needed for such projects to cross the feasibility threshold.

While this study focuses on isolated island systems, an important question is whether the proposed framework can be extended to grid-connected urban energy systems. Compared with islands, grid-connected cities benefit from interregional electricity trade, larger system scales, and stronger balancing capacity, which fundamentally alter both system costs and reliability requirements. As a result, the feasibility constraints identified in this study, particularly those arising from isolation, limited economies of scale, and high redundancy requirements, may be less binding in mainland contexts. Nevertheless, the core analytical framework developed here, which links system-level costs with local affordability, remains conceptually applicable. Extending this framework to grid-connected cities would require incorporating transmission networks, market interactions, and cross-regional balancing mechanisms. Such an extension represents an important direction for future research and may help evaluate whether similar feasibility gaps persist in more interconnected energy systems.

Although this global framework provides a robust foundation for strategic planning, several limitations remain. First, the analysis focuses on techno-economic feasibility and may not fully capture socio-political, regulatory, and supply-chain constraints that can critically shape project implementation. Second, the affordability indicator is based on the national average income and may not fully reflect intra-island income heterogeneity. This can overstate household payment capacity where national aggregates are elevated by unequal income distributions, tourism income, remittances, or high-income enclaves37. The affordability sensitivity indicates that reducing the affordability benchmark increases global infeasible shares and pushes Southeast Asia toward near-complete infeasibility, while substantially deepening affordability shortfalls among quadrants 5 and 6 islands (see Supplementary Note 4). The national-average benchmark should therefore be interpreted as a conservative lower-bound estimate of affordability-constrained infeasibility in low-income and high-inequality island contexts. Third, the climate-resilience module focuses on cyclone-related wind and wave disruptions to exposed energy infrastructure, while storm surge, coastal inundation, and long-term sea-level rise are outside the present modeling scope. Incorporating these hazards would likely further increase resilience costs and widen feasibility gaps for low-lying islands and atolls. Fourth, the analysis considers electricity, heating, and cooling systems, while the transport sector is not explicitly included due to limited globally consistent data and the additional assumptions required for modeling mobility and electrification pathways, potentially leading to a conservative estimate of total system costs. In addition, the modeling framework adopts a standardized community-scale representation rather than explicitly resolving differences in infrastructure scale, grid integration, or pre-existing energy systems across islands. These factors may influence costs, system configurations, and implementation pathways in practice. Explicit carbon-pricing mechanisms are also not incorporated; while their inclusion would likely strengthen the competitiveness of low-carbon systems, the heterogeneity of such policies places this beyond the scope of the present study. Despite these limitations, the results remain robust across a wide range of assumptions, suggesting that the identified feasibility gap reflects a structural rather than a modeling-driven challenge. Addressing this gap will require going beyond techno-economic optimization. Future research should integrate socio-political, regulatory, and financing dimensions, incorporate more spatially and socially disaggregated data, and link standardized assessments with context-specific system models that reflect local infrastructure and transition dynamics. Evaluating innovative financing and policy mechanisms under diverse regional conditions will be essential to translate techno-economic potential into feasible and equitable energy transitions for island systems worldwide.

Methods

Scenario settings

Six scenarios were developed to evaluate the combined effects of climate-related disasters and technological progress on island energy transitions. In the climate-related scenarios, disruptions are represented through tropical-cyclone-related wind and wave hazards affecting exposed energy infrastructure.

Under the Ideal scenario, no climate disaster is considered, and technology costs correspond to 2020 levels. The baseline scenario includes the impacts of climate disasters under 2020 conditions, with 2020 technology costs. The climate stress scenario includes the impacts of climate disasters under 2050 conditions, while keeping technology costs at 2020 levels. The TP2030 scenario, where TP denotes technological progress, considers 2050 climate-disaster conditions with projected 2030 technology costs. The TP2040 scenario considers 2050 climate-disaster conditions with projected 2040 technology costs. The TP2050 scenario considers 2050 climate-disaster conditions with projected 2050 technology costs.

Island integrated energy system model

An island integrated energy system (Fig. 5) was developed, formulated as a multi-energy framework that integrates five energy sectors, including electricity, heating, cooling, gas, and hydrogen. These sectors are coupled through a set of conversion and storage technologies, enabling flexible energy flows and sector coupling within the system. In this study, all five sectors are explicitly considered to capture system-level interactions and ensure a comprehensive assessment of cost and resilience. The system incorporates external energy inputs from wind, solar, wave, and liquefied natural gas (LNG). LNG is procured from the nearest base station and transported to the island (data source38). Biomass and geothermal resources are not included due to their highly site-specific nature, limited availability across most islands, and the lack of globally consistent high-resolution datasets. In addition, biomass supply is subject to land-use constraints and climate-related uncertainties39–41, while geothermal deployment depends on favorable geological conditions and involves high upfront exploration costs42,43. Energy conversion and storage facilities couple these resources to meet electricity, heating, and cooling demands. These include electrical energy storage, thermal energy storage, cooling energy storage (CES), proton exchange membrane water electrolyzers (PEMWE), hydrogen storage, hydrogen fuel cells, air conditioning (AC), electric boilers (EB), vaporizers, and combined heat and power (CHP) units.

Schematic of the island integrated energy system.

The diagram illustrates the main components and energy flows in a representative island integrated energy system. Renewable energy sources include photovoltaic generation (PV), wind turbines (WT), and wave energy converters (WEC). Generated electricity can directly meet electrical load, be stored in electrical energy storage (ESS), or be converted to other energy carriers. A proton exchange membrane water electrolyzer (PEMWE) produces hydrogen for hydrogen storage and hydrogen fuel cells. Electricity also supports air conditioning (AC) for cooling demand and electric boilers (EB) for thermal demand, with thermal energy stored in thermal energy storage (TES). Liquefied natural gas (LNG) is vaporized to supply gas energy to combined heat and power (CHP) units, which generate both electricity and thermal energy. Cooling energy can also be supplied by cooling energy storage (CES). The system integrates electricity, hydrogen, gas, heat, and cooling carriers to meet electrical, thermal, and cooling loads on islands.

The model’s cost and operational parameters are derived from the U.S. National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s (NREL) annual technology baseline (ATB) moderate scenario44 and relevant literature45–48. The ATB provides a standardized and widely used framework for projecting future technology costs, in which the moderate scenario represents a policy-neutral baseline under current technology-learning assumptions. Given the rapid decline of storage costs in recent years, we use the ATB advanced and conservative scenarios to capture uncertainty around faster and slower future technology-learning pathways, including alternative storage-cost reduction trajectories. The robustness of these results to these assumptions is assessed through sensitivity analysis across these scenarios (see Supplementary Note 5). Detailed parameters fall into three categories in Supplementary Method 2, comprising techno-economic parameters for system components, projected cost-reduction parameters for future technology scenarios, and key operational and economic parameters used in dispatch and reliability-constrained optimization.

The system is scaled for a standardized 500-person community, which serves as a representative community-scale design basis for the analysis. This benchmark is not intended to reproduce the full infrastructure scale of each island, but rather to enable consistent comparison across diverse geographic, climatic, and socioeconomic contexts. This community-scale representation reflects typical decentralized energy system applications (e.g., microgrids and hybrid renewable systems), where system performance is primarily governed by local resource availability and demand characteristics rather than large-scale infrastructure effects. In line with the International Renewable Energy Agency’s (IRENA) analysis of off-grid systems (Case 7)49, this scale allows for meaningful capacity deployment (typically 0.5–2 MW) while maintaining manageable system complexity. To evaluate the robustness of this assumption, we further test alternative benchmark population sizes spanning 500–10,000 residents (Supplementary Note 3). The results confirm that, although absolute system costs vary with scale, the relative cost distribution and feasibility outcomes remain highly stable across population ranges. For islands with smaller populations, the system is scaled proportionally to their actual population size to maintain per capita energy adequacy, consistent with the standardized community-scale modeling framework.

Island selection criteria

A total of 1830 global islands were selected according to four criteria. Each island had to be inhabited, ensuring socioeconomic relevance; cover a minimum area of at least 10 km2, enabling renewable infrastructure; lie at least 10 km from the mainland, minimizing continental influence; and have a population below 3.5 million, excluding mainland-like energy systems50. Population data were sourced from LandScan51; island area and mainland distance data were obtained from ref. 52. These selected islands are treated as individual analytical units in the global assessment, ensuring that each node represents a comparable and relatively self-contained energy system suitable for decentralized energy analysis, and are further assigned to IPCC AR6 reference regions based on their geographical coordinates (see Supplementary Method 1), providing a standardized and policy-relevant framework for regional aggregation and comparison.

Renewable potential and demand assessment

Hourly solar photovoltaic (PV) and wind turbines (WT) generation profiles were simulated using the Renewables.ninja platform53,54, leveraging historical meteorological data (ERA5, MERRA-2) and performance models. The simulations used data from 2020, with each island represented by its centroid coordinates.

PV systems were modeled as fixed-tilt installations with tilt angles optimized for maximum annual yield. The azimuth was set to south-facing (Northern Hemisphere) or north-facing (Southern Hemisphere). To avoid artificial capacity constraints during optimization, the maximum AC power input was scaled proportionally to the population (2.0 kW person−1), with system losses set at 10%. WT simulations used Vestas V90 2000 turbines at an 80 m hub height, with the maximum AC power input similarly set to 2.0 kW person−1.

Wave energy density (kW m−1) and significant wave height (m) data under the shared socioeconomic pathway 5–8.5 (SSP5–8.5) scenario were sourced from a global wave model dataset55. To fully explore the configuration potential of wave energy facilities in the optimization model, the maximum length for such facilities was set to 1 km per island.

Hourly heating and cooling demands were simulated using the Renewables.ninja methodology56. Heating demand is initiated when the ambient temperature falls below region-specific heating thresholds, while cooling demand is activated when the temperature exceeds cooling thresholds. The calculations incorporate regional parameters as defined in Supplementary Table 9.

The electrical load typically comprises heating/cooling-related demand and a base electricity demand (e.g., for lighting and appliances) that is independent of thermal needs. Given the availability of heating/cooling demand data for the islands, only the base electricity demand was modeled. For modeling simplicity, this base demand was decomposed into an illumination-dependent lighting load and a time-varying, activity-dependent load57, as formulated in Supplementary Eq. 1 in Supplementary Method 3. The demand profiles represent community-scale energy use and primarily reflect residential consumption patterns. The modeled electrical load (including lighting and activity-dependent demand), together with temperature-driven heating and cooling demands, captures the main characteristics of typical island energy use. Commercial and industrial energy demands are treated outside the core representation. Including these sectors would increase overall demand levels and system costs, but is unlikely to alter the identification of broad feasibility patterns primarily driven by affordability constraints.

Climate data for hazard modeling

Typhoon hazards were modeled using 3-hourly near-surface wind speed data from the MRI-AGCM3-2-S_highresSST model from Phase 6 of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6), under the SSP5–8.5 scenario58, accessed via the Earth System Grid Federation (ESGF). This high-resolution model has demonstrated enhanced capabilities in simulating tropical cyclones. These data are used to characterize cyclone-related wind exposure and to derive the hazard intensity metrics used in the reliability module. Together with wave-height inputs for exposed marine energy infrastructure, the Climate Stress scenario represents wind- and wave-related disruptions to exposed energy assets. Storm surge, coastal inundation, and long-term sea-level rise are not explicitly simulated because they require additional island-scale topographic, shoreline-exposure, and asset-location data that are not consistently available at the global scale.

Energy system optimization model

To determine the optimal energy system configuration and operational strategy for each island under various scenarios, a comprehensive mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) model was developed. This optimization model is designed to co-optimize long-term investment decisions (i.e., the capacity of generation and storage assets) and short-term operational scheduling (i.e., the hourly dispatch of energy) to minimize the total annualized cost of the system.

Detailed operational-cost components, technology-specific constraints, and energy-balance equations are provided in Supplementary Eqs. 2–39 in Supplementary Method 4.

The optimization model was implemented as a MILP problem in Python and solved using Gurobi. Each annual optimization was performed over 2920 time steps (2928 for the 2020 base year, a leap year) at 3-h resolution. A relative mixed-integer programming (MIP) gap tolerance of 1% was specified, together with a maximum runtime of 1200 s for each optimization. Additional implementation details and solver settings are provided in Supplementary Method 6. The model’s main constraints include the supply-demand balance for each energy carrier (electricity, heating, and cooling) at each time step, the operational limits of each technology component, and the system reliability constraint described below.

Climate resilience and system reliability

To assess system reliability under climate change, the climate-induced failure dynamics of renewable energy equipment are incorporated into the model. In this study, failure refers specifically to weather-dependent disruptions, including (i) physical damage caused by extreme hazards (e.g., high wind speeds or wave impacts) and (ii) operational shutdowns triggered by safety constraints under severe weather conditions. Random technical failures unrelated to weather (e.g., aging or mechanical faults) are not explicitly modeled, as the focus is on climate-induced risks. Failure modeling is applied only to climate-exposed components, including WT, PV, wave energy converters (WEC), and LNG transport and handling facilities. These components are directly exposed to environmental stressors and are therefore highly sensitive to extreme wind and wave conditions. In contrast, other system components, such as electrical and thermal storage units, electrolyzers, and conversion devices, are typically installed in sheltered or indoor environments (e.g., equipment rooms or protected facilities) and are less directly affected by external climate hazards. As a result, their failure dynamics are not explicitly modeled.

The failure probability of each exposed component is determined using hazard-dependent vulnerability curves parameterized as functions of wind speed or wave height. These curves represent simplified fragility relationships that capture the nonlinear increase in failure likelihood under extreme conditions and are widely used in energy system resilience studies. In practice, detailed fragility curves for energy infrastructure require experimental or proprietary data that are typically not publicly available. As a result, simplified functional representations are commonly adopted in the literature to approximate failure behavior under extreme conditions. In this study, the vulnerability functions are implemented using piecewise linear approximations, which capture the key transition from normal operation to failure while enabling integration within the MILP framework. Accordingly, the failure probability Pfail,i is expressed in Eq. 4 as a function of wind speed Vt or wave height Ht. The full vulnerability functions, parameter values, stochastic failure-state implementation, and availability constraints are provided in Supplementary Eqs. 40–44 in Supplementary Method 5.

Component failure events are simulated using a Monte Carlo approach over a large ensemble (e.g., 10,000) of annual timelines. In these simulations, once a component fails, it remains inoperable until repairs are completed, capturing the persistence of disruption under extreme events. This modeling framework incorporates both damage-induced failures and weather-driven operational disruptions as described above. To manage the computational complexity arising from the large number of stochastic scenarios, a scenario reduction technique is applied. The generated failure timelines are characterized based on key statistical features of failure patterns, including outage duration, frequency, and the co-occurrence of failures across components, and are subsequently grouped using clustering methods. The optimal number of representative scenarios is determined using silhouette analysis, and a representative scenario is selected from each cluster for use in the optimization model. This approach preserves the dominant patterns of climate-induced disruptions while ensuring computational tractability.

System reliability is enforced through a constraint on the normalized expected energy not served (NEENS). The expected energy not served (EENS) is calculated as the probability-weighted sum of energy shortfalls across all representative failure scenarios. The NEENS constraint in Eq. 5 limits unserved energy to less than 0.1% of the total annual demand for each energy type, ensuring a high level of supply security even under climate stress.

Economic feasibility gap assessment

To evaluate the economic feasibility of island energy transitions, a feasibility gap metric is used as a simple and transparent diagnostic measure. This metric links techno-economic system costs with local affordability constraints and enables consistent comparison across islands. The calculation proceeds in three steps.

First, for each island’s transition pathway, the LCOE is adopted as a standard measure of system cost. The LCOE represents the minimum average electricity price required for a project to break even over its lifetime—that is, to achieve a net present value of zero. It reflects the full lifecycle cost of the energy system, including capital investment, operation and maintenance (O&M), fuel, and replacement costs, normalized by total electricity generation. The validity of the modeled LCOE values is further assessed against published island project benchmarks and techno-economic studies (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Table 13).

Second, to approximate local payment capacity, household electricity expenditure is assumed not to exceed 10% of per capita income. This value is treated not as a uniquely correct threshold but as a standardized, globally comparable upper-bound benchmark consistent with international energy-poverty thresholds (typically 6–10%)59–61. Using per-capita income data from the World Bank62 and electricity consumption data for each island, an affordable electricity price representing the maximum socially sustainable tariff is derived.

Finally, the feasibility gap (Gf) is defined in Eq. 6 as the difference between the affordable electricity price and the LCOE.

A positive gap (Gf>0) indicates that the project is economically feasible within local payment capacity. A negative gap (Gf<0) implies that project costs exceed locally affordable payment capacity under current conditions, indicating a need for additional external support to reach economic feasibility.

Sensitivity analysis

To verify the robustness of the main findings, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted on key model assumptions. Overall, parameter variations within plausible ranges did not alter the core conclusions. In several cases, they further reinforced the existence of a large feasibility gap driven by structural inequality.

For the climate model and scenarios, the disaster-risk assessment uses the high-resolution MRI-AGCM3-2-S climate model to represent cyclone-related wind exposure under the high-emission SSP5–8.5 scenario. This setting provides a forward-looking stress test of wind- and wave-related disruptions to exposed energy infrastructure. Storm surge, coastal inundation, and long-term sea-level rise lie beyond the present modeling scope, as representing them would require additional island-scale topographic, shoreline-exposure, and asset-location data. For low-lying islands and atolls, incorporating these hazards would likely increase required resilience investments and deepen the associated feasibility gaps.

For the affordability threshold, the baseline benchmark assumes that electricity expenditure should not exceed 10% of per capita income, consistent with the upper bound commonly used in the energy-poverty literature. To test the robustness of this assumption, we evaluate alternative thresholds of 6, 8, and 12% (in addition to the 10% baseline), spanning the commonly adopted empirical range (see Supplementary Note 4). Across all tested thresholds, the overall distribution of the feasibility gap remains broadly stable, with similar central tendencies and spreads. While adopting a stricter threshold (e.g., 6–8%) lowers the affordability ceiling and shifts the break-even boundary downward, and a more relaxed threshold (e.g., 12%) has the opposite effect, these changes do not materially alter the underlying structure of economic viability across islands. Consistent with this, the aggregate share of economically infeasible islands varies only modestly across thresholds and remains above half in all cases. This indicates that the existence of a widespread feasibility gap is robust to reasonable variations in the affordability assumption, rather than being driven by the specific choice of the 10% benchmark. To further examine the direction of bias introduced by national-average income, we also conducted a stylized lower-income affordability sensitivity. Optimized system costs and LCOE were held unchanged, while each island’s affordability threshold was rescaled to 75, 50, and 25% of the national-average benchmark. These scenarios test how feasibility outcomes change if household payment capacity is lower than the national average income. The sensitivity shows that lower affordability benchmarks increase infeasible shares globally and push Southeast Asia toward near-complete infeasibility under moderate-to-severe reductions, while deepening the affordability shortfall among quadrants 5 and 6 Southeast Asian islands (see Supplementary Note 4).

For future technology costs, the projections are based on the moderate scenario from the NREL ATB, a widely used and policy-neutral representation of expected technological learning. The ATB framework also defines advanced and conservative scenarios to capture uncertainty in future cost trajectories. Because storage costs have experienced rapid declines in recent years, the Moderate pathway should be interpreted as a central technology-learning case rather than a lower-bound estimate of future storage costs. Sensitivity analysis across these scenarios shows that variations in technology cost assumptions shift absolute LCOE levels, with median values ranging from 0.1508 to 0.1982 USD kW h−1, while feasibility outcomes remain largely unchanged, with the share of economically infeasible islands varying between 51.4 and 55.9% (Supplementary Note 5). The advanced ATB pathway represents a faster technology-learning case, including more rapid declines in storage costs. Although this pathway further improves system economics, more than half of the islands remain economically infeasible, indicating that faster storage-cost reductions alone do not remove the affordability constraints that dominate feasibility outcomes in many low-income island contexts.

For the standardized community model, a benchmark community size of 500 people is used to isolate the independent effects of geographic, climatic, and affordability conditions within a globally comparable framework. This approach reflects a community-scale modeling perspective rather than attempting to reproduce full island-scale systems. To evaluate the robustness of this assumption, sensitivity analyses are conducted across alternative benchmark population sizes ranging from 500 to 10,000 residents (see Supplementary Note 3). The results show that while absolute system costs vary with scale, the relative distribution of LCOE remains centered near the 500-person benchmark across all population ranges. Furthermore, feasibility outcomes are highly stable, with 98–100% of islands retaining unchanged feasible/infeasible status across tested scales. These findings indicate that, within the relevant community-scale domain, the identification of the global feasibility gap is not sensitive to the assumed benchmark population size.
