Impact of moodle-based clinically oriented blended teaching on educational environment and clinical competence in dental materials: a prospective quasi‑experimental study
Authors: Kexiao Chen, Zhanyu Huang, Yinan Wang, Xiaoran Zhao, Weini Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental materials science, a foundational discipline in stomatology, faces persistent challenges in traditional pedagogy: voluminous content, outdated textbooks, and a disconnect between theoretical knowledge and clinical application. Students often memorize material parameters passively without developing the capacity to translate this knowledge into real-world clinical decision-making. Blended learning platforms such as Moodle offer a promising avenue to bridge this gap through clinically oriented supplementary instruction. This study aimed to construct and evaluate a Moodle-based, clinically oriented online supplementary course in dental materials, and to examine its differential effects on students at different educational stages.MethodsA prospective quasi-experimental design with pre- and post-test comparisons was employed. A total of 60 undergraduate dental students were enrolled, comprising 30 fourth-year students (pre-clinical, G4) and 30 fifth-year students (clinical internship, G5). All participants completed a 4-week Moodle-based online supplementary course featuring H5P interactive videos, virtual case decision-making modules, and structured peer-review exercises. Learning outcomes were assessed via a self-developed Clinical Case Test (CCT) scored independently by two clinical teachers (ICC > 0.8), a modified Dundee Ready Education Environment Measure (DREEM) inventory (Cronbach’s α = 0.947), and a course satisfaction questionnaire (Cronbach’s α = 0.927). Statistical analysis was performed using paired and independent samples t-tests with α = 0.05.ResultsBoth groups perceived the educational environment positively (overall DREEM score rate 82.35%). After the intervention, G4 students demonstrated a significant improvement in case analysis scores (24.30 ± 4.34 to 28.33 ± 6.76, P = 0.0083), while total CCT scores showed marginal significance (70.83 ± 7.79 to 75.73 ± 11.46, P = 0.0583). G5 students showed no significant change in total scores (75.77 ± 8.17 to 77.53 ± 9.86, P = 0.4532) but significant improvement in case analysis (29.57 ± 4.80 to 33.87 ± 4.97, P = 0.0012). Post-intervention, G4 CCT scores were statistically indistinguishable from G5 baseline scores (P = 0.5170), indicating that the intervention elevated pre-clinical students to near-internship levels. DREEM subscale comparisons revealed G4 scored significantly higher than G5 in Students’ Perception of Learning (SPL: 47.40 ± 6.66 vs. 43.30 ± 9.00, P = 0.050) and Social Self-Perception (SSP: 13.03 ± 1.99 vs. 11.80 ± 2.55, P = 0.042). Course satisfaction was high overall (G4: 87.77 ± 11.74; G5: 83.20 ± 13.33), with 71.67% of students affirming that blended teaching most helped with “knowledge integration and clinical transformation.”ConclusionMoodle-based clinically oriented blended teaching enhances dental students’ clinical case analysis competence, particularly for students at the pre-clinical stage. The platform effectively bridges the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical application, positioning it as a valuable supplementary tool for clinical pre-education. Introduction

Dental materials science constitutes a critical foundational discipline within stomatology, encompassing materials science, biomedical science, and clinical dental practice. With the rapid advancement of knowledge and the continuous emergence of novel biomaterials and application technologies, the field has expanded considerably in both breadth and depth. However, traditional pedagogical approaches in dental materials education face several persistent challenges: voluminous content requiring extensive memorization, textbook materials that lag behind technological innovations, and—most critically—a disconnect between theoretical knowledge and its clinical application [1–3]. The prevailing teacher-centered, knowledge-delivery model often results in students passively memorizing material parameters without developing the capacity to flexibly apply theoretical knowledge to authentic clinical scenarios, thereby failing to meet the demands of comprehensive dental professional training.

Consequently, dental students commonly encounter a predicament upon entering clinical clerkships: their knowledge of dental materials remains disconnected from actual material selection in clinical practice. They struggle to adapt quickly to the integrated judgment of material properties required for clinical decision-making. This gap between pre-clinical theoretical instruction and clinical application represents a well-documented challenge in dental education globally [4].

In recent years, Problem-Based Learning (PBL) and case-oriented teaching have been widely adopted in medical education [5–7]. Their pedagogical core lies in using clinical problems to drive knowledge acquisition and cultivate clinical reasoning abilities. However, in dental materials courses, constraints including limited class hours and uneven distribution of teaching resources make it difficult to provide every student with adequate clinical scenario training within the classroom. With the deepening of educational informatization, blended learning has emerged as an important pathway to resolve this contradiction [8]. Online teaching platforms, with their openness, interactivity, and scalability, provide technical support for constructing clinically oriented supplementary teaching systems. By front-loading systematic learning resources, clinical case libraries, and interactive assessment tools through online platforms, students can autonomously acquire and consolidate professional knowledge beyond foundational theoretical instruction, facilitating a shift from “teacher-centered” to “student-centered” learning.

Research on the systematic application of Moodle platforms for clinically oriented curriculum design in dental materials remains scarce, and differential effectiveness assessments targeting students with varying levels of clinical experience constitute a notable gap in the literature. To address this need, the present study constructed a clinically oriented online supplementary course in dental materials based on the Moodle system. Departing from the traditional linear chapter structure organized by material categories (metals, ceramics, polymers), the course reorganized teaching content around authentic clinical scenarios including caries restoration, fixed prosthodontics, implant restoration, and orthodontics. The course systematically integrated micro-lecture videos, virtual simulation cases, self-test question banks, and discussion forums. A prospective quasi-experimental design was employed, using the modified DREEM inventory, a course satisfaction questionnaire, and pre- and post-test score comparisons to systematically evaluate the impact of this teaching model on students’ clinical case analysis competence, overall educational environment perception, and learning experience across different grade levels.

The central research questions guiding this study were: (1) Can a clinically oriented online supplementary course built on Moodle improve dental students’ knowledge mastery of dental materials, clinical competence, and learning attitudes? (2) Are there differences in the effects of this supplementary teaching between students at pre-clinical and clinical internship stages? We hypothesized that the clinically oriented supplementary course would significantly improve students’ case analysis ability, with greater effects observed in pre-clinical (fourth-year) students compared to those already engaged in clinical internship (fifth-year).

Methods

Study design

A prospective quasi-experimental design with pre- and post-test comparisons was employed. The study was conducted at Shantou University Medical College, China, between February and April 2025. Fourth-year and fifth-year undergraduate dental students were recruited as two independent groups representing distinct educational stages: pre-clinical (G4) and clinical internship (G5).

Participants

Participants were fourth-year and fifth-year undergraduate students majoring in stomatology at Shantou University Medical College. Inclusion criteria were: (1) full-time enrollment; (2) voluntary participation with signed informed consent; (3) independent access to a computer or mobile device with internet connectivity for Moodle platform login. Exclusion criteria were: (1) offline course attendance rate below 80%; (2) students unable to participate throughout the semester due to leave of absence or exchange programs; (3) simultaneous participation in other educational research projects that might cause interference. The dropout criterion was inability to complete the full pre-test—online supplementary course—post-test sequence. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to determine the required sample size. Based on an estimated medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.80) for educational intervention studies in dental education, with α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and power = 0.90, the analysis indicated a minimum requirement of 34 participants per group for independent-samples t-tests and 23 per group for paired-samples t-tests. Given the actual enrollment constraints at Shantou University Medical College and the aforementioned inclusion criteria, the maximum achievable sample was 30 per group after matching. Post-hoc sensitivity analysis confirmed that the final sample size (n = 30 per group) provided 80% power to detect effect sizes of Cohen’s d ≥ 0.74 for independent t-tests and Cohen’s dz ≥ 0.53 for paired t-tests, corresponding to medium-to-large effects by conventional benchmarks. This power is adequate for detecting educationally meaningful improvements, though small effects (d < 0.30) may be underpowered.

Intervention

All enrolled students received a 4-week Moodle-based online supplementary course in dental materials. The course was designed as a clinically oriented supplement to the existing offline theoretical curriculum. Key instructional modules included:

H5P Interactive Videos: Clinical operation videos (e.g., resin composite filling, impression taking) were enhanced with embedded branching decision points at critical procedural nodes, prompting students to make active choices and reinforcing memory of key steps.

These components collectively formed a continuous “learn—think—practice” pedagogical loop. The platform construction and content development required approximately six months, including: teaching team training in the H5P authoring tool (approximately 1 month), course content design and video recording (approximately 2 months), case library construction and scriptwriting (approximately 2 months), and online testing with iterative refinement (approximately 1 month).

The standardized post-test, identical in dimension and difficulty to the pre-test, was administered immediately following the 4-week intervention. The modified DREEM inventory and course satisfaction questionnaire were distributed concurrently after the post-test.

Outcome measures and instruments

Three categories of outcome measures were employed to capture the multidimensional impact of the intervention:

Clinical Case Test (CCT): A self-developed instrument assessing students’ knowledge mastery and clinical competence in dental materials. The test comprised objective questions and case analysis items focusing on material selection rationale, key procedural judgment, and complication management. Case analysis responses were scored independently by two clinical teachers using a standardized rubric. Inter-rater reliability was excellent (ICC > 0.8).

Clinical Case Test (CCT): A self-developed instrument assessing students’ knowledge mastery and clinical competence in dental materials. The test comprised objective questions and case analysis items focusing on material selection rationale, key procedural judgment, and complication management. Case analysis responses were scored independently by two clinical teachers using a standardized rubric. Inter-rater reliability was excellent (ICC > 0.8).

Modified DREEM Inventory: The Dundee Ready Education Environment Measure (DREEM) is a validated 50-item instrument widely used internationally to assess students’ perceptions of their educational environment. For this study, a modified 33-item Chinese version was used, covering five dimensions: Students’ Perception of Learning (SPL, 11 items), Students’ Perceptions of Teachers (SPT, 6 items), Students’ Academic Self-Perceptions (SAP, 6 items), Students’ Perceptions of Atmosphere (SPA, 7 items), and Students’ Social Self-Perceptions (SSP, 3 items). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The total score ranges from 0 to 165, with higher scores indicating more positive perceptions. Internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.947).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Between-group comparisons at baseline and post-intervention were analyzed using independent samples t-tests. Within-group pre- and post-intervention comparisons were analyzed using paired samples t-tests. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. Given the sample size constraints (see Sect. "Participants"), sensitivity power analyses were conducted post hoc using G*Power 3.1 to determine the minimum detectable effect sizes at 80% power.

Following the 4-week intervention, G4 students demonstrated a marginal improvement in total CCT score (70.83 ± 7.79 to 75.73 ± 11.46, P = 0.0583) and a statistically significant improvement in case analysis score (24.30 ± 4.34 to 28.33 ± 6.76, P = 0.0083). This result strongly indicates that the Moodle-based supplementary intervention elevated pre-clinical students’ case analysis competence to a level statistically indistinguishable from that of students already engaged in clinical internships.

Qualitative analysis of open-ended responses revealed that 71.67% of students affirmed that the blended teaching model most helped with “knowledge integration and clinical transformation.” However, 21.67% of students noted that “interaction and communication were insufficient,” and 35% expressed a desire for “personalized guidance.” These findings indicate that while the platform successfully promoted clinical reasoning development, opportunities for interactive and individualized support remain important areas for improvement.

Discussion

The present study provides empirical evidence that a Moodle-based, clinically oriented blended teaching model enhances pre-clinical students’ clinical case analysis competence, with differential effects across educational stages. Fourth-year students—who had completed the offline theoretical course but had not yet entered clinical clerkships—demonstrated significant improvement in case analysis scores following the 4-week intervention, with their post-test performance reaching a level statistically indistinguishable from the baseline performance of fifth-year internship students. After a 4-week intervention, the case analysis scores of fourth-grade students and fifth-grade students significantly improved, but senior students barely moved the needle on total scores. This finding carries important implications for dental education practice and theory.

Clinical competence development and stage-differentiated effects

The significant improvement in G4 students’ case analysis scores (P = 0.0083), combined with the finding that their post-test scores did not differ significantly from G5 baseline scores (P > 0.05), strongly supports the pedagogical value of clinically oriented supplementary instruction for pre-clinical students. Prior to the intervention, G4 students had learned dental materials primarily through didactic lectures and laboratory exercises, with limited exposure to authentic clinical decision-making contexts. The Moodle-based course provided structured opportunities to engage with clinical scenarios, make material selection decisions based on patient-specific factors, and receive immediate feedback—experiences that closely approximate the cognitive demands of clinical practice.

For G5 students, the absence of significant change in total CCT scores (P = 0.4532) alongside significant improvement in case analysis (P = 0.0012) suggests a nuanced pattern of benefit. Fifth-year students, by virtue of their ongoing clinical internships, likely possessed a more complete and organized knowledge base of dental materials. The supplementary course therefore did not add substantial new factual knowledge but rather served to refine and restructure existing knowledge into more efficient clinical decision pathways. This interpretation aligns with the observed improvement in case analysis scores and the high satisfaction ratings in the “Competence and Transfer” dimension (86.50%), suggesting that the platform helped internship-phase students activate and connect their existing knowledge to solve complex clinical problems.

The post-intervention gap remaining between G4 and G5 case analysis scores (28.33 ± 6.76 vs. 33.87 ± 4.97, P = 0.0007) underscores the irreplaceable value of authentic clinical exposure. While the virtual case simulations provided valuable practice in clinical reasoning, the complexity and unpredictability of real patient interactions, combined with tactile and visual feedback from actual clinical procedures, contribute to competence development in ways that digital simulations cannot fully replicate. This finding is consistent with experiential learning theory, which posits that direct experience is a powerful catalyst for transforming abstract knowledge into situated competence [9, 10].

Educational environment perception and DREEM findings

The overall DREEM score rate of 82.35% indicates that students across both groups perceived the Moodle-based learning environment positively. This level of endorsement is comparable to or exceeds DREEM scores reported in previous studies of dental and medical education environments internationally [11, 12].

The differences observed between SPL and SSP dimensions across groups provide valuable insights into understanding the distinctive learning requirements at various stages. G4 students’ higher SPL scores (47.40 ± 6.66 vs. 43.30 ± 9.00, P = 0.050) may reflect their greater benefit from structured, high-quality online resources delivered before clinical exposure. At this educational stage, students are actively building foundational knowledge and welcome comprehensive, well-organized instructional materials that connect theory to clinical application. The H5P interactive videos and virtual cases directly addressed this need by presenting material properties within clinically meaningful contexts, thereby enhancing perceived learning value [13].

Conversely, G5 students’ lower SSP scores (13.03 ± 1.99 vs. 11.80 ± 2.55, P = 0.042) likely reflect their fragmented schedules during clinical internships across different hospital departments, combined with a higher need for social interaction and peer collaboration that the current platform design did not fully satisfy. Fifth-year students are distributed across various clinical rotations, making synchronous peer interaction difficult. This finding highlights the importance of tailoring platform social features to students’ evolving connectivity needs as they progress through the curriculum. For instance, during the clinical internship phase, the “virtual classroom” functionality of the Moodle platform should be enhanced. However, as the DREEM was administered only post‑intervention, these interpretations remain hypotheses that require validation in future studies with longitudinal designs.

Course satisfaction and theoretical implications

The high overall satisfaction scores, particularly in the Cognition and Comprehension (86.77%) and Competence and Transfer (86.50%) dimensions, corroborate the CCT performance findings and provide convergent validity for the intervention’s effectiveness. The strong endorsement of “knowledge integration and clinical transformation” by 71.67% of students in open-ended responses further confirms that students recognized the platform’s value in bridging theory and practice. This qualitative-quantitative convergence strengthens confidence in the conclusion that clinically oriented blended teaching successfully addresses the persistent knowledge-application gap in dental materials education.

The lower satisfaction score in Interaction and Feedback (82.80%), coupled with students’ explicit requests for more personalized guidance and enhanced communication, points to a recognized limitation of asynchronous online learning. In medical education, students at both pre-clinical and clinical stages have strong needs for immediate feedback and expert validation of their clinical reasoning. Pre-clinical students require timely correction of misconceptions about material selection, while clinical interns need opportunities to discuss complex cases with peers and faculty across different specialties. Addressing this gap will require intentional design of synchronous interaction mechanisms—such as scheduled live Q&A sessions, small-group discussion forums moderated by faculty, and peer tutoring systems where advanced students support junior learners.

The finding that G5 students reported lower mean satisfaction and DREEM scores than G4 students, while counterintuitive at first glance, becomes interpretable when considering their differing reference points. Fifth-year students, having been exposed to authentic clinical environments, developed higher expectations for the fidelity and practicality of educational resources. The virtual simulations, while educationally valuable, may have appeared less compelling to students who were already managing real clinical cases. Fourth-year students, by contrast, lacking any clinical exposure, found the simulated cases highly engaging and clinically relevant. This pattern suggests that the Moodle platform’s supplementary value may be greatest during the pre-clinical transition phase, a conclusion that supports positioning the platform as a “clinical pre-education bridge” tool.

Instructional design and observational learning theory

Item-level analysis of the DREEM inventory revealed that G4 students’ highest-scoring items were “teachers serve as clinical role models” (mean 4.467/5) and “online learning resources are rich and diverse” (mean 4.433/5). The co-occurrence of these high scores is theoretically meaningful. Fourth-year students, lacking direct clinical experience, depend heavily on faculty as accessible exemplars of clinical expertise. Through micro-lectures, case demonstrations, and forum discussions, instructors’ analytical reasoning becomes internalized as a cognitive template for students’ own emerging clinical thinking. This process aligns with Bandura’s observational learning theory [14], which posits that learners construct professional competence by imitating the behaviors, language, and decision-making patterns of role models. The finding that teacher role-modeling and resource richness achieved simultaneous high ratings suggests that the current instructional design successfully established an “online knowledge acquisition—offline thinking internalization” pedagogical loop.

For G5 students, the highest-rated items shifted toward practical utility: “anytime-anywhere online learning” (4.10/5) and “clinical case discussions stimulate learning interest” achieved the strongest endorsement. This cross‑sectional difference between G4 and G5 suggests that, compared with their pre‑clinical peers, G5 students placed greater value on flexible resource access and case‑based learning. As students gain hands-on clinical experience, their learning needs evolve from structured knowledge delivery toward flexible, just-in-time resource access and advanced case discussion. This developmental trajectory is consistent with self-regulated learning theory and carries direct implications for adaptive platform design: pre-clinical phases should emphasize instructor modeling and structured scenarios, whereas internship phases should prioritize mobile accessibility, real-world case libraries, and expert consultation channels.

CBE, PBL, and new medicine: empirical validation of an integrated framework

Competency‑Based Education (CBE), Problem‑Based Learning (PBL), and the “New Medicine” initiative form a mutually reinforcing pedagogical logic chain within this study.

The CCT results directly validate the effectiveness of CBE principles. The core of CBE lies in its orientation toward clear, measurable competency goals, emphasizing that learners demonstrate their knowledge integration and decision‑making abilities in real or simulated contexts, rather than merely accumulating study hours or memorizing facts [15]. In the dental materials science course, students are not passive recipients of material parameters, instead, a complete decision-making process must be completed in a simulated clinical scenario, including information collection, performance comparison, material selection, and explanation of the reasons for selection. This exemplifies what CBE terms “integrated competence”, which is the ability to translate discrete material properties into clinical decisions tailored to specific patients and conditions. Furthermore, CBE stresses self‑assessment and reflection. The high score among Grade 5 students on “CCT increases my confidence in my abilities” (4.1/5) indicates that learners developed positive self‑efficacy after completing competency‑oriented tasks — a key psychological mechanism through which CBE promotes deep learning [16–18].

Second, the design and use of H5P interactive videos embody the core advantages of blended learning strategies and concretely implement the “context‑driven” philosophy of PBL. H5P interactive videos embed abstract material performance parameters into authentic clinical scenarios, requiring students to actively make choices and receive immediate feedback during viewing. This instant feedback mechanism reflects the PBL learning cycle of problem‑driven, immediate verification, reflection and adjustment. Unlike traditional post‑class exercises, H5P’s embedded quizzes interrupt linear viewing, forcing students to pause, retrieve information, make judgments, and receive feedback. This process promotes deeper encoding of knowledge and prompt correction of misconceptions more effectively than passive watching.

Overall, this course breaks with the linear structure organized by material categories and reorganizes content around real clinical scenarios such as prosthodontics, implantology, and orthodontics. The overall design aligns with the dual requirements of the “New Medicine” initiative for clinical reasoning ability and digital literacy. The New Medicine movement emphasizes interdisciplinary collaboration (medicine‑engineering integration), competency development, clinical orientation, and the cultivation of literacy for adapting to digital healthcare environments [19]. The convergence between subjective scale ratings and objective CCT improvements demonstrates that digital tools are not isolated technical add‑ons but cognitive scaffolds deeply embedded in the development of clinical reasoning ability.

Implications for platform optimization

The stage-differentiated findings of this study provide an empirical basis for adaptive platform design. For pre-clinical students, the priority should be maintaining and enhancing the “teacher model + structured resources + virtual simulation” triad that proved most effective. For clinical interns, three enhancements are recommended: (1) strengthening mobile-friendly interfaces to support fragmented, on-demand learning during clinical rotations; (2) developing a comprehensive real-world case repository spanning multiple specialties and complication types; and (3) introducing adaptive assessment features that adjust difficulty based on individual performance trajectories. Additionally, implementing “virtual community” functions—such as cross-hospital case-sharing forums and live expert consultations—would address the social belonging needs expressed by fifth-year students.

On the technical front, artificial intelligence offers a feasible pathway to address the persistent gaps in personalised guidance and interactive feedback. As Thurzo [20] argued, AI is evolving from a tool into an autonomous agent, capable of delivering immersive simulations, personalised learning paths, and instant feedback in medical education. For example, integrating an intelligent tutoring system could answer routine questions (e.g., material property comparisons) around the clock, thereby reducing the instructor‘s burden. AI‑driven learning analytics can identify students at risk of falling behind based on their interaction patterns and automatically recommend tailored remedial resources. In parallel, simulated decision‑support tools such as a “material selection assistant” would allow students to input patient‑specific parameters and receive evidence‑based recommendations with graded levels of support, transforming the platform from a passive resource repository into an active cognitive partner — a vision that aligns with Thurzo’s emphasis that AI should complement, rather than replace, the uniquely human elements of empathy, trust, and moral reasoning in medicine. However, implementing AI‑driven decision support also introduces critical challenges regarding data sources, algorithmic bias, and ethical oversight.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the single-center design restricts generalizability. All participants were recruited from one institution (Shantou University Medical College). Although the sample size (n = 60) was adequate to detect the observed effect sizes, it was insufficient to detect small effects and limited the statistical power for subgroup analyses. Replication at multiple dental schools with diverse curricular structures would strengthen external validity. Second, the 4-week intervention duration, while sufficient to detect immediate cognitive gains, does not permit conclusions about long-term retention or transfer to authentic clinical performance. A longitudinal follow-up tracking students into their clinical clerkships and measuring actual clinical decision-making quality would address this gap.

Third, the integration of platform log data (online time, clickstream patterns, resource access frequencies) with achievement and perception outcomes was not completed in this study. Future analyses employing learning analytics techniques could reveal the specific learning behaviors most predictive of clinical competence gains, enabling data-driven personalization of instructional pathways. Finally, the DREEM inventory, while validated internationally, was applied as a post-intervention measure only. To directly evaluate how intervention measures change students’ perception of the educational environment over time, a pre-test and post test design is still needed. Furthermore, although the modified DREEM inventory and course satisfaction questionnaire underwent systematic content validation and pilot testing, the constrained pilot sample size (n = 30) precluded definitive confirmatory factor analysis; the psychometric evidence should be regarded as preliminary, and future studies with larger samples should conduct confirmatory factor analysis to establish structural validity conclusively.

Conclusion

This prospective quasi‑experimental study provides robust empirical evidence that a Moodle-based, clinically oriented blended teaching model enhances dental students’ clinical case analysis competence, particularly for pre-clinical students. The intervention bridged the gap between theoretical knowledge of dental materials and clinical application, elevating fourth-year students’ case analysis performance to a level statistically indistinguishable from that of fifth-year internship students. Students perceived the educational environment positively and reported high satisfaction, especially regarding knowledge integration and clinical competence transfer. The identified stage-differentiated effects provide a compelling rationale for adaptive, stage-tailored platform design in dental education.
