Protocol to identify the signaling pathways of quorum sensing system in Burkholderia cenocepacia
Authors: Shihao Song, Binbin Cui, Xiaohan Kong, Yinyue Deng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for identifying the components of a quorum sensing signaling system in bacteria. We describe the steps for characterizing the novel response regulator and receptor of the cis-2-dodecenoic acid (BDSF) quorum sensing signaling system in Burkholderia cenocepacia. The technical assays of this protocol include generating a random mutant library, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), electrophoretic mobility transfer assay (EMSA), microscale thermophoresis (MST), and molecular simulation docking.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Li et al.1 and Yang et al.2

Before you begin

Many bacterial species employ quorum sensing (QS) to communicate and coordinate their group behavior.3 Our previous studies reported that Burkholderia cenocepacia, a gram-negative pathogen that can cause serious infections in immunocompromised and cystic fibrosis patients, uses the cis-2-dodecenoic acid (BDSF) QS signaling system to control its physiology and virulence.2,3,4,5 Our recent research demonstrated that the fatty acyl-CoA ligase BCAM2136 (designated DsfR), which contains a leucine zipper motif, acts as a downstream transcriptional regulator of the BDSF QS signaling system to control the important biological functions and virulence of B. cenocepacia.1 Here, we used the B. cenocepacia H111 strain as a model bacterium to systematically elucidate the identification process of the components of the BDSF QS signaling system. This provides important reference for the identification of receptors for quorum sensing signaling molecules in the future.

The bclACB operon is controlled by both the BDSF and N-acyl-homoserine lactone (AHL) QS systems in B. cenocepacia H111.6 To fully investigate the regulatory protein of B. cenocepacia, we studied the Tn5 insertion mutant library of B. cenocepacia H111 carrying a lectin-encoding bclACB operon-lacZ promoter fusion plasmid that we previously constructed by collecting colonies that were light blue on LB plates supplemented with X-gal (5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indoyl-β-d-galactoside). Because the insertion mutation of dsfR resulted in a light blue colony with the bclACB operon promoter-lacZ fusion plasmid, we hypothesized that dsfR may control bclACB operon expression levels. Next, we investigated the transcriptional regulatory function of DsfR. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP-Seq) technology was used to analyze the promoter positions of target genes directly bound to DsfR. An electrophoretic mobility transfer assay (EMSA) was used to verify the interaction between DsfR and the promoter DNA of the target genes. We also determined the binding affinity between DsfR and its ligand (BDSF) using microscale thermophoresis (MST). Computational docking was used to predict the bound conformations and active sites of DsfR. The entire protocol requires 3–4 months to complete.

Transposon-mediated random mutagenesis of bacterial genomes has been demonstrated as a potent genetic technique for the discovery of genes and regulatory elements that influence particular phenotypes. This protocol involves the introduction of Tn5 transposons in B. cenocepacia H111(PbclACB-lacZ) to generate random libraries of mutants.1.Construction of the B. cenocepacia H111(PbclACB-lacZ) reporter strain.a.Extract the pME2-lacZ plasmid (pME6010 carrying a full-length lacZ) and digest it with HindIII and XhoI enzymes. Recover the resulting digestion products for future use.b.Use the genomic DNA from B. cenocepacia H111 as a template to amplify the bclACB gene cluster promoter with primers PbclACB-F and PbclACB-R.c.Connect the bclACB promoter sequence to the pME2-lacZ plasmid using T4 DNA ligase (NEB).d.Screen for the positive recombinant PbclACB -lacZ plasmid.i.Transform the aforementioned products into DH5α chemically competent cells (18265017, Thermo Scientific).ii.Spread them on LB plates supplemented with 20 μg/mL tetracycline and 40 μg/mL X-gal.iii.After a 12 h incubation period at 37°C, proceed to screen the positive clones.Note: Please ensure that all aforementioned procedures are conducted under aseptic conditions.e.Preparation of electrocompetent cells of B. cenocepacia H111.i.Culture B. cenocepacia H111 strain at 37°C in LB medium.ii.When the culture medium reached an OD600 of 1.0, chill the culture for 5 min and centrifugate at 4°C for 8 min at 5,000 × g.iii.Wash the cells twice by gentle resuspension in 20 mL of ice-cold 10% glycerol, followed by centrifugation as above.iv.Resuspend the washed cells in 10% glycerol or either use them directly for electrotransformation.f.Validate the recombinant vector through sequencing, and then electroconvert it into the electro competent B. cenocepacia H111 cells.g.The electrotransformation program is as follows: Add 0.1 μg recombinant plasmid to 100 μL of thawed competent cells.Note: The parameters for setting the electric shock conditions are 2.5 kV and 5 ms, respectively. The electrical conversion efficiency of host bacteria needs to be greater than 109 cfu/μg DNA.h.Immediately recover the electroporated cells with 1 mL fresh SOC liquid medium after electroporation.i.Transfer to a tube and incubate on a shaker at 37°C for 60 min to facilitate cell outgrowth.j.Spread them on LB plates supplemented with 20 μg/mL tetracycline and 40 μg/mL X-gal. After a 12 h incubation period at 37°C, proceed to screen the positive clones.k.Prepare the above-mentioned B. cenocepacia H111(PbclACB-lacZ) into the electrocompetent cells (Figure 1A).Figure 1Graphical depiction of the process involved in generating a random mutant library(A) Transform the pME2-lacZ fusion plasmid, which contains the promoter of bclACB, into B. cenocepacia H111.(B) Introduce the EZ-Tn5 transposon into the B. cenocepacia H111(PbclACB-lacZ) strain via electroporation. Expected outcomes

This protocol describes the complete experimental protocol for identifying the components of QS signaling pathways in bacteria. By constructing a random mutant library, we aimed to identify regulatory proteins that are linked to the regulation of the target gene. The formation of a light blue colony containing the bclACB operon promoter lacZ fusion plasmid was attributed to the insertion mutation of Tn5 transposons. The use of inverse PCR and DNA sequencing technology to verify the precise integration site of transposons can facilitate the identification of regulatory proteins responsible for modulating the expression of target genes. To confirm the downstream transcriptional regulatory proteins of the QS signaling system, the promoter binding sequence of the target gene, which interacts directly with DsfR, can be identified using ChIP-Seq technology. The interaction between DsfR and the promoter can be confirmed using EMSA. For the confirmation of receptor proteins, the binding affinity between DsfR and BDSF will be determined using MST. The anticipated binding conformation and active site of DsfR with the ligand can be predicted using docking calculations. The DsfR and ligand binding sites were determined by conducting additional genetic assays and in vitro binding experiments. By conducting thorough research and analysis, the signaling pathways of QS can be comprehensively analyzed.

Limitations

Although this protocol can identify QS signaling pathways in bacteria, it has several limitations. First, the analysis of signal receptor binding conformations depends on computer-simulated molecular docking, which may deviate from the actual three-dimensional structure of the receptor protein, potentially influencing the analysis outcome. Second, in contrast to receptor proteins possessing transmembrane structures, this experimental protocol is better suited for the identification of intracellular receptor proteins in bacteria. Transmembrane proteins often exhibit intricate structural features, such as multiple domains, loops, and transmembrane fragments, which can pose challenges for their expression, purification, and structural analysis. Transmembrane proteins possess extensive hydrophobic domains that traverse the lipid bilayer, securing their attachment to the membrane. Consequently, challenges may arise in the expression of these proteins in aqueous environments, such as misfolding, aggregation, or degradation. The aforementioned factors have impeded the subsequent functional and structural investigation of transmembrane proteins.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The electrical conversion rate of transposons is insufficient (2. construction of a random mutant library, Steps a-b).

Potential solution

The number of transposon clones is dependent on the transformation efficiency of competent bacterial cells. This experiment should be performed with freshly prepared competent bacterial cells, and the electroporated cells should be immediately recovered after electroporation. Even slight delays in initiating the cell recovery process will result in a reduced number of transposition clones.

Problem 2

The ChIP-Seq datasets have considerable background noise (4. ChIP-Seq and ChIP-Seq analysis, step g).

Potential solution

Elevated levels of background noise in ChIP-Seq experiments can lead to a reduction in the signal-to-background ratio, thereby hindering the accurate identification of peaks. Immunoprecipitation was conducted with the minimal necessary quantity of antibody to avoid nonspecific binding and subsequent background noise.

Problem 3

The resolution of the EMSA experimental complex is insufficient (5. EMSA, step j).

Potential solution

The resolution of protein complexes is contingent upon their stability throughout the process of electrophoresis. The composition and concentration of the gel and running buffer components may be modified to increase the stability of the complexes. The addition of low-molecular-weight cofactors or nonspecific stabilizers such as glycerol or ethylene glycol in gel buffers and running buffers can increase the stability of complexes.

Problem 4

During capillary scanning, the initial fluorescence of the sample appears as a split peak (6. MST assay, steps b-d).

Potential solution

The inclusion of descaling agents (0.05% Tween 20 and 0.1% Pluronic F-127) in the binding buffer may attenuate the impact of adsorption.

Problem 5

The trajectories of the MST exhibit irregularities, suggesting the existence of aggregates (6. MST assay, steps c-e).

Potential solution

The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C before being transferred into the capillaries.
