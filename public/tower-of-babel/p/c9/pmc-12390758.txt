Integrative multi-omic analysis reveals a PAX8-driven gene network linking tumor stemness to therapy response in ovarian cancer
Authors: José M Santos-Pereira, Amancio Carnero, Sandra Muñoz-Galván
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transcription factor PAX8 is expressed in most ovarian tumors, being associated with increased tumorigenesis. Although recent studies have addressed the gene regulatory functions of PAX8 in ovarian cancer, an integrative analysis of multi-omic and patient data is required to identify the core regulatory network of PAX8 and its prognostic and therapeutic value. Here, we integrate PAX8 chromatin binding and accessibility data in ovarian cancer cells with transcriptomic and patients’ data to gain insight into the core gene regulatory network orchestrated by PAX8 in ovarian tumors. Integration of differential chromatin accessibility, transcription factor binding, and gene expression upon PAX8 knockout provides a core regulatory network that explains most of the genes regulated by PAX8. We combine these target genes with patient expression data and find a PAX8 gene signature associated with tumor stemness, a property related to therapy resistance. Indeed, we show that the PAX8 gene signature predicts disease outcome and response to therapy in ovarian cancer patients. Finally, we validated experimentally our results from bioinformatic analyses, thus reassuring their robustness. Our findings uncover a PAX8 core network that represents a promising strategy for targeted antitumor therapies and open new pathways to fight against ovarian cancer resistance.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is the most lethal gynecological malignancy and is responsible of 4.7% of cancer-related deaths in women worldwide [1]. Histologically, OC can be divided in epithelial tumors (90% of cases), germ cell tumors (3% of cases), and sex cord-stromal tumors (2% of cases), of which epithelial tumors can be classified in serous, endometroid, clear cell, and mucinous [2]. While type I tumors comprises low-grade serous OC, endometroid, clear cell, and mucinous carcinoma, as well as non-epithelial tumors, type II tumors comprise high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSC), which is the most frequent (70%–80% of cases) and malignant [3]. It is becoming increasingly accepted that the preneoplastic lesions leading to HGSC are mostly originated in the fallopian tube epithelium (FTE), while the origin in the ovarian surface epithelium only occurs in some OC types [4, 5]. These early lesions in the fallopian tubes are called serous tubal intraepithelial carcinomas and have common features with the originated tumors, such as TP53 mutations or increased expression of cell cycle and DNA repair genes [6–8]. Another common feature between the FTE and HGSC is the consistent expression of PAX8, a lineage-specific transcription factor (TF) with essential roles during embryonic development and tumorigenesis.

The paired box (PAX) family of lineage-specific TFs is composed of nine members in mammals, Pax1–9, with multiple roles during embryogenesis and cancer development [9]. They are classified in four groups based on their structural features and expression patterns during embryonic development and can have up to four functional domains [10]: an N-terminal paired domain composed of PAI and RED sub-domains with DNA-binding activity, a conserved octapeptide motif, a partial homedomain that can also have DNA-binding activity, and a C-terminal transactivation domain. Pax TFs can form heterodimers with other homeodomain-containing TFs (e.g. HOX and SOX factors) [11], and some of them have been shown to have pioneering activity, i.e. they are able to recognize their DNA-binding motif within chromatin in a non-accessible conformation [12]. PAX8 is a master TF with roles in embryonic development and cancer [10,13]. During embryogenesis, PAX8 is expressed and required for the development of the thyroid gland, the renal system, and the reproductive track [14]. PAX8−/− mice show thyroid hypoplasia and die shortly after weaning [15], and although thyroid hormone replacement was able to allow mutant mice to survive, they are infertile, showing also abnormalities in the Müllerian and efferent ducts [16, 17]. In female adults, PAX8 is expressed in the FTE, in secretory cells that can self-renew and differentiate into ciliated cells to maintain the oviduct function [18]. As said above, PAX8 expression is maintained in those tumors, and its overexpression leads to increased survival and proliferation of tumor cells [19–21]. Indeed, PAX8 is amplified in HGSC patients and highly expressed in endometrial and OC patients [22–24].

In recent years, several studies have attempted to understand the role of PAX8 in OC development at the transcriptomic and epigenomic levels by taking advantage of next-generation sequencing techniques as RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, and ATAC-seq [23–30]. Although they have provided valuable new data contributing to ascertain PAX8 function in OC, the heterogeneity of the results likely due to the use of different OC cell lines, requires an integrative analysis that provides new clues about the gene regulatory network controlled by PAX8 and the possible factors that cooperate with it. Here, we perform an integrative multi-omic analysis of OC data to find genes that could be used as therapeutic targets for the treatment of ovarian tumors. For this, we have combined PAX8 chromatin binding data (ChIP-seq, CUT&RUN, and CUT&Tag) and chromatin accessibility data (ATAC-seq) with TF binding prediction in OC cell lines to obtain the TF network controlled by PAX8 in HGSC. Integrated analyses of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq in OC cells upon PAX8 knockout with OC patient databases allowed us to identify the core of the gene regulatory network orchestrated by PAX8 in OC and to define a PAX8 gene signature whose high expression predicts disease outcome and patient survival. Finally, we validated experimentally our discoveries in OC cell lines.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Cells were cultured according to the manufacturer’s recommended procedures in McCoy (MES-OV line) or RPMI (CAOV-3 and OVCAR3 lines) and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere.

Gene transfer

The gene transfer was performed as previously described [24]. The two small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) against PAX8 were provided by Thermo Fisher.

Sphere-forming assay

In total, 2 × 103 cells were resuspended in 1 ml of complete MammoCultTM Basal Medium (Stemcell Tech) and seeded in ultralow attachment plates. The cultures were imaged, the tumorspheres were counted, and their diameters were quantified using CellSenseDimension software on Days 2, 3, and 4.

Western blot analyses

Western blots were performed according to standard procedures. Information of antibodies and dilutions is shown in Supplementary Table S1.

RT–qPCR

The total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen), and complementary DNA was generated from 1 μg of RNA with MultiScribe Reverse Transcriptase (Applied Biosystems). Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) was performed using a TaqMan Assay (Applied Biosystems) with probes. The relative messenger RNA (mRNA) expression was calculated as 2−ΔCt relative to the ACTB gene. Information of probes is shown in Supplementary Table S1.

ChIP-seq, CUT&RUN, and CUT&Tag analyses

ChIP-seq reads from OVCAR3 cells (PRJNA655844) were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 human genome using Bowtie [25] with parameters -m 1 -S –chunkmbs 500. CUT&RUN reads from Kuramochi and FT282 cells (GSE227779) and CUT&Tag reads from COV362 cells (PRJNA749469) were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 human genome using Bowtie2 [26] with parameters –local –very-sensitive-local –no-unal –no-mixed –no-discordant –phred33 -I 10 -X 700. Conversion of SAM alignment files to BAM and duplicate removal were performed using Samtools 1.9 [27]. Conversion of BAM to BED files, and peak analyses, such as overlaps or merges, were carried out using the Bedtools 2.29.2 suite [28]. Conversion of BED to BigWig files was performed using the genomecov tool from Bedtools and the wigToBigWig utility from UCSC [29]. Peaks were called using MACS2 2.1.1.20160309 algorithm [30]. For ChIP-seq, an FDR < 0.0001 was used for each replicate, and common peaks were selected for further analyses. For CUT&RUN and CUT&Tag, where only one replicate was available, and FDR < 0.001 was used. For visualization purposes, reads were extended 300 bp.

Heatmaps and k-means clustering were generated using computeMatrix and plotHeatmap tools from the Deeptools 3.5 toolkit [31]. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and correlation analyses were performed using the multiBigwigSummary, plotPCA and plotCorrelation tools from the same toolkit. TF motif enrichment was calculated using the Simple Enrichment Analysis (SEA) algorithm [32] with standard parameters. The obtained motifs were grouped by TF families, and the most significant motif of each family was plotted. Determination of target genes for the genomic regions with differential TF binding was performed using the GREAT 3.0.0 tool [33], with the basal plus extension association rule with standard parameters (5 Kb upstream, 1 Kb downstream, 1 Mb maximum extension).

ATAC-seq analysis

ATAC-seq reads from CAOV3, COV362, Kuramochi, OVCAR3, OVCAR8, and FT282 cells (GSE114964, PRJNA749469, GSE227779, DRA010685) were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 human genome as previously described [34]. Briefly, we used Bowtie2 [26] for read alignment with parameters -t –no-unal –no-mixed -X 2000. A minimum of 130 bp of fragment length was used to select nucleosome-free regions, and the precise Tn5 cutting site was determined as the position −4 (minus strand) or +5 (plus strand) from each read start, and this position was extended 5 bp in both directions. Conversion of SAM alignment files to BAM and duplicate removal were performed using Samtools 1.9 [27]. Conversion of BAM to BED files, and peak analyses, such as overlaps or merges, were carried out using the Bedtools 2.29.2 suite [28]. Conversion of BED to BigWig files was performed using the genomecov tool from Bedtools and the wigToBigWig utility from UCSC [29]. Peaks were called using MACS2 2.1.1.20160309 algorithm [30] with parameters –nomodel –extsize 100 –shift -45 -q 0.01 -g 3.3e9. For visualization purposes, reads were extended 100 bp.

Differential accessibility analysis of control and PAX8ko COV362 cells (PRJNA749469) was performed using DESeq2 1.18.1 package in R 3.4.3 [35] from merged peaks called with MACS2 with FDR < 0.05, and setting a corrected P < .05 as the cutoff for statistical significance of the differential accessibility. Heatmaps were generated using computeMatrix and plotHeatmap and tools from the Deeptools 3.5 toolkit [31]. PCA and correlation analyses were performed using the multiBigwigSummary, plotPCA and plotCorrelation tools from the same toolkit. TF motif enrichment was calculated using the SEA tool [32] with standard parameters. The obtained motifs were grouped by TF families and the most significant motif of each family was plotted.

For footprinting analyses, we used TOBIAS 0.12.9 [36]. First, we performed bias correction using ATACorrect and calculated footprint scores with ScoreBigwig, both with standard parameters. Then, we used BINDetect to determine the differential TF binding for all vertebrate motifs in the JASPAR database [37] and for one-to-one comparisons among HGSC and FTE cell lines and among control and PAX8ko COV362 cells. Aggregated ATAC-seq signal at footprints was visualized using PlotAggregate. Finally, the pool of TF binding sites from differentially bound TFs between control and PAX8ko COV362 cells was used to build a TF-gene activation network starting in PAX8 and reaching three interaction levels. This network was visualized using Cytoscape v3.8.2 [38]. Determination of target genes for the genomic regions with differential TF binding was performed using the GREAT 3.0.0 tool [33], with the basal plus extension association rule with standard parameters (5 Kb upstream, 1 Kb downstream, 1 Mb maximum extension). Overlap among genomic regions containing TF footprints was calculated by extending footprints ±100bp.

RNA-seq analysis

RNA-seq reads from control and PAX8ko COV362 cells (PRJNA749469) were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 human genome using STAR 2.5.3a [39] and counted using the htseq-count tool from the HTSeq 0.8.0 toolkit [40]⁠. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 1.18.1 package in R 3.4.3 [35] setting a P value < 0.05 as the cutoff for statistical significance of the differential expression. Enrichment of GO Biological Process terms was calculated using David 2021 [41] with an FDR < 0.05 as statistical cutoff. Heatmaps were plotted using pheatmap 1.0.12 package in R 3.4.3 (https://github.com/raivokolde/pheatmap).

HiC analyses

HiC paired-end reads from OVCAR3 cells (GSE195677) were mapped to the GRCh38/hg38 human genome using BWA [42]. Then, ligation events (HiC pairs) were detected and sorted, and PCR duplicates were removed, using the pairtools package [43]. Unligated and self-ligated events (dangling and extra-dangling ends, respectively) were filtered out by removing contacts mapping to the same or adjacent restriction fragments. The resulting filtered pairs file was converted to a tsv file that was used as input for Juicer Tools 1.13.02 Pre85, which generated multiresolution hic files. These analyses were performed using published scripts [44]: the hic_pipe.py script was first used to generate tsv files with the filtered pairs, and the filt2hic.sh script was then used to generate Juicer hic files. HiC matrices at 10 Kb resolution, normalized with the Knight–Ruiz method [45], were extracted for downstream analysis. Visualization of normalized HiC matrices, insulation scores and topologically associated domain (TAD) boundaries were calculated and visualized using FAN-C [46]. TAD boundaries were called using the insulation score method [47]. Briefly, the average number of interactions of each bin were calculated in 250-Kb square sliding windows (25 × 25 bins); then, these values were normalized as the log2 ratio of each bin’s value and the mean of all bins to obtain the insulation score for each bin; next, minima along the insulation score vector were calculated using a delta vector of ±100 Kb (±10 bins) around the central bin. The genomic regions located between adjacent boundaries were considered as TADs, and the Pax TAD defined as the genomic interval chr2:113202500-113442500.

Patient data analyses

We performed the meta-analyses of gene expression in OC patients using the R2 Genomics analysis and visualization platform (http://hgserver1.amc.nl) to analyze the PAX8 expression levels in tumor and non-tumor ovarian samples, or in patients with complete or partial response to treatment, resistant or sensitive; to determine the PAX8-correlated genes; and to plot heatmaps of patient expression. To study PAX8 expression between tumoral and non-tumoral samples, we used the GSE18520, GSE40595, and GSE38666 databases with paired tumoral and non-tumoral sample data; to analyze gene expression only in tumoral samples, we also included GSE73168 and GSE204748 databases, which do not have control samples. The statistical significance of expression values was assessed using the Students’ t-test (P < .05) and the cutoff for correlation analyses was established in a Pearson’s R > 0.4 or < −0.4. Heatmaps show the z-score of gene expression. Hierarchical clustering was calculated using the average linkage method with Euclidean distances. Chemotherapy response was described as the clinical status of the patient after the first-line treatment and defined as complete versus partial response. Regarding platinum sensitivity, tumors were classified as sensitive when the disease-free survival was higher than 180 days and resistant when it was equal or lower than 180 days. Enrichment of GO Biological Process terms was calculated using David 2021 [41] with an FDR < 0.05 as statistical cutoff. Venn diagrams were drawn using the webtool of the Bioinformatics & Evolutionary Genomics group at the University of Gent (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be).

Patient survival was analyzed in databases with available survival data using the Kaplan–Meier plotter platform [48] with the scan method, which searches for the optimum survival cutoff based on statistical analyses (log-rank test), thereby identifying the most significant expression cutoff. The PAX8 signature expression was analyzed using the ovarian samples of TNMplot.com [49], which includes RNA-seq of 1341 unique OC samples from the GEO, GTex, TCGA, and TARGET databases. TNMplot applies a standardized normalization and batch effect correction pipeline prior to data integration to adjust for inter-dataset variability while preserving true biological signals. We used the gene signature analysis to calculate the mean of the selected genes across patients in a one-by-one basis and to provide a summary plot using RNA-seq data.

Results

Expression and regulation of PAX8 in ovarian cancer

Previous studies showed that PAX8 is highly expressed in ovarian tumors [22, 50]. To study in deep the relevance of PAX8 expression in OC, we first tested its expression levels in three public databases (GSE40595, GSE38666, and GSE18520) [51–59]. These databases comprise HGSC patients with available non-tumoral samples, being HGSC the most frequent and malignant OC subtype. We found that PAX8 expression was significantly increased in tumoral versus non-tumoral samples in the three databases (Fig. 1A), consistent with a reported role of PAX8 in ovarian tumors [22, 50]. Since PAX8 is a master regulator in OC cells, we reasoned that the study of its gene regulatory network could help to identify core genes relevant for tumorigenesis and possible vulnerabilities to OC treatment.

It is well known that PAX8 is a marker of OC and the secretory FTE, which has been proposed as the tissue of origin of most ovarian tumors [5]; however, little is known about gene regulation upstream and downstream of PAX8. First, we aimed to gain insight into the possible mechanisms activating PAX8 expression in OC. For this, we inspected chromatin 3D physical contacts within the PAX8 locus in the OVCAR3 cell line by genome-wide chromosome conformation data (HiC) [52]. This revealed an intense interaction signal around the PAX8 gene that was compatible with the formation of a TAD and that we defined as the PAX8 TAD (Fig. 1B).

Next, we reasoned that the putative cis-regulatory elements (i.e. mainly promoters and enhancers) located within the PAX8 TAD could be responsible for PAX8 expression, and that the analysis of TF binding sites within these enhancers would provide new clues about PAX8 gene regulation. To search for enhancers active in OC cells, we analyzed published ATAC-seq data, which allowed the identification of open chromatin regions [53]. We found 25 open chromatin regions within the PAX8 TAD in OVCAR3 cells (Fig. 1B). In order to infer possible regulators of PAX8 expression, we first searched for TF motifs enriched in these regions and found 16 enriched motifs (Fig. 1B). However, the mere presence of a motif does not imply that the TF is actively bound. Since TF-bound regions are protected from TAGmentation by the Tn5 enzyme (see Methods), we overcame this limitation and predicted TF binding by calculating footprints from the ATAC-seq signal around motif sequences [36]. It is worth mentioning that calculating TF footprints from ATAC-seq data using Tn5 bias correction leads to the reliable identification of footprints for ∼65% of TFs [36], and therefore there may be other TFs involved in PAX8 expression that are not detected using this approach. We found footprints for 13 out of the 16 TFs with enriched motifs distributed within the PAX8 TAD (Fig. 1B). Finally, we observed three regions within the PAX8 TAD with higher contact frequencies with the PAX8 promoter (Fig. 1B) and hypothesized that the TF binding sites located within them may be more relevant for PAX8 regulation in OC cells. These regions included putative cis-regulatory elements with SOX4, SOX6, USF3, MEF2A, and HOXC11 footprints (Fig. 1B), suggesting that these factors may participate in the regulation of PAX8 expression. Altogether, these data indicate that PAX8 regulation in OC cells may be controlled by different lineage-specific regulators that have been involved in cancer as proto-oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes, with PAX8 acting as a readout of these activities.

Differential transcription factor networks operate in ovarian cancer and fallopian tube epithelium cells

Since PAX8 is a master TF with high expression in ovarian tumors, the analysis of its downstream gene regulatory network could provide new partner regulators with relevance for therapy. To this end, we first used available chromatin binding data of PAX8 in three HGSC tumoral cell lines and one non-tumoral FTE cell line [23, 54, 55] (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B). Surprisingly, we found that only 0.8% of all the identified PAX8 binding sites were common among the four cell lines, while 88.6% were cell-line specific (Fig. 1C), indicating a high heterogeneity of PAX8 binding sites across these cell lines. Next, we associated the PAX8 binding sites to their putative target genes and found that 24.3% of genes were shared among all cell lines (Fig. 1D), while only 26.5% of genes were cell-line specific. This suggests that PAX8 may regulate a common gene regulatory network through a rewired network of chromatin binding sites in these cell lines. Several examples illustrate the differential binding profile of PAX8 near common target genes, including PAX8 itself, EPHA2, and SOX17 loci (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

Next, we sought to identify cell line-specific regions bound by PAX8. For this, we performed unsupervised clustering of all PAX8 binding sites across cell lines and found six clusters: clusters 1 and 4 were highly bound by PAX8 in COV362 cells; clusters 2 and 5 in OVCAR3 cells; cluster 3 in Kuramochi cells; and cluster 6 in FT282 cells (Fig. 1E). Next, we searched for enriched TF motifs in these clusters and found a high enrichment of the PAX motif in all clusters (Fig. 1F), as expected. Other enriched motifs allowed us to identify possible PAX8 partners in gene regulation. These included the families AP-1, C2H2 zinc finger (including KLF and SP families), TEAD, SOX, Forkhead, and MADS, among others, which showed different enrichment degrees across cell lines. It is worth mentioning that the GATA and Runx families were highly enriched specifically in the cluster corresponding to PAX8 binding sites non-tumoral cells, suggesting that these TFs may act as PAX8 partners specifically in the FTE but not in OC. Altogether, these data suggest that PAX8 regulates target genes by using different sets of regulatory elements and different TF partners between tumoral and non-tumoral cells.

We aimed to deepen into the possible factors that regulate the OC gene regulatory network. For this, we analyzed ATAC-seq data, which provides open chromatin regions interpreted as cis-regulatory elements, of the previous cell lines and expanded our study by including the CAOV3 and OVCAR8 HGSC cell lines [53–55] (Supplementary Fig. S2). Taking again advantage of the ability of ATAC-seq data to estimate TF binding based on footprints, as shown above (Fig. 1B), we first calculated the TFs with the highest changes in chromatin binding across cell lines and found very similar families to the ones identified by PAX8 binding data, including PAX, AP-1, SOX, zinc fingers, and GATA families, among others (Fig. 2A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S3). We confirmed the reliability of our TF footprint analyses by comparing the footprints in bound versus unbound motifs (i.e. sites with and without detected footprints; Supplementary Fig. S3) and by analyzing real chromatin binding in bound versus unbound motifs for the TFs PAX8 and SOX17 (Supplementary Fig. S4). The one-to-one comparison of TF binding among cell lines showed a high heterogeneity across tumoral cells (Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Dataset). However, the comparison of TF binding between non-tumoral and tumoral cells showed a high binding of AP-1 and GATA families in non-tumoral cells compared with any of the analyzed tumoral cell lines, while the latter ones showed a high binding of the KLF and SP families of zinc finger TFs (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Dataset). These data suggest a differential role of KLF and SP factors in ovarian tumors compared with its tissue of origin, the fallopian tube, where AP-1 and GATA families may play a more relevant role.

PAX8 controls a network of transcription factors in ovarian cancer cells

While the previous analyses highlight differences in TF binding among cell lines, we wondered whether PAX8 could be responsible for these differences by affecting chromatin accessibility directly or indirectly. For this, we first calculated differential chromatin accessibility between COV362 OC cells control and PAX8 knockout (PAX8ko) [54] to identify genomic regions with increased or decreased accessibility upon PAX8 elimination, which would correspond to regions where PAX8 is inhibiting or promoting accessibility, respectively (Fig. 3A). Next, we combined these data with chromatin binding of PAX8 (Fig. 1) to identify regions in which differential accessibility occurs as a direct (PAX8 binding) or indirect (no PAX8 binding) consequence of PAX8 absence. We observed that ∼20% of differentially accessible regions in PAX8ko showed PAX8 binding in control cells (Fig. 3B). We then calculated enriched motifs and found that PAX motifs were highly enriched in regions with decreased accessibility in PAX8ko (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, regions showing an indirect decrease in chromatin accessibility were highly enriched in AP-1 and C2H2 zinc-finger motifs, including the KLF and SP families, consistent with the comparison of tumoral and non-tumoral cells (Fig. 2C). This suggests that these TFs may act downstream of PAX8 to promote chromatin accessibility in OC cells. Several examples illustrate direct and indirect changes in chromatin accessibility, such as the SOX9 and NKX1-1 loci (Fig. 3D). These data indicate that PAX8 rewires chromatin accessibility in OC cells both by direct chromatin binding and by altering the binding of downstream regulators.

As said, the analysis of motifs within accessible regions is not a direct evidence of active TF binding. To overcome this limitation, we measured differential TF binding due to PAX8 absence based on the footprints detected by ATAC-seq in OC cells. We found that the absence of PAX8 led to a high decrease in the binding of PAX factors, as expected due to PAX8ko, which was validated by a decrease of PAX8 chromatin binding around PAX8 footprints upon its knockout (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S6). The next family with the highest decrease in binding was the TEAD family involved in Hippo signaling pathway, including TEAD1-4, followed by multiple SOX proteins, which are involved in pluripotency and differentiation of multiple cell lineages [56] (Fig. 4A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S6). This suggests that PAX8 could be mediating target gene regulation in OC cells through these TFs. On the other hand, there were several motif families showing increased binding in PAX8ko cells, including the AP-1 family of bZIP factors and the KLF and SP families of C2H2 zinc finger factors (Fig. 4A and B), which are involved in diverse cellular functions as proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [57]. This suggests that these TFs may be repressed by PAX8.

(C) TF-gene activation network of PAX8 calculated from differentially bound TFs in panel (A) until the third interaction level. (D) Venn diagrams showing the overlap among genomic regions bound by PAX8, TFAP2A, KLF4, and SP3 (top), or among genes associated to these regions (bottom).

Next, we aimed to explore how these activities potentially regulated by PAX8 were connected. To verify this, we simulated the activation cascade triggered by PAX8 by constructing a TF-gene activation network. This network is based on the presence of footprints within enhancer or promoter regions of target TF genes. [36]. We limited this analysis to the genes encoding factors that we identified as differentially bound in PAX8ko cells (Fig. 4A and B). We began by placing PAX8 at the first level and identified its direct targets, which form the second level, by searching for PAX8 footprints near TF genes. Next, we searched for footprints of the second level near other TF genes to identify indirect PAX8 targets, constituting the third level. The analysis of this network showed that PAX8, either directly or indirectly, regulates the expression of 49 genes encoding regulators, which were found to be differentially bound to the genome following PAX8 knockout. (Fig. 4C). Among them, there were three factors whose interaction networks were clearly more prominent, regulating most of the PAX8 downstream targets: TFAP2A, KLF4, and SP3. Other downstream factors included members of the SOX and TEAD families, as well as members of the AP-1, MADS, KLF, and SP families, among others (Fig. 4C). We next analyzed the overlap among genomic regions showing binding of PAX8, TFAP2A, KLF4, and SP3 and found that most of them were TF-specific, except for a higher overlap between the closely related KLF4 and SP3 factors. Overall, there were only 0.025% of regions showing co-binding of the four regulators (Fig. 4D). However, we found a much higher overlap among the genes targeted by these binding sites, with 10.5% of common genes (Fig. 4D), suggesting that PAX8 and its main partners co-regulate common genes through distinct regulatory elements.

Taking together, our observations indicate that PAX8 regulates chromatin accessibility in OC by an activation cascade of TFs that includes TFAP2A, KLF4, and SP3 as the most likely core network.

Transcriptomic analysis of PAX8 absence in HGSC cells

We wanted to test whether PAX8-driven alterations in chromatin accessibility had an impact in the transcriptome. For this, we analyzed RNA-seq data in control and PAX8ko cells [54] and calculated differentially expressed genes (Fig. 5A and B). Next, we wondered whether the PAX8 TF core network could explain the misregulation of these genes. For that, we took all the binding sites of PAX8, TFAP2A, KLF4, and SP3 detected by footprint analyses (Fig. 4) and assigned them to nearby genes, identifying putative target genes. Of these genes, 77.2% were also differentially expressed in PAX8ko cells (Fig. 5C), and therefore their misregulation can be explained by the alteration of the core TF network formed by PAX8, TFAP2A, KLF4, and SP3 upon PAX8ko. The genes regulated by each one of these TFs were similarly composed of up-regulated and down-regulated genes (Supplementary Fig. S7).

GO enrichment analyses showed that up-regulated genes controlled by the PAX8 core network were enriched in biological functions as apoptosis, cell cycle, the respiratory chain, or protein transport (Fig. 5D), suggesting that PAX8 is normally repressing genetic programs leading to apoptosis and metabolic changes. In contrast, down-regulated genes were enriched in transcriptional regulation, cell division and DNA replication and repair (Fig. 5D), suggesting that PAX8 promotes not only the expression of TF genes but also cell proliferation and DNA repair. While the induction of replication and prevention of apoptosis suggest a function of PAX8 promoting tumor growth in OC, the increase in DNA repair functions has been linked to chemoresistance, suggesting a potential mechanism by which PAX8 might promote the high resistance rates of OC to antitumor therapy.

PAX8 regulatory network define a stemness gene signature

We wondered whether our previous observations had implications for OC patients. For this, we first tested whether the differentially expressed genes associated with the PAX8 core network could distinguish ovarian tumors from non-tumoral FTE tissues. For that, we used a patient database containing both types of samples and clustered tissues according to their expression levels of the PAX8 core gene regulatory network. For both the up- and down-regulated genes, we found that FTE and OC tissue samples were clustered apart following the expression profiles of these genes, and clear trends in these profiles could be distinguished among these groups of samples (Supplementary Fig. S8A). This suggests that tumoral tissues have a distinct transcriptomic profile dependent on PAX8 compared with the non-tumoral tissue. Next, we tested the ability of the PAX8 gene regulatory network to distinguish OC patients from healthy donors. We found that both the up- and down-regulated genes were able to cluster apart OC patients from controls, and that their expression profiles were highly different (Supplementary Fig. S8B), indicating that the PAX8 transcriptomic signatures also differentiates tumoral from non-tumoral samples. It is worth noting that the expression levels of PAX8 target genes were much more heterogeneous in tumoral than in non-tumoral samples, which agrees with our observations of distinct PAX8 binding profiles and TF partners in different OC cell lines and suggests that ovarian tumors are highly heterogenous at the epigenomic and transcriptomic levels, even though they may share common features.

Then, we tested whether ovarian tumors could be defined by these shared common features related to the PAX8 regulatory network. For that, we first calculated all genes whose expression was significantly correlated with that of PAX8 in four different patient databases (GSE18520, GSE40595, GSE73168, and GSE204748) [51, 58, 59], either positively or negatively, as a proxy of genes that might be regulated by PAX8 in patients. Then, we analyzed whether these genes overalapped with the differentially expressed genes that are targets of the PAX8 core network in OC cell lines (Fig. 5C) and found 183 common genes (Fig. 6A). GO Biological Process enrichment analyses of these genes identified terms that included “cell differentiation,” but also “stem cell maintenance,” “stem cell proliferation,” and “canonical Wnt signaling pathway” (Fig. 6B), suggesting that PAX8 regulates genes involved in the balance between differentiation and tumor stemness, a property that is known to be associated with lower patient survival and resistance to therapy in OC [60]. Then, we focused our analyses on genes involved in the maintenance of stem cells. We found that, among the “stemness genes” correlated with PAX8 expression in OC patients, seven of them were also differentially expressed genes in PAX8ko cells and targets of the PAX8 core network (Fig. 6C). These genes included MECOM and SOX17, which encode TFs that are bona fide PAX8 partners in OC gene regulation [23, 54]; the epithelial stem cell marker EPCAM; the FGF ligand-encoding gene FGF2; the Wnt ligand-encoding gene WNT7A, which is involved in the development of the Mullerian duct [61]; and the RNA polymerase subunit-encoding gene POLR2H.

We then wondered whether these genes, which may represent the core of the PAX8 network in OC relevant for patients, and PAX8 itself could define a gene signature predicting the disease outcome. For this, we first clustered the tumor and non-tumor samples of the GSE18520 and GSE40595 OC datasets [51, 58] according to the expression of these seven genes and found that they clearly separated control from tumor samples, being FGF2 less expressed in patients and the remaining 6 genes more expressed in patients (Fig. 6D). This observation was reproduced in an additional database (GSE38666) [62] that was not used for the correlation analyses due to its small number of samples (Fig. 6D). Next, we performed a pan-cancer analysis using data from multiple OC databases (see Methods) to check the expression levels of the PAX8 signature genes in patient versus control samples. We found that FGF2 expression was significantly lower and that WNT7A, SOX17, PAX8, MECOM, and EPCAM expression was significantly higher in OC patients than in control samples, with a lower increase for POLR2H expression (Fig. 6E). Therefore, we discarded FGF2 from subsequent analyses in which the expression of all the genes was analyzed together. Indeed, the expression of the whole PAX8-derived gene signature (FGF2 excluded) was better predictor in tumoral than in non-tumoral samples (P = 1.19e-65; Fig. 6F), indicating that the high expression of these genes can effectively diagnose the disease. Interestingly, alterations of these six genes that mostly included gene amplification and increased expression was found in almost 60% of patients of the Pan Cancer Atlas (Supplementary Fig. S9), supporting the idea that the identified PAX8-derived gene signature associated with tumor stemness is relevant for ovarian tumorigenesis.

The expression of the PAX8-derived gene signature predicts lower patient survival and bad response to treatment

We wondered whether the PAX8 gene signature was associated with differences in patient survival. To assess this, we used the patient survival data (GSE18520) [51] and found that a high expression of the PAX8-derived gene signature was associated with a decrease in patient overall survival that was close to statistical significance (Fig. 7A). Indeed, using the larger serous OC TCGA study, we found that the high expression of the PAX8 gene signature was associated with significantly lower overall survival, progression-free survival, and post-progression survival in OC patients (Fig. 7B), indicating that the PAX8 gene signature also predicts disease outcome. We compared these results with patient survival stratifying based only in PAX8 expression and found no statistically significant differences between patients with high or low PAX8 expression (Supplementary Fig. S10), reinforcing the robustness of the signature as a survival predictor. Finally, we wondered whether the PAX8 gene signature was associated with response to treatment and therapy resistance. Using data from serous OC patients (GSE63885) [63], we found that the expression of individual genes of the PAX8-derived gene signature was higher in patients showing a partial response to platinum treatment (Fig. 7C), suggesting that a high expression of these genes can predict a worse response to anticancer treatment. In addition, 4 out of 6 genes also showed higher expression in patients resistant to therapy, i.e. with tumor relapse within 6 months after treatment (Fig. 7D). Taking the six genes together, their expression levels were significantly higher in non-responder than in responder patients (P = 1.9e-5; Fig. 7E), indicating that the PAX8 gene signature predicts a worse response to platinum treatment. Altogether, we have identified a six-gene PAX8 signature with high expression in OC patients that can predict a lower patient survival, a worse response to platinum-based treatment and a lower time to tumor relapse.

Expression of PAX8 in OC cells promotes tumor stemness and therapy resistance

Finally, we decided to validate the role of PAX8 promoting stem cell-like features and therapy resistance in OC cell lines. For this, we depleted PAX8 expression using two independent siRNAs (siPAX8 versus siC) and chose the previously analyzed Kuramochi, CAOV3, and OVCAR3 cell lines, plus an additional cell line, MES-OV, to validate the results in an independent line (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Fig. S11). First, we checked the levels of PAX8 mRNA and protein upon its depletion by RT-qPCR and western blot, respectively, observing a reduction in both cases for cells transfected with siPAX8 (Fig. 8A and Supplementary Fig. S11A). Next, we checked whether PAX8 depletion in our OC cells had an effect in the expression of PAX8 target genes belonging to the stemness gene signature identified above (Fig. 6C). We found that cells depleted of PAX8 showed a reduction in the expression of EPCAM, WNT7A, and SOX17 genes belonging to the signature, as expected, as well as KLF4, another PAX8 target identified by ATAC-seq (Fig. 8B and Supplementary Fig. S11B). In addition, we also noticed reduced expression of the stemness-associated genes NANOG, OCT4, SOX2, and SOX9 (Fig. 8B and Supplementary Fig. S11B), suggesting that PAX8 promotes stem-like features in OC cells. To check this, we generated tumorspheres, which are 3D cultures enriched in cancer stem cells (CSCs) generated in low attachment conditions, in OC cells with or without PAX8 depletion. We observed a reduction in the number of tumorspheres and in their size in the three analyzed cell lines (Fig. 8C and Supplementary Fig. S11C), indicating that PAX8 promotes the formation of CSCs. Since CSCs have been associated with resistance to conventional anti-tumor therapies, we assessed whether PAX8 promoted resistance to cisplatin, carboplatin, and paclitaxel in OC cells. We calculated the IC50 of the three cell lines to these compounds with or without PAX8 depletion and found a reduction in their IC50 values in cells depleted of PAX8 that was statistically significant in most cases (Fig. 8D and Supplementary Fig. S11D). This indicates that PAX8 is involved in tumor stemness and therapy resistance in OC cells, results that are consistent with our observations using in silico analyses of multiomic and patient data.

Discussion

PAX8 is a marker of FTE secretory cells whose expression is maintained in HGSC. Here we perform multi-omic analyses to study the gene regulatory network controlled by PAX8 in OC, shedding light into the molecular mechanisms of ovarian tumorigenesis and proposing new genes for targeted therapies. Previous studies focused on epigenomic and transcriptomic data in OC cell lines have contributed to our current knowledge about the role of PAX8 in ovarian tumors. However, the differences obtained with multiple cell lines, which may resemble the heterogeneity of real tumors, make necessary the integration of multiple data for the identification of common mechanisms leading to ovarian tumors. Here we integrate available ChIP-seq, CUT&RUN, CUT&Tag, ATAC-seq, and RNA-seq data in HGSC cell lines with transcriptomic patient data. This allowed us to uncover a PAX8-dependent signature composed of 6 genes (PAX8, SOX17, MECOM, EPCAM, WNT7A, and POLR2H) that can predict patient survival and disease outcome. This signature may be targeted by new therapies to overcome the high chemoresistance rates of OC.

Comparison of PAX8 chromatin binding in three OC and one FTE cell lines shows the high variability of genome-wide PAX8 binding sites across cell lines, with only few common binding sites. Despite this poor overlap, almost 25% of genes located in proximity to PAX8 binding sites are common among cell lines, suggesting that PAX8 binding is rewired in different cell lines but keeping a more stable network of regulated genes (Fig. 1). Variation in TF binding even among closely related cell lines is a common phenomenon [64]. Although this variability might come from nucleotide changes affecting the motif, this has been observed in only a small fraction of variable binding sites, e.g. in NFκB binding across human lymphoblastoid cell lines [65]. However, allele-specific TF binding is an extensive phenomenon that may also come from alterations in proximal motifs affecting the binding of cooperating regulators [66]. For example, differential binding sites of PPARγ in mouse white adipose tissue from different strains were enriched in polymorphic sites bound by CEBPα and glucocorticoid receptor, which often co-localize with PPARγ [67]; also, most enhancer variants in red blood cells map to motifs of signaling TFs at sites co-bound with master regulators [68]. These observations reinforce the notion that TF binding often depends on the presence of other factors that bind cooperatively to cis-regulatory elements. To identify cooperating partners of PAX8 in different cell lines, we clustered PAX8 binding sites and analyzed motif enrichment, finding common and specific families that may cooperate with PAX8 in different contexts. These observations were corroborated by the estimation of differential binding based on TF footprints in cell lines, which revealed differences in the binding of families that mostly corresponded with the ones identified using ChIP-seq data (Fig. 2), although other TFs whose footprints are not detected by this approach could be involved. The one-to-one analysis of differential binding among HGSC cell lines highlighted these differences. However, we observed clear differences between tumoral and non-tumoral cells, with a higher prevalence of AP-1 factor binding in FTE cells and a higher binding of KLF and SP factors in HGSC cells.

A deep analysis of differential accessibility in control versus PAX8ko HGSC cells showed thousands of putative cis-regulatory elements in which TF binding was altered upon PAX8 absence (Fig. 3). Many of these differences did not involve PAX8 binding, suggesting indirect effects in the binding of downstream factors. Indeed, we found the expected decreased binding of PAX motifs plus an additional decrease in the binding of SOX and TEAD TFs as the families with the highest decrease (Fig. 4). Consistently, an independent study found that differential PAX8 binding sites were located close to TEAD binding sites [69]. Another study found an enrichment of single nucleotide variants in PAX8 and Tead4 binding sites in primary ovarian tumors [70]. Since TEAD proteins are TFs of the Hippo signaling pathway, these data connect PAX8 and OC with such pathway. Indeed, we have previously shown that the Hippo pathway is a driver of tumor stemness and chemoresistance in ovarian tumors that can be targeted to suppress such resistance [24]. Regarding the SOX family, SOX17 has been identified as a driver of FTE differentiation together with PAX8 and Runx3 [71]. These data are also consistent with our identification of RUNX motifs in FTE-specific PAX8 binding sites. In OC, PAX8 and SOX17 physically interact, bind to common genomic regions, and regulate a subset of common genes, promoting lineage survival and angiogenesis [54, 72]. On the other hand, we found increased binding of AP-1, KLF, and SP factors upon PAX8 knockout. However, in this case little is known about a possible cooperation with these TFs, although ChIP-seq experiments in thyroid cells identified AP-1 and SP factors, as well as the architectural protein CTCF, as PAX8 partners [73].

We built the PAX8 TF-gene activation network in COV362 cells (Fig. 4) and found three TF genes connected with PAX8 with a high density of downstream interactions: TFAP2A, KLF4, and SP3, suggesting that they are key regulators downstream of PAX8. TFAP2A was recently described to promote cervical cancer [74]; KLF4 has been proposed as a marker of bad prognosis in solid tumors such as colorectal cancer [75, 76]; and SP3 has been involved in the maintenance of breast CSCs [77]. The identification of this network allowed us to determine putative target genes and to compare them with PAX8 targets based on RNA-seq (Fig. 5). Interestingly, the network was able to explain most of the genes miss-regulated in the absence of PAX8, strengthening the significance of the results. Among these target genes, we found up-regulated genes involved in apoptosis and down-regulated genes involved in transcriptional regulation, DNA repair, and cell cycle. These functions are consistent with the ones obtained for genes with differential expression among FTE and HGSC cell lines in another study [69] and among control and PAX8-depleted OVCAR4 cells [78]. This suggests roles of PAX8 inhibiting apoptosis and promoting tumor cell proliferation and chemoresistance. Indeed, multiple studies have identified PAX8 as a gene required for proliferation, migration, and invasion of OC cells [79–83].

We have integrated our identified PAX8 regulatory network in OC cells with gene expression data from OC patients to identify a PAX8-dependent gene signature that may be involved in tumor stemness (Fig. 6). Comparison of the differentially expressed genes controlled by the PAX8 core network and the genes whose expression is correlated with PAX8 in OC patients showed an enrichment of genes regulating stem cell proliferation and maintenance. Among these genes, we found five whose expression was positively correlated with PAX8 in OC patients: SOX17, MECOM, EPCAM, WNT7A, and POLR2H. These genes and PAX8 itself constitute a gene signature with high expression in ovarian tumors compared with healthy ovarian tissue and able to predict lower patient survival and poor response to therapy. Interestingly, some of these genes have been previously identified as PAX8 partners. As discussed above, SOX17 is a known PAX8 interacting partner, and MECOM has been described to form a complex with PAX8 [23]. Moreover, PAX8, SOX17 and MECOM have been proposed as master regulators of HGSC [55, 78]. On the other hand, we have identified additional genes involved in stemness and correlated with PAX8 across patients and cell lines. EPCAM is a marker of epithelial cells that is expressed in a population of stem-like cells at the distal end of the fallopian tube, which is a likely site of origin of OC [83, 84]. WNT7A was recently identified by another study as part of the PAX8 regulon in OC cells and patients [85]. Finally, POLR2H encodes a subunit of the RNA polymerases I, II, and III with no previous direct relationship described with PAX8. The genes of the PAX8 signature are collectively and individually overexpressed in a pan-cancer analysis of OC. Consistent with a possible role of PAX8 in tumor stemness and chemoresistance, their higher expression correlates with a worse response to therapy and, in some cases, with resistance to therapy (Fig. 7). Indeed, we corroborated these observations in our OC cell lines, showing that PAX8 promotes the expression of genes of the signature. In addition, we also observed lower formation of tumorspheres, which are enriched in CSCs, in PAX8-depleted cells, as well as lower resistance to compounds that are used for OC treatment, including cisplatin, carboplatin and paclitaxel (Fig. 8). These observations suggest that the PAX8 gene signature, identified across OC patients and PAX8 knockout OC cells, could be used as a prognostic marker to identify patients not responding to conventional therapies.

The identification of this PAX8-derived gene signature may also have implications for anticancer treatment. Thus, selective targeting of cells expressing PAX8 and its core gene regulatory network could increase therapy efficiency and reduce effects in other tissues. Several strategies have been proposed to inhibit PAX8 and its network. Hardy et al. used treatment with micelle-encapsulated thiostrepton to reduce PAX8 protein levels, tumor growth, and metastasis in OC [86]. Other studies tested the effect of compounds blocking PAX8 activity. These included EG1, which blocks DNA binding of the closely related Pax2, Pax5, and PAX8 TFs; and BG-1, which targets their transactivation domains. Both achieved a reduction of renal and OC cell proliferation [87, 88]. Other strategies involve the targeting of genomic regions regulated by PAX8. This is the case of histone deacetylase inhibitors, which can reduce transcription of PAX8 and downstream target genes by altering the epigenetic state of the superenhancer at the PAX8 locus. This reduced tumor growth acting synergistically with cisplatin [85]. Finally, other studies have used CDK12/13 inhibitors, including THZ1 and ZSQ1722, to reduce PAX8 and SOX17 expression levels, showing a reduction of OC cell growth both in vitro and in vivo [54, 78]. These data suggest that the pharmacological targeting of the PAX8-derived gene signature identified here is a promising strategy to increase the efficacy and specificity of chemotherapy, improving patient survival and reducing unwanted effects in other tissues.

In conclusion, the multiomic analysis that we have performed using chromatin binding, accessibility, and transcriptomic data in OC cells, combined with transcriptomic data in ovarian tumors, has allowed us to identify a PAX8-derived gene signature associated with tumor stemness. This signature is conserved across multiple patient cohorts and cell lines, and thus it may represent the core of the PAX8 gene regulatory network leading to therapy resistance in OC. The fact that combined expression of the signature genes is associated with poor survival and a worse response to treatment suggests that targeting this core regulatory network could be an efficient method to overcome therapy resistance in OC patients. Moreover, our results may also have implications for other cancer types, where the study of the gene network regulated by the relevant master TFs could help to find new targeted therapies against tumors.
