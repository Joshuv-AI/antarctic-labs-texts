Transmission components of hand, foot, and mouth disease in Guangdong Province and their association with meteorological factors: a modelling study
Authors: Hui Tang, Xudan Chen, Yueqian Wu, Jie Sun, Yikun Chang, Jinwei Chen, Junxi Li, Yongkang Lin, Yiguo Liufu, Danyang Song, Xinru Luo, Kexing Zhang, Yu Wang, Qilan Wen, Lei Zhang, Wangjian Zhang, Zhicheng Du, Yuantao Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) is a significant public health challenge, particularly in Guangdong Province, China. Although spatio-temporal dynamics of HFMD have been studied, inter-regional transmission patterns remain underexplored. This study investigates these patterns and the influence of meteorological factors to provide insights for more effective prevention strategies in the context of environmental factors.MethodsWe collected HFMD cases surveillance, population, and meteorological data from 21 cities in Guangdong from 2009 to 2017. Endemic-epidemic multivariate time-series model was used to analyze the case data, decomposing the HFMD risk into endemic, autoregressive, and spatio-temporal components, and investigating the influence of temperature and relative humidity on these components.ResultsHFMD in Guangdong shows distinct seasonal peaks in the second and fourth quarters, with regional clusters in Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Foshan, and Dongguan. The endemic-epidemic model indicates that 86.57% (95%CI: 77.17%-97.08%) of infection risk is due to autoregressive transmission, 9.50% (95%CI: 8.46%-10.65%) to spatio-temporal transmission, and 3.94% (95%CI: 3.33%-4.60%) to endemic factors. Shenzhen, Foshan, Meizhou, and Zhanjiang have autoregressive transmission rates of 89%-94.08%, while Yunfu, Jiangmen, Zhaoqing, and Shantou have import rates of 12.27%-21.44%. HFMD risk is positively correlated with temperatures below 25 °C for the endemic component and above 25 °C for the autoregressive and spatio-temporal components.ConclusionHFMD transmission in Guangdong is primarily driven by the autoregressive component, with temperature influencing different transmission components. Health authorities should enhance local preventive measures during high-risk seasons and adjust control strategies based on various transmission components to mitigate HFMD spread.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11799-1.Keywords: Meteorological factors; Hand, foot, and mouth disease; Transmission patterns; Spatio-temporal analysis

Introduction

Hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) is an acute infectious disease caused by various enteroviruses and characterized by high infectivity [1]. Over the past few decades, HFMD outbreaks have occurred frequently worldwide and have become a significant global public health challenge [2–5]. Between 2010 and 2014, China reported 87% (9.8 million out of 11.3 million) of the global HFMD cases to the World Health Organization [6]. Furthermore, from 2014 to 2018, China’s annual incidence rate of HFMD reached 168 per 100,000 population, accounting for 57% of notifiable category C infectious diseases [7]. From 2020 to 2023, the incidence of HFMD has consistently ranked among the top three for Class C infectious diseases. Regionally, Guangdong Province was particularly affected, with an incidence rate about 1.5 times the national average in 2018, and 2.5 times in 2019 and 2021 [8, 9]. These figures highlight the seriousness of the HFMD situation in China, particularly in Guangdong Province, and its challenges to the public health system. To alleviate this burden, Guangdong Province has implemented routine public health measures including ongoing surveillance and case management for HFMD control. In August 2016, the province further launched a vaccination program against EV71.

Different regions may experience distinct patterns of HFMD outbreaks and epidemics, making it crucial to understand their spatio-temporal dynamics for effective prevention and control strategies. Although present studies have evaluated the spatio-temporal dynamics of HFMD using spatial descriptions or spatio-temporal models [10–14], there is relatively little research on the transmission patterns within and between regions and their temporal changes. In certain geographical areas, HFMD is likely predominantly associated with historical cases within the region. Conversely, in other regions, the transmission of the disease may be primarily facilitated through inter-regional human interactions, suggesting that outbreaks may be linked to infection sources in adjacent areas. Understanding whether local or inter-regional dynamics primarily drive HFMD transmission is crucial for developing effective, targeted intervention strategies. Despite the significance of these transmission patterns, research on HFMD spread, particularly the interplay between within-region and between-region transmission, remains limited. This gap hinders a comprehensive understanding of HFMD spread and its temporal variations. Moreover, these studies do not account for the relevant influencing factors within and between regions.

Beyond transmission patterns, a variety of factors may affect the transmission of HFMD, with meteorological conditions being among the most significant. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated the effect of temperature and humidity on the overall HFMD incidence [12, 15, 16]. For example, a meta-analysis of 11 studies showed that for every 1 °C increase in temperature and every 1% increase in humidity, the HFMD incidence rate increased by 1.05 (95% CI: 1.02–1.08) and 1.01 (95% CI: 1.00–1.02.00.02) [17], respectively. However, while these studies have established the association between meteorological factors and HFMD incidence, they have primarily focused on overall incidence rather than exploring the distinct transmission risks within and between regions. The specific impact of temperature and humidity on intra- and inter-regional transmission dynamics remains underexplored, creating a gap in understanding the role of meteorological factors in HFMD transmission risks within and between regions.

Our study aims to understand the transmission patterns of HFMD in Guangdong Province by addressing key gaps in current research. We apply an endemic-epidemic multivariate time-series model to analyze HFMD cases reported between 2009 and 2017, quantifying the transmission risk both within and between regions to capture the spatial and temporal dynamics of the disease. Additionally, we investigate the impact of meteorological factors, such as temperature and humidity, on these transmission patterns, with a specific focus on their differential effects on intra- and inter-regional transmission. Our study aims to enhance the understanding of HFMD transmission mechanisms, providing valuable insights to guide the development of more effective surveillance, prevention, and control strategies.

Materials and methods

Data

HFMD surveillance data

China officially included HFMD into the national statutory infectious disease surveillance system in 2008. Subsequently, the updated HFMD Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines (2018 Edition) revised the diagnostic criteria for confirmed cases compared to the 2010 version. To maintain consistency in diagnostic standards throughout the research period, this study defines the time frame from 2009 to 2017. Our study focuses on 21 cities in Guangdong Province. We obtained weekly HFMD cases surveillance data from the Guangdong Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention, covering the period from 1 January 2009 to 31 December 2017. To explore the spatio-temporal transmission patterns of the disease, we aggregated the case data at the city level and divided the time scale into weeks. A weekly time scale is widely adopted in infectious disease research [18, 19], as it helps to reduce daily fluctuations and short-term anomalies.

Population data

We obtained population data for the 21 cities of Guangdong Province from the Guangdong Statistical Yearbook (source: https://data.cnki.net) for the period from 2009 to 2017. Our study assumes that each city’s weekly population share remains constant throughout the year and equals its annual average. Detailed data on regional population are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Meteorological data

We obtained daily temperature and relative humidity data for the study period from the China Meteorological Data Service Center (source: http://data.cma.cn/). These data were sourced from national monitoring stations, with one station per city, and were used to represent the meteorological conditions of the entire city. We then calculated the weekly average meteorological data to represent the weather conditions for each week.

Statistical analysis

We employed a two-stage modeling approach to analyze the transmission components of HFMD across different regions and the impact of meteorological factors on each component. In the first stage, we used the endemic-epidemic multivariate time-series model proposed by Held et al. as the primary research framework for investigating the spatio-temporal transmission dynamics of HFMD. This model decomposes the risk of HFMD cases into endemic, autoregressive, and spatio-temporal components [20, 21]. This method can simulate the transmission dynamics of HFMD both between and within different regions and has been successfully applied to studies of other infectious diseases, such as dengue fever in Guangdong, China [22], measles in Vietnam [23], and COVID-19 in Rwanda [24].

Results

Descriptive analysis of spatio-temporal distribution

From 2009 to 2017, Guangdong Province reported a total of 2,850,969 HFMD cases, with an average incidence rate of 296.85 per 100,000 residents, ranging from 91.39 per 100,000 in 2009 to 353.79 per 100,000 in 2017. The HFMD epidemic in Guangdong exhibited distinct seasonal and periodic patterns, with consistent annual epidemic curves across the 21 cities, typically peaking twice each year (Figure S1). Figure 1(a) shows that the primary peak occurred in the second quarter (May to July), and a secondary peak was observed in the fourth quarter (September to November). All 21 cities reported cases, with significant regional clustering, primarily in Guangzhou (506,301 cases), Shenzhen (354,390 cases), Foshan (303,191 cases), and Dongguan (305,240 cases). These four cities account for 51.53% of the total cases. Figure 1(b) indicates that the spatial distribution of HFMD incidence is highest in central cities and gradually decreases towards the surrounding cities with increasing distance.

Analysis of meteorological factors influencing HFMD transmission

The analysis result reveals different trends in the influence of temperature and relative humidity on HFMD transmission in different fitted components. The HFMD incidence risk to the endemic component increases with temperature up to 25 °C, after which it decreases. In contrast, the autoregressive and spatio-temporal components show an initial decrease in incidence risk, followed by an increase near 25 °C (Fig. 4). The overall correlation between relative humidity and HFMD incidence risk across all components is weaker (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study uses the endemic-epidemic multivariate time-series model to analyze the reported HFMD cases in Guangdong Province from 2009 to 2017. The incidence risk is decomposed into autoregressive, spatio-temporal, and endemic components, and the effects of temperature and relative humidity on these components are further investigated. The study finds that HFMD in Guangdong Province has clear seasonal and periodic characteristics, with the main peak occurring in the second quarter. HFMD risk in Guangdong is primarily driven by autoregressive effects, while spatio-temporal effects are significant in regions such as Shantou, Zhaoqing, Jiangmen, and Yunfu. In addition, temperature and humidity have different trends in influencing HFMD transmission in different components. These findings deepen our understanding of the epidemiological characteristics of HFMD, capture its transmission patterns in different regions, and provide important clues for further preventive measures.

Spatio-temporal trends

The results show that the occurrence of HFMD in Guangdong Province has significant seasonality, which is consistent with the bi-annual epidemic pattern observed in Hong Kong, Taiwan, Vietnam, and Xiamen [30–33]. The seasonality of HFMD in Guangdong aligns with previous studies suggesting that warm and humid conditions may facilitate virus transmission [30]. The secondary peak may be related to the end of school holidays and the subsequent gathering of students. The seasonal differences in HFMD incidence characteristics in different regions suggest that Guangdong Province should implement preventive measures for HFMD from May to July and from September to November.

HFMD also shows spatial heterogeneity, with studies showing that high HFMD incidence areas are mainly distributed around provincial capitals, likely due to population density and economic factors [34]. Our study finds that in Guangdong Province, the epidemic is mainly concentrated in economically developed and densely populated areas such as Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Foshan, and Dongguan. Studies from other regions, like Sichuan, Urumqi, and Gansu, also find that areas with higher per capita GDP and population density have higher HFMD risk [35–37]. In China, economically developed regions often have higher population densities [38], and frequent contact facilitates the spread of the disease. Therefore, it is crucial to implement strict monitoring and control measures in these regions.

Autoregressive, spatio-temporal, and endemic transmission components

An important feature of this study is that it distinguishes between cases spread within and between regions, taking into account endemic behavior. This helps to identify appropriate public health interventions for specific areas. Intra-regional transmission accounts for the majority of total transmission risk (86.57%) and dominates in most cities, indicating that HFMD in Guangdong Province is primarily driven by local cases. This dominance of autoregressive transmission may be attributed not only to the pathogen but also to socio-economic and infrastructural factors such as high population density and elevated per capita GDP. These factors potentially intensify transmission through increased contact frequency. Cities such as Shenzhen, Foshan, and Meizhou demonstrate this trend, emphasizing the need for rapid interventions and localized control measures early in outbreaks to prevent further spread. While the spatio-temporal and endemic components contribute less overall, their impact in specific areas is significant. For example, spatio-temporal effects are more pronounced in Yunfu, Jiangmen, Zhaoqing, and Shantou, suggesting possible transmission from neighboring regions. This underscores the importance of regional collaboration and cross-border surveillance, particularly in areas with high inter-regional movement. Endemic transmission plays a smaller role overall but is slightly higher in Qingyuan, Zhongshan, and Shaoguan, suggesting persistent low-level transmission possibly linked to environmental conditions or viral reservoirs. These findings highlight the need for long-term public health measures, such as improved sanitation and enhanced viral surveillance, to mitigate endemic transmission risks.

Impact of meteorological factors on transmission components

This study reveals the complex effects of temperature and relative humidity on HFMD transmission. Temperature above 25 °C is negatively correlated with the HFMD risk of the endemic component, possibly because the endemic component is mainly driven by viral characteristics rather than direct contact transmission. Previous studies showed that moderate temperatures facilitated the growth and transmission of HFMD viruses [39], but when temperatures exceeded 25 °C, the viral infectivity and activity decreased [40], supporting our findings. Additionally, earlier research showed that the relationship between temperature and HFMD incidence followed an “M”-shaped [41, 42] or inverted “V”-shaped pattern [43, 44], with HFMD incidence increasing at temperatures above 25 °C. This is consistent with our observations in the autoregressive and spatio-temporal components, possibly because warmer weather encourages more outdoor activities [45], increasing the frequency of interpersonal contact and thereby enhancing HFMD transmission. The impact of relative humidity was relatively weak, primarily affecting the endemic component and manifesting as increased HFMD risk at both high and low humidity levels, consistent with findings from Sichuan and other Chinese regions [35, 46]. However, its effect was significantly weaker than temperature and confined to this specific component. Notably, previous studies mainly focused on the total number of HFMD cases, whereas this study is the first to decompose the incidence risk into different components and analyze their relationships with meteorological factors. This provides a new perspective for understanding the transmission mechanisms of HFMD and offers important guidance for public health prevention and control strategies. Further research is needed to explore the role of other environmental factors to enhance our understanding of their contribution to the disease’s spread.

There are some limitations to our study. First, we assume a homogeneously mixed population without considering social contact patterns. Future research could include social contact patterns into endemic-epidemic multivariate time-series models to better understand transmission within and between age groups. Second, the absence of virological serotyping data (e.g., EV-A71, CV-A16) precludes analysis of strain-specific transmission dynamics, potentially obscuring differential effects of meteorological drivers across pathogen subtypes. Third, surveillance data may suffer from underreporting or misreporting issues, which may be more pronounced in rural or remote areas with less access to healthcare facilities. Although the addition of random effects can improve the representativeness of results [47], future studies should focus on the impact of underreporting during epidemics and expand spatio-temporal analysis to regional and national levels.

Conclusion

In Guangdong Province, the transmission of HFMD is primarily driven by autoregressive mechanisms, with temperature and relative humidity exerting distinct effects on its various transmission components. It is recommended that public health authorities strengthen local prevention and control measures at the municipal level during high-risk seasons. The autoregressive-driven transmission pattern observed in Guangdong, which is shaped by the region’s unique socio-demographic and environmental conditions, may offer a valuable reference for other high-incidence areas within and outside China that share similar meteorological, economic, and demographic characteristics.
