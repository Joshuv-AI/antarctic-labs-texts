Integrative Mechanistic Investigation of the Anticancer Effects of Panax notoginseng in Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Jaemoo Chun, Sarah Shin, Jeeyoun Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, necessitating the development of novel multi-targeted therapeutic agents. This study investigates the anticancer effects of Panax notoginseng extract (PNE) against CRC using an integrative approach of network pharmacology and experimental validation. Phytochemical profiling via LC–MS identified major ginsenosides, including Rb1, Rg1, and Rd. Network pharmacology analysis revealed potential targets such as Bcl-xL, STAT3/CDK1, and IL-2, which are associated with apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, and immune modulation, respectively. Experimental results demonstrated that PNE significantly inhibited the proliferation of HCT 116 and HT-29 CRC cells, induced G0/G1 phase arrest by modulating CDK4/6 and p21/p27, and promoted apoptosis by regulating BCL2 family proteins. Furthermore, PNE treatment suppressed tumor growth in a CT26-bearing syngeneic mouse model. These findings highlight that PNE exerts potent anticancer effects through multi-pathway modulation, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic candidate for CRC.

1. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a global health challenge, ranking as the third most diagnosed malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Despite substantial progress in surgical interventions and the advent of targeted therapies and immunotherapies, the prognosis for patients with advanced or metastatic CRC remains suboptimal [2]. A critical hurdle in current oncology is the limited efficacy of single-target agents due to the molecular heterogeneity of tumors and the development of chemoresistance [3]. Consequently, recent therapeutic paradigms have shifted toward identifying multi-targeted strategies capable of simultaneously disrupting distinct oncogenic signaling networks. In this context, phytomedicines, characterized by their structural diversity and multi-component nature, have re-emerged as a pivotal resource for overcoming the limitations of conventional chemotherapy [4].

Natural products have long served as a valuable source for drug discovery due to their structural diversity and ability to modulate multiple targets [5,6]. Panax notoginseng is a widely utilized medicinal herb known for its diverse pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory [7], antioxidant [8], and anticancer effects [9,10,11,12]. The primary bioactive components of P. notoginseng are saponins, specifically ginsenosides Rb1, Rg1, and Rd, and notoginsenoside R1, which have demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in various malignancies including colorectal, lung, and liver cancers [13]. Mechanistically, these saponins exert antitumor effects by modulating multiple signaling cascades. For instance, ginsenoside Rd has been shown to induce mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis by regulating the balance of Bcl-2 family proteins [14]. Ginsenoside Rb1 regulates the p53-p21-CDK2 axis, leading to cell cycle arrest [15]. Furthermore, recent studies indicate that notoginsenoside R1 and ginsenoside Rd can inhibit the STAT3 pathway, a critical driver of tumor survival and immune evasion [16,17]. However, herbal extracts exert their therapeutic effects not through a single compound but through the synergistic interaction of multiple constituents acting on diverse targets [18]. Although the pharmacological effects of individual ginsenosides and P. notoginseng itself have been investigated, the integrated molecular mechanism of P. notoginseng remains largely unexplored. Specifically, there is a lack of comprehensive studies identifying the common therapeutic targets shared by these diverse constituents and the convergent signaling pathways they simultaneously modulate to exert anticancer effects. Consequently, deciphering the holistic mode of action of P. notoginseng against CRC remains a significant challenge due to this chemical complexity, necessitating the use of a more systemic analytical framework.

Recent advances in systems biology have enabled the application of network pharmacology to uncover the molecular mechanisms of complex natural products [19]. This approach is particularly relevant for CRC, which is driven by dysregulated signaling networks involving multiple oncogenic pathways such as MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and STAT3. Previous studies have successfully utilized network pharmacology to elucidate the multi-target mechanisms of other herbal medicines against CRC. For instance, the anticancer effects of the herbal decoction Wei-Tong-Xin against CRC were predicted and validated to involve the modulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [20]. Similarly, Licorice extracts were found to exert anti-CRC effects by inducing apoptosis and inhibiting the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK signaling pathway [21]. By integrating chemical profiling, bioinformatics, and interactome analysis, network pharmacology provides a comprehensive insight to predict active compounds and their molecular targets, bridging the gap between traditional wisdom and modern molecular therapeutics [22]. Current rigorous standards in phytomedicine research emphasize an integrative strategy that combines computational predictions with robust chemical characterization and experimental validation to ensure the reliability of mechanistic insights [23].

In this study, we investigated the anticancer effects and underlying molecular mechanisms of P. notoginseng extract (PNE) against CRC using an integrative framework. To date, no study has systematically integrated network pharmacology with in vitro and in vivo validation to elucidate the multi-target mechanisms of PNE in CRC. We first characterized the chemical profile of PNE using UHPLC–QTOF–MS to ensure quality consistency. Subsequently, we employed network pharmacology to predict key oncogenic targets and validated these predictions through in vitro and in vivo assays. Specifically, we investigated the regulatory effects of PNE on key oncogenic signaling pathways to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying its ability to induce G0/G1 cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, suggesting its potential as a promising multi-targeted phytotherapeutic agent for CRC.

2. Results

2.1. Chemical Composition of PNE

To identify the bioactive constituents of PNE, qualitative analysis was performed using UHPLC–QTOF–MS. As shown in Figure 1, the chemical profile of PNE revealed a diverse array of saponins. A total of ten major ginsenosides were putatively identified based on their retention times, precursor m/z values, and MS/MS fragment patterns (Table 1). Notably, ginsenoside Rb1 exhibited the highest peak intensity, followed by significant levels of ginsenoside Rg1 and ginsenoside Rd. Other identified constituents included notoginsenoside R1, vina-ginsenoside R8, ginsenoside Rh1, notoginsenoside R2, ginsenoside Re, ginsenoside Rk1, and ginsenoside Rg3. The observed m/z values showed high mass accuracy with Δppm values ranging from 0.51 to 4.22. These ginsenosides are well-known for their anticancer activities, suggesting that their synergistic effects may contribute to the overall therapeutic efficacy of PNE against CRC.

2.2. Network Pharmacology Analysis Identifies Key Targets and Pathways of PNE

To elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the anticancer effects of PNE, network pharmacology analysis was performed using the major ginsenosides identified by LC–MS. A total of 74 predicted targets associated with PNE compounds were identified, and a compound–target interaction network was constructed to visualize the relationships between these ginsenosides and their corresponding targets (Figure 2A). Among these targets, STAT3, IL2, and APH1A were identified as the most highly connected hub nodes, suggesting their central regulatory roles in mediating the biological effects of PNE. In addition to these primary hubs, several other targets, including FGF1, FGF2, VEGFA, PTAFR, HSD11B2, HPSE, HSP90AA1, and RORC, exhibited notable connectivity within the network. To determine their relevance to CRC, 869 disease-related targets were compiled from the GeneCards, and Venn diagram analysis revealed 27 overlapping targets, representing the potential therapeutic targets of PNE in CRC (Figure 2B).

To further characterize the molecular relevance of these overlapping targets, a compound–target network was generated using the 27 shared targets (Figure 3A). In this network, major ginsenosides were linked to multiple CRC-associated targets, illustrating the multi-component and multi-target nature of PNE. Several targets, such as STAT3, IL2, FGF2, VEGFA, HSP90AA1, LGALS3, LGALS4, CDK1, and BCL2L1, showed connections to more than one compound, suggesting that these pathways may represent converging points of PNE’s pharmacological activity. To examine functional interactions, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database (Figure 3B). The 27 targets formed a highly interconnected network, with STAT3, AKT1, MAPK14, CDK1, VEGFA, and HSP90AA1 emerging as central hubs based on interaction density. To elucidate the biological significance of these targets, Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed. As shown in Figure 3C, significant enrichment was observed in biological processes such as anoikis, apoptotic regulation, cell migration, cell cycle, ERK1/ERK2 cascade, AKT pathway, and VEGF receptor signaling. Cellular component analysis indicated enrichment in the PI3K complex and mitochondria, whereas molecular function terms highlighted protein kinase activity and MAP kinase activity. Consistent with these findings, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis identified major cancer-related signaling pathways, including AKT, STAT, MAPK, and VEGF, as significantly enriched (Figure 3D).

2.3. PNE Inhibits Cell Proliferation and Induces Apoptosis in CRC Cells

To validate the antiproliferative effects predicted by network pharmacology, we examined the impact of PNE on CRC cell viability using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Figure 4A, PNE treatment significantly reduced cell proliferation in both HCT 116 and HT-29 cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner. PNE treatment for 24 or 48 h resulted in a progressive decline in cell viability, with concentrations exceeding 1000 µg/mL producing a marked cytotoxic effect (p < 0.001). These results indicate that CRC cells are highly sensitive to PNE-induced growth inhibition. Next, to investigate whether this reduction in cell viability was attributed to the induction of apoptosis, flow cytometric analysis was performed using Annexin V/PI staining. As shown in Figure 4B, PNE treatment led to a significant increase in the apoptotic cell population in both HCT 116 and HT-29 cell lines compared to the control group. Quantitative analysis confirmed a dose-dependent elevation in the percentage of Annexin V-positive cells, demonstrating that PNE triggers programmed cell death. These results suggest that PNE suppresses CRC cell growth by inducing apoptosis, consistent with the network pharmacology prediction that PNE targets key regulators of apoptotic signaling pathways.

2.4. PNE Induces Cell Cycle Arrest in CRC Cells

To determine whether the growth inhibitory effect of PNE was associated with cell cycle arrest, flow cytometric analysis was performed. As shown in Figure 5A, PNE treatment markedly modulated the cell cycle distribution in both HCT 116 and HT-29 cells. In HCT 116 cells, treatment with 1000 µg/mL PNE resulted in a significant accumulation of cells in the G0/G1 phase (p < 0.001), accompanied by a reduction in the S phase population. Interestingly, at a higher concentration of 1500 µg/mL, the arrest pattern shifted toward the G2/M phase, with a concurrent depletion of S phase cells. HT-29 cells exhibited a dose-dependent increase in the G0/G1 fraction with a corresponding decrease in the S phase. These findings indicate that PNE suppresses CRC cell proliferation by blocking cell cycle progression and inhibiting DNA synthesis. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this cell cycle arrest, we employed a complementary approach using Western blot analysis and quantitative reverse transcription (qRT)-PCR. Western blot analysis primarily focused on key cell cycle regulators to validate the functional protein changes responsible for the observed G0/G1 arrest. In parallel, qRT-PCR was performed to verify the transcriptional modulation of potential pathways associated with cell cycle regulation predicted by our network pharmacology analysis, including genes involved in cell proliferation and survival. As shown in Figure 5B, PNE treatment led to a dose-dependent decrease in the levels of CDK4, CDK6, and Cyclin D1, which are critical drivers of the G1–S transition. Conversely, the expression levels of the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p21 and p27 were upregulated, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed blockade of cell cycle progression. These protein expression patterns were corroborated at the transcriptional level. As shown in Figure 5C, the mRNA levels of proliferation-related genes, including BCL2L1, BCL2, CDK2, CDK4, and PCNA, were significantly downregulated following PNE treatment. In contrast, the expression of TGFB1 and CDKN1A (p21) was markedly increased. The upregulation of TGFB1 aligns with the induction of CDKN1A, suggesting that PNE may activate the TGF-β signaling pathway to induce cell cycle arrest.

2.5. PNE Regulates Kinase Signaling and Apoptosis-Related Protein Expression

To identify the specific signaling pathways driving the PNE-mediated anticancer effects, a human phospho-kinase array was performed using lysates from HCT 116 cells. Because the principal enriched pathways are fundamentally regulated by phosphorylation mechanism, we performed phospho-kinase and apoptosis proteome arrays to functionally validate these downstream effectors. The array results, supported by densitometric quantification, demonstrated that PNE treatment induced distinct changes in the phosphorylation profiles of multiple signaling proteins (Figure 6A). Interestingly, PNE exerted a dual effect on STAT3 phosphorylation. The phosphorylation level of STAT3 at Tyr705 was significantly reduced. In contrast, phosphorylation at Ser727 was markedly upregulated. This suggests that PNE blocks the STAT3-mediated survival signaling pathway (Tyr705) but triggers stress-associated responses (Ser727). Consistent with this suppression of survival signals, phosphorylation of CREB and ERK1/2 was reduced. Conversely, phosphorylation of stress-responsive kinases, specifically JNK, p38, c-Jun, GSK-3β, GSK-3α/β, MSK1/2, HSP27, YES, PRAS40, and WNK1, was markedly increased. Since Ser727 on STAT3 is a known downstream target of MAPK signaling, its upregulation aligns with the observed activation of the JNK/p38 stress pathways. To further validate the downstream consequences of these signaling alterations, the expression of apoptosis-related proteins was analyzed using a human apoptosis array (Figure 6B). Consistent with the activation of stress signaling, PNE treatment significantly upregulated the expression of pro-apoptotic factors, including death receptor 5 (DR5), Fas-associated protein with death domain (FADD), HO-1, clusterin, and cleaved caspase-3. In contrast, the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins, such as cIAP-1 and Claspin, was downregulated. Collectively, the upregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins and the downregulation of survival proteins demonstrate that PNE effectively induces programmed cell death.

2.6. PNE Induces Apoptosis by Modulating MAPK and STAT3 Signaling Pathways

To validate the signaling alterations identified in the phospho-kinase array and further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying PNE-induced apoptosis, we performed Western blotting to assess key proteins in the MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and STAT3 pathways. As shown in Figure 7A, PNE treatment significantly inhibited the phosphorylation of ERK1/2, a major driver of cell proliferation. In contrast, the phosphorylation levels of stress-associated MAPKs, JNK and p38, were markedly upregulated. To further delineate the specific signaling pathways involved, we examined the PI3K/AKT and AMPK pathways, which are critical regulators of cell survival and energy metabolism. However, Western blot analysis revealed that PNE treatment did not significantly alter the phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, or AMPK (Figure 7B). Interestingly, PNE exerted a dual effect on STAT3 phosphorylation (Figure 7C). PNE effectively suppressed the activation marker p-STAT3 (Tyr705), whereas it enhanced the phosphorylation of p-STAT3 (Ser727). This distinctive modulation reinforces the link between mitochondrial stress signaling and PNE treatment. These results indicate that PNE modulation of the upstream MAPK and STAT3 signaling pathways is closely associated with the activation of the core apoptotic process. Specifically, the inhibition of STAT3 (Tyr705) phosphorylation correlated with the downregulation of its transcriptional targets, Bcl-2 and Survivin, thereby shifting the balance towards apoptosis. Concurrently, the activation of stress-responsive kinases (JNK/p38) aligned with the cleavage of Caspase-9 and PARP, suggesting that PNE creates a pro-apoptotic signaling environment. To conclusively link these signaling alterations to apoptotic cell death, we examined key oxidative stress and apoptosis markers. As shown in Figure 7D, PNE treatment induced robust expression of HO-1 and the autophagy marker LC3A/B. Furthermore, PNE treatment triggered the activation of the caspase cascade, evidenced by the dose-dependent decrease in procaspase-8 and procaspase-9, concurrent with the cleavage of PARP (Figure 7E). Notably, PNE differentially regulated anti-apoptotic proteins by significantly downregulating Survivin and Bcl-2 while markedly increasing Mcl-1 expression, substantiating that PNE-induced apoptosis is closely associated with oxidative stress and autophagic regulation.

2.7. PNE Suppresses Tumor Growth in a CRC Syngeneic Mouse Model

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of PNE in vivo, we established a CT26 murine CRC syngeneic model. As shown in Figure 8A, oral administration of PNE effectively inhibited tumor progression in a dose-dependent manner. While the low-dose group (0.5 g/kg) showed a growth pattern similar to the control, the high-dose group (1.5 g/kg) exhibited a marked suppression of tumor growth. Significant differences in tumor volume between the high-dose PNE group and the control group were observed starting from day 21 (p < 0.05) and became more pronounced by the endpoint at day 24 (p < 0.01). At the end of the experimental period, the tumors were excised and photographed to visually compare the therapeutic efficacy. The tumors from mice treated with 1.5 g/kg PNE were smaller than those from the control and low-dose groups (Figure 8B). Tumor weight was also significantly decreased only in the high-dose group (Figure 8C). Throughout the treatment period, we monitored the body weight of the mice. No significant loss of body weight was observed in any of the PNE-treated groups compared to the control (Figure 8D). To further confirm the safety of PNE, we performed H&E staining on major organs, including the liver and kidneys (Figure 8E). Histological examination revealed no signs of structural abnormalities, inflammation, or necrosis in the liver and kidney tissues of the high-dose group. Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that PNE exerts potent antitumor activity against CRC without inducing observable systemic toxicity.

3. Discussion

Despite significant advancements in therapeutic strategies, CRC remains a major global health challenge due to high recurrence rates and drug resistance [24]. Consequently, there is a growing interest in natural products that can target multiple oncogenic pathways simultaneously with minimal side effects [25]. In the present study, we employed an integrative approach combining network pharmacology with experimental validation to elucidate the anticancer mechanisms of PNE. Our findings demonstrate that PNE exerts potent antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic effects in CRC cells and suppresses tumor growth in a syngeneic mouse model, primarily by modulating the MAPK and STAT3 signaling pathways and inducing G0/G1 cell cycle arrest. A distinguishing feature of this study, which sets it apart from conventional network pharmacology research, is the establishment of a comprehensive ‘Chemical–Target–Phenotype’ workflow. Unlike studies that rely solely on public databases for compound identification, we performed the network analysis on actual constituents verified via LC–MS profiling. Furthermore, the 27 overlapping targets identified in this study were not intended to serve as individual targets for experimental validation, but rather as the computational basis to map the PPI network and identify core signaling pathways. Through Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway analyses, we discovered that these targets are linked to the STAT3, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK signaling pathways, with ‘Cell Cycle’ and ‘Apoptosis’ emerging as the most significant biological processes. Consequently, our experimental design was focused on validating these predicted signaling cascades and biological phenotypes rather than verifying each gene individually. Therefore, our experimental validation was designed not only to verify the upstream targets identified in the network, but to functionally validate whether the bioinformatically predicted modulation of these key pathways translates into the activation or suppression of their specific downstream effectors in vitro. This pathway-guided approach allowed us to experimentally confirm that PNE inhibits STAT3 and MAPK phosphorylation, validating the bioinformatic predictions at the signaling level. Guided by these predictions, we further investigated the specific downstream effectors driving the resulting phenotypes, identifying CDK4/6 as the drivers of G0/G1 arrest and Caspases/PARP as the executioners of apoptosis. This step-by-step validation confirms that the actual biological responses align precisely with the network-predicted modulation of core signaling cascades.

Such a comprehensive approach is essential because natural products have long served as a cornerstone of drug discovery, offering distinct advantages over synthetic single-target drugs due to their structural diversity and capacity to modulate multiple oncogenic pathways simultaneously [26]. Although isolated compounds are frequently prioritized in modern drug development for their target specificity, the utilization of whole plant extracts presents unique therapeutic benefits in oncology [27]. These extracts comprise complex phytochemicals capable of exerting synergistic effects via a ‘multi-component, multi-target’ mechanism. In contrast to single agents that act on a specific pathway, the diverse bioactive constituents within an extract can interrogate multiple signaling cascades concurrently [28]. This holistic modulation not only enhances therapeutic efficacy but may also impede the emergence of drug resistance. PNE is a prime example of such a medicinal herb. Indeed, numerous studies have extensively documented the anticancer potential of P. notoginseng and its saponin fraction against various malignancies [7,11,12]. However, deciphering the precise mode of action for such a multi-component extract remains a challenge due to the complex synergistic interactions among its constituents. While the phenotypic efficacy of P. notoginseng is well-established, a holistic understanding of how its diverse bioactive compounds systematically perturb the robust signaling networks specifically driving CRC is still evolving. To address this complexity, as demonstrated in our workflow, we employed network pharmacology as a foundational analytical tool.

Network pharmacology has emerged as a powerful tool for deciphering the multi-component, multi-target mechanisms of herbal medicines [29]. Our in silico analysis identified STAT3, MAPK, and PI3K/AKT as potential core targets of PNE ginsenosides. While network pharmacology provides a predictive roadmap, experimental validation is crucial to confirm biological relevance, as computational predictions do not always translate to cellular phenotypes [30]. Interestingly, although the PI3K/AKT pathway was predicted as a major target, our experimental results revealed that PNE did not significantly alter the phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, or AMPK. This discrepancy highlights the necessity of experimental validation, suggesting that in the context of CRC, PNE bypasses the PI3K/AKT axis and selectively targets the MAPK and STAT3 pathways to exert its therapeutic effects.

Uncontrolled cell proliferation due to cell cycle dysregulation is a hallmark of cancer [31]. Our data showed that PNE induced G0/G1 phase arrest in both HCT 116 and HT-29 cells. Although CDK1 was identified as a central hub gene in our network pharmacology analysis, we focused our experimental validation on CDK4 and CDK6. This selection was driven by the specific phenotypic outcome observed in our cell cycle analysis, which indicated a G0/G1 phase arrest. Mechanistically, this arrest was linked to the downregulation of Cyclin D1 and CDK4/6, which are essential mediators of the G1-to-S phase transition. Furthermore, targeting these regulators aligns with our finding that STAT3 is a core target of PNE. Since STAT3 is well-established to transcriptionally activate Cyclin D1 and CDK4 to promote cell cycle progression [32], evaluating CDK4/6 levels serves as a critical functional validation of the STAT3-mediated signaling cascade targeted by PNE. In addition, PNE treatment significantly upregulated the expression of p21 and p27, well-known cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors. The observed increase in TGFB1 mRNA levels suggests that PNE may activate TGF-β signaling, which is known to induce CDKN1A (p21) expression, thereby reinforcing the cell cycle blockade. These results indicate that PNE effectively halts the proliferation of CRC cells by restoring cell cycle checkpoints.

Beyond cell cycle arrest, the induction of apoptosis is a critical strategy for cancer therapy [33]. We found that PNE shifted the balance of MAPK signaling by inhibiting the survival-promoting ERK pathway while activating the stress-responsive JNK and p38 pathways. The activation of JNK and p38 is often associated with cellular stress and apoptosis. Consistent with this, PNE treatment upregulated HO-1 and LC3, markers of oxidative stress and autophagy, respectively. This suggests that PNE induces a state of cellular stress that overwhelms the adaptive capacity of CRC cells, leading to apoptotic death. Mechanistically, this was evidenced by the dose-dependent consumption of procaspase-8 and procaspase-9, indicating their proteolytic activation. Notably, the reduction in procaspase-8 aligns with the upregulation of DR5 and FADD, reinforcing the involvement of the extrinsic pathway. In parallel, the consumption of procaspase-9 is mechanistically supported by the modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins, which serve as critical upstream regulators of the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway. Crucially, the functional execution of these convergent cascades was confirmed by the robust cleavage of PARP, the primary physiological substrate of effector caspases. This molecular execution is fully consistent with the statistically significant increase in the apoptotic cell population observed in our quantitative Annexin V/PI flow cytometry analysis, substantiating the caspase-dependent apoptotic mechanism of PNE.

A particularly novel finding of this study is the dual modulation of STAT3 and the distinct regulation of Bcl-2 family proteins. STAT3 is a transcription factor that drives survival and proliferation in cancer [34,35]. We observed that PNE significantly inhibited the activation of STAT3 at Tyr705, while intriguingly enhancing phosphorylation at Ser727. While Tyr705 phosphorylation induces nuclear translocation, Ser727 phosphorylation has been linked to mitochondrial stress [36]. Concurrently, we observed a distinct upregulation of Mcl-1, contrasting with the downregulation of Bcl-2 and Survivin. Given the robust induction of HO-1, a marker of oxidative stress, the increase in Mcl-1 likely represents a compensatory survival response of CRC cells attempting to resist PNE-induced stress [37]. However, the substantial activation of caspases and the significant reduction in anti-apoptotic proteins indicate that the pro-apoptotic potency of PNE overwhelms this compensatory mechanism, ultimately driving the cells toward apoptosis. This highlights PNE’s capacity to switch cancer cell signaling from survival to stress-induced death.

Regarding the physiological relevance of the concentrations used in this study, it is important to consider the unique anatomical context of CRC. Unlike other solid tumors that rely on systemic circulation for drug delivery, CRC is directly exposed to ingested substances in the intestinal lumen. Pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated that for orally administered phytochemicals, such as PNE and curcumin, local concentrations in the gut can reach significantly higher levels than in the plasma [38,39]. Therefore, the tested concentrations are physiologically achievable and clinically relevant in the context of CRC treatment via oral administration. Furthermore, herbal medicines often exert therapeutic effects at higher concentrations through multi-target modulation rather than acute cytotoxicity, distinguishing their mechanism of action from potent cytotoxic chemotherapeutic agents [40].

Finally, the in vitro efficacy of PNE was successfully translated to an in vivo setting. Oral administration of PNE significantly suppressed tumor growth in CT26-bearing mice without causing systemic toxicity, as indicated by stable body weights and normal liver/kidney histology. This safety profile is a significant advantage for potential clinical translation. These results suggest that PNE is a promising candidate for further development as an anticancer agent.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Chemicals and Reagents

Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), trypsin-EDTA solution, and penicillin/streptomycin were purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD were purchased from Biolegend (San Diego, CA, USA). Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Antibodies against CDK4, Cyclin D1, CDK6, p27, p21, CDK2, p-ERK1/2, ERK1/2, p-JNK, JNK, p-p38, p38, p-STAT3 (Tyr705), p-STAT3 (Ser727), STAT3, p-AKT, AKT, p-PI3K, PI3K, p-AMPKα, AMPKα, Cleaved PARP, Caspase-9, Survivin, Caspase-8, Bcl-2, Mcl-1, HO-1, LC3A/B, and β-actin, as well as secondary antibodies, were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA).

4.2. Preparation of PNE

The roots of P. notoginseng were purchased from Naemome Dah Herbal Medicine (Ulsan, Republic of Korea). A voucher specimen (PNE-2023) was deposited at the Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine (Daejeon, Republic of Korea). To prepare the extract, dried roots of P. notoginseng were extracted with 70% ethanol for 24 h at room temperature. This extraction procedure was selected to maximize ginsenoside extraction efficiency, as previously reported [41]. The extract was filtered, concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator, and subsequently lyophilized to obtain a powdered extract.

4.3. Phytochemical Analysis of PNE

Qualitative analysis of PNE was performed using a UHPLC (1290 infinity II LC system, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled with a Q-TOF MS (6546 Q-TOF, Agilent Technologies) system. Chemical constituents were separated on a Zorbax Extend-C18 column (80 Å, 2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 µm, Agilent Technologies). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B) at a flow rate of 0.1 mL/min. The linear gradient was as follows: 5% B (0–2 min), 5–50% B (2–5 min), 50–85% B (5–20 min), and 85% B (20–25 min), followed by re-equilibration for 5 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ionization mode with a mass range of m/z 50 to 1700. The MS parameters were set as follows: gas temperature 325 °C, drying gas 5 L/min, nebulizer 15 psi, fragmentor 125 V, skimmer 65 V, and capillary voltage 3500 V. MS/MS data were acquired using a narrow isolation window (1.3 Da) at a collision energy of 20 V. Data were processed using MS-DIAL version 4.90 (RIKEN, Yokohama, Japan) and compounds were identified based on retention times, precursor ion m/z, and MS/MS fragment patterns using RIKEN PRIMe website (http://prime.psc.riken.jp).

4.4. Network Pharmacology Analysis

Potential molecular targets of P. notoginseng compounds were predicted using a SwissTargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch, accessed on 23 August 2024). CRC-related targets were retrieved from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org, accessed on 23 August 2024) with a relevance score > 7.0. Overlapping targets between PNE and CRC were identified using Venny 2.1 tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny, accessed on 23 August 2024). Cytoscape version 3.10.2 software (https://cytoscape.org) was utilized to construct the compound-target network. PPI networks were analyzed using the STRING version 11.5 database (https://string-db.org). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of the biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions, as well as KEGG pathway analysis, were performed using the DAVID (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov) bioinformatics resource.

4.5. Cell Culture

Human CRC cells (HCT 116 and HT-29) and murine CRC cells (CT26) were obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Korea). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The medium was replaced every 2–3 days, and cells were sub-cultured upon reaching 80–90% confluence using a 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution.

4.6. Cell Viability Assay

The inhibitory effect of PNE on CRC cell proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. HCT 116 and HT-29 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/well and then treated with varying concentrations of PNE (250, 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 µg/mL) for 24 h. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 3 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to the untreated control group.

4.7. Apoptosis Analysis

Apoptotic cell death was quantified using Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD staining. HCT 116 and HT-29 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4.0 × 105 cells/well and treated with PNE for 24 h. After treatment, both floating and adherent cells were collected, washed with DPBS, and resuspended in binding buffer. The cells were then stained with Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. The percentage of apoptotic cells (early and late apoptosis) was determined using a BD LSRFortessa™ X-20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo 10.10.0 software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

4.8. Cell Cycle Analysis

To evaluate the effect of PNE on cell cycle distribution, HCT 116 and HT-29 cells were seeded in 10 cm dishes at a density of 1.5 × 106 cells/well and treated with PNE for 24 h. Following incubation, the cells were harvested, washed twice with cold DPBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol at −20 °C overnight. The fixed cells were then washed and stained with FxCycle™ PI/RNase Staining Solution (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The DNA content of the stained cells was analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa™ X-20 flow cytometer. The distribution of cells in the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases was quantified using FlowJo 10.10.0 software.

4.9. Western Blot Analysis

Proteins were extracted using Cell Lysis Buffer II (Invitrogen) supplemented with Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail and PMSF. Protein lysates were mixed with 4× LDS sample buffer and 10× sample reducing agent (Invitrogen), followed by denaturation at 75 °C for 10 min. The samples were separated on 8–12% Bis-Tris gels using MOPS SDS running buffer and subsequently transferred to a 0.45 µm nitrocellulose membrane (Invitrogen) using the SureLock Tandem Transfer System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The membranes were blocked with EzBlock Chemi (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies. After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using the SuperSignal West Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and detected with an ImageQuant LAS 4000 luminescent image analyzer (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA).

4.10. qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and cDNA was synthesized using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad). The relative mRNA levels of apoptosis-related genes (BCL2, BCL2L1, and BBC3), cell cycle-related genes (CDKN1A, CDK2, CDK4, and PCNA), and signaling modulator TGFB1 were normalized to GAPDH expression. Data were calculated and analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are shown in Table 2.

4.11. Proteome Array

The expression levels of phosphorylation-related and apoptosis-related proteins were evaluated using the Proteome Profiler™ Human Phospho-Kinase Array Kit and Human Apoptosis Array Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, total proteins were extracted and their concentrations were determined using the Qubit Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein lysates (300 µg) were incubated with the array membranes overnight at 4 °C. After washing to remove unbound proteins, the membranes were incubated with a biotinylated detection antibody cocktail, followed by streptavidin–HRP. The protein signals were visualized using chemiluminescent detection reagents and detected with an ImageQuant LAS 4000 luminescent image analyzer. The densitometric analysis of the array spots was performed using ImageJ version 1.54 software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

4.12. Tumor-Bearing Mice and Treatment

Female Balb/c mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from Saeron Bio (Uiwang, Republic of Korea) and acclimated for one week prior to the experiment. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine (Approval number 23-082). To establish the syngeneic tumor model, CT26 murine CRC cells (1 × 105 cells) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. The mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 7 per group): control (saline), PNE 0.5 g/kg, and PNE 1.5 g/kg. The dosages of PNE used in this study were determined based on the previous studies regarding the therapeutic efficacy and safety of PNE [42,43]. PNE was administered orally once daily for 21 consecutive days. Tumor growth was monitored every three days by measuring the length (L) and width (W) with digital calipers, and tumor volume (V) was calculated using the formula: V = (L × W2)/2. Body weight was recorded to monitor potential systemic toxicity. At the end of the treatment period, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors, as well as the liver and kidneys, were harvested for further histological analysis.

4.13. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

The harvested tumor, liver, and kidney samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 24 h at 4 °C and embedded in paraffin. The tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 4 µm using a microtome. Histological examination was performed by staining the sections with H&E solution to visualize the nuclei and cytoplasm. The morphological changes in the tumor tissues and potential structural abnormalities in the liver and kidneys were observed under an optical microscope to assess the efficacy and safety of PNE.

4.14. Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using an unpaired Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups, or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data visualization and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

5. Conclusions

This study demonstrates that PNE exerts potent anticancer effects against CRC through a multi-targeted mechanism involving G0/G1 cell cycle arrest and the induction of apoptosis. Our integrative approach, combining network pharmacology with experimental validation, revealed that PNE selectively modulates the MAPK and STAT3 signaling pathways, independent of the PI3K/AKT axis. These findings not only elucidate the molecular basis of PNE’s efficacy but also highlight the value of experimental verification in systems pharmacology. While this study focused on intrinsic cancer cell signaling, future investigations should extend to the potential immunomodulatory effects of PNE within the tumor microenvironment. Collectively, our data strongly support the potential of P. notoginseng as a promising therapeutic candidate or adjuvant for the treatment of colorectal cancer.
