Clinical outcomes and complication rates of minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis (MIPO) versus open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) in midshaft clavicular fractures: a comparative study
Authors: Gaowei Lin, Shixiong Zheng, Yushu Zhang, Qisheng Liu, Heliang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConventional open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) remains the standard approach for midshaft clavicle fractures, yet its traumatic nature has driven the need for minimally invasive techniques. Locking minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis (MIPO) demonstrates clinical potential through biomechanical superiority and tissue-preserving characteristics.ObjectiveTo compare the clinical efficacy and safety of MIPO versus ORIF for midshaft clavicular fractures.MethodsA retrospective cohort study included 203 patients (MIPO group:101 cases; ORIF group:102 cases) treated between 2022 and 2024. Baseline characteristics, functional outcomes (DASH and Constant-Murley scores), and complications were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U and chi-square tests.ResultsNo baseline differences were observed (P > 0.05). Functional outcomes were equivalent between groups (DASH score: P = 0.906; Constant-murley score: P = 0.646). The overall complication rate was significantly lower in the MIPO group than in the ORIF group (7.92% vs. 36.27%, P < 0.001), particularly in nonunion (0% vs. 5.88%, P = 0.039) and sensory disturbance (6.93% vs. 27.45%, P < 0.001). This advantage is also evident in the subgroup of patients with AO type 2 C fractures, where MIPO similarly demonstrates a significantly lower incidence of these two complications.ConclusionMIPO achieves comparable functional recovery to ORIF while reducing postoperative complications, supporting its prioritization in midshaft clavicular fracture management.

Introduction

Midshaft clavicle fractures, which account for approximately 75% of all clavicular fractures [1], represent one of the most common injuries of the upper extremity. Owing to their anatomical and biomechanical importance, the management of these fractures has a direct impact on shoulder function, rehabilitation outcomes, and long-term quality of life [2]. Traditionally, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) has been regarded as the standard treatment for displaced midshaft clavicle fractures, providing reliable fracture reduction and stable fixation [3]. However, the conventional ORIF technique requires a relatively large surgical incision and extensive soft tissue dissection, which may increase the risks of neurovascular injury, infection, and postoperative scar hypertrophy, as well as compromise the periosteal blood supply that is essential for fracture healing [4, 5].

In response to these limitations, minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis (MIPO) has been introduced as an alternative fixation strategy that emphasizes biological fixation and preservation of the fracture hematoma and surrounding soft tissues [6–9]. MIPO has demonstrated promising results in promoting early functional recovery and reducing postoperative complications in various long bone fractures. Nevertheless, despite its growing popularity, clinical evidence comparing MIPO and traditional ORIF for midshaft clavicle fractures remains inconsistent, particularly with regard to union time, functional outcomes, and complication profiles. Few studies have comprehensively analyzed whether fracture morphology, as classified by the AO system, influences the relative advantages of each surgical approach.

Recently, comparative studies by Wang et al. [10] and Shakya et al. [11] have further confirmed the functional equivalence and overall safety of MIPO. However, the relative superiority of MIPO in the context of increasing fracture complexity, as stratified by the AO classification system, has not been thoroughly explored. Specifically, there is a paucity of data on whether the biological advantage of MIPO translates into a lower risk of nonunion in comminuted fracture patterns. Therefore, the present study aims not only to evaluate and compare the overall clinical efficacy and complication rates of MIPO versus ORIF but, more importantly, to conduct pre-planned subgroup analyses based on AO fracture types to determine the indications in which MIPO provides superior benefits.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

This study was a single-center retrospective cohort study, and the study data were collected from patients with mid-clavicle fractures who underwent surgical treatment between January 2022 and December 2024 in the Department of Orthopedics of Fuzhou Second General Hospital. All patient information was signed with informed consent for the study, and patient data were anonymized.

Inclusion criteria were (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) imaging support for closed midclavicular fractures; and (3) cases treated with MIPO or ORIF surgery and with postoperative follow-up of ≥ 6 months.

Exclusion criteria were (1) open fracture; (2) combined neurovascular injury; (3) pathologic fracture; (4) multiple injuries, and incomplete follow-up data.

After strict screening, a total of 203 patients were included, including 101 cases in the MIPO group and 102 cases in the ORIF group. This study was reviewed and approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee of Fuzhou Second General Hospital (Approval Number 2025373).

Surgical techniques and operational details

MIPO Group: patients underwent minimally invasive internal fixation with locking plates. A small incision (approximately 1 –2 cm) is made in the distal clavicle to create a subcutaneous tunnel for plate insertion. The plate is fixed with locking screws to ensure stable fixation of the fracture segment, as shown in Fig. 1. This technique adhered to the principle of relative stability: the plate served as a bridging construct that preserved the fracture hematoma and permitted controlled micro-motion, thereby promoting secondary bone healing.

ORIF Group: Patients undergo traditional open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF). A larger incision is made above the clavicle to expose and directly reset the fracture. Plates were fixed using standard screws.

Intraoperative reduction assessment

To evaluate the adequacy of primary reduction, intraoperative fluoroscopy was performed after implant placement in three views: an anteroposterior view and two oblique views (15°-30° cephalic tilt and 15°-30° caudal tilt), with the contralateral healthy clavicle used as a reference for length and overall alignment. Reduction was considered acceptable when the following criteria were met: (1) at least two-thirds cortical contact with partial overlapping permitted in comminuted areas; (2) angulation < 10° in both the superior-inferior and anterior-posterior planes; (3) clavicular shortening ≦ 1 cm compared with the contralateral side; and (4) satisfactory plate fit on the bone surface, indicating acceptable rotational alignment. All patients included in the study fulfilled these criteria on final intraoperative imaging.

Postoperative management and follow-up evaluation

In both groups, patients were instructed to perform passive shoulder joint activities within 24 h after surgery, and weight-bearing of the affected upper extremity was strictly limited for 6 weeks. The exact postoperative rehabilitation instructions were followed in both groups. The follow-up time was 13.56 ± 4.19 months in the MIPO group and 13.63 ± 3.94 months in the ORIF group. Functional assessment was conducted using the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) and the.

Constant-Murley Shoulder Function Score by two independent orthopedic surgeons who were blinded to reduce bias.

All complications were prospectively recorded and verified using predefined criteria: plate breakage was defined as radiographic evidence of a clear implant discontinuity; incision infection was diagnosed according to criteria requiring antibiotics or debridement; internal fixation failure referred to loss of reduction (angular deformity > 10° or shortening > 15 mm relative to the immediate postoperative radiograph) or screw loosening necessitating revision; nonunion was defined as absence of bridging callus on at least three of four cortices in two orthogonal views at 9 months postoperatively, combined with persistent pain or motion at the fracture site, with equivocal cases confirmed by CT or reoperation for failed healing before 9 months; sensory disturbance included any subjective numbness or hypoesthesia in the supraclavicular or deltoid region, confirmed by a standardized pinprick test and categorized as transient (resolution within 6 months) or persistent. All imaging data were independently reviewed by two experienced radiologists blinded to the surgical technique, and any disagreement was resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0. Continuous variables were biased information, expressed as median (quartiles) [M (P25, P75)], and comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The count data were presented as both the number of cases and the corresponding percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test, with the application of the continuity-corrected version when any expected frequency was between 1 and 5, or Fisher’s exact probability test if any expected frequency was less than 1. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subgroup efficacy and complication analysis based on Fracture AO typing

To further evaluate the efficacy and complication occurrence of different Fracture AO typing, we performed a subgroup analysis. The results showed no difference in DASH score and Constant-Murley score between MIPO and ORIF in groups 2 A, 2B, and 2 C (P > 0.05, Table 4). In terms of complications, the change in sensitivity of MIPO was significantly lower than that of ORIF for either Fracture AO typing (P < 0.05, Table 4). It is worth noting that the incidence of nonunion in MIPO was also significantly lower than that in ORIF in group 2 C (P < 0.05, Table 4).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that MIPO achieves comparable functional recovery to ORIF in the treatment of midshaft clavicle fractures, as evidenced by similar DASH and Constant-Murley scores. However, MIPO was associated with a significantly lower overall complication rate, underscoring its clinical advantages in terms of safety and tissue preservation. Specifically, the incidences of nonunion and sensory disturbance were markedly lower in the MIPO group than in the ORIF group. This trend remained consistent in subgroup analyses of AO type 2 C fractures, suggesting that MIPO offers distinct benefits even in more complex fracture patterns.

Previous studies have reported similar findings. For instance, a comparative analysis of conventional and minimally invasive techniques for midshaft clavicle fractures found no significant difference in functional outcomes, as measured by the DASH and Constant-Murley scores [12]. Likewise, a meta-analysis confirmed that MIPO yields functional results comparable to ORIF, further supporting the current findings [13]. Additionally, a retrospective study comparing open plating versus MIPO for acute displaced clavicular shaft fractures reported similar union rates and functional scores, but noted advantages in cosmetic outcomes for MIPO [14]. Furthermore, a prospective study evaluated MIPO for clavicular shaft fractures and found it to be a viable option with low complication rates compared to traditional methods [15].

In contrast, although a recent comparative study reported similar complication rates between the two techniques [16], the present study identified a significantly lower nonunion rate in the MIPO group. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in fracture complexity, patient demographics, refinements in surgical technique, or the larger sample size of the current study (n = 101 vs. 22 in the previous study), which enhances the statistical reliability of our conclusions and provides a more representative estimate of clinical outcomes. Moreover, a 2021 study highlighted that supraclavicular nerve injuries were significantly less frequent in MIPO compared to ORIF, aligning with our findings on sensory disturbances [17].

Importantly, subgroup analysis based on the AO fracture classification revealed that MIPO consistently reduced the incidence of nonunion and sensory disturbance compared with ORIF, particularly in AO type 2 C fractures. These findings are both novel and clinically significant, indicating that the benefits of MIPO extend beyond simple fracture stabilization to complex and comminuted fracture patterns. Consistent with our results, a recent study on minimally invasive fixation for comminuted midshaft clavicle fractures reported no major complications among 19 patients with AO type 2B or 2 C fractures over a 16-month follow-up period [18]. In addition, a study demonstrated clinical and radiological benefits of MIPO with mini-open technique, showing comparable or better outcomes than open approaches in similar fracture types [19]. Similarly, a comparison showed shorter operative times and reduced complications with MIPO [20].

The perioperative advantages observed in our study are supported by recent comparative evidence. The study by Wang et al. also reported that MIPO yielded superior outcomes to ORIF across multiple measures, such as shorter operative time, reduced blood loss, smaller incisions, decreased postoperative pain, and fewer neurological complications, leading to significantly higher patient satisfaction [10]. Both studies consistently noted a markedly lower incidence of sensory disturbance with MIPO, reinforcing its anatomical benefit in supraclavicular nerve preservation. Furthermore, neither investigation reported postoperative infections in either group, a result likely related to standardized antibiotic prophylaxis, strict aseptic practice, and the minimal soft-tissue exposure associated with minimally invasive approaches.

The lower complication rate observed in the MIPO group can be attributed to several biomechanical and biological factors. Unlike conventional ORIF, which requires wide exposure and periosteal stripping for direct fracture reduction, MIPO is performed through small incisions and subcutaneous plate tunneling [21, 22]. This approach minimizes iatrogenic soft-tissue trauma, preserves the periosteal and endosteal blood supply critical for fracture healing, and reduces the risk of fragment devascularization 13. This preservation of the surrounding soft tissues consequently leads to a lower rate of complications, particularly nonunion and sensory disturbance [23]. From a clinical perspective, MIPO also provides smaller incisions that are associated with improved cosmetic outcomes and potentially less postoperative pain [24]. Moreover, because MIPO involves limited dissection around the supraclavicular nerves, the risk of postoperative sensory deficits is further diminished compared with ORIF [25]. Supporting this, a comparative analysis in 2025 found that minimally invasive techniques led to better patient satisfaction and fewer hardware-related issues [26].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, despite efforts to match baseline characteristics and blind outcome assessments. The choice of surgical technique was based on surgeon preference and patient factors, which could not be entirely standardized. Second, although our sample size (n = 203) was larger than that in many previous studies, further multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with larger cohorts are needed to validate these findings. Finally, cosmetic outcomes and patient satisfaction were not quantitatively assessed in this study. Future research incorporating patient-reported outcome measures and long-term functional follow-up is warranted to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of MIPO versus ORIF.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that MIPO provides functional outcomes equivalent to ORIF while significantly reducing complication rates, particularly nonunion and sensory disturbance. These advantages are especially evident in complex AO type 2 C fractures. Collectively, the findings support MIPO as a safe, effective, and cosmetically favorable alternative to conventional ORIF for midshaft clavicle fractures in modern clinical practice.
