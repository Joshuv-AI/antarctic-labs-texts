Ambient Light‐Activatable Luminescent Particle‐Embedded Conformal Patch for Photochemical Tissue Bonding and Photobiomodulated Healing
Authors: Kyungjoo Jo, Seong‐Jong Kim, Seung Oh Jung, Kyeongtaek Lee, Mungu Kim, Kwanghyeon Park, Yewon Seo, Boram Park, Dongchul Jo, Jongshin Kim, Jin Huh, Hyemin Kim, Sei Kwang Hahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBiophotonic healthcare devices have been widely investigated for photochemical tissue bonding (PTB), photobiomodulation (PBM), photodynamic therapy (PDT), and photothermal therapy (PTT). However, their clinical applications remain limited due to the short light penetration depth and the incompliant light source, including externally powered light‐emitting diodes (LEDs). Here, we present a stretchable photonic patch embedded with biocompatible room light‐ and sunlight‐activatable luminescent particles (RSLPs) in an elastomeric matrix. This patch enables ambient light‐activatable phototherapy without an external light source by absorbing broadband ambient light and converting it into red luminescence. The emission promotes PTB, fibroblast proliferation, and M1/M2 macrophage polarization for tissue regeneration, activating photosensitizers to induce collagen crosslinking and wound sealing. The conformal patch maintains optical performance even under movement, enabling continuous phototherapy during daily life activity. Remarkably, after PTB‐driven sealing, wound healing is accelerated in both linear and circular wounds, mediated by PBM‐induced tissue regeneration. A further clinical study on normal human facial skins demonstrates that a single PBM treatment with the patch acutely improves viscoelastic recovery and attenuates superficial erythema. This ambient light‐activatable biophotonic patch platform would pave a big avenue toward PTB, PBM, and various biomedical applications.

Introduction

Light‐based therapies have garnered great attention in biomedical applications due to their capability to non‐invasively modulate biological responses, promote tissue repair, and enhance wound healing [1, 2]. By using specific wavelengths to trigger cellular and molecular activity, phototherapy offers a safe and localized alternative to surgical techniques [3, 4]. Among various approaches, photochemical tissue bonding (PTB) and photobiomodulation (PBM) are distinct yet complementary strategies. While PTB improves tissue integrity by inducing collagen crosslinking at wound interfaces [5, 6]. PBM stimulates tissue regeneration via collagen synthesis and cellular proliferation [7, 8]. Notably, PTB is clinically applied in ophthalmology, such as riboflavin‐assisted ultraviolet (UV) corneal crosslinking to treat keratoconus or reinforce corneal tissue post‐surgery. Both modalities can replace sutures and adhesives in wound closure with non‐invasiveness, improved healing kinetics, and better cosmetic outcomes [9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

The clinical need for minimally invasive, self‐managed wound care continues to grow with increasing surgical procedures and emphasis on postoperative recovery outside of hospital settings. This demand is particularly pronounced in plastic and reconstructive surgeries involving aesthetically critical or highly exposed regions, which require rapid healing and minimal scarring [14, 15]. However, the clinical translation of phototherapy remains limited due to inconvenient light delivery systems that require external power, such as light‐emitting diode (LED) arrays, fiber optics, or battery modules [7, 8, 16]. Despite recent advances in miniaturized photonic devices and wireless LEDs, these platforms still face challenges in terms of cost, complexity, and regulatory approval [17, 18, 19]. Moreover, maintaining continuous luminescence reduces patient compliance and treatment adherence. The absence of wearable, self‐managed technologies capable of delivering sustained, non‐invasive stimulation often leads to delayed recovery and suboptimal outcomes, underscoring the urgent need for a power‐independent and compliant phototherapeutic system.

Here, we first report a wearable photonic patch incorporating room light‐ and sunlight‐activatable luminescent particles (RSLPs) for ambient light‐driven phototherapy by both PTB and PBM (Figure 1a). The wearable photonic patch harvests ambient light, defined as room light and sunlight, and converts it into the therapeutic red luminescence without an external power source. Specifically, the red luminescence converted by RSLPs induces PTB via chlorin e6 (Ce6)‐mediated collagen crosslinking and PBM through mitochondrial activation and enhanced fibroblast activity (Figure 1b). The photonic patch maintains stable luminescence under mechanical strain and supports on‐body operation. In the following, we describe the synthesis and optical characteristics of the photonic patch, evaluate the therapeutic effects in vitro and in vivo, and demonstrate the effective PTB and PBM for wound regeneration in incisional and excisional wound models under ambient‐light conditions (Figure 1c,d). Finally, we assess translational potential by demonstrating measurable PBM effects on normal human facial skin under room light.

Results and Discussion

Design Rationale for Ambient Light‐Responsive RSLPs

We designed RSLPs capable of harvesting energy from ambient light and converting it into therapeutic red emission light to overcome the power‐dependent limitation of conventional phototherapy platforms, such as LEDs [7, 8] and optical fibers [16]. For this purpose, the particles should simultaneously satisfy several requirements: broad excitation by daily ambient light, therapeutic red emission, rechargeable energy storage, and stable integration into a stretchable elastomeric patch for wearable application. Among various red‐emissive materials, we selected afterglow luminescent particles (ALPs) to design RSLPs based on three key characteristics in the following. First, defect and trap states in ALPs can capture and store photogenerated charge carriers under broad‐wavelength excitation [5, 20]. The energy states of defects, such as sulfur (S) and oxygen (O) vacancies, are located below the conduction bands, and the trap depths of these vacancies are broad enough to absorb various wavelengths of external energy. Second, ALPs can demonstrate a higher power density of photoluminescence (PL) than upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) [19] and carbon dots [21], making them suitable for biomedical applications [22, 23, 24]. Third, trap‐mediated afterglow and rechargeability can allow the particles to provide red emission after transient interruption of room light or sunlight. Although the primary therapeutic dose is delivered by PL during exposure to ambient light, afterglow luminescence (AL) can serve as a short‐lived rechargeable phototherapy [25, 26].

To demonstrate our hypothesis, we selected calcium strontium sulfide (CaSrS) particles as the host material based on three key considerations. The first hypothesis is that CaS (bandgap: 3.57–4.43 eV) [27] and SrS (bandgap: 2.46–3.82 eV) [28] can form a heterojunction structure, facilitating band offsets that promote electron excitation into the conduction band (CB) under a broader spectrum of external energy [29]. The second is that S vacancies can be an effective defect state for trapping electrons, thereby expanding the excitation wavelength of CaSrS particles to broadly absorb energy from ambient light [5, 30, 31]. Furthermore, by controlling the annealing temperature and atmosphere to prevent the formation of sulfate (SO4), a greater abundance of S vacancies can be generated, creating defect states with a broad trap depth below the conduction band. The third is that CaSrS particles can be red‐light emitting RSLPs necessary for PBM by doping europium (Eu) and thulium (Tm) ions [32]. To further clarify the rationale of material selection, we compared representative red‐emissive and persistent luminescent materials, including organic fluorophores/photosensitizers, quantum dots, carbon dots, UCNPs, and conventional SrAl2O4‐ and ZnGa2O4‐based persistent phosphors (Table S1). This table clearly shows that the selected RSLPs of CaSr(S, SO4):Eu, Tm satisfy the key requirements for the present wearable phototherapy platform, including broad activation under ambient light, red emission matching to PTB and PBM, trap‐mediated rechargeability, no need for external excitation sources, and integration into stretchable patches.

Synthesis and Structural Analysis of RSLPs

The RSLPs of CaSr(S, SO4):Eu, Tm were synthesized via a two‐step process, starting with the preparation of CaSrS:Eu, Tm particles through a solid‐state reaction under a reducing atmosphere, followed by partial oxidation in ambient air at room temperature (Figures S1 and S2 for CaSrS:Eu, Tm particles; Discussion S1). This partial oxidation significantly improved the compatibility of RSLPs with the Ecoflex matrix, resulting in enhanced mechanical and physical stability of the composite (Figure S3). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed that the surface morphology slightly changed after oxidation, with the formation of sharp, needle‐like structures on the oxidized particles (Figure S4a), in contrast to the smooth surface of unoxidized particles (Figure S1a). The distinct morphological phenomenon of RSLPs was uniformly observed at a large scale (Figure 2a). The elemental mapping and energy dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the increased atomic concentration of oxygen and the partial oxidation on the surface of CaSr(S, SO4):Eu, Tm particles (Figure 2b,c; Figure S4b,c) compared to those of CaSrS:Eu, Tm particles in Figure S1. Other elements were homogeneously distributed, including partially resolved peaks of Eu2+ and Tm3+ ions. X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis also validated the presence of oxygen on the surface of CaSr(S, SO4):Eu, Tm particles, with the emergence of an O 1s peak and an oxygen‐associated shift in the S 2p3/2 peak (Figure 2d and Figure S5). To corroborate these observations with structural evidence, we next conducted x‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. The results revealed that oxidation led to the formation of SrSO4 phases and an increase in grain size in CaSr(S, SO4):Eu, Tm particles compared to CaSrS:Eu, Tm particles (Figure 2e,f). The lattice parameters and unit cell volume remained largely unchanged, although a slight reduction was observed in crystallinity.

Optical Analysis and Mechanism of RSLPs

Optical analyses revealed that the RSLPs exhibited broad absorption between 300 and 600 nm, accompanied by red emission centered at 640 nm, under both PL and AL conditions (Figure 2g,h). These results indicated that sulfur vacancies functioned as effective electron trap states, thereby broadening the excitation range compared to previously reported CaSrS particles [32] and enabling efficient absorption of ambient light. The PL and AL emissions were positioned near the red boundary of the International Commission on Illumination (CIE) chromaticity diagram, indicating a highly saturated red emission with excellent color purity (Figure 2i). The PL and AL images showed a significant luminescent intensity visible to the naked eye for more than 300 s without an external light source (Figure 2j). The absolute AL intensity at a wavelength of 640 nm corresponded to an irradiance of approximately 6.31 × 1011 photons·s−1·cm−2·sr−1 (mg/mL)−1 when embedded in Ecoflex. The afterglow persisted for over 1800 s, and the decay profile showed a rapid initial decay followed by a slower afterglow tail, with the characteristic decay times of 15.42, 41.04, and 87.21 s to reach 1/2, 1/4, and 1/8 of the initial intensity, respectively (Figure 2k). The particles also exhibited remarkable rechargeability and optical stability under repeated charging with room light (Figure 2l). Thus, AL could provide emission during transient interruption of room light in wearable use, while the therapeutic red‐light dose would be primarily delivered by room light‐induced PL. To further evaluate the long‐term PL stability of RSLPs, the PL intensity was monitored for 12 h under room light (Figure S6). The room light‐induced PL intensity remained stable at 0, 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h, confirming the stable red emission during prolonged operation. The broadened absorption and enhanced luminescence might be attributed to defect engineering and heterojunction formation (Figure 2m). The RSLPs contained positively charged substitutional defects ([TmCa] + and [TmSr]+) and sulfur vacancies ([Vs]2+) that acted as electron traps, delaying the recombination of electrons and holes until thermal energy released the electrons back into the conduction band. These defects absorbed broader excitation energy, and band offsets at heterojunction interfaces effectively broadened the conduction band energy window. Then, the energy from the recombination of electrons and holes was non‐radiatively transferred to Tm3+ and Eu2+ emitters, thereby emitting enhanced and prolonged radiative irradiation.

Optimization and Characterization of Ecoflex/RSLP Patches

To develop a wearable platform for ambient light‐activatable phototherapy, RSLPs were embedded in a stretchable Ecoflex elastomer matrix to function as a silicone‐based emission layer (Figure 3a). A transparent Ecoflex layer was introduced beneath the RSLP‐loaded emission layer to enhance mechanical stability and prevent direct contact between RSLPs and the skin at the attachment interface. The RSLP concentration in Ecoflex was optimized to 400 mg/mL (Figure 3b, Figures S7 and S8; Discussion S2), and 1.1 mL of this mixture was used per patch to balance light penetration and mechanical stability. The optimized patches featured an emission layer thickness of 1.11 mm (Figure 3c and Figure S9), which was selected to ensure sufficient light penetration while maintaining structural integrity.

Cross‐sectional SEM imaging revealed a uniform distribution of RSLPs within the emission layer of Ecoflex/RSLP patches (Figure 3d). The distinct bilayer structure, separated by a transparent intermediate layer, was corroborated by elemental mapping, which localized Ca, Sr, and S to the RSLP‐containing layer and Si and O to the Ecoflex layer (Figure 3e,f). The thicknesses of the top and bottom layers were approximately 1.12 and 0.22 mm, respectively. In addition, top‐view SEM images showed complete embedding of the RSLPs within the Ecoflex matrix, supported by dominant Si signals and no detectable RSLP‐associated elemental signals at the surface (Figure S10). The optical properties of Ecoflex/RSLP patches were further characterized under various light sources, showing strong red emission at 640 nm in both PL and AL spectra across a broad excitation range of 300 to 600 nm (Figure 3g,h). Based on the correlation between PL and AL intensities under varying power densities of external light sources, we derived an analytical equation (Figure 3i). This relationship indicated that AL intensity scaled with PL intensity, allowing AL to serve as a visually comparable and quantitatively reliable proxy for PL because it could be readily captured without interference from external illumination. Figure 3j,k shows the top‐view photographs and PL images of the patches. To further assess the optical performance under ambient‐light conditions, we evaluated AL under UV light, room light, and sunlight using an imaging setup for sunlight‐driven AL, as illustrated in Figure S11. Notably, the AL emissions under UV light, room light, and sunlight were visible to the naked eye and sustained for over 300 s without any external light source (Figure 3k,l). To clarify the optical dose delivered under room light or sunlight, we quantitatively measured PL power densities (Figure 3m and Figure S12 for AL power densities). The spectrum of white room light exhibits main peaks at blue (475 nm), green (540 nm), and red (600 nm) wavelengths, whereas that of sunlight commonly shows a continuous curve of irradiance mainly in the wavelength range of 400–800 nm. The power density of room light and sunlight is in the order of 0.1 and 10 mW cm−2, respectively. Because the experiments under uncontrolled room light can be affected by the light source, distance, angle, surrounding reflection, and experimental setup, we established a controlled room light‐matched white LED illumination for in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo experiments. Specifically, room light was directly measured at a distance of 1.5 m from the light source, which corresponded to a power density of 0.7477 mW cm−2. The room light‐matched white LED was positioned at 15 cm from the sample, and the irradiance on the patch plane was adjusted to the power density of room light (0.7477 mW cm−2). Under this condition, Ecoflex/RSLP patches delivered the PL power density of 0.1640 mW cm−2, demonstrating 21.92% conversion efficiency. The measured PL power density was slightly higher under sunlight, showing 0.2020 mW cm−2. After turning off ambient light, the initial AL power density of room light and sunlight was 0.0292 and 0.0368 mW cm−2, respectively. Based on the measured PL power density, the wearable patch could deliver cumulative optical doses of 1.18 J cm−2 for 2 h, 5.31 J cm−2 for 9 h, and 7.08 J cm−2 for 12 h. Thus, the patch could be used as a phototherapeutic platform with long‐durating low‐irradiance instead of a high‐power tunable light source [7, 8]. This dosing profile would enable biologically relevant cumulative fluence by the exposure to room light and by AL to provide emission during the transient interruption of room light. Furthermore, we evaluated the safety of the patches by assessing heat generation and residual particulates, as detailed in Discussion S3 (Figures S13–S15). Considering the reported irradiance and dose ranges required for effective PTB and PBM [5, 6, 7, 8], these findings demonstrated that Ecoflex/RSLP patches could reliably support both therapies under everyday ambient‐light conditions, facilitating seamless integration into daily wound care routines without the need for external power sources.

Strain‐Tolerant Optical Performance of Ecoflex/RSLP Patches

The mechanical stretchability of Ecoflex/RSLP patches was evaluated under varying strains to assess their suitability as wearable phototherapeutic tools for application onto diverse body areas, including areas subject to extreme bending or daily movement. The patches exhibited intensive PL and AL brightness at 0% strain and retained their luminescence even under 100% strain (Figure 4a). Although the normalized power density slightly decreased due to the enlarged area under fixed RSLP concentration, the intensity reduction of optimized Ecoflex/RSLP patches was negligible compared with Ecoflex/RSLP patches of low RSLP concentration (Figure 4b and Figure S16; Discussion S4). Additionally, the normalized PL power density of Ecoflex/RSLP patches remained stable during repeated stretching between 0% and 100% strain (Figure 4c). Specifically, the PL intensity remained stable under 100% strain but gradually decreased when returned to 0% strain over multiple cycles, eventually converging to the value observed at 100% strain. This behavior suggested that although strains beyond 100% could be applied, slight plastic deformation might occur with repeated use. In contrast, under repeated 50% strain, the patches recovered their original PL power density after release to 0% strain without residual deformation, supporting long‐term wearable use where repeated stretching during attachment and daily motion is inevitable (Figure 4d,e). This stability was preserved under room light or room light‐matched white LED illumination, and we further examined the optical stability of the patches under repeated folding (Figure S17). After repeated 180° folding/unfolding cycles, the normalized power density remained close to the initial value without an apparent cycle‐dependent decrease, indicating that repeated folding did not measurably impair the luminescent output of the embedded RSLPs. Moreover, the patches demonstrated an ultimate tensile strength at 520% strain and mechanical failure at around 600% strain, confirming the remarkable flexibility and suitability for skin‐mounted applications (Figure S18).

Wearable Photonic Patches With Skin‐Adhesive Hydrogels

The photonic patch was prepared as a tri‐layer structure consisting of an Ecoflex/RSLP emission layer, a transparent Ecoflex layer, and a hydrogel adhesive layer composed of dopamine‐conjugated hyaluronic acid crosslinked with iron ions (HA‐DOPA/Fe) (Figure 4f and Figure S19; Discussion S5). Because catechol/Fe coordination systems can absorb visible light, we further evaluated the effect of HA‐DOPA/Fe adhesive on optical transmission or photothermal heating. The adhesive hydrogels were applied only along the peripheral edge of the Ecoflex/RSLP patch, leaving the central tissue‐facing optical window uninterrupted. Although HA‐DOPA/Fe exhibited broad visible absorption, the absorbance decreased toward the red region and remained relatively low near the 640 nm emission wavelength of the Ecoflex/RSLP patch (Figure S20). Thermal imaging of the adhesive‐integrated patch before and after UV irradiation showed no appreciable heat accumulation in the adhesive region, reflecting that HA‐DOPA/Fe did not introduce a significant photothermal contribution under the tested condition (Figure S21). In addition, the integrated patch maintained visible red AL after brief UV charging over 12 h, indicating that the edge‐applied HA‐DOPA/Fe adhesive did not impair the rechargeability or long‐term luminescent performance of the Ecoflex/RSLP patch (Figure S22). The adhesive performance was assessed by in vivo skin application and removal tests in mice (Figure 4g,h), resulting in strong adhesion to tissue and clean detachment of HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogel both cases of alone and integrated with the patch. We selected the hydrogel form of HA‐DOPA/Fe, because the patch form was too thin to provide sufficient adhesive strength. To compare the performance, we fabricated three variants, HA‐DOPA patch, HA‐DOPA/Fe patch, and HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogel (Figure 4i), and evaluated their tensile strength by ex vivo porcine skin tests (Figure 4j). The HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogel exhibited the highest tensile strength among all groups, primarily due to the high density of catechol functionalization, which further enhanced both the cohesive network integrity and interfacial adhesion [33]. To further quantify the adhesive and interfacial mechanical behavior of the HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogel, additional parameters were calculated from tensile curves (Figures S23 and S24). The HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogel exhibited an extension at a maximum stress of 4.16 ± 0.35 mm, a post‐peak deformation window of 1.05 ± 0.06 mm, a baseline‐corrected apparent detachment work of 135.3 ± 21.6 J m−2, and a post‐peak softening slope of −71.74 ± 3.10 kPa mm−1. Considering the adhesive area between the tissues, these parameters supported the conformal and durable interfacial performance with post‐peak softening response of the hydrogel for facile detachment. Collectively, these results established the feasibility of the photonic patch as a soft, stretchable, and light‐responsive platform with skin‐adhesive properties suitable for daily wearable phototherapeutic applications.

Ambient Light‐Induced Collagen Crosslinking for Photochemical Tissue Bonding

PTB enhances tissue integrity through the direct crosslinking of collagen at the incision sites, while PBM promotes tissue regeneration primarily by stimulating collagen proliferation. We examined the PTB with Ce6, a red light‐responsive photosensitizer, to induce collagen crosslinking by the activation of photonic patches (Figure S25; Discussion S6). An ex vivo setup was designed to evaluate the collagen crosslinking induced by Ce6 and the patches (Figure 5a). Tensile strength measurements confirmed the highest crosslinking of porcine tissues in the group treated with both Ce6 and the patches under room light‐matched white LED illumination, compared to other groups treated with Ce6 or the patches alone (Figure 5b). Furthermore, real‐time monitoring of collagen fibrillogenesis revealed a marked increase in optical density in the group treated with both Ce6 and the patches under room light, reflecting successful light‐induced collagen crosslinking (Figure 5c). Because Ce6 can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon photoactivation, we further examined ROS generation under room light‐matched white LED illumination (Figure S26). The Ce6+Patch group showed higher ROS generation than the Ce6 or Patch‐only groups, whereas the Patch‐only group did not show any meaningful ROS generation. These results indicate that Ce6 was photoactivated by patch‐derived red emission under the treatment condition. In PTB, photoactivated Ce6 generated short‐lived reactive species that oxidatively activated collagen molecules at the wound interface, producing collagen‐derived radical or oxidized intermediates. These intermediates could trigger covalent crosslinking between adjacent collagen chains/fibrils, leading to interfacial collagen bonding and tissue sealing. In consistent with this mechanism, Ce6+Patch treatment showed the highest increase in collagen fibrillogenesis‐associated turbidity and superior ex vivo tissue bonding strength under room light‐matched irradiation. Thus, the ROS generated in the Ce6+Patch group was interpreted primarily as a photochemical trigger for collagen crosslinking and tissue bonding, rather than as a PDT‐like cytotoxic or inflammatory stimulus. Besides, the residual afterglow sustained Ce6‐mediated photochemical activity during the transient interruption of room light (Figure S27). AL did not dominate the therapeutic dose, but it functionally contributed by partially sustaining Ce6 photoactivation and collagen crosslinking during the transient light‐off intervals, making the patch as a PL‐dominant and AL‐assisted phototherapy platform. To ensure clinical relevance and safety, we further evaluated the cytocompatibility of Ce6 used in tissue bonding. A cytotoxicity assay demonstrated that Ce6 was highly biocompatible, maintaining over 90% cell viability at concentrations up to 30 µg/mL, which was used in previous and subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies (Figure S28).

Ambient Light‐Induced Fibroblast Proliferation and Biocompatibility of Photonic Patches

To investigate the biological effects of photonic stimulation under ambient light for PBM, we applied Ecoflex/RSLP patches with HA‐DOPA/Fe adhesives to NIH3T3 fibroblast cultures and evaluated the cellular behavior over time under room light, following the illustrated setup (Figure 5d). DAPI staining revealed significantly increased cell numbers in the patch‐treated group under room light compared to the control and light‐only groups (Figure 5e). The proliferation effect was quantitatively analyzed on each group by normalizing the DAPI fluorescence, which revealed significantly enhanced proliferation in the group treated with both the patches and room light (Figure 5f). On the other hand, there was no difference in proliferation between the control group and light‐only group. Live‐cell scratch assays further confirmed the enhanced wound closure in the group treated with the patches and room light, particularly at 24 h, suggesting that the red light emitted from the patches effectively stimulated cellular migration (Figure 5g). The quantitative analysis of cell migration revealed an extensive increase of cell proliferation in the group treated with the patches and room light (Figure 5h). Especially, the proliferation rate between days 1 and 3 increased by approximately 1.94‐fold, which was faster than the 1.50‐fold increase observed between days 3 and 5, suggesting that red emission from the photonic patches boosted cellular proliferation. On the other hand, cell viability remained above 90% throughout the incubation of the patches with NIH3T3 cells for 192 h, confirming the notable biocompatibility of the patch for biomedical applications (Figure 5i).

Synergistic Linear Wound Healing by Optimized Phototherapy In Vivo

To evaluate in vivo therapeutic effect of RSLP/Ecoflex patches with HA‐DOPA/Fe adhesives, we employed two mouse wound models: linear incision and circular excision wounds. Considering that ambient light exposure typically lasts approximately 12 h per day, the photonic patches were removed after each treatment (Figures S29 and S30; Discussion S7). To determine the optimal treatment frequency for promoting linear wound healing, we evaluated three regimens of single administration, every other day, and daily treatment. A single treatment of the patches with Ce6 under room light for 12 h was performed following the illustrated timeline (Figure 6a). In the context of linear wounds, the treatment was designed to simultaneously induce PTB and PBM (Figure 6b). Remarkably, a single administration achieved superior wound closure with wound healing progression (Figure 6c). Specifically, on day 1, the Ce6+Patch group exhibited significantly higher tensile strength compared to the Suture group, indicating that PTB played a predominant role in the rapid tissue sealing (Figure 6d). Concurrently, the enhancement in wound closure was attributable to PBM‐induced cellular proliferation. The synergistic effect was further confirmed by well‐aligned and tightly approximated wound margins on both front and back sides of the excised mouse skin (Figure 6e). On day 7, tensile strength was significantly higher in the Ce6+Patch group than in the Patch only and Suture groups (Figure 6f), confirming its superior therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, the Patch‐only group, which lacked the functional wound sealing, benefited primarily from PBM‐driven tissue remodeling and therefore showed weaker mechanical recovery than the Ce6+Patch group.

We next examined whether repeated treatments would further improve the outcome or not (Figures S31 and S32). Remarkably, the Ce6+Patch group showed minimal improvement with additional treatments, as the initial PTB‐driven tissue sealing had already been completed after the first treatment. This observation was corroborated by tensile strength measurements with negligible variation across treatment frequencies (Figure 6g). Because repeated treatments would reduce patient convenience and compliance, a single 12‐h Ce6+Patch treatment was considered the optimal condition for linear wound repair. Overall, superior therapeutic outcomes of Ce6+Patch treatment resulted from both PTB and PBM, enabling immediate tissue approximation and accelerated wound closure.

In Vivo Immune Modulation and Regenerative Remodeling in Linear Wounds

The anti‐inflammatory effects of PTB and PBM were further supported by the downregulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokine (IL‐1β) and upregulation of anti‐inflammatory cytokine (TGF‐β), particularly evident in the Patch‐only and Ce6+Patch groups (Figure 6h,i). On day 2, when wound healing was actively progressing, the Ce6+Patch group exhibited lower levels of inflammatory cytokines and elevated levels of regenerative cytokines compared to the control group. This response was likely due to effective tissue approximation induced by ROS‐mediated collagen crosslinking for PTB, which created an early wound‐edge stabilization and a more favorable microenvironment for tissue regeneration. By day 4, cytokine levels in all groups, excluding the untreated controls, returned to the baseline, suggesting the completion of the proliferative phase of wound healing. These effects were mediated by PBM‐induced macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, playing a central role in the inflammation resolution and tissue regeneration [34, 35]. Histological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome staining on day 7 revealed that the Ce6+Patch group achieved the most complete re‐epithelialization, with well‐organized epidermal layers and densely arranged collagen fibers similar to the Normal control group (Figure 6j and Figure S33). The Patch‐only or Suture groups displayed significant epithelial restoration along with appreciable collagen accumulation. However, the Suture group showed abnormally thickened epidermis with scar‐like morphology, likely resulting from mechanical wound closure. In contrast, the Control and Ecoflex groups showed the fragmented epithelial architecture and disorganized collagen deposition. Immunofluorescence imaging on day 7 further corroborated the histological findings. The Suture, Patch‐only, and Ce6+Patch groups exhibited normalized marker expression comparable to that of adjacent unwounded tissue, suggesting the completion of regenerative processes (Figure 6k, Figures S34 and S35). In contrast, the cytokine expression of K14 and CD31 was elevated in incompletely healed wounds from the Control and Ecoflex groups, reflecting the ongoing epithelial proliferation and neovascularization. Similarly, the expression levels of α‐SMA and TNF‐α, reflecting myofibroblast activation and sustained inflammation, were elevated in poorly healed wounds. Taken together, the synergistic linear wound healing by our wearable photonic patch resulted in more complete tissue regeneration, effective inflammation resolution, and the suppression of fibrotic remodeling.

PBM‐dominated Circular Wound Healing by Optimized Phototherapy In Vivo

To identify an effective treatment schedule for circular wounds, we evaluated different treatment durations and frequencies (Figures S36–S38; Discussion S8). Double 12‐h treatments administered on alternate days were selected for subsequent experiments, as circular wounds primarily depend on PBM rather than PTB (Figure 7a). Specifically, circular wounds required sustained anti‐inflammatory activity and enhanced cellular proliferation for meaningful wound healing (Figure 7b). Time‐lapse imaging confirmed progressive wound closure in both the Patch‐only and Ce6+Patch groups throughout the treatment period, with a notably accelerated and nonlinear healing phase observed particularly between days 2 and 4 (Figure 7c and Figures S39–S41). To our knowledge, such a distinct acceleration phase has been rarely described in prior PBM‐based wound healing studies [7, 8], potentially because conventional LED protocols typically provide intermittent illumination with limited duration compared with 12‐h treatment. In contrast, the photonic patches enabled continuous treatment over extended periods, enhancing the cumulative PBM dose and thereby promoting proliferative responses beyond those achievable with shorter exposures. Quantitative analysis revealed a significant reduction in wound area by day 4 in consistent with a nonlinear healing trajectory, suggesting that ambient light‐driven PBM therapy effectively accelerated the proliferation phase of wound repair (Figure 7d,e). To further examine the healing dynamics of the Patch‐only and Ce6+Patch groups, we analyzed the wound closure at each interval using the normalized wound area in Figure 7d (Figure S42). Importantly, the closure profiles at each interval of the Patch‐only group were slightly higher than those of Ce6+Patch group, indicating that the addition of Ce6 did not substantially accelerate circular wound closure, exceeding the effect of the photonic patch alone. This result supported a PBM‐dominated healing mechanism in circular excision wounds. In addition, statistical analysis confirmed that both the Patch‐only and the Ce6+Patch groups showed minimal improvement with additional treatments (Figure 7f,g). Notably, the daily treatment slightly delayed healing, potentially due to insufficient time for the natural clearance of apoptotic cells in the circular wound environment.

In Vivo Immune Modulation and Regenerative Remodeling in Circular Wounds

Unlike linear wounds, where PTB contributes significantly to approximating wound edges and suppressing inflammation, circular wounds with their broader surface area and slower healing demand sustained and robust anti‐inflammatory modulation. This requirement was supported by quantitative analysis, which showed persistently elevated levels of the pro‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐1β on both day 2 and 4 in the Control group (Figure 7h,i). Conversely, the expression of TGF‐β, a marker of M2 macrophage polarization, was significantly upregulated in the Patch‐only and Ce6+Patch groups across both time points, with a further increase on day 4. This elevated expression likely reflected enhanced cellular proliferation and accelerated wound remodeling, indicating that sustained PBM facilitated M2 polarization, thereby promoting efficient resolution of inflammation and tissue regeneration. Furthermore, H&E and Masson's trichrome staining on day 10 revealed that both the Patch‐only and Ce6+Patch groups exhibited advanced re‐epithelialization, characterized by the remodeled epidermal layer and uniformly distributed collagen fibers (Figure 7j). In contrast, the Control and Ecoflex groups showed fragmented epithelial integrity and sparse, disorganized collagen structures (Figure S43). Marker expression in the Patch‐only and Ce6+Patch groups returned to levels comparable to that of adjacent healthy tissue, suggesting more complete tissue regeneration (Figure 7k, Figures S44 and S45). In consistent with the anti‐inflammatory and anti‐fibrotic effects previously observed in the linear wounds, these reductions confirmed the conclusion that sustained PBM promoted M2 macrophage polarization, facilitating inflammation resolution and tissue remodeling in circular wound healing. The difference between the Patch‐only and Ce6+Patch groups was relatively modest in the circular wound model, likely due to the limited role of Ce6‐mediated PTB and the predominance of PBM as the primary therapeutic mechanism. These findings collectively validated the RSLP/Ecoflex patches, combined with HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogels, as a power‐free photonic therapy platform adaptable to diverse wound geometries and therapeutic demands, highlighting its translational potential for clinical application.

Preliminary Human Evaluation of Ambient Light‐Induced Photobiomodulated Healing

Building on the in vitro and in vivo evidence for PBM with the ambient light‐activated photonic patch, we next conducted a preliminary human skin‐contact study on normal facial skin to evaluate wearable operation, skin compatibility, and acute PBM‐associated biophysical changes under room light. Most clinical PBM protocols for skin applications typically employ brief irradiation repeated several times per week over multiple weeks, with single short exposures yielding only modest shifts in clinical or biophysical readouts [36, 37]. Here, we leveraged PL and afterglow emission of the photonic patch to deliver a single PBM treatment on intact facial skin under room light (Figure 8a). Ordinary indoor room light was sufficient to continuously recharge the patch during wear to produce red emission, whereas afterglow remained visible even in the absence of room light, confirming power‐free light delivery on skin during daily activities (Figure 8b). To compare day‐to‐day changes in skin viscoelastic behavior while minimizing sensitivity to baseline offsets and probe preloading, the representative traces on day 0 and day 1 were normalized to the maximum angular displacement (Figure 8c,d; Discussion S9). Following PBM treatment, the post‐unloading trajectory converged to a lower residual level on day 1, consistent with a more complete elastic recovery toward the pre‐load state after release of tensile loading. To quantify these viscoelastic behavior, creep proportion index and plastic proportion index were calculated from the normalized traces (Figure 8e). Among these proportional indices, a notable change was a reduction in the plastic proportion index in the treated group, from 0.6548 to 0.5708 (12.83% reduction), indicating a lower fraction of irreversible deformation remaining after unloading and improved viscoelastic recovery. As proportional indices did not represent absolute angular deformation and recovery kinetics, the same datasets were baseline‐subtracted using the initial mean displacement (Figure 8f,g). PBM did not produce a marked difference in net maximum displacement between conditions (see Discussion S12), but the recovery time constant (τrecovery) decreased from 0.895 to 0.488 s (45.47% reduction) after treatment, demonstrating notable accelerated mechanical recovery after deformation (Figure 8h).

Facial skin appearance was assessed using multimodal imaging over a fixed square region of interest (ROI) on each cheek (Figure S46). DermaVision Pro provided spatial maps of the S‐gray, pore, and wrinkle in parallel‐polarized imaging (PPI) mode and melanin and erythema indices in cross‐polarized imaging (CPI) mode (Figure 8i). To quantitatively compare day‐to‐day changes while minimizing baseline differences between cheeks, all indices were averaged over the ROI and normalized to the day 0 value of each cheek (Figure 8j,k). The S‐gray index of the contralateral control cheek decreased by 5.74%, whereas the PBM‐treated cheek increased by 16.13%, indicating an overall improvement in brightness and tone uniformity at the treated site. In addition, the erythema index of the control cheek increased by 4.52%, whereas the PBM‐treated cheek decreased by 41.92%, consistent with a substantial reduction in superficial vascular redness after PBM treatment. Together, these findings demonstrated a clinically relevant, ambient light‐activated PBM effect of the wearable patch, marked by improved viscoelastic recovery, enhanced skin brightness, and rapid attenuation of superficial erythema without substantially altering overall mechanical deformability.

Conclusion

We developed a single wearable biophotonic platform, enabling rapid and robust tissue regeneration with minimal treatment for patient convenience. As compared in Table S2, our patches uniquely integrate both PTB and PBM, unlike most systems targeting a single functional mechanism [38, 39, 40]. Complete wound closure was achieved with only one administration in linear wound models and full healing in circular wounds within two 12‐h treatments by day 10, outperforming most systems that required 12 to 16 days of repeated treatment [8, 9, 11, 12, 13]. Focusing specifically on phototherapy‐based technologies (Table S3), the photonic patches further distinguish themselves by eliminating the need for external power sources, such as batteries or lasers, which are commonly required in LED‐ or NIR‐based platforms [7, 41]. Compared to luminescent materials with low power density [3, 42, 43] and short lifetime [44, 45, 46], our system demonstrates, for the first time, that inorganic luminescent particles can provide therapeutic‐level light delivery under ambient light, advancing beyond imaging‐focused systems toward clinically effective phototherapy [47, 48, 49, 50]. Collectively, these comparisons highlight our light‐harvesting therapeutic patch as a wearable and ambient light‐activatable platform for biophotonic wound therapy. In addition to preclinical wound models, a pilot human evaluation on normal human facial skin showed that a single 9‐h PBM treatment with the photonic patch acutely enhanced viscoelastic recovery and attenuated superficial erythema while preserving surface texture, supporting the translational feasibility and safety of power‐free PBM delivery in cosmetically sensitive regions. Specifically, the biophotonic patches demonstrate strong clinical potential for postoperative and aesthetic care, including facial plastic surgery and dermatologic wounds, enabling home‐based treatment, reducing clinical burden, and broadening therapeutic access beyond the reach of emerging biomaterials [51, 52, 53]. By combining material design, photonic engineering, and regenerative biology, our system would lay the foundation for next‐generation, patient‐friendly wound care solutions that are clinically feasible, scalable, and suitable for everyday usage.

Synthesis of RSLPs of CaSr(S, SO4):Eu, Tm

Room light and sunlight‐activatable luminescent particles (RSLPs) of CaSr(S, SO4):Eu, Tm were synthesized via a solid‐state reaction. Precursor materials including CaCO3 (1 mmol), SrCO3 (0.17 mmol), Eu2O3 (0.0004 mmol), thulium (III) acetate hydrate (0.0014 mmol), and H3BO3 (0.01 mmol, used as a flux) were thoroughly ground in an agate mortar for 30 min. Sulfur (4 mmol) was subsequently added to the mixture, followed by thermal treatment at 1100°C for 1 h under a reducing atmosphere (5% hydrogen in argon). The resulting product was then allowed to cool to room temperature. The synthesized CaSrS:Eu, Tm particles were stored under vacuum conditions before use. To obtain partially oxidized RSLPs of CaSr(S, SO4):Eu, Tm, particles were exposed to ambient air at room temperature for 24 h.

Characterization of RSLPs of CaSr(S, SO4):Eu, Tm

The morphology, elemental composition, and particle size of the synthesized RSLPs were characterized using field‐emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‐SEM) equipped with dual‐energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (JSM‐7800F, JEOL Ltd., Akishima, Japan). To assess elemental valence states, x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using an ESCALAB 250 system (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). Crystal structure analysis was conducted using x‐ray diffraction (XRD) with a D/MAX‐2500‐PC diffractometer (Rigaku Co., Akishima, Japan) equipped with Cu‐Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å), operating at 100 kV and 40 mA. Origin software (Microcal Software Inc., Northampton, MA) was employed to analyze crystallinity. Grain size and lattice constants were derived from the XRD data using the Scherrer equation and Bragg's law, respectively. Unit cell volume was estimated based on the lattice structure and constants.

Photoluminescence (PL) and afterglow luminescence (AL) spectra of RSLPs were obtained using a Horiba FluoroLog Fluorometer spectrophotometer (HORIBA Ltd., Kyoto, Japan). The samples were placed in 1‐cm quartz cuvettes, and PL emission spectra were recorded within the 580–700 nm range under 365 nm excitation. Additional excitation wavelengths, ranging from 300 to 600 nm, were applied to evaluate the spectral response at 640 nm. For AL measurements, the excitation light was switched off, and the samples were briefly charged with room light‐matched white LED (0.7 W, 15 cm distance, 0.7477 mW cm−2) for 5 s, after which AL spectra were recorded over the 580 to 700 nm range. The power density of room light or room light‐matched white LED was measured at a distance of 1.5 m from the light source using a power meter (PM100D, Thorlabs, Newton, New Jersey) coupled to a power sensor (S120VC, Thorlabs, Newton, New Jersey) with the reference power density used as a calibration standard. To monitor AL decay profiles, a microplate fluorometer (Fluoroskan Ascent FL, Thermo Scientific) was used. RSLPs were loaded into a 96‐well plate, and AL intensity was tracked for 30 min following light cessation. Rechargeability was assessed by subjecting the samples to room light for 5 s before each trial, and the resulting AL signals were measured for 5 min. To measure long‐term PL stability, RSLPs were continuously exposed to room light for 12 h, and PL intensity was measured at 0, 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h using a power meter coupled to a power sensor. PL images were acquired using a charge‐coupled device (CCD) camera (Canon EOS 100D; Canon, Tokyo, Japan) under room light‐matched white LED illumination. Afterglow images were sequentially captured every 30 s following LED exposure for 5 s.

Preparation of Adhesive HA‐DOPA/Fe Hydrogels

HA‐DOPA conjugates were synthesized through EDC/NHS coupling chemistry. Briefly, HA (100 kDa, 0.25 mmol, 100 mg) was fully dissolved in deionized (DI) water at a concentration of 5 mg mL−1. The solution was adjusted to pH 5.5 and sequentially mixed with EDC (1 mmol, 191 mg) and NHS (1 mmol, 120 mg). Subsequently, dopamine hydrochloride (1 mmol, 191.4 mg) was added, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 24 h at 40°C while maintaining an acidic pH. Then, purification was performed using a dialysis tube (molecular weight cut‐off, MWCO = 7000 Da) against acidified DI water (pH 5.5) for 2 days to prevent oxidation of the catechol groups, followed by dialysis in neutral DI water for an additional day for pH adjustment. The purified HA‐DOPA was freeze‐dried for 3 days. Structural confirmation and catechol conjugation efficiency were assessed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (DRX500, Bruker), where the degree of catechol substitution was quantified by comparing peak areas at 6.8–7.2 ppm and 1.8–2.2 ppm.

For hydrogel formation, a 5 wt.% (w/v) HA‐DOPA solution was prepared by dissolving the lyophilized conjugates in DI water, and 100 µL aliquots were dispensed into petri dishes. Separately, a 120 mm Fe3+ solution was prepared by dissolving iron (III) chloride hexahydrate in 5% (v/v) aqueous acetic acid. To form HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogels, 20 µL of the Fe3+ solution was added to the HA‐DOPA solution and mixed for 30 s (Fe3+‐DOPA molar ratio = 1:3). For patch formation, HA‐DOPA solutions were dried at room temperature for 24 h. Photographs of HA‐DOPA solutions, dried HA‐DOPA patches, and HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogels were acquired using a CCD camera.

Ex Vivo and In Vivo Adhesive Properties of HA‐DOPA/Fe Hydrogels

The adhesive strength of HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogels was evaluated using ex vivo porcine skin and in vivo mouse skin models. For ex vivo analysis, porcine skin was sectioned into 1 × 2 cm2 samples, with a 1 × 1 cm2 central region designated for bonding. A universal testing machine (Instron 3340, Instron Co., Norwood, MA) equipped with a 10 N load cell was used to assess tensile strength at a constant extension rate of 5 mm/min. A volume of 100 µL of each formulation, including PBS (control), HA‐DOPA patch, HA‐DOPA/Fe patch, and HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogel, was applied to the bonding region. A second piece of porcine skin was placed on top to form a two‐layered construct. The assembled samples were incubated in a humidified environment at room temperature for 30 min to allow for adhesion. During testing, the ends of each specimen were clamped with forceps and pulled in opposite directions to determine tensile performance. Maximum tensile strength and failure points were derived from stress–strain curves (n = 4). The tensile curves were further analyzed to calculate additional interfacial parameters. The extension at maximum stress was defined as the displacement corresponding to the maximum tensile strength. The apparent work of detachment was calculated by integrating the tensile strength‐extension curve after baseline correction of the initial stress value (Equation 1). (1) W=∫σδdδ σ and δ represent tensile strength and extension, respectively. The integrated value was directly converted to tensile‐mode interfacial work per unit area. The post‐peak softening slope was obtained by linear fitting of the descending branch of the curve after the maximum stress point. In vivo adhesion was visually confirmed by applying HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogels onto the mouse dorsal skin. Photoimages were captured using a CCD camera both before and after detachment of the hydrogel patches.

Preparation and Characterization of Ecoflex/RSLP Patches

Ecoflex/RSLP patches were prepared by embedding synthesized luminescent particles into Ecoflex 00–45. Components A and B of Ecoflex were mixed thoroughly according to the manufacturer's instructions. A 0.4 mL aliquot of the mixture was poured into a 3.5 cm diameter petri dish and allowed to cure at room temperature for 4 h, forming the base layer. For the top layer, RSLPs were incorporated into the Ecoflex mixture at concentrations ranging from 0 to 1200 mg/mL. A total volume from 1.0 to 1.6 mL of the mixture was poured into the pre‐prepared patch dish to create double‐layered patches. The bilayer patches were additionally cured for 4 h at room temperature.

Cross‐sectional morphology and elemental composition of Ecoflex/RSLP patches were evaluated via FE‐SEM equipped with EDS. The photoimages of the patches were taken using a CCD camera. Mechanical properties were assessed using an Instron 3340 system (10 N load cell, 5 mm/min), with specimens stretched bi‐directionally while recording peak tensile force and failure points from the stress–strain curves. PL and AL spectra of Ecoflex/RSLP patches were acquired using a Horiba FluoroLog fluorometer. PL emission was recorded across a wavelength range of 550–700 nm with excitation wavelengths of 300–600 nm. For AL analysis, the patches were charged under a white LED light for 5 s after deactivating the excitation source, and emissions were measured in the 580–700 nm range. For PL imaging, the front side of the patches was irradiated with a UV flashlight (365 nm, 6 W, distance: 3 cm) or a room light‐matched white LED, and the back side was imaged using a CCD camera. Afterglow images of both sides were captured post‐irradiation. Time‐resolved afterglow images were taken every 1 min. Relative afterglow intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (n = 4). Temperature changes of Ecoflex/RSLP patches on porcine skin were monitored every 30 s using a thermal imaging camera (A325SC, FLIR) under UV or room light‐matched white LED illumination.

The degradation behavior of Ecoflex/RSLP patches (1 × 1 cm2) was monitored for 24 h in 5 mL of PBS at 37°C. At predetermined time points, 200 µL of the PBS solution was sampled and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS to maintain constant conditions. The optical density at 450 nm was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer (EMax Endpoint Reader, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA) (n = 4). To quantify RSLP release during degradation, the PL intensity of collected samples was measured using a microplate fluorometer (Fluoroskan Ascent FL, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) (n = 4), with excitation and emission wavelengths at 584 and 650 nm, respectively. Pre‐calibrated values for optical density and PL intensity of RSLP were used to determine the degradation rate.

Stretchability and Light Emission Under Strain

Ecoflex/RSLP patches were stretched from 0% to 100% using a customized stretcher (n = 4). Photoimages of the patches were acquired every 10% strain increment using a CCD camera. After charging the front side of the patches with UV or room light‐matched white LED illumination for 5 s, PL and AL images of the back side were captured. To evaluate durability, the patches were subjected to five stretch‐release cycles over 0% to 50% or 0% to 100%. After exposure to UV or room light‐matched white LED illumination, back‐side PL or AL images were taken, and the relative intensity was measured using a power meter coupled to a power sensor (n = 4). To further evaluate deformation, the patches were subjected to repeated 180° folding/unfolding cycles, and the power density was measured after each cycle (n = 4).

Preparation and Characterization of Ecoflex/RSLP Patches With HA‐DOPA/Fe Adhesives

Ecoflex/RSLP patches were prepared by pasting HA‐DOPA/Fe adhesive hydrogels along the edges of the patches. HA‐DOPA/Fe adhesives were dissolved in DI water, and the absorbance spectrum was recorded to evaluate whether the adhesives affected the optical and thermal performance of the patches. Thermal images of the HA‐DOPA/Fe‐integrated Ecoflex/RSLP patches were acquired before and after UV irradiation using a thermal imaging camera. Temperature changes were assessed from regions corresponding to the central optical window and the HA‐DOPA/Fe adhesive region. To assess whether HA‐DOPA/Fe integration affected the recharging process and long‐term luminescent function, AL images of the integrated patches were acquired at 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h. At each time point, patches were briefly charged with UV light, and AL images were recorded immediately after light cessation using identical camera settings. These photonic patches were applied to ex vivo porcine tissue and in vivo mouse skin for 30 min to evaluate their adhesive properties. After patch application, the patches were gently removed to confirm clean detachment without residue from either the Ecoflex/RSLP patch or the HA‐DOPA/Fe hydrogel.

Collagen Fibrillogenesis of Photonic Patches

Collagen fibrillogenesis was evaluated at 37°C using a temperature‐controlled microplate spectrophotometer (EMax End point ELISA reader, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA). Type I collagen, extracted from rat tail tendons, was dissolved in 20 mm acetic acid at a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL and subsequently diluted in 0.2 m HEPES buffer and 10× PBS (pH 7.4) at an 8:1:1 volume ratio. The resulting solution was neutralized on ice. Then, 200 µL of the collagen solution was mixed with 200 µL of either PBS or Ce6 solution (100 µm), and 200 µL of the final mixture was transferred to each well of a 96‐well plate. Samples were divided into four groups: (i) PBS with room light (control), (ii) photonic patches, (iii) Ce6 solution with room light, and (iv) photonic patches with Ce6 and room light (n = 4). In groups (ii) and (iv), photonic patches were adhered to the bottom of each well. Samples were irradiated from below with room light‐matched white LED for 10 min. To assess the contribution of AL during transient light interruption, samples were also subjected to repeated on/off fractional irradiation [31, 54]. During the on period, samples were exposed to room light‐matched white LED illumination, whereas during the off period, the external LED was turned off and only residual AL from the Ecoflex/RSLP patch was available for Ce6 activation. Collagen fibrillogenesis was monitored by measuring absorbance at 405 nm at 1‐min intervals over time. To minimize the contribution of intrinsic Ce6 absorbance at 405 nm, all collagen fibrillogenesis curves were baseline‐corrected by subtracting the absorbance at 0 min from each time point.

ROS Generation of Photonic Patches

ROS generation during patch‐mediated Ce6 photoactivation was measured using Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG). Samples were divided into Control, Patch, Ce6, and Ce6+Patch groups. Ce6 was used at the same concentration as in the collagen fibrillogenesis assay. In the Patch‐containing groups, Ecoflex/RSLP patches were placed beneath the sample wells, and all samples were irradiated from below using room light‐matched white LED under the same geometry used for collagen fibrillogenesis. SOSG fluorescence was recorded at predetermined time points during irradiation using a microplate reader. The fluorescence intensity was baseline‐corrected to the initial value at 0 min and plotted as time‐dependent ROS generation.

Ex Vivo Photochemical Tissue Bonding Effect of Photonic Patches

The ex vivo tensile strength of bonded mouse skin was evaluated using a universal testing machine (Instron 3340, Instron Co., Norwood, MA) equipped with a 10 N load cell and operated at a constant extension rate of 5 mm/min. Mouse skin was cut into 1 × 2 cm2 sections, with a defined bonding area of 1 × 1 cm2. To each bonding site, 10 µL of PBS (control) or a Ce6 solution (1 mm) was applied, and a second skin piece was placed on top to form a bilayer construct. Then, photonic patches were adhered to the assembled specimens and illuminated with room light‐matched white LED for 2 h. After irradiation, the samples were incubated under humid conditions at room temperature for 30 min to facilitate bonding. Tensile testing was performed by securing both ends of each specimen with forceps and applying bi‐directional tension. The maximum tensile strength and point of failure were determined from the resulting stress–strain curves (n = 4).

In Vitro Biocompatibility of Photonic Patches

NIH3T3 fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 vol% FBS and 1 vol% penicillin‐streptomycin. Cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. To evaluate the cytotoxicity of degradation products, photonic patches (diameter, 3.5 cm) were pre‐incubated in 10 mL of DMEM for 24 h. Subsequently, 200 µL of the extract was added to each well (n = 8). In parallel, Ce6 solutions at varying concentrations (0–50 µg/mL, 200 µL) were also added to separate wells and incubated under identical conditions for 24 h (n = 8). Then, the media in all wells were replaced with fresh media containing 10 vol% CCK‐8 reagent, followed by a further 2 h of incubation. Cell viability was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

In Vitro Cell Migration and Proliferation Effects of Photonic Patches

Cell migration was assessed via scratch healing assays. NIH3T3 fibroblasts were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well in 24‐well plates and cultured at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator until confluence. For scratch creation, strips of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, 10 mm × 1 mm) were placed in the wells prior to cell seeding. After 24 h, the PDMS strips were removed to produce uniform scratches. Photonic patches were then attached to the bottom of the plate, and cells were exposed to room light‐matched white LED illumination for 12 h in the incubator. Cell migration into the scratch area was monitored by capturing optical microscope images at 0, 12, and 24 h.

Cell proliferation was visualized by confocal microscopy. NIH3T3 cells were seeded into confocal imaging plates at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells per well and cultured under standard conditions. A subset of the samples was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with DAPI prior to treatment as a control reference. Photonic patches were attached to the bottom of the plates and irradiated with room light‐matched white LED for 12 h (n = 4). After an additional 12 h of incubation, cells were rinsed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were obtained using a confocal microscope (TCS SP5, Leica), and the extent of proliferation was quantified using ImageJ software. Quantitative cell proliferation was further evaluated via the CCK‐8 assay. NIH3T3 cells (1.0 × 104 cells per well) were seeded into 96‐well plates and cultured at 37°C under 5% CO2. Photonic patches were attached to the bottom of the plates and irradiated with room light‐matched white LED illumination for 12 h on day 0 and day 1 (n = 4). At predetermined time points (0, 24, 72, and 120 h), the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10 vol% CCK‐8 reagent and incubated for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to determine cell viability.

In Vivo Wound Healing Effect of Photonic Patches

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Pohang University of Science and Technology and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Approval No.: POSTECH‐2024‐0056). To evaluate the effects of photonic patches on PTB and PBM, an in vivo incision wound model was employed. Six‐week‐old female BALB/c nude mice were anesthetized, and a 1 cm‐long dorsal incision was made using a surgical blade. The mice were divided into the following treatment groups: (i) PBS (20 µL, control), (ii) Ce6 (500 µm, 20 µL), (iii) Ecoflex, (iv) Sutures, (v) photonic patches, (vi) photonic patches with Ce6. Ce6 solution was topically applied to the wound, followed by coverage with the photonic patch. To prevent patch detachment by the mice, a transparent adhesive film (Tegaderm 1626W, 3M, St. Paul, MN) was applied around the patch margins. All groups were irradiated with room light‐matched white LED for 12 h on day 0. Wound sites were monitored for 7 days post‐treatment, and images were acquired at designated time points using a CCD camera. On day 1, mice treated with sutures or photonic patches containing Ce6 were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation, and 1 × 2 cm2 tissue samples were harvested (n = 4). After 30 min of humid incubation, the mechanical strength of tissue adhesion was assessed using an Instron machine (10 N load cell, 5 mm/min extension rate). Tissues were pulled bidirectionally until complete separation, and the peak tensile strength was recorded.

The in vivo excisional wound healing effect of photonic patches was assessed using an 8 mm circular wound model. After anesthesia, wounds were created on the dorsal flanks of BALB/c nude mice using a biopsy punch (Kasco, Sialkot, Pakistan). The mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 4 per group): (i) PBS (20 µL, control), (ii) Ce6 (500 µm, 20 µL), (iii) Ecoflex, (iv) photonic patches, (v) photonic patches with Ce6. Ce6 was applied dropwise to the wound area, followed by patch application. Tegaderm was used to secure the patches. All groups received 12 h of room light‐matched white LED illumination on days 0 and 2. Wound closure was tracked over 10 days, and digital images were captured at predefined intervals using a CCD camera. Wound areas were quantified using ImageJ software. Interval wound closure was calculated from the normalized wound‐area data to compare the healing dynamics of the Patch and Ce6+Patch groups in the circular wound model. For each interval, the wound‐area reduction was calculated as Equation (5): (5) ΔAt1−t2=At1−At2 where At1 and At2 are the normalized wound areas at the beginning and end of each interval, respectively.

Histological and Immunohistochemical Analysis

Tissue specimens (1 × 1 cm2) were collected after 7 and 10 days from the incision and excision wound models, respectively, following euthanasia via CO2. The samples were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for histological analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to assess wound closure, while Masson's trichrome (MT) staining was performed to evaluate collagen deposition. Stained sections were visualized using an optical microscope.

For immunofluorescence staining, tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval, permeabilization, and blocking steps were then performed. Sections were incubated overnight at room temperature with primary antibodies: CD31 (dilution ratio = 1:200), α‐SMA (dilution ratio = 1:200), TNF‐α (dilution ratio = 1:200), and K‐14 (dilution ratio = 1:400). After rinsing, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies, Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti‐rabbit IgG H&L (dilution ratio = 1:400), and Alexa Fluor 546 goat anti‐rat IgG H&L (dilution ratio = 1:400) for 4 h at room temperature. Finally, the sections were mounted with VECTASHIELD anti‐fade mounting medium containing DAPI and stored at room temperature for 24 h before imaging. Fluorescence signals were visualized using a confocal microscope (TCS SP5, Leica) to investigate the localization and differentiation of immune‐related markers at the wound site.

Quantification of Cytokine Expression

On days 2 and 4 following incisional and excisional wounding, mice were euthanized via CO2, and tissue specimens were collected for cytokine analysis (n = 4). Skin tissues were immersed in a cell lysis buffer, minced into small pieces, and mechanically homogenized. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 20 min to remove debris, and the resulting supernatants were collected for analysis. Cytokine levels of IL‐1β and TGF‐β in the wound lysates were quantified using ELISA kits, following the manufacturer's protocols.

Experiments on Human Subjects

A human pilot study was conducted in healthy adult volunteers (n = 3; 25–30 years) to assess acute effects of a wearable photonic patch via PBM on normal facial skin. All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. The photoimages of attaching the patches were taken using a CCD camera with and without room light. The protocol was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Pohang University of Science and Technology and determined to be exempt from ethics committee review (Approval No. PIRB‐2025‐E043). Although the trial was exempt, all participants were informed about the procedures and potential risks, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to enrolment. This pilot study was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS; KCT0012151), a WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) Primary Registry.

For each participant, one cheek was assigned to PBM treatment, and the contralateral cheek served as an untreated control. At each measurement time point, both cheeks were gently cleansed with a non‐alcoholic wipe three times and acclimated for 10 min at room temperature before data acquisition. On day 0, mechanical and optical measurements were obtained as a baseline from both cheeks. A wearable photonic patch (diameter, 3.5 cm) was then applied to the central malar region of the treatment side and worn for 9 h under room light. Participants were instructed to avoid skincare products or makeup and to minimize vigorous facial movements during exposure. The control cheek remained uncovered. Immediately after patch removal, the cleansing and acclimation procedure was repeated, and mechanical and optical measurements were acquired from both cheeks on day 1.

Mechanical Assessment in Clinical Trials

Mechanical properties were assessed at the cheek using a dermal torque meter (DTM310, Diastron Ltd., UK) equipped with a circular probe that laterally displaced the skin by a controlled rotational stimulus. The probe was gently placed on the malar region and subjected to a constant torque for 10 s, followed by complete unloading for an additional 10 s. The instrument continuously recorded the angular displacement θ(t) three times to decrease the impact of pre‐loaded angular displacement at the initial measurement.

Three parameters were extracted from θ(t): θinitial, θmax, and θresidue. The initial displacement θinitial was defined as the mean of θ(t) over the first 0.1 s after load onset, the maximal displacement θmax was defined as the single maximum value of θ(t) during the 10 s loading period, and the residual displacement θresidue was defined as the displacement at the end of the unloading period. The creep proportion index was defined as the fraction of the total deformation attributable to time‐dependent creep, and the plastic proportion index represented the fraction of irreversible deformation remaining relative to the total deformation, following the equation (6): (6) CPI=θmax−θinitial/θmax,PPI=θresidue/θmax

Each curve was peak‐normalized by dividing θ(t) by the corresponding θmax to decompose the overall deformation into instantaneous elastic, time‐dependent creep, and residual plastic components.

To quantify absolute deformability and recovery kinetics, curves were baseline‐subtracted as Δθ(t) = θ(t) − θinitial, and the net maximal deformation was defined as Δθmax = θmax − θinitial. Recovery after unloading (from the unloading onset t = tu to the end of acquisition) was fitted with a single‐exponential function, following the equation (7): (7) Δθt=Δθresidue+Δθtu−Δθresidue×exp−t−tu/τrecoveryt≥tu to extract the recovery time constant τrecovery. Three repeated measurements were acquired per site, removed the first measurement, and averaged to obtain representative creep proportion index, plastic proportion index, Δθmax, and τrecovery values for each cheek and time point.

Optical Skin Imaging in Clinical Trials

Changes in skin appearance were quantified using a multimodal skin analysis system (DermaVision Pro, PSI Co., Korea). For each cheek and time point, images were acquired sequentially under parallel‐polarized imaging (PPI), cross‐polarized imaging (CPI), and UV illumination. Head position and camera‐to‐face distance were stabilized using a chin rest and forehead support to ensure reproducible imaging geometry.

A square region of interest (ROI) corresponding to the patch‐treated area (or the anatomically matched location on the contralateral control cheek) was defined on the color image and automatically transferred to the PPI, CPI, and UV images. From the ROI, the following indices were extracted using the built‐in analysis software. S‐gray represented overall skin brightness and tone uniformity, pore and wrinkle indices reflected surface roughness and microrelief, and melanin and erythema indices reflected pigmentary and vascular components, respectively. To enable within‐subject comparisons while accounting for baseline asymmetry between cheeks, each index was normalized to the corresponding baseline value measured on the same cheek at day 0. Each participant served as their own control.

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. One‐tailed statistical analysis was performed using Student's t‐tests. The values for * p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; **** p < 0.0001 expressed statistical significance. For the human pilot study, Statistical analysis and normalization were performed using the SigmaPlot 10.0 software (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA) and Origin software (Microcal Software Inc., Northampton, MA).
