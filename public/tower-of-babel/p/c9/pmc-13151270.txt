Rare taxa enhance microbial network complexity and drive nitrification and denitrification processes in river ecosystems
Authors: Xiaoliang Jiang, Xiang Xiong, Miaomiao Cai, Gang He, Quanfa Zhang, Wenzhi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicrobial communities, which drive most ecological processes and functions, contain not only a few highly abundant species but also a large number of rare species. However, little is known about the relative importance of abundant and rare microorganisms to network complexity and biogeochemical cycling, especially at large spatial scales. Here, we investigated nitrifying and denitrifying communities using high-throughput sequencing of archaeal amoA and bacterial amoA, nirK, and nirS genes, and we determined nitrification and denitrification rates in channel sediments, riparian rhizosphere soils, and riparian bulk soils of 30 rivers across China.ResultsRare nitrifiers and denitrifiers exhibited broader environmental thresholds than abundant taxa. Co-occurrence network analysis revealed that all topological features (e.g., node degree and eigenvector centrality) had higher values for rare taxa than for abundant taxa. In addition, major modules (i.e., clusters of closely interconnected microbes) in the network were mainly composed of rare taxa, indicating that rare taxa play a vital role in maintaining the network complexity of nitrifying and denitrifying communities. Random forest analysis showed that nitrification rates were significantly associated only with the β-diversity of rare bacterial nitrifiers. Denitrification rates were largely influenced by the β-diversity of both rare and abundant nirK-type denitrifiers. These results suggested that rare microbial taxa play an important but previously underestimated role in biogeochemical processes.ConclusionsOverall, this study highlights that, compared with abundant taxa, rare taxa contribute more to microbial network complexity and nitrogen cycling processes in river ecosystems at the continental scale.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-026-00879-7.Keywords: Denitrification, Diversity, Keystone species, Nitrification, Wetlands

Background

Microbial communities are typically composed of not only a low proportion of abundant taxa but also a high number of low-abundance or rare taxa [1]. Abundant and rare microbial taxa often differ in physiological and ecological characteristics (e.g., growth rate, predation resistance, and metabolic potential) and exhibit distinct biogeographical distribution patterns [2–5]. In recent years, a growing body of evidence suggests that, compared with abundant microorganisms, rare microorganisms respond differently to environmental change or perturbation [6–8]. For example, Wan et al. [9] reported that rare bacteria and fungi exhibited narrower environmental niche breadths or thresholds than abundant ones in wetland soils across the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, China.

A wide variety of microorganisms generally form highly complex ecological networks in natural ecosystems [10]. Associations among different microbes within a community have become a major topic in microbial ecology in recent years [11–13]. Co-occurrence network analysis is frequently applied to infer potential microbial associations shaped by environmental heterogeneity and resource availability, although it may not fully capture true species interactions [14]. Topological features of co-occurrence networks, such as node number, edge number, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality, can be indicative of microbial network complexity [15–17]. Additionally, co-occurrence networks allow us to identify the keystone species in a microbial community [14, 16]. Such networks have been used to explore microbial interactions in a wide range of ecosystems from aquatic habitats to farmlands, grasslands, and forests [4, 14, 18, 19]. However, the relative importance of abundant and rare microorganisms to network complexity remains poorly understood, particularly at large spatial scales.

Microorganisms play a profound role in regulating numerous ecological processes and functions [20, 21]. Nitrogen (N) cycling processes, such as nitrification and denitrification, which convert ammonium (NH4+) to nitrate (NO3−) and ultimately to gaseous N, contribute to N removal and nitrous oxide (N2O) emissions in aquatic ecosystems [22, 23]. Despite their low abundance in the environment, rare microorganisms have been shown to be key drivers of soil enzyme activities, crop yield, and ecosystem multifunctionality [13, 24, 25]. However, although the importance of rare taxa has been increasingly recognized in recent years [1], the degree to which rare microbial species contribute to N cycling processes has been less investigated.

In this study, we investigated nitrifying and denitrifying communities using high-throughput sequencing of archaeal amoA and bacterial amoA, nirK, and nirS genes, and we measured nitrification and denitrification rates in channel sediments, riparian rhizosphere soils, and riparian bulk soils from 30 rivers across China (Fig. 1). We hypothesized that rare nitrifiers and denitrifiers play a central role in maintaining microbial network complexity and regulating nitrification and denitrification processes because they represent broad microbial diversity and are often functionally redundant. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to (1) assess the environmental niche breadths of rare and abundant nitrifiers and denitrifiers across diverse environmental gradients, (2) identify the relative importance of rare and abundant nitrifiers and denitrifiers to microbial network complexity, and (3) determine the contributions of rare and abundant nitrifiers and denitrifiers to nitrification and denitrification processes at the continental scale.

Results

Distribution patterns of abundant and rare nitrifiers and denitrifiers

A total of 6,380,797 and 6,093,060 high-quality sequences were clustered into 795 and 763 OTUs for the archaeal amoA and bacterial amoA genes, and 5,297,189 and 4,383,945 sequences were clustered into 7347 and 10,968 OTUs for the nirK and nirS genes, respectively. Rare taxa comprised 77.6% and 69.3% of the OTUs for the archaeal amoA and bacterial amoA genes, respectively, but their total relative abundance accounted for only 1.0% and 1.1% of the archaeal amoA and bacterial amoA gene sequences. In contrast, a relatively low proportion of OTUs (8.4% for archaeal amoA and 12.2% for bacterial amoA) were classified as abundant taxa, yet these taxa accounted for 95.0% and 94.5% of the archaeal amoA and bacterial amoA sequences, respectively. Both abundant and rare archaeal amoA taxa were mainly assigned to Crenarchaeota, Thaumarchaeota, and unclassified taxa, whereas abundant and rare bacterial amoA taxa were primarily assigned to Nitrosomonas, Nitrosospira, Nitrosovibrio, and unclassified taxa (Fig. S3).

In terms of denitrifiers, 86.9% and 85.1% of OTUs for the nirK and nirS genes were identified as rare taxa, respectively, whereas only 2.2% and 1.4% of OTUs were classified as abundant taxa. Similarly, rare denitrifiers accounted for only 12.2% and 20.1% of the total nirK and nirS genes sequences, respectively. Abundant nirK taxa were mainly assigned to Paracoccus, Bradyrhizobium, and unclassified taxa, whereas rare nirK taxa were primarily assigned to Bradyrhizobium, Bosea, and unclassified taxa. For nirS, abundant taxa were mainly assigned to Sulfurifustis, Pseudomonas, Rhodanobacter, Azoarcus, Bacillus, and unclassified taxa, whereas rare taxa were almost entirely assigned to unclassified taxa (Fig. S3).

Environmental responses of abundant and rare nitrifiers and denitrifiers

Environmental niche breadth was calculated to reflect the species niche breadth of rare and abundant taxa in response to environmental change. Interestingly, the rare nitrifiers generally showed a broader range of environmental thresholds than the abundant nitrifiers for most measured environmental and vegetation variables, except TFe for archaeal amoA and PC, TN, and TOC for bacterial amoA (Fig. 2A, B). Similarly, compared with abundant denitrifiers, the rare nirK- and nirS-type denitrifiers showed higher environmental thresholds for almost all tested variables, with the highest threshold values observed for SO42− (Fig. 2C, D).

Importance of abundant and rare nitrifiers and denitrifiers in network complexity

The archaeal amoA network consisted of 422 nodes (OTUs) linked by 1265 edges (Fig. 3A). It contained 14 nodes belonging to abundant taxa (3.32%), 14 nodes belonging to common taxa (3.32%), and 394 nodes belonging to rare taxa (93.36%). The inner connections (edges) of abundant, common, and rare taxa were 8, 3, and 1,227, respectively (Fig. 3A). Moreover, there were 6 edges between abundant and common taxa, 6 edges between abundant and rare taxa, and 15 edges linking common and rare taxa. The bacterial amoA network was composed of 2570 edges and 389 nodes which included 25 abundant OTUs (6.43%), 39 common OTUs (10.03%), and 325 rare OTUs (83.54%) (Fig. 3B).

The nirK network consisted of 3,639 nodes (OTUs) linked by 85,278 edges (Fig. 3C). A total of 40, 76, and 3,523 nodes belonged to abundant, common, and rare taxa, respectively. The inner edges of abundant and common taxa were only 21 and 34, respectively. In comparison, the inner connections of rare taxa were particularly strong, with 85,103 edges. The nirS network contained 1,444 nodes and 1,886 edges (Fig. 3D). There were 12, 33, and 1,399 nodes belonging to abundant, common, and rare taxa, respectively. The nirK co-occurrence network was more complex than the nirS network, as indicated by higher values of network parameters such as numbers of nodes and edges and average degree (Table S3).

The network topological features (degree, betweenness centrality, eigenvector centrality, and hubs) were calculated to assess the relative importance of abundant and rare nitrifiers and denitrifiers to network complexity (Table 1). In general, the rare nitrifiers/denitrifiers were found to have higher values of topological features than abundant nitrifiers/denitrifiers (Table 1). Several major ecological clusters (modules) were identified in the co-occurrence networks of nitrifiers and denitrifiers, and they were mainly composed of rare OTUs (Table 2). Within these modules, rare nitrifying bacteria were largely unclassified taxa, whereas rare denitrifying bacteria were primarily affiliated with Proteobacteria (Fig. S4).

Contributions of rare and abundant nitrifiers and denitrifiers to N cycling processes

The PNR was significantly associated only with the β-diversity (MDS1) of rare bacterial nitrifiers (Table 3). However, the PDR was significantly influenced by the α- and β-diversity indices of both rare and abundant denitrifiers (Table 3). Random forest analysis showed that the MDS1 of rare bacterial nitrifiers explained only 8.2% of the variance in PNR (Fig. 4A). In comparison, the β-diversity (MDS1 and MDS2) of rare nirK- and nirS-type denitrifiers accounted for up to 33.5% of the PDR variance, while the β-diversity of abundant taxa (nirK-type denitrifiers) explained only 8.8% of the total variance (Fig. 4B). The α-diversity indices did not contribute significantly to the prediction of either PNR or PDR.

Discussion

Importance of rare taxa in shaping microbial ecological networks

It is well known that different microbial species within an ecosystem are interconnected through the exchange of materials, energy, and information, resulting in complex interactions such as predation, competition, mutualism, and parasitism [17, 44]. Molecular ecological networks, with species (OTUs) as nodes and interactions among species as edges, are widely used to simplify vast and complex ecological relationships and to reveal species interactions and ecosystem dynamics [45]. The complexity of microbial ecological networks can be characterized by various network topological indices, including average degree, network size, connectivity, average clustering coefficient, relative modularity, and average path length [11]. In general, greater ecological network complexity is positively correlated with network stability. In a long-term warming experiment in Oklahoma, USA, Yuan et al. [17] found that the complexity of microbial networks in grassland soils enhanced network stability, which enabled the microbial community to perform ecological functions normally [46].

In this study, the bacterial amoA and nirK ecological networks possessed more nodes and edges and had higher average degree than the archaeal amoA and nirS networks, respectively (Table S3), indicating greater network complexity in the bacterial amoA and nirK communities. Connectance, defined as the proportion of realized links relative to all possible links, was found to be higher in bacterial amoA and nirK networks than in archaeal amoA and nirS networks (Table S3). This result is not surprising and is in line with those of Yuan et al. [17], who reported that connectance increased significantly with network complexity. The higher complexity of the bacterial amoA and nirK networks in this study was likely because different types of nitrifiers and denitrifiers had distinct physiological and metabolic characteristics, including their adaptation to pH, moisture, temperature, organic matter, and nutrient availability (Fig. S2).

We observed that in comparison to abundant nitrifiers/denitrifiers, rare nitrifiers/denitrifiers had more nodes and edges and higher values for other network topological features (e.g., betweenness centrality) (Fig. 3 and Table 1), suggesting that rare taxa had more complex interactions than abundant ones [19]. Furthermore, we found that major modules (i.e., clusters of closely interconnected microbes) in the network were mainly composed of rare taxa (Table 2). However, the rare taxa of nitrifiers in the major modules were predominantly unclassified taxa, highlighting current limitations in our understanding of nitrifier diversity and suggesting that these unclassified rare taxa may play important yet undiscovered roles in nitrification. In contrast, nirK and nirS genes occur mainly in Proteobacteria [47, 48], which likely explains why rare denitrifiers within the co-occurrence network modules were largely composed of Proteobacteria. Hernandez et al. [49] proposed that modularity can stabilize microbial communities by limiting the negative impact of losing a species to its own module, thus preventing the effects of extinction of that taxon from propagating to affect the remaining species in the network. These results suggest that rare rather than abundant taxa play a central role in maintaining the complexity of nitrifying and denitrifying networks. In contrast to our results, Jiao et al. [50] reported that abundant bacterial taxa were more often located in central positions within a network than rare ones in oil-contaminated soils. This might be because the soils under study were in an unstable state, and some rare taxa were activated and enriched to degrade oil pollution and became abundant species. However, Xiong et al. [13] showed that rare taxa play a central role in the fungal co-occurrence network and contribute greatly to the stability of the fungal community in agricultural soils. Similarly, Shu et al. [25] reported that even during long-term succession, rare rather than abundant bacteria also dominated the complexity and stability of microbial ecological networks on the Loess Plateau of China. Our findings, together with these observations, highlight the importance of rare taxa in sustaining the complexity and stability of microbial ecological networks. One potential explanation is that rare microorganisms represent broad diversity and are often functionally redundant [1, 51].

Rare taxa play a critical role in regulating N cycling rates

Our study showed that the diversity indices of bacterial amoA and nirK contributed more to PNR and PDR compared with those of archaeal amoA and nirS, respectively (Fig. 4). This result is not surprising and is consistent with the results of some previous reports. For instance, Zhang et al. [52] found that active bacterial amoA outnumbered archaeal amoA and dominated the nitrification activity in acidic paddy soils by using a DNA-stable isotope probing experiment. Kou et al. [53] reported that the β-diversity of nirK-type denitrifiers was more important than that of nirS-type denitrifiers in explaining the soil denitrification rates along an elevation gradient on Mount Gongga, China. Our results can be partly ascribed to the relatively complex and stable ecological networks of bacterial amoA and nirK (Table S3). Another possible reason is that different types of nitrifiers/denitrifiers have different sensitivities to soil chemical and physical properties such as pH, texture, and redox conditions [53–55].

We further found that the diversity of rare nitrifiers/denitrifiers contributed more to soil nitrification and denitrification rates than that of abundant ones (Fig. 4). Most of our knowledge of the biodiversity–ecosystem functioning (BEF) relationship is still based on the entire community, and little attention has been given to the relative functional importance of abundant and rare microorganisms. Recent studies have shown that rare microorganisms play an over-proportional role in regulating biogeochemical processes and may be a hidden driver of ecosystem functioning [1, 50, 51]. For instance, Zhang et al. [19] demonstrated that the diversity of rare species, rather than of abundant species, contributed the most to explaining the variation in ecosystem multifunctionality of agricultural fields. There are several possible explanations for these results. First, rare taxa represent a large functional gene pool and may be functionally dissimilar from abundant ones [1]. Accordingly, a high diversity of rare microbes is likely to support ecological function by offering either unique or complementary metabolic pathways. Second, compared with abundant taxa, rare taxa are more likely to contain metabolically active microorganisms that can serve as keystone species in sustaining ecosystem functioning [4]. Third, the presence of rare microorganisms can induce metabolic responses in abundant microorganisms and therefore indirectly enhance the functionality of abundant microorganisms [1]. Finally, compared with abundant taxa, rare taxa were more tolerant to environmental changes, as shown by broader environmental thresholds (Fig. 2). Strong environmental adaptability can ensure that rare microorganisms perform their functions normally.

The threshold for defining rare OTUs is influenced by several factors, including experimental design, sequencing depth, and the specific data analysis pipeline used. For example, in datasets with shallow sequencing depth, applying a 0.01% cutoff may inadvertently exclude low-abundance taxa that are potentially ecologically important. Moreover, different OTU clustering strategies, such as similarity-based clustering versus amplicon sequence variant (ASV) inference, treat rare sequences differently, affecting the identification and interpretation of rare OTUs. Therefore, adopting a more inclusive definition of microbial diversity is crucial to ensure that conservation strategies encompass rare taxa, thereby supporting ecosystem resilience, particularly in relation to N cycling. Furthermore, a deeper understanding of the functional roles of rare taxa can support more targeted approaches to managing key nutrient cycling processes, such as nitrification and denitrification. This, in turn, can promote more efficient N use by plants and contribute to improved water quality in river ecosystems.

Conclusions

Our results showed that, at the continental scale, microbial ecological networks associated with bacterial amoA and nirK were more complex than those associated with archaeal amoA and nirS in river ecosystems, respectively. In addition, rare taxa, rather than abundant taxa, played a central role in maintaining the network complexity of nitrifying and denitrifying communities. Soil nitrification rates were significantly related only to the β-diversity of rare bacterial nitrifiers, and soil denitrification rates were largely influenced by the β-diversity of both rare and abundant nirK-type denitrifiers. Collectively, our findings provide new insights into our understanding of the ecological importance of rare microorganisms and highlight the critical but previously underestimated role of rare taxa in microbial network complexity and N cycling processes. Future research should focus on using synthetic microbial communities to further understand the functional importance of rare microbes and the ecological consequences of rare species loss.

Methods

Site description and sample collection

Thirty higher-order rivers across eastern China, including the Yangtze, Yellow, Huaihe, and Songhuajiang Rivers, were selected from south to north for soil and sediment sampling in September 2018 (Fig. 1 and Table S1). For each river, a representative sampling site was established nonrandomly in the middle and lower reaches, where riparian wetlands were well developed and generally formed relatively wide bands. At each site, channel sediments (0–20 cm), riparian rhizosphere soils (0–20 cm), and riparian bulk soils (0–20 and 40–60 cm) were collected. Within the main channel of each river site, approximately 200 g of surface sediment was sampled randomly from three subsites using a homemade sampler and mixed thoroughly to form a composite sediment sample. Riparian rhizosphere soils of dominant plant species were collected according to Fan et al. [15]. Briefly, a 1 × 1 m vegetation plot was established in the representative plant community in the riparian wetlands at each site, and the most dominant plant species within this plot was excavated at a depth of 20 cm using a spade. After shaking the soils loosely attached to the plant roots, we collected the remaining soils tightly adhering to the roots (i.e., rhizosphere soils) using a sanitized soft brush. Surface (0–20 cm) and deep (40–60 cm) bulk soils were sampled from a bare area in the riparian wetlands using a soil drilling machine. Each soil or sediment sample was separated into two parts. One part was kept in a portable refrigerator at 5 °C to measure nitrification, denitrification, and physicochemical properties, and the other part was immediately stored in liquid N2 for microbial analysis.

Measurements of nitrifying and denitrifying communities

We extracted soil and sediment DNA from replicate subsamples using the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (MoBio Laboratories Inc., CA, USA). DNA concentrations were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Four functional genes (archaeal amoA and bacterial amoA, nirK and nirS) coding for key enzymes in the nitrification and denitrification processes were amplified using universal primers. The primer sequences and PCR conditions are listed in Table S2.

The PCR products were purified and paired-end sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 sequencer (Illumina, CA, USA) according to the standard protocols by Beijing BioMarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Sequencing data were deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive database (accession numbers: PRJNA749332, PRJNA749382, PRJNA749386, and PRJNA749390 for archaeal amoA, bacterial amoA, nirK, and nirS, respectively). The raw sequences were merged using FLASH and subsequently quality-filtered using QIIME version 1.17. Briefly, the raw sequence data were first demultiplexed, and primers were removed using the cutadapt [26]. Paired-end reads were then assembled with FLASH v1.2.7 with a minimum overlap length of 10 bp [27]. Low-quality sequences were removed based on the following criteria: chimera sequences were excluded, and sequences with a length shorter than 150 bp or with an average quality score below 30 were discarded [28]. The sequences with more than 95% identity were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using the Mothur software by the furthest neighbor algorithm. The 95% cutoff was chosen because it minimizes the likelihood of artifactual OTU assignments resulting from sequencing errors [29]. OTUs were generated instead of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) to provide a conservative unit for community-level ecological analyses. Because OTU clustering reduces over-partitioning caused by intragenomic variation in 16S rRNA genes, it was considered appropriate for analyses of microbial community structure and its functional associations [30, 31]. This workflow also facilitated direct comparison with our previously published river microbiome studies [32, 33]. The α-diversity indices (OTU richness, Chao1, and Shannon) of each sample were calculated with the package ‘vegan’ in R. To estimate β-diversity, non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis was performed based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities between samples, and MDS1 (primary axis) and MDS2 (secondary axis) were considered β-diversity indices (Fig. S1). OTUs with a relative abundance above 0.1% of the total sequences were designated “abundant”, those with a relative abundance below 0.01% were classified as “rare”, and the remaining OTUs (0.01–0.1%) were defined as “common” [19, 34].

Determination of nitrification and denitrification rates

The potential nitrification rate (PNR) of each soil or sediment sample was assessed using the shaken soil slurry method [35–37]. Briefly, approximately 10 g of fresh soils or sediments was mixed with 100 mL of phosphate buffer (1 mM, pH 7.4) and 0.5 mL of (NH4)2SO4 (0.25 M) in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask. All flasks were then incubated on a horizontal shaker (180 rpm) at room temperature for 24 h. At 1, 4, 10, 16, and 24 h after the start of the incubation, approximately 5 mL samples were collected from the slurry, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min and filtered through 0.45 μm membrane filters. The NO3– concentrations of the filtrates were measured using an automatic nutrient analyzer (EasyChem plus, Systea, Italy). The PNR was calculated from the linear increase in NO3– concentration over the incubation period.

The potential denitrification rate (PDR) of each soil or sediment sample was determined by the acetylene (C2H2) inhibition technique [38–40]. Specifically, approximately 20 g of fresh soils or sediments was mixed with a 20 mL incubation solution consisting of 0.1 g L−1 KNO3, 0.18 g L−1 glucose and 1 g L−1 chloramphenicol in a 250 mL brown bottle. All bottles were then sealed, purged with 99.999% N2 for 2 min, injected with 25 mL of 99.999% C2H2 and incubated in the dark for 4 h at room temperature. Then, 5 mL of headspace gas samples was collected from each bottle after shaking vigorously using a syringe at both the start and end of incubation. The N2O concentrations of the gas samples were detected using a gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890, CA, USA). The PDR was calculated from the difference between the initial and final N2O concentrations [38].

Measurements of vegetation structure and environmental factors

In the field, the latitude, longitude and altitude of each sampling site were determined with a global positioning system (Unistrong, Beijing, China). For each 1 × 1 m plot in the riparian zones, the plant cover (PC) was visually evaluated in the field by a 1 × 1 m sampling frame that was subdivided into 10 × 10 cm grid cells. All plants (including roots and shoots) in each plot were then harvested to determine the fresh plant biomass (PB). Plant richness (PR) was defined as the number of vascular plant species recorded in each plot.

The climate parameters, including mean annual temperature (MAT) and mean annual precipitation (MAP), were extracted from the China Meteorological Data Sharing Service System (http://data.cma.cn/). Soil or sediment physicochemical properties, including pH, electrical conductivity (EC), moisture, texture (i.e., the percentage of sand, silt, and clay), total carbon (TC), total organic carbon (TOC), NH4+, NO3–, total N (TN), total phosphorus (TP), total iron (TFe), ferrous iron (Fe2+), available iron (AFe) and sulfate (SO42−) were determined as described in detail in our previous studies [41, 42].

Statistical analyses

To measure the threshold values of rare and abundant nitrifiers and denitrifiers in response to environmental factors and vegetation structure (i.e., environmental niche breadth), we conducted threshold indicator taxa analysis (TITAN) using the ‘TITAN2’ package in R [34]. More details about this method can be found in our previous papers [9].

To examine the network complexity of nitrifying and denitrifying communities, co-occurrence networks based on the Spearman's correlation between OTUs were constructed using the package ‘WGCNA’ in R and visualized in Gephi version 0.9.2 software [15]. Only robust correlations with Spearman’s correlation coefficients > 0.7 and false discovery rate (FDR)‐adjusted P values < 0.01 were kept in the final co-occurrence networks [15, 16]. The node and edge numbers of the co-occurrence networks and topological features, including degree, betweenness centrality, eigenvector centrality, and hubs, were calculated to assess the complexity of the microbial network [14]. In addition, the modules (i.e., ecological clusters) of networks were identified by modular analysis in Gephi version 0.9.2 software, and the relative abundance of each module was calculated by averaging the standardized relative abundance (z-score) of the taxa that belong to each module [13, 43].

To explore the contributions of rare and abundant nitrifiers and denitrifiers to nitrification and denitrification processes, Spearman correlation and random forest analyses were performed. Furthermore, to evaluate the direct and indirect effects of the diversity of rare and abundant nitrifiers and denitrifiers on potential nitrification and denitrification rates, partial least squares-path modeling (PLS-PM) was performed using the package ‘plspm’ in R.
