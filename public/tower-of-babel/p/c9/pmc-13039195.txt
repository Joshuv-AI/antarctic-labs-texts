High-fidelity numerical framework for crashworthiness evaluation of passenger car body structures under full-frontal impact
Authors: Baskar Ponnusamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Frontal collisions are among the most severe crash modes, requiring robust front-end design to ensure occupant safety. High-fidelity finite element (FE) simulations play a crucial role in evaluating energy absorption, intrusion patterns, and crash pulse fidelity during early vehicle design. This study develops and validates a high-fidelity finite-element model of a passenger-car BIW subjected to a 64 km/h full-frontal impact, addressing those limitations. The proposed framework couples global crash metrics energy balance, deceleration pulse, intrusion, and sectional forces with spatial plastic-strain mapping using shotgun plots to evaluate localized deformation behavior. Validation was performed against NCAP crash data and published finite-element models, showing close agreement: peak deceleration = 32 g (–1.5% error), pulse duration = 87 ms (–3.3%), and toe-pan intrusion = 123 mm (+ 2.5%). More than 92% of the initial kinetic energy was absorbed as internal plastic work with total energy balance error < 5%, confirming numerical stability. Shotgun analysis indicated that 68% of crash-box elements and 54% of front-rail elements exceeded the 0.15 strain threshold, identifying dominant energy-absorbing regions. The framework provides a reproducible, simulation-only approach for assessing crashworthiness and structural optimization without requiring experimental testing. The methodology can be readily extended to multi-condition crash scenarios and lightweight design applications, offering a cost-effective foundation for predictive safety evaluation in next-generation vehicle architectures.

Introduction

Frontal collisions remain among the highest-risk crash modes for vehicle occupants, making front-end energy management and body-in-white deformation control central to vehicle safety and lightweight design. Modern analytical and numerical tools allow precise investigation of stiffness matching, pulse characteristics, and multi-condition crash responses. These enable targeted optimization of crash boxes, rails, and sub-frame interfaces to enhance energy absorption and maintain occupant survival space.

High-fidelity explicit finite element simulations are now essential in evaluating deformation, force histories, and energy absorption of crash-critical structures. However, their predictive capability depends on accurate material modeling, mesh quality, and experimental calibration through techniques such as high-speed imaging and digital image correlation (DIC). Studies show that hybrid approaches combining component-level testing with full-system FE correlation yield the best reproducibility and regulatory credibility.

Recent innovations including foam-filled, lattice, origami, and composite crash absorbers—alongside machine learning–driven optimization workflows have expanded the crashworthiness design space, especially for electric vehicles (EVs), where battery protection and lightweighting must coexist with structural integrity. Yet, despite these advances, linking localized deformation mechanisms with global crash responses remains a persistent challenge. Most previous research focuses on isolated components or simplified substructures, often lacking full BIW interaction or unified energy assessment. Moreover, limited integration of strain-based failure indicators with global force–time histories restricts comprehensive evaluation of structural performance. Addressing this deficiency requires a detailed FE framework capable of correlating local folding and plastic strain evolution with global crash energy management behavior.

Frontal crash simulation and validation form the foundation of credible crashworthiness research. Explicit FE methods calibrated through DIC-based strain fields have proven effective in predicting folding progression, stress localization, and damage initiation1,2. Benchmarking simulated deceleration pulses and crush profiles against experimental datasets or NCAP references is a standard validation practice3. Component-level crash-box and rail tests further assist in tuning constitutive and failure parameters for full-vehicle FE models4.

Analytical and semi-empirical pulse models complement FE simulations by providing rapid stiffness matching and energy-path estimates before detailed computation3. Such reduced-order tools define feasible energy-absorption ranges specific energy absorption (SEA) and peak crush force (PCF) and establish target envelopes for optimization5. Hybrid analytical–numerical approaches now achieve robust front-end architectures that maintain acceptable pulse shapes under rigid, deformable, and oblique barrier conditions6. This accelerates design exploration while preserving interpretability.

Advances in absorber topology and materials have yielded notable gains in SEA and progressive folding control. Foam-filled and lattice geometries, origami composites, and bio-inspired re-entrant structures enhance crush efficiency while moderating peak loads7–9. Sustainable alternatives, such as wood-based and natural-fiber tubes, have also demonstrated competitive crash performance10. Gradient and functionally graded fillers enable controlled buckling sequences beneficial for EV battery enclosure protection11. Recent studies on modular metamaterials and bio-inspired absorber systems emphasize tailored plateau stresses and tunable collapse modes that improve specific energy absorption and force efficiency12–14. These works motivate using standardized absorber metrics for objective comparison across novel topologies.

High-dimensional crash design increasingly relies on multi-objective optimization (MOO) and surrogate-based acceleration to manage computational cost. Taguchi–GRA and NSGA-II approaches have been applied to crash-box and rail design, efficiently identifying Pareto-optimal trade-offs between intrusion, mass, and peak deceleration6,15. Recent efforts combine physics-informed surrogates with neural networks to rapidly evaluate battery-structure crashworthiness11,16, confirming the growing role of data-driven design in vehicle safety.

Integrating structural response improvements with occupant injury metrics represents a critical research frontier. Coupling validated BIW FE models with Human Body Models (HBMs) or dummy-based indicators such as HIC, Nij, or chest deflection enables quantification of injury reduction due to structural modifications17. Although such fully coupled validation workflows remain scarce, they are increasingly recognized as essential for translating structural crash gains into measurable occupant protection1,7.

The present study addresses these gaps by developing a high-fidelity BIW frontal crash model that incorporates balanced meshing, calibrated material properties, and strict energy verification. Beyond conventional global metrics deceleration pulse, intrusion, and absorbed energy the model introduces a novel damage fraction assessment based on shotgun-plot analysis to quantify the spatial extent of plastic deformation in critical components. This integration of global and local indicators bridges the disconnect between macro-scale crash response and micro-scale deformation. The framework consolidates energy balance, sectional force histories, intrusion patterns, and literature-based validation into a single, reproducible methodology. The acknowledged challenge of achieving reliable, fully simulation-based validation is thus tackled by demonstrating a data-consistent, literature-verified workflow adaptable for early-stage BIW design optimization and eventual coupling with occupant injury models.

Methodology

Mesh statistics: the final discretized model comprises approximately ≈220,000 shell elements and ≈230,000 nodes, with element aspect ratios maintained below 5.0 in all refined regions and a minimum aspect ratio near 1.2. Mass distribution and total BIW mass were controlled to remain within ± 2% of the target curb mass by adjusting element thickness assignments and local mass balancing.

Justification for element type and size: quadrilateral shell elements with reduced integration are standard for explicit crash problems due to their computational efficiency and ability to represent large in-plane membrane strains and bending-dominated folding (Belytschko et al., references). The chosen local refinement (3–4 mm) ensures that the characteristic fold wavelength and local bend radii are resolved by multiple element rows, limiting artificial stiffening and reducing hourglass sensitivity. A local mesh convergence study was performed on the front-rail/crash-box assembly (see Sect. "Output variables and evaluation metrics"), confirming that reducing element size from 7 to 5 mm changed peak sectional force and toe-pan intrusion by less than 3%, validating the selected discretization.

The BIW structure was discretized predominantly using reduced-integration shell elements with stiffness-based hourglass control, which are well suited for large-deformation crash simulations involving thin-walled structures. This formulation ensures computational efficiency while maintaining adequate bending response when combined with mesh quality control. Solid elements, where used for localized components, employed fully integrated formulations to suppress spurious zero-energy modes. Hourglass energy was continuously monitored and remained below 5% of the total internal energy, confirming the numerical robustness of the selected element formulations.

All crash simulations in this study were performed using the explicit finite element solver Altair RADIOSS, with HyperMesh used for preprocessing and HyperView used for post-processing. The element formulations, material models, and contact definitions described in the following subsections correspond to RADIOSS implementation.

The finite element model represents a body-in-white (BIW) structure, and the total mass corresponds to the BIW-only mass calculated from the discretized shell elements. No lumped masses were introduced to account for interior components, glazing, powertrain, or trim systems. Consequently, the applied initial kinetic energy reflects the BIW mass at the prescribed impact velocity. This modelling approach is appropriate for assessing structural deformation, load-path evolution, and intrusion behavior at the BIW level.

Failure and damage modelling assumption—No explicit damage initiation, fracture strain criterion, or element deletion was implemented in the present model. All structural components were allowed to undergo large elasto-plastic deformation without material erosion. This approach is appropriate for evaluating global crash response and intrusion behavior, where continuous deformation governs the load transfer and energy absorption mechanisms. The resulting intrusion predictions therefore reflect conservative structural deformation trends rather than localized fracture-driven collapse.

Contact and Constraint Definitions—Contact interactions between BIW components and between the BIW and the rigid barrier were modeled using a penalty-based contact formulation. Solver-recommended adaptive penalty stiffness factors were employed to balance contact enforcement and numerical stability. A constant friction coefficient representative of steel–steel interaction was used for self-contact, while frictionless contact was assumed at the rigid barrier interface. Shell thickness offsets were automatically considered within the contact algorithm. Soft constraint options were adopted where applicable to prevent artificial stiffness and to allow realistic folding kinematics during impact.

The finite element model includes only the body-in-white structure and excludes subsystems such as seats, dashboard assembly, wiring harnesses, powertrain components, battery system, HVAC units, and steering column. These components were omitted to isolate the structural crash response of the BIW. Although such subsystems may locally influence stiffness and mass distribution, the dominant intrusion mechanisms under full-frontal impact are governed by deformation of the front rails, firewall, toe-pan, and A-pillar base. Accordingly, the reported intrusion results reflect conservative BIW-level behavior.

While strain-rate effects and fracture models were not directly implemented due to data limitations, stress–strain curves were calibrated against values reported for BIW-grade steels in NCAP/IIHS validated simulations. Key structural components modelled

The finite element model includes all major load-bearing components of the body-in-white. These comprise the front-rail assemblies and crash boxes that initiate the primary crush response; the A-pillars, B-pillars and C-pillars forming the cabin frame; the firewall and toe-pan assembly that governs intrusion into the occupant compartment; the floor cross-members and sills providing lateral stiffness; and the roof rails supporting global bending resistance. These components are explicitly referred to throughout the results and discussion to describe deformation patterns, load-path activation, and energy-absorption behaviour.

Joints and connections

Spot-welded connections between major BIW components (rails, firewall, A-pillar) were represented using tied contacts, an approach commonly adopted in validated crashworthiness models. The weld distribution was based on design practice, ensuring structural continuity. Seam welds and adhesives were homogenized as continuous connections to reduce complexity while preserving global stiffness. Connections between BIW components, including regions corresponding to spot-welded and seam-welded joints, were represented using tied contact definitions. This modeling strategy enforces kinematic compatibility between adjoining panels and is appropriate for evaluating global crash response and load-path development in full-vehicle simulations. Detailed connector-level representations of individual welds or adhesive layers were not included, as the present study focuses on overall structural deformation and intrusion patterns rather than local joint failure mechanisms.

Contact definitions

Automatic Surface-to-surface contact with penalty formulation was defined between structural components, supplemented by friction coefficients of 0.2 between steel interfaces. Contact stability was monitored by ensuring penetration energies remained negligible (< 3% of total internal energy), following Radioss/LS-DYNA best practice guidelines3.

Mesh sensitivity and numerical convergence

To ensure mesh-independent results, a local mesh sensitivity analysis was conducted in the front rails and crash box regions, where progressive folding dominates energy absorption. Three mesh configurations coarse (10 mm), baseline (7 mm), and refined (5 mm) were compared. The variation in peak sectional force and maximum toe-pan intrusion between baseline and refined meshes was less than 3%, confirming numerical convergence. The 5–8 mm element size was thus selected as optimal for balancing accuracy and computational efficiency.

The shell elements used in this study employed a fully integrated Belytschko–Tsay formulation with hourglass control and five integration points through thickness, as recommended in Radioss guidelines. The bilinear elastic–plastic model was adopted after preliminary calibration against published strain–stress curves for BIW-grade steel1,2, ensuring realistic energy absorption and deformation characteristics. These refinements collectively enhance the physical fidelity of the developed finite element model.

The results of the mesh sensitivity study (Table 3 and Fig. 4) indicate that further refinement beyond a 7 mm element size produces negligible improvement in both force and displacement predictions. The convergence of peak sectional force and intrusion values within ± 3% demonstrates mesh independence, ensuring the robustness of the selected discretization. This level of refinement aligns with the recommendations of Radioss/LS-DYNA crash modelling standards3 and validated BIW studies1,2. Table 4 shows that both peak sectional force and toe-pan intrusion decrease monotonically with mesh refinement, with the 7 mm mesh deviating by less than 10–11% and the 5 mm mesh by approximately 13–15% relative to the coarse mesh. This confirms that the adopted mesh lies within the asymptotic convergence region. Element aspect ratios were controlled during preprocessing and maintained within recommended limits for explicit crash analysis. Results and discussion

Energy balance and model stability

The global energy history is shown in Fig. 5, which illustrates the conversion of initial kinetic energy into internal and contact energies during the crash event. The results indicate that over 92% of the total input energy is dissipated as internal energy, while contact and hourglass energies remain below 5%. This confirms numerical stability and credibility of the FE model, consistent with recommendations from Radioss/LS-DYNA guidelines3. Such energy partitioning is in agreement with validated crash studies where the majority of impact energy is absorbed through plastic deformation in front rails and crash boxes1,2. The stable energy response provides confidence that the subsequent stress, displacement, and acceleration outputs can be interpreted reliably for design evaluation.Fig. 5Variation of Total energy with respect to Time.

Shot-gun plot

The shotgun plot in Fig. 7 provides a spatially resolved map of a selected element-level scalar (e.g., maximum plastic strain, maximum principal strain, or peak element strain energy) either at a given time-step or as the maximum-over-time for each element. This visualization highlights the chronology and location of local yielding and potential element deletion/failure and is especially useful for identifying the sequence in which crashboxes, rails, and joints contribute to energy absorption. In the present model, the shotgun plot reveals that initiating plasticity concentrates at the crashbox edges and the toe-pan fillet, then propagates into the longitudinal rails; A-pillar elements reach elevated strain levels later, indicating progressive energy redistribution consistent with observed folding patterns2,8.Fig. 7Resultant force histories for the left and right shotgun structural sections during the frontal crash event.

Displacement and intrusion patterns

Figure 8. shows a maximum intrusion of approximately 120 mm occurs in the toe-pan region, while noticeable lateral displacement is seen in the lower A-pillar. These results align with the intrusion magnitudes reported in validated NCAP simulations for similar BIW structures, which typically range from 110 to 130 mm under 64 km/h rigid-barrier impacts1,3. Excessive toe-pan intrusion is a known contributor to lower limb injury risk, as identified in occupant safety studies17. The predicted deformation pattern indicates that although the BIW model effectively absorbs impact energy, additional design attention to the toe-pan and firewall reinforcement is required to minimize intrusion into the occupant space.Fig. 8Time history of toe-pan intrusion measured using spring elements located at the firewall–toe-pan junction on the left and right sides of the vehicle.

The intrusion sequence observed in Fig. 8 reflects a coupled deformation mechanism in which front-rail collapse drives rearward motion of the firewall, producing upward rotation and inward displacement of the toe-pan. Simultaneously, load transfer to the A-pillar base results in localized deformation and inward translation of the lower cabin frame. These interactions collectively define the intrusion pattern into the footwell region, demonstrating how the rail, toe-pan, and A-pillar deformations evolve in coordination during the crash event.

Deformation of the B-pillar

Figure 10 illustrates the deformation of the B-pillar region during the frontal impact. While the highest stresses and intrusions are concentrated in the toe-pan and A-pillar, the B-pillar also shows localized bending and stress accumulation at its lower joint with the rocker panel. The maximum displacement recorded in this region is approximately 25 mm, which remains within acceptable limits compared to values reported in validated crash simulations of similar BIW models1,7. The structural integrity of the B-pillar is critical for preserving side-impact protection and overall compartment strength, as its collapse can compromise rollover and side crashworthiness17. The simulation shows that the B-pillar retains its global load-carrying capability, though minor deformation at its base suggests the need for reinforcement in the rocker–pillar joint. The magnitude of these forces lies within the expected range reported for validated mid-size steel BIW crash models subjected to 64 km/h rigid-barrier impacts7,8. Symmetry between left and right rails indicates balanced energy absorption, a desirable feature for maintaining stability during impact. Similar folding-induced force fluctuations have been documented in experimental crash box tests10. Total resultant force

Figure 12 presents the total resultant force transmitted through the front structure as a function of time during the frontal impact. This resultant is computed as the vector sum of sectional reaction forces at the primary load paths (left + right rails, crashboxes, and major attachments) and therefore represents the net structural resistance presented to the barrier. Compared to previous studies, this work consolidates a simulation-only methodology that leverages literature-based validation, a recognized approach in crashworthiness research2,7. While experimental validation and occupant injury metrics would further strengthen the findings, the present study contributes valuable insights into BIW crash behavior under frontal impact.

The methodological contribution of this work lies in its integrated approach coupling global crash indicators such as acceleration pulse, intrusion, and absorbed energy with local strain-based damage evaluation using shotgun plots. The mesh sensitivity and impulse–momentum checks establish numerical credibility, while the validated deceleration and intrusion results confirm physical fidelity. The study establishes a reproducible methodology that integrates global crash metrics with spatial damage quantification through the novel damage-fraction approach. This dual-scale interpretation links macro-level structural response with micro-level material deformation, enabling targeted reinforcement assessment and forming a comprehensive digital workflow adaptable to various material grades and vehicle classes.

The present study focuses on establishing and validating a numerically robust BIW crash model rather than conducting a parametric optimization. While sensitivity analyses involving thickness variation, joint strength modification, or strain-rate parameter tuning are valuable for design optimization, such studies are beyond the scope of this work. The validated framework presented here provides a reliable baseline for future sensitivity and optimization studies aimed at improving crash performance.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated a high-fidelity finite element model of a passenger car body-in-white (BIW) subjected to a 64 km/h full-frontal crash. The model demonstrated robust numerical stability, with energy balance error below 5% and over 92% of initial kinetic energy dissipated through plastic deformation. Key crashworthiness indicators including peak deceleration (32 g), pulse duration (87 ms), and maximum toe-pan intrusion (123 mm) closely matched values reported in NCAP crash databases and validated finite element studies, confirming the predictive reliability of the simulation chain.

Shotgun plot analysis quantified the spatial distribution of plastic strain, revealing that 68% of crash box elements and 54% of front rail elements exceeded the 0.15 strain threshold, confirming their dominant role in energy absorption. Balanced left–right rail forces and realistic acceleration pulses further substantiated the model’s credibility. This spatial damage-fraction metric provides a direct, quantitative basis to prioritize targeted reinforcements and mass-efficient design changes within the BIW load-paths. The findings highlight the toe-pan and A-pillar base as critical regions requiring structural reinforcement to mitigate intrusion and enhance occupant survival space. The validated model provides a robust platform for design optimization, sensitivity studies, and integration with human body models (HBMs) or dummy injury metrics to directly link structural performance with occupant safety outcomes.

The framework presented here provides a foundation for integrating occupant injury models, lightweight material substitutions, and machine-learning-assisted optimization in future work. Its demonstrated accuracy within benchmark limits positions this methodology as a cost-effective and scalable tool for early-stage automotive body design and safety evaluation in emerging mobility platforms.
