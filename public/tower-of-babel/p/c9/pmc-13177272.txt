A Transdisciplinary Allied Health “Flying Squad” to Overcome Non‐Medical Barriers to Discharge From Hospital: A Feasibility Study
Authors: Aruska N D'Souza, James Fitzpatrick, Kate Palmer, Hana McDonald, Jane Jolley, Genevieve Juj, Casey L Peiris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRationaleAcutely hospitalised patients may be medically ready for discharge but face psychosocial and other non‐medical barriers to returning home. This increases their length of stay and may lead to exposure to hospital associated risks such as hospital‐acquired infections. To address these non‐medical delays, a transdisciplinary allied health service (the Flying Squad, n = 1.9 Full Time Equivalent professionals) was established at The Royal Melbourne Hospital.Aims and ObjectivesTo evaluate the feasibility (demand, implementation, practicality, acceptability and limited efficacy) of this service.MethodA single‐site observational study was conducted to determine the demand (number of referrals and patient characteristics including indicators of complexity via the Blaylock Risk Assessment Screening Score; BRASS), implementation (timing of referrals and home visits completed), practicality (case examples, adverse events), acceptability (compliments and complaints) and limited efficacy (estimated number of hospital days saved through the Flying Squad intervention [anticipated minus actual length of stay], discharge destination, hospital length of stay and 28‐day readmissions).ResultsOver 12 months, 147 participants were seen by the Flying Squad professionals (median age 64 years [Interquartile Range 51–78 years], 61% male, on day 14 [Interquartile age 7–24] of their hospital admission). Median BRASS scores were 14 (Interquartile Range 9–19] indicating “at risk of requiring extended discharge planning”. Fifty‐four home visits were completed for 34 (23%) participants, and one adverse event was reported (fall with no injuries during a home visit). The service won two hospital Awards for Excellence. The median length of hospital stay was 26 [13–45] days; 80 (54%) participants were discharged directly home, and 13 (9%) were readmitted after 28 days. It was estimated that the Flying Squad saved 756 hospital days over 12 months.ConclusionThe Flying Squad was a feasible service that may assist with addressing non‐medical barriers to discharge.Keywords: feasibility studies, hospitals, length of stay, patient discharge, readmissions

Introduction

Hospitalised patients may be medically ready to return home, but may face barriers to discharge such as homelessness, unavailability of post‐discharge facilities, awaiting a new package of care, impaired decision‐making capacity or an absence of a guardian [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. As a result, these patients may spend additional time in an acute hospital [5]. Delayed discharges are problematic and have financial, hospital system and patient outcome implications. In a systematic review of 10 international studies, it was estimated that non‐medical delays to discharge equated to more than £100,000 ($177,000 Australian Dollars, AUD) per year for a ward of 30 beds in the United Kingdom [6]. Aside from the additional cost to the health system, delayed discharges increase emergency department waiting times, as medically stable patients occupy beds that could be utilised by patients with acute medical needs [7, 8]. Patients are also at risk of hospital associated complications such as deconditioning and infection [6, 9, 10, 11, 12], which may lead to a further increase in hospital length of stay. Longer hospital stays are also associated with reduced patient satisfaction and staff burnout [3].

Background

To address non‐medical delays to discharge, the Royal Melbourne Hospital developed the Flying Squad, a transdisciplinary service that commenced in November 2021. This service aimed to address barriers to discharge, optimise patient outcomes and reduce hospital length of stay. The Flying Squad works alongside a traditional medical multidisciplinary model of care to provide additional problem‐solving strategies and creative solutions to facilitate discharge when there are non‐medical delays. There is opportunity for this team to stay involved with the patient for short periods after hospital discharge to ensure adequate adjustment to illness in home environment. The Flying Squad was aptly named to reflect the transdisciplinary and flexible nature of their roles and to highlight their ability to provide their skills and expertise as required with any patient across the hospital's continuum of care. To date, there has been no formal evaluation of this novel service. Thus, the aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility (demand, implementation, practicality, and limited efficacy) of the Flying Squad service at the Royal Melbourne Hospital to inform future research and highlight changes required to optimise the model of care.

Methods

Design

This single site, observational cohort study utilised Bowen's feasibility framework [13] to evaluate the demand, implementation, practicality, acceptability and limited efficacy of a novel, transdisciplinary service specifically aimed to address barriers to discharge and facilitate timely and safe discharge. The study intervention (the Flying Squad) is described according to the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist [14] (Appendix 1) and results are reported following Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (STROBE) [15]. Ethical approval was received from the study hospital (QA2023010).

Setting

This study was conducted at the Royal Melbourne Hospital, a large tertiary hospital and one of the largest health care providers in Australia, from 17 May 2022 to 17 May 2023. The Royal Melbourne Hospital is the designated state‐wide provider for trauma and infectious disease and is a centre for excellence for tertiary services such as neurosciences, nephrology, cardiology, virtual health and management of the diabetic foot.

Intervention

The Flying Squad was established in November 2021 as a transdisciplinary model of care designed to work alongside the traditional multidisciplinary team [16]. The Flying Squad aimed to address non‐medical delays to discharge that exceeded the typical scope of acute allied health, nursing or medical teams, but were essential for facilitating safe, timely, and effective discharge. This service assists patients requiring extra support to achieve their discharge goals when they are not eligible for alternative pathways, such as rehabilitation, community supports, or funding. The Flying Squad consisted of two to three senior allied health professionals: an occupational therapist, physiotherapist, social worker and clinical neuropsychology Registrar (registered psychologist who is undertaking specialised training in clinical neuropsychology) in different combinations over the study period (described in the implementation section of the results). Key referral indicators to the Flying Squad included acute hospital inpatients who had an extended hospital length of stay, complex discharge needs, a history of frequent hospitalisations, safety concerns, those with no clear discharge destination or those without insurance or funding impacting discharge. Professionals in the Flying Squad worked in an allied health transdisciplinary model and collaborated closely to respond to the needs of patients and provide practical discharge solutions. Examples of transdisciplinary care and working outside of usual roles where additional training had been provided included a neuropsychologist attending a home visit, measuring house dimensions and assisting with equipment provision; sourcing specialist equipment or items and funding (where the patient could not afford this) or an occupational therapist assisting with specific tracheostomy care tasks, medication administration and percutaneous endoscopic gastroscopy feeding. Further intervention details are provided in Appendix 1.

Participants

All participants who were seen by the Flying Squad during the study period were included in this study. Patients not accepted for Flying Squad intervention included patients requiring end‐of‐life care, acute psychiatric intervention or those with clear discharge plans.

Demand

Demand was measured by recording the number of referrals to the Flying Squad and the participant demographics, including age, reason for admission (via diagnostic reporting group) and gender. The key indicators of complexity for eligible participants were also recorded via the Blaylock Risk Assessment Screening Score (BRASS) and the Flying Squad. The BRASS is a 10‐item tool measuring age, living situation, cognition, behaviour, function, mobility, sensory deficits, medical problems, medications and previous admissions [17]. Higher scores indicate an increased risk of not being able to be discharged home and a prolonged length of hospital stay [17]. The measure has been validated in medical and surgical inpatient populations [18, 19]. Other common factors related to prolonged hospital stay (e.g., a change in weight bearing status) were also recorded.

Implementation

The staffing model and changes over time are described. Implementation was evaluated by recording the time from hospital admission to referral to the Flying Squad, the time spent with participants and the number of home visits completed.

Practicality

Practicality was recorded through case examples and by assessing the number of adverse events using the hospital's internal reporting systems.

Acceptability

Acceptability was measured by recording the number of compliments and complaints received regarding the Flying Squad using the hospital's internal reporting system.

Limited Efficacy

Limited efficacy was calculated by estimating the number of days saved through the Flying Squad. For example, if the participant had a documented planned length of stay of 30 days to allow for weight bearing restrictions, but the Flying Squad sourced an alternative discharge option that enabled discharge in 14 days, the estimated days saved would be 16 days. A Flying Squad member estimated bed days saved upon the patient's discharge from the inpatient setting. Another team member, who may or may not have been involved in the patient's care, would then conduct an independent file review and provide their own estimate, blinded to the initial estimate. If there was a discrepancy, the more conservative option (i.e. less days saved) was selected. Discharge destination, hospital length of stay and readmissions after 28‐days were also collected.

Sample Size and Data Analysis

As this was a feasibility study, sample size was determined by the number of participants referred to the Flying Squad during the study period. Data were analysed using IBM® SPSS® Statistics (Version 28.0, Armonk, New York, USA). Baseline descriptive data are presented as frequencies (percentage) for categorical data and median [Interquartile range, IQR] for numerical variables, as data were nonparametric. Agreement for estimations of length of stay days saved was calculated using percentage agreement [20]. Differences between participants who likely had a reduced length of stay as a result of Flying Squad interventions and those who did were calculated via Mann‐Whitney U or Chi Square tests.

Results

Demand

During the 12‐month data collection period, 156 people were referred to the Flying Squad and 147 participants received intervention from the Flying Squad (median age 64 [51–78] years, male 61%, Table 2). Approximately half of the referrals were for patients admitted under the general medical units, and the main reasons for admission were conditions of the musculoskeletal, nervous and circulatory systems, respectively (Table 2). As per the BRASS, 60 (41%) participants were at risk of requiring further discharge planning, 52 (35%) were at risk for home care services, and 35 (24%) were at risk for placement other than home (Table 2, Appendix 2). The nine patients who were referred to the Flying Squad but not seen were either discharged prior to Flying Squad review (n = 6) or had a plan for discharge in place when the Flying Squad reviewed (n = 3) and therefore did not require additional Flying Squad intervention.

Implementation

At its inception in November 2021, the Flying Squad was staffed with an Occupational Therapist (1.0 EFT), Social Worker (1.0 EFT) and Clinical Psychologist (0.2 EFT). In October 2022, the Flying Squad was staffed by a full time Occupational Therapist only with a Neuropsychologist (0.2EFT) available for consult; and in January 2023, the Flying Squad transitioned to being staffed by a full time Occupational Therapist and Physiotherapist, and then finally an Occupational Therapist (1 EFT), Physiotherapist (0.8 EFT) and Clinical Neuropsychology Registrar (0.6 EFT) in July 2023. These changes in staffing were due to staff resigning and positions being filled by people from other professions. Therefore, on average over the data collection period, the Flying Squad was staffed at 1.9 EFT allied health professionals.

Participants were referred to the Flying Squad at a median of 14 [7–24] days after hospital admission. The Flying Squad spent 980 h in total with the 147 participants over 979 patient encounters (on average 7 h with each patient). Fifty‐four home visits were completed for 34 (23%) different participants.

Practicality

The Flying Squad were able to deliver a range of interventions utilising existing resources and creative solutions. For example, following a haemorrhagic stroke with subsequent left sided hemiparesis and fall, a 44‐year‐old overseas visitor presented to our hospital. The patient did not have any insurance or family within Australia; their passport had expired, and their Australian visa was imminently expiring. The treating team recommended rehabilitation on acute hospital discharge. Given patient would be required to self‐fund this in Australia, they requested to complete their rehabilitation in their country of origin. Due to the patient's functional levels (requiring assistance of two persons for transfers and mobility, as well as experiencing cognitive‐communication impairments), they were not able to contact their consulate to arrange a new passport, repatriation or rehabilitation. The Flying Squad liaised with and attended the consulate to obtain an emergency, same‐day passport using a photo they took of the patient on the ward. The Flying Squad also co‐ordinated the repatriation process and facilitated boarding of this repatriation flight and transfer to rehabilitation in the patient's country of origin. One adverse event was reported during the study duration. A participant had a fall during a home visit with the Flying Squad. There were no injuries sustained, and no immediate first aid was required. Contributing factors included multiple previous falls, a cluttered environment (six out of nine on the Clutter Image Rating Scale) [21], and the participant was not using their prescribed gait aid.

Acceptability

During the study period, the Flying Squad received two nominations for “You Made A Difference” Awards (a hospital initiative where teams or individuals who demonstrate living the hospital values, commitment to patient care and/or safety culture are recognised). A member of the Flying Squad won the “Excellence Together” award at the Royal Melbourne Hospital Allied Health Awards 2022 for their contribution while in this role, and the service also won the “Excellence in Allied Health” award at the Royal Melbourne Hospital Celebrating Excellence Awards 2022. No complaints were received.

Limited Efficacy

The median length of hospital stay for patients referred to the Flying Squad was 26 [13–45] days, and most people were able to return to their place of residence on discharge (n = 97, 66%). It was estimated that the Flying Squad saved 756 hospital days in total. Percentage of agreement for initial estimations of length of days saved was 79%. Eighty (54%) of participants were discharged home, and 13 (9%) were readmitted after 28 days. It was estimated that at least one hospital day was saved in 77 (52%) participants, and the overall median hospital days saved was 1 [0–4] per patient, with a range from zero to 90 days. There was no difference in age (p = 0.15), legal sex (p = 0.23) or discharge home (p = 0.51) between participants whose hospital stay was likely reduced by Flying Squad Intervention (64 [49–75 years, 65% male, 57% discharged home) and whose stay was not (64 [54–81] years, 57% male, 51% discharged home). Participants whose length of stay was likely reduced by the Flying Squad had slightly lower BRASS scores (13 [8–17]) compared to those whose length of stay did not change (15 [10–20], p = 0.04), though median scores for both groups still fell within the “at risk for discharge planning” range.

Discussion

This study found that there was adequate demand for the transdisciplinary Flying Squad service. The Flying Squad was successfully implemented despite multiple changes to staffing, possibly indicating the transdisciplinary nature of the service allowed for changes in professional disciplines. The service was safely delivered and the Flying Squad saw participants for an average of six encounters of approximately 1 h in duration, which is double the time reported for acute physiotherapists and occupational therapists in acute settings, both at this site and in the literature (15–30 min) [22, 23]. Notably, service delivery often involved interventions and strategies not usually conducted in an acute setting (e.g., 34 home visits), and one patient encounter could take 4–7 h on a single day, leaving less time for other patients or other duties. The service was well accepted by patients and the organisation, and likely associated with bed day savings, facilitating discharge directly home as well as reducing readmission rates. Considering these potential benefits, the Flying Squad may lead to other benefits to the hospital, staff and patients, such as improving patient and staff satisfaction, reducing the risk of hospital associated injuries and reducing associated hospital costs [6, 24]. Notably, patients requiring Flying Squad intervention did not always have high BRASS scores (indicating the need for discharge planning and being more at risk for placement other than home), and this may impact timely referral to this service.

Delays in referral to the Flying Squad may have negatively impacted the efficacy of the service and could be addressed in the future. Participants waited a median of 14 [7–24] days from their hospital admission before being referred to the Flying Squad. This is substantially longer than the average hospital stay in Australia of 5.4 days [25]. This could be due to awaiting medical stability, clinical indecision or the patients usual team exploring all available discharge options before considering the senior expertise and flexible problem‐solving approach of the Flying Squad. To improve efficiency, screening tools may help to identify patients earlier in their hospital stay. The BRASS was specifically developed to identify patients who were at risk of requiring specialised discharge planning and who may not be able to return home [17]; however, in our study, this tool did not identify a third (n = 52, 35%) of patients who required this additional specialised discharge service. Understanding the characteristics and complexity of patients who accessed the Flying Squad may inform future referrals, particularly if this information were embedded into the electronic medical record and prompted automatic referral to the Flying Squad. This may enable appropriate interventions to occur in a timely manner. Given the relatively low numbers over the data collection period (n = 147) and diversity of the patients referred, further examination was not possible in this study. dtives to facilitate timely referral to this service could include alternative outcome measures such as the Risk Assessment of Complex Discharge Index (which identifies patients who may have social barriers to discharge) [26], or measures such as the Post‐Acute Care at Discharge (PACD), Higher Care at Discharge Index (HCDI) or Alpha Functional Independence Measure (which measure function, home supports and medical problems and have been associated with determining discharge destination) [18, 27]. A routine transdisciplinary allied health screening early in the admission may also assist with timely referral to the Flying Squad [28, 29], though future research is required to determine the most appropriate measure or process.

A transdisciplinary model of care in which Flying Squad staff were given autonomy to step outside usual clinical boundaries (but remain within scope of practice) was an important factor in the success of this service. This differs from other Allied Health Interdisciplinary Practitioner roles, which often focus on early assessment of patients and ensuring appropriate referrals are made [28]. Our model required the Flying Squad members to be willing to learn new skills and have excellent negotiation strategies that are underpinned by a strong clinical reasoning foundation. For example, the occupational therapist within the Flying Squad assisted with teaching the tracheostomy care (Case study three, Table 3) in a patient who was to be discharged with a permanent tracheostomy. While this would not be usual practice for an occupational therapist to perform this role at the study site, it was done this way to ensure continuity of care, educate the patient in their own home and leverage the rapport already established between the patient and the occupational therapist. The Flying Squad staff member received peer training in line with the hospital's tracheostomy competency policy to facilitate this. Similarly, home and community visits from the acute hospital are not standard practice but were vital to manage the uncommon barriers to discharge. As supported by a recent systematic review of allied health transdisciplinary models of care, such processes require not only flexibility, willingness to learn new skills and a strong clinical reasoning foundation from the Flying Squad staff members, but also the support of senior management [28]. Other reported factors influencing successful implementation of transdisciplinary models in allied health include a willingness to share knowledge and a collaborative, stable non‐competitive workforce with clear governance structures in place [28]. Selection of the optimal mix of allied health professionals within such a model of care, as well as consideration of the legal and professional boundaries, are important considerations for future implementation [28]. Future research should also consider the cost implications of such roles.

Strengths and Limitation

This study utilised Bowen's framework [13] to evaluate the feasibility of the Flying Squad. However, it was conducted at a single site and had no control group; therefore, it is unclear whether the limited efficacy findings of this study are specifically due to the Flying Squad. This study is designed as a feasibility study to inform readers if the demand, implementation, practicalities, acceptability and limited efficacy testing when establishing a novel transdisciplinary model of care. Future research could consider a randomised controlled trial with an unexposed control group to establish the impact of the Flying Squad on important outcomes such as length of stay. While we utilised two independent reviewers to estimate length of days saved, only an estimate could be provided. We did not complete an economic evaluation of this service to determine if the bed days saved outweighed the cost of funding the Flying Squad clinicians. We also did not measure patient satisfaction with having an additional team provide care, nor did we measure clinician perceptions of having another team member, potentially of the same allied health discipline. Future research could consider a more in‐depth exploration of patient and clinician attitudes and perspectives through interviews, focus groups or surveys.

Conclusion

Over a year, there was demand for a team of transdisciplinary allied health practitioners to provide solutions to non‐medical barriers to hospital discharge. Two‐thirds of participants who were referred to the Flying Squad were able to be discharged home, and it was estimated that this service saved bed days. Future studies should consider a robust health economic evaluation to determine if the days saved outweigh the cost of providing this service. Future research should also explore which patients are most likely to benefit from this service to enable more timely referral and potentially facilitate earlier discharge.

The primary aim was to expedite discharge for medically cleared patients with complex needs or atypical barriers to discharge in the acute setting. Additionally, the Flying Squad assists patients requiring extra support to achieve their discharge goals when they are not eligible for alternative pathways, such as rehabilitation, community supports, or funding. Their roles also aim to ensure that all discharge options and access to services are thoroughly explored and facilitated.What Materials: The Flying Squad has individual mobile phones, laptops, access to hospital fleet cars, tool kits and home visit bags, and personal protective equipment (PPE) including hazmat suits and boot covers. They also have access to senior clinicians and managers for complex pro blem‐solving and negotiation, including disability liaison officers and executive support. Procedures: The Flying Squad operated under the Social Work & Diversity department and involves a hybrid‐transdisciplinary model that collaborates with, and as an adjunct to, existing multidisciplinary teams and community services, without replacing them. Referrals are assessed from all acute inpatient units, with priority given to medically cleared patients. The Flying Squad reviews the patient and provides interventions based on clinical indications. The Flying Squad also meet with the Nurse Unit Managers, the Divisional Directors of Nursing and hospital executive staff for weekly complex care meetings and to discuss patients who have or are at risk of having an extended hospital length of stay as well as attending operational meetings, professional development and completing mandatory training.Who providedThe Flying Squad consisted of 1.9 to 2.4 full time equivalent (FTE) senior clinicians, including a mix of occupational therapists (OT), physiotherapists, social workers, and clinical neuropsychologists. These senior clinicians have specialised training and experience in handling complex patient needs and facilitating safe discharges. Upskilling and competency training is obtained for areas outside of expertise (e.g., medical for medication dispensing, dietetics for enteral feeding education, speech pathology for laryngectomy care, wound nurse for specialised wound management, and pharmacy for medication titration plans).HowThe Flying Squad primarily deliver care via face‐to‐face interactions. Due to the complexity and associated psychological factors, the Flying Squad often work in pairs. There is further off‐site work conducted, such as in the community and home visits. Additional work can be completed remotely, such as file reviews, phone calls, and team meetings to guide the stakeholders and to be a key communicator.WhereThe Flying Squad have a non‐clinical office space within the acute hospital, as well as access to areas of the acute hospital such as patient rooms, gyms and courtyards. Additional work is completed in the community.When and how muchThe Flying Squad works from 7:30 AM to 4:00 PM on weekdays. Occasionally, work is done outside of these times if required (evenings, weekends), with the time taken in lieu. Interventions can range from a one or two sessions spanning a few hours to an entire day, depending on what is needed to successfully discharge the patient into the community.TailoringThe Flying Squad was initially funded as a pilot program but then obtained ongoing funding in May 2023 after demonstrating effectiveness with bed days saved and recognition of enhanced patient care by staff and patients.ModificationsThe Flying Squad was initially staffed with an occupational therapist, social worker and clinical psychologist. In January 2023, the Flying Squad transitioned to being staffed by an occupational therapist and physiotherapist, and then finally an occupational therapist, physiotherapist and clinical neuropsychology registrar in July 2023. These changes in staffing were due to staff resigning and positions being filled by people from other professions.Modifications were also made to department and supervision models to align with other allied health interdisciplinary practitioners within the hospital. Initially the Flying Squad received supervision from the Allied Health Deputy Director and formed part of their professional (Occupational Therapy, Social Work, Clinical Psychology) teams. In January 2023, the Flying Squad moved to management under the Social Work and Diversity Department, and finally under the Occupational Therapy Department in March 2024.
