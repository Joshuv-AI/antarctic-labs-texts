Generics in Medicaid increased in price and shifted to larger manufacturers amid 2022-2023 amphetamine shortage
Authors: Anthony Yu, Rosanna Smart, Andrew Mulcahy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn late 2022, several manufacturers of immediate-release (IR) amphetamine-based stimulant drugs reported shortages to the FDA. Regulatory requirements for amphetamine and other controlled substances, such as manufacturer-specific quotas set on raw materials, may exacerbate shortage effects on price and quantity.MethodsUsing 2018-2024 Medicaid State Drug Utilization Data, we studied the impact of the 2022-2023 amphetamine shortage on spending, spending per prescription (ie, prices), and dispensing of amphetamine products.ResultsDespite a significant decline in prescriptions dispensed per enrollee following the shortage announcement, gross spending per enrollee increased by 7% between 2022-Q2 and 2023-Q1. Prices for generic IR products increased by 6% between 2022-Q2 and 2023-Q1 and accounted for more than half of spending growth. Changes in prescription volume varied significantly by manufacturer, with relatively stable trends through the shortage for larger generic manufacturers and sharp declines for smaller manufacturers.ConclusionDespite substantial government oversight in controlled substance markets, prices increased for generics during the shortage. Differences in dispensing across manufacturers implied that, in Medicaid, larger firms adapted more effectively to supply disruptions.Keywords: Medicaid, drug shortage, amphetamine, amphetamine shortage, spending, prescription prices, stimulants, controlled substances, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder

Introduction

Over 10 million patients in the United States (U.S.) use stimulants to treat attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD),1 and reduced access to these medications can lead to worsening symptoms, declines in daily functioning, and broader impacts on health and educational outcomes.2-4 In 2022-2023, amphetamine-based and, subsequently, other stimulant drugs used to treat ADHD were reported by several manufacturers to be in shortage. Patients, providers, and professional organizations have voiced concerns about reduced utilization, negative health consequences, and potential economic harms.5 Consumers could face higher out-of-pocket costs for stimulant drugs. They may also be forced to substitute to more expensive alternatives.

Regulatory requirements for controlled substances, including stimulants, may lead shortages to have different effects compared with shortages of other generic medications. For controlled substances, manufacturing is limited by the national and firm-level quotas on raw materials set each year by the Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA).6 Quotas can affect the likelihood of a shortage ever occurring as well as firm responses during a shortage. First, depending on how the DEA implements them, quotas can limit (eg, by discouraging new entry) or enhance (eg, by distributing production allowances across multiple manufacturers to avoid single-source dependence) competition. Increased competition may increase systematic risk for shortages since firms have less incentive to invest in supply chains, but could also decrease idiosyncratic risk since there are more manufacturing sites.7 Second, during a shortage, rigid quotas may limit the ability of existing manufacturers to ramp up production, exacerbating price increases or prolonging the shortage's duration.8 Alternatively, proactive DEA management of production allowances could help alleviate these problems.

Despite widespread concern about the 2022-2023 amphetamine shortage, empirical evidence of its effects on utilization is limited.9-11 Currie and Malinovskaya (2026) showed that the decline in dispensing coincided with a sharp drop in US imports of raw amphetamine, not with binding DEA quotas, which the authors interpreted as suggesting that international supply chain disruptions were likely the cause of the shortage.11 We build upon this literature by providing additional evidence on the effects of the shortage on gross spending and gross spending per prescription (ie, prices) paid by Medicaid, which primarily enrolls low-income individuals.

Existing evidence on the effects of shortages on spending and prices, which has mostly focused on drugs covered under Medicare Part B (eg, sterile injectable) and commercial payers, has shown modest increases of 4% to 16% during and after generic shortages.12-15 Changes in spending stemming from shortage may be passed on to the patient through out-of-pocket spending.16 However, it is unclear whether existing findings translate to the context of controlled substances or to medications dispensed by retail pharmacies, where regulatory restrictions and rebates may produce differential price and expenditure responses. In addition, there is limited evidence on heterogeneity in shortage effects by formulation or manufacturer, factors that may be particularly important when production is constrained by DEA quotas.

To address these gaps, this study examines utilization and price responses to the 2022-2023 amphetamine shortage in Medicaid. Among those insured by Medicaid, estimates suggest approximately two percent of adults (5 million) and 13% of children aged 3-17 (4.5 million) have ADHD.17,18 Among those with ADHD in Medicaid, stimulant use ranges between 38% and 81% across states.17,18 Although Medicaid enrollees may not face increased out-of-pocket prices, stimulant shortages likely have important broader clinical, economic, and social costs.16 We analyzed national trends in Medicaid and estimated effects following the initial 2022 stimulant shortage announcement on amphetamine utilization, total gross spending, and gross spending per prescription (ie, price). Because of the unique nature of aggregate and manufacturer-specific quotas on the active ingredients of stimulant controlled substances, we also explored whether trends and effects varied across manufacturers.

Methods

Data sources and measures

To assess how spending and prices changed during the amphetamine shortage, we obtained shortage announcement dates for amphetamine products from the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP) and Food and Drug Administration (FDA) websites.19,20 ASHP shortage announcements, primarily intended for healthcare providers, are based on voluntary reports from practitioners and verified with manufacturers. ASHP announced a shortage for several branded and generic amphetamine IR products manufactured by a single company (Teva Pharmaceuticals) on July 22, 2022 (and amphetamine ER products on August 25, 2022) or 2022-Q3; ASHP added more affected generic products, including those manufactured by other companies, to their shortage lists on October 5, 2022. FDA announcements are based on both manufacturer reports of national production disruption and ASHP data. The FDA announced a shortage for multiple amphetamine IR products (involving eight different manufacturers) on October 12, 2022, one quarter after the ASHP announcement. Multiple amphetamine products remained on both the ASHP and FDA shortage lists as of the end of our study period. Recognizing that the timing of shortage announcements did not necessarily correspond to the timing of supply issues, we used the earlier ASHP announcement date (2022-Q3) to explore effects of the reported shortage. Using the FDA date (2022-Q4) instead did not affect our results.

For utilization, spending, and price at the point of dispensing, we used publicly available Medicaid State Drug Utilization Data (SDUD) between 2018-Q1 and 2024-Q4. SDUD was reported quarterly by states to the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) for the Medicaid Drug Rebate Program. Total spending included amounts reimbursed by both Medicaid and non-Medicaid (eg, co-pay, coinsurance, or other private coverage) to pharmacies. Spending and utilization information were provided at the 11-digit National Drug Code (NDC) level by state, payer (fee-for-service or managed care), and calendar quarter. To identify amphetamine-related NDCs and the specific manufacturer, package size, strength, brand/generic status, and formulation (ie, extended release [ER] or immediate release [IR]), we used the Medi-span Electronic Drug File.

We measured the number of prescriptions for each NDC directly from the SDUD data field. We flagged an NDC as a branded product if, in Medi-Span, the NDC was coded as a: brand drug; single-source, co-licensed product; or multi-source, originator product. An NDC was considered to be generic otherwise. ER vs IR formulations were identified by whether the Medi-Span generic product name contained the text “extended release.” We used the manufacturer name variable from Medi-Span to identify manufacturer.

For analysis, we aggregated data to the drug by quarter level. We defined a drug as the distinct combination of manufacturer name, strength, form, package size, and formulation. These drug-level data were used to estimate changes in mean aggregate spending, prescriptions dispensed, and price (ie, gross spending per prescription). To document national trends and to construct price indices, we aggregated gross spending and prescriptions to the national level. To account for potential differences in Medicaid enrollment over time, total spending and the number of prescriptions were divided by the total number of Medicaid enrollees based on information collected from states by CMS.21 Spending measures were converted into 2018-Q1 real dollars using the seasonally adjusted Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U).

Primary analyses

Our primary analyses focused on amphetamine IR products since ASHP-reported shortages for IR products predated reported shortages for amphetamine ER products, and the FDA did not report amphetamine ER products as in shortage. However, Appendix A presents results from analogous analyses of the ER products.

National trends in gross spending, utilization, and prices

To study the potential relationship between the amphetamine shortage and total prescriptions dispensed and total gross spending, we plotted national trends in Medicaid. To study the price level, we constructed a Fisher price index, which is the geometric mean of the Laspeyres and Paasche price indices. The Laspeyres index measures inflation by dividing total spending at current prices and volume constant at their initial level by spending in the base period. The Paasche price index measures inflation by dividing total spending by total spending of the current units at base period prices. Our results did not depend on the particular price index chosen. We also assessed the contribution of price and volume to spending growth following the shortage announcement by adapting the decomposition approach from Aitken et al. (see Appendix B).22 Finally, we constructed and plotted trends in overall market concentration among manufacturers, measured using the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI).

Drug-level association of shortage with prescriptions dispensed, spending, and prices

To assess the statistical significance of these trends, we used an interrupted-time series approach to evaluate the association between the amphetamine shortage and prescriptions dispensed per 100 000 Medicaid enrollees, gross spending per 100 000 Medicaid enrollees, and gross price per prescription. Using drug-level data between 2020-Q2 and 2024-Q4 and a linear regression, we estimated the change in the mean log outcome (eg, prescriptions dispensed, spending, gross price per prescription) as well as change in slope of the mean log outcome in 2022-Q3. See Appendix C for specification details. We interpreted the intercept change in 2022-Q3 as the effect of the amphetamine shortage. We estimated these regressions pooling all amphetamine IR drugs, as well as stratified by manufacturer.

Heterogeneity by formulation and manufacturer

We conducted analogous analyses of trends in prescriptions, gross spending, and price level stratified by manufacturer. We focused on the top three manufacturers by revenue in the SDUD for generic amphetamine IR products in 2021, the year prior to the shortage announcement. For amphetamine IR generics, the top three manufacturers in 2021 were Teva, Lannett, and Sandoz, which were responsible for 44%, 10%, and 8% of expenditures, respectively. We grouped all other generic manufacturers with lower market share into an “other” category of “smaller manufacturers.” There were between 20 and 28 of these “smaller manufacturers” in a given quarter, with an average market share across firms around 2% for amphetamine IR in 2022-Q2. We assessed trends in branded products separately from generics.

Limitations

This study has several data limitations. First, SDUD does not capture all amphetamine spending within Medicaid. In addition to suppressing data with a small number of prescriptions (<11), it is limited to drugs eligible for statutory Medicaid drug rebates and does not include drugs dispensed to Medicaid enrollees by providers or their contracted pharmacies participating in the 340B program. However, there is nothing to suggest that the 340B program would have affected stimulant dispensing in the SDUD differentially around the time of the shortage announcement. Second, the SDUD captured gross payments to pharmacies rather than the average acquisition cost pharmacies paid wholesalers. Therefore, observed price changes could reflect shifts in dispensing fees, pharmacy margins, payment rates, or underlying drug acquisition costs. Third, the use of gross spending per prescription as our price measure did not account for Medicaid rebates and additional supplemental discounts in some states, which would offset a portion of the price changes. Medicaid drug rebates are larger for branded drugs compared with generic drugs; rebates depend on the average manufacturer price or the lowest available price to any provider.23 Generic drugs in Medicaid likely have low rebates; in non-Medicaid, generic drugs have zero or low discounts for generics.24 Fourth, the use of the manufacturer name to identify the manufacturer in the Medi-span data was imperfect due to potentially complex ownership arrangements, likely overstating the number of firms.

This study has two main design limitations. First, the interrupted time series analyses assumed that counterfactual outcomes would follow the pre-shortage trend, meaning that no other aggregate shocks took place at the time of the shortage. Although not directly testable, we examined effects on non-stimulant medications for ADHD (Appendix Table C1) to validate our design. The effects we estimated on non-stimulant gross spending per prescription were not statistically significantly different from zero (Estimate = 0.01, 95% CI: −0.01, 0.02) and were statistically different compared with effects estimated for amphetamines. Second, the official shortage announcement dates used to distinguish our pre-shortage from post-shortage periods provide only limited information on the actual nature of the shortage. Shortage announcements did not necessarily reflect when an actual shortfall in production occurred,25 although our results suggest a shock to amphetamine production did occur for at least some manufacturers, consistent with Currie and Malinovskaya (2026).11

Results

Gross spending, utilization, and prescription prices

Figure 1 plots quarterly gross spending and prescriptions of amphetamine IR products dispensed per 100 000 Medicaid enrollees from 2018 through 2024. Gross spending per 100 000 enrollees declined from $50 018 in 2018-Q1 to $41 456 in 2020-Q1, before sharply dropping to $37 643 in 2020-Q2 shortly after implementation of shutdowns in response to the COVID-19 pandemic national emergency. Gross spending per 100 000 enrollees subsequently increased, reaching $43 176 by mid-2021. Prescriptions dispensed per enrollee also declined in mid-2020, but then returned to pre-2020 levels in 2021 and remained relatively stable through mid-2022, fluctuating around 1400 prescriptions per 100 000 enrollees.

Gross spending and prescriptions per 100 000 enrollees for immediate release amphetamine (2018-2024). Note: Spending was adjusted for inflation and reported in 2018Q1 real dollars. A vertical line at 2020-Q1 denotes the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. A vertical line at 2022-Q3 denotes the start of the shortage period. Source: Medicaid State Drug Utilization Data linked to Medi-span Data.

Following the initial ASHP amphetamine shortage announcement in 2022-Q3, gross spending per 100 000 enrollees remained stable for one quarter then increased sharply to over $42 000 in 2023-Q1, a 7% increase compared with approximately $39 000 in 2022-Q2. Spending remained elevated in 2023, before declining to about $34 000 by the end of 2024. Over the same period, amphetamine IR prescriptions dispensed decreased to a minimum of 1230 per 100 000 enrollees in 2023-Q1. However, by mid-2024, prescriptions recovered and increased to around 1500 prescriptions dispensed per 100 000 enrollees, above the pre-shortage peak.

The post-shortage increases in expenditures and declining prescriptions dispensed were explained by a spike in prescription prices, which increased by 12% in 2023-Q1 relative to 2022-Q4 or 6% relative to 2022-Q2 (Figure 2). The price level then returned to pre-shortage levels in 2024. More formally decomposing the change in gross spending per enrollee over this period showed that changes in generic prices were primarily responsible for the growth in Medicaid spending on amphetamine IR products in 2023 (Appendix Figure B1). Between 2022-Q2 and 2023-Q1, spending per 100 000 enrollees increased by approximately $2,700, up from $39 400 per 100 000 enrollees in 2022-Q2. Rising generic prices contributed roughly $5300 to this increase, while declining generic volume offset it by $3800; changes in the price and quantity of branded medications accounted for the remainder.

Interrupted time series analyses results at the drug-level, using 2022-Q3 as the start of the shortage, mirror these descriptive findings. Prescription prices increased a statistically significant 14% (column 1 of Appendix Table C2). Regression estimates indicate that generic drug prices increased similarly (Estimate = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.11, 0.17), while those for brand drugs did not (Estimate = −0.06, 95% CI: −0.09, −0.03). Total amphetamine IR prescriptions per enrollee declined by 24% (95% CI: −0.47, −0.01) after 2022-Q3 (column 2 of Appendix Table C2). While the drug-level change in spending per enrollee at 2022-Q3 was not statistically significant (Estimate = −0.10, 95% CI: −0.34, 0.14; column 3 of Appendix Table C2), estimates are consistent with some degree of increased spending.

Market concentration and utilization by manufacturer

Prior to the shortage, the market for amphetamine IR was becoming less concentrated, with HHI decreasing from just under 3000 in 2018-Q1 to just above 1000 in 2022-Q2 (see Figure 3). Following the shortage announcement, HHI increased to over 2000 at the start of 2023 before returning to pre-shortage levels by 2024.

Consistent with these changes to market concentration, smaller manufacturers were increasing their share of Medicaid dispensing prior to the shortage announcement but experienced a sharp decline in dispensed prescriptions at the start of 2023 before recovering by the start of 2024 (Figure 3). Dispensing for drugs manufactured by Teva, the largest firm and manufacturer initially reporting supply issues, declined slightly at the end of 2022, but increased at the start of 2023, corresponding to when other firms were dispensing the least. Both Lannett and Sandoz, which had smaller market share than Teva, had relatively flat trends in aggregate dispensing before and after the announced shortage date.

Regression estimates of the effect of the shortage on prescriptions dispensed per 100 000 enrollees vary across manufacturers. Smaller manufacturers experienced a decline of 26% (95% CI: −0.56, 0.04; P < 0.1) after 2022-Q3 (column 2, Appendix Table C2), similar to the estimate when pooled across all manufacturers. In contrast, the estimated effect on generic Teva prescriptions dispensed per enrollee is small and positive at 3% and not statistically significant (95% CI: −0.06, 0.13).

Prescription prices by manufacturer

Figure 4 displays Fisher price indices for amphetamine IR by manufacturer. Since not all firms were in the market in 2018, these indices are normalized to one in 2022-Q2. Similar to the aggregate price index, price indices for generic manufacturers were on a downward trajectory prior to the shortage. All price indices for generic manufacturers experienced a large jump-starting in the 2023-Q1, which corresponded with the largest decline in prescriptions dispensed. The increase in prescription prices in 2023-Q1 relative to 2022-Q2 for Teva's generic products was less than that for smaller generic manufacturers: a 6% point increase compared with a 10% point increase, respectively. Finally, the price level for branded amphetamine IR was relatively flat between 2018 and 2024; there was no noticeable deviation following the shortage announcement.

Discussion and conclusion

The recent amphetamine shortage, first announced in 2022, has received substantial attention for its potential to disrupt treatment for millions of patients with ADHD, but relatively few empirical evaluations have examined its effects on health care utilization and spending. We found that, despite the significant aggregate decline in dispensed prescriptions for amphetamine products during the shortage, Medicaid gross spending on amphetamine drugs increased. Total expenditures increased due to price increases across amphetamine IR drugs made by generic manufacturers. We estimated that prices, measured as gross spending per prescription, increased by 6% to 12% for approximately one year before reverting to pre-shortage levels. Although prescription prices increased across all generic manufacturers, we found that volume declines came primarily from smaller manufacturers; large manufacturers appeared relatively unaffected or recovered quickly. These findings are consistent with, but not dispositive of, recent evidence on ADHD precursor declines prior to the shortage and claims by some smaller manufacturers citing shortages of an active ingredient as causing their supply issues.11,20

Our estimated effects on gross spending per prescription were similar in magnitude to those estimated by Dave et al.14 across a broader set of generic drug shortages. The uneven effects on volume by manufacturer may illustrate the potential underinvestment of smaller firms to insulate themselves from supply chain disruptions, an issue that is well-documented in the broader market for generic drugs.26

Given that, the patterns we find are consistent with the existing literature on generic drug shortages, our results do not suggest that the price and utilization effects of the amphetamine shortage were due to particular regulatory features of the stimulant market, such as DEA quotas. However, to uncover how the DEA quotas explicitly affected the stimulant shortage or shortages of other controlled substances, more transparency is needed around how quotas are determined and modified in response to market concerns. Future research could expand our analysis to look at other market segments, such as commercial insurance and Medicare, examine other controlled and non-controlled substances, and assess whether changes in dispensing during shortages are correlated with manufacturer size or degree of market competition.
