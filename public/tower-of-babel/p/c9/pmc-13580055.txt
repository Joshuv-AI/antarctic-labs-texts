Effectiveness of a Preschool Vision Screening Program for Early Detection of Refractive Errors: Evidence From a Prospective Study in Uzbekistan
Authors: Shakhnoza T. Iskandarova, Malika M. Miralimova, Nodira R. Yangieva, Nigora S. Mukhamedova, Salima K. Nazarova, Bakhriddin M. Eshdavlatov, Rano A. Zakirkhodjaeva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Preschool vision screening was feasible under routine urban conditions.•A substantial proportion of children had previously undetected visual impairment.•Screening demonstrated high referral completion within a preschool-based model.•Refractive errors were identified in a significant proportion of screened children.•Questionnaire and environmental data provided contextual public health insights.

INTRODUCTION

Uncorrected refractive error is a major, largely preventable cause of childhood visual impairment worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.1,2 It can adversely affect learning and long-term quality of life, whereas delayed detection in early childhood increases the risk of amblyopia and persistent visual dysfunction.1, 2, 3

The preschool age provides a critical window for early detection, because routine eye examinations are uncommon, and visual deficits often remain undetected until school entry.4 International guidelines recommend vision screening before formal education, typically between 3 and 6 years.4,7 In this setting, visual acuity testing with instrument-based refraction is widely used as a practical screening approach.5,6

Many health systems rely on passive, symptom-driven pediatric eye care, leading to delayed detection in preschool children, who often cannot report visual difficulties. Limited awareness, a lack of structured screening, and restricted access further reduce timely care.6

Although refractive errors are well documented, evidence on preschool screening performance in routine settings remains limited, particularly in middle-income countries.2,9 Organized screening can detect previously unrecognized refractive errors,5,6 but variations in protocols limit comparability, and noncycloplegic refraction requires careful evaluation.8,9

In Uzbekistan, data remain limited, and detection largely relies on passive care, highlighting a gap in early identification. To contextualize public health value, patterns of detection without organized screening may be considered. A descriptive comparison with routine ophthalmic presentations can reflect the stage at diagnosis in the absence of systematic programs.10

In this context, this study evaluated a preschool vision screening program in urban Uzbekistan. Screening coverage, diagnostic yield, and referral completion were assessed to evaluate the program’s performance and feasibility for the early detection of refractive errors.

METHODS

Study Population

A prospective, institution-based vision screening study was conducted from January 2025 to January 2026 in Tashkent, Uzbekistan, as a program-level evaluation of a preschool screening initiative. The program was implemented with authorization from the Ministry of Health and in collaboration with local healthcare providers and preschool institutions.

A total of 24 state preschools across all 12 administrative districts (2 per district) were included to ensure urban coverage. The study population comprised children aged 5–7 years attending the participating institutions. All children who were present on screening days with parental consent were eligible; no additional exclusion criteria were applied.

Sampling was determined by the program structure, with preschools as the cluster units and all eligible children invited. In total, 1,284 children were screened, providing stable descriptive estimates of screening coverage, referral rates, and diagnostic yield.

Administrative permission was obtained from the Ministry of Preschool and School Education (Approval Number 05-43-373, January 22, 2025). In accordance with national regulations, separate ethics committee approval was not required. All procedures were noninvasive and were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians.

Measures

Vision screening was conducted by 2 trained investigators (a PhD candidate and an ophthalmologist, DSc) following a standardized protocol. Interobserver repeatability was assessed to ensure consistency, and both investigators were trained in the use of the instrument prior to data collection.

On average, 55–60 children were screened per day. All instruments were operated according to the manufacturer’s instructions and were calibrated before use. Primary vision screening was conducted in preschool classrooms under standardized lighting conditions. Visual acuity was assessed monocularly at 5 m using an age-appropriate optotype chart (Orlova), with the right eye tested first. The end point was the smallest line correctly identified. No habitual correction was used. Screening was performed between 9:00 AM and 12:00 PM.

Noncycloplegic autorefraction was performed using a handheld autorefractor (Plusoptix S12 Vision Screener, Plusoptix GmbH, Nuremberg, Germany). Screening outcomes were based on visual acuity in the worse eye; children with borderline (Vis=0.8–0.9) or reduced (Vis<0.8) visual acuity were classified as screen positive and referred for a confirmatory examination.

Children who were unable to complete the screening were excluded. Parents were informed of the results, and follow-up was encouraged through communication with caregivers and preschool staff. Confirmatory ophthalmic examinations were conducted at a specialized pediatric eye care center in Tashkent and included visual acuity testing and objective refraction.

Noncycloplegic refraction was performed initially, with the refractive error confirmed using objective refraction before and after cycloplegia. Refractive errors were classified by spherical equivalent: myopia (≤ −0.50 D; mild > −3.0 D, moderate −3.25 to −6.0 D, high > −6.0 D), hyperopia (≥ +2.0 D; mild < +2.0 D, moderate +2.25 to +5.0 D, high > +5.0 D), and astigmatism (≥ 1.0 D; mild < 1.0 D, moderate 1.25–3.0 D, high > 3.0 D). Other conditions included amblyopia (n=44), strabismus (n=3), and 1 case of anophthalmia.

A structured parental questionnaire, based on standard clinical history taking, collected data on family history, prior eye examinations, screen exposure, outdoor time, near-work distance, lighting conditions, visual symptoms, parental awareness, and barriers to care. The questionnaire was distributed to parents of all screened children (n=1,284) through preschools and was completed on site or at home; 583 responses (45.4%) were included in the analysis. The questionnaire is provided in the Appendix Materials (available online). Information on prior ophthalmologic examination was obtained from parental responses.

Data were analyzed at the aggregate level. A composite risk score based on refractive, behavioral, environmental, and hereditary factors was used to classify children into low-, moderate-, and high-risk groups (Table A.1, available online). The findings were used for population-level analysis only and were not linked to individual screening or clinical outcomes.

Indoor illumination in preschool classrooms was measured using a digital lux meter under routine conditions and compared descriptively with the recommended standards. Primary outcomes were screening coverage, positive screening rate, and diagnostic yield of confirmed refractive errors. Secondary outcomes included referral completion, distribution of refractive errors, questionnaire-based risk factors, and classroom illumination.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was descriptive, reflecting the program-level design; no hypothesis testing was performed. The sample size was determined by the operational scope of the screening program. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages with 95% CIs. No adjustment for clustering was applied. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and Microsoft Excel 2019 without imputation for missing data.

RESULTS

A total of 1,284 preschool children aged 5–7 years completed primary vision screening. The mean age of the screened cohort was 6.39±0.58 years. Children aged 6 years constituted the largest proportion of the sample (62.2%), followed by those aged 7 years (26.3%) and 5 years (11.5%). Boys accounted for 51.4% of the screened population. Completed parental questionnaires suitable for analysis were returned for 583 children (45.4%). Findings from the questionnaires are summarized in Table A.2 (available online). It should be noted that questionnaire-based findings represent a subset of the study population and are distinct from the full screened cohort. Although only 0.7% of children in the overall screened population (n=1,284) had previously undergone an ophthalmologic examination, as determined during screening, questionnaire-based responses reflect parental reports within the subset of returned questionnaires.

At the time of the survey, 25.0% of children in the questionnaire subset had never undergone an ophthalmologic examination. Questionnaire-based risk stratification classified 9.3% of children as high risk and 47.9% as moderate risk at the population level. More than half of the parents (59.2%) demonstrated high awareness of the importance of early detection of visual problems, and 75.6% expressed positive perceptions of preschool vision screening programs.

Reported preventive practices included limiting children’s screen time (67.1%) and scheduling regular eye examinations (34.1%). The most commonly reported barriers to seeking ophthalmologic care were the absence of perceived complaints (40.3%), a lack of information (29.9%), limited time (28.0%), and restricted access to specialists (22.1%). Questionnaire findings were used exclusively for population-level descriptive assessments and were not applied to individual screening or referral decisions.

Indoor illumination was measured using a luxmeter (Testo 540, Testo SE & Co. KGaA, Titisee-Neustadt, Germany). The distribution of lighting levels across the classrooms is presented in Table A.1 (available online). Most classrooms demonstrated illumination levels below the recommended standards for visual tasks. Specifically, a substantial proportion of classrooms had lighting levels below 400 lux, whereas fewer met the recommended threshold of ≥500 lux. These findings were considered in the descriptive evaluation of the environmental conditions potentially affecting visual health.

DISCUSSION

This study provides program-level evidence on preschool vision screening under routine urban conditions in Uzbekistan, demonstrating substantial detection of previously unrecognized visual problems, with high referral completion and operational feasibility. A total of 25.9% of children were screen positive, and 13.7% had confirmed refractive errors. These findings are consistent with reports from other settings, where a significant proportion of refractive errors remain undetected without systematic screening.3,5,6 The observed diagnostic yield may reflect the inclusion of borderline visual acuity thresholds, consistent with screening strategies that prioritize sensitivity.4,7

Referral completion reached 90.4%, indicating strong program acceptability. This may reflect structured implementation within preschool settings, direct communication with families, and accessible follow-up care. In contrast, passive, symptom-driven pathways are associated with delayed presentation and lower service utilization.2

The distribution of refractive errors—predominantly hyperopia, followed by myopia and astigmatism—is consistent with patterns reported in other preschool populations.6,7 These findings support the importance of early detection in this age group.

Parental questionnaire data indicate that nearly one quarter of children had never undergone an eye examination prior to screening, despite generally positive attitudes. Similar gaps between awareness and service utilization have been reported and may reflect structural barriers, limited access, or an absence of symptoms.10 Preschool-based screening may improve access and population coverage.

Most classrooms had illumination levels below recommended standards, providing contextual information on visual environments in preschool settings and supporting the consideration of environmental factors in vision health strategies.9 Focusing on children aged 5–7 years is appropriate, because this period coincides with increasing visual demands before school entry. Screening at this stage enables the early identification of visual risks. This study represents one of the first program-level evaluations of preschool vision screening in Uzbekistan and provides context-specific evidence to inform its implementation in similar settings. Strengths include the large city-wide sample across all districts, high screening coverage, standardized testing conditions, and the assessment of program-level indicators relevant to public health and service delivery.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Children who did not meet referral criteria were not examined, precluding assessment of screening sensitivity and specificity. Noncycloplegic refraction may have introduced measurement variability, particularly for hyperopia, although this reflects real-world practice. Screening was based on visual acuity and was not designed to detect other conditions, such as amblyopia, strabismus, or accommodative disorders. Questionnaire data were available for a subset of participants and were analyzed descriptively, limiting individual-level inference. Finally, the study was conducted in urban preschools, which may limit generalizability to rural or underserved populations.

CONCLUSIONS

Preschool vision screening under routine urban conditions was feasible and effective. Among 1,284 children aged 5–7 years, 25.9% were screen positive, and 13.7% had confirmed refractive errors, most of whom were without prior ophthalmologic examination. Referral completion was 90.4%, indicating strong acceptance of and linkage to care. Questionnaire findings revealed an unmet need for early eye care despite positive attitudes. These results support the integration of preschool vision screening into health programs to improve early detection and prevent avoidable visual impairment.
