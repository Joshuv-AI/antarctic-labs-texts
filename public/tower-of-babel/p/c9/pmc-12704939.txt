Climate change drives habitat specialization and local extirpation, causing niche reduction in an endemic chipmunk
Authors: Hailey N Jacobson, Jennifer K Frey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change is a driver of species extirpation, particularly for local endemics. The niche reduction hypothesis provides a conceptual framework to understand how the realized niche of a declining species is reduced from its historical niche to its contemporary niche due to threats. The Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk (Neotamias quadrivittatus australis) is an example of an endemic montane mammal for which climate change may be a threat. We used occupancy models developed for historical sites to evaluate the extent of extirpations and their causes. We used occupancy models developed for random sites to evaluate the contemporary distribution and realized niche. Our results suggest that the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk has undergone broadscale extirpation (extirpated from 64% of sites) with a lower elevation range boundary contraction of 262 m in the past 30 yr. Chipmunks were extirpated from hotter and more arid biotic communities and persisted in drainages that provide cooler and more mesic conditions. Chipmunks avoided conifer forest, which were an integral part of their historical niche, suggesting that the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk may have undergone a reduction in its realized niche. The Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk has become a de facto “functional habitat specialist” restricted to climate change refugia, further increasing its vulnerability to climate change and other threats.

Climate change is regarded as a potent factor currently driving species extinctions, and the impact of climate change to biodiversity loss is predicted to increase into the future (Thomas et al. 2004; Loarie et al. 2008; Isaac 2009). Some of the organisms most vulnerable to climate change are cold-adapted species that exist at high latitudes or elevations (Pacifici et al. 2018) and island endemics (Leclerc et al. 2020). Like species occurring on oceanic islands, many montane species are restricted to “sky islands” of habitat surrounded by unsuitable environments that limit dispersal and result in geographic isolation, local environmental adaptations, endemism, and heightened vulnerability to climate change (La Sorte and Jetz 2010). Sky island ecosystems are expected to be reduced to higher elevations by increasing temperatures, leading to habitat loss and potential extinctions of endemic taxa (La Sorte and Jetz 2010). Although recent research has focused on predicting the future response of species to climate change (e.g., Chen et al. 2011), the extent of contemporary extirpations has been poorly studied except primarily in some well-studied taxa (e.g., birds) where there are more robust long-term monitoring datasets (e.g., Palacio et al. 2020).

A reduction in the realized niche of a species—defined as the occupied portion of the environmental space where a species can theoretically occur—can result in a decrease in distribution, genetic diversity, adaptive capacities, and tolerance to threats (Holt 2009; Scheele et al. 2017). Therefore, it is imperative that researchers understand processes behind niche reduction. The recently proposed “niche reduction hypothesis” (NRH) provides a framework to understand how a declining realized niche is reduced from the historical niche—defined as the realized niche before decline—to the contemporary niche—defined as the remaining occupied portion of the historical niche after a decline—due to threats (e.g., habitat loss, competition, invasive species, disease, climate change; Scheele et al. 2017). These threats can be amplified or reduced due to environmental, biotic, and evolutionary processes and reduced by tolerance of a species to the threats, resulting in heterogeneity of niche reductions across its declining distribution (Scheele et al. 2017). The NRH can aid in identifying possible restoration sites, environmental refugia, and reintroduction areas, thereby improving the management of a species in decline (Scheele et al. 2017).

Habitat selection reflects behavioral choices that result in disproportionate use of a resource compared to its availability (Hall et al. 1997) and describes an important facet of a realized niche. Habitat selection is hierarchical in nature, and the largest scale—first-order selection—is the selection of sites across landscapes (Johnson 1980). Occupancy modeling is a statistical framework used to analyze and understand distribution and landscape-scale habitat selection using detection–non-detection data to estimate site-level occupancy probability while accounting for imperfect detection (MacKenzie et al. 2002). Researchers have estimated extirpation by conducting contemporary resurveys at historical sites and used occupancy variables in the model to infer causes of extirpation (e.g., Wineland et al. 2019) and thus potential processes behind niche reduction. On the other hand, species distribution models (SDMs) based on “presence only” occurrence data typically have been used to test shifts in species distribution and niche breadth under the NRH framework (e.g., McDonald et al. 2018; Moore et al. 2019; Alexander et al. 2022). However, SDMs focus on the correlation between species occurrences and environmental variables while assuming perfect detection and do not directly address a species behavioral choice regarding the use of environmental variables, and therefore, may not be the most robust method for accurately determining species distribution and niche breadth compared to occupancy modeling.

An example of an endemic montane sky island taxon for which extirpations may be occurring is the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk (Neotamias quadrivittatus australis). The subspecies is endemic to the Organ Mountains in southcentral New Mexico, and it represents the southernmost population of the species and is highly isolated from other populations (Patterson 1980). The Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk may be at risk of extirpation because of patchy and marginal habitat. The US Army considers the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk as a species at-risk of being federally listed in accordance with the Endangered Species Act (ESA; Wilhoit et al. 2016) and it is currently listed as threatened under the New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act due to its vulnerability as an endemic subspecies living in a small, isolated habitat (NMDGF 2016). These chipmunks are considered to be threatened by habitat loss due to human activities, wildfire, and climate change (NMDGF 2016; ­Schweiger and Frey 2021).

Historical studies described the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk as associated with an array of biotic communities including coniferous forest and woodland, for example, dominated by Ponderosa Pine (Pinus ponderosa), Twoneedle Pinyon Pine (Pinus edulis), and Alligator Juniper (Juniperus deppeana); montane scrub, for example, dominated by Gambel Oak (Quercus gambelii) and Alderleaf Mountain Mahogany (Cercocarpus montanus); chaparral, for example, dominated by Sonoran Scrub Oak (Quercus turbinella); Madrean oak woodlands, for example, dominated by Gray Oak (Quercus grisea); and arroyos, that is, drainages that rarely have flowing water, including at relatively low elevation (Patterson 1980; Sullivan 1996; NMNHP 1998). A SDM based on historical occurrence records indicated that these chipmunks selected high elevation areas with increased spring precipitation, increased rock cover, and increased coniferous forest and woodland, chaparral, and lower montane riparian land cover types (Frey and Kopp 2013). However, a more recent study of contemporary habitat selection at the home range, within home range, and microhabitat scales reached fundamentally different conclusions (­Schweiger et al. 2021). Schweiger et al. (2021) determined that chipmunks avoided coniferous forests and instead selected relatively shady and steep areas in drainages that create a cool, wet micro-climate and contained montane scrub. This fundamental difference between historical and contemporary habitat use suggests that the subspecies may have undergone a niche reduction.

We used occupancy modeling to evaluate extirpation, landscape scale habitat selection, and niche reduction of the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk. Our objectives were to (i) determine the extent of any extirpations, their causes, and range shifts; (ii) to determine the contemporary niche and distribution; and (iii) to determine whether extirpations (if they occurred) were due to niche reduction or habitat loss. Therefore, we hypothesized that extirpations of the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk have occurred in more arid biotic communities and areas with warmer microclimates, resulting in a range shift. We hypothesized that contemporary first order habitat selection will be consistent with lower scales of habitat selection similar to results in Schweiger et al. (2021) and therefore that chipmunks will select areas with cooler microclimates and montane scrub, while avoiding conifers on the landscape scale. We hypothesized that N. q. australis has experienced niche reduction rather than habitat loss.

Methods

Sample area.

We conducted the study in the Organ Mountains, Doña Ana County, located in south-central New Mexico (Fig. 1). The mountain range is part of the Madrean sky island archipelago and is exceptionally rugged and difficult to access, with needle-like spires rising to the highest peak (2,743 m; Fig. 1D). Emblematic of sky islands, montane ecosystems in the Organ Mountains are highly isolated from those in other mountain ranges by the surrounding lower elevation Chihuahuan Desert, and display relic populations and endemism (Ditto and Frey 2007). The climate of the Organ Mountains is characterized by mild winters, hot summers, and monsoonal rains from July to September (Schweiger and Frey 2021). Daily mean temperatures range from 6.6 °C in the winter to 24.7 °C in the summer (Schweiger and Frey 2021). In the past 50 yr, the climate in the region has experienced increased temperature and drought severity (Fig. 2).

The general vegetation pattern of the Organ Mountains follows an elevation gradient (Dick-Peddie and Moir 1970; NMNHP 1994; ­Supplementary Data SD1). The low-elevation foothills communities are Chihuahuan semi-arid grassland and warm temperate Madrean woodland communities. At mid-elevations are 2 shrubland communities—chaparral and montane scrub. Chaparral is found on relatively xeric slopes and is dominated by drought tolerant shrubs. Montane scrub is found on cooler and more mesic slopes and is dominated by moderately drought- and cold-tolerant shrubs. Sotol (Dasylirion spp.) occurs across the elevational gradient from semi-arid grassland to montane scrub. The next highest elevation zones are the coniferous and mixed woodland communities. Warm temperate areas have mixed woodland, whereas cold temperate areas have Rocky Mountain woodland. The highest elevation community is cold temperate forest (i.e., montane coniferous forest). Riparian communities are restricted to drainages with more frequent water. Arroyo riparian communities occur in dry gullies at lower elevation.

The majority of the Organ Mountains are managed by 3 federal entities: Fort Bliss Military Reservation (FBMR); White Sands Missile Range (WSMR); and Bureau of Land Management (BLM; Fig. 1C). FBMR manages the southeastern portion of the range and the majority of habitat for chipmunks (Frey and Kopp 2013). The WSMR portion includes Texas Canyon on the west-central edge of the mountains. On both military bases human access is restricted to military activity (e.g., heavy artillery ranges, military aircraft flights, hiking, and road construction). The northwestern and southwestern portions of the range are managed by the BLM. Most of the BLM portion is within the recently designated Organ Mountains Wilderness Area and the Organ Mountains-Desert Peaks National Monument and is used for recreation (e.g., hiking, rock climbing, and horseback riding) and livestock grazing. Private land occurs in the southwestern and northern portions of the range.

Overview of the study design.

We evaluated the extent of any extirpations by resurveying sites where chipmunks were historically detected (Fig. 3). An occupancy model (i.e., survey adequacy model) was created that focused on the detection probabilities of our surveys and used the detection probabilities to infer if sites were truly extirpated versus a false negative due to survey error (Fig. 3). We also created an occupancy model (i.e., extirpation causes model) to test covariates that we hypothesized influenced chipmunk extirpation or persistence (Fig. 3). Persistence probability was defined as the occupancy probability derived from surveys at historical sites. We evaluated elevational range shifts by determining the lowest and highest elevations for historical records and comparing them to sites where we detected chipmunks.

Schematic overview of methods used to evaluate extirpation and the contemporary niche and distribution of Neotamias quadrivittatus australis in the Organ Mountains, Doña Ana County, New Mexico, United States, October 2020 to December 2021.

We evaluated contemporary landscape-scale habitat selection by creating an occupancy model (i.e., contemporary habitat selection model) based on surveys at randomly selected sites across the mountain range and using covariates that we hypothesized influenced habitat use (Fig. 3). This occupancy model was interpreted as the contemporary realized niche of a chipmunk. We also created an occupancy model (i.e., contemporary spatial model) using surveys at random sites but based only on spatial data, which allowed us to project the model across the study area to predict contemporary distribution and habitat suitability (Fig. 3).

To determine if extirpations were due to niche reduction or habitat loss, we compared the contemporary niche with descriptions of the historical niche. If habitat loss caused extirpations, we expected habitat selection to remain the same, but that chipmunks would avoid conditions where habitat has changed. If niche reduction caused extirpations, we expected contemporary habitat selection to be restricted to a subset of that used in the historical period and that chipmunks would avoid habitat formerly used. Details of the hypotheses and predictions for detection and occupancy models are given in Supplementary Data SD1.

Chipmunk surveys.

We used remote cameras to survey for chipmunks because they are more logistically feasible to transport and use in a rugged wilderness area than live traps, they are noninvasive and therefore prevent trap related mortality, and they can be deployed at multiple sites simultaneously and continuously without constant maintenance. We defined a survey as a 24-h period (midnight to midnight). We used results of a pilot study to determine that surveys conducted in spring and fall had highest detection probabilities, requiring the cameras be deployed a minimum of 13 consecutive days in the spring and 20 consecutive days in the fall to achieve at least 0.90 and 0.85 probability of detecting a chipmunk at a site, respectively (Jacobson 2024). We deployed 1 remote motion-sensing camera (either Reconyx HP2 or PC850, Reconyx, Holmen, ­Wisconsin) at each site (Supplementary Data SD1). All photographs were identified to species by 2 independent observers and cataloged using Colorado Parks and Wildlife Photo Warehouse (Newkirk 2016). No other chipmunk species occurred in the study area. We collected covariate data at each site in the field and from a geographic information system (GIS; Supplementary Data SD1). All procedures were approved by the New Mexico State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (permit # 2020-022) and followed guidelines of the American Society of Mammalogists (Sikes et al. 2016).

Statistical analysis.

Modeling approach

We used information theory (Burnham and Anderson 2002) for model selection using a multi-stage approach (Morin et al. 2020). We compared models using corrected Akaike’s Information Criterion for small sample sizes (AICc; Burnham and Anderson 2002). We conducted all statistical analyses in program R (R Development Core Team 2012) and the occupancy analyses using the “unmarked” (Fiske and Chandler 2020) and “MuMIn” packages (Bartoń 2020).

We limited each set of a priori models by the number of sites surveyed for the analysis (i.e., maximum of 61 models for extirpation analyses and 162 models for contemporary habitat selection analyses). To avoid overparameterization, we followed the “rule of thumb” of limiting occupancy models to no more than 1 parameter per 10 sites (Pavlou et al. 2015); therefore, we limited the survey adequacy detection model and the extirpation causes model to 4 variables and the contemporary habitat selection models to 14 variables, as we included the detection (p) and occupancy (Ψ) intercepts as 1 parameter each. Variables were created to address our predictions both in the field and GIS (Tables 1 and 2; Supplementary Data SD1). We considered variables correlated if Pearson’s correlation coefficient ≥ |0.7| and removed the variable with lower AICc.

We followed the methods outlined in Leroux (2019) to screen for models with uninformative parameters and removed those models from consideration (Arnold 2010; Leroux 2019; Sutherland et al. 2023). We calculated variance inflation factor (VIF) to assess multicollinearity of variables within each model, and if there was multicollinearity (i.e., VIF > 5), we removed the model from consideration. We considered all remaining models as competitive. We averaged across models if there were multiple competitive models (Arnold 2010). We calculated the confidence intervals for the 2 intercepts on the probability scale using an inverse-link transformation (i.e., R function plogis), while we did not transform the covariates when calculating confidence intervals.

Extirpation models

We compiled a list of historical records of Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunks from 1903 to 2019 (Supplementary Data SD2) using published literature, unpublished reports, museum records in Arctos (2021), GBIF (2025), VertNet (Constable et al. 2010), museum records maintained by New Mexico State University, iNaturalist (2021), and a database of occurrences maintained by FBMR. We eliminated redundant records and retained only the original data source. Based on our interpretation of the locality description, we georeferenced records using Delorme USA TOPO version 10 topographical maps (DeLorme US Software 2012). We ranked all records by reliability and precision using an adaptation of the system created by Frey et al. (2013; Supplementary Data SD3). We considered records that had reliability of class A-D and precision of class 1 to 2 as acceptable. We rarefied the acceptable historical sites by 120 m and conducted surveys at 61 acceptable historical sites. The acceptable historical records were from 1973 to 2019. We surveyed the 61 acceptable historical sites from 1 October 2020 to 8 December 2020, 25 February 2021 to 2 July 2021, and 10 October 2021 to 8 December 2021 (­Supplementary Data SD3). To the best of our knowledge, we deployed cameras within 90 m (i.e., similar to the radius of a chipmunk home range; Schweiger et al. 2021) of the acceptable historical sites.

To evaluate the adequacy of surveys at the historical sites and to prevent false-negative error, we used a null occupancy model with a maximum of 4 detection covariates (i.e., survey adequacy model; Supplementary Data SD3). We focused solely on the detection model because our most important concern was whether a historical site was truly extirpated or if we failed to detect a chipmunk that was at the site. To evaluate causes of extirpation, we were more concerned with the variables on occupancy (i.e., persistence) since these tested our predictions about the causes of extirpation. Consequently, for the extirpation causes model, we restricted the detection model to a single variable so that we still accounted for imperfect detection and used a maximum of 3 covariates on persistence (i.e., extirpation causes model; Supplementary Data SD3).

We calculated the overall detection probability of surveys at each historical site based on the survey adequacy model and calculated persistence probability based on the extirpation causes model. We categorized each site based on detection or lack of detection of chipmunks and detection and persistence probabilities. We estimated a naïve extirpation rate as the percentage of acceptable historical sites where chipmunks were not detected. We calculated conservative extirpation rates as the percent of historical sites with detection probability > 0.70, 0.75, 0.80, 0.85, 0.90, and 0.95, and no chipmunk detected (Supplementary Data SD3). To calculate extirpation probability, we subtracted persistence probability from 1.00. Using the predict function and the survey adequacy detection model and extirpation causes model, we calculated the mean daily detection probability and mean extirpation probability. We evaluated range shifts by comparing the maximum and minimum elevations of historical locations versus elevations in our surveys. We considered historical records without a chipmunk detection during our surveys to be a true absence if detection probability > 0.85 (MacKenzie et al. 2018).

Contemporary habitat selection models

To identify survey locations, we used a map of 4 land cover types considered important to chipmunks based on vegetation and topography (i.e., deep drainage, montane coniferous forest, Rocky Mountain woodland, and montane scrub; Jacobson 2024), and we identified areas accessible to humans, which we defined as slope < 45° (accessible area). Within the accessible area, we stratified random sites within each land cover type in proportion to the range-wide distribution of each type. We spaced sites > 120 m, which exceeded the radius of the home range (105 m) of a chipmunk (Schweiger et al. 2021). Spacing sites for total independence based on potential chipmunk dispersal (1,146 m; McKibben 2022) was logistically infeasible because we would not be able to survey > 100 sites in the 4 land cover types. We interpreted occupancy probability as probability of use because of lack of strict independence. We surveyed 161 random sites from 25 February 2021 to 2 July 2021 (Supplementary Data SD4).

We determined the best detection model for surveys at random sites by using a maximum of 6 detection covariates and a null occupancy model (Supplementary Data SD4). To determine the contemporary niche, we used the best detection model along with a maximum of 8 occupancy covariates derived from field-collected and GIS data (i.e., contemporary habitat selection model; Supplementary Data SD4). To determine contemporary distribution, we used the top detection model along with a maximum of 8 GIS variables (i.e., contemporary spatial model; Supplementary Data SD4). We projected the top contemporary spatial model to the study area. We calculated the area of predicted habitat at 0.75, 0.5, and 0.25 probability of use. We defined habitat patches as interconnected areas with a probability of use ≥ 0.5 and considered complete or partial barriers of poor-quality habitat as areas with a probability of use < 0.5. Using the predict function and the top models for contemporary habitat selection analyses, we calculated the mean daily detection probability and mean probability of use.

Results

Extirpation models.

We detected chipmunks at 22 of 61 acceptable historical sites (naïve persistence = 36.1%; Supplementary Data SD3). There was 1 competitive survey adequacy model (Table 3) that included escape cover (boulder and shrub cover near the camera), scent lure age, and chipmunk abundance (Euclidean distance to major drainage, presence of rock outcrop). Detection probability increased with increasing boulder and shrub cover near the camera and Euclidean distance to base of deep drainage or cliff, decreasing scent lure age, and presence of a rock outcropping (Table 4; Fig. 4). Discussion

Extirpation and range contraction.

Results suggest that the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk has been extirpated from 64% of resurveyed historical sites. This extirpation rate is higher than that estimated for other montane small ­mammals—for example, Urocitellus beldingi in the Sierra Nevada Mountains, California (42%; Morelli et al. 2012); and Ochotona princeps (15% and 28%; Stewart et al. 2015 and Beever et al. 2003, respectively, in the Sierra Nevada Mountains, California and Great Basin area, Nevada). In addition, the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk has also undergone a pronounced range contraction. In the past 30 yr, it has retracted upslope 87.0 m per decade—262 m total. The lowest elevation historical location (1,511 m) was from the late 1990s, suggesting that the range contraction has occurred in the last 30 yr. Additional evidence for a range shift is demonstrated by the contemporary spatial model that predicted probability of use near zero for historical locations at low elevations and the significant (P < 0.0001) increase in persistence probability with increased elevation (Fig. 6). The lower elevation boundary range contraction is similar to upward shifts of 86.5 m per decade estimated for other exclusively montane species in the San Juan Mountains and Front Range, Colorado (McCain et al. 2021). However, this upward shift outpaces known contractions of other chipmunk species, except one. Moritz et al. (2008) determined that N. senex, N. alpinus, N. quadrimaculatus, and N. speciosus contracted upslope 100.7 m, 62.9 m, 5.0 m, and 12.8 m per decade, respectively, in Yosemite National Park, California. Rowe et al. (2010) determined that N. umbrinus contracted upslope 25.0 m per decade in the Ruby ­Mountains, Nevada.

While responses to climate change can be varied, including contractions, shifts, and expansions (e.g., Moritz et al. 2008; Rowe et al. 2010; McCain et al. 2021), montane species commonly shift upwards (Chen et al. 2011). However, expansion of the upper elevation range boundary may not be possible for the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk because the mountain range has limited height and the highest elevations may be uninhabitable terrain. The Organ Mountains are relatively smaller and shorter than other mountain ranges such as the Sierra Nevada and Rocky Mountains, where small mammals have experienced upward range shifts. Further, many of the highest elevations of the Organ Mountains were predicted to have low-quality habitat (i.e., flat ridgelines). Flat areas are exposed to more direct sunlight, and this increased exposure to solar radiation contributes to higher temperatures (Estevo et al. 2022) and may result in areas that are relatively arid. Patterson (1980) thought that chipmunks did not inhabit the rock spires at the highest elevation due to a lack of resources (e.g., food and burrows). The rock spires may be uninhabitable terrain because solid bedrock lacks horizontal complexity provided by vegetation, potentially increasing exposure to predators (Wywialowski 1987).

The Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk is vulnerable to rapid range contraction and extirpation because it is a cold-adapted diurnal montane chipmunk (McCain and King 2014) living at the southern edge of its distribution surrounded by desert (Parmesan and Yohe 2003; Beever et al. 2011). Further, the chipmunk has a small distribution in a relatively hot and dry mountain range. Since these chipmunks exist in a marginal environment, they may have a limited capacity to tolerate climate change and other threats (Scheele et al. 2017). Further, species living in marginal habitat are thought to be more prone to extinction (Hampe and Petit 2005). The life history of these chipmunks may already be impacted by living on the edge of their tolerances. The species has a bimodal reproduction pattern and has the earliest and latest parturition periods known for Neotamias species with breeding being associated with periods of reduced water loss and heat stress (Patterson 1980). Daily activity patterns of these chipmunks are influenced by climate such that in a warming climate chipmunks will reduce activity in early summer—potentially leading to a decline in reproductive output—while increasing activity in winter—potentially heightening the risk of predation (Schweiger and Frey 2021). Further, our results for habitat selection at the landscape scale add to similar results at the home range, within-home range, and microhabitat scales (Schweiger et al. 2021), indicating that these chipmunks select for cool microclimate refugia across all scales of habitat selection.

Niche reduction.

Results support our predictions that the Organ Mountain Colorado Chipmunk has undergone niche reduction. Historically, chipmunks were associated with coniferous forests and woodlands, montane scrub, chaparral, and both upper and lower elevation drainages—essentially most non-desert ecosystems found in the mountains (Patterson 1980; Sullivan 1996; NMNHP 1998; Frey and Kopp 2013). Currently, chipmunks are only persisting in a portion of the historical niche—upper drainages and cooler Gamble Oak montane scrub communities lacking conifers. Further, chipmunks are avoiding ponderosa pines on the landscape scale and were more likely to be extirpated at sites with the presence of at least 1 Gray Oak or Oneseed Juniper, suggesting that they are avoiding portions of their historical niche.

Our results suggest that climate, rather than habitat loss, is the threat that caused reduction in the realized niche of the species. Over the past 50 yr, minimum and maximum temperature and drought severity have increased significantly (Fig. 2). There has been a 3 °C increase in minimum temperature since 1970, and annual conditions have been hotter and drier than the mean since the 1990s (Fig. 2). Further, to our knowledge, there has been no land use or management changes or wildfires that could have caused the widespread extirpation of the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk. During field work, we did not observe habitat loss or land use changes, except at 1 historical site located in a recent burn scar (i.e., Abrams Fire of 2012), where we detected a chipmunk. Climate change has already been predicted to change daily activity patterns of these chipmunks (Schweiger and Frey 2021). Similarly, climate stressors, not the size of an area nor isolation, best predicted persistence for O. princeps in the Great Basin (Beever et al. 2011).

There were no climate variables (i.e., PRISM and spatial microclimate) in our top models. However, 1 variable (slope at dispersal scale) in the contemporary habitat selection and contemporary spatial models was negatively correlated (Pearson correlation coefficient |r| > 0.70) with several temperature variables, indicating that increasing occupancy probability was associated with cooler and more mesic conditions. One reason that climate variables were not better represented in our top models may be because of the large spatial and temporal scale of the PRISM data since habitat selection occurring at smaller scales (Suggitt et al. 2011) and microclimates (e.g., soil and vegetation level or below) may be more important to small mammals (Kay 1977; Porter and Kearney 2009; Huey et al. 2012). However, topographic variables in the top models influence microclimate, whereas plant variables are indicative of local climates. Major drainages (contemporary habitat selection and contemporary spatial models) provide cooler and more mesic microclimates than surrounding uplands (Schweiger et al. 2021). Northerly aspect (Suggitt et al. 2011; Singh 2018), increasing slope (Šafanda 1999), and increasing ruggedness (positive selection in extirpation causes, contemporary habitat selection, and contemporary spatial models) reduce solar radiation and hence provide relatively cooler microclimates whereas less rugged areas have more solar radiation and hence provide hotter microclimates (Estevo et al. 2022). Increased rock cover (positive selection in the contemporary habitat selection model) provides shady and relatively cooler microsites in fissures and under rocks (Otto et al. 2015). Gambel oaks (positive selection in the contemporary habitat selection model) occur in relatively cool microclimates (NMNHP 1994), whereas gray oaks, oneseed junipers, and sotols (avoidance in extirpation causes model) occur in relatively warmer microclimates (Harris et al. 2023). Ponderosa pines (avoidance in contemporary habitat selection model) occur in upland areas on relatively low slopes with relatively little rock cover (personal observation), all conditions associated with relatively warmer microclimates. High woody plant diversity (positive selection in contemporary habitat selection model) may occur in drainages because of increased water availability (Schweiger et al. 2021).

Broader implications.

Climate change has acted as an “escalator to extinction” for sky island species because rising temperatures cause upslope contractions, resulting in smaller distributions (Marris 2007). Climate change also can further isolate montane species (Ahmadi et al. 2019). Identifying and managing climate refugia are important for montane species already undergoing upslope contractions due to climate change (Morelli et al. 2016). In the case of the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk, it has not only experienced upslope contraction but also has contracted from uplands to drainages. Higher elevations and drainages may act as environmental refugia that ameliorate tolerances of the chipmunk to climate change (Scheele et al. 2017). In contrast, although these chipmunks historically used conifer forests and woodlands and lower-elevation drainages, these conditions may now exceed their tolerance to the current climate. Conifer trees are long-lived and can persist at sites that are today too warm (i.e., “zombie forest”; Hill et al. 2023). Thus, while historically conifer trees may have been a good indicator of chipmunk habitat, contemporary conditions where conifer trees are growing may be too warm to support chipmunks, which have relatively short generation times. In the Organ Mountains, montane forest occurs in relict patches, suggesting that the historical climatic conditions already were restricting the distribution of conifer trees. If the existing stands are destroyed, such as via wildfire or disease, it is possible the loss will be permanent under the current climate. Although our analyses suggested that chipmunks are avoiding areas with conifer trees, the trees still might provide crucial food resources for chipmunks (e.g., seeds, berries, and fungi).

Formerly, the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk was a habitat generalist; however, our results suggest that niche reduction has caused the subspecies to currently act as a habitat specialist. These chipmunks are selecting microclimate refugia on all spatial scales of habitat selection (Schweiger et al. 2021; this study), suggesting that their habitat selection is now very narrow and that they are currently exhibiting habitat specialist behaviors to climate refugia habitat. We conclude that climate change has caused a behavioral shift in the chipmunk from generalist to a “functional specialist,” which we define as a generalist species that became a de facto specialist via niche reduction. As a functional specialist, the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk may now be even more vulnerable to an array of threats (Dennis et al. 2011; Habel and Schmitt 2012) including climate change, land conversion, fragmentation, habitat loss, and disease. Habitat specialists have an increased risk of extinction (McKinney 1997; Colles et al. 2009). Due to the reduction in niche, there may be a disconnect with key resources including potential food sources (e.g., conifer mast, juniper berries, fungi) common in conifer woodlands and forests and dispersal corridors via low elevation drainages.

Strength of methods.

Our study was successful in using habitat selection methods via occupancy models to evaluate extirpation, range contraction, and niche reduction at a landscape scale. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use occupancy models under the framework of the NRH; other studies used SDMs (e.g., McDonald et al. 2018; Moore et al. 2019; Alexander et al. 2022; Geyle et al. 2025). Using occupancy modeling, we were able to account for imperfect detection (MacKenzie et al. 2002) and determine that the extirpations at historical sites were likely true losses. Accounting for false absences is especially important for a declining cryptic and rare species that is difficult to detect and has low detection probability, as does the Organ Mountains Colorado Chipmunk. We mitigated the risk of overpredicting extirpation by selectively resurveying historical records, focusing exclusively on the highest quality records. This strategic approach involved ranking historical data based on reliability and precision, ensuring that our efforts were concentrated on revisiting only the most trustworthy and accurate locations.

While our data collection process was comprehensive, it is important to note that there was a potential sampling bias because we did not survey slopes > 45° due to accessibility and safety while conducting field work. However, we took other steps to minimize sampling bias overall by randomly stratifying our sites, which ensured representation of different landcover types that are important to the chipmunks. To reduce sampling bias at the survey level, we accounted for detection probability by incorporating covariates hypothesized to influence detection, and we standardized survey protocols to ensure consistency in data collection, minimizing variation in sampling effort and methodology.

Management implications.

Trailing edges of a species range (southernmost populations of northern hemisphere species) are important for the conservation of genetic diversity because they may add unique diversity to the species (­Ashcroft et al. 2012). These edges harbor populations with high future adaptive capacity (Razgour et al. 2013), potentially making them better adapted to warmer climates compared to populations at the center of a species range (Habibzadeh et al. 2021). Therefore, we recommend heightened conservation status of this southern-margin population. In line with the NRH, we propose to not only protect the habitat represented by their contemporary niche but also the habitat of the historical niche, which may be potential areas for reintroduction and assisted colonization (Scheele et al. 2017). We recommend that managers should prevent habitat loss in drainages, montane scrub, Madrean oak woodland, and conifer woodlands and forests. Protecting the lower part of drainages is important because they may provide pathways for dispersal between fragmented patches. Protecting conifer woodlands and forest is important even if the chipmunk may not be selecting for conifers because the chipmunk may still be using the trees for food resources.
