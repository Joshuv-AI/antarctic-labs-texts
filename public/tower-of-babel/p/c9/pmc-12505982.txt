Trends in the global burden of childhood meningitis between 1990 and 2021 and projections to 2035: based on the global burden of diseases database 2021
Authors: Duo-Duo Li, Xin-Quan Sang, Qian Huang, Li Wang, Yi-Shuai Ren, Li-Ping Deng, Wei-Hong Lu, Xiang-Tao Wu, Ya-Li Xu, Xi-Xia Guo, Feng-Lian Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to assess trends in the incidence, mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years between 1990 and 2021 worldwide.MethodsThis study analysed the incidence, mortality and DALYs of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years in 204 countries between 1990 and 2021 and used a mixed effects regression model to calculate the average annual percentage change. The Markov chain Monte Carlo method was used to quantify uncertainty (95% uncertainty interval).ResultsIn 2021, the global incidence of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years was 66.24 per 100,000, and the mortality rate was 5.59 per 100,000. Between 1990 and 2021, the global incidence decreased by 282.70%, and the mortality rate decreased by 310.8%. The incidence and mortality rates were highest in neonates and children aged 1–5 months, at 1,185.02 per 100,000 and 541.35 per 100,000, respectively. According to the socio-demographic index (SDI) stratifications, low SDI areas have the heaviest burden. Pathogen analysis showed that the main pathogens causing meningitis in children aged 0–14 years worldwide in 2021 included Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis and Klebsiella pneumoniae, of which S. pneumoniae caused 1.69632 million DALYs, N. meningitidis caused 1.41484 million DALYs and K. pneumoniae caused 1.19393 million DALYs. By 2035, N. meningitidis will become the main pathogen.ConclusionThe burden of meningitis in children has decreased significantly worldwide, but those in low-income areas and newborns still face severe challenges. Streptococcus pneumoniae, N. meningitidis and K. pneumoniae are the main pathogens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24787-6.Keywords: Meningitis, Children, Global burden of disease, Morbidity, Mortality, Pathogens

Introduction

Meningitis is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in children, posing a particular threat to children aged < 5 years [1]. Globally, meningitis kills hundreds of thousands of children each year, and survivors often suffer from long-term sequelae, such as movement disorders, cognitive impairment, visual and hearing impairment and hydrocephalus [2–4]. Although the promotion of vaccines, such as pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) vaccine, and antibiotic treatment over the past three decades have significantly reduced the burden of meningitis [5], the distribution of the disease shows significant regional differences. The World Health Organization points out that the mortality rate of children with meningitis in low-income countries is still > 10 times that of high-income countries [6], highlighting the severity of global health inequality.

In Africa, the main pathogens causing meningitis are Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis, which are associated with high mortality and long-term complications [1]. The risk of death from infection with S. pneumoniae is significantly higher than from infection with H. influenzae [7]. In recent years, the epidemiological pattern of pathogens has undergone significant changes. Streptococcus pneumoniae and N. meningitidis remain the main pathogens; however, the incidence of gram-negative bacilli, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli, in newborns and premature infants has increased, which may be related to healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) and increasing resistance to antibiotics [8]. In addition, the disruption of routine immunisation and health services by the COVID-19 pandemic may have reversed some of the prevention and control results. Studies have shown that the global Hib and PCV13 coverage in 2020 decreased by 5–7% compared with 2019, especially in low-income countries [9], which may have led to a resurgence or re-emergence of vaccine-preventable diseases. The most comprehensive pre-COVID-19 assessment, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 systematic analysis of meningitis burden, documented substantial global progress but also highlighted persistent regional gaps.

Although existing studies provide valuable information about trends in single pathogens or specific regions, there is a lack of multidimensional and systematic analyses of the global burden of paediatric meningitis. For example, the GBD 2019 study revealed that the overall burden of meningitis has decreased; however, it did not conduct an in-depth analysis of the dynamic changes in pathogen distribution under different socio-demographic index (SDI) strata [10]. Although some studies focus on the changes in pathogens in high-income countries, they ignore the rising trend of gram-negative bacterial infections in middle- and low-income countries [11]. In addition, the long-term impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on non-COVID-19 infectious diseases has not been fully assessed, especially the impact on the prevention and control of paediatric meningitis, which urgently needs to be analysed in depth based on the latest data.

Using GBD 2021 data, this study aims to (1) analyse the spatiotemporal changes in the incidence, mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years worldwide between 1990 and 2021, (2) reveal the differences in disease burden among different SDI regions, age groups and pathogens, (3) evaluate the trend changes before and after the COVID-19 pandemic (2019–2021), (4) predict the disease burden between 2022 and 2035 and (5) provide evidential support for optimising vaccine strategies, antimicrobial drug use and global health resource allocation.

Research methods

Data source

This study is based on public data from the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries and Risk Factors 2021 Study (GBD 2021). The GBD database is led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation at the University of Washington. Its official website is https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2021 [12]. The data in the GBD database cover the incidence, mortality, DALYs and aetiology attribution analysis results of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years in 204 countries and regions around the world between 1990 and 2021. The data include the SDI, the World Bank income classification and the GBD super-regional stratification data. The pathogen-related data are generated through the GBD pathogen attribution model, combined with epidemiological surveys, microbiological surveillance and literature data. In addition, the study uses relevant codes (such as A39.0 and G00.0) of the 9th and 10th revisions of the International Classification of Diseases for disease diagnosis classification. These data provide an important basis for analysing the spatiotemporal changes in the global meningitis burden and the distribution of pathogens.

Study design

A retrospective time series analysis was used to assess changes in the disease burden of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years between 1990 and 2021.

Research participants and groups

Age groups: newborns (0–28 days), 1–5 months, 6–11 months, 12–23 months, 2–4 years, 5–9 years and 10–14 years.

Socio-demographic index stratification: According to the SDI, the countries are divided into five categories – high SDI (> 0.80), upper-middle SDI (0.70–0.80), middle SDI (0.60–0.70), lower-middle SDI (0.50–0.60) and low SDI (< 0.50).

World Bank income classification: high income, upper-middle income, lower-middle income, low income.

GBD super regions: include 21 regions, such as Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and Southeast Asia/East Asia/Oceania.

Pathogen types: S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, group B streptococcus, H. influenzae, E. coli, K. pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria monocytogenes, other bacterial pathogens and viral aetiologies of meningitis. The pathogen attribution section analyses the temporal changes and regional differences by comparing the disease contributions of the 10 major pathogens.

Variable definition and measurement

Morbidity/mortality rates are reported as the annual number of cases or deaths per 100,000 population, using age-standardised incidence rates (ASIRs) and 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs).

Disability-adjusted life years: These were calculated by combining years of life lost due to premature death (YLLs) and years of life lost due to disability (YLDs) according to the GBD standard method; DALYs = YLLs + YLDs. Calculation of YLLs: number of deaths × standard life expectancy (refer to GBD standard life table). Calculation of YLDs: number of cases × disability weight × average disease duration (based on the severity of disease sequelae). The disability weight (0–1) is defined according to the GBD 2021 standard (e.g. the weight of meningitis sequelae is 0.053–0.533).

Socio-demographic index stratification: This is based on per capita income, education level and fertility rate; regions are divided into five SDI groups – high, upper-middle, middle, lower-middle and low.

Results presentation: The prediction indicators included the total number of cases, age-specific number of cases, ASIRs and age-standardised mortality rates. Visualisation was achieved using R software to generate graphics, such as the standardised incidence rate prediction graphics for men and women, for easy interpretation and dissemination.

Pathogen attribution: Assignment of the proportion of contribution of each pathogen to the meningitis burden was based on microbiological evidence and statistical modelling.

Statistical analysis methods

This study used multiple statistical models to analyse the global burden of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years. First, a mixed effects model was used to assess the association between the SDI, age group and disease burden and to control heterogeneity among countries. Second, the Bayesian spatiotemporal model was used to correct for missing data and reporting bias, and a 95% UI was generated to ensure the robustness of the results. In addition, the Joinpoint regression model was used to calculate the AAPC of incidence, mortality and DALYs to identify the turning point of the trend change. The BAPC model was used to predict the incidence of meningitis in children worldwide between 2022 and 2035. The pathogen attribution analysis was based on the GBD cause–harm–risk framework, and the contribution of each pathogen to the burden of meningitis was allocated through counterfactual analysis. Microbiological surveillance and literature data were combined to ensure the accuracy of the attribution results. This study took multiple control measures to reduce the impact of data bias on the results. First, for areas with high underreporting rates, Gaussian process regression was used to interpolate missing data to ensure data integrity. Second, Markov chain Monte Carlo simulations (10,000 iterations) were used to generate 95% UIs to quantify the uncertainty of the model parameters. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of different model assumptions (such as a change in disability weight of ± 20%) on the results and verify the robustness of the model. Finally, the leave-one-country-out method was used to verify the stability of the predictions and ensure the applicability of the model in different geographical regions. Together, these methods ensured the reliability and reproducibility of the research results.

Ethical statement

This study used publicly available anonymised data in accordance with the GBD data use agreement, and no additional ethical approval was required. All analyses followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki to ensure personal privacy and data security.

Result

Incidence of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years in 2021

In 2021, the global incidence of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years was 66.24 cases per 100,000 people (95% UI, [55.03–78.41]), totalling 1.33 million cases (1,332,734.452 cases). By region, the highest incidence rate was found in lower-middle SDI regions, at 157.43 cases per 100,000 people (95% UI, [157.43–184.47]), totalling 724,530 cases. The highest incidence rate by income level was in low-income countries, at 136.63 cases per 100,000 people (95% UI, [115.75–160.16]), with a total of 394,290 cases. By geographic region, the highest incidence rate was in Sub-Saharan Africa, at 166.78 cases per 100,000 people (95% UI, [140.07–195.85]), totalling 793,780 cases (Appendix 1).

Among 204 countries and territories, the incidence of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years ranged from 1.98 cases per 100,000 people (95% UI, [1.48–2.63]) in Cyprus to 370.67 cases per 100,000 people (95% UI, [313.46–435.09]) in South Sudan (Appendix 1). The highest incidence was observed in neonates, at 1,185.02 cases per 100,000 people (95% UI, [965.44–1,469.36]), followed by children aged 1–5 months, at 541.35 cases per 100,000 people (95% UI, [446.54–650.62]). The incidence rate gradually decreased with age, reaching the lowest level in children aged 10–14 years, at 18.93 cases per 100,000 people (95% UI, [12.42–27.17]) (Appendix 1).

Between 1990 and 2019, the global incidence of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years decreased by 282.70% (95% UI, [249.10–316.10]) from 164.80 cases (95% UI, [132.10–200.46]) per 100,000 people to 68.76 cases (95% UI, [57.03–81.35]) per 100,000 people. Among all age groups, the incidence rate decreased the most in children aged 12–23 months, by 343.60%, whereas the least decrease occurred in children aged 10–14 years, at 211.90% (Appendix 1).

Global mortality rate of meningitis among children aged 0–14 years in 2021

In 2019, before the COVID-19 epidemic, an estimated 13.07 million (95% UI, [3.47–5.75]) children aged 0–14 died from meningitis, with a mortality rate of 6.52 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [4.89–8.58]). The mortality rate decreased by 310.80% (95% UI, [278.10–343.40]) since 1990. After subdividing by age groups, similar to the incidence rate, the mortality rate decreased the most in the 12–23-month age group, by 373.70% (95% UI, [345.00–402.30]). In 1990, 48,225.18 (95% UI, [38,474.60–59,328.55]) deaths occurred in this age group, while in 2019, the number of deaths was 16,398.92 (95% UI, [11,121.96–23,464.66]) (Appendix 2).

In 2021, a total of 112,370 children aged 0–14 years died from meningitis worldwide, with a mortality rate of 5.59 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [4.02–7.66]). After stratification by SDI region, the highest meningitis mortality rate occurred in the low SDI region, with 68,600 deaths and a mortality rate of 14.91 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [10.09–22.26]). According to the World Bank stratification, the highest meningitis mortality rate occurred in the low-income region, with 36,960 deaths and a mortality rate of 12.18 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [8.60–18.33]). By GBD super-region, Sub-Saharan Africa had the highest mortality rate, with 75,020 deaths and a mortality rate of 15.76 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [10.24–24.17]) (Appendix 2).

Among 204 countries and territories, the meningitis mortality rate in children aged 0–14 years in 2021 ranged from 0.01 per 100,000 (95% UI, [0.01–0.01]) in Andorra to 36.56 per 100,000 (95% UI, [25.10–50.32]) in South Sudan (Figure 1, Appendix 3). Among age groups, the highest mortality rate was observed in neonates, at 129.69 per 100,000 (95% UI, [100.76–170.78]), gradually decreasing with age to the lowest rate of 1.42 per 100,000 (95% UI, [1.20–1.73]) in children aged 10–14 years (Appendix 2).

Incidence of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years in 2021 (per 100,000)

In 2021, neonatal meningitis deaths were concentrated mainly in low SDI areas. Among 204 countries and territories, 174 countries had neonatal meningitis mortality rates >100 per 100,000 people. Globally, the neonatal meningitis mortality rate was 129.69 per 100,000 (95% UI, [100.76–170.78]). Specifically, it was 307.32 (95% UI, [237.51–418.48]) in low SDI countries, 109.20 (95% UI, [83.31–148.22]) in lower-middle SDI regions, 38.50 (95% UI, [30.16–49.88]) in middle SDI regions, 13.28 (95% UI, [10.89–15.82]) in upper-middle SDI regions and 6.38 (95% UI, [5.64–7.13]) in high SDI regions. Although low and lower-middle SDI countries had the highest burden in 2021, these countries also demonstrated the greatest improvement in mortality over time (Appendix 3).

Global mortality, incidence and disability-adjusted life years rates of meningitis among children aged 0–14 years in 1990–2021

Globally, between 1990 and 2021, the mortality and incidence rates of meningitis among children aged 0–14 years showed a downward trend, with the mortality rate declining by 69.20% (95% UI, [58.90–76.40]) from 18.12 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [15.56–21.44]) to 5.59 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [4.02–7.66]) (Figure 2). After stratification by sex, the morbidity and mortality rates were higher in male participants than in female participants across all age groups and were particularly pronounced in the 1–12-month age group. For boys, the mortality rate decreased by 68.90% (95% UI, [54.00–77.60]) from 19.17 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [15.89–22.86]) to 5.96 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [4.03–8.59]) (Figure 3). For girls, the mortality rate decreased by 59.90% (95% UI, [57.90–61.80]) from 17.02 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [14.28–20.88]) to 5.19 per 100,000 people (95% UI, [3.78–7.01]) (Figure 2).

Etiological distribution of disability-adjusted life years and deaths from meningitis in children worldwide by age group in 2021

In 2021, bacterial pathogens accounted for the overwhelming majority of DALYs in children aged 0–14 years. In descending order, the leading bacteria were S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, K. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, E. coli, S. aureus, L. monocytogenes and group B streptococcus. Viral aetiologies collectively ranked below these bacterial causes. The top three pathogens with the highest DALYs were S. pneumoniae with 1,696,320 DALYs (95% CI, [1,197,38–2,393,33] DALYs), N. meningitidis with 1,414,840 DALYs (95% CI, [1,040,13–1,911,67] DALYs) and K. pneumoniae with 1,193,930 DALYs (95% CI, [820,41–1,683,85] DALYs) (Appendix 5).

When divided by SDI, K. pneumoniae was the leading pathogen causing DALYs for children aged 0–14 years in the high SDI area, resulting in 4,855.78 DALYs (95% CI, [4,170.43–5,665.75]), followed by group B streptococcus, resulting in 4,394.322 DALYs (95% CI, [3,855.99–4,930.09]) and E. coli, resulting in 4,106.730 DALYs (95% CI, [3,558.87–4,720.87]). In the upper-middle to low SDI areas, S. pneumoniae was the leading pathogen causing DALYs for children aged 0–14 years. The top three pathogens in the middle SDI to low SDI regions were the same as those in the world, but the second and third pathogens in the upper-middle SDI region were opposite to those in the world, namely K. pneumoniae and N. meningitidis, which caused 15,115.36 DALYs (95% CI, [12,541.66–18,402.32]) and 14,403.58 DALYs (95% CI, [12,395.66–17,025.66]), respectively. When stratified by the World Bank, the characteristics presented were similar to those of the SDI regions. When divided into GBD super-regions, the top three DALYs pathogens in the high-income region were the same as those in the high SDI region, and the top three DALYs pathogens in Central Europe, Eastern Europe and Central Asia were consistent with those in the upper-middle SDI region; the rest of the GBD super-regions were consistent with the top three pathogens in the world (Appendix 5).

Stratification of 204 countries and regions: In 2021, among the 204 modelled countries and regions, 100 countries/regions represented by the Maldives had the highest number of DALYs caused by S. pneumoniae;64 countries (or regions) represented by Lithuania had the highest number of DALYs for meningitis in children aged 0–14 years caused by K. pneumoniae; 35 countries (or regions) represented by Armenia had the highest number of DALYs for meningitis in children aged 0–14 years caused by N. meningitidis; 3 countries (or regions) had the highest number of DALYs for meningitis in children aged 0–14 years caused by group B streptococcus; inBermuda, H. influenzae had the highest number of DALYs for meningitis in children aged 0–14 years; in Andorra, other bacterial pathogens had the highest number of DALYs for meningitis in children (Appendix 6). Streptococcus pneumoniae caused the most DALYs for all seven age groups studied (Figure 4).

The top three pathogens causing the highest DALY and death toll for meningitis-related diseases in the neonatal age group were group B streptococcus, K. pneumoniae and viral aetiologies of meningitis. Changes in the ranking of causes of meningitis-related deaths among children worldwide in 1990, 2000, 2010 and 2021

In 2021, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the top three pathogens causing deaths from meningitis in children aged 0–14 years worldwide were S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis and K. pneumoniae. Among them, S. pneumoniae was the first pathogen, causing 22,360 deaths (95% CI, [1.62–2.97]), N. meningitidis was the second, causing 18,280 deaths (95% CI, [1.38–2.38]) and K. pneumoniae was the third, causing 15,780 deaths (95% CI, [1.34–2.16]) (Appendix 7).

Since 1990, the pathogen with the most significant decrease in mortality was H. influenzae, which decreased by 488.5% (95% CI, [427.8–547.8]), whereas the mortality rate of group B streptococcus decreased the least, by 237.6% (95% CI, [214.0–261.2]). The second largest decrease was N. meningitidis, which decreased by 439.0% (95% CI, [381.9–495.7]), whereas the mortality rate of viral meningitis decreased by 290.5% (95% CI, [263.3–317.6]). Among all age groups, the mortality rate of children aged 12–23 months decreased the most (Appendix 7).

In 2021, S. pneumoniae remained the leading pathogen causing death in children aged 0–14 years. The top three pathogens causing death in economically developed regions (such as high SDI and high-income countries) were K. pneumoniae, group B streptococcus and E. coli. In contrast, in other regions, S. pneumoniae remained the leading pathogen, but the second and third positions were different. For example, the top three pathogens in the upper-middle SDI and upper-middle income regions of the World Bank were S. pneumoniae, K. pneumoniae and N. meningitidis (Appendix 7).

Before the COVID-19 pandemic, the ranking of pathogens causing meningitis changed significantly between 1990 and 2019. The top three in 1990 were N. meningitidis,S. pneumoniae and K. pneumoniae, whereas in 2019 they wereS. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis and K. pneumoniae. It is worth noting that the ranking of H. influenzae dropped significantly from fourth in 1990 to ninth (Figure 5, Appendix 7).

In 2021, the causes of meningitis deaths varied across countries. Among the 204 countries modelled, deaths due to S. pneumoniae were highest in Papua New Guinea, whereas deaths due to K. pneumoniae were highest in 78 countries, including Singapore, and deaths due to N. meningitidis were highest in 27 countries, including the Marshall Islands (Appendix 6).

Since the COVID-19 pandemic, the ranking of pathogens causing meningitis deaths in children aged 0–14 years did not change considerably between 2019 and 2021. The mortality burden caused by S. pneumoniae decreased the most, by approximately 14.5% (95% CI, [8.6–20.3]), whereas the mortality burden caused by viral meningitis decreased the least, by approximately 13.2% (95% CI, [18.0–8.2]) (Figure 6, Appendix 8).

Overall, between 2019 and 2021, the global incidence of non-COVID-19 paediatric meningitis decreased by 3.7% (95% UI, [2.8–4.6]), the mortality rate decreased by 14.3% (95% CI, [9.0–19.6]) and the DALYs rate decreased by 14.2% (95% CI, [9.0–19.3]) (Appendix 8).

Projected burden of childhood meningitis in 2035

Between 2021 and 2035, the number of deaths, mortality and DALYs rate of children aged 0–14 years in the world shows a projected downward trend, with the number of deaths decreasing from 112,370 in 2021 to 44,640 in 2035. According to the prediction of pathogens, N. meningitidis is expected to be the leading pathogen of death and DALYs by 2035, with the mortality rate caused by N. meningitidis being approximately 1.367 (95% UI, [0.015–21.885]) per 100,000 and the DALYs rate being approximately 168.05 (95% UI, [0.941–3,314.453]) per 100,000.

Discussion

This study analysed the GBD data of paediatric meningitis, conducted a comprehensive assessment of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years and predicted it to 2035. The study revealed the pathogen distribution, regional and age-specific differences in incidence, mortality and DALY burden, as well as prognostic differences associated with specific pathogens. Specifically, bacteria, such as meningococci, E. coli and streptococci, are the main pathogens that cause bacterial meningitis in children.

Between 1990 and 2021, the global incidence of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years decreased by 282.7% (AAPC −3.18%) and the mortality rate decreased by 310.8% (AAPC−3.50%); however, the mortality rate in low SDI areas (14.906 per 100,000) was still 73 times that in high SDI areas, revealing that the global gap in disease burden continues to widen [13].

Studies have shown that the incidence of meningitis in children worldwide has dropped by 282.7%, and the mortality rate has dropped by 310.8%, which is mainly attributed to the implementation of vaccination programmes, especially vaccination against Hib meningitis [14]. The incidence rate has dropped significantly from 4.84 cases per 100,000 child-years to 0.67 cases. This shows that vaccination has played an important role in reducing the number of meningitis cases.

Despite the global decline in morbidity and mortality, mortality rates in low SDI regions remain 73 times higher than in high SDI regions. This suggests that these regions still face major challenges in terms of vaccination coverage and health resources [1, 3]. Despite the significant decline in the global burden of meningitis, low SDI regions still face severe challenges. This gap is closely related to vaccine coverage, access to health resources and health system resilience [12]. For example, PCV13 coverage in low SDI regions is much lower than that in high SDI regions [15, 16].

Although funding from the Gavi Vaccine Alliance has partially alleviated the problem of vaccine accessibility, systemic barriers, such as cold chain transportation and training of primary health workers, still need to be overcome [17]. The global burden of childhood meningitis has declined markedly in the past few decades, mainly due to vaccination programmes. However, the high mortality rate in low SDI areas shows that global health inequalities still exist, and continued public health efforts are needed to narrow this gap.

This study is an independent analysis of the newborn population (aged 0–28 days), which makes up for the limitation of the previous GBD study that the newborn data were aggregated in ‘children under 5 years old’ and provides direct evidence for perinatal intervention. The mortality rate of neonatal meningitis (129.694 per 100,000) is significantly higher than that of other age groups and is 91 times that of children aged 10–14 years, indicating that perinatal infection prevention and control is still a weak link.

The high mortality rate of neonatal meningitis is directly related to prematurity, low birth weight and inadequate medical conditions [18]. For the first time, the SDI level, age group, geographic region and pathogen type were systematically integrated to reveal the heterogeneity of the disease burden and provide targets for precise intervention. For example, the high burden of neonatal meningitis in low SDI areas was associated with K. pneumoniae. Group B streptococcus andK. pneumoniae are the main pathogens, especially in low SDI areas, where nosocomial infections and antibiotic resistance increase the difficulty of treatment [19]. It is recommended to promote prenatal screening for group B streptococcus, standardised procedures for neonatal intensive care and the rational use of broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Analysis and quantification of the contribution of 10 pathogens revealed that the rising trend of gram-negative bacterial infections in high-income areas challenged the traditional perception of ‘single dominance of S. pneumoniae’. Streptococcus pneumoniae is the leading pathogen globally (DALYs accounted for 28.6%), although in high SDI areas, K. pneumoniae has become the dominant pathogen (DALYs accounted for 34.1%), reflecting the association between pathogen distribution and medical conditions and antibiotic use patterns.

Streptococcus pneumoniae is a common pathogen of community-acquired bacterial meningitis, which is associated with high morbidity and mortality. Although vaccination has reduced morbidity, many survivors still face severe brain damage and sequelae[20]. The pathogen enters the central nervous system through disruption of the blood–brain barrier, triggering a strong inflammatory response and leading to neuronal damage [20, 21]. With the use of vaccines and antibiotics, the emergence of non-vaccine serotypes and increasing resistance to antibiotics have posed challenges to treatment [22, 23].

In areas with a high SDI, K. pneumoniae has become the main pathogen of bacterial meningitis, especially highly virulent and carbapenem-resistant strains [24–26]. It may also be associated with HAIs and the spread of carbapenem-resistant strains [11]. Highly virulent K. pneumoniae can cause severe community-acquired infections with high mortality and disability, limiting treatment options [24].

Hospital infection control strategies, such as hand hygiene standards and environmental disinfection, need to be optimised, and drug resistance surveillance networks need to be strengthened. In contrast, the continued high incidence of pneumococcal pneumonia in low SDI areas suggests that PCV vaccine coverage still needs to be improved, and attention should be paid to serotype replacement [27]. The dominance of pneumococcal pneumonia and K. pneumoniae in different regions reflects the association between pathogen distribution and medical conditions and antibiotic use patterns. Effective surveillance and treatment strategies are essential to control the spread of these deadly infections.

Between 2019 and 2021, the incidence and mortality of meningitis decreased by 3.7% and 14.3%, respectively; however, the decline was significantly lower than the AAPC, suggesting that the pandemic may have reduced the effectiveness of prevention and control by interfering with immunisation services. By 2035, the global mortality and DALYs of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years are expected to decline, and N. meningitidis will become the leading pathogen causing deaths and DALYs. Neisseria meningitidis is one of the main pathogens causing meningitis, especially in children. Although vaccination has significantly reduced the incidence of certain serogroups, N. meningitidis remains an important public health challenge [28]. The pathogen remains the main cause of meningitis in many areas, especially in areas with low vaccine coverage [28]. The prevalence of different serogroups may affect the formulation of vaccine strategies [29, 30].

Although the burden of meningitis has not rebounded appreciably in the short term, disruptions in immunisation services caused by the pandemic may increase the risk of future outbreaks. Models predict that if the 5% decline in PCV coverage from 2020 to 2021 continues, the incidence of pneumococcal meningitis in low SDI areas may rebound by 8%–12% in 2030 [9]. There is an urgent need to repair immunisation gaps through catch-up programmes, community mobilisation and digital health tools.

In South American countries, the implementation of meningitis vaccination programmes has significantly reduced the burden of meningitis, especially for serogroups covered by the vaccine [31]. However, the COVID-19 pandemic led to a decline in vaccination rates, which may have affected the control of vaccine-preventable diseases [32]. In the UK, non-pharmaceutical interventions during COVID-19, such as social distancing, led to a significant decrease in hospitalisations for childhood meningitis [33]. Similarly, studies in South Korea also showed a significant decrease in the incidence of childhood meningitis after the COVID-19 outbreak [34]. Vaccination services in many countries were disrupted during the pandemic, leading to a decline in vaccination rates [14]. For example, surveys showed that many parents postponed or cancelled their children’s vaccination appointments due to the epidemic [35]. In a Chinese study, meningitis outbreaks still occurred even in communities with high vaccine coverage, suggesting that existing immunisation strategies may have limitations in long-term prevention [36]. Although the incidence and mortality of meningitis decreased between 2019 and 2021, the decrease was less than expected, which may be related to the interruption of immunisation services during the COVID-19 pandemic. Strengthening vaccination and continuous monitoring are the key to future prevention and control.

Future prevention and control strategies

Studies have suggested that vaccination should have been stepped up in the later stages of the pandemic, especially for adolescents, to maintain herd immunity [31]. Vaccination strategies should be continuously monitored and adjusted to respond to possible disease recurrence and vaccine failure [32].

Limitations of the study

The accuracy of our findings depends on that of the GBD database, which integrates surveillance data, epidemiological studies and modelling. In particular, in low SDI regions, meningitis surveillance and reporting systems are often inadequate, which may lead to underreporting of cases, deaths and long-term sequelae. Despite the use of Bayesian spatiotemporal modelling and Gaussian process interpolation to address missing data, residual bias and inaccuracies remain.

The GBD data lack detailed clinical information, including specific clinical presentations, disease severity, underlying comorbidities and treatment outcomes. This limitation prevents a deeper understanding of the disease burden in clinically distinct patient subgroups.

Pathogen attribution is based on modelling assumptions based on regional microbiological surveillance, systematic reviews and GBD. Microbiological diagnostics, especially in resource-limited settings, are inconsistent or imperfect, which may lead to underreporting of pathogens such as gram-negative bacteria and resistant strains.

We analysed only short-term trends (2019–2021), which limits our understanding of the long-term impact of COVID-19-related disruptions in immunisation and health care services. Although our data suggest that the meningitis burden was slightly affected during the pandemic, long-term consequences, such as recurrence due to vaccination gaps or reduced herd immunity, remain uncertain and require ongoing monitoring.

Projections to 2035 were made using a BAPC model, which assumes historical trends and stable patterns in vaccination coverage and antimicrobial use. Sudden changes in public health policies, vaccination rates or antibiotic resistance or the emergence of novel pathogens could lead to biases in the projected burden.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the changes in the global disease burden of meningitis in children aged 0–14 years between 1990 and 2021 and revealed the following key findings: the global burden of meningitis has declined significantly, although the disease burden in low SDI regions and newborn groups remains high, reflecting the severity of health inequality; the distribution of pathogens is in dynamic change, and S. pneumoniae is the leading pathogen worldwide, although K. pneumoniae infection is on the rise in high SDI regions, suggesting a potential threat of healthcare-associated infection and antibiotic resistance; the COVID-19 pandemic had a limited short-term impact on meningitis prevention and control, but may increase the risk of future disease outbreaks due to interference with immunisation services. Based on these findings, the following policy recommendations are made: give priority to strengthening the prevention and control of neonatal meningitis in low-income areas, promote group B streptococcal screening and antibiotic prevention and improve intensive care capacity; optimise vaccine strategies, increase PCV and Hib vaccine coverage and pay attention to serotype replacement and monitoring of drug-resistant strains; repair the disruption of COVID-19 to immunisation services, and rebuild the immune barrier through re-vaccination programmes and community mobilisation; strengthen global cooperation and support the construction of health systems and pathogen surveillance networks in low SDI areas, to further reduce the burden of meningitis.
