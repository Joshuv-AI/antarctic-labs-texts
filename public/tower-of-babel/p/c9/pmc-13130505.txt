Protecting healthcare workers? Effects of a multilevel intervention targeting emotional demands
Authors: Louise Schubert Paaske, Nicolai Simonsen Damslund, Ida Elisabeth Huitfeldt Madsen, Sofie Østergaard Jaspers, Line Planck Kongstad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare workers are frequently exposed to high emotional demands through encounters with patient suffering, death, worries, and aggressive reactions. Such demands have been linked to an elevated risk of mental health problems, particularly depression. Despite growing recognition of these challenges, evidence on the effectiveness of workplace interventions remains limited. This study evaluates the impact of a multilevel intervention implemented in a Danish hospital to prevent and reduce the adverse consequences of emotional demands.MethodsWe used nationwide register data and a difference-in-differences design to estimate the effect of the intervention, which was introduced on 1 January 2021 and lasted three years. The study population comprised all employees at the intervention hospital and comparable control hospitals from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2023. The outcomes were healthcare costs related to general practitioner services and a composite measure capturing costs related to mental healthcare in primary care and redeemed prescriptions for antidepressant and anxiolytic medications. Marginal effects of the intervention were obtained from two-part models combining probit or logistic regression to estimate the probability of incurring any healthcare costs and generalised estimating equations to model costs conditional on positive values.ResultsOur findings show no overall effect of the intervention on healthcare costs. However, subgroup analyses suggest heterogeneous effects across groups of healthcare employees, including indications of cost-reducing effects on minor mental healthcare costs among nurses and on general practitioner-related costs among doctors.ConclusionThe intervention produced no measurable overall effects on healthcare costs; however, the limited statistical power to detect small overall effects means that modest intervention impacts cannot be ruled out. These findings highlight the need for further research on strategies to prevent and manage emotional demands at work.Keywords: Emotional demands, Healthcare workers, Healthcare costs, Mental health, Workplace intervention

Background

Work-related stress has emerged as a significant occupational risk factor in industrialised nations, which has led to a remarkable financial burden at both organisational and societal levels. Organisations incur costs due to absenteeism, decreased productivity and high employee turnover. These impacts extend beyond the workplace, affecting society through productivity-related losses, increased healthcare utilisation and medical costs [1, 2]. A recent Danish register-based study estimated that work-related stress is associated with substantial labour market costs due to sickness absence and reduced labour market participation. Using the expected labour market affiliation (ELMA) method and national register data from 2012–2020, the study estimated the total annual labour market costs of work-related stress in Denmark to exceed €1.1 billion [3]. These findings highlight the considerable economic burden of work-related stress and suggest a substantial potential for cost savings through effective preventive interventions.

In recent years, mental health problems among healthcare workers have gained growing attention as a critical occupational health concern [4, 5]. One of the factors associated with occupational stress is high emotional demands at work, which has been defined as ‘the extent to which the job requires sustained emotional effort because of interactional contact with clients’ [6, 7]. Healthcare workers often face emotional demands due to frequent exposure to patients’ suffering or death; caring for their emotional needs, sorrows and worries; or handling their aggressive reactions [8]. All of the above are unavoidable components of healthcare work, which is why high levels of emotional demands within healthcare settings are a focus area in many countries [8–10].

High emotional demands have been linked to increased risks of depression [10–12], antidepressant treatment [13] and long-term sickness absence [7, 14, 15], with even stronger effects when combined with high workload and time pressure [16]. The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated existing vulnerabilities in the healthcare workforce, highlighting the urgent need for sustainable strategies and organisational-level interventions to protect mental health in clinical settings [5, 17] either by preventing the exposure to high emotional demands or by improving the management of the demands, for instance by strengthening resources in the workplace to balance the demands [18]. Intervention studies have aimed to prevent or manage emotional demands at work by improving resources, and only a few have demonstrated a measurable effect [19–23]. In the 2010’s intervention research focused on improving resources more broadly to reduce the experience of emotional demands. One Canadian study [19], which evaluated a participatory intervention in a hospital setting, found decreased burnout but no significant change in emotional demands compared to a control hospital. Similarly, Danish studies have investigated the effect of different interventions focusing on self-rostering in hospital care [20] and improving communication and cooperation in a hospital [24]. However, none of these studies showed any impact on emotional demands [20, 24]. Recent research has added nuance to the understanding of emotional demands in healthcare settings, by addressing more specific resources relevant for emotional demands in terms of specific preventive practices. An Australian quasi-experimental study in residential aged care showed that a participatory work redesign intervention at the organisational level significantly reduced time pressure and emotional demands in a high-strain, resource-constrained setting, illustrating that emotional demands are shaped by structural conditions rather than solely individual experiences [22]. Likewise, a recent cross-national study within the social, health, and service sectors across 31 European countries shows that organisational policies can reduce emotional demands in service-oriented sectors [23]. Finally, a recent Danish observational study has pointed to specific preventive work-practices associated with lower levels of burnout in jobs with high emotional demands [25–27]. However, we still lack knowledge on how to improve these practices. Collectively, the existing literature underscores the complexity involved in mitigating the adverse effects of emotional demands on employee health. Although these studies have shown that organisational interventions are an effective way to improve the health and well-being of staff and, moreover, prevent burnout, only a few multilevel organisational interventions have focused on emotional demands [22–24].

Given that emotional demands cannot be eliminated in healthcare, interventions increasingly aim to support healthcare workers’ mental well-being by strengthen their capacity to manage and cope with these demands. However, there is still insufficient documentation in the literature regarding effective interventions focusing on emotional demands in order to improve psychological outcomes, which has implications for healthcare costs and broader economic impacts. The present study is part of a large mixed-methods evaluation of a multilevel hospital intervention to prevent and manage adverse consequences of emotionally demanding work. Before the present study, a process evaluation were performed, which showed an overall successful implementation [28]. In parallel with the present study, an effect evaluation is ongoing, examining the impact of the intervention on sickness absence and staff turnover. The objective of this study is to assess the impact of the intervention on mental health-related healthcare and pharmaceutical costs in the primary sector. Using a difference-in-differences (DiD) design and employing two-part models, we estimate the effects of the intervention on healthcare costs related to minor mental health conditions among hospital employees. The control group consisted of employees from a comparable hospital within the same administrative region, selected based on parallel trends in outcomes during the three-year period preceding the intervention.

Unlike most previous studies, which rely primarily on associations, our study combines a quasi-experimental design with two-part models to approximate the causal impact of a hospital-level intervention on healthcare costs related to minor mental health conditions. In doing so, we not only provide new empirical evidence on the effects of organisational interventions targeting emotional demands, we also enhance the methodological rigour of the literature by moving beyond association-based approaches.

Institutional settings

The Danish healthcare system

The Danish healthcare system is universally accessible, is primarily funded through general taxation and offers healthcare services to all residents that are free at the point of use. The system promotes equity and ensures comprehensive coverage for the entire population [29]. Healthcare provision is organised across three administrative levels: the state, five regions, and 98 municipalities. General practitioners (GPs) play a central role as the first point of contact and act as gatekeepers to specialised care, including hospital-based somatic and mental health services, as well as publicly reimbursed private-practice specialists and psychologists operating within the national health insurance scheme. Hospitals and specialist care are primarily operated by the regions, while municipalities are responsible for preventive services, rehabilitation, and social care. A key feature of the Danish healthcare sector is the long-standing routine registration of healthcare contacts in nationwide administrative and health registries, enabled by a unique personal identifier assigned to all residents, which allows complete population coverage and individual-level linkage across data sources [29].

The Danish hospital sector

Hospitals are publicly owned and predominantly managed by the five regional authorities, which are also responsible for planning and financing hospital services [29]. The Danish healthcare system is characterised by a high degree of central coordination, but hospitals retain considerable autonomy in organising local working environments and implementing workplace interventions. Most hospital employees are publicly employed under national collective agreements and work within standardised frameworks for staffing, scheduling and occupational health and safety regulations [30]. This institutional setup enables the implementation of large-scale organisational interventions within a relatively controlled setting while also allowing for comparative analysis across institutions. In Denmark, there has been a public focus on mental health in recent years, including efforts related to mental well-being and stress prevention in the workplace [31, 32]. The evaluated intervention was funded as part of a national initiative to reduce sickness absence in the public sector.

The intervention

The present study is part of an evaluation of a multi-level intervention implemented at one regional hospital and designed to prevent and mitigate the negative impact of high emotional demands in the workplace. The intervention hospital is located in a rural area. The characteristics of the residents in the catchment area of the hospital differ from the average Danish population by having a higher mean age and a lower socio-economic status. For several years, the rural location has challenged the hospital’s ability to recruit and retain medical staff. The process evaluation found that contextual factors such as high turnover, time pressure and overcrowding had a considerable impact on the implementation during the intervention period [28]. Further contextual information is provided in [28].

The intervention was initially planned to last approximately one and a half years but was ultimately extended to three years, running from 1 January, 2021 to 31 December, 2023. The overarching objective of the intervention programme was to improve the prevention and management of emotional demands across all organisational levels using an IGLO-model (Individual, Group, Leader and Organisational) with the ultimate objective of reducing sickness absence. At the individual level, psychological first aid training was part of the programme and around 85% of all staff completed the training. Several different activities were offered at the group level, including simulation-based training in de-escalation as well as debriefing, and a digital course on handling emotional demands, which was followed by joint discussions in department-level groups. At the leadership level, thematic workshops were held on how to manage sickness absence related to emotional strain. At the organisational level, each department formed a dedicated collaboration team comprising a line manager, a union representative and a work environment representative. These teams were trained to facilitate group discussions associated with the digital course component of the intervention. The intervention was delivered by a team of external consultants operating independently of the research team and implemented in close collaboration with the hospital. The role of the research team was solely to evaluate the intervention. This encompassed both a qualitative process evaluation and a quantitative assessment of its impact on sickness absence, staff turnover and relevant healthcare costs.

The majority of the activities across all levels were rolled out between January 2021 and December 2022 (first phase), where the individual-level implementation rate reached an acceptable level. Specific courses on handling emotional demands for doctors and secretaries were not conducted and completed until 2023 (second phase). For more details on the programme theory of the intervention, the specific activities and a comprehensive description of the implementation, we refer to the process evaluation by Jaspers et al. [28].

Methods

Data sources

This study is based on data from nationwide administrative and health registers in Denmark. Access to these government-maintained registries provides longitudinal data sources that contain routinely collected administrative, healthcare, and medical information. Individual-level linkage across data sources and lifelong follow-up are possible due to the unique personal identification number assigned to all Danish residents [29]. By making use of these detailed register data, we can assess the health effects based on use of healthcare services and pharmaceuticals in a real-world public sector context.

We identify our population of hospital employees using Statistics Denmark’s National Register of Work Absences, which includes monthly information on employment, including the production unit number (p-number) of the employer. A p-number is a unique identification number assigned by the Danish Central Business Register to each physical workplace or location where a company performs its activities. It is used to distinguish between different Danish worksites under the same company, which share a single central business register number. The p-number specifies the exact location where an employee works on a monthly level – in this study, a specific hospital.

Socio-demographic characteristics, including age, sex, ethnicity, marital status and number of children, were obtained from the Danish Civil Registration System [33]. Information on socioeconomic status, specifically annual income, was sourced from the Income Statistics Register [34], whereas educational attainment was retrieved from the Danish Education Register [35].

Study population

The study population comprises all hospital staff who were employed at the intervention hospital at any time during the study period from 2018 to 2023. As hospitals in Denmark are administered by the regional authorities [29], it is essential to select control hospitals from within the same region to account for regional variation in hospital governance. Comparable control hospitals located in predominantly rural areas were selected based on parallel trends in all outcome measures during the pre-intervention period (2018–2020). Staff observations from these hospitals were subsequently pooled into a single control group. Staff who were employed at both the intervention and control hospitals within the same year are excluded from the study population. This ensures that individuals are not simultaneously exposed to both intervention and control conditions within the same period, which could otherwise contaminate the comparison between groups.

Outcomes

Pathways to mental healthcare in primary care

General practitioners (GPs) play a central role in the Danish healthcare system in the management of minor mental health conditions. As a first step, they offer talk therapy in their own clinics. In addition, psychometric tests are performed through which the need for evaluation and potential further referral is evaluated. In most cases, general practitioners prescribe antidepressant or anxiolytic medication when clinically indicated and refer patients for further treatment when additional or specialised care is required. In this way, GPs act as primary ‘gatekeepers’ with the authority to refer patients with mental health problems to psychologists or, if they have more severe mental health conditions, to specialists operating in private practice or community outpatient clinics, as well as to inpatient and outpatient hospital services [36]. Although there is universal coverage, patients must make partial out-of-pocket copayments for private psychologist consultations following a GP referral and for prescription medicines [29]. Psychological treatment can also be purchased in the private healthcare sector with full out-of-pocket payment and without a referral from a general practitioner. Medications administered during treatment in public hospitals are provided free of charge to patients, but prescriptions redeemed in community pharmacies in the primary healthcare sector are subject to some patient copayments [37].

Within this system, Danish hospital employees do not generally have access to separate occupational healthcare services for clinical mental health treatment beyond advisory or preventive functions. When seeking treatment for mental health conditions, hospital employees primarily use the same publicly funded healthcare services as the general population. This includes access to GPs, psychologists, psychiatrists, and hospital-based mental healthcare services through the standard referral pathways described above, with the GP as gatekeepers. Occupational health services, where available, mainly provide workplace-related assessments, guidance, and preventive initiatives, and do not typically offer clinical mental health treatment.

Healthcare cost outcomes

The study examines two healthcare cost outcomes derived from nationwide registers. The first outcome captures GP-related healthcare costs. These costs include all reimbursed GP services and are obtained from the Danish National Health Service Registry [38], which contains detailed information on primary care contacts, including service codes and associated reimbursement fees. GP visits are included as a broad outcome measure because general practitioners serve as the primary point of contact for individuals experiencing mental health problems in Denmark. Importantly, consultations related to stress or other minor mental health conditions may occur without generating specific mental health service codes or prescriptions. Including overall GP visit costs therefore allows us to capture healthcare utilisation potentially related to such “silent” or non-coded mental health conditions.

The second outcome is a composite measure capturing costs related to the management and treatment of minor mental health conditions in primary care, including mental healthcare services in the primary sector and redeemed prescriptions for antidepressant and anxiolytic medications [36]. The composite outcome includes costs associated with GP-administered psychometric tests, GP-provided talk therapy, psychologist consultations, and redeemed prescriptions for antidepressants (ATC: N06AB and N06AX) and anxiolytics (ATC: N03AE, N05BA, N05CD, and N05CF).

Costs related to primary care services are obtained from the Danish National Health Service Registry, where each service code is linked to a nationally regulated reimbursement fee [39, 40]. In the present study, we use aggregated costs associated with GP visits as an outcome measure. The detailed service-level information enables consistent identification of relevant services included in the composite outcome measure, such as psychometric tests, GP talk therapy, and psychologist treatment. Costs related to prescription medications are obtained from the Danish National Prescription Registry [29], which records the total dispensing price, including VAT, for each redeemed prescription. For the composite outcome, total mental healthcare costs are calculated as the sum of these components.

All costs are price-adjusted to the January 2023 price level using the Danish net price index from Statistics Denmark [41]. Costs are originally recorded in Danish kroner (DKK) and converted to euro (EUR) using a fixed exchange rate of 7.45 DKK per EUR, corresponding to the exchange rate during the study period.

Analytical strategy

We apply a difference-in-differences (DiD) design to evaluate the impact of the intervention. This quasi-experimental approach is well suited for assessing longitudinal intervention effects when randomised trials are not feasible. By including a control group (comparable hospitals without intervention), we form a DiD model, which strengthens causal inference by accounting for underlying secular trends and external factors that influence outcomes over time [42]. Our empirical strategy is guided by the recent practitioner-oriented framework for DiD designs proposed by Baker et al. [43]. To verify the parallel trends assumption, we estimate an event study specification with year indicators and 2019 as the reference year, and assess whether the estimated pre-intervention coefficients differ systematically between treatment and control hospitals. The event study graphs provide a visual assessment of whether pre-intervention trends are unconditionally parallel across both components of the two-part model.

Study period

The first of January, 2021 serves as the index date in our analysis, and the post-period runs until 31 December, 2023, which aligns with the implementation period. Consequently, the analysis captures only the short-term effects of the intervention.

At the intervention hospital, staff were gradually enrolled in activities at all four organisational levels, which resulted in a progressive increase in implementation over the three-year period. By the end of 2022, the level of implementation was considered sufficient for the intervention to plausibly generate an overall effect. Based on this, the post-intervention period in our analysis is divided into two phases: Phase 1 (January 2021 to December 2022) and Phase 2 (January 2023 to December 2023). These phases are compared with the pre-intervention period (January 2018 to December 2020).

The study period coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, but both the intervention hospital and all control hospitals were subject to the same national infection control policies, which helps mitigate potential bias from macro-level shocks. Importantly, descriptive statistics for the period 2020–2023 show parallel trends in COVID-19 cases across the municipalities in which the intervention and control hospitals are located (Fig. 2 in Appendix). Access to primary care may have been restricted during parts of the pandemic, potentially influencing overall healthcare utilisation and thereby our outcome measures. However, trends in annual contacts with general practitioners likewise followed parallel trends across the municipalities where the intervention and control hospitals are located (Fig. 3 in Appendix).

Although baseline demographic and socioeconomic characteristics may differ between the intervention hospital and the control hospitals, we do not include covariates in the model. The DiD framework accounts for time-invariant differences between groups, provided that the pre-intervention trends in outcomes are parallel across both components of the two-part model. Moreover, any intervention-induced changes in time-varying staff characteristics would likely operate through changes in staff composition, for example through differential staff turnover. Such changes may themselves constitute part of the intervention effect and therefore represent potential mediating mechanisms rather than confounders, implying that adjusting for them could introduce bias and potentially attenuating the estimated effect [43].

Two-part difference-in-differences model

We estimate the effect of the intervention using a two-part model. The reason for this is the zero-inflated nature and positively skewed cost distribution of the outcome variables, which is why the model (1) is implemented in two parts. We chose to use Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) for the analysis because we are investigating the effects of a multilevel hospital intervention with a focus on estimating the population average effect. Furthermore, the GEE approach is well-suited for non-normally distributed response variables, which aligns with the nature of our data, in which not all outcomes follow a normal distribution [44]. GEE is particularly suitable for this type of analysis because it accounts for the correlation of repeated measures within the same individual or hospital, which is common in longitudinal or clustered data [45]. By using GEE, we can obtain robust estimates of the population-level effects of the intervention, even in the presence of such correlations. Moreover, GEE does not require a precise specification of the correlation structure, making it a flexible approach for handling complex data where different potential correlation structures can be applied to test the robustness of the estimates. In this study, we select the correlation structure based on assumptions about the underlying correlation patterns in the data. Specifically, we expect that both an autoregressive structure (AR-1), where correlations attenuate over time, and an exchangeable structure, where correlations remain constant within individuals, are plausible representations of the data. Therefore, we estimate all models using both correlation structures to assess the sensitivity of the results to this specification and to evaluate the robustness of our findings [46].

For each outcome we select the optimal model specification - the distribution and link function for each part - using 10-fold cross-validation. Crucially, rather than tuning Part 1 and Part 2 independently, we evaluate each candidate specification based on the combined prediction from both components, calculated as the predicted probability of non-zero costs from Part 1 multiplied by the predicted cost conditional on positive costs from Part 2. We then select the specification that minimises the mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and observed costs across the validation folds. MAE is chosen because it is less sensitive to extreme values, which are common in healthcare cost data, while providing a single metric that evaluates the overall predictive performance of the two-part model. The final model specification differs slightly across outcomes. For GP-related costs, Part 1 is specified as a probit model and Part 2 as a gamma distribution with an identity link. For costs related to mental health care utilisation, Part 1 is specified as a logit model and Part 2 likewise as a gamma distribution with an identity link.

Marginal effects

The combined marginal effects of the DiD estimator are calculated as the product of expectations from the first and second parts of the models, thereby accounting for both the probability of any positive costs (part 1) and the conditional expectation given positive costs (part 2). Subgroup analysis

As part of the analytical strategy, we conduct two subgroup analyses to explore potential heterogeneity in the intervention effects. The process evaluation of the intervention concluded that the implementation was successful but that there were differences in implementation for different subgroups. Specifically, doctors engaged less with the intervention, as doctors as a group, were characterised by more hierarchical structures, which was a barrier for sharing emotional vulnerability and initiating preventive actions. The rest of the healthcare personnel, on the other hand, generally exhibited high levels of implementation [28]. This highlights the need to examine whether the intervention may have different effects for key staff groups. Based on insights from the process evaluation we perform subgroup analyses for the two largest and most central professional groups; nurses and doctors, as these groups may experience the effects of the intervention effects differently. The categories nurses and doctors are defined based on highest educational attainment, where doctors include all levels of the medical profession.

Results

Descriptives

The baseline characteristics of the intervention and control group in year 2019 are presented in Table 1. Overall, staff at the intervention hospital is comparable to staff at the control hospitals. The only significant difference is a lower income in the intervention group compared to the control group (€41,666.55 vs. €44,616,16, p < 0.001) and fewer children under 18 years (0.71 vs. 0.80, p < 0.001). In terms of educational attainment, the intervention group has a slightly lower proportion of individuals with primary school (3.6% vs. 4.8%) but a higher proportion of individuals with upper secondary or vocational education compared to the control group (31.5% vs. 28.8%) and a lower proportion of individuals with a higher education (21.5% vs. 22.8%). Moreover, the intervention group appears to have higher GP-related costs than the control group. While baseline differences exist, these differences are accounted for in the DiD design, which focuses on changes over time rather than baseline levels, provided that the parallel trends assumption is satisfied.

Subgroup analysis

The event-study plots in Appendix Figs. 5 and 6 show parallel pre-intervention trends for both outcomes for the two subgroups of nurses and doctors, respectively. The parallel trends is fulfilled in both parts of the two-part model and robust to specification on correlation structure.

Nurses

The baseline characteristics for nurses in the intervention and control hospitals in 2019 are presented in Table 2 in Appendix. Overall, nurses at the intervention hospital are broadly comparable to nurses at the control hospitals. The only statistically significant difference is a slightly lower proportion of female staff in the intervention group compared with the control group (94.2% vs. 96.7%, p = 0.009). No significant differences are observed in age, income, number of children, children below 18 years, or civil status. As expected, all nurses have a medium-long higher education. Baseline healthcare costs are also similar between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences in either GP-related costs or minor mental healthcare costs.

Figure 1, Panel B and C, reports subgroup results for nurses and doctors. For nurses (Panel B), the intervention show a statistically significant cost-reducing effect on costs related to minor mental health in Phase 2, and these findings are fairly robust to the choice of correlation structure. The yearly cost reduction of approximately €16 (Table 3 in Appendix) corresponds to a relative cost reduction of approximately 80% when comparing the marginal effect estimate with the mean baseline costs for minor mental health costs among nurses on approximately €20 EUR (Table 2 in Appendix). When assessing the event-study plots in Appendix Fig. 5, Panel B, there are indications that the statistically significant marginal effects on costs related to mental health care are driven by a statistically significant reduction in the level of costs among users of mental health care.

Doctors

The baseline characteristics for doctors in the intervention and control hospitals in 2019 are presented in Table 4 in Appendix. Overall, doctors at the intervention hospital differ somewhat from those at the control hospitals. Doctors in the intervention group are on average younger than those in the control group (40.61 vs. 43.63 years, p < 0.001) and have a lower equivalent disposable income (€52,008.44 vs. €61,276.43, p < 0.001). They also have fewer children on average (1.02 vs. 1.44, p < 0.001) and fewer children below the age of 18 (0.58 vs. 0.88, p < 0.001). In terms of civil status, a larger proportion of doctors in the intervention group are single compared to the control group (57.8% vs. 41.8%), while a smaller proportion are married (42.2% vs. 57.4%). As expected, all doctors have a long higher education. Baseline healthcare costs are broadly similar across groups, with no statistically significant differences observed in GP-related costs or minor mental healthcare costs.

The subgroup analysis for doctors, Fig. 1, Panel C, shows statistically significant effects on GP-related costs in phase 2 of the intervention. In relative terms, the cost-reducing effect of approximately €9 (Table 2 in Appendix) corresponds to an 31% decrease when compared with the yearly baseline costs for doctors on approximately €29 (Table 4 in Appendix). However, for doctors in Phase 2 there is no statistically significant cost-reducing effect on mental health-related costs, and this result is robust to the choice of correlation structure.

Discussion

Our results show no overall effect of the intervention on mental health-related costs. However, subgroup analyses indicate a cost-reducing effect on mental health-related costs among nurses and GP-related costs among doctors.

Across both phases, the marginal effects for the full sample of hospital staff were small for both outcomes, suggesting no substantial changes in healthcare costs at the workforce level. However, the minimum detectable effect (MDE) calculations Table 5 in Appendix indicate that the study was powered to detect cost changes of approximately €6–€7 for general practitioner costs and €8–€9 for minor mental healthcare costs. Effects smaller than these thresholds fall below the detectable range of the study. Consequently, the null findings in the main analysis cannot be interpreted as evidence of no intervention effect, as modest cost changes of around ±€6 (approximately 10% of mean GP costs) and ±€8 (around 40–45% of mean minor mental healthcare costs) may remain undetected.

The subgroup analyses further suggest that intervention effects may differ across staff groups. For nurses, a reduction in minor mental healthcare costs is observed in Phase 2, while for doctors a reduction in general practitioner costs emerges in the same period. In contrast, the minimum detectable effect for minor mental healthcare costs among doctors is particularly large, implying that even substantial effects would be difficult to detect statistically in this subgroup. As such, the absence of detectable effects for this outcome among doctors cannot be interpreted as evidence that the intervention had no meaningful impact.

Taken together, the analyses suggest heterogeneous effects across different groups of professionals. Group-specific responses i different directions could therefore attenuate the aggregate estimates for the full workforce, potentially driving the overall effects towards zero despite meaningful changes within particular staff categories. When interpreted in light of the power calculations, the results indicate no evidence that the intervention had an overall effect on costs. However, we cannot rule out that the absence of statistically significant effects may be driven by limited statistical power.

Our findings are in line with the recent studies, which generally reports mixed or modest effects of organisational interventions [22, 23]. The interventions in earlier studies have varied in focus area and intervention activities [19–23]; nonetheless, they have highlighted the need for more knowledge on effective strategies to prevent and manage emotional demands at work. A key implication of our findings is that future research may benefit from recognising that multilevel organisational interventions can have heterogeneous effects across staff groups.

The interpretation of the process evaluation is that there was a genuine improvement in preventive practices at the hospital with a high level of implementation among healthcare personnel such as nurses [28]. However, despite the organisational anchoring of the intervention, the hospital itself did not have sufficient influence on key contextual factors, such as high turnover, time pressure and overcrowding, or on patient composition (which is shaped by local demographics and results in higher emotional demands due to a high degree of patient suffering, death, sorrow, worry and frustration) [28]. Previous research suggests that interventions are difficult to implement in organisations already facing substantial work environment challenges, even though the need for change is greatest in these settings [47]. Therefore, we interpret the results as indicating that the intervention might produce further positive results than improved preventive practices in more favourable contextual conditions. In contrast, if these conditions remain constant, implementing a multilevel intervention of this kind may not be effective. The results of the process evaluation suggest that there are potentially effective components within the intervention that should not be dismissed based solely on the effect evaluation [28].

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

One of the strengths of our study is the use of valid Danish register data, which provides reliable and comprehensive information on costs related to mental health-related healthcare utilisation. The strong design – employing a DiD approach with a control group – enables robust causal inference by controlling for underlying trends and external factors. In addition, the study benefits from extensive population coverage, including a large and representative sample of healthcare workers from both intervention and control hospitals, which ensures the generalisability and robustness of the findings. Another important strength is the application of two-part models, which account for the semi-continuous nature of cost data, separating the probability of any use from the level of costs conditional on use. This approach provides more precise and nuanced estimates of intervention effects, improving the analytical robustness and allowing us to capture effects that simpler models might overlook.

Limitations

We considered COVID-19 as a potential confounder. Although municipality-level transmission appears to have evolved similarly across the intervention and control hospital catchment areas Fig. 2 in Appendix, we cannot determine whether the virus affected the two populations differently; if it did, health care utilisation and costs related to mental health could have been influenced. The intervention hospital serves an older population, yet we found no indication that it faced a disproportionate pandemic burden relative to the control hospitals. The intervention hospitals dedicated COVID-19 ward closed shortly after the initial outbreak due to low admissions, and staff turnover remained stable during the study period. Moreover, trends in GP contacts evolved similarly across the municipalities served by the intervention and control hospitals Fig. 3 in Appendix, suggesting comparable shocks to primary-care access. We consider it reasonable to assume that the majority of employees at both the intervention and control hospitals reside in the surrounding areas. Accordingly, any pandemic-related reduction in primary-care access likely affected both groups similarly and is therefore unlikely to bias our DiD estimates. We nonetheless acknowledge COVID-19 as a contextual limitation, as the pandemic may have affected health systems more broadly during the study period. We are not aware of publicly available data that would permit a systematic comparison of the overall COVID-19 burden across hospitals (e.g., organisational reconfiguration, staff redistribution, expanded testing activities, or changes in inpatient treatment). However, we have no reason to believe that the intervention hospital experienced a greater burden than the control hospitals.

As the intervention was implemented in an actively working population, healthcare costs were relatively low on average and likely highly skewed, with a small proportion of employees accounting for a large share of total costs. This may limit the ability to detect effects at the aggregate level unless these high-cost individuals were directly affected by the intervention. We address this challenge by applying two-part models that separately capture changes in having any healthcare costs and changes in costs conditional on positive costs. Nevertheless, a floor effect cannot be ruled out, potentially constraining the scope for observing reductions in mental healthcare costs following the intervention. Moreover, the intervention may primarily have contributed to preventing the onset of new minor mental health conditions rather than reducing existing healthcare costs. In such a scenario, healthcare costs would be expected to remain close to baseline levels or to increase more slowly compared with the control group rather than decline. Investigating the potential preventive effect on the onset of new mental health conditions would require a longer follow-up period, which was not possible due to data restrictions. Interpretation of the findings is further complicated by the COVID-19 pandemic, during which access to healthcare services may have been restricted. This may have dampened expected increases in healthcare costs in both groups and obscured the relative difference-in-differences effect, which is estimated relative to baseline healthcare costs observed before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic.

One limitation of the study is that five departments at the intervention hospital were not included in the intervention. The decision not to involve these departments was a pre-implementation decision because their management was associated with other hospitals in the region. Unfortunately, we were unable to identify these individuals to exclude them from the analysis. As a result, approximately 10% of the employees at the intervention hospital are misclassified as ‘treated’, and we must assume that our results represent a lower bound of the potential effects.

Moreover, we do not have information on whether smaller-scale or department-specific initiatives may have been implemented at individual control hospitals. However, to our knowledge, no major workplace interventions comparable to the intervention under study were implemented at the control hospitals during the study period. Hence, we reduce the risk of bias arising from unobserved hospital-specific changes by including three control hospitals rather than relying on a single comparison site. To the extent that any unobserved initiatives aimed at improving the working environment for staff were implemented at control hospitals during the study period, such interventions would likely attenuate the estimated treatment effect. Consequently, our estimated effect should be interpreted as a conservative (lower-bound) estimate of the intervention’s impact.

Policy implications

The findings of this study raise important considerations for policies on the societal value of investing in workplace mental health support, particularly within publicly funded systems. The study contributes to the ongoing debate on how best to allocate resources to support healthcare workers’ mental health and the broader implications for workforce management in the public sector. Although the intervention may have had effects for specific subgroups, and modest effects at the workforce level cannot be ruled out, the absence of detectable overall effects highlights the challenges of achieving measurable system-level changes through multilevel interventions targeting emotional demands in healthcare when counteracting contextual constraints persist. In particular, it remains uncertain whether investments in the prevention and management of high emotional demands will generate substantial organisational impacts if fundamental challenges, such as overcrowding, high staff turnover and time pressure, remain unresolved. This evaluation therefore raises questions about where in the organisation interventions should be prioritised and suggests that policymakers may need to consider addressing upstream structural issues alongside downstream psychosocial interventions if the goal is to support a sustainable hospital workforce.
