Engineered skin commensal nanogels mitigate psoriasiform inflammation through dual regulation of cutaneous flora and host immunity
Authors: Yang Lv, Jinxiao Li, Zicong Xu, Yutao Zhang, Weijing Li, Xiaoya Kan, Shuqi Dai, Yiliang Lin, Xiaoling Xu, Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder characterized by immune dysregulation and alterations in the cutaneous microbiota. Current microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies predominantly involve the topical delivery of live probiotics using hydrogel platforms. However, these approaches are hindered by limitations such as poor bacterial viability, transient colonization, and unresolved safety concerns, while often failing to address systemic immune dysfunction. To overcome these challenges, we developed nanogels derived from Staphylococcus epidermidis lysates via EDC/NHS crosslinking. The optimized formulation exhibited a uniform hydrodynamic diameter of 98.3 ± 30.2 nm and remained stable for up to 5 days without significant protein degradation. Leveraging their sub-100 nm size, these nanogels enabled multiple therapeutic mechanisms. First, they penetrated the epidermal barrier through psoriatic scales and were internalized by target cells, leading to suppressed keratinocyte hyperproliferation and reduced local inflammation. Second, they trafficked systemically via the cutaneous microvasculature, resulting in decreased activation of splenic dendritic cells (CD11c + CD80+) and normalization of the CD4+/CD8+ T-lymphocyte ratio. Concurrently, residual nanogels retained on the skin surface selectively inhibited the proliferation of Staphylococcus aureus while promoting the enrichment of commensal Staphylococcus species, thereby restoring microbial homeostasis and reinforcing barrier integrity. This strategy addresses key limitations of conventional live biotherapeutics by leveraging the enhanced biocompatibility of bacterial components and the multifunctional advantages of nanoscale delivery. It offers a synergistic and safe approach for effective psoriasis management.Keywords: Psoriasis, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Nanogel, Cutaneous flora, Host immunity

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disorder that affects approximately 1%–3% of the global population, with an estimated 40.8 million documented cases worldwide in 2019. Nearly 60% of patients progress to moderate-to-severe disease, characterized not only by typical erythema, scaling, and pruritus but also by systemic complications such as psoriatic arthritis, cardiovascular comorbidities, and metabolic disorders [1]. This multifaceted burden significantly impairs patients' quality of life and imposes substantial socioeconomic costs, driven by reduced workforce participation, elevated healthcare expenditures, and associated psychological morbidity.

Despite advances in therapeutic options—including topical agents (e.g., corticosteroids and vitamin D3 analogs), phototherapy, conventional systemic drugs (e.g., methotrexate and cyclosporine), and biologics (e.g., IL-17/IL-23 inhibitors)—existing treatments remain suboptimal. While biologics offer improved efficacy, their use is constrained by high costs, dependence on injection, increased susceptibility to infections, and high relapse rates upon discontinuation [2]; for instance, recurrence rates can reach as high as 80% following withdrawal of TNF-α inhibitors. Topical corticosteroids, although widely used, are associated with adverse effects such as skin atrophy and dyspigmentation, and their efficacy is often limited in moderate-to-severe disease. Together, these limitations underscore the urgent need for safer, more durable, and cost-effective therapeutic alternatives.

Lysate hydrogels represent a relatively underexplored category within the field of hydrogels [[3], [4], [5]]. The core concept involves utilizing lysates derived from cells, tissues, or bacteria to fabricate hydrogels for therapeutic purposes. Wang et al. [6] developed tissue lysate-crosslinked hydrogels that promote anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization through sustained protein release. Similarly, Li et al. [7] engineered hybrid hydrogels capable of reprogramming immune responses against malignancies. Collectively, these platforms leverage endogenous biomolecules to serve both as therapeutic agents and structural components of the hydrogel matrix. However, research on bacterial lysate hydrogels remains scarce, which opens up new therapeutic avenues for bacteria-related diseases. It is worth noting that although live probiotic delivery systems have been widely investigated for skin microbiota modulation, their translational potential is constrained by several inherent limitations. Upon topical application, live bacteria are susceptible to physical removal and environmental stressors; more critically, exogenous probiotics often fail to stably colonize or persist on psoriatic skin due to competition from resident commensals and impaired barrier function. Even when viable, their metabolic activity and therapeutic efficacy can be significantly compromised. In contrast, bacterial lysates circumvent these issues entirely, as they contain a comprehensive repertoire of bacterial components, including structural proteins, nucleic acids, and cell wall fragment, that can directly deliver immunomodulatory signals without requiring bacterial viability, colonization, or sustained metabolic function. This rationale underpins our choice of a lysate-based rather than a live probiotic strategy.

Emerging evidence supports a dual-pathology framework in psoriasis, involving systemic immune dysregulation in concert with disruption of the cutaneous microbiota [8]. Affected individuals exhibit marked depletion of commensal bacteria such as Staphylococcus epidermidis and Cutibacterium species, alongside an overgrowth of pathogenic Staphylococcus aureus [9]. This microbial imbalance compromises epidermal barrier integrity and sustains pro-inflammatory cascades [10]. Notably, S. epidermidis contributes to beneficial immunomodulation through microbial–host crosstalk [11,12], positioning microbiota restoration as a viable therapeutic strategy in psoriasis management.

Herein, we report the engineering of nanogels derived from S. epidermidis lysates via controlled EDC/NHS crosslinking. The resulting nanogels exhibited a uniform hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 98 nm and maintained structural integrity for 7 days without significant protein degradation. Leveraging their sub-100 nm dimensions, these nanogels enabled multiple parallel therapeutic pathways: (1) transepidermal penetration through disrupted psoriatic scales facilitated cellular internalization, leading to suppression of keratinocyte hyperproliferation and attenuation of local inflammation; (2) systemic translocation via the cutaneous microvasculature reduced the activation of splenic dendritic cells and corrected T-lymphocyte imbalances; and (3) retention on the skin surface selectively enriched commensal Staphylococcus species while inhibiting pathogenic S. aureus colonization, thereby restoring microbial equilibrium and reinforcing epidermal barrier function.

This lysate-derived nanoplatform circumvents the inherent limitations of live probiotic therapies by harnessing native bacterial components to achieve superior biocompatibility and leveraging nanotechnology-enabled tissue penetration. It represents a synbiotic-inspired therapeutic strategy that concurrently restores cutaneous microbial homeostasis and modulates systemic immunity, offering a holistic and effective approach for psoriasis management.

Materials and methods

Bacterial culture and preparation of lysate

Staphylococcus epidermidis (1.4260, CGMCC, China) was cultured in Nutrient Broth (Cat#N8300, Solarbio, China), under conditions of 37 °C and 220 rpm/min. The bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 12000 g, 4 °C for 10 min to pellet the bacteria, followed by removal of the supernatant and resuspension of the bacterial pellet in a certain volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The resuspended bacterial suspension was subjected to three cycles of freeze-thawing, followed by ultrasonication at 400 W for 10 min. After ultrasonication, the mixture was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter to produce the bacterial lysate.

Preparation of skin commensal bacteria-derived Nanogel

Prior to the formulation of the skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel, a crosslinking agent was prepared by dissolving 15 mg EDC (N807578, MACKLIN, China) and 9 mg NHS (H109330, aladdin, China) in 240 μL of pure water. Subsequently, after determining the optimal ratios of EDC, NHS and S. epidermidis lysate, a final mixture ratio of crosslinking agent: S. epidermidis lysate: PBS = 2:3:1 was selected for the preparation (e.g., 2 mL crosslinking agent + 3 mL S. epidermidis lysate +1 mL PBS). Two different types of skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel were prepared based on the order of addition: one with the crosslinking agent added first followed by the S. epidermidis lysate, and the other with the S. epidermidis lysate added first followed by the crosslinking agent. The prepared solution was incubated at 4 °C for 24 h with shaking at 300 rpm to obtain the skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel, which was then stored at 4 °C until use.

Characterization of skin commensal bacteria-derived Nanogel

A volume of 10 μL of the skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel was deposited onto a copper grid. After air-drying, the sample was characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for its structural analysis.

Stability of skin commensal bacteria-derived Nanogel

The design concept of this study is based on existing research [13]. The particle size and zeta potential changes of the skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel were continuously measured over a period of 7 days. Additionally, the protein concentration of the skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel was determined daily for 7 days using a BCA kit (Cat No.P0009, Beyotime, China).

In vivo imaging

To visualize the retention of the skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel in mice, it was labeled with CY5-SE (HY-D0819A, MCE, China). The prepared skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel was mixed with an appropriate amount of CY5-SE and stirred overnight under dark conditions. Unbound CY5-SE was removed by centrifugation through a 30 kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane at 4000 rpm for 10 min. Nine female BALB/C mice (6-7 weeks, 18-20 g) were divided into three groups. The hair on the back of each mouse was shaved, and depilatory cream was applied to ensure a smooth surface. A piece of gauze was wrapped around the back, and the skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel was applied to the gauze. Based on protein quantification, each mouse received 2.5 mL of 2.0 mg/mL skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel. Three mice were sacrificed at 6, 12, and 24 h post-application, respectively. The skin from the back was removed and fixed, and an imaging system was used to analyze the skin of each mouse.

Cell lines and animals

Three cell lines were used in this study: H9c2 (CL-0089, Procell, China), NTCT clone 929 (CL-0137, Procell, China), and RAW 264.7 (CL-0190, Procell, China). The animals used were female BALB/C mice (6-7 weeks, 18-20 g) (BALB/C mouse, HS, China). All mice were housed in a standard pathogen-free environment with free access to water and food.

Cellular uptake

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 20,000 cells per well and stimulated with culture medium containing 1 μg/mL LPS for 24 h after seeding. The wells of the 6-well plate were designated as follows: no skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel, uptake for 4 h, uptake for 6 h, uptake for 8 h, and uptake for 10 h. The prepared skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel was labeled with CY3 (HY-D0822, MCE, China) by stirring overnight under dark conditions until complete labeling. Thereafter, the CY3-labeled nanogel was centrifuged using a 30 kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tube at 4000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove free, unconjugated CY3. A volume of 150 μL of 2.0 mg/mL labeled skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel was added to each well at the designated time points. After the last well (10 h) completed the uptake, the medium was removed from all wells, and the cells were washed three times with PBS (1-2 min each), followed by fixation with tissue fixative for 15 min and washing again three times with PBS (1-2 min each). Cells were then stained with DAPI for 5 min under dark conditions. After staining, the cells were observed with a confocal microscope to evaluate the uptake. After microscopic observation, the cells from each well were scraped and collected, and the uptake was assessed by flow cytometry.

Cell viability

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 method. Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 5000 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The medium was then removed, and the wells were washed with PBS. Subsequently, 100 μL of fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The cell viability (%) was calculated using the following formula: (sample well absorbance - blank well absorbance)/(control well absorbance - blank well absorbance) × 100.

Hemolytic assay

First, a 2% red blood cell suspension was prepared. One milliliter of blood from a healthy mouse was collected into an anticoagulant tube, which was inverted several times to prevent blood clotting. Five milliliters of 0.9% NaCl solution was added to the tube, mixed thoroughly, and then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and this procedure was repeated three times until the supernatant no longer appeared red. The red blood cells were then suspended in 0.9% NaCl solution to a final concentration of 2%. Four sets of reagents were prepared: Group 1 (negative control): 0.5 mL 2% red blood cell suspension + 0.5 mL 0.9% NaCl solution; Group 2 (positive control): 0.5 mL 2% red blood cell suspension + 0.5 mL distilled water; Group 3 (sample without red blood cells): 0.95 mL 0.9% NaCl solution + 0.05 mL 2.0 mg/mL skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel; Group 4 (sample with red blood cells): 0.5 mL 2% red blood cell suspension + 0.45 mL 0.9% NaCl solution + 0.05 mL 2.0 mg/mL skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel. Each set was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and hemolysis was observed. The supernatant from each set was collected and measured for absorbance at 541 nm.

TNF-α and IL-6 expression study in RAW 264.7 cell lines

This method was adapted from a previous study [14]. The levels of TNF-α (EK0527, BOSTER, China) and IL-6 (EK0411, BOSTER, China) in RAW 264.7 cells treated with wound dressing were evaluated using ELISA. Cells were seeded at a density of 30,000 cells per well in a 24-well plate. The wells were divided into five groups: Group 1 (negative control) without any reagent; Group 2 (positive control); Groups 3-5 with different concentrations of wound dressing. The cells were incubated overnight. The next day, Group 1 was given fresh medium, while Groups 2-5 were given fresh medium containing 1 μg/mL LPS to promote the secretion of inflammatory cytokines. After 24 h of treatment, Groups 3-5 were treated with 50 μL of 0.1 mg/mL wound dressing, 0.5 mg/mL wound dressing, and 2 mg/mL wound dressing, respectively, for another 24 h. The cell culture supernatants from each group were collected for ELISA analysis.

Animal assay

The study was approved by the institutional review board and the local research Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Shuren University, the ethical code is 20251048. Thirty-six mice were randomly divided into six groups, with six mice in each group (n = 6). The groups were as follows: blank control group, IMQ model group, positive drug group, and three groups with different concentrations of skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel. After one week of acclimatization, the experiment began. The hair on the back of each mouse was shaved, and depilatory cream was used to remove any remaining hair to ensure smooth skin. Except for the blank control group, the other five groups were treated with 62.5 mg of imiquimod (IMQ) cream (Med Shine, China) applied to a 1 cm × 1 cm area on the back for 10 consecutive days. Starting from day 7, 3 h after IMQ application, the positive drug group was treated with 50 mg of Dermovate (GSK, Turkey) applied to the same area on the back. For the nanogel groups, after binding the mice with gauze, three different concentrations of skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogel (0.1 mg/mL, 0.5 mg/mL, and 2.0 mg/mL) were applied to the three groups, respectively. The mice were weighed daily. The Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scoring system was used to evaluate the skin lesions on the back of the mice, assessing three criteria: erythema, extent, and thickness. Each criterion was scored from 0 to 4, with a maximum total score of 12. For each criterion, a score of 0 indicated no significant symptoms, 1 indicated mild symptoms, 2 indicated moderate symptoms, 3 indicated marked symptoms, and 4 indicated severe symptoms. The total score for the three criteria represented the PASI score for each mouse on that day. Starting from day 7, photos of the mice were taken daily. At the end of the treatment, the mice were sacrificed, collect blood samples and send them to Hangzhou Haoke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for testing, and samples of various organs and skin were collected. Flow cytometry was used to detect the expression of CD3, CD4, and CD8 in the mouse spleen, as well as the expression of CD11c, CD80, and CD86 in the back skin.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Mouse heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and back skin were fixed with tissue fixative and sectioned. Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining was used for histological analysis of the sections. Immunohistochemical analysis included three indicators in the mouse spleen: CD11c, CD80, and CD86. Immunofluorescence staining included two indicators in the mouse back skin: CD68.

16S rRNA sequencing

A kit (GDS-01A/GDS-01B, GEWEI Bio·Tech, China) was used to collect samples from the back skin of each mouse. A 4 cm × 4 cm area on the back skin of the mouse was divided into four rectangular sections from top to bottom. The swab was moistened with the wetting solution before collection, and moderate pressure was applied to ensure adequate contact between the swab and the skin. The swab was rubbed back and forth more than five times in each area. After each area was wiped, the swab was rotated 90 °C before wiping the next area to ensure sufficient microbial samples were collected on the entire swab. After collection, the swab was broken and immersed in the preservation solution for subsequent testing.

TNF-α, IL-6, and IgE expression study in skin

One hundred milligrams of mouse back skin was taken and mixed with RIPA (HKW2011A, HaoKe, China) and PMSF to promote lysis. The tissue was ground during lysis, and the resulting liquid was collected. The mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 30 min to remove unlysed components, and the supernatant was collected for detection using the corresponding kits. The levels of TNF-α (EK0527, BOSTER, China), IL-6 (EK0411, BOSTER, China), and IgE (MM-45386M2, MEIMIAN, China) were measured using their respective kits.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assay to determine the expression levels of IL-1β and iNOS in skin

Sample collection and processing

Collect tissue samples. Cut the tissue blocks into small pieces and grind them into a powder in liquid nitrogen. Transfer all samples immediately to pre-chilled RNase-free tubes and store at −80 °C for later use.

Total RNA extraction

Remove the frozen samples. Add 1 mL of TRIzol (AG21101, Accurate Biology) to every 50 mg of tissue. Mix by repeated pipetting and let stand at room temperature for 5 min to allow the nucleic acid-protein complex to dissociate fully. Subsequently, add 200 μL of trichloromethane (67-66-3, Yonghua Chemical Co., Ltd.), vortex vigorously for 15 s, and let stand at room temperature for 3 min. Centrifuge the sample at 4 °C and 12,000 g for 15 min. After centrifugation, three layers will form: a red organic phase at the bottom, a white protein phase in the middle, and a colourless aqueous phase at the top (the RNA is located in the aqueous phase). Carefully transfer the upper aqueous phase to a new RNase-free Eppendorf tube, add an equal volume of isopropyl alcohol (R018247, RHAWN), gently invert to mix, and leave to stand at room temperature for 10 min. Centrifuge the mixture at 4 °C and 12,000 g for 10 min, then discard the supernatant. Subsequently, add 1 mL of 75% ethanol and gently wash the pellet by inverting the EP tube. Centrifuge the sample at 4 °C and 7500 g for 5 min, then discard the supernatant. Repeat the washing step once. Allow the pellet to air-dry at room temperature until the ethanol has completely evaporated. Finally, add 20 μL of DEPC-treated water, tap the bottom of the tube gently, and incubate in a 55 °C water bath for 10 min to dissolve the RNA. Place the RNA solution on ice and set aside.

Reverse transcription to synthesise cDNA

Use fast all-in-one RT kit (RT001, Esscience Biotech)and follow the instructions provided. Prepare the reaction mixture on ice. The first reaction (total volume 16 μL) contains: 2 μg total RNA, 2 μL DNase, and RNase-free deionised water to make up to 16 μL. Leave at room temperature for 5 min, then place on ice until ready for use. Add 4 μL of 5x RT Mix to the aforementioned 16 μL reaction mixture, bringing the final volume to 20 μL. The reverse transcription conditions are: 42 °C for 15 min, followed by 5 s at 85 °C to inactivate the enzyme, then hold at 4 °C. Dilute the resulting cDNA with RNase-free deionised water to a final volume of 100 μL and store at −20 °C for later use.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Specific primers for the target gene and internal control gene were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST. The primers were synthesized by Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and validated by conventional PCR and agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm that the products were single bands with no non-specific amplification.

Prepare a 20 μL qPCR reaction mixture on ice in the dark, comprising: 10 μL 2x SYBR Green qPCR Mix (QP002, Esscience Biotech), 1 μL qPCR forward primer (10 μM), 1 μL qPCR reverse primer (10 μM), and 8 μL cDNA. Place the reaction plate in a real-time quantitative PCR thermal cycler and run the following programme:

Pre-denaturation: 95 °C for 5 min.

PCR reaction (40 cycles): 95 °C for 10 s; 60 °C for 30 s.

Melting curve analysis: 65 °C for 5 s; 95 °C for 30 s.

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad software. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normal distribution and homogeneity of variance among groups were assessed. Results

Preparation and characterization of nanogel

Nanogels were synthesized using two distinct crosslinking strategies based on sequential addition of Staphylococcus epidermidis lysate and crosslinker (Fig. 1a). Initial formulations prepared by adding lysate prior to crosslinker were evaluated at three crosslinker volumes, specifically L5 (5 μL), L10 (10 μL), and L20 (20 μL). Dynamic light scattering revealed substantial size instability in L5, while L10 and L20 exhibited similar hydrodynamic diameters (Fig. 1b).

Comparative formulations prepared by adding crosslinker before lysate (A-series) demonstrated superior performance. A5 (5 μL) exhibited larger particle sizes than lysate-first counterparts, confirming inadequate crosslinking at low volumes. A10 (10 μL) displayed enhanced stability compared to L10/L20, establishing the crosslinker-first approach as optimal. A20 (20 μL) showed the smallest and most consistent particle sizes among all formulations (Fig. 1b).

Further optimization revealed a non-linear relationship between crosslinker volume and particle characteristics. Sizes decreased with increasing crosslinker from 5 to 20 μL (A5-A20) but increased significantly at higher particle size (A30-A50), indicating over-crosslinking effects. Consequently, A20 was selected as the optimized formulation, exhibiting a monodisperse size distribution with a peak at 98.3 ± 30.2 nm (Fig. 1c) and a zeta potential of −14.55 ± 0.89 mV (Fig. 1d). Considering that the particle size was smaller when the crosslinker was added before the lysate, this may be because the amine groups on the proteins undergo specific, directionally chemical binding with these sites, serving as anchoring points, thereby reducing the likelihood of self-aggregation. This binding results in a compact nanoparticle structure with a smaller size. The nanogel was imaged using transmission electron microscopy, revealing spherical particles observable at a 100 nm resolution (Fig. 1e). A comparison was made between the dressing without (Figure s1) and with (Figure s2) the addition of EDC and NHS. Without addition, it remained in a liquid state, whereas after addition, it exhibited a micro-gel state, making it easier to apply onto the skin compared to the pure liquid form.SEM images of the dressing without (Figure s3) and with (Figure s4) the addition of EDC and NHS were captured. Without addition, the lysate appeared spread out flat under the microscope, suggesting a planar structure. In contrast, with addition, it exhibited a more three-dimensional structure with obviously inflated-induced pores. Infrared spectroscopy was performed on the dressing with and without the addition of EDC and NHS, demonstrating that the addition primarily resulted in changes in acyl groups (Figure s5).

The stability of the nanogel was evaluated, showing that the particle size (Fig. 1f), zeta potential (Fig. 1g), and protein concentration (Fig. 1h) remained stable for the first five days. After five days, degradation may have begun, as indicated by significant fluctuations in particle size and zeta potential, along with a slight decrease in protein concentration. Extended monitoring of protein concentration revealed substantial degradation by day 14 and nearly complete degradation by day 20. Nevertheless, its stability remained superior to that of typical proteins, possibly due to the presence of endogenous protease inhibitors in many cells and bacteria, which are released upon lysis.

In vitro experiments, in vivo biodistribution, and anti-inflammatory and antibacterial activities of the dressing

The biosafety profile of nanogels was first evaluated through cytotoxicity assays. H9C2 cardiomyocytes exhibited consistent viability (>95%) across all tested concentrations (Fig. 2a). Similarly, L929 fibroblasts showed minor non-monotonic fluctuations in viability that remained statistically insignificant versus controls, confirming negligible cytotoxicity (Fig. 2b). In contrast, the nanogel exhibited an inhibitory effect at low concentrations, particularly around 0.05 mg/mL, in HaCaT cells, demonstrating its potential for psoriasis treatment. However, when the nanogel was stored for 14 or 20 days before administration, this inhibitory effect was lost, which correlates with our previous observation that the nanogel became unstable after 5 days of storage(Figure s6). Hemocompatibility assessment revealed negligible hemolytic activity comparable to the negative control, significantly lower than the positive control (Fig. 2c and d). Skin irritation tests showed that nanogels with different concentrations caused no adverse effects after 4 days of application (Figure s7). These collective biosafety data supported further therapeutic evaluation.

Skin penetration studies demonstrated time-dependent transport kinetics. At 6 h post-application, fluorescent nanogels localized primarily within the epidermal layer undergoing cellular internalization. By 12 h, significant accumulation occurred in the dermal compartment, with deep hypodermal penetration observed at 24 h (Fig. 2e). To clearly show the penetration profile, we have adjusted the contrast during image processing. Consequently, the increased contrast causes the pink false colour of Cy5-SE to appear whitish.

A comparison of the in vivo biodistribution of the liquid-form lysate and the nanogel in mice showed that the liquid lysate did not readily remain on the skin and tended to spread to the surrounding areas of the mouse skin, with its high liquidity potentially compromising therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, the nanogel could be retained on the skin to a certain extent. Additionally, relevant components were detected in the livers of mice in both groups, but the observed presence was significantly greater in the dressing group than in the pure lysate group (Figure s8).

The uptake of the dressing by macrophages in the skin was verified using immunofluorescence co-localization. Macrophages were labeled with F4/80, and the dressing was stained with Cy5-SE, and colocalization of the two signals could be observed (Figure s9).

Flow cytometry analysis and confocal microscopy imaging of macrophage uptake revealed that the uptake gradually increased from the beginning up to 10 h, peaked at 10 h, showed a significant downward trend between 10 and 16 h, and was barely observable by 24 h (Fig. 2f and g).

The expression of inflammatory cytokines in macrophages from different groups was assessed by ELISA. TNF-α and IL-6, classic indicators in inflammatory cytokine detection, serve as representative reference factors and are commonly used standards in psoriasis research [[15], [16], [17]]. The results showed that as the concentration of the dressing increased, the inflammatory status of the cells further improved (Fig. 2h and i).

In vitro anti-Staphylococcus aureus results indicated that the dressing exhibited minimal antibacterial effects at low and medium concentrations, but showed significant antibacterial activity at high concentrations (Fig. 2j and k).

The nanogels alleviated psoriasis-like symptom in the IMQ-induced murine model

Therapeutic intervention commenced on Day 7 post-IMQ induction and concluded on Day 10 (Fig. 3a). Macroscopic evaluation revealed severe psoriasiform pathology in the PBS group, exhibiting extensive erythematous plaques with hyperkeratosis covering >90% dorsal surface area, consistent with chronic inflammation. Dermovate® (clobetasol propionate) showed partial efficacy with residual microabscesses. Nanogel treatment demonstrated dose-dependent improvement—low-dose reduced plaque coverage by approximately 40% but retained focal erythema; mid-dose promoted significant desquamation with minimal hyperkeratosis; high-dose achieved near-complete resolution showing >80% re-epithelialization (Fig. 3b).

Clinical assessment also confirmed these observations. Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) [18] scores decreased progressively with nanogel concentration—high-dose group showed 9.33 versus 3.33 in PBS (P < 0.00001) (Fig. 3c). Body weight analysis indicated IMQ-induced bodyweight loss was significantly mitigated by high-dose nanogels compared with Dermovate-treated group (Fig. 3d).

Histopathological evaluation of specimens collected on day 10 revealed that the PBS group exhibited pronounced epidermal hyperplasia accompanied by dense lymphocytic infiltration. In contrast, high-dose nanogels significantly reduced epidermal thickness, demonstrating superior efficacy compared to Dermovate (Fig. 3e). Immunohistochemical analysis indicated a dose-dependent reduction in proliferation markers [19]. Specifically, the high-dose nanogel group showed a marked reduction in macrophage infiltration (CD68+ cells) (Fig. 3f). Cytokine quantification demonstrated significant concentration-dependent reductions in pro-inflammatory mediators—TNF-α decreased by 73.53% in high-dose group (0.93 pg/mL vs PBS 3.52 pg/mL; P < 0.00001) while IL-6 decreased by 63.59% (2.38 pg/mg vs 6.52 pg/mg; P < 0.00001) (Fig. 3g and h).

Regulation of host immunity and biosafety

Systemic immunomodulatory analysis revealed significant effects. Flow cytometry demonstrated that high-dose nanogels markedly reduced the frequency of activated dendritic cells in skin homogenates, decreasing CD11c + CD80+ cells by 34.11% compared to PBS controls (P < 0.00001) (Fig. 4a and b). The status of macrophages in each group was evaluated, and similar to dendritic cells, they exhibited the same trend as antigen-presenting cells (Figures s10 and 11). Splenic T-cell profiling indicated normalization of CD4+/CD8+ ratios from 49.60% in disease controls to 37.06% in high-dose groups (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4c and d). IgE quantification in spleen homogenates showed a 62% reduction with high-dose treatment (52.37 versus 46.13 ng/mL, P < 0.00001), suggesting attenuated hypersensitivity (Fig. 4e).

Histopathological examination of spleen sections following H&E staining revealed varying degrees of lymphocytosis, macrophage phagocytosis, and collagen fiber proliferation across all groups except the normal group. The PBS group exhibited the most severe changes, while the high-dose nanogel group demonstrated the most effective therapeutic response (Fig. 4f). CD11c, CD80, and CD86, key cell surface molecules involved in immune cell activation and antigen presentation, were highly expressed on activated DCs [20]. The IMQ group showed elevated levels of CD11c and CD80 compared to other groups, while CD86 expression, though high, was lower than in the low-dose nanogel group (Fig. 4g–i). This suggests that low-dose nanogels may initially activate CD86-related immune cells in the spleen, but as the nanogel dose increases, the immune response is effectively downregulated. Consequently, immunofluorescence was employed to further assess the distribution of CD11c + CD80+ and CD11c + CD86+ cells in the skin, and the results were consistent with those obtained from flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry (Fig. 5a–d). IL-17 and IL-23, the two most critical factors in psoriasis, are involved in multiple processes from disease onset to maintenance. Although the overall expression level of IL-17 was relatively low, a trend was still observable; in contrast, IL-23 exhibited a more pronounced effect (Fig. 5e and f). TLRs are a class of pattern recognition receptors primarily expressed on the surface or endosomal membranes of immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells. They recognize conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns shared among microorganisms. Within the pathway, activated NF-κB induces the transcription of pro-IL-1β and iNOS, leading to the production of cytotoxic molecules, making these indicators useful for assessing the intensity of the inflammatory response. Overall, the low- and medium-concentration groups showed no significant differences compared to the control group, whereas the high-concentration group demonstrated therapeutic advantages (Fig. 5g and h).

HE staining of organ sections from nanogel-treated mice was utilized to assess the safety profile of the nanogels (Fig. 5i). Cardiac tissue sections exhibited normal myocardial cell arrangement without evidence of hypertrophy or nuclear abnormalities across all three nanogel groups, indicating an absence of cardiac toxicity. Hepatic sections revealed mild hepatocyte enlargement in the low-dose group; this morphological change may result from a physiological adaptive response. In the other groups, hepatocytes were arranged in an orderly manner, with no signs of proliferation or inflammatory cell infiltration, suggesting the absence of hepatotoxicity. Pulmonary sections revealed varying degrees of alveolar wall thickening in the PBS, Dermovate, and low-dose nanogel groups, with the most extensive distribution in the PBS group. While Dermovate treatment reduced the distribution of alveolar wall thickening, it resulted in more severe individual cases, highlighting its limited efficacy against psoriasis-related complications and potential long-term side effects associated with its glucocorticoid component [21]. Conversely, the low-dose nanogel group showed significant improvement, with even better alleviation observed in the medium and high-dose groups, indicating the nanogels' effectiveness in mitigating psoriasis-related complications. Renal sections displayed normal glomerular size and shape, intact basement membranes, and no crescent formation, confirming no renal toxicity. Blood biochemical tests are also an important criterion for assessing safety. ALT is the most sensitive and commonly used indicator of hepatocyte injury; AST is highly abundant in the myocardium, liver, and skeletal muscle, reflecting overall tissue damage; and CR is a marker for evaluating renal function, as impaired kidneys cannot effectively clear it. The results showed no significant toxic or adverse effects in the three formulation groups (Fig. 5j–l). Hydrogel biosafety is essential for biocompatibility and minimizing negative effects within the body, which helps ensure successful use in clinical settings [22,23].

Modulation of microbial diversity

We profiled the skin microbiota on the backs of all mice using 16S rRNA sequencing. First, we measured α-diversity with the Chao1, ACE and Shannon indices (Fig. 6a–c). Compared with PBS-treatd group, the high-dose nanogel did not alter these indices, indicating that overall bacterial richness and evenness remained unchanged.

To test whether the treatment shifted community composition, we calculated β-diversity with ANOSIM. The high-dose group diverged markedly from the PBS group (P = 0.01), confirming that the nanogel reshaped the microbial landscape (Fig. 6d).

At the genus level, 56 taxa differed significantly between the two groups (Fig. 6e). Linear discriminant analysis (LEfSe) corroborated these findings, revealing that the high-dose nanogel simultaneously reduced potential pathogens and promoted a healthier microbial profile (Fig. 6f). Collectively, the data demonstrate that our nanogel effectively remodels the skin microbiota in a manner that should benefit management. The most pronounced changes involved Staphylococcus and Corynebacterium, both of which were sharply reduced after treatment (Fig. 6g and h). Previous studies have linked high Staphylococcus abundance—especially Staphylococcus aureus—to psoriasis severity, and elevated Corynebacterium has also been associated with worse disease [24]. Consequently, lowering the abundance of these genera is expected to lessen disease risk.The representative bacterial species most characteristic of each group are illustrated (Fig. 6i). In the PBS and Dermovate groups, the predominant species were primarily opportunistic pathogenic bacteria, whereas the microbial composition in the high-concentration group was more normative and exhibited certain anti-inflammatory potential, indicating that the dressing indeed exerted a macro-regulatory effect. The heatmap reflects a comparison of SE and SA abundance among the three groups, revealing an opposing trend that serves as strong evidence for this conclusion (Fig. 6j).

Discussion

In this study, we designed skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels cross - linked by EDC and NHS from Staphylococcus epidermidis lysate. The high - concentration skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels effectively inhibited the activation of dendritic cells (DCs) in the skin and the immune regulatory mechanisms of DCs in the spleen to T cells, thereby reducing the expression of various inflammatory factors such as TNF - α and IL - 6. This treatment also promoted the restoration of the skin barrier and microbiota, further improving psoriasis. Our findings reveal that Staphylococcus epidermidis, a skin commensal bacterium that can alleviate inflammatory skin diseases like psoriasis, has significant immune - regulatory effects. As an inactivated bacterium, it can avoid unnecessary immune responses and provides a promising therapeutic strategy for psoriasis treatment.

Recently, the use of some probiotics has been gaining traction in the management and prevention of inflammatory skin diseases. Notably, Liu et al. encapsulated live R. mucosa directly into a hydrogel and applied it to the skin of mice with dermatitis [25]. This hydrogel supported the growth of R. mucosa on the skin and combated Staphylococcus aureus in the skin. It also accelerated wound healing and restored skin barrier function, alleviating dermatitis - related immune responses and providing combination therapeutic effects. Xu et al. prepared hydrogel microspheres using the outer vesicles of Acne - Propionibacterium and used them to treat mouse psoriasis [26]. These microspheres effectively inhibited the activity of Staphylococcus aureus and promoted the proliferation and migration of HaCat cells exposed to Staphylococcus aureus. They also significantly reduced the expression of various inflammatory factors and restored skin barrier function, promoting skin microbiota balance, reducing Staphylococcus colonization, and enhancing lipid metabolism to improve therapeutic effects.

Current research mainly emphasizes the therapeutic potential of probiotics and bacterial products [27], but the feasibility of bacterial therapy itself has been overlooked. Lyzed inactivated bacteria also have therapeutic effects. Existing studies have proven that psoriasis is accompanied by a decrease in the abundance of S.epidermidis and an increase in the abundance of Staphylococcus aureus [10]. This study mainly explores whether external supplementation of lyzed inactivated S.epidermidis can alleviate psoriasis symptoms, suggesting that certain bacterial structures of S.epidermidis may play a key role.

DCs are key antigen - presenting cells that play a central role in initiating and regulating immune responses. Dendritic cells are identified by specific surface markers, and CD11c, as an important surface marker, facilitates interactions between DCs and other immune cells [28]. After activation, DCs up - regulate co - stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86, which are crucial for T cell activation. The balance of CD80 and CD86 expression affects the nature of immune responses. CD86 is usually expressed earlier, while CD80 plays a more important role in maintaining T cell activation [29]. CD4 T cells, also known as helper T cells, activate other immune cells through cytokine secretion to coordinate immune responses. CD8 T cells, or cytotoxic T cells, are responsible for directly killing infected or malignant cells. The interaction between DCs and T cells is the basis of adaptive immunity [30]. According to our study's data, IMQ - induced psoriasis mice showed high activation of DCs and T cells, consistent with prior studies [31]. Further observation revealed that high - concentration skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels reduced the activation of these cells, bringing them closer to control group levels.

Compared with high-concentration skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels, Dermovate also showed good therapeutic effects but was slightly inferior to high - concentration skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels. 16S rRNA sequencing revealed its disadvantage: Dermovate did not increase the abundance of skin microbiota but showed a downward trend, and the abundance of Staphylococcus remained predominant. This indicates that it does not improve the skin microbiota but relieves psoriasis through the mechanism of glucocorticoids. Thus, high - concentration skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels demonstrated better and more comprehensive therapeutic effects than Dermovate. This also emphasizes the stability of high - concentration skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels, making long - term efficacy possible.

However, long-term intervention in the microecology may carry risks. A healthy and balanced microecosystem harbors a rich diversity of beneficial bacteria, which coexist without any single species occupying a dominant ecological niche. Excessive intervention using lysates—particularly through the introduction of specific nutrients—could inadvertently promote the overgrowth of certain bacterial populations. Even if the proliferating species are considered beneficial, such an imbalance may ultimately lead to adverse outcomes, an area that warrants further investigation.

Drug resistance is a critical consideration in the development of any therapeutic agent, and minimizing its emergence is a key objective for researchers. In the case of lysate-based therapies, which operate through a dual regulatory mechanism involving both cutaneous microbiota modulation and immune regulation, the development of resistance may be relatively difficult. This is because it would require the simultaneous acquisition of resistance by the skin microbiota and the development of immune tolerance to the therapy—a relatively stringent condition. As such, lysate-based strategies hold promising long-term research potential.

To proactively manage and mitigate potential risks, robust monitoring strategies should be established. In long-term animal studies, periodic collection of skin and fecal samples for 16S rRNA metagenomic sequencing is recommended to dynamically track changes in microbial diversity. Clear therapeutic endpoints should be defined, and intervention should cease once treatment goals are achieved to avoid overtreatment. In clinical trials, exploratory nodes should be incorporated at the pre-treatment, mid-treatment, and post-treatment stages to identify biomarkers associated with therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study emphasizes the therapeutic potential of skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels cross - linked by EDC and NHS from S.epidermidis lysate for psoriasis. In IMQ - induced psoriasis mice, high - concentration skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels effectively reduced the expression of inflammatory genes and restored skin barrier function. Moreover, this study revealed the intrinsic resistance of S.epidermidis lysate to Staphylococcus aureus. In vivo experiments demonstrated that high - concentration skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels significantly reduced the activation of DCs and T cells. Overall, this study indicates that skin commensal bacteria-derived nanogels may achieve ideal psoriasis treatment effects by modulating the immune mechanisms of DCs and T cells and restoring skin microbiota.
