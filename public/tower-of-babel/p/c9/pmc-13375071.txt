Glofitamab in Heavily Pretreated Mantle Cell Lymphoma Patients: A Multicenter Real‐World Case Series
Authors: Natalia Rotter, Markus Raderer, Agnes List, Ferdinand Haslbauer, Olga Saini, Alexander Egle, Teresa Magnes, Michael Leisch, Emine Kaynak, Veronika Buxhofer‐Ausch, Beatrice Casadei, Felix Keil, Holger Rumpold, Georg Hess, Richard Greil, Pier Luigi Zinzani, Thomas Melchardt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRelapsed or refractory mantle cell lymphoma (r/r MCL) remains difficult to treat despite therapeutic advances.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included 10 patients with r/r MCL treated with glofitamab across five sites in Austria and Italy before European approval (January 2023–May 2024).ResultsPatients were heavily pretreated (median 4.5 prior lines; 5/10 post‐CAR‐T). After a median of five cycles, responses were observed in 7/9 evaluable patients, including complete response in 4/9. Cytokine‐release syndrome occurred in 7/10 (mostly Grade 1–2), including grade 3 events in 2/10, while ICANS occurred in 2/10 patients, including one Grade 4 event. Three patients received glofitamab as bridging therapy and all proceeded to CAR‐T infusion.ConclusionGlofitamab showed encouraging activity and manageable toxicity in heavily pretreated MCL, including as bridging therapy to CAR‐T‐cell treatment.Trial RegistrationThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.

Relapsed or refractory (r/r) mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a therapeutic challenge in clinical practice despite new therapeutic options such as CD19‐directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy or new Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKi) [1, 2]. Similar to other B‐cell lymphomas, the bispecific CD20/CD3 antibody glofitamab has shown promising results in Phase II trials in heavily pretreated MCL patients [3, 4]. Due to pending approval, very limited real‐world data have been published so far. This study aims to provide additional insight into glofitamab's safety and efficacy profile in a real‐world, heavily pretreated patient population.

This investigator‐initiated retrospective data collection analyzed 10 patients with r/r MCL from 5 sites in Austria and Italy who were treated with glofitamab between January 2023 and May 2024 before European Medicines Agency approval for this indication. Before approval for diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) in July 2023, glofitamab was administered through a compassionate use program; thereafter, patients received glofitamab as off‐label therapy after providing informed consent according to institutional standards. Consent for treatment and for inclusion of anonymized clinical data in institutional or registry‐based datasets was obtained from all patients. All Austrian patients were included in the European Mantle Cell Lymphoma (EMCL) registry after informed consent in a cooperative project of the Austrian Group for Medical Tumor Therapy (AGMT) and the EMCL registry. Response was assessed in all patients who received at least one cycle of glofitamab and had at least one post‐baseline disease assessment using the Lugano criteria [5]. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune‐effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were assessed using the consensus guidelines from American Society of Transplantation and Cellular Therapy [6]. Other toxicities were graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0.

Ten patients, all male (10/10), were included with a median age of 65 years (range, 45–77) at first glofitamab infusion. Patient demographics are depicted in Table 1. Seven (7/10) patients had a high‐risk secondary Mantle Cell International Prognostic Index (MIPI), with a median ECOG Performance Status of 1 (range, 0–2) at first glofitamab infusion. Median number of prior therapies was 4.5 (range, 2–6) and five patients (5/10) were refractory to their last line of treatment. All patients had already received the BTKi ibrutinib, while five patients (5/10) had prior autologous stem cell transplantation (SCT) and prior anti‐CD19 CAR T‐cell therapy, respectively. Three patients had received bendamustine as a prior therapy.

The dosage of obinutuzumab administered before glofitamab was 2000 mg in six patients, and 1000 mg in the remaining four patients. All patients were hospitalized during glofitamab ramp‐up. CRS was observed in 7/10 patients during Cycles 1–2 and was mostly Grade 1 or 2 with the use of tocilizumab in five patients (5/10). Then, 2/10 patients developed Grade 3 CRS with short admission to the intermediate care with cardiovascular monitoring. ICANS occurred in 2/10 patients (Grade 1 and 4). The patient with grade 4 ICANS had known secondary CNS involvement and achieved partial remission (PR) with clearance of malignant cells in cerebrospinal fluid and radiologic response on MRI. ICANS occurred after Cycle 9, leading to discontinuation of glofitamab. Neurologic symptoms resolved completely following treatment with anakinra, without evidence of disease progression. Seven infections occurred in 4 patients during glofitamab treatment, mainly of Grade 2 (fungal pneumonia, CTCAE Grade 3; COVID pneumonia, CTCAE Grade 3; Parainfluenza virus infection, CTCAE Grade 2; upper respiratory infection [Rhinovirus], CTCAE grade 2; 2 CMV reactivations, CTCAE Grade 2). No infection led to permanent treatment discontinuation.

Median follow‐up was 8.6 months (range, 1 day–16.6 months) with eight patients alive at data cut‐off. One patient with multiple comorbidities died suddenly in the hospital setting under clinical monitoring the day after the first dose of glofitamab without observed CRS symptoms; a causal relationship to glofitamab could not be established. The other patient, refractory to glofitamab, died due to a disease progression (PD). Glofitamab therapy was stopped after nine cycles in one heavily pretreated patient with CNS involvement achieving a PR in MRI and clearance of MCL in cerebrospinal fluid due to ICANS Grade 4 and a newly diagnosed therapy‐related myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) not related to glofitamab.

After a median of 5 cycles (range, 1–12), best response to glofitamab was complete remission (CR) in 4/9 and PR in 3/9 evaluable patients, whereas one patient had a PD and another had a mixed response (Figure 1). Progression free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) at 6 months were both 78.8% (95% CI 56.4%–100%), with a median PFS and OS not reached at a median follow‐up of 8.6 months. Given the small sample size and short follow‐up, these survival estimates should be interpreted with caution. At data cut‐off, glofitamab treatment was ongoing in three patients.

Three patients received glofitamab as “bridging” therapy (BT) before CAR T‐cell treatment with brexucabtagene autoleucel (brexu‐cel). To mitigate the risk of T‐cell dysfunction potentially induced by prior T‐cell‐redirecting therapy, leukapheresis for CAR‐T cell manufacturing was performed before glofitamab administration. After a median of 2 cycles (range 2–6) of glofitamab, all three patients successfully proceeded to brexu‐cel infusion with a CR, a PR and a mixed response prior to CAR‐T infusion. All three patients (3/3) had a CR as best response 2 months after CAR‐T therapy and remained in CR after a median follow‐up of 12.2 months. Five patients received glofitamab after CAR T‐cell failure (4 brexucabtagene autoleucel, 1 tisagenlecleucel), with 2 patients achieving a CR (2/5) and 2 a PR (2/5).

Our retrospective real‐world analysis provides additional evidence supporting the clinical activity of glofitamab in patients with r/r MCL. In a published Phase I/II study including 60 patients treated after a median of 2 prior lines, the overall and complete response rates were 85.0% and 78.3%, respectively [4]. In contrast, patients in our cohort were more heavily pretreated (median of 4.5 prior lines), and the majority had high‐risk features, which likely explains the lower CR rate observed.

Three patients had prior exposure to bendamustine; however, no apparent differences in response or toxicity were observed, although conclusions are limited by the small sample size. Similarly, no clear association between obinutuzumab pre‐treatment dose and CRS incidence or efficacy was observed.

Despite these differences, we observed encouraging responses in 7/9 evaluable patients, supporting the clinical activity of glofitamab in a real‐world setting. Notably, three patients (3/9) received glofitamab as bridging therapy and were able to proceed to CAR T‐cell infusion, suggesting a potential role in this setting. However, due to the limited duration of treatment, long‐term outcomes cannot be reliably assessed in this cohort.

The safety profile was consistent with previous studies, with predominantly low‐grade CRS. Grade 3 CRS was observed in two patients with complete recovery. While no new safety signals were identified, rare events such as late‐onset ICANS and sudden in‐hospital death without preceding CRS were observed, although a causal relationship remains uncertain [4].

CAR T‐cell therapy with brexucabtagene autoleucel achieved high CR rates with a median PFS of 25.8 months in the pivotal ZUMA‐2 trial [7]. Real‐world evidence shows that progressive disease is the most common reason for failure to reach cell harvest or infusion in r/r MCL [8, 9, 10]. In our analysis, three heavily pretreated patients were able to reach CAR T‐cell infusion after a short BT with glofitamab with all of them achieving a CR post‐CAR T‐cell therapy, eventually. Clinical and preclinical evidence is increasing that prior bispecific antibody treatment does not impair CAR‐T efficacy and may even enhance disease control [11, 12]. These data support the feasibility of sequential immunotherapy strategies in r/r MCL, although larger studies are needed.

In conclusion, these retrospective real‐world results suggest encouraging clinical activity of glofitamab in heavily pretreated patients with r/r MCL with a manageable safety profile and predominantly low‐grade CRS. Responses were also observed in patients treated after CAR T‐cell therapy failure and in patients receiving glofitamab as bridging therapy to CAR T‐cell therapy. However, given the retrospective nature, small sample size and short follow‐up, these data should be interpreted with caution. Larger prospective trials are needed to further define the role of glofitamab monotherapy in r/r MCL.

Ethics Statement

This retrospective study analyzed anonymized data during routine clinical care and was conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical standards. Glofitamab treatment in r/r MCL was performed either within a compassionate use program before approval for diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) in July 2023 or subsequently as off‐label therapy based on individual treatment decision and written informed consent. All patients provided informed consent for treatment with glofitamab and for inclusion of their anonymized clinical data in institutional or registry‐based datasets, including the EMCL registry in Austria. Formal ethics committee approval was not required for retrospective anonymized data analyses according to local regulations.

Consent

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before initiation of treatment and for inclusion of their anonymized clinical data in this study. No identifiable personal data are included in the manuscript.
