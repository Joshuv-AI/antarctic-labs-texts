Ambient Air Pollution and Glaucoma: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies
Authors: Divya Jain, Bitesh Kumar, Anjan K Dhua, Sharanyaa Krishnamurthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, and environmental risk factors beyond intraocular pressure (IOP) are increasingly being investigated. Ambient air pollution, particularly fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅), may contribute to glaucomatous optic neuropathy through oxidative stress and vascular dysregulation; however, existing evidence remains fragmented.ObjectiveTo systematically review and quantitatively synthesize the association between ambient air pollution exposure and glaucoma risk, IOP, and structural markers of glaucomatous optic neuropathy.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was registered with PROSPERO. PubMed and Embase were searched from inception to 30th December 2025, for observational studies evaluating ambient air pollution exposure and glaucoma-related outcomes. Random-effects meta-analysis using generic inverse-variance methods was conducted for studies reporting associations between long-term PM₂.₅ exposure and glaucoma. Other pollutants were synthesized narratively.ResultsEighteen observational studies from diverse geographic regions were included, of which eight contributed to the PM₂.₅ meta-analysis. Higher long-term PM₂.₅ exposure was associated with an increased risk of glaucoma (pooled RR = 1.12; 95% CI, 1.06–1.18), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 78.7%). Associations for NO₂, NOx, PM₁₀, and CO were directionally positive but heterogeneous. Structural changes, including RNFL and GCIPL thinning, and modest IOP elevations were also reported.ConclusionLong-term PM₂.₅ exposure is associated with increased glaucoma risk, suggesting a potential environmental contribution; however, causality cannot be established from the available observational evidence. These findings highlight the need to integrate ocular outcomes into environmental health policy and to prioritize prospective studies capable of addressing confounding and temporality.Keywords: Air pollution, Environmental exposure, Glaucoma, Meta-analysis, Optic neuropathy, PM₂₅

Introduction

Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting >76 million individuals globally, with projections exceeding 110 million by 2040.1 The disease is characterized by progressive retinal ganglion cell loss and optic nerve head damage, often—but not exclusively—associated with elevated intraocular pressure (IOP). Although IOP remains the principal modifiable risk factor, a substantial proportion of patients develop glaucomatous damage despite normal IOP levels, indicating that additional systemic and environmental factors may contribute to disease pathogenesis.2

Ambient air pollution represents a major global public health challenge and is currently ranked among the leading contributors to morbidity and premature mortality worldwide.3,4 Chronic exposure to pollutants such as fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), ozone (O₃), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), and carbon monoxide (CO) has been robustly linked to cardiovascular, respiratory, and neurodegenerative diseases through mechanisms involving oxidative stress, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular dysregulation.5 Reflecting the growing recognition of these health risks, the World Health Organization (WHO) substantially lowered the recommended annual PM₂.₅ exposure threshold from 10 µg/m³ in 2005 to 5 µg/m³ in its 2021 air quality guidelines.

Emerging evidence suggests that the deleterious effects of air pollution may also extend to ocular health. Prior studies have demonstrated associations between air pollution exposure and a range of anterior and posterior segment ocular conditions, including conjunctivitis, dry eye disease, cataract formation, and retinal structural changes.6–8 Pollutants may affect the eye through direct contact with the ocular surface or indirectly via systemic circulation, inducing oxidative stress, microvascular compromise, and inflammatory responses within ocular tissues.9 Notably, these mechanisms overlap substantially with pathways implicated in glaucomatous optic neuropathy.

Despite increasing interest in this area, the evidence linking ambient air pollution to glaucoma remains fragmented, with studies varying in design, exposure assessment, outcome definitions, and analytical approaches. Moreover, no comprehensive synthesis has quantitatively evaluated the association between air pollution, particularly PM₂.₅, and glaucoma risk. Given the global burden of both air pollution exposure and glaucoma-related vision loss, clarifying this relationship has important clinical and public health implications. We therefore conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesize the existing evidence on ambient air pollution exposure and glaucoma risk, including related structural and functional ocular outcomes.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42025629917) and conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive search of PubMed and Embase was performed from database inception to 30th December 2025. Search terms combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH and Emtree) with free-text keywords related to air pollution and glaucoma. The core search string was:

(“air pollution” OR “particulate matter” OR “PM₂.₅” OR “PM₁₀” OR “nitrogen dioxide” OR “ozone”) AND (“glaucoma” OR “intraocular pressure” OR “optic nerve” OR “visual field”)

The individual search strategies are as follows:

EMBASE—('air pollution'/exp OR 'particulate matter'/exp OR 'nitrogen dioxide'/exp OR 'ozone'/exp OR 'environmental exposure'/exp OR 'airborne pollutants') AND ('glaucoma' OR 'open-angle glaucoma' OR 'angle-closure glaucoma' OR 'intraocular pressure' OR 'IOP' OR 'optic nerve damage' OR 'POAG' OR 'PACG') AND ('risk factor'/exp OR 'epidemiology'/exp OR 'exposure'/exp)

PUBMED—(“air pollution”[MeSH Terms] OR “air pollutants”[MeSH Terms] OR “particulate matter”[MeSH Terms] OR “PM₂.₅” OR “PM₁₀” OR “nitrogen dioxide” OR “NO₂” OR “ozone” OR “carbon monoxide” OR “sulfur dioxide” OR “SO₂” OR “traffic pollution” OR “environmental pollution”) AND (“glaucoma”[MeSH Terms] OR “open-angle glaucoma” OR “angle-closure glaucoma” OR “intraocular pressure” OR “IOP” OR “optic nerve damage”) AND (“risk factors” OR “prevalence” OR “incidence” OR “progression” OR “association” OR “exposure” OR “longitudinal study” OR “case–control study” OR “cohort study”)

Data Extraction

Two reviewers (DJ and AD) independently extracted data using a standardized template. Extracted variables included study characteristics, population demographics, study design, exposure assessment methods, pollutants evaluated, exposure duration, glaucoma outcomes, effect estimates with confidence intervals, covariate adjustments, and study limitations. Where necessary, exposure metrics were harmonized to allow comparison across studies. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus and, when necessary, adjudicated by a third reviewer (VV).

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was assessed for all included studies using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for cohort and case–control studies and a modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for cross-sectional studies.

Statistical Analysis

Random-effects meta-analysis using a generic inverse-variance approach was performed for studies reporting associations between long-term PM₂.₅ exposure and glaucoma. Effect estimates were harmonized and pooled as risk ratios. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. Acute glaucoma studies were analyzed as a prespecified subgroup. All analyses were conducted using standard meta-analytic methods.

Results

Study Characteristics

A total of 185 records were identified through searches of two electronic databases. After the removal of duplicates and screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 18 studies were included in the final analysis. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of study selection process

Study Selection and Study Characteristics

The included studies comprised population-based cohort studies (n = 11), cross-sectional studies (n = 4), and time-series analyses evaluating acute glaucoma events (n = 3).

Geographically, the studies were conducted across Asia (n = 12), Europe (n = 3), and North America (n = 3). Asian studies were predominantly from China (n = 6) and Taiwan (n = 4), with additional studies from South Korea (n = 2). European cohorts included studies from the United Kingdom (n = 2) and France (n = 1), while North American studies originated from Canada (n = 2) and the United States (n = 1).

Large population-based cohorts included the UK Biobank (n = 3 analyses), the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS; n = 2), the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (n = 1), and the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD; n = 2).

Pollutants Assessed

Across the included studies, the most frequently evaluated pollutant was fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅; n = 14 studies). Other pollutants assessed included nitrogen dioxide (NO₂; n = 7), PM₁₀ (n = 4), sulfur dioxide (SO₂; n = 4), ozone (O₃; n = 4), and carbon monoxide (CO; n = 3).

In addition, PM₂.₅ chemical constituents, including black carbon, ammonium, sulfate, nitrate, and organic matter, were specifically examined in two studies, both conducted within large Chinese population-based cohorts.

Exposure assessment methods varied and included land-use regression models (n = 4), satellite-based or machine-learning spatiotemporal models (n = 5), and government air-quality monitoring networks (n = 9). Exposure windows ranged from short-term daily or weekly averages (n = 3 studies) to long-term annual or multi-year averages (n = 15 studies) (Table 1).10,27

Glaucoma Outcomes and Related Measures

Clinical glaucoma outcomes were heterogeneous across studies. Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) was evaluated in 10 studies, while primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) was specifically assessed in four studies. Acute angle-closure glaucoma (AACG) outcomes, typically captured through emergency visits or hospital admissions, were examined in three time-series studies. Normal-tension glaucoma (NTG) was explicitly evaluated in two studies, and childhood glaucoma was assessed in one study.

In addition to diagnosed glaucoma, IOP was analyzed as a primary or secondary outcome in five studies. Structural imaging outcomes were reported in five studies, including retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness (n = 3) and macular ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) thickness (n = 2). Measures of ocular or peripapillary vascular perfusion were reported in one study.

Association between PM₂.₅ Exposure and Glaucoma

Eight observational studies were included in the quantitative synthesis assessing the association between ambient PM₂.₅ exposure and glaucoma. Using a random-effects model with inverse-variance weighting, higher PM₂.₅ exposure was associated with a significantly increased risk of glaucoma (pooled RR = 1.12, 95% CI, 1.06–1.18; p < 0.0001). Individual study effect estimates were consistently above unity, with the strongest association observed in the CHARLS-based cohort study, which reported a 66% higher risk of glaucoma per interquartile range increase in PM₂.₅ exposure. Moderate between-study heterogeneity was observed (χ² = 37.93, df = 7, p < 0.00001), reflecting variability in exposure assessment methods, population characteristics, and outcome definitions across studies. The pooled estimate remained robust across contributing studies, supporting a positive association between long-term PM₂.₅ exposure and glaucoma risk (Fig. 2).

Heterogeneity and Sensitivity Analyses

Statistical heterogeneity among the studies included in the PM₂.₅ meta-analysis was assessed using the Cochran Q test and quantified with the I² statistic. Moderate heterogeneity was observed across studies (τ² = 0.00; χ² = 37.93, df = 7; p < 0.00001), with an I² value indicating that a substantial proportion of the variability in effect estimates was attributable to between-study differences rather than chance. Given the anticipated methodological and population-level heterogeneity across observational studies, a random-effects model was considered appropriate and was therefore applied for the primary analysis.

To examine the robustness of the pooled estimate, sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach, whereby each study was sequentially excluded from the meta-analysis. The direction and magnitude of the pooled risk ratio remained largely unchanged following the exclusion of individual studies, with pooled estimates consistently indicating an increased risk of glaucoma associated with higher PM₂.₅ exposure. No single study disproportionately influenced the overall effect size or statistical significance, supporting the stability of the findings.

Other Pollutants: PM₁₀, NO₂, NOx, and CO

For PM₁₀, two studies, one evaluating childhood glaucoma and one assessing acute glaucoma events, reported positive associations, yielding a pooled relative risk of 1.12 (95% CI, 0.95–1.32; I² = 97%; n = 2 studies).

Associations between NO₂ and acute glaucoma were examined in two time-series studies, both demonstrating statistically significant increases in AACG-related hospital visits, with a pooled relative risk of 1.12 (95% CI, 1.09–1.15). Similarly, CO exposure was associated with increased acute glaucoma risk in two studies, yielding a pooled relative risk of 1.06 (95% CI, 1.01–1.12).

For NOx, SO₂, and O₃, effect estimates were directionally positive in most studies (n = 4–5 studies combined), particularly in high-pollution Asian settings such as Taiwan, Hefei, and Guangzhou. However, heterogeneity in exposure metrics and outcome definitions precluded formal quantitative pooling for these pollutants.

Structural and Functional Ocular Changes

Structural and functional ocular outcomes provided additional evidence supporting the pollution–glaucoma association. RNFL thinning in relation to long-term PM₂.₅ and black carbon exposure was reported in three imaging-based studies, including the Alienor cohort from France. GCIPL thinning associated with higher PM₂.₅ exposure was demonstrated in two studies, both derived from the UK Biobank.

Short-term PM₂.₅ exposure was associated with modest but statistically significant increases in IOP in three studies, with exposure windows ranging from one to three months. Reduced peripapillary vascular density associated with traffic-related air pollution was reported in one study involving African American participants.

High-Risk and Susceptible Populations

Evidence of differential susceptibility was reported in six studies. Increased pollution-related glaucoma risk was observed among older adults (n = 4 studies), individuals with oxidative stress–related genetic variants (n = 1 study), and residents of chronically high-pollution regions (n = 5 studies). Populations residing in rural–urban transitional zones were identified as particularly vulnerable in two studies, suggesting that both biological susceptibility and environmental context may modify the impact of air pollution on glaucoma risk.

Risk of Bias

Risk of bias was assessed for all included studies using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort and case–control studies and a modified NOS for cross-sectional studies. Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was moderate to high. Risk-of-bias assessment was performed for all 18 included studies. Overall, the methodological quality was moderate to high, with the majority of studies judged to be at low risk of bias. Among the eight studies contributing to the PM₂.₅ meta-analysis, six studies (n = 6) were judged to be at low risk of bias, while two studies (n = 2) were considered to have a moderate risk of bias. No study was classified as having a high risk of bias.

Most studies (n = 6) scored highly in the selection domain, reflecting the use of large, population-based cohorts and clearly defined study populations. The comparability domain was adequately addressed in most studies (n = 6), with adjustment for key confounders, including age, sex, socioeconomic status, smoking, and relevant comorbidities. The principal sources of potential bias were related to exposure assessment, where reliance on area-level or model-based estimates of PM₂.₅ may have introduced exposure misclassification, and outcome ascertainment, particularly in some studies (n = 3) using administrative databases or self-reported glaucoma diagnoses. Despite these limitations, outcome assessment was generally consistent within studies, and follow-up duration was adequate in all cohort studies (n = 7).

Importantly, no study was excluded from quantitative synthesis on the basis of risk of bias, and the overall direction and magnitude of effect estimates were consistent across studies with varying risk profiles, supporting the robustness of the pooled findings. In addition, sensitivity analyses restricted to studies at low overall risk of bias yielded effect estimates comparable to those of the primary analysis, further reinforcing the robustness of the observed association between ambient PM₂.₅ exposure and glaucoma risk. Publication Bias

Publication bias was assessed for the primary PM₂.₅–glaucoma association using visual inspection of a funnel plot (Fig. 3). The funnel plot demonstrated approximate symmetry, with studies distributed on both sides of the pooled effect estimate and no clear evidence of small-study effects or asymmetry. Larger studies were located near the center of the plot, while smaller studies showed the expected wider dispersion.

Funnel plot assessing publication bias for the association between PM₂.₅ exposure and glaucoma risk

Given the limited number of studies included in the quantitative synthesis (n = 8), formal statistical tests for funnel plot asymmetry were not emphasized, and the assessment was interpreted cautiously. Overall, there was no strong visual evidence of publication bias influencing the pooled estimate; however, the possibility of unpublished null studies cannot be completely excluded.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we found consistent evidence that long-term exposure to ambient fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅) is associated with an increased risk of glaucoma. Pooled analysis of eight population-based observational studies demonstrated a statistically significant association between higher PM₂.₅ exposure and glaucoma risk, with a 12% increase in risk observed in the primary meta-analysis. Importantly, this association remained robust across sensitivity analyses and was not driven by any single study, supporting the stability of the findings. Narrative synthesis of additional pollutants suggested directionally similar, though less consistent, associations for traffic-related pollutants such as nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) and nitrogen oxides (NOx), whereas evidence for PM₁₀, ozone, and gaseous pollutants was heterogeneous.

Our findings extend prior epidemiological evidence linking air pollution to adverse ocular outcomes, including elevated IOP, retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thinning, and optic nerve vulnerability.10,24 While earlier studies primarily focused on functional or subclinical ocular endpoints, the present synthesis demonstrates that long-term PM₂.₅ exposure is also associated with clinically diagnosed glaucoma. The magnitude of association observed in large cohorts from Asia, Europe, and North America suggests that this relationship is not restricted to a single geographic or ethnic population, enhancing the generalizability of the findings.

Notably, the strongest effect estimates were observed in studies with longer exposure windows and higher ambient PM₂.₅ concentrations, particularly in Asian settings, raising the possibility of cumulative dose effects. These findings are consistent with emerging evidence from neurodegenerative research, which increasingly recognizes air pollution as a potentially modifiable environmental risk factor for chronic neurovascular and neurodegenerative conditions.28

Several biologically plausible mechanisms may explain the observed association between PM₂.₅ exposure and glaucoma. Fine particulate matter is capable of penetrating the alveolar–capillary barrier, entering systemic circulation, and inducing chronic oxidative stress and low-grade inflammation.29 Oxidative stress plays a central role in glaucomatous optic neuropathy, contributing to retinal ganglion cell apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired axonal transport. PM₂.₅-induced reactive oxygen species generation may therefore exacerbate susceptibility of the optic nerve head to glaucomatous damage.30

Vascular mechanisms are also likely to be involved. Exposure to PM₂.₅ has been associated with endothelial dysfunction, impaired autoregulation, and microvascular ischemia.31 Given that optic nerve perfusion is critically dependent on finely regulated microcirculation, pollution-related vascular dysregulation may predispose individuals to glaucomatous optic neuropathy, particularly in normal-tension glaucoma phenotypes. Imaging studies reporting reduced peripapillary vascular density and thinner ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer measurements in pollution-exposed populations further support this hypothesis.

In addition, emerging evidence suggests that air pollution may influence IOP through systemic inflammatory pathways, autonomic imbalance, or altered aqueous humor dynamics.22 Although the observed IOP elevations are modest, even small shifts in population-level IOP distributions may translate into meaningful increases in glaucoma incidence. Together, these oxidative, vascular, and pressure-related mechanisms provide a coherent biological framework linking long-term PM₂.₅ exposure to glaucoma development and progression.

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis have important clinical and public health implications. Clinicians should be attentive to environmental exposure history, especially in patients residing in areas with chronically elevated PM₂.₅ concentrations. Several patient subgroups may warrant heightened clinical vigilance. First, urban populations in high-pollution environments represent a population-level risk group, given their sustained long-term exposure to fine particulate matter and traffic-related pollutants. Second, older patients may be disproportionately susceptible, as the mechanisms implicated, oxidative stress, vascular dysregulation, and neuroinflammation, are compounded by age-related decline in antioxidant capacity and microvascular resilience. Third, the normal-tension glaucoma (NTG) phenotype deserves particular attention. Since NTG, by definition, occurs independent of IOP elevation, and given that the proposed mechanisms of air pollution-related optic neuropathy (vascular compromise and oxidative injury) are precisely those invoked in NTG pathogenesis, patients presenting with NTG in high-pollution settings may represent a clinically distinct and environmentally exposed subgroup worthy of further investigation. While our findings do not yet support a change in formal screening protocols, environmental exposure history may become a relevant component of glaucoma risk profiling, particularly as urban and industrial air pollution exposures become increasingly quantifiable through validated tools. Future prospective studies should evaluate whether integrating pollution exposure into established risk calculators improves early detection of glaucoma in susceptible individuals.

At the population level, these findings reinforce the need to consider ocular health outcomes in air quality regulations and environmental health policies, which have traditionally focused on cardiovascular and respiratory disease. Reductions in ambient PM₂.₅ concentrations through improved emission controls and urban planning could yield ancillary benefits in preventing or delaying glaucoma onset. Furthermore, the results underscore the importance of integrating environmental exposure data into public health strategies for chronic eye diseases and highlight the need for interdisciplinary collaboration among ophthalmology, environmental health, and policy stakeholders. Future research incorporating individual-level exposure assessment and longitudinal ocular outcomes will be essential to refine risk stratification and inform targeted prevention strategies.

Several limitations of this study merit consideration. All included studies were observational in design, comprising cohort, cross-sectional, and time-series analyses, and, as such, causal inference is not possible. Residual confounding, exposure misclassification, and variability in glaucoma ascertainment methods represent important sources of potential bias that should be explicitly considered when interpreting the pooled effect estimates. Second, exposure assessment relied predominantly on area-level or model-based estimates of ambient PM₂.₅, which may not fully capture individual-level exposure variability, leading to potential nondifferential misclassification and attenuation of effect estimates.

Third, heterogeneity in outcome definitions, including reliance on administrative codes or self-reported glaucoma diagnoses in some studies, may have introduced outcome misclassification. However, sensitivity analyses restricted to studies with clinically ascertained outcomes yielded comparable results, supporting the robustness of the findings. Fourth, the number of studies included in the meta-analysis was modest, limiting the power to formally detect publication bias and to conduct detailed subgroup analyses. Finally, most studies were conducted in high- or middle-income countries, and data from low-income regions with high pollution burdens remain scarce. Despite these limitations, the consistency of findings across diverse populations, the robustness of sensitivity analyses, and the strong biological plausibility collectively strengthen the inference that long-term PM₂.₅ exposure is a relevant environmental risk factor for glaucoma.

Future research should aim to strengthen causal inference and refine risk stratification by integrating more precise individual-level exposure assessment with longitudinal ophthalmic outcomes. Prospective cohort studies incorporating personal exposure monitoring, indoor air pollution measures, and time–activity patterns would help reduce exposure misclassification inherent in area-level models. In addition, multipollutant modeling approaches are needed to disentangle the independent and combined effects of traffic-related pollutants, particularly PM₂.₅ and nitrogen oxides, on glaucoma risk.

Mechanistic studies linking ambient air pollution exposure to ocular biomarkers, such as retinal microvascular perfusion, oxidative stress markers, and mitochondrial dysfunction, would further elucidate biological pathways underlying glaucomatous damage. Incorporation of genetic susceptibility profiles and gene–environment interaction analyses may also help identify subpopulations at heightened risk. Finally, future studies from low- and middle-income countries, where pollution levels are often highest and glaucoma detection remains limited, are essential to improve global risk estimates and inform equitable public health interventions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis provides comprehensive evidence that long-term exposure to ambient fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅) is associated with an increased risk of glaucoma. The association was consistent across diverse populations, robust to sensitivity analyses, and supported by biologically plausible mechanisms involving oxidative stress, vascular dysfunction, and neurodegeneration. Causality cannot be established from the available observational evidence, and prospective studies with individual-level exposure assessment and longitudinal ophthalmic outcomes will be needed to confirm these findings. Given the global burden of glaucoma and the widespread exposure to air pollution, these findings have important implications for both clinical practice and public health policy. Recognition of environmental exposures as modifiable risk factors may contribute to earlier identification of at-risk individuals and support broader efforts to improve air quality. Continued interdisciplinary research integrating ophthalmology, environmental health, and policy science will be critical to translating these findings into effective prevention strategies.
