Genome‐wide association study and polygenic risk scores of eosinophilia in Taiwan
Authors: Hsing‐Fang Lu, Su Boon‐Yong, Chi‐Ya Yang, Jiu‐Yao Wang, Yen‐Ting Chang, Fuu‐Jen Tsai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEosinophilia, characterised by elevated eosinophil levels, is associated with various allergic and inflammatory conditions. This study aimed to elucidate the genetic determinants of eosinophil levels by examining polygenic risk scores (PRS) and identifying significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs).MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive genome‐wide association study (GWAS) on eosinophil levels using a large cohort, comprising 221 851 controls (eosinophil levels ≤ 5%) and 11 200 cases (eosinophil levels > 5%) in Taiwan. The analysis included covariates such as age, sex and the first 10 principal components to account for population stratification. Polygenic risk scores were calculated, and their associations with eosinophil levels were evaluated.ResultsThe control group exhibited a mean eosinophil level of 47.81 (SD, 21.08) and was composed of 55.32% females and 44.68% males. The case group had a mean eosinophil level of 43.27 (SD, 22.27) and consisted of 38.6% females and 61.4% males. The GWAS identified several SNPs with significant associations, including rs7646596, rs8191981, rs140105250, rs77143352, rs12535759, rs11327184, rs16917546, rs76738175, rs11568075, rs9557175 and rs2801127, with rs7646596 showing the most significant P‐value. PRS analysis indicated that individuals with higher polygenic risk scores were significantly more likely to have elevated eosinophil levels.ConclusionThis study identified several genetic loci significantly associated with eosinophil levels, highlighting the polygenic nature of eosinophilia. The robust associations, particularly with SNP rs7646596, offer valuable insights into the genetic underpinnings of eosinophilia and related conditions.Keywords: biological networks, genome‐wide association study, human leukocyte antigen genotypes, phenome‐wide association study, polygenic risk score

Introduction

Eosinophils are a crucial type of white blood cell involved in the body's immune response. The regulation of eosinophil counts is complex and influenced by various genetic factors, which can have significant implications for eosinophil‐associated diseases such as allergies, 1 asthma, 2 and certain autoimmune disorders. 3 Understanding these genetic influences is essential for developing effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. 4

Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have emerged as a powerful approach to identify genetic variants associated with specific traits, including eosinophil counts. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 GWAS involves scanning the genomes of large cohorts to find single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to the trait of interest. 9 , 10 This method has been instrumental in uncovering numerous loci that contribute to eosinophil regulation. For instance, previous studies have identified significant associations near genes such as IL1RL1, 2 IL5, 11 and SH2B3, which play critical roles in eosinophil biology. GWAS on eosinophils has identified significant genetic loci influencing eosinophil counts. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 A notable study by Ferreira et al. identified 31 loci associated with eosinophil levels, implicating genes involved in immune response and inflammation, such as IL1RL1, IL5 and GATA2. These findings highlight the genetic basis of eosinophil regulation, which is critical for understanding conditions such as asthma and allergies. The study also underscored the importance of eosinophils in various immune‐mediated diseases and provided insights into potential therapeutic targets.

Complementing GWAS, polygenic risk scores (PRS) aggregate the effects of multiple genetic variants across the genome to estimate an individual's genetic predisposition to a trait or disease. In recent studies, PRS can enhance the predictive power of genetic data, enabling the identification of individuals at higher risk for disease conditions. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

This study aimed to elucidate the genetic basis of eosinophil regulation by combining GWAS and PRS approaches. By analysing a large and diverse population, the study seeks to identify significant genetic loci associated with eosinophil counts and assess the predictive power of PRS for various eosinophil‐associated phenotypes. The findings can inform the development of genetic screening tools and personalised treatment strategies, ultimately improving the management of eosinophil‐related diseases. In this study, GWAS, PRS and PheWAS were performed in patients with hypereosinophilia using the Taiwanese Han population from China Medical University Hospital (CMUH).

Methods

Sample collection

The study samples were collected from the China Medical University Hospital Precision Medicine Project cohort (CMUH, Taiwan). This cohort contained electronic medical record (EMR) information for each sample. We calculated the mean value of the eosinophil percentage for each individual and classified those with eosinophil levels greater than 5% as the case group, while the rest were designated as the control group. We also collected age and sex information as covariates. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and the Ethics Committee of the Human Studies Committee of China Medical University Hospital.

Genotyping quality control and imputation

We used PLINK 1.9 for the genotyping data quality control process. Single nucleotide polymorphisms with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.001, call rate < 0.98, monomorphic variants and Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) P‐value < 1 × 10−6 were excluded from the analysis. 18 , 19 Samples with a call rate < 0.98, heterozygosity > 5 standard deviations or duplicated samples were discarded. Outliers based on principal component analysis (PCA) from the 1000 Genomes East Asian (EAS) population (> 3 interquartile ranges [IQR] for PC1 and PC2) were removed to ensure the homogeneity of the EAS cohort. Variants with an INFO score > 0.3 were retained for association analysis.

Association study

We used age, sex and the first 10 principal components (PC1 to PC10) as covariates in our analysis. Saige was used to perform the association analysis between high and low eosinophil levels. We used GCTA to conduct conditional analysis and identify independent signals. Cytoband and functional annotation were also performed to better understand the potential implications of each significant SNP.

Pathway analysis

To identify genes potentially involved in regulating eosinophil, we mapped variants passing a significance threshold of P < 1 × 10−6 to their respective genes. We conducted canonical pathway analysis using MAGMA. Gene sets were derived from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) and included pathways from KEGG, Reactome and Gene Ontology (GO) categories. For each gene set, the mean association P‐value of all genes within the set was calculated and compared against the distribution of gene association P‐values across the entire genome. This competitive approach evaluates whether the genes in a particular set are more strongly associated with the trait than genes not included in the set. To correct for multiple testing, a false discovery rate (FDR) q‐value < 0.05 was considered significant.

PRS construction

We used 70% of our cohort to construct the training GWAS summary statistics. PRSice‐2 was used to select the most significant SNPs based on the eosinophil summary statistics from the training group. We then applied the effect sizes to the remaining 30% of the samples and used PLINK 2.0 to sum the effect allele dosages for each individual, generating the eosinophil PRS. To investigate the association between different quantiles of eosinophil PRS groups and eosinophil count, we divided the eosinophil PRS into 20 quantiles (0–1th, 1–2th, 2–3th, 3–5th, 5–10th, 10–20th, 20–30th, 30–40th, 40–50th, 50–55th, 55–60th, 60–70th, 70–80th, 80–90th, and 90–95th, 95–96th, 96–97th, 97–98th, 98–99th, 99–100th). The 50th to 55th quantile range served as the reference group.

Evaluation of eosinophil PRS for disease prediction and the correlation with IgE

We collected four phenotypes from the EMR using the PheWAS package: asthma, asthma with exacerbation, chronic obstructive asthma and chronic obstructive asthma with exacerbation. To assess the predictive utility of the eosinophil PRS, we evaluated its association with these phenotypes by calculating the area under the curve (AUC), with and without adjustment for covariates (age, sex and PC1 to PC10). Additionally, we tested the correlation between eosinophil PRS and serum IgE levels using Pearson's correlation.

Results

Study population and eosinophil count characteristics

The study included a total of 233 051 participants divided into control and case groups based on eosinophil (Eos) counts. Participants with Eos ≤ 5% were classified as controls, and those with Eos > 5% were classified as cases. The Case Group (Eos > 5%) included 11 200 individuals with a mean eosinophil count of 43.27 (SD = 22.27). There were 4323 females (38.6%) and 6877 males (61.4%). The Control Group (Eos ≤ 5%) composed of 221 851 individuals with a mean eosinophil count of 47.81 (SD = 21.08). The gender distribution was 122 736 females (55.32%) and 99 115 males (44.68%).

Another novel signal, rs140105250 (β = 0.032, P = 4.83 × 10−15), is located on Chromosome 2 between the MIR4776‐1 and IKZF2 genes. These loci may influence eosinophil regulation and related biological processes.

Pathway analysis

The MAGMA gene set analysis (Figure 2, Supplementary table 1) identified several pathways significantly enriched for associations with Eosinophil (Eos.) GWAS data, indicating potential biological processes and signalling pathways involved. The most enriched pathway was PID MET TFPATHWAY, with a highly significant P‐value of 1.35 × 10−8 and a β of 0.843. This pathway is involved in calcineurin‐regulated NFAT‐dependent transcription in lymphocytes, suggesting its potential role in immune regulation. Another significantly enriched pathway was WP WHITE FAT CELL DIFFERENTIATION (β = 0.955, P‐value = 1.97 × 10−8), highlighting the involvement of fat cell differentiation processes. Additionally, the REACTOME INTERLEUKIN 2 FAMILY SIGNALLING pathway emerged as significant (β = 0.897, P = 5.39 × 10−8), implicating immune signalling pathways in the phenotype.

Gene set analysis using the GWAS catalogue identified several significant pathways associated with the trait under investigation (Figure 3, Supplementary table 2). Notably, asthma was the most significantly enriched gene set, containing 698 genes, 18 of which were significant (adjusted P = 2.11 × 10−9). This suggests a strong association with eosinophil‐related genes involved in asthma pathways. Eosinophil counts emerged as another highly significant pathway (adjusted P = 4.64 × 10−8), with key genes such as IKZF2, GATA2, C3orf27, RPN1, IL5, RAD50, CDK6, ZNF365, EGR2, UBAC2, IL4R, ALOX15 and PELP1 being identified. This finding further supports the successful replication of previously published GWAS data. Interestingly, pathways associated with other autoimmune diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease (n = 12, adjusted P = 3.80 × 10−5), multiple sclerosis (n = 10, adjusted P = 3.99 × 10−5) and Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada syndrome (adjusted P = 1.54 × 10−4), were also significantly enriched. These results suggest shared genetic architecture between eosinophil counts and autoimmune or inflammatory conditions, indicating potential overlaps between these diseases and eosinophil‐related processes (Table 3).

Polygenic risk score (PRS) analysis

Polygenic risk scores were calculated using the PRSice tool, incorporating age, sex and the first 10 principal components (PC1–PC10) as covariates. The model fit, as measured by the R 2 value, was evaluated across different P‐value thresholds (P t), with the highest fit observed at a threshold of 5.00 × 10−8, corresponding to a highly significant R 2 value (Figure 4). As the P‐value threshold increases, allowing more genetic variants to be included, the model fit decreases slightly but remains stable across the range of thresholds from 0.001 to 1. We found that the inclusion of more variants beyond the most significant ones does not substantially improve the model's predictive ability. The highest statistical significance is observed at the lowest P‐value threshold, as shown by the darker red colour in the bar representing P T = 5e−08. A total of 17 SNPs, selected with a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10−8, were used to construct the PRS. The P‐value for the model at this threshold was extremely significant (1.29 × 10−41), highlighting the robustness of the association.

The odds ratios (ORs) for the phenotype across different strata of polygenic scores are shown in Figure 5c. The ORs increase as the PRS rises, indicating a stronger association between higher PRS and the phenotype. These results underscore the significant relationship between PRS and eosinophil levels, with individuals in the case group demonstrating higher PRS than those in the control group. The observed increase in odds ratios with higher polygenic scores further suggests a strong genetic predisposition influencing eosinophil levels. Overall, this analysis demonstrates that the EOS PRS is highly correlated with eosinophil levels, providing valuable insight into the genetic basis of this trait.

Application of EOS PRS and predictive power

We further evaluated the predictive power of the EOS PRS combined with covariates (age, sex and PC1–PC10) for various asthma‐related phenotypes using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. For asthma, the model including both PRS and covariates achieved an AUC of 0.5033, compared to 0.5077 for covariates alone, and 0.5184 for the PRS alone. Similarly, for asthma with exacerbations, the PRS combined with covariates resulted in an AUC of 0.532, compared to 0.533 for covariates alone and 0.514 for the PRS alone. These results suggest that the current EOS PRS does not show strong predictive ability for asthma or asthma with exacerbation, indicating that further refinement or additional genetic factors may be necessary to improve the model's prediction accuracy for these phenotypes.

EOS PRS and IgE

We assessed the correlation between the eosinophil PRS and serum IgE levels, a key marker of allergic responses commonly elevated in eosinophil‐associated conditions such as asthma and atopic dermatitis [1, 4]. No significant correlation was found between eosinophil PRS and serum IgE levels (Pearson's correlation coefficient = 0.003, P = 0.73). These findings underscore the complexity of genetic regulation within immune responses and suggest that eosinophil counts and serum IgE levels are governed by separate genetic mechanisms. Consequently, comprehensive models integrating multiple immune biomarkers may be required to enhance the accuracy of disease prediction in eosinophil‐associated diseases.

Discussion

Our study investigated the genetic underpinnings of eosinophil counts using GWAS and PRS in a large, diverse population. The results provided valuable insights into the genetic factors influencing eosinophil regulation and their potential implications for eosinophil‐associated diseases such as asthma.

The SNP rs7646596 has been investigated for its association with eosinophil levels. Eosinophils are a type of white blood cell involved in the body's immune response, particularly in diseases such as asthma, 2 allergies and parasitic infections. Research has shown that individuals carrying certain alleles of rs7646596 tend to have higher eosinophil counts, which could predispose them to eosinophil‐related conditions. Understanding the genetic basis of eosinophil regulation through variants such as rs7646596 provides valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms of immune‐related diseases and potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

SNPs were significantly associated with eosinophil levels. These significant SNPs often clustered, suggesting specific genomic regions associated with eosinophil levels. Specific chromosomes, including Chromosomes 1, 3, 10 and 16, have shown higher densities of significant SNPs in our study, highlighting potential key regions or genes involved in the regulation of eosinophil levels. These findings suggest certain genetic loci are significantly associated with eosinophil levels, which could be critical for understanding the genetic basis of eosinophil regulation and related diseases.

Utility of polygenic risk scores

The PRS analysis demonstrated the utility of combining genetic data with covariates (age, sex and PC1–PC10) to predict eosinophil‐associated phenotypes. The PRS showed that there was no significant predictive power for chronic obstructive asthma and its exacerbations but less so for asthma or asthma with exacerbation. These findings suggest that while PRS can enhance EOS level prediction, its utility may vary depending on the specific phenotype.

The lack of significant predictive power for asthma and asthma with exacerbation could be because of the multifactorial nature of these conditions, where environmental and lifestyle factors play substantial roles. 12 Nonetheless, the strong predictive power for chronic obstructive asthma highlights the potential of PRS in identifying individuals at higher risk for severe eosinophil‐related conditions, enabling early intervention and personalised treatment strategies.

Clinical relevance and implications

The findings from this study may have significant clinical implications. The identified genetic loci and the EOS PRS could contribute to the development of genetic screening tools for early identification of individuals at risk for elevated eosinophil levels. This, in turn, could aid in the early detection and management of conditions related to eosinophil dysregulation. Clinicians should consider integrating genetic information, alongside clinical and lifestyle factors, when diagnosing and treating patients. This holistic approach can improve personalised care strategies, particularly in the context of conditions influenced by eosinophil levels.

Limitations

Despite the promising findings, several limitations exist. First, although there is a large population size from Taiwan, it may still lack adequate representation of certain ethnic groups, which could limit the generalisability of the results. Future research should aim to include a larger population size with more diversity. Second, reliance on self‐reported data for certain phenotypes can introduce bias and affect the accuracy of the associations. Incorporating clinical diagnoses and longitudinal data could improve the robustness of the findings. Third, while PRS showed strong predictive power for certain conditions, their clinical utility remains limited by the relatively small effect sizes of individual SNPs and the complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors. Therefore, developing more comprehensive predictive models that integrate genetic, environmental and lifestyle data is crucial for enhancing the accuracy and applicability of PRS.

Conclusion

Our study identified several novel genetic signals associated with EOS levels. Pathway analysis revealed multiple biological pathways potentially involved in the regulation of eosinophil counts. Additionally, the EOS PRS demonstrated a strong correlation with eosinophil levels, reinforcing its potential as a predictive tool. These findings provide valuable insights into the genetic regulation of eosinophil counts and offer implications for better understanding eosinophil‐associated diseases.
