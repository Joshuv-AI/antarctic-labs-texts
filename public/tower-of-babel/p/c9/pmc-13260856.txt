Moving beyond moral indignation: global health governance in an era of defunding
Authors: Marleen Bekker
Published: 2026

On 9 May 2025, Europeans celebrated Europe Day. This day marked the 75th anniversary of the Schuman Declaration, which laid the foundations for the European Union and paved the way for an unprecedented era of prosperity, peace, democracy, solidarity, and cooperation in Europe.

Currently, however, within Europe and at a global scale, many countries are witnessing profound political and democratic challenges. Governments of different parties have made structural budget cuts to public health systems as well as academic science infrastructures. Many countries face a battle over democratic values and institutions. International institutions for multilateral health governance such as the WHO, the Paris Climate Agreement and the Pandemic Treaty face both withdrawal from high-income countries such as the USA as well as competing bilateral agreements trading data for aid to lower- and middle-income countries. The collapse of health services in the Global South has already led to tens of thousands of deaths and increasing incidence of HIV and other infectious diseases. Furthermore, the new European Commission is also considering a withdrawal from global health funds. It has already silently ended internal support for environmental, climate, and health NGOs, thereby reducing the level playing field for lobbyists in Brussels and Strasbourg. Propositions for the Multiannual Financial Framework remove the autonomous health programme, make it part of the Competitiveness Fund, and prioritize pharmaceutical intellectual property rights.

To globally govern such change requires a change in global governance. To explore possible pathways, at the EPH conference in November 2025, the EUPHA Public Health Politics and Policies Section and the Public Health Economic Section jointly organized a panel workshop on the ‘global health implications of the US foreign aid review for the EU in global health governance’, with the aim of revisiting EU capacities and positioning in global health governance and exploring options for action. In this issue, Scott Greer, João Vasco Santos, Scott Burris, and David Patterson present their views on the issue from political science, economics, and law. They combine critical analysis with historical awareness and institutional self-reflection: How do we move beyond astonishment, moral indignation, and paralysis?

In an attempt to explain why key EU member states are disinvesting in international development aid instead of leveraging the global health infrastructure at relatively low transaction costs, we argue that the anti-science and anti-public health policies of the past year require introspection. Which operational practices and communication strategies within the public health sector may have contributed to a decline in public trust? Emphasizing the importance of addressing health inequities while such disparities have continued to widen over recent decades does not indicate that the public health sector has been successful in achieving this objective. Similarly, advocating for health policies grounded in human rights has not prevented the persistent violation of those rights among certain populations and in specific regions. Furthermore, highlighting the financial, material, and social co-benefits of investing in health has not deterred decision-makers from reducing overall social welfare provisions.

The point is not that we should refrain from such ambitious calls. We need to do it better, smarter, more politics-savvy and more grounded in the public’s and civil society’s lived reality. The key message emerging from the perspectives is that the international public health community can reclaim its position through a three-fold approach. Explaining the robust economics of high-income countries’ (HICs) dependence on global health investments, combined with vigilant legal scrutiny of often opaque administrative regulations and the strategic use of international legal institutions, must be integrated with a substantive reorientation of public service performance—including health systems—toward addressing unmet needs and strengthening public support. Such a comprehensive approach is necessary to restore the political legitimacy underpinning both global and national funding commitments.
