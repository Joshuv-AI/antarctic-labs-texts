Introduction

Bainbridge-Ropers syndrome (BRPS) is an uncommon neurodevelopmental disorder primarily caused by mutations in the Additional Sex Combs Like 3 (ASXL3) gene, located on chromosome 18. These mutations lead to a loss of function in ASXL3, contributing to the clinical manifestations observed in individuals with BRPS, including developmental delay, intellectual disability, feeding difficulties, hypotonia, and distinctive facial features [1, 2]. ASXL3 is mammalian homologs of drosophila Additional sex combs-like 3 gene. Together with ASXL1 and ASXL2, they consist of ASXL family members and are assumed to be epigenetic regulators that are involved in hereditary neurological disorders, malignancies and congenital heart disease. Disruption of this regulatory function is thought to underlie the neurodevelopmental phenotype [3, 4]. Clinically, affected individuals often exhibit autistic features, failure to thrive, and distinct craniofacial features such as arched eyebrows, prominent forehead, and a high-arched palate [4, 5]. Most reported cases are de novo truncating variants, including nonsense and frameshift mutations. In this case report, we present a novel heterozygous ASXL3 gene variant located on chromosome 18 (chr18:31318777–31318779; NM_030632.3; exon 11), characterized by a deletion of three nucleotides ATA at c.1409_1411 and replaced by TT (c.1409_1411delinsTT), resulting in a frameshift mutation in the amino acid sequence (p. His470Leufs*14). This study aims to enhance the comprehensive understanding of BRPS for pediatricians and contribute to the expanding genotype-phenotype spectrum associated with ASXL3 mutations.

Case history

A 4-month-and-6-day-old male infant presented to the outpatient clinic with a 3-day history of fever, cough, and wheezing. Prior to admission, the patient had received oral medications including oseltamivir phosphate granules, erythromycin, and compound aminophenazone solution, but symptoms persisted. Due to recurrent fever and respiratory distress, he was admitted to the Department of Infectious Diseases with a provisional diagnosis of pneumonia, congenital laryngomalacia, and severe influenza A. During pregnancy, the fetus was noted to have constitutional weakness and a small cranial-thoracic cavity. After delivery, the patient was admitted to Henan Maternal and Child Health Hospital for 5 days due to respiratory distress, during which continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy was administered for 1 day. The patient was discharged with multiple neonatal conditions, including transient tachypnea, mild cardiac and metabolic abnormalities, and structural anomalies. At two months of age, chest CT indicated pneumonia in the absence of acute respiratory symptoms.

Differential diagnosis, investigations and treatment

On admission, the patient appeared lethargic and tachypneic, with coarse breath sounds and diffuse moist rales. Blood gas analysis revealed critical values, including a pH of 7.221 (reference range: 7.35–7.45), an acid-base balance (T) of 7.221 (reference range: 7.35–7.45), and a lactate level of 6.9 mmol/L (reference range: 0-2.2 mmol/L), indicating a critical condition. The chest X-ray demonstrated increased lung markings, heterogeneous lung transparency, and focal areas of increased density in both lung lobes (Fig. 1). The patient presented with lethargy, tachypnea, bilateral coarse breath sounds, as well as abundant moist rales and wheezing on auscultation. Metabolic acidosis was suspected and managed through correction of acidosis, fluid replacement therapy, and administration of antimicrobial agents. Elevated white blood cell (17.51 10^9/L; reference range: 4.3–14.2 10^9/L) counts and neutrophil ratio (64.6%; reference range: 7–56%) indicated a concomitant bacterial infection. Initial treatment included correction of acidosis, intravenous fluids, and antimicrobial therapy. The patient was placed under cardiac monitoring, oxygen supplementation, and nebulization. On the third day, following consultation with endocrinology and genetic metabolism specialists, whole exome sequencing (WES) was initiated due to recurrent respiratory distress, unexplained metabolic abnormalities, developmental concerns, and failure to thrive—features suggestive of a possible genetic syndrome. Trio-WES was performed to investigate potential de novo or pathogenic variants. No consanguinity, family history of genetic disease, or similar phenotypes were identified in the parents or siblings. By day 4, the patient’s condition had improved, with normalization of temperature and stabilization of oxygen saturation. The critically ill status was discontinued. On day 6, a KL-6 test was performed to assess pulmonary interstitial involvement, and peramivir treatment was discontinued. On day 7, discharge was requested by the patient’s parents. The patient showed improvement, with a normal body temperature, in improved condition with stable vital signs and mild residual cough.

Outcome and follow up

At discharge, caregivers were instructed on home care, including maintaining a warm environment, performing chest physiotherapy, and administering oral cefaclor suspension (1/3 packet, three times a day for three days) as well as ambroxol oral solution (for 3–5 days). Immediate medical attention was recommended if the patient developed fever, dyspnea, aggravated cough, or other symptoms. Trio-WES analysis confirmed a de novo heterozygous ASXL3 gene variant (c.1409_1411delinsTT; p. His470Leufs*14), involving the deletion of nucleotides c.1409 to c.1411 replaced by TT. Detailed information regarding NGS testing can be found in the supplementary materials. This mutation was not reported by either parent through family segregation analysis (Fig. 2). No clinical manifestations were observed in the patient’s two sisters (aged 3 years and 4 months and 1 year and 7 months, respectively).

According to the ACMG guidelines, this variant was classified as pathogenic based on the following criteria: PVS1 (null variant causing a frameshift in a gene where loss of function is a known mechanism of disease), PS2 (confirmed de novo variant with parental verification), and PM2_Supporting (absent from population databases, and not reported with a benign interpretation in ClinVar or the literature). Notably, no existing reports on this specific site were identified in the ClinVar database. Therefore, this represents the first reported instance of an ASXL3 frameshift mutation resulting from the deletion of nucleotides c.1409 to c.1411, followed by replacement with nucleotides TT. Although it meets the ACMG criteria for pathogenicity, the absence of functional studies warrants further investigation to clarify its precise role in disease pathogenesis and its clinical significance.

Discussion

BRPS is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by a complex array of clinical features, including developmental delay, intellectual disability, distinctive facial features, and various congenital anomalies. BRPS was initially described by Bainbridge et al. in 2013, followed by the subsequent identification of mutations in the ASXL3 gene as its underlying genetic cause [2]. The ASXL3 gene, located on chromosome 18q12.1, encodes a protein involved in epigenetic regulation and chromatin remodeling. ASXL3 belongs to the ASXL family, which encompasses ASXL1 and ASXL2. While mutations in ASXL1 and ASXL2 are associated with Bohring-Opitz syndrome and Shashi-Pena syndrome respectively, it is specifically mutations in ASXL3 that give rise to BRPS [1]. BRPS exhibits diverse clinical manifestations that can vary significantly among affected individuals. Patients may encounter a spectrum of developmental challenges, encompassing feeding difficulties, intellectual disability, global developmental delay, hypotonia, and specific craniofacial features [5–7]. Furthermore, the syndrome is associated with medical challenges such as sleep disruption and behavioral issues like self-injury [1]. While most reported cases are diagnosed later in infancy or childhood, our case was genetically confirmed at four months of age, allowing earlier clinical recognition and intervention.

In this case report, we present the clinical and genetic findings of a Chinese infant boy with BRPS. The patient initially presented with pneumonia, congenital laryngomalacia, and severe influenza A. Additionally, the patient exhibited developmental delay, patent foramen ovale, and various congenital anomalies. Although individually non-specific, the combination of these features prompted consideration of an underlying syndromic disorder. WES analysis revealed a novel heterozygous ASXL3 gene variant, a frameshift mutation caused by the deletion of nucleotides c.1409 to c.1411 and subsequent replacement with TT (c.1409_1411delinsTT). To our knowledge, this variant has not been documented in the current literature or public databases.

Given the limited number of BRPS cases described in China, further case aggregation is essential to broaden our understanding of the ASXL3 mutation spectrum. Most patients with BRPS have moderate to severe intellectual disability and profound language impairment; some present with comorbid autism spectrum disorder or epilepsy [8]. Despite the absence of clinical manifestations in the patient’s two siblings, genetic testing is still recommended for prevention and early diagnosis. Due to the non-specific nature of the main clinical features associated with BRPS and the limited number of reported cases, WES technology is increasingly employed for identifying pathogenesis and establishing definitive diagnoses [9]. Unfortunately, due to the lack of effective treatment options currently available, the prognosis for individuals affected by this condition remains poor, only symptomatic treatment can be administered. Therefore, prioritizing early identification of clinical features such as feeding difficulties, growth disorders, and distinct facial characteristics is essential to improve genetic examination processes and ensure accurate diagnosis for affected individuals.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have identified a novel heterozygous variant in the ASXL3 gene, which has not been reported. This discovery expands the known mutational spectrum of ASXL3-associated BRPS and provides valuable data for future variant interpretation. Further investigations are warranted to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying BRPS, explore the genetic heterogeneity of the syndrome in diverse populations, and develop potential targeted therapies. Clinically, this case underscores the importance of early genomic testing in infants with unexplained multisystem manifestations, contributing to more accurate genetic counseling and informed prenatal decision-making.
