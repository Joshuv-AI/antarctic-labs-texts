Rare earth mining in Myanmar: an environmental health crisis hidden by conflict
Authors: Hein Thu, Minn Khant Kyaw Linn
Published: 2026

Rare earth elements (REEs), a group of 17 metals including neodymium, dysprosium, and terbium, are indispensable to modern industry. They are critical components in permanent magnets for electric vehicle motors, wind turbines, consumer electronics, defense systems, and medical imaging equipment. Global REE production reached 390,000 metric tonnes in 2025, with demand for magnetic rare earths projected to triple by 2035.1,2 The accelerating green energy transition has intensified global competition for these minerals, particularly heavy rare earths (HREEs) essential for high-performance magnets. Yet behind this technological promise lies a largely invisible environmental health crisis unfolding in an conflict zone: Myanmar.

Myanmar is now one of the largest rare earth producers in the world, yielding 22,000 tonnes of rare earth oxide equivalent in 2025.1 Between 2017 and 2024, Myanmar exported over 290,000 tonnes of rare earth material to China, valued at more than 4.2 billion USD, with nearly 85% of that value generated after the February 2021 military coup.3 Mining is concentrated in Kachin State (Pangwa, Chipwi, and Momauk townships) and expanding into Shan State, where the United Wa State Army controls operations near the Chinese border. Nearly 400 mining sites now operate across Kachin State alone, representing a 194% increase since the February 1, 2021 military coup.3,4 All mining is effectively unregulated: no environmental impact assessment has ever been conducted for any rare earth operation in Myanmar.3,4 These mining areas are contested by multiple armed actors. The Kachin Independence Army seized the primary Pangwa–Chipwi mining belt from junta-aligned militias in late 2024, while other armed organizations control separate mining territories.5 The Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project ranks Myanmar second on its global Conflict Index, with over 58,000 violent events recorded since 2020.6 Rare earth revenues now constitute a major financing source for armed groups on all sides of the conflict, creating incentives against regulation.

Although no epidemiological studies exist from Myanmar’s mining zones, evidence from analogous Chinese rare earth regions reveals multi-system impact on health due to toxicity. A study of communities in southern Jiangxi Province of China found altered liver function, reduced serum protein, and blood biochemistry abnormalities7 Each tonne of rare earth produced generates an estimated 75 cubic metres of acidic wastewater and one tonne of radioactive residue. A 2025 review of REE toxicity studies suggested that chronic REE exposure may be associated with multi-system effects, including respiratory inflammation and fibrosis, hepatic injury, skeletal accumulation, neurotoxicity, and reproductive toxicity. However, the evidence remains heterogeneous and is largely derived from in vitro, animal, occupational, and environmental exposure studies rather than definitive population-based human studies.8 Exposure pathways and risk profiles also vary by population: workers in mining and processing settings may face higher-intensity exposure through inhalation of REE-containing dust and fine particulates, as well as direct chemical handling, while nearby and downstream communities may experience lower-dose but chronic exposure through contaminated water, soil, crops, food chains, and airborne particulates.9,10 Children and pregnant women may be particularly vulnerable because of developmental susceptibility and higher relative intake of contaminated air, water, or food per body weight.11,12 Myanmar’s environmental regulatory framework such as 2015 Environmental Impact Assessment Procedures13 was never robustly enforced. The dominant extraction method, in-situ leaching, involves injecting ammonium sulphate and hydrochloric acid directly into mountainsides, a practice banned in most jurisdictions. China’s exports of ammonium sulphate to Myanmar surged from 93,000 tonnes in 2015 to 1.5 million tonnes in 2023.4 Workers handle these chemicals without protective equipment, and communities report respiratory illness, skin diseases, increased miscarriages, and contaminated water sources.3,4 The lack of comprehensive REE-specific occupational exposure standards, beyond limited element-specific limits such as yttrium, underscores the need for detailed worker-protection guidance, including exposure assessment, dust suppression, ventilation, respiratory protection, chemical handling protocols, and biological and environmental monitoring.14

No environmental health monitoring of any kind exists in mining areas, cross-border contamination has already been detected with arsenic and cadmium exceeding World Health Organization limits in Thailand’s Mekong tributaries downstream of Myanmar mining sites, affecting communities in Chiang Rai Province.15 Perhaps most concerning from a governance perspective, major mandatory conflict-minerals frameworks such as the EU Conflict Minerals Regulation, focus on tin, tantalum, tungsten, and gold rather than rare earth elements, leaving rare earth supply chains from conflict-affected settings largely outside comparable mandatory due diligence requirements.16 The global health community should not remain silent. We call for: first, expansion of international conflict mineral frameworks to explicitly include rare earth elements with enforceable health impact provisions; second, establishment of cross-border epidemiological surveillance in the Mekong basin, leveraging Thai and regional research institutions where direct mine-site access remains impossible; and third, mandatory rare earth supply chain due diligence that holds downstream manufacturers accountable for sourcing from unregulated conflict zones. The health costs of the clean energy transition must not be borne invisibly by the most vulnerable.
