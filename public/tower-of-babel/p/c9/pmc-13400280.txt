Tri-frequency ultrasound-assisted extraction of phenolic compounds from Citrus maxima/grandis peels: Process optimization and cultivar-dependent phytochemical profiling
Authors: Faryal Asif, Liu Jiangtao, Li Yabo, Peng Yu, Xiao Jianbo, Tao Hongxun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Optimized tri-frequency ultrasound-assisted extraction to maximize phenolic compounds recovery from Citrus maxima peels.•Demonstrated TFUAE as a green and sustainable extraction method with improved efficiency.•Revealed regional and cultivar-dependent variation in pomelo peels phytochemicals and antioxidant properties.•Highlighted potential applications of pomelo peel extracts in functional foods.

Introduction

Pomelo (Citrus maxima/grandis), a member of Rutaceae family is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions, particularly in China, which accounts for more than half of the global pomelo production[1]. Beyond its desirable sensory characteristics, pomelo fruit is valued for its rich nutritional profile plus diverse phyto-chemicals including; phenolic compounds, flavonoids and anthocyanins. However, pomelo consumption generates a substantial volume of peel waste, representing approximately 40 % of total fruit weight, which habitually discarded as agricultural waste and results in environmental pollution plus loss of natural resources[[2], [3]].

Pomelo waste is divided into albedo (white inner layer of peels), flavedo (peels colored peripheral surface) and seeds. Albedo and flavedo accounts for approximately 30 % of the total weight of pomelo fruit[4]. Pomelo peels (PPs) including flavedo and albedo, have traditionally been utilized in Chinese medicine due to their reported detoxifying, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory and phlegm alleviation properties[[5], [6]]. Previous studies have demonstrated that PPs are valuable source of bioactive compounds including phenolic acids, flavonoids and other secondary metabolites with strong antioxidant, antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties. [[4], [7]]. Consequently, sustainable valorization of pomelo peel waste has attracted increasing interest for the development of functional ingredients and natural antioxidants.

Efficient extraction of bioactive compounds is a critical step towards the valorization of plant-derived materials[[8], [9]]. Traditional extraction techniques like: conventional solvent extraction, maceration or hydro-distillation are often associated with prolonged extraction time, high solvent consumption or substantial energy requirement and limit extraction efficiency, destroy compounds structure as well as raise environmental concerns respectively [[10], [11]].

In contrast, ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) has emerged as an efficient green extraction technology due to its reduced extraction time, lower solvent usage, enhanced extraction yield and better preservation of thermosensitive compounds[[12], [13]]. Extraction efficiency of UAE is mainly attributed to acoustic cavitation, where the formation and implosive collapse of microbubbles generate localized shear forces, turbulence, pressure gradients and microjets which disrupt plant cell walls, facilitate solvent penetration and accelerate mass transfer of intracellular compounds into the extraction medium[14].

Recently, multi-frequency ultrasound systems have attracted increasing attention because of their improved cavitation behavior and extraction performance compared with conventional mono-frequency systems. As mono-frequency ultrasound generates localized cavitation regions and uneven acoustic energy distribution, thereby limiting extraction uniformity. Although dual-frequency systems partially improve cavitation dynamics, sequential tri-frequency ultrasound-assisted extraction (TFUAE) provides a more dynamic cavitation environment through periodic frequency switching [[15], [16]]. Different ultrasonic frequencies generate cavitation bubbles with varying sizes and collapse intensities, which influence tissue disruption, solvent diffusion and mass transfer differently. Sequential switching among frequencies continuously renews cavitation conditions, minimizes cavitation shielding and acoustic dead-zone formation and improves extraction efficiency. Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of multi-frequency ultrasound systems for enhanced phytochemical recovery from various plant matrices[[17], [18]]. However, the application of sequential TFUAE for phenolic extraction from Citrus maxima peels remains largely unexplored.

Extraction efficiency is strongly influenced by operational parameters, plant matrix characteristics and solvent properties. In particular, solvent polarity plays a crucial role in determining phytochemical solubility, solvent–matrix interactions and diffusion behavior[[19], [20]]. Moreover, optimization of extraction conditions using single-factor approaches may overlook interactions among multiple process variables. Therefore, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) has been widely employed as an effective statistical tool for multivariable optimization and evaluation of interactive effects among extraction parameters to maximize recovery of target compounds[21].

Therefore, present study aimed to optimize sequential TFUAE conditions to maximize phenolic compounds recovery from peels of seven pomelo cultivars collected from distinct regions of China. Effect of solvents (ethanol, methanol and water) on recovery of total phenolic compounds was comparatively evaluated. In addition, antioxidant cultivar-dependent variation in phenolic composition and antioxidant properties was investigated. Overall, this work aspires to propose a green and sustainable strategy for valorization of PPs waste through cavitation-enhanced extraction of bioactive phenolics with potential applications in food preservation and functional food development.

Chemicals and reagents

Distilled water, methanol, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), folin-Ciocalteu reagent, sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), gallic acid, sodium nitrite (NaNO2), aluminum chloride (AlCl3), quercetin, Na2HPO4·12H2O, NaH2PO4·2H2O, potassium ferrocyanide [K4Fe(CN)6], trichloro acetic acid (C2HCl3O2), ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), DPPH (2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl), ethanol, ABTS [2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)], potassium persulfate (K2S2O8), ascorbic acid, acetonitrile, HPLC grade standards (morin, rutin, quercetin, catechin, kaempferol, quinic acid, coumaric acid, gallic acid, ferulic acid, chlorogenic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, cinnamic acid and protocatechuic acid). All the chemicals used were of analytical grade or higher.

Sample preparation

All the PPs were washed thoroughly with distilled water to eradicate any dust, cut into small pieces and desiccated in a hot air oven (Prad DHG-9245A, China) at 50 ℃ until constant weight was attained. Dried samples were ground by using food-grade grinder with intermittent operation to avoid heat accumulation till fine powder attained (Fig. 1). Then powdered samples were sieved (60 mesh), coded (Table 1) and stored in the polythene bags at 4 ℃ until further analysis. desiccant was added to prevent samples from moisture absorption or humidity.Fig. 1Visual images of samples.

Tri-frequency ultrasound-assisted extraction (TFUAE)

TFUAE was performed with the elaborated method of[22]with some modification by using a three-frequency planar divergent ultrasonic equipment developed by School of Food & Biological Engineering at Jiangsu University (Zhenjiang, China). The ultrasonic system consisted of three ultrasonic generators operating at frequencies of 20, 40, and 60 kHz with corresponding ultrasonic powers of 220, 240 and 260 W respectively. Briefly, 1 g of each dried sample was mixed separately with ethanol, methanol or distilled water as extraction solvent and ultrasonicated under sequential frequency-switching mode with pulsed sonication cycles of 5 s ON and 5 s OFF. Extraction experiments were performed at different solid-to-liquid ratio, temperature and time according to the experimental design presented in Table 2. Following extraction, samples were centrifuged at 6000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. Experimental design for TFUAE optimization

Three factors Box-Behnken design (BBD) with response surface methodology (RSM) was used to optimize TFUAE conditions. Three different solvents ethanol, methanol and water were used as extraction medium. For each extraction medium identical experimental plan was used as produced via Design Expert software (Version 13, Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, USA). 17 experimental runs with five replicates at center point were used to extract PPs powder by TFUAE. Factors detail along with their levels is presented in Table 2. TPC and TFC values were taken as response variables. All the experiments were performed and 3D response surface plots approach was used to visualize the individual as well as interactive properties of process factors on the total yield of secondary compounds (TPC and TFC). Statistics of analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to investigate the model significance.

Comparative analysis

Comparative analysis was performed to evaluate the extraction efficiency enhancement achieved by TFUAE.

Conventional solvent extraction (CSE)

CSE was performed by adapting the method of[23]. 1 g powdered sample was mixed with 40 mL of distilled water. Extraction was carried out for 40 min at 40 °C in a shaking incubator (Innova 42, New Brunswick Scientific, Canada) at 150 rpm. Then, supernatant was collected by extract centrifugation (6000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C) to assess total secondary metabolites.

Mono and dual frequency UAE

PPs powder was extracted with the water as solvent (1:40 g/mL) at 40 °C for 40 min by using frequency planar divergent ultrasonic (FPDU) equipment (laboratory scale) to investigate the effect of mono frequency UAE (20, 40, 60 kHz) and sequential dual frequency UAE (20 + 40, 20 + 60 and 40 + 60 kHz) on total yield of TPC and TFC[24].

Total phenolic content (TPC)

As defined by[25], Folin-Ciocalteu method was used with few modifications to identify TPC. Briefly, diluted extracts (1:9; v/v) were mixed with Folin-Ciocalteu (10 %, v/v) followed by Na2CO3 solution (7.5 %, w/v). For reaction, mixture was incubated in the dark for 60 min and absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-1601, Shimadzu, Japan). Gallic acid was used as standard for calibration (Y = 0.0154x − 0.0177, R2 = 0.9941) and results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per gram dry weight of pomelo peel (mg GAE/g DW).

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

TFC was determined by using aluminum chloride colorimetric assay as described by[26]. Briefly, diluted extract (1:9; v/v) was mixed sequentially with NaNO2 solution (5 %), aluminum chloride solution (10 %) and NaOH solution (0.1 M). Then reaction mixture was adjusted to final volume with distilled water, incubated for 40 min and absorbance was recorded at 510 nm. Quercetin was used as calibration standard (Y = 0.0197x − 0.0109, R2 = 0.9972) and results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalents per gram dry weight of pomelo peel (mg QE/g DW).

Antioxidant activity assays

Antioxidant activity of the extracts was evaluated by using DPPH radical scavenging assay, ABTS radical cation decolorization assay and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay. These methods assess antioxidant potential through different reaction mechanisms involving either organic radical scavenging or metal ion reduction.

DPPH radical scavenging assay

This analysis was performed by following the process described by[27] with slight modifications. Diluted extract (1:9; v/v) was mixed with 0.1 mM DPPH ethanolic solution and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 520 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-1601, Shimadzu, Japan). Ascorbic acid was used as standard and antioxidant activity was expressed as mg ascorbic acid equivalents per gram dry weight (mg AAE/g DW)[28].

ABTS radical scavenging assay

This experiment was performed by following the process reported by [29]with slight changes. Firstly, ABTS reagent was prepared by mixing 7 mM ABTS plus 2.45 mM potassium persulfate (K2S2O8) solutions (1:1; v/v) then kept in dark for 16 h at room temperature to complete the reaction. After that the reagent was diluted with water to get the working solution of ABTS having absorbance 0.80 at 734 nm. For the assay, each diluted extract (1:9; v/v) with 2 ml of ABTS working solution was vortexed, incubate for 6 min, absorbance was taken at 734 nm and results were stated as mg AAE/g DW.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

FRAP was analyzed by using potassium ferricyanide method described by[30]. Diluted extracts (1:9; v/v) were mixed with phosphate buffer solution (0.2 M, pH 6.6) plus potassium ferrocyanide solution (1 %; w/v) followed by incubation at 50 °C for 20 min. After that TCA solution (10 %, w/v) was added and vortexed well. Then, each vortex solution was mixed with distilled water in separate test tube, FeCl3 solution (0.1 %; w/v) was added and absorbance was taken at 700 nm. Ascorbic acid was used for calibration (Y = 0.0008x − 0.003, R2 = 0.995) and results were expressed as mg ascorbic acid equivalents per gram dry weight (mg AAE/g DW).

HPLC-VWD analysis of major phenolic compounds

Major phenolic compounds present in pomelo peel extracts were analyzed by using an HPLC-VWD system (Agilent 1260 Infinity, Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with a Zorbax Eclipse C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). The chromatographic procedure was adapted from [[31], [32]] with minor modifications. Prior to analysis, selected extracts were filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane and injected into HPLC vials with the injection volume of 20 μL. Formic acid (0.1 %; v/v, solvent A) and acetonitrile (100 %, solvent B) were used as mobile phase in linear gradient method. The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C with the flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. Chromatographic detection was performed at 250 and 284 nm for monitoring phenolic compounds and flavonoids. Identification of compounds was based on the comparison of retention time with corresponding standards while for quantification calibration curves of corresponding standards were used.

FTIR analysis

FTIR analysis was carried out for optimal extracts of pomelo peels. 2 mg of each freeze-dried extract was placed on FTIR spectroscope sample holder and subjected to spectral analysis. FTIR spectra were analyzed with FTIR spectrophotometer (NICOLET iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) in a range from 550–4000 cm−1 with 4 cm−1 spectral resolution [33]. The IR spectra were reported in % transmittance and based on peak values functional groups were identified in comparison with the literature.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were conducted in triplicate and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using Statistics software (version 8.1), while RSM analysis was conducted using Design-Expert software (version 13.0, Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by least significant difference (LSD) test was applied to determine significant differences among means at p < 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate relationships between secondary metabolites and antioxidant activities. OriginPro 2024 software was used for FTIR spectral processing and graphical visualization.

ANOVA results exposed that most linear, interaction and quadratic terms significantly influenced TPC and TFC responses (p < 0.05), although a few non-significant effects were observed depending on extraction solvent and response variable. In particular, extraction time showed a non-significant effect on TFCE and TFCM responses, whereas solid-to-liquid ratio showed a non-significant influence on TPCE and TFCE. Moreover, most interaction terms (A × B, B × C and A × C) significantly affected extraction efficiency, especially in methanolic and water extracts.

The developed quadratic regression models were statistically significant (p < 0.05), confirming their suitability for predicting optimal TFUAE conditions. The coefficients of determination (R2) ranged from 0.9345 to 0.9998, indicating the upright precision of model whereas adjusted coefficients (R2 adj.) ranged from 0.8503 to 0.9994 showed a well-meaning agreement among experimental plus predicted values. In addition, low coefficients of variation (CV < 10 %) further confirmed the precision and reproducibility of the developed models. Our findings are partially in agreement with those established by [[34], [35]]. In conclusion, the statistical analysis confirmed that the developed models were adequate and reliable for optimizing TFUAE parameters to maximize the recovery of TPC and TFC from PPs.

Effect of process factors

Effect of time

The influence of extraction time on TPC and TFC recovery under TFUAE conditions is illustrated in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3. Initially, increasing extraction time enhanced the recovery of phenolic and flavonoid compounds; however, a decline in extraction yield was observed when sonication time exceeded 40 min. During initial stage improved extraction efficiency attributed to intensified acoustic cavitation, enhanced solvent penetration and progressive disruption of pomelo peel cell structures, which collectively facilitated the release of intracellular phenolic compounds into extraction medium. Moreover, prolonged ultrasonication increases solvent–matrix contact time and promotes mass transfer of target compounds. Although, excessive extraction time negatively affected TPC and TFC recovery. This reduction associated with degradation and oxidation of thermolabile phenolic and flavonoid compounds caused by prolonged ultrasonic exposure and localized thermal effects generated during cavitation collapse. In addition, prolonged extraction reduces cavitation efficiency because of solvent evaporation and attenuation of ultrasonic energy transfer. Similar trends have been reported in previous TFUAE studies, where shorter extraction durations were found sufficient to achieve maximum recovery of bioactive compounds while minimizing degradation effects [[21], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]].

Effect of temperature

Extraction temperature significantly influenced the recovery of phenolic and flavonoid compounds during TFUAE, as shown in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3. Increasing temperature up to 40 °C positively affected TPC and TFC extraction efficiency. Moderate temperature elevation likely enhanced extraction performance by improving solvent diffusivity, increasing solubility of phenolic compounds, reducing solvent viscosity and accelerating mass transfer between plant tissues and extraction solvent. Furthermore, elevated temperature facilitates disruption of hydrogen bonding interactions between cell wall polysaccharides and bound phenolic compounds, thereby improving the release of intracellular metabolites. In ultrasonic-assisted systems, moderate heating also intensifies cavitation effects by improving bubble formation and collapse dynamics, which contributes to enhanced cell wall disruption and solvent accessibility. However, extraction yields declined when temperature exceeded 40 °C. Excessive temperatures reduce cavitation intensity because of increased vapor pressure inside cavitation bubbles, resulting in less violent bubble collapse. In addition, higher temperatures promote degradation of thermosensitive phenolic and flavonoid compounds, thereby reducing extraction efficiency. Similar temperature-dependent extraction behavior has been reported in previous ultrasound-assisted extraction studies involving plant bioactive compounds [[37], [41], [42]].

Effect of solid/liquid ratio

The solid-to-liquid ratio significantly affect extraction efficiency during TFUAE. As presented in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3, increasing solvent volume initially enhanced TPC and TFC recovery; however, further increase beyond the optimum ratio (1:40 g/mL) resulted in reduced extraction efficiency. The initial improvement in extraction yield accredited to enhanced concentration gradients between plant material and extraction solvent, which promote mass transfer and facilitate diffusion of phenolic and flavonoid compounds into the solvent phase. Higher solvent availability also improve contact between solvent molecules and plant particles, thereby increasing extraction efficiency. Conversely, excessive solvent volume negatively affects extraction performance. Higher solid-to-liquid ratios reduce ultrasonic energy density within the extraction medium and weaken cavitation effects. Moreover, excessive dilution increases diffusion distance between plant particles and solvent molecules, thereby limit mass transfer efficiency and reduce extraction yield. Similar observations have been reported in previous ultrasound-assisted extraction studies, where moderate solvent ratios provided superior extraction efficiency compared with excessive solvent volumes [[17], [43], [44]].

Optimum extraction conditions

Objective of the optimization process was to determine TFUAE conditions which maximize the recovery of secondary compounds. Based on the developed RSM models, the optimum extraction conditions for all solvent systems were determined as extraction temperature of 40 °C, extraction time of 40 min, and solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:40 g/mL. The experimental results obtained under optimized conditions showed good agreement with predicted values, confirm the adequacy and reliability of the developed RSM models. Similar optimization trends have been reported in previous studies investigating ultrasound-assisted extraction of plant secondary compounds[[36], [41], [44]]. Overall, the findings demonstrated that TFUAE is an effective green extraction strategy for maximizing secondary compounds recovery.

CSE produced the lowest TPC and TFC values, reaching only 64.84 ± 0.11 and 73.74 ± 0.33 mg GAE/g DW and mg QE/g DW, respectively. In contrast, TFUAE exhibit highest values, yielding 339.81 ± 0.21 mg GAE/g DW for TPC and 384.82 ± 0.41 mg QE/g DW for TFC, corresponding to approximately 4.24- and 4.22-fold higher compared with CSE. On the other hand, mono-frequency UAE improved extraction efficiency compared with CSE but remained lower than that of dual-frequency and tri-frequency systems. In term of dual-frequency UAE extraction efficacy was higher than CSE and MFUAE but lower than TFUAE as accomplished in (Fig. 4 A & B).

CSE yielded lowest values due to its reliance on passive diffusion and limited cell wall disruption although mono-frequency UAE significantly improve yields compared to CSE via acoustic cavitation, which disrupt cell walls and improve solvent dispersion. On the other hand, dual-frequency UAE further increase recovery by producing diverse cavitation bubbles sizes and collapse dynamics, leading more uniform cavitation and enhanced mass transfer whereas TFUAE leads to superior release of TPC and TFC. Sequential action of three frequencies forms a multi-scale cavitation condition and equally indorse localized cell rupture and macro-scale turbulence. This synergetic effect diminished cavitation dead zones, ensure more homogeneous energy dispersal and maximize solvent–solute interaction. Notably, TFUAE not only provide the highest recovery but also signify a sustainable and greener extraction approach as it save more energy and time, reduce solvent usage and evade degradation of thermal sensitive bio-actives. Similar extraction enhancement effects of multi-frequency ultrasound systems have been reported for purple eggplant peel, watermelon rind and Pingyin rose extracts[[18], [22], [45]].

Based on these findings, all pomelo peel cultivars were subsequently extracted under the optimized TFUAE conditions to evaluate the effects of cultivar type and extraction solvent on secondary compounds yield, composition and antioxidant potential.

Impact of cultivars on TPC and TFC

Cultivars exposed significant impact on both TPC and TFC as presented in (Fig. 5 A and B). The highest TPC was observed in CTP (375.91 ± 0.49 mg GAE/g DW), whereas the lowest value was recorded in RSP (264.05 ± 0.67 mg GAE/g DW). Similarly, TFC varied markedly among cultivars, with CP exhibiting the highest value (384.82 ± 0.37 mg QE/g DW) and RSP showing the lowest flavonoid content (126.62 ± 0.56 mg QE/g DW). Observed differences among cultivars accredited to genetic variability, geographical origin, environmental conditions, agronomic practices and differences in metabolic pathways involved in phenolic biosynthesis. Secondary compounds are strongly influenced by genotype-environment interactions, which regulate the accumulation of phenolic acids, flavonoids and other bioactive compounds in plant tissues. In citrus species, phenolic biosynthesis is particularly sensitive to climatic conditions, sunlight exposure, temperature, soil composition and water availability. Consequently, cultivars originating from different regions exhibit substantial variation in phytochemical composition despite belonging to the same species. Similar cultivar-dependent variations in phenolic and flavonoid contents have previously been reported for pomelo and other citrus fruits, supporting the conclusion that cultivar selection is an important determinant of phytochemical quality and potential biological activity [26].Fig. 5Evaluation of secondary compounds and antioxidant potential of pomelo peels extracts (mg/g DW); Total phenolic content (A), Total flavonoid content (B), DPPH (C), ABTS (D), FRAP (E).

Impact of cultivars on anti-oxidant potential

Antioxidant potential of pomelo peel extracts was evaluated by using three well-known assays; DPPH, ABTS and FRAP DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays (Fig. 5C-E), as it cannot be assessed by using only one method because of the intricate nature of phytochemical contents and its response mechanism dependency[46]. Therefore, a single assay cannot comprehensively describe the antioxidant potential of complex plant extracts. Among the investigated cultivars, GOP exhibited the highest DPPH radical scavenging activity (3921.22 ± 1.71 mg AAE/g DW), whereas CP showed the lowest activity (1432.22 ± 2.32 mg AAE/g DW). In contrast, ABTS scavenging activity was highest in RSP (7754.44 ± 1.76 mg AAE/g DW) and lowest in CP (798.89 ± 1.08 mg AAE/g DW). For FRAP, the highest reducing power was observed in CTP (3441.11 ± 12.01 mg AAE/g DW), while GHP exhibited the lowest value (1675.10 ± 9.92 mg AAE/g DW).

Differences among antioxidant assays likely reflect variations in the composition and structure of phytochemicals present in each cultivar. DPPH and ABTS assays primarily evaluate radical scavenging capacity, whereas FRAP measures electron-donating and reducing abilities. Since phenolic compounds differ in hydroxylation patterns, glycosylation, molecular size and redox properties thus individual compounds respond differently depending on the assay employed. Furthermore, antioxidant activity is often governed by synergistic interactions among multiple phytochemicals rather than by individual compounds alone. Therefore, cultivars exhibiting moderate phenolic contents may still demonstrate strong antioxidant activity because of favorable phytochemical composition and synergistic effects among constituents. Similar assay-dependent variations in antioxidant activity have been reported for different pomelo cultivars and other citrus fruits[47].

Influence of extraction solvents on TPC, TFC and AOP

Current study showed significance impact of extraction solvents on the recovery of total phenolic, flavonoid and antioxidant potential from pomelo peels (Fig. 6A). As compared with water, TPC extraction decreased by 66.79 % and 25.01 %, while TFC extraction decreased by 67.67 % and 73.36 % when ethanol and methanol were used, respectively. AOP also affected by extraction solvents as for DPPH and FRAP; water > methanol > ethanol whereas for ABTS water > ethanol > methanol order was observed. DPPH and FRAP potential reduced 5.79 % and 33.91 % by methanol, 46.37 % and 65.33 % by ethanol respectively whereas ABTS potential reduced 29.71 % by ethanol and 69.31 % by methanol as compared to water. In line with our findings, previous studies also reported variation among solvents extraction efficacy via different spectacles. Primarily, solvent polarity and viscosity play critical role in determining the extraction efficiency. Polarity index as well as viscosity of water (10.2, 0.89 cP), methanol (5.1, 0.60 cP) and ethanol (4.3, 1.20 cP) differ respectively. Consequently, water higher polarity with average viscosity favors extraction of citrus-derived phenolics and flavonoids compared with ethanol and methanol under the present extraction conditions. As phenolic compounds possess varying number of hydroxyl groups that strongly interact with polar solvents through hydrogen bonding whereas solvents with low viscosity have low density but high diffusivity and diffused effortlessly into the apertures of the plant material but with low polarity affect phytochemical recovery [48].

Secondly, it has been reported that water not only dissolve polar compounds but also act as inflaming medium for cells and infiltrates deeper than ethanol or methanol, intensify extraction proficiency and increase contact external area amongst plant matrix as well as solvent. Moreover, higher antioxidant activities observed in water extracts are consistent with their elevated TPC and TFC values, suggesting that phenolic compounds contribute substantially to AOP [49]. From practical perspective, water propose additional advantages because it is inexpensive, non-toxic, environmentally friendly and suitable for food and pharmaceutical applications [50]. Thus, the combination of water and TFUAE represents a promising green extraction strategy for sustainable valorization of pomelo peel waste.

Lastly in contrast to soluble phenolic compounds (traced in cell vacuoles), flavonoids along with insoluble polyphenols positioned in cell wall via binding to proteins plus polysaccharides through hydrophobic interactions and protein denaturation triggered by ethanol or methanol avert the dissolution of polyphenols [42]. Similar solvent-dependent extraction behavior has been reported for citrus by-products and other plant matrices, where highly polar solvents frequently achieved superior recovery of phenolic compounds and antioxidant activities [[49], [51]]. In conclusion, selection of solvent play key role in the extraction of secondary compounds depending on their impending application.

The predominance of quinic acid, catechin, rutin and coumaric acid suggests that these compounds contribute substantially to the overall phenolic composition and antioxidant potential of pomelo peels. Previous studies (Table 4) have demonstrated that quinic acid and chlorogenic acid possess strong antioxidant and protective biological activities, while catechin, rutin, quercetin and kaempferol are recognized for their radical scavenging, anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective properties. These findings demonstrate that pomelo peels represent a valuable source of diverse phenolic compounds and that cultivar selection plays a critical role in determining phytochemical quality.

Pearson's correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between phytochemical constituents and antioxidant activities of pomelo peel extracts (Fig. 6B). The results demonstrated that antioxidant activity was associated with specific phenolic compounds rather than with total phenolic or flavonoid contents alone.

DPPH radical scavenging activity showed significant positive correlation with quinic acid, rutin and coumaric acid whereas negative correlation was observed with gallic acid and cinnamic acid. ABTS activity exhibited positive correlation with rutin, coumaric acid, morin and quercetin, while negative correlation was observed for protocatechuic acid, TPC, TFC and cinnamic acid. In contrast, FRAP displayed strong positive correlations with ferulic acid, chlorogenic acid, kaempferol, quinic acid and catechin, whereas negative correlations were observed with cinnamic acid and protocatechuic acid.

The differing correlation patterns among antioxidant assays reflect their distinct reaction mechanisms. DPPH and ABTS primarily evaluate free radical scavenging capacity, whereas FRAP measures electron-donating and reducing abilities. Consequently, compounds possessing different hydroxylation patterns, conjugated structures and redox potentials contribute differently depending on the assay employed [[21], [37], [38], [39]]. The strong correlations observed for rutin, catechin, chlorogenic acid and quinic acid suggest that these compounds are major contributors to the antioxidant properties of pomelo peel extracts.

Remarkably, TPC and TFC were not consistently correlated with all antioxidant assays, indicating that antioxidant activity depends not only on total phenolic concentration but also on the composition, structure and synergistic interactions of individual compounds. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies investigating citrus phytochemicals and antioxidant mechanisms [[38], [62]]. Although the present study focused primarily on antioxidant properties, the biological relevance of PP extracts may extend beyond radical scavenging activity. Future studies should investigate the bioavailability and interaction of these compounds within biological systems to further elucidate their functional potential and support the development of value-added food and nutraceutical applications.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that sequential tri-frequency ultrasound-assisted extraction (TFUAE) is an effective and green approach for recovering bioactive secondary metabolites from pomelo (Citrus maxima) peels. Response surface methodology successfully optimized the extraction process, identifying 40 °C, 40 min and a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:40 (g/mL) as the optimum conditions. Among the tested solvents, water provided the highest extraction efficiency for phenolics, flavonoids and antioxidant activity. Comparative analysis confirmed that TFUAE outperformed conventional extraction as well as mono- and dual-frequency ultrasound systems, highlighting the advantages of sequential frequency switching in enhancing cavitation and mass transfer. Significant cultivar-dependent differences were observed in phytochemical composition and antioxidant potential. HPLC-VWD analysis identified thirteen major phenolic compounds, while correlation analysis revealed that individual phenolics contributed differently to antioxidant mechanisms. Overall, the findings indicate that TFUAE combined with water extraction offers a sustainable strategy for the valorization of pomelo peel waste and provides a promising source of natural bioactive compounds for potential applications in food, nutraceutical and functional product development. However, future studies should employ advanced metabolomic approaches (e.g., LC-MS/MS), investigate additional cultivars from diverse growing regions and evaluate the bioavailability and biological efficacy of the recovered compounds in cellular and in vivo systems.
