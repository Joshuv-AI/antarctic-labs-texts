An innovative hybrid imaging protocol for whole-body 18F-FDG-PET/MRI in cardio-oncology: a proof-of-concept study
Authors: Yaqiong Zhou, Hua Lin, Chuan Huang, Yuanwei Xu, Yangjie Li, Chunchao Xia, Qing Zhang, Yuchi Han, Xiaohong Ou, Yucheng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly identification of cardiac involvement in oncology patients is critical but challenging. To date, no validated protocol integrates dedicated cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) within routine whole-body (WB) 18F-FDG PET/MRI examinations.ObjectivesThis proof-of-concept study evaluated the feasibility and effectiveness of a novel hybrid imaging protocol combining CMR and WB 18F-FDG PET/MRI for oncology patients.MethodsFifteen patients with suspected or confirmed multiple myeloma (MM) were enrolled. All participants scheduled for oncologic 18F-FDG PET/MRI underwent an integrated protocol incorporating dedicated CMR sequences prior to WB PET/MRI. The CMR sequences included cine imaging, native T1 and T2 mapping, and feature-tracking to assess cardiac structure, function, and tissue characteristics. Feasibility, safety, and the prevalence of clinically relevant cardiac abnormalities were evaluated.ResultsThe protocol was successfully completed in all participants without adverse events, enabling comprehensive cardiac and oncologic assessment within an average procedure time of 115 min (cumulative scanning time 65.5 ± 9.8 min). Clinically relevant cardiac abnormalities were identified in 4 patients (27%), including reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, concentric hypertrophy, and increased myocardial 18F-FDG uptake.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the feasibility of a novel, time-efficient, integrated 18F-FDG PET/MRI protocol combining oncologic staging with comprehensive cardiac assessment in a single examination. This approach serves as an ideal strategy for cardio-oncology, bridging the gap between cancer treatment and cardiac care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40959-025-00377-8.Keywords: Cardio-oncology, PET/MRI, Cardiac magnetic resonance, Cardiac PET, Hybrid imaging, Workflow

Introduction

Novel therapeutic options have significantly improved survival and long-term outcomes in various cancers [1]. Unfortunately, these advancements often bring new challenges, particularly concerning cardiovascular toxicity related to the malignancy and its associated therapies [2–4]. In this context, early detection of cardiotoxicity is crucial for implementing timely and effective treatment strategies, highlighting the urgent need for more sensitive cardiac imaging techniques.

While echocardiography is the first-line imaging approach for assessing cardiac abnormalities [5, 6], it is not sensitive for detecting early cardiac involvement and is unable to identify early changes in myocardial tissue characteristics [7]. Additionally, echocardiography could be limited with low spatial resolution, poor acoustic windows, and operator-dependent processing [8].Longitudinal strain derived from speckle-tracking echocardiography suffers from reproducibility with scanner and operator dependency [9, 10]. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is a non-invasive, high-resolution imaging modality that provides comprehensive insights into cardiac structure, function, and myocardial histology [11, 12]. Furthermore, the advent of feature-tracking CMR technology enables precise assessment of myocardial motion and deformation, facilitating the early detection of myocardial injury by evaluating myocardial strain capacity [13].

Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) labeled with fluorine-18, when combined with positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), offers a robust nuclear medicine diagnostic approach that is increasingly utilized for staging, restaging, and therapeutic monitoring of various cancers [14, 15]. The application of cardiac 18F-FDG PET imaging offers critical insights into cardiac health in patients with cancer. Myocytes uptake 18F-FDG through glucose transporters, and variations in its uptake can indicate underlying inflammation, metabolic disturbances, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which may occur prior to contractile dysfunction [16, 17]. Increased 18F-FDG uptake in myocardial tissue signifies ongoing inflammation and metabolic abnormality, facilitating early intervention to minimize cardiac damage. Thus, this imaging technique acts as a potential biomarker for cardiotoxicity associated with cancer or its treatments, enabling clinicians to identify high-risk patients and implement proactive management strategies.

Combining 18F-FDG imaging with CMR presents a promising opportunity to leverage the complementary strengths of both modalities [18, 19]. While 18F-FDG PET imaging effectively detects myocardial inflammation and abnormal metabolism, CMR offers high-resolution imaging of cardiac structures and tissue characteristics. Theoretically, integrating these two modalities can encompass the full spectrum of pathological changes and functional alterations, significantly enhancing the diagnosis and management of cardiotoxicity. An important advantage of this approach is that whole body (WB) 18F-FDG PET/MRI is already routinely used in oncologic patients to detect cancer and monitor treatment responses through the degree of 18F-FDG uptake by tumors and metastases [20]. Evaluation of the heart can be accomplished from an already scheduled 18F-FDG PET/MRI scan without exposing the patient to additional radiation or take additional time.

However, despite these advantages, no validated clinical workflow currently exists that integrates dedicated CMR sequences within routine WB PET/MRI examinations. As a result, comprehensive assessment of both cancer burden and subclinical cardiac involvement typically requires multiple imaging studies, increasing patient burden, cost, and logistical complexity, and potentially delaying diagnosis of cardiotoxicity.

This study hypothesizes that integrating a CMR scanning module into the routine WB 18F-FDG PET/MRI in oncologic patients can establish an effective integrated imaging protocol (HuaXi Protocol) for the early detection, assessment, and monitoring of cardiac abnormalities before, during, and after cancer treatment. This proof-of-concept study aims to evaluate the feasibility and efficiency of the HuaXi Protocol in oncological patients, providing a comprehensive approach for future research.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This prospective study was approved by the Review Board of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval Number: 2023. NO. 2414). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The study consecutively recruited patients suspected or confirmed to have multiple myeloma (MM) who were scheduled for routine 18F-FDG PET/MRI scans from August 2024 to October 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: adults aged 18 years and older, patients suspected or confirmed to have MM based on the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) consensus [21], patients already scheduled for a routine 18F-FDG PET/MRI scan for oncological assessment, and patients who provided written informed consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria included: patients with severe comorbid conditions that could confound the assessment of cardiotoxicity, such as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, severe renal impairment, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); patients with contraindications to MRI, such as implanted pacemakers, defibrillators, or other non-MRI-compatible devices; pregnant or breastfeeding women; patients with severe claustrophobia that cannot be managed with mild sedation; and patients unable to comply with the imaging protocol due to cognitive impairment or other reasons.

Patient preparation

An essential aspect of imaging inflammation and metabolism is strict dietary preparation, which suppresses physiological cardiomyocyte uptake of 18F-FDG [22, 23]. This ensures that tracer uptake is limited to active inflammatory cells and those associated with abnormal metabolism in the myocardium. Patients should begin this preparation approximately 72 h before the test, consuming a high-fat, low-carbohydrate ketogenic diet during the daytime and fasting overnight for 3d, as described in the literature [24]. If patients had diabetes, their blood glucose level should be under 11.1 mmol/L, and insulin was withheld for 6 h prior to the study. PET/MRI was rescheduled if these criteria were not met [25].

HuaXi protocol for PET/MRI

All patients underwent a WB 18F-FDG PET/MRI examination using a 3.0T PET/MRI system (SIGNA, GE Healthcare, USA). Full-body coverage (from vertex to mid-thigh) was obtained through a nine-station acquisition of contiguous body regions using a head coil, neck coil, two anterior surface coils on the thorax and abdomen, and table-embedded posterior coils.

The HuaXi imaging protocol consists of two main components: CMR scan followed by simultaneous WB PET/MRI. This imaging protocol was designed to ensure the basic requirements of WB PET/MRI imaging without extending the total procedure time. Patients received an intravenous injection of 18F-FDG (0.1 mCi/Kg) manufactured in accordance with standard methods using the 18F-FDG synthesis module. After a 45-minute tracer uptake period, the CMR scan was performed.

The specific protocol for CMR included the following components:

Location imaging: Localizers, pilot views for two-, three-, and four-chamber assessments, a three-slice short-axis stack, and a transverse dark blood single-shot fast spin echo stack for anatomical evaluation (3 min).

Overall, the CMR took approximately 15–20 min. Following the CMR imaging, simultaneous WB PET and WB MRI imaging (from the top of the head down to under the knees or through to the feet, with arms along the body) was performed approximately 60–70 min post-tracer injection. PET data were reconstructed using the 3D ordinary Poisson ordered-subset expectation maximization provided by the manufacturer with 2 iterations, 28 subsets, and Gaussian post-smoothing of 5 mm full width at half maximum (voxel size: 3.1 × 3.1 × 2.8 mm³). The WB-MRI imaging protocol included, as recommended by the international guidelines [26]:

WB 3D Gradient Echo T1-weighted magnetic resonance attenuation correction (AC)-Dixon with controlled aliasing in parallel imaging for PET reconstruction and generating fat fraction maps.

WB 3D Gradient Echo T1-weighted magnetic resonance attenuation correction (AC)-Dixon with controlled aliasing in parallel imaging for PET reconstruction and generating fat fraction maps.

WB axial 3D T1 LAVA-Flex (slice thickness 4 mm, FOV 400 mm, matrix 256 × 180, TR/TE: 5.0/Minimum ms) and T2 weighted (slice thickness 6 mm, FOV 400 mm, matrix 320 × 256, TR/TE: 4000/90 ms).

WB axial 3D T1 LAVA-Flex (slice thickness 4 mm, FOV 400 mm, matrix 256 × 180, TR/TE: 5.0/Minimum ms) and T2 weighted (slice thickness 6 mm, FOV 400 mm, matrix 320 × 256, TR/TE: 4000/90 ms).

WB axial diffusion-weighted (DWI) with two b values (0 and 800 s/mm2) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) calculation and 3D-MIP reconstructions of highest b-value images, WB axial T2 weighted (6-mm contiguous slices, FOV 400 mm, matrix 128 × 128, TR/TE: 6250/Minimum ms).

WB axial diffusion-weighted (DWI) with two b values (0 and 800 s/mm2) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) calculation and 3D-MIP reconstructions of highest b-value images, WB axial T2 weighted (6-mm contiguous slices, FOV 400 mm, matrix 128 × 128, TR/TE: 6250/Minimum ms).

The acquisition time for simultaneous WB PET/MRI ranged from 40 to 55 min. Overall, the total procedure time was approximately 110 to 130 min, with a total scanning time of 60 to 80 min. This protocol met the basic requirements of WB PET/MRI imaging without extending the total procedure time, thereby maintaining efficiency and cost-effectiveness. To establish scanner-specific reference ranges for native T1 and T2 mapping as well as for global longitudinal strain (GLS) and global circumferential strain (GCS), we prospectively enrolled 14 age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers without known cardiovascular disease or malignancy. All controls underwent CMR on the same PET/MRI system using identical acquisition sequences and post-processing methods. Reference ranges for T1, T2, GLS, and GCS values were defined as the mean ± 2 standard deviations (SD) derived from these controls.

Image processing and analysis

Two nuclear medicine physicians and two radiologists, each with 3 and 6 years of board-certified experience respectively, independently reviewed the CMR and PET images. Blinded to all identifying information, they utilized the fusion software available on the GE Healthcare workstation. Any discrepancies encountered during the analysis were resolved through collaborative discussion. Initially, myocardial segments exhibiting increased radiotracer uptake compared to the left ventricular (LV) blood pool, were identified and analyzed according to the American Heart Association (AHA) 17-segment model. Three-dimensional regions of interest for each myocardial segment were meticulously delineated on short-axis fusion images. The software automatically selected a threshold of 40–50% of the maximum standardized uptake value (SUV) to delineate the volume of interest (VOI) for the myocardial regions exhibiting heightened uptake [27]. Key parameters, including SUVmax, SUVmean, and the volume of SUV uptake, were automatically generated for further analysis. Additionally, the tissue-to-blood ratio (TBR), defined as the ratio of myocardial wall SUVmax to LV blood pool SUVmean, was systematically recorded [27].

Post-processing of the CMR images was performed using the commercially available Medis Suite 3.2 software (Medis, Leiden, the Netherlands), adhering to the guidelines set forth by the Society of Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance [28]. We quantified biventricular volume, LV diameter, and LV mass (LVM) by manually delineating the endocardial and epicardial myocardial contours on consecutive short-axis cine images from the base to the apex. This process facilitated the calculation of biventricular ejection fraction (EF). Additionally, myocardial global longitudinal strain (GLS) and global circumferential strain (GCS) were obtained through CMR feature tracking. Moreover, native T1 mapping and T2 mapping values were measured on the same mid-ventricular short-axis slice by delineating the endocardial and epicardial myocardial contours.

WB PET/MRI images analysis was performed by the aforementioned nuclear medicine physicians by viewing the images in the trans axial, coronal, and sagittal planes.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD if normally distributed, or as median with interquartile range (IQR) if not normally distributed. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic information

The study included 15 patients and 14 controls. Groups were comparable in sex distribution (60.0% vs. 57.1% female), age (64.1 ± 11.2 vs. 61.5 ± 12.8 years; P = 0.56), and BMI (23.2 ± 3.2 vs. 24.0 ± 2.9 kg/m²; P = 0.47). Patients had slightly higher blood pressure and heart rate, though differences were not significant. Comorbidities were present only in the patient group. In terms of diagnosis, the majority (60%) were diagnosed with MM, followed by 26.7% with monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), 13.3% with lymphoma and 6.7% with solitary plasmacytoma. The PET/MRI scans were primarily performed for initial assessment or staging (73.3%), with 20% for recurrence detection and 6.7% for evaluating therapy response.

Among the 15 patients included in the study, six patients with MM had received prior chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy before PET/MRI imaging. The remaining nine patients (60.0%) were treatment-naïve at the time of imaging, including all three patients with MGUS (managed with observation alone), one patient with solitary plasmacytoma, two patients with lymphoma, and three patients with MM who had not yet initiated systemic therapy. For these untreated MM patients, first-line treatment plans included proteasome inhibitors and/or immunomodulatory agents in accordance with institutional protocols and clinical staging. In terms of disease stage, 10 patients (66.7%) were classified as having advanced-stage disease (Durie-Salmon stage III for MM a or Ann Arbor stage III–IV for lymphoma), while 5 patients (33.3%) had early-stage disease. This distribution reflects the heterogeneous but clinically representative population encountered in real-world cardio-oncology practice. Cardiac findings of 18F-FDG PET

Three patients exhibited 18F-FDG uptake in the myocardium. Two patients exhibited diffuse uptake across all 17 segments of the LV, with one of them also showing focal uptake in the right ventricle (RV) (Fig. 2). The volume of SUV uptake was 246 mL and 256 mL, with corresponding SUVmax values of 8.84 and 7.56, SUVmean values of 5.41 and 5.15, and TBR values of 4.2 and 3.59, respectively. Another patient showed focal uptake in the anteroseptal septum and LV lateral wall, with 82.3 mL uptake, SUVmax of 4.7, SUVmean of 2.2, and TBR of 2.1 (Fig. 3). In the remaining 12 patients, quantitative analysis revealed no significant 18F-FDG uptake in the myocardium, with an SUVmax of 1.7 (range: 1.2–2.5), SUVmean of 1.4 (range: 1.0-2.2), and TBR of 0.95 (range: 0.87–1.48), as shown in Table 3.

Cardiac findings of CMR

CMR measurements showed no statistically significant differences between patients and controls across key volumetric and functional indices. LV end-diastolic volume index (LVEDVi) was comparable between groups (84.3 ± 19.4 vs. 87.4 ± 6.7 mL/m², P = 0.58), as were LV end-systolic volume index (LVESVi), LV mass index (LVMi), and LV ejection fraction (LVEF). Mean LVEF in patients was 57.6 ± 11.3%, with two patients (13.3%) having LVEF < 50%. RV parameters, including RVEDVi, RVESVi, and RVEF, also showed no significant differences, although a trend toward lower RVEF was observed in patients (60.9 ± 12.8% vs. 55.2 ± 5.8%; P = 0.14). Parametric mapping demonstrated slightly higher native T1 and T2 values in patients (1248.8 ± 85.9 ms and 50.9 ± 4.8 ms, respectively) compared with controls (1216.9 ± 47.2 ms and 48.7 ± 3.2 ms), though differences were not statistically significant. Using thresholds defined as the mean plus two SD of controls (1311.3 ms for T1 and 55.1 ms for T2), 3 patients (20.0%) were found to have elevated T1 values, and 3 patients (13.3%) demonstrated increased T2 values.

Incidental cardiac findings were noted in four cases, deemed significant compared to existing clinical knowledge. One patient demonstrated severely reduced LV function (LVEF 33%) and LV dilation (LV end-diastolic diameter 60.5 mm), with elevated native T1 (1428 ms) and T2 (57.2 ms) values exceeding established thresholds. This patient also exhibited diffuse 18F-FDG uptake in the LV and focal uptake in the RV (Fig. 2). After evaluation by a cardiologist, this patient received an angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI), a beta-blocker, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), and diuretics. Another patient showed marked LV hypertrophy (LV wall thickness 17.5 mm) and diffuse FDG uptake in the LV, with elevated T1 (1325 ms) and normal T2 (53.0 ms) values. A third patient had moderately reduced LVEF (46%) with normal T1 (1280 ms) and T2 (52.0 ms), while a fourth had isolated RV dysfunction (RVEF 44%) and normal mapping values without FDG uptake. Notably, in two patients—one with elevated T1 values and the other with elevated T2 values—no structural or functional cardiac abnormalities were detected on CMR.

Discussion

This proof-of-concept study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and potential of incorporating cardiac imaging into standard an oncological 18F-FDG PET/MRI protocol. Our findings suggest that the HuaXi protocol for PET/MRI is a promising method for assessing cardiac health in this vulnerable population, offering insights into both oncological and cardiovascular conditions through a one-stop examination, without additional financial/time burden or radiation exposure.

The HuaXi protocol streamlined the imaging workflow by combining dedicated CMR and WB PET/MRI into a single session with an average total duration of approximately 115 min, which is comparable to standard PET/MRI protocols reported in the literature [29]. The examination was well tolerated by all participants, with no adverse events, underscoring its feasibility and safety in clinical practice. Unlike most prior hybrid imaging approaches that focus exclusively on either cardiac or oncologic assessment, this protocol enables simultaneous evaluation of both domains. For example, dedicated cardiac PET/MRI protocols have demonstrated utility in detecting myocardial inflammation in conditions such as sarcoidosis and myocarditis but do not include oncologic staging [30]. Conversely, workflows that combine PET/CT for tumor assessment with separate CMR examinations provide complementary information but require multiple appointments, increasing logistical complexity and patient burden. Our approach addresses these limitations and has the potential to improve the early identification of cardiotoxicity in oncology care. Furthermore, careful pre-imaging preparation was essential to suppress physiological myocardial 18F-FDG uptake and enhance the specificity of metabolic assessment, reflecting established strategies from prior cardiac PET protocols [31, 32].

Among the patients who underwent the Huaxi PET/MRI protocol, some unexpectedly exhibited cardiac abnormalities. This demonstrates the significant potential of the Huaxi protocol for identifying clinically relevant cardiac abnormalities. In our cohort, nearly all participants were referred from the Hematology Outpatient Department of our hospital, and the majority were patients with MM. This focus was intentional, as MM carries a high prevalence of subclinical cardiac involvement—subclinical amyloid deposits affecting the myocardium occur in up to 30% of cases and can lead to progressive heart failure and other serious complications. Consequently, this study aimed to assess the feasibility, safety, and workflow integration of the HuaXi Protocol in a well-defined high-risk group as a representative proof-of-concept setting. Prior to PET/MRI scan, none of the patients had undergone any cardiac imaging evaluations, including echocardiography or CMR. This lack of prior assessment may explain the relatively high proportion of incidental cardiac anomalies observed in our study. Among these patients, CMR directly impacted clinical management for two individuals: one had significant contractile dysfunction, while the other presented with notable LV hypertrophy. The latter patient was suspected of having cardiac amyloidosis and subsequently underwent gadolinium-enhanced CMR.

The applications of the HuaXi PET/MRI protocol extend beyond merely identifying and monitoring cardiac damage; they also provide insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying cardiac injury. Any cardiac insult may induce inflammation, which is required to commence healing. The inflammatory response may be evident shortly after cardiac injury. Inflammatory leukocytes demonstrate elevated metabolic activity characterized by increased glucose utilization [17]. 18F-FDG is taken up avidly by metabolically active macrophages [33]. Consequently, the recruitment of these cells as part of the inflammatory response leads to increased accumulation of 18F-FDG, as visualized by PET [34]. Moreover, as energy production is essential to cardiac function, a reduction in β-oxidation may induce a compensatory increase in glycolysis that can be visualized non-invasively by 18F-FDG PET [35]. In our study, three patients exhibited 18F-18 FDG uptake in the myocardium. Among these patients, two showed significant functional or structural abnormalities, while the third showed focal uptake without notable functional or structural issues. After a careful evaluation of the CMR imaging, it was found that this patient had relatively high T2 mapping values (57.1 ms). Other possibilities to consider include whether the phenomenon of increased myocardial 18F-FDG uptake is transient and whether it serves merely as a cardioprotective mechanism, which might reverse once cell damage is resolved. Therefore, prospective and long-term studies are essential to confirm a potential association between increased myocardial 18F-FDG signals and subsequent changes in cardiac structure, function, and myocardial histology.

Clinical implications and future perspectives

This study has important implications for the clinical management of oncological patients. First, the HuaXi protocol for PET/MRI offers a promising approach for the comprehensive assessment of both oncological and cardiovascular conditions through a single, streamlined examination, without imposing additional testing on patients. All protocols and sequences used in this study can be directly shared with interested researchers, enhancing its impact and repeatability. Second, although establishing a universal molecular imaging biomarker for simultaneous oncologic and cardiotoxic assessments is challenging due to the diversity of pathological processes, various molecular imaging biomarkers present promising avenues for personalized approaches. The HuaXi protocol offers insights that allow for the design of integrated PET/MRI protocols tailored to the characteristics of PET-derived molecular imaging biomarkers specific to different tumors [36].

Limitation

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it is important to note that our study cohort was relatively small and primarily consisted of patients with MM. This homogeneity of the population was intentional, as the initial goal of this proof-of-concept investigation was to establish the feasibility and safety of the HuaXi Protocol in a defined clinical context. However, we recognize that this limits the generalizability of our findings to broader oncologic populations, particularly patients with solid tumors who may exhibit different patterns of cardiotoxicity and metabolic activity. For example, anthracycline-based chemotherapy and targeted therapies used in breast and lung cancer may have distinct cardiac effects compared to those commonly administered in hematologic malignancies [37]. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these preliminary results to other cancer types. Future research should prioritize the inclusion of patients with a wide spectrum of malignancies to better characterize the diagnostic performance, reproducibility, and clinical impact of this integrated imaging approach across diverse oncologic settings. Second, the lack of a head-to-head comparison with standard imaging protocols restricts our ability to definitively establish the superiority of the HuaXi PET/MRI protocol over existing methods. Furthermore, while the study effectively assessed cardiac health, it did not include long-term follow-up data to evaluate the clinical implications of incidental findings on outcomes. Third, the HuaXi PET/MRI protocol only includes non-contrast CMR, which is less sensitive for detecting myocardial abnormalities like fibrosis and amyloidosis, and in some cases, contrast-enhanced CMR may still be necessary.

Conclusion

In summary, this proof-of-concept study demonstrated the feasibility of integrating CMR into routine oncological 18F-FDG PET/MRI protocols for detecting cardiac abnormalities in oncology patients. The HuaXi protocol provides comprehensive and non-invasive oncological and cardiac assessments. It has the potential to identify early cardiac damage in cancer patients without increasing scanning time, financial burden, or radiation exposure. Further clinical validation and application are needed to fully establish its utility in routine practice.
