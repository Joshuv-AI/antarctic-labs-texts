The Relationship Between Levels of Greenery and Perceived Clinical Signs of Non‐Communicable Diseases in Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Yao Kouassi Kan Théodore, Wandan Eboua Narcisse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImportanceUrban green spaces may reduce the prevalence of non‐communicable disease (NCD) symptoms by mitigating environmental risk factors such as pollution and stress. However, evidence from African cities remains limited.ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between levels of urban greenery and the perceived prevalence of clinical signs of non‐communicable diseases in selected municipalities of Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire.Design, Setting, and ParticipantsThis cross‐sectional study was conducted from 2018 to 2021 in five municipalities of Abidjan, selected to reflect different socioeconomic strata. A total of 359 adult residents were randomly selected using a two‐stage sampling method. Vegetation levels were assessed using Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), derived satellite imagery, and survey data were collected on self‐reported symptoms, demographics, and behavioral factors.ExposureResidential greenery levels, categorized into low (< 6%), medium (6–15%), and high (15–40%) vegetation cover based on NDVI classification at the municipal level.Main Outcomes and MeasuresThe primary outcomes were the frequencies of self‐reported clinical signs associated with five NCD categories: cardiovascular, respiratory, musculoskeletal, psychological, and digestive. Associations were assessed using multivariable linear regression models adjusted for sociodemographic, behavioral, and dietary confounders.ResultsModerate greenery (6–15%) was associated with a lower prevalence of cardiovascular (β = –0.59; 95% CI: –1.27 to 0.09; p = 0.091) and respiratory (β = –0.62; 95% CI: –1.27 to 0.02; p = 0.057) symptoms, although not reaching conventional statistical significance. No significant association was found between greenery and musculoskeletal, psychological, or digestive symptoms. Male gender was significantly associated with fewer cardiovascular, respiratory, and psychological symptoms. Alcohol consumption was positively associated with musculoskeletal, psychological, and digestive symptoms.Conclusions and RelevanceThese findings suggest that moderate residential greenery may be associated with a reduced prevalence of certain NCD symptoms, particularly cardiovascular and respiratory, in a sub‐Saharan African context. Urban greening policies could offer a cost‐effective strategy to improve public health in rapidly urbanizing cities such as Abidjan.

Introduction

Rapid urbanization is one of the primary health challenges of our time, especially in developing countries where spatial transformations often lack accompanying sustainable development policies [1]. In sub‐Saharan Africa, this urban growth has led to increased land artificialization, heightened air pollution, and the fragmentation of green spaces [2]. Abidjan, the economic capital of Côte d'Ivoire, exemplifies this trend: over the past few decades, the city has undergone significant densification, resulting in land pressure that has marginalized green spaces, which are vital for enhancing urban life quality [3].

Simultaneously, the burden of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) is rapidly increasing in low‐ and middle‐income countries. According to the World Health Organization [4], NCDs—such as cardiovascular diseases, chronic respiratory diseases, diabetes, and certain musculoskeletal disorders—account for over 41 million deaths annually worldwide, with more than 77% occurring in developing nations. In Africa, this epidemiological transition presents a dual vulnerability: populations are exposed to traditional risk factors for NCDs (such as unbalanced diets, sedentary lifestyles, stress, and pollution) while lacking equitable access to protective environments like green spaces [5].

In urban settings, an increasing number of studies have demonstrated that the presence and quality of green spaces correlate with improvements in various health indicators. Urban greenery is associated with lower stress levels [6], reduced depressive symptoms [7], lower blood pressure [8], and enhanced overall cardiovascular health [9]. The mechanisms at play are diverse: thermal regulation [10], improved air quality [11], reduced urban noise [12], stimulation of physical activity [13], and psychological restoration [14, 15].

However, most of this research has been conducted in high‐income countries, and limited data are available for large African cities [16]. In Côte d'Ivoire, very few studies have empirically explored the relationship between urban greening and health indicators [17]. In a context like Abidjan, where access to urban infrastructure is marked by significant inequalities, it is essential to examine how greenery may influence clinical signs associated with non‐communicable diseases (NCDs), such as shortness of breath, chest pain, headaches, or sleep disturbances.

This study aims to address this gap by analyzing the association between the level of greenery and the prevalence of perceived clinical signs of NCDs across several municipalities in Abidjan. It employs a mixed‐methods approach that combines environmental, sociodemographic, and clinical data collected through surveys. The primary objective was to test the hypothesis that a more vegetated urban environment is linked to a reduction in the frequency of certain symptoms indicative of chronic pathologies. This research seeks to make a significant contribution to the literature on urban health in Africa, emphasizing the importance of green infrastructure as a means of health prevention.

Methodology

Geographic Area

The choice to use the municipal scale as the spatial unit of analysis was based on methodological considerations aimed at minimizing bias related to individual mobility. Although buffer zones around residential addresses are commonly used to estimate environmental exposure, they are subject to the issue of uncertain geographic [18, 19]. This bias arises because individuals often do not spend most of their daytime hours at home, but rather in activity spaces such as schools or workplaces, making residential exposure less representative.

The municipal scale, as a stable administrative unit, better reflects the daily activity spaces of Abidjan residents. It also helps reduce the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP) and limits spatio‐temporal uncertainty. This choice ensures consistency with available socioeconomic and health data, which are typically collected at the municipal level.

To strengthen the validity of this approach, individuals who had lived in the survey area for less than 1 year or who traveled frequently outside their municipality were excluded. This exclusion criterion helped reduce potential bias related to short‐term residence or high external mobility, ensuring that the measured exposure to greenery was more stable and representative of participants' daily environments.

Sampling Procedure

The sampling strategy adopted in this study aimed to ensure both socioeconomic representativeness and methodological rigor. It was implemented in three stages: classification of municipalities by living standards, selection of households within these municipalities, and selection of individual respondents within households.

Classification of Municipalities by Living Standards

The twelve municipalities of Abidjan were first classified into three socioeconomic strata low (NVF), medium (NVM), and high (NVE) based on data from the 2013 National Household Survey conducted by the National Institute of Statistics (INS). This classification combined several indicators, including household income, electricity expenditure, housing characteristics (type and tenure), and ownership of a personal vehicle. Using principal component analysis (PCA), municipalities were grouped according to their socioeconomic profiles. Peripheral municipalities such as Anyama and Songon, characterized by higher poverty indices, represented the low‐income category, whereas Plateau, with the lowest poverty index, represented the high‐income category. This stratification ensured that the municipalities selected for the survey reflected the full spectrum of living conditions within Abidjan's urban fabric [20]. (Table 1).

Household Selection

Within each selected municipality, households were selected using a two‐stage stratified random sampling design consistent with INS procedures. The sampling frame was constructed from census district data, where each district comprises approximately 150 to 350 households. A systematic random selection was then applied to ensure proportional representation across different socioeconomic and spatial contexts—from densely populated informal settlements to more affluent residential areas. This design strengthened the internal validity of the study while reducing potential sampling bias.

Individual Selection

Within each selected household, one adult respondent was chosen using the Kish method, as adapted by the International Labour Organization. This technique ensures random and unbiased selection of individuals within households, thereby minimizing interviewer bias and ensuring demographic representativeness. Eligible participants were aged between 16 and 65 years and had resided in the municipality for at least 1 year. Individuals who traveled frequently outside their municipality or had recently moved into the area were excluded to minimize exposure misclassification and strengthen the ecological validity of the analysis.

This three‐tiered sampling approach ensured that the final sample of 359 individuals was representative of Abidjan's urban population across socioeconomic, spatial, and demographic dimensions, while maintaining consistency with national statistical standards and minimizing potential selection bias.

Data Collection Techniques

Greenery Extraction Using GIS

The level of greenness was assessed by differentiating it from other ground objects using the NDVI (Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI). This index is widely employed to highlight vegetation cover in studies examining the relationship between vegetation and health [8]. NDVI values range from −1 to +1, where negative values indicate surfaces that are not covered by vegetation, characterized by higher reflectance in the red spectrum compared to the near‐infrared spectrum. Vegetation cover typically has NDVI values between 0.1 and 0.7, with higher values indicating denser vegetation.

The sentinel images from 2018 were utilized for this analysis, as images from 2019 to 2021 were compromised by cloud cover. The process of discriminating vegetation cover was conducted in two phases: a pre‐processing phase involving atmospheric corrections on the source image (Sentinel 2018) using the semi‐automatic extension of QGIS, followed by the calculation of the NDVI to generate an NDVI image. The second phase involved classification, during which the NDVI image was extracted at the municipal scale. The NDVI image was then binarized using the thresholding method, with a threshold set at 0.2. This threshold was specifically chosen to effectively differentiate the lagoons that surround certain municipalities. The lagoons exhibit algal cover, which complicates the distinction between vegetation cover and lagoon areas. The binarized image was subsequently polygonised to extract the vegetation cover using the GDAL algorithm, which includes a “polygonised raster to vector” extension (Figures 1 and 2).

Extraction chain of the vegetation cover of the municipalities. Source satellite images acquired in 2018 were first subjected to atmospheric correction before calculating the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI). Household Survey

This survey was conducted after obtaining the necessary ethical approval, as well as the informed consent of the heads of the households involved (code of ethics, consent). It involved collecting data through self‐reported declarations regarding sociodemographic, behavioral, food hygiene, and noncommunicable disease (NCD) factors. The number of individuals to be interviewed was determined using the Cochran formula for a population proportion vulnerable to NCDs, which is 37% [21, 22]. This resulted in a sample size of 359 individuals distributed across the selected municipalities, considering the population size in these areas. The developed questionnaire comprised four sections, including sociodemographic information that collected basic details about the respondents, such as their age, sex, education level, and professional activity. The clinical signs of NCDs were reported by the respondents themselves.

One individual per household was selected using the Kish method [23], as modified by the International Labour Organization [24]. The Kish sheet (or Kish grid) is a standardized selection table developed by Leslie Kish (1949) that ensures the random selection of one respondent among all eligible household members. Each member of the household is assigned a number according to age and gender, and the table indicates which person to interview based on the household's serial number. This method guarantees an unbiased and equal probability of participation for all eligible individuals. Efforts to minimize selection bias included the use of stratified random sampling across municipalities, taking into account different socioeconomic strata. A consent letter, along with the Kish sheet and the questionnaire, was provided to the heads of households. For those who consented and encountered difficulties during selection or questionnaire administration, interviews were conducted face‐to‐face using the ODK Collect tool (Sheet, Questionnaire).

Non‐Communicable Disease Survey

Data collection focused on the clinical signs associated with the five main categories of non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) selected for this study. Participants were asked to indicate the presence or absence of clinical signs that they experienced frequently in their daily lives and that could potentially require medical consultation. The selection of symptoms was based on a review of the scientific literature to ensure the validity and relevance of the assessment. The final questionnaire was developed from the literature‐derived symptom list and subsequently adapted to improve comprehension and facilitate field data collection. The disease categories included cardiovascular diseases (CVD), respiratory diseases (RSD), musculoskeletal disorders (MSK), psychological disorders (MPS), and digestive diseases (DGI). Confounding Factors

The survey collected a range of variables categorized into sociodemographic, behavioral, and hygiene‐related factors. Sociodemographic information included sex, age, and professional activity. Respondents were asked to specify their professional status by selecting from nine predefined categories: self‐employed, employed in the private or public sector, volunteer, student, retired, unemployed, disabled, or homemaker. These categories were designed to reflect the diversity of occupational situations in the Ivorian urban context, including informal and non‐salaried roles [25]

Behavioral factors focused on daily habits with potential health implications. Participants were asked whether they smoked daily, including cigarettes, pipes, or other substances. For alcohol consumption, they were asked whether they drank alcohol daily and, if so, to indicate the frequency using five categories: daily, 5–6 days a week, 1–4 days a week, 1–3 days a month, or less than once a month. They also reported the average number of drinks consumed per day. These indicators were selected based on their relevance to urban health risk profiles in West African cities [26].

Hygiene‐related factors included dietary habits, particularly the consumption of fruit and vegetable sauces, which are common in local diets. Respondents were asked whether they consumed these items and to specify the number of times per week. This variable served as a proxy for nutritional quality and access to fresh produce, which may vary across socioeconomic strata and influence vulnerability to non‐communicable diseases [27]

Together, these variables were used to adjust for potential confounding effects in the analysis of the relationship between greenery exposure and health outcomes, ensuring that both global standards and local realities were taken into account [28].

Data Analysis

Greenness Level

The greenness level corresponds to the proportion of vegetation cover observed at the municipal scale. The boundaries of the municipalities were extracted from a BNETD map, georeferenced using a geographic information system (GIS). This proportion was calculated as the ratio of green surface area to total land use area. The variable obtained, initially a continuous numerical value, was then transformed into a discrete variable with three categories using the K‐means discretization method. This method, which belongs to unsupervised classification techniques, aims to group observations into a defined number of classes (or clusters) to minimize the variance within each group while maximizing the differences between the groups. In other words, it allows municipalities with similar greenness levels to be grouped together to facilitate comparative analysis. The defined categories are as follows: [0; 6[%, corresponding to low vegetation cover; [6; 15[%, indicating medium vegetation cover; and [15; 40%, representing high vegetation cover.

Non‐Communicable Diseases (NCDs)

NCDs were determined based on the presence of clinical signs of the five selected non‐communicable diseases within the population. Each clinical sign was coded in a binary manner (Presence = 1; Absence = 0). NCDs were thus considered categorical variables, with their values obtained by summing the clinical signs associated with each disease.

Confounding Factors

Gender was considered a binary categorical variable with “Male” coded as 1 and 0 for female, while age was treated as a discrete variable with three categories: [16; 34[, [34; 49[, and [49; 65]. Professional activity was classified as an ordinal categorical variable: “lowly occupied,” “less occupied,” and “highly occupied,” with scores ranging from 1 to 3, respectively. The discretization of these variables was performed using the K‐means method. Respondents who were unemployed, retired, disabled, or homemakers were classified as lowly occupied individuals. Students (including primary or secondary school students, university students, or those in vocational training) were considered less occupied, as they benefit from alternating school or university periods with holidays and vacations. Volunteers, self‐employed workers, and employees in the private or public sector were categorized as busy individuals whose leave cannot exceed more than 1 month per year.

For behavioral factors, the variable “Tobacco consumption” was categorized into two groups: “smoker” and “non‐smoker,” coded as 0 for non‐smokers and 1 for smokers. Individuals who began smoking less than 30 days ago were classified as non‐smokers. Alcohol consumption levels were binarized into 0 for non‐consumers and 1 for consumers, with specific codes assigned based on frequency: daily = 5, 5–6 days per week = 4, 1–4 days per week = 3, 1–3 days per month = 2, less than once a month = 1, and do not drink = 0. This categorization was implemented due to the abnormal distribution of the data. Regarding dietary hygiene factors, the variable “fruit consumption,” which accounts for the number of fruits consumed per week, was treated as a continuous variable. Similarly, the variable “vegetable consumption,” representing the number of vegetable servings consumed in a week, was also considered a continuous variable. For interviewees who did not consume fruits or vegetables, a value of 0 was assigned. For those who could not provide details on their consumption, vegetable servings per week were treated as missing variables.

Statistical Analysis

In this study, a preliminary spatial descriptive analysis was conducted to standardize the different variables collected at the municipal level. Each indicator was converted into relative frequencies (percentages) to enable direct comparison between municipalities with unequal population sizes. This transformation concerned three groups of variables: (1) the prevalence of non‐communicable disease (NCD) symptoms, (2) the frequency of risk behaviors such as smoking or alcohol consumption, and (3) food hygiene practices including fruit and vegetable intake. These standardized indicators were then used as input variables in subsequent statistical modeling to assess their relationship with greenness level and other confounding factors.

For NCDs, the frequency was calculated by dividing the total number of self‐reported clinical signs in each municipality by the product of the number of individuals surveyed and the number of clinical signs considered for each condition. This method allows the data to be weighted according to the sample size and the symptomatic specificity of the pathologies, thus ensuring better comparability of the results across different spatial units.

Regarding behavioral and hygiene factors, frequencies were calculated by dividing the number of individuals reporting a given behavior by the total number of individuals surveyed in the municipality, then multiplying the result by 100. This approach quantifies the prevalence of the studied behaviors and practices at the local level and highlights spatial disparities that may guide public health actions.

Furthermore, an analysis of absolute numbers was also conducted for all other factors studied. This analysis aims to provide a better understanding of the raw distribution of responses in each municipality, in addition to the frequency, to account for the population volumes involved and to enhance the descriptive robustness of the results.

To assess the effect of greenness level on the perceived prevalence of clinical signs associated with non‐communicable diseases (NCDs), multiple linear regression analysis was performed. This approach allowed us to model the association between the dependent variable (NCDs) and greenness level while accounting for potential confounders. Confounding was addressed through multivariable regression analysis incorporating sociodemographic and behavioral covariates, including risk behaviors and dietary habits. The selection of covariates included in the multivariable models was based on theoretical relevance and evidence from the literature regarding their role as potential confounders in the relationship between greenery and non‐communicable disease outcomes. No preselection of variables based on univariable p‐values was performed, in line with recommendations to avoid unstable model selection procedures [29]. The model estimated adjusted β coefficients, reflecting the average effect of each explanatory variable on the prevalence of NCD symptoms, independent of the influence of other variables. The level of greenery, the primary variable of interest, was introduced into the model in categorical form, with the category corresponding to the lowest vegetation cover ([0; 6[%) used as the reference group.

The results are presented as β coefficients, accompanied by their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) and corresponding p‐values. A statistical significance level of 5% (p < 0.05) was retained. The spatial description of the data was conducted using QGIS, and the effect of the greenness level on the perceived prevalence of clinical signs associated with non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) was analyzed using R software.

Results

Sociodemographic Distribution of Data

The study population exhibited a balanced distribution across the main sociodemographic characteristics. Women were slightly overrepresented (57%) compared with men (43%), reflecting the demographic structure typically observed in urban areas of Abidjan. The age distribution was dominated by young adults aged 16–34 years (61%), followed by the 34–49 years (25%) and 49–65 years (14%) groups. This predominance of the working‐age population provides a suitable framework for analyzing behavioral and environmental determinants of non‐communicable diseases (NCDs).

Employment status (ACPF) indicated that the majority of participants were classified as “highly active” (60%), while “low active” and “less active” individuals accounted for 21% and 20%, respectively. Inter‐municipal variations showed a high concentration of highly active individuals in Plateau (75%), reflecting its administrative and economic role within the district, whereas Yopougon presented a more heterogeneous occupational structure.

Behavioral and Food Hygiene Factors

Risk behaviors were generally low within the population. Tobacco (6%) and alcohol consumption (9%) remained limited, with minor variations between municipalities. However, dietary patterns revealed an overall insufficient intake of fruits and vegetables compared with nutritional recommendations. On average, fruit consumption was reported at 3.5 ± 4.1 days per week and vegetable consumption at 2.1 ± 1.9 days per week. The lowest levels were observed in Marcory and Treichville, possibly due to economic constraints or reduced accessibility to fresh produce. These findings indicate a nutritional imbalance that may heighten vulnerability to NCDs.

Distribution of Clinical Signs of NCDs

Analysis of perceived clinical signs revealed marked differences among municipalities. Cardiovascular (CVD = 1.27 ± 2.26) and respiratory (RSD = 1.25 ± 2.18) symptoms showed the highest mean frequencies, particularly in Adjamé and Yopougon—areas characterized by high urban density and contrasting environmental conditions. Musculoskeletal (MSD = 0.64 ± 1.41), psychological (MPS = 0.57 ± 1.22), and digestive (DGI = 0.46 ± 0.98) symptoms were less frequent overall, suggesting heterogeneity in the perceived prevalence of different NCD categories. These inter‐municipal variations likely reflect the combined influence of environmental, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors on self‐reported health outcomes.

Environmental Factors: Level of Greenness

Exposure to urban greenery varied significantly across municipalities. Approximately 42% of respondents lived in areas with intermediate vegetation cover (6–15%), 29% in low‐greenery areas (0–6%), and 29% in highly vegetated areas (15–40%). Adjamé, Treichville, and Marcory were characterized by low vegetation cover, whereas Plateau and Yopougon accounted for most respondents living in moderately or highly green environments. This spatial heterogeneity reflects differences in urban planning and access to green spaces within Abidjan, which may influence the health benefits associated with environmental exposure. Effect of Clinical Signs of NCDs on Greenness Level

Effect of Greenery on the Prevalence of Clinical Signs of CVD

The results revealed an inverse association between residential greenery and the clinical signs of cardiovascular disease (CVD). Compared with the reference group (0–6% greenery around the home), participants exposed to an intermediate level of greenery (6–15%) had a β coefficient of –0.59 (95% CI: –1.27 to 0.09; p = 0.0911), indicating a marginal and non‐significant trend toward fewer cardiovascular symptoms. The highest exposure category (15–40%) also showed a negative but non‐significant association (β = –0.47; 95% CI: –1.23 to 0.28; p = 0.2164).

Among the control variables, sex was significantly associated with clinical signs of CVD, with men exhibiting a lower prevalence of symptoms than women (β = –1.00; 95% CI: –1.60 to –0.39; p = 0.0013). Other variables, including tobacco use, alcohol consumption, fruit and vegetable intake, age, and occupational activity (ACPF status), were not significantly associated with CVD symptoms. Overall, these findings suggest a marginal, non‐significant inverse trend between greenery exposure and cardiovascular symptoms (Figure 3).

Effect of Greenery on the Prevalence of Clinical Signs of RSD

A similar marginal, non‐significant inverse association was observed for respiratory diseases (RSD). Compared to the reference group (exposure < 6%), participants living in areas with intermediate greenery (6–15%) had a β coefficient of –0.62 (95% CI: –1.27 to 0.02; p = 0.057), suggesting a possible but inconclusive trend toward fewer respiratory symptoms. For the highest exposure class (15–40%), the relationship remained negative but not significant (β = –0.36; 95% CI: –1.07 to 0.35; p = 0.323).

Male gender was associated with a significantly lower prevalence of respiratory symptoms (β = –0.80; 95% CI: –1.37 to –0.23; p = 0.006), while other variables such as health behaviors, diet, age, and occupational activity were not significantly related to RSD. These results indicate that moderate greenery exposure may suggest a reduction in respiratory symptoms, though the evidence remains statistically inconclusive (Figure 4).

Effect of Greenery on the Prevalence of Clinical Signs of MSK

No statistically significant relationship was found between greenery and musculoskeletal symptoms (MSK). Compared to the reference category (0–6% greenery), the coefficients for the 6–15% (β = –0.27; 95% CI: –0.71 to 0.16; p = 0.216) and 15–40% (β = –0.34; 95% CI: –0.82 to 0.14; p = 0.163) categories were not significant.

Significant associations were instead observed for behavioral variables. Smoking (β = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.03 to 1.60; p = 0.041) and alcohol consumption (β = 0.47; 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.89; p = 0.024) were both linked to higher musculoskeletal symptoms, whereas vegetable intake showed a marginal and weakly positive association (β = 0.10; 95% CI: –0.00 to 0.20; p = 0.050). No significant effects were found for sex, age, or occupational activity. These results highlight the predominant influence of behavioral factors over environmental exposure to greenery in explaining musculoskeletal symptoms (Figure 5).

Effect of Greenery on the Prevalence of Clinical Signs of MPS

No significant association was identified between greenery and psychological symptoms (MPS). Compared to the reference category (0–6%), both intermediate (6–15%; β = –0.05; 95% CI: −0.42 to 0.32; p = 0.784) and high (15–40%; β = –0.03; 95% CI: −0.39 to 0.33; p = 0.703) greenery exposures were non‐significant.

However, alcohol consumption (β = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.72; p = 0.0438) and intermediate age (34–49 years; β = 0.50; 95% CI: 0.12 to 0.88; p = 0.0101) were significantly associated with higher psychological symptoms, while male gender showed a protective effect (β = –0.53; 95% CI: –0.86 to –0.20; p = 0.0018). These findings suggest that sociodemographic and behavioral variables play a more substantial role than environmental greenery in explaining psychological outcomes (Figure 6).

Effect of Greenery on the Prevalence of Clinical Signs of DGI

The association between greenery and digestive symptoms (DGI) was marginal and non‐significant. Compared with the reference group (0–6%), participants exposed to an intermediate level of greenery (6–15%) had β = –0.26 (95% CI: –0.53 to 0.01; p = 0.0625), suggesting a weak, non‐significant inverse trend. The highest exposure level (15–40%) also showed a non‐significant result (β = –0.15; 95% CI: –0.42 to 0.15; p = 0.3256).

Among the control variables, alcohol consumption (β = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.08 to 0.60; p = 0.0098) and male gender (β = –0.34; 95% CI: –0.58 to –0.10; p = 0.0054) were significantly associated with digestive symptoms, consistent with established evidence linking these factors to gastrointestinal function. No significant effects were found for age, fruit and vegetable intake, or occupational activity (Figure 7).

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate a marginal and non‐significant trend toward an association between higher residential greenery and a lower prevalence of clinical signs of certain non‐communicable diseases (NCDs), particularly cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and respiratory diseases (RSD). Although the observed relationships did not reach statistical significance, they suggest possible protective effects of greenery that warrant further investigation. Conversely, no significant associations were found for musculoskeletal (MSK), psychological (MPS), or digestive (DGI) symptoms, suggesting that the influence of greenery may vary depending on the physiological systems involved.

Greenery and Cardiovascular Diseases (CVD)

The results show a marginal, non‐significant inverse relationship between residential greenery and clinical signs of CVD, consistent with numerous studies suggesting a beneficial cardiovascular effect of exposure to green spaces. A meta‐analysis of 50 studies reported a significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality among individuals residing in greener environments [30]. Similarly, a large cohort study conducted in South Korea by Lee et al. (2020) [31] found that an increase in NDVI around the home was associated with a 15% reduction in the risk of hospitalization for cardiovascular diseases.

The underlying mechanisms often proposed include reduced exposure to air pollutants [32], stress mitigation through restoration effects [33], and increased opportunities for physical activity and social interaction [34]. Although the associations observed in our study were not statistically significant, their direction aligns with the biological plausibility and existing evidence supporting the cardiovascular benefits of greenery.

Greenery and Respiratory Diseases (RSD)

The marginal, non‐significant inverse trend observed between greenery and respiratory symptoms is consistent with previous research suggesting that green environments may mitigate respiratory risks. A systematic review reported that proximity to vegetation was associated with lower risks of chronic respiratory symptoms and mortality [35]. Moreover, a study in Spain found a lower prevalence of asthma among children living in greener neighborhoods [36].

The potential explanations include the role of vegetation in improving air quality and reducing ambient concentrations of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) [37]. However, the lack of statistical significance in our results indicates that these effects might be subtle, context‐dependent, or influenced by unmeasured confounding variables such as local pollution sources, plant allergen exposure, or socioeconomic disparities.

Greenery and Mental Health (MPS)

The relationship between greenery and psychological symptoms in our study was weak and non‐significant, reflecting the heterogeneity often reported in the literature. Some meta‐analyses have demonstrated a significant reduction in depression and anxiety in populations exposed to greener environments [6, 7], while others have found mixed or null results [38]. For example, a U.S. cohort study observed a decrease in antidepressant prescription rates with increasing residential greenery [9].

These inconsistencies may stem from contextual differences in social cohesion, cultural perception of nature, and accessibility of green spaces. Although our findings do not confirm a protective association, they remain consistent with the idea that contact with nature may contribute to psychological well‐being through stress reduction and restorative effects.

Greenery and Musculoskeletal (MSK) and Digestive (DGI) Diseases

No statistically significant associations were observed between greenery and musculoskeletal or digestive symptoms. However, exploratory evidence in other studies suggests that access to natural environments may promote mobility, reduce sedentary behavior, and indirectly mitigate musculoskeletal discomfort, particularly among older adults [13].

For digestive symptoms, a marginal, non‐significant inverse trend was observed for moderate greenery exposure (6–15%), echoing emerging hypotheses on the “biodiversity hypothesis.” This theory suggests that exposure to diverse environmental microbiota in green spaces may beneficially influence gut microbiota composition and reduce inflammation [39]. Nevertheless, these mechanisms remain speculative and require further investigation in larger, longitudinal studies.

Mechanistic Hypotheses and Public Health Implications

Several biological and behavioral pathways could underlie the observed trends. Green environments have been associated with lower allostatic load through reductions in cortisol levels and improvements in heart rate variability [40]. They may also improve air quality, reduce exposure to urban heat, and promote physical activity—all of which are known protective factors against CVD and RSD [11, 41].

Moreover, contact with environmental microorganisms may modulate immune function and inflammatory pathways, potentially contributing to resilience against certain NCDs [39].

From a public health perspective, even marginal or non‐significant trends may have meaningful implications, particularly when integrated into long‐term urban planning. The incorporation of vegetation into residential areas should thus be viewed not only as an aesthetic or recreational asset but also as a potential determinant of population health. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longitudinal designs are needed to confirm these findings and clarify the causal pathways linking greenery exposure to various NCD outcomes.

Although the observed associations are consistent with previous literature, they should be interpreted with caution because the statistical evidence remained marginal. Consequently, the policy implications of these findings should be considered exploratory and require confirmation through studies using individual‐level exposure measures and longitudinal designs.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships between exposure to greenery and the prevalence of non‐communicable disease (NCD) symptoms. The observed associations reflect correlations at a single point in time and may be influenced by unmeasured variables. Second, the data on clinical signs were self‐reported, which may introduce recall or reporting bias. Participants' perception and reporting of symptoms may vary depending on health literacy or individual sensitivity.

Another important limitation concerns the assessment of greenery exposure. The level of greenery was estimated using NDVI‐derived vegetation cover at the municipality level rather than at the individual residential level. Consequently, variations in exposure within municipalities could not be captured, and some degree of exposure misclassification may have occurred. Future studies should use finer spatial scales and individual‐level measures of greenness exposure to improve the precision of the estimated associations.

In addition, the categorization of clinical symptoms by summation may oversimplify the diversity of individual symptom profiles. This approach was chosen for analytical simplicity and comparability with similar exploratory studies, but future research could use clustering or binary classification methods to validate these associations in larger datasets.

The observed associations reflect correlations at a single point in time and may be influenced by unmeasured variables. In particular, the use of medication was not included among the control variables, even though it can influence the manifestation or perception of clinical symptoms. This limitation may have introduced a residual confounding effect, especially for chronic conditions that require regular pharmacological treatment. Future research should therefore explicitly account for medication use in order to better isolate the effects of environmental exposures.

Furthermore, although NDVI is a widely used and objective proxy for vegetation cover, it does not capture actual use, accessibility, or quality of green spaces—all of which can significantly influence health outcomes. Residual confounding may also exist, despite statistical adjustments, particularly regarding health behaviors that are difficult to measure precisely. However, it is important to note that no distortion in the sample size or selection bias related to individual characteristics was observed, thanks to the use of stratified random sampling and a balanced sociodemographic distribution. Finally, the generalizability of the findings may be limited to the urban context of Abidjan, and caution should be exercised when extrapolating to other regions.

Conclusion

This study suggests that residential exposure to moderate levels of urban greenery may be associated with a lower prevalence of cardiovascular and respiratory symptoms. However, these associations remained marginal and did not reach conventional statistical significance, and therefore should be interpreted with caution. No evidence of significant associations was observed for musculoskeletal, psychological, or digestive symptoms, while several behavioral and sociodemographic factors were significantly related to the reported clinical signs of NCDs.

The use of geospatial tools combined with community‐based surveys provided useful insights into potential spatial inequalities in environmental exposure and health outcomes. Although the observed trends are consistent with findings reported in previous studies, their implications for public health and urban planning should be considered exploratory and require confirmation through larger studies using individual‐level measures of greenery exposure and longitudinal designs.
