Feasibility and safety of short-duration aerobic training in non-hematological cancer patients hospitalized for chemotherapy treatment
Authors: William de Lima Selles, Leticia Jeremias Ferreira Pereira, Giovanni Marini Moura, Beatriz Marques Lima, Diego Wisnieski da Silva, Adriana Claudia Lunardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt is unclear whether moderate-intensity aerobic exercise is feasible and safe for inpatients with non-hematological cancer receiving one cycle of chemotherapy.ObjectivesTo evaluate adherence, compliance, acceptability, adverse events, and clinical stability during a short-term moderate-intensity aerobic training in non-hematological inpatients undergoing one cycle of chemotherapy.MethodsThis feasibility and safety study included adults with non-hematological cancer inpatients receiving one cycle of chemotherapy. Patients participated in four consecutive days of moderate-intensity aerobic training using a cycle ergometer, during which vital signs and perceived exertion were monitored. Following the intervention, patients were assessed for the acceptability of intervention. Adherence rates, compliance, and adverse events related to the training were also recorded.ResultsTwenty patients (mean age 61 ± 15 years, mean BMI 25 ± 8 kg/m2; 75% with digestive cancers, 50% physically active) were analyzed. Adherence to the intervention was 92%, compliance was 68.6%, and acceptability was reported at 100%. Vital signs and perceived exertion remained stable throughout all training sessions (heart rate P = .99, peripheral O2 saturation P = .54, mean arterial blood pressure P = .79, sensation of dyspnea P = .97). Two patients reported adverse events during training (vomiting and extreme fatigue), while three experienced adverse effects post-training (tremor, nausea, and back pain).ConclusionsShort-term moderate-intensity aerobic training for inpatients with non-hematological cancer undergoing one cycle of chemotherapy is feasible and safe.

Short-term moderate-intensity aerobic exercise has been demonstrated to be both safe (with no significant variations in vital signs and minimal occurrence of adverse events) and feasible (with high acceptability and adherence rates) for hospitalized patients undergoing one cycle of chemotherapy. Moreover, patients in this population not only accept but also report feeling better after participating in daily exercise sessions.

Introduction

Cancer poses a significant challenge to global public health, and Brazil is no exception, with an estimated 704000 new cases projected annually for the 2023-2025 period, excluding non-melanoma skin cancer.1 Chemotherapy remains one of the primary therapeutic approaches to combat cancer; it is a systemic treatment designed to disrupt cell proliferation by targeting the DNA, RNA, and metabolism of cancer cells.2 In the United States, cancer treatment costs have soared to approximately $158 billion annually,3 while in Brazil, these expenses exceed R$3.5 billion.4 These funds are allocated to various needs, including healthcare teams, medications used directly and indirectly in treatment, medical equipment, and hospitalization days.

Chemotherapy is associated with a range of side effects, such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and febrile neutropenia, which often necessitate additional medications for relief. These adverse effects not only significantly diminish patients’ quality of life but also negatively impact adherence to prescribed treatment regimens, leading to increased hospitalization rates.5 Consequently, physical exercise has been investigated as a non-pharmacological intervention to alleviate chemotherapy’s adverse effects.5,6 Studies have shown that exercise can enhance physical function,7–12 reduce fatigue,6–8,10,12 relieve symptoms,5,9,12 and improve the overall quality of life for cancer patients.7,8,10,12

Previous randomized studies on exercise in cancer patients have consistently demonstrated that structured exercise during chemotherapy is feasible in outpatient settings, with reported adherence rates ranging from 70% to 82%.5,10,12,13 The OptiTrain study reported adherence rates of approximately 75%-82% for supervised exercise sessions in women diagnosed with breast cancer who planned to receive adjuvant chemotherapy, with a low incidence of exercise-related adverse events. Similarly, Courneya et al. also reported adherence rates between 72% and 82% in different exercise modalities in multicenter outpatient studies.5 The PACES study demonstrated 71% adherence for home-based low- and moderate- to high-intensity exercise programs administered to patients with primary breast or colon cancer scheduled to undergo adjuvant chemotherapy.12 Despite the positive results reported, all these studies were conducted in outpatient populations and did not specifically investigate the feasibility or safety of exercise interventions. There is no evidence regarding adherence, compliance, safety, and feasibility of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise for hospitalized patients during chemotherapy cycles.

Hospitalized patients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy represent a particularly underexplored population in exercise oncology research. In contrast to outpatient settings, inpatient chemotherapy is characterized by short and variable lengths of stay, frequent medical procedures, acute treatment-related symptoms, and greater clinical instability. These factors may substantially influence the feasibility, safety, and implementation of exercise interventions and limit the generalizability of findings from ambulatory trials.14,15

Therefore, our objective was to evaluate adherence, compliance, acceptability, adverse events, and fluctuations in vital signs during short-term aerobic training in adult inpatients during a one cycle of chemotherapy.

Methods

Study design

This is a feasibility and safety study of a randomized controlled trial with 2 parallel groups, 1:1 allocation and blinded assessment, approved by the ethics committee (CAAE: 50436221.7.3001.5463) (ReBEC RBR-6b4zwx3).16 The study was conducted in the oncology ward of the Instituto de Assistência Médica ao Servidor Público Estadual, São Paulo-SP, Brazil, between April 2023 and April 2024 and followed the CONSORT checklist extension for pilot and feasibility trials (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials extension to randomized pilot and feasibility trials).16 For this phase of feasibility and safety test of the short-term moderate-intensity aerobic exercise protocol, only patients allocated to the group that received aerobic training were analyzed. The control group from the RCT received only a primer with guidelines.

Eligibility criteria

Adult patients diagnosed with cancer and hospitalized for at least one cycle of chemotherapy. Patients without musculoskeletal, cognitive, or clinical limitations that could interfere with the proposed assessments and interventions, such as heart disease (moderate to severe aortic or pulmonary stenosis, decompensated heart failure, advanced cardiac arrhythmias, myocarditis, or unstable coronary insufficiency, for example), lung disease (decompensated chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and/or asthma, for example), decompensated diabetes mellitus and systemic arterial hypertension, and serum hemoglobin <8 g/dL or hematocrit <25% or platelets <30 000 mm5,17–19 were included in this study.

Patients who required transfer to the intensive care unit or for any reason had chemotherapy treatment contraindicated or discontinued would be excluded from the study.

Procedures

First, patients hospitalized in the Oncology Ward who, after screening, were considered eligible to participate in the study were informed about the objectives of the study and asked to sign the informed consent form. Then, patients were evaluated regarding anthropometric and clinical characteristics. After the initial evaluation, inpatients performed aerobic exercise sessions and received a booklet with guidelines on how to remain active during hospitalization.20 Then, the aerobic training was initiated and performed for four consecutive days because this is the most usual time interval for each chemotherapy cycle in this ward. Every day, before performing the daily aerobic exercise session, patients were asked about the perception of any discomfort related to the previous day’s training (such as muscle or joint pain in the lower limbs), and vital signs were recorded. If for some reason the patient had chemotherapy interrupted, the exercises were also interrupted, and as soon as chemotherapy treatment was resumed, the exercises were also resumed. Patients were reassessed on the last day of the chemotherapy cycle regarding the occurrence, frequency, intensity, and discomfort of the proposed intervention. During and after the study intervention period, patients who needed it would receive routine physiotherapy care provided by the physiotherapy team of the Institute of Medical Assistance to State Public Servants (IAMSPE-SP)21.

Assessments

Clinical and anthropometric assessment

A standardized printed form was used to collect the following information: personal data (name, age, sex, place of birth, contact information, education level, monthly household income, marital status, and occupation) and anthropometric data (height and weight measured according to the recommendations of the National Institute of Health, Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute)22; current disease diagnosis; pre-existing conditions; smoking history (current or past); alcohol use history; current medications; previous chemotherapy or radiotherapy; purpose of current chemotherapy (neoadjuvant, adjuvant, primary treatment, or palliative); and whether the patient currently engages or has previously engaged in physical activity, including the frequency of such activity.

Feasibility outcomes

Acceptability

refers to patients’ perception of the adequacy of the intervention in alleviating their problem, in a way that is reasonable, appropriate, and convenient for its application in real life. At the end of treatment, acceptability was evaluated using the Weiner scale,23 which was translated and adapted for Brazilian Portuguese.24 Response options for all questions were as follows: “strongly disagree” (1 point), “disagree” (2 points), “neither agree nor disagree” (3 points), “agree” (4 points), and “strongly agree” (5 points).

Acceptability refers to stakeholders’ perception of whether a given treatment is agreeable or satisfactory. This was assessed through the patient’s responses to the following statements: (1) “I approve of physical exercise during chemotherapy treatment”; (2) “Physical exercise during chemotherapy treatment is appealing to me”; (3) “I enjoyed physical exercise during chemotherapy treatment”; and (4) “I would recommend physical exercise during chemotherapy treatment.” The data were presented as absolute numbers for each response and as the percentage for each classification, from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (5 points) for all questions (1 to 4). Acceptability was considered adequate if at least 80% of total responses were either “agree” (4 points) or “strongly agree” (5 points).

Adherence

Refers to the extent to which participants attend or complete prescribed exercise sessions. It was calculated based on the percentage of individuals who completed the training (four sessions) among those who started the intervention. Adherence was considered adequate if at least 80% of eligible patients adhered to aerobic training and if 80% or more of the patients remained until the end of the training protocol.

Compliance

Reflects how faithfully participants follow the prescribed exercise dose, including intensity, duration, and modality. It was analyzed by comparing the prescribed exercise intensity with physiological responses during training sessions, using heart rate monitoring (percentage of heart rate reserve calculated by the Karvonen formula) and perceived exertion recorded with the modified Borg scale during cycle ergometer exercise.

Safety outcomes

Clinical stability

Patients’ heart rate, peripheral oxygen saturation, systemic blood pressure, sensation of dyspnea, and physical fatigue were daily monitored before, during, and after the exercise. A significant variation in vital signs (an increase in heart rate greater than 30%, a drop in peripheral oxygen saturation by more than 4 points, an increase in systolic or diastolic blood pressure by more than 2 points, and a sensation of dyspnea or physical fatigue greater than 8 points) was considered indicative of clinical instability.

Adverse effects of aerobic exercise

Patients were asked daily before and during all training sessions about the occurrence of symptoms such as nausea, dizziness, muscle pain, fatigue, or vomiting. The adverse effects were classified as mild if no intervention was necessary to maintain clinical stability, as moderate if minimal and noninvasive intervention was indicated, and as severe if prolongation of hospitalization or critical care was indicated.22

Intervention

All patients analyzed for this study participated in an aerobic training protocol using a cycle ergometer during their hospitalization undergoing one cycle of chemotherapy. Training was performed in one daily session lasting 40 min for 4 consecutive days or for the duration of the chemotherapy cycle. Exercise using the cycle ergometer began with a 5-minute warm-up and progressed to 30 min of exercise at a speed corresponding to 40%-60% of the heart rate reserve calculated by the Karvonen formula25 [(220 − age) – (resting heart rate * % intensity) + resting heart rate], ending with 5 min of recovery. The objective was to maintain the patient’s score of “5 to 6” in the perception of dyspnea/physical fatigue measured by the modified Borg scale26 (a score that means that the patient’s dyspnea/physical fatigue is above “moderate effort” and below “very intense effort”) during exercise on the cycle ergometer. The modified Borg scale presents a range of scores from 0 (no perception of exertion) to 10 (extreme exertion).26

Thus, when the patient was considered fit by the physiotherapist, the speed on the cycle ergometer was gradually increased until the patient reached 60% of the heart rate reserve. The patients had their heart rate and peripheral oxygen saturation (Palmsat 2500 pulse oximeter, NONIN), dyspnea and physical fatigue (Borg scale), and blood pressure (Premium aneroid sphygmomanometer and Littmann Classic II stethoscope) recorded before, during, and after each session of the physical training protocol. The session could be interrupted at any time if the patient presented signs or symptoms, such as dizziness, dyspnea, fatigue, or nausea. If the patient needed any emergency care during the period in which he was undergoing physical training supervised by the study physiotherapist, the same physiotherapist would contact the on-call medical team responsible for the patient in the oncology ward to provide the necessary care to stabilize the patient.

Statistical analysis

For the sample size estimation, considering 9% of the total sample size from the RCT as recommended by the international literature for pilot studies, 80% power, and an acceptable error of 5%, the estimated size was 5 participants. However, since 4 outcomes were analyzed, 20 subjects were included for this analysis.27

For the analysis of outcomes, descriptive analysis was used to describe acceptability, adherence, compliance, and possible adverse events. For the analysis of vital signs, after descriptive analysis and the Shapiro-Wilk normality test with visual analysis, one-way ANOVA was performed. For all these analyses, IBM SPSS software, version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York), was used.

Results

Forty-five eligible patients were invited to participate in the study between April 2023 and April 2024. Seven patients declined participation (four due to extreme fatigue, two due to generalized pain, and one without providing a reason). Of the 38 patients included in the main study, 18 were randomized to the control group and were not considered in this feasibility and safety study (Figure 1). Therefore, 20 patients were included in the present feasibility and safety evaluation of a clinical trial. Five (25%) patients underwent their first cycle of chemotherapy, 10 (50%) underwent previous surgical cancer treatment, and 3 patients were on their last chemotherapy cycle. Although the study employed a randomized design, it was conducted as a feasibility trial. Therefore, the analyses were not powered nor intended to compare outcomes between groups. The primary focus was to assess feasibility outcomes, including adherence, compliance, safety, and acceptability of the intervention.

The average hospitalization duration was 6.58 ± 3.57 days, and during the follow-up period, no patient required transfer to the intensive care unit.

During the intervention period, adherence was 92%, which is considered adequate, with only one patient failing to complete all days of the intervention due to back pain (withdrawal on the second day of the intervention). Regarding acceptability, 100% of the participants approved of physical exercise during chemotherapy treatment. The questions received the following mean scores: (1) “I approve of physical exercise during chemotherapy treatment” = 4.5 ± 0.52 points; (2) “Physical exercise during chemotherapy treatment is appealing to me” = 4.75 ± 0.58 points; (3) “I enjoyed physical exercise during chemotherapy treatment” = 4.81 ± 0.4 points; and (4) “I would recommend physical exercise during chemotherapy treatment” = 4.81 ± 0.4 points. Additionally, 81.25% of the participants found physical exercise during chemotherapy treatment to be appealing, reported enjoying the exercise during treatment, and would recommend it to others. The perceived global effect reported by the patients was 3 ± 1.82 points for chemotherapy-related symptoms and 0 ± 2.78 points for the impact of chemotherapy on mobility.

Regarding compliance analysis, only one of the 20 patients performed one less exercise session than planned. Furthermore, 98% of Borg scale effort measurements were different from zero during the active exercise periods, that is, outside the planned warm-up and cool-down periods of the training session. Among these Borg measurements, 68.6% were within the predefined target range for training (score 4-6). Heart rate increased from rest to exercise and decreased during recovery, indicating adequate cardiovascular responses; however, heart rate values during exercise frequently fell below the individualized target range, suggesting autoregulation of intensity based on perceived exertion. Overall, these findings indicate partial compliance with the prescribed exercise intensity.

In terms of adverse events of aerobic exercise, one patient experienced vomiting during the exercise session, needing medication, while another patient reported extreme fatigue (6 points on the Borg scale) after training. Additionally, one patient reported tremors, another experienced nausea, and one reported muscle pain following a training session. No instances of dizziness were reported after the training. Adverse events specifically related to the exercise intervention during chemotherapy were systematically assessed. One mild event and one moderate were reported and attributed to the intervention rather than to chemotherapy. The overall frequency of these events was low and consistent with previous systematic reviews showing that moderate aerobic exercise, including cycling during chemotherapy, is safe, with only mild, self-limiting adverse events reported in oncology populations.

Discussion

The results of this study revealed a high adherence rate (93.75%) among oncology patients participating in a physical exercise program during one cycle of chemotherapy. The unanimous acceptability, with 100% of participants approving of physical activity and 81.25% finding it appealing, along with reports of satisfaction and willingness to recommend it to other patients in the same treatment condition, is particularly notable.

The low incidence of exercise-related adverse events and the stability of vital signs during the intervention confirm the feasibility and safety of the exercise program. Additionally, the analysis of patient-reported effects indicates a trend towards complete improvement in chemotherapy-related symptoms. These findings suggest that short-term moderate intensity aerobic exercise may be a feasible, safe, and beneficial strategy for patients undergoing one cycle of chemotherapy.

In this feasibility study, patient compliance with prescribed exercise intensity was partial, both considering heart rate values frequently below individualized targets and the limited proportion of sessions reaching the predefined Borg range. This pattern is consistent with evidence in exercise oncology, which indicates that patients undergoing chemotherapy tend to self-regulate exercise intensity based on symptom burden and treatment-related side effects, rather than strictly following heart rate-based prescriptions.28,29 Perceived exertion is widely recognized as a valid and pragmatic method for monitoring and prescribing exercise intensity in clinical populations, particularly when physiological responses are variable or blunted. Therefore, although strict compliance with heart rate targets was not consistently achieved, the reliance on perceived exertion reflects a patient-centered and clinically appropriate approach, supporting the feasibility of aerobic exercise implementation during chemotherapy.28–30

Supporting the safety of exercise for oncology patients, a previous systematic review assessed the feasibility and safety of this intervention, as well as its effects on reducing symptom burden. The authors reported adverse events in less than 2% of cases, corroborating the findings of this study and further underscoring the high safety of physical exercise during cancer treatment.31–33 The strengths of this study are evident. First, the high adherence rate to the physical exercise program indicates not only the feasibility of the intervention but also complete acceptance by patients in the challenging clinical context of chemotherapy treatment in a hospital setting. Another strength lies in the high methodological rigor, as only validated scales were used to assess all measured outcomes. The safety of the intervention was demonstrated by the absence of significant changes in vital signs during the training sessions and the few mild adverse events reported. It is important to note that none of the adverse events compromised the clinical stability of the participants, reinforcing that moderate-intensity physical exercise is a safe intervention for oncology patients. Furthermore, patient feedback on finding the exercises appealing and recommending them to others in similar clinical conditions provides additional positive reinforcement for the study’s findings.

Despite the positive feasibility and safety findings, the implementation of structured exercise programs in hospital settings may face practical challenges. Hospital-specific barriers include the availability of trained staff to supervise exercise sessions, the need to comply with infection control protocols, and limitations related to equipment availability and physical space within inpatient units.34,35 Additionally, the dynamic clinical condition of hospitalized patients and frequent medical procedures may restrict the timing and consistency of exercise delivery. These factors should be considered when translating exercise interventions into routine inpatient oncology care and when designing future larger-scale trials.34,35

The primary limitation of this study was the sample size. Although it followed literature-based criteria for estimating the required population, the small sample size may limit the generalizability of the intervention’s effectiveness to larger populations. However, this is a feasibility and safety study for a larger clinical trial involving 64 patients, and the sample size was sufficient to ensure the external validity of the results.27 Additionally, the heterogeneity of the sample, which included patients at different stages of the disease and different phases of oncology treatment, may have complicated the homogeneous analysis of results, as patients in their first chemotherapy cycle may not be as debilitated as those in later cycles. Finally, there was no collection of qualitative insights or feedback from the patient feedback, which could enrich the interpretation of the results of this study.

Nevertheless, our findings are consistent with previous studies involving larger sample sizes and patients at the same stage of treatment.11,13 In a randomized controlled trial, a protocol involving both aerobic and resistance exercises was found to be safe, as no adverse events occurred. Moreover, it demonstrated superior outcomes in terms of self-esteem, aerobic fitness, and reduction in body fat percentage compared to usual care in 242 breast cancer patients undergoing outpatient oncology treatment.11 Similarly, a systematic review found that physical activity during adjuvant chemotherapy in women with breast cancer was beneficial and that aerobic exercise was the most effective in reducing fatigue.13

In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest that moderate-intensity aerobic exercise is a feasible and safe intervention during one cycle of chemotherapy and should be considered as part of the therapeutic approach. This is particularly important due to the exercise program’s adaptability to the individual needs and limitations of patients. It is essential, however, that there be continued monitoring of clinical safety criteria for the appropriate prescription of exercise intensity and volume,5,15,36 as well as awareness of potential adverse effects. Additionally, patient acceptance of exercise highlights the importance of education regarding the benefits of physical activity, even during treatment, which can enhance patient adherence and satisfaction with the intervention, while also offering potential therapeutic benefits. We emphasize that this is preliminary evidence and that studies with specific subpopulations are needed to better support the clinical adoption of these results.
