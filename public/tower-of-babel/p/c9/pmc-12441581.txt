Early-life exposure to a mixture of phenols and respiratory health in preschool children
Authors: Alicia Guillien, Sam Bayat, Ines Amine, Anne Boudier, Sarah Lyon-Caen, Joane Quentin, Zoltán Hantos, Séverine Valmary-Degano, Amrit K. Sakhi, Cathrine Thomsen, Claire Philippat, Rémy Slama, Valérie Siroux
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

It is widely recognised that early-life stages play a crucial role in programming future health. This perspective, known as the Developmental Origins of Health and Diseases hypothesis, suggests that environmental exposures during sensitive periods of development may have long-term health effects [1]. Regarding respiratory health, fetal life and infancy are a cornerstone of healthy lung development, during which exposure to harmful substances could lead to impaired lung growth and function and subsequently to respiratory diseases such as asthma and COPD [2–4].

Phenols are ubiquitous compounds found in everyday products, including plastic containers for food and receipts (bisphenol A and bisphenol S), personal care products and cosmetics (parabens, triclosan, benzophenone), toothpaste (triclosan), pharmaceutical products (parabens) and textiles (triclosan). These compounds have been linked to multiple adverse health effects, including oxidative stress, inflammation and endocrine disruption, giving rise to growing public health concerns. Specifically regarding respiratory health, epidemiological studies have established potential links between early-life exposure to phenols and development of asthma and altered lung function [5] through different biological mechanisms, including an increase in oxidative stress [6], a shift of the immune response towards T-helper 2 cells and a reduction in T-regulatory cells, and induction of an increase in proinflammatory cytokine production [7, 8]. Studies on child respiratory health have focused on prenatal exposure to phenols [9–14] or included cross-sectional exposure measured in school-aged children [15–20], while no study has focused on postnatal exposures in infancy.

The correlations between exposure biomarkers measured in mothers during pregnancy and in infants are generally weak [21, 22], suggesting that prenatal exposure cannot be used to predict infant exposure. Additionally, while phenols are nonpersistent chemicals with very short half-lives, most studies investigating their effects on respiratory health rely on unique spot urinary samples, which has been shown to bias association estimates towards the null hypothesis and limit statistical power [23]. To overcome this limitation, it has been shown that collecting and pooling several samples per participant provides more accurate exposure assessment [24]. Consequently, studies collecting repeated biosamples on several occasions, including infancy, are warranted to accurately assess the association patterns across the different early-life exposure windows.

Individuals are typically exposed to complex mixtures of phenols; the synergistic or antagonistic effects of these mixtures may lead to different health association estimates when exposure compounds are jointly analysed, as compared to their additive effects when considered separately. By applying clustering techniques to analyse combined prenatal exposures to various endocrine disruptors, previous studies based on the Center for the Health Assessment of Mothers and Children of Salinas (CHAMACOS) and the Suivi de l'Exposition à la Pollution Atmosphérique durant la Grossesse et Effets sur la Santé (SEPAGES) cohorts were able to identify mixtures of compounds, some of which were associated with respiratory health [13, 14].

This prospective study is aimed at characterising time-specific exposure profiles during pregnancy and infancy and subsequently assessing their associations with respiratory health up to 3 years.

Methods

Study population

The study, conducted using data from the SEPAGES cohort (2014–2017, Grenoble, France) [25], included 484 pregnant women who were enrolled before the 19th gestational week and followed throughout their pregnancy, along with subsequent follow-ups of their children. The study was approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes (CPP Sud-Est) and the Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL).

Urine sampling and assessment of phenol exposure biomarker

The collection and assessment of biomarker levels of exposure to phenols have been described in detail elsewhere [22]. Briefly, urine samples were collected at four different time points: from the mother during the second (T2) and third trimesters (T3) of pregnancy, and from the child at approximately 2 months old (M2) and 1 year old (Y1). For mothers, three urine samples (morning, midday, evening) were collected per day over a week during each trimester. For infants, one urine sample per day over a week was collected using a cotton gauze insert placed in the stool-free nappy.

Using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry, we measured the total (free and conjugated) urinary concentrations of 12 phenols: four parabens (methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, butylparaben), benzophenone-3, triclosan, triclocarban and five bisphenols (A, S, F, B, AF), in all available maternal and child pooled samples, as previously described [26].

Bisphenols AF, B, F and triclocarban were detected in <5% of the pooled samples and were excluded from this analysis. Compounds with >30% of samples below the limit of detection were dichotomised as detected versus non-detected. This applied to butylparaben and bisphenol S at all time points and to propylparaben at M2. Finally, compounds considered to be continuous were log transformed to reach normality.

Respiratory health assessment

At 2 months of age, lung function was evaluated during natural sleep using tidal breathing flow-volume loops (TBFVL) and the multiple-breath nitrogen washout (N2MBW) technique, following American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines [27] and using version 3.3.2 of Spiroware (www.ecomedics.com/products/spiroware-software/). The TBFVL parameters analysed were tidal volume (VT) and the ratio of time to peak tidal expiratory flow to expiratory time (tPTEF/tE), derived from 30–50 regular breaths. N2MBW was used to measure functional residual capacity (FRC) and lung clearance index (LCI), defined as the number of breaths needed to reduce nitrogen concentration below 2.5%. Because pure oxygen can cause transient hypoventilation and decrease VT [28], FRC and LCI values were corrected for hypoventilation using a regression-based standardisation method [29]. On average, three valid N2MBW tests were retained and averaged per child. Increased LCI and decreased FRC, VT and tPTEF/tE are indicators of reduced lung function.

At 36 months, oscillometry was conducted using a tremoflo C-100 device (Thorasys Systems, Montreal, Canada) that complies with current European Respiratory Society standards [30]. This technique involved applying pressure waves at frequencies from 7 to 41 Hz during tidal breathing to assess respiratory system input impedance, and therefore no active participation or breathing manoeuvres were required. Three to five acceptable measurements were averaged for analysis, focusing on the following parameters: reactance at 7 Hz (X7), resistance at 7 Hz (R7), the frequency dependence of resistance between 7 and 19 Hz (R7–19) and the area of the reactance curve (AX). Additionally, a 10 Hz tracking signal was applied in a 20 s measurement to assess the intra-breath changes in respiratory impedance (Zrs), as a research modality in the tremoflo device. The recordings of pressure (P), flow (V̇) and volume (V) were imported in custom-made software. Fast Fourier transformation was used to compute R and X at 10 Hz (R10 and X10, respectively), after band-pass filtering of V̇ and P between 8 and 12 Hz. Linear interpolation was used to establish zero-crossings of V̇, i.e. the time of end expiration (eE) and end inspiration (eI), and the corresponding R10 and X10 values. Because the latter represent zero-flow values, they approximate resistance and reactance of the respiratory system without the influence of flow nonlinearities in the upper airways. This allowed us to calculate the corresponding end-expiratory and end-inspiratory R (ReE, ReI) and X (XeE, XeI) and the change in R and X between eE and eI (ΔR and ΔX, respectively). Increased R7, R7–19, AX and ΔR, along with decreased X7 and a more negative ΔX, indicate reduced lung function [31].

Parents completed respiratory questionnaires based on the International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood protocol at 2, 12, 24 and 36 months, administered by SEPAGES fieldworkers. Wheezing and bronchitis over the first 36 months were identified by affirmative responses to questions regarding the presence of these symptoms in the past 12 months (or since birth for the 2-month questionnaire). Doctor-diagnosed asthma was confirmed by positive answers to questions about asthma occurrence and medical confirmation. Bronchiolitis was defined based on reports of the condition in the first 2 years of life.

Statistical analyses

Based on the literature and previous studies on the SEPAGES cohort, we built a directed acyclic graph to identify covariates: gestational age (in weeks, only for association with lung function at 2 months), child's sex (boy versus girl), child's height (in cm) and weight (in kg) at the time of examination, still being breastfed at 2 months (yes versus no), maternal age at conception (in years), maternal pre-pregnancy body mass index (in kg·m−2), parity (nulliparous versus primiparous/multiparous), maternal passive smoking during pregnancy (yes versus no), child passive smoking up to 3 years (yes versus no, only for association with lung function and asthma, wheezing and bronchitis up to 3 years), parental history of rhinitis or asthma (yes versus no), parental highest diploma (<5/≥5 years after high school) and season of examination (only for association with lung function at 2 months and 3 years).

Multiple imputation-based chained equations [32] were applied to impute missing data (0–10% of missing data per covariate) for covariates and five complete datasets were created.

An unsupervised clustering analysis was performed on phenol compounds at each time point (T2, T3, M2 and Y1) using the VarSelLCM method to identify profiles of exposure to phenols [33]. The optimal number of clusters was selected based on ease of interpretation of results and statistical performance (log-likelihood).

Associations between the identified profiles and respiratory health were assessed using regression-based models adjusted for the above-mentioned covariates. We fitted a mutually adjusted model with all the exposure profiles in a single model, allowing us to estimate the independent effect of each time-specific exposure profile, for each respiratory health parameter. Analyses regarding lung function at 3 years were repeated with a supplemental adjustment for bronchiolitis in the first 2 years to address whether early-life respiratory infection could play a role in the effects of early-life exposure to phenols on lung function. Additionally, these models were also conducted separately for each time-specific exposure profile to assess the consistency of associations when the potential confounding or interdependencies between exposure variables were not considered.

The trajectories of children's exposure to phenols across time were defined and their associations with respiratory health were further assessed using regression-based models adjusted for the above-mentioned covariates.

Finally, for descriptive purposes, we performed an exposome-wide association study (ExWAS) by fitting one regression-based model for each phenol compound at each time point for all respiratory outcomes [34]. For this analysis, all continuous outcomes were standardised (divided by their standard deviation (sd)) in order to obtain comparable estimates.

At M2, urine was collected from around 80% of participants a few days after respiratory examination. Therefore, analyses related to lung function at 2 months only included prenatal exposures, to avoid analyses with exposures measured after the health outcome.

Identification of profiles of phenol exposures at each time point

Overall, at each of the four time points, two phenol exposure profiles were identified. These were characterised by low versus high overall exposure to some phenols (figure 1 and supplementary tables S3 and S4). However, the phenols influencing this clustering varied across time. At T2, mothers from the high profile (n=150, 41%) had higher prenatal exposure to all phenols than mothers from the low profile, except for bisphenol S, which had similar exposure levels in both profiles. At T3, mothers from the high profile (n=158, 44%) had higher prenatal exposure to all phenols, in particular to methylparaben, ethylparaben and propylparaben, than mothers from the low profile, except for bisphenol S and benzophenone-3, which had similar levels in both profiles. At M2 and Y1, infants from the high profile (M2: n=142, 39%; Y1: n=180, 50%) had higher exposure to parabens than those from the low profile.

Very similar results were found when exposure profiles at each time point were considered in separate models (supplementary figure S3), though the associations between high prenatal (T2 and T3) exposure profiles and lower X7 levels were slightly stronger and statistically significant in single-exposure profile models compared with the mutually adjusted model. Analyses on prenatal exposure to phenols on lung function at 3 years further adjusted on bronchiolitis up to 2 years showed very similar associations to those in the main analysis (supplementary figure S4).

Finally, similarly to our main analysis, no association was found between exposure trajectories to phenols and respiratory health at 2 months and respiratory diseases up to 3 years while associations were found with lung function at 3 years (supplementary figure S5 and supplementary tables S8 and S9). Children in the high trajectory had lower X7 and higher AX, indicating poorer lung function. Additionally, children in the mostly high trajectory had higher R7 and ΔR, also indicating poorer lung function. Finally, children from the fluctuating trajectory had lower X7 and higher R7 and R7–19. All other associations were in the expected direction, although not statistically significant, probably due to the limited sample size in each category.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between the mixture of synthetic phenols in early life, including pregnancy and infancy exposures, and children's respiratory health in the first 3 years of life. Overall, no clear pattern of association between exposure profiles with phenol compounds and lung function measured at 2 months and respiratory diseases up to 3 years was observed, but profiles with high phenol exposure were consistently associated with decreased lung function at 3 years. In particular, high prenatal (T2) exposure to phenols was associated with higher R7–19 and lower ΔX levels. In addition, high postnatal exposure to phenols, in particular to parabens, was associated at 2 months with lower X7, and at 1 year with higher levels of AX, R7 and ΔR. Analyses exploring the effect of trajectories of exposure to phenols on respiratory health showed that children in the high and mostly high trajectories had lower lung function parameters at 3 years compared to those in the low trajectory.

Consideration of longitudinal exposure assessment

In our study, we focused on eight synthetic phenol compounds measured at four time points, including both prenatal and early postnatal assessments. As already demonstrated [21, 22], between-compound correlations measured on the same biological samples are sometimes moderate-to-strong. Clustering methods are advantageous when dealing with multiple correlated variables, because they can effectively reveal underlying exposure patterns that cannot be considered in single-exposure analysis or in multivariate regression models integrating two-way interaction terms. This approach tends to be increasingly used in the context of endocrine disruptor exposures [13, 14, 36]. To handle complex interactions, some previous studies applied mixture models, such as Bayesian kernel machine regression, which are also able to capture the potential nonlinear relationships between phenols and health outcomes, but are not suitable to consider longitudinal exposure data [37]. By applying mutually adjusted regression models, jointly incorporating time-specific phenol exposure profiles identified by cluster analyses, our study was able to take into account the variability of exposure over time. The strong similarity of the association results between the mutually adjusted models (jointly analysing all time-specific exposure profiles) and the single-exposure profile models (separately analysing exposure profiles at each time point) is in line with the low correlation between exposure profiles over time. It suggests that the associations between postnatal exposure and children's lung function at 3 years do not reflect the impact of prenatal exposure, thus both pregnancy and infancy are important target windows for interventions.

Interpretation of results

While our study did not show an association between exposure to a mixture of phenol compounds and lung function at 2 months, several deleterious associations were reported at 3 years, mainly with parabens. This discrepancy may have several, potentially cumulative, explanations: 1) TBFVL analysis and oscillometry do not measure the same parameters, 2) the TBFVL and N2MBW techniques used at 2 months may be less sensitive to changes induced by phenol exposure, and 3) phenol exposure up to 2 months may have a delayed effect on children's lung function. It is noteworthy that most of the reported associations with oscillometry parameters (four out of six) involved exposures occurring after the 2-month examination, which were therefore not considered in the analyses using TBFVL and N2MBW.

Interestingly, the associations reported with lung function at 3 years were overall consistent with the various time-specific exposure profiles and indicated lower reactance and higher resistance, suggesting the development of airway obstruction in the presence of phenols. Among the six reported associations, two involved exposures at T2, one at M2 and three at Y1, indicating a possible higher sensitivity window during infancy. To the best of our knowledge, only one study has investigated the effect of exposure to phenols on lung function in preschool children [14]. This study, also based on the SEPAGES population, focused on prenatal exposure to both phenols and phthalates, and suggested that prenatal exposure to mixtures of phthalates reduced lung volume in early life. The present study builds upon this work by further investigating the impact of early-life exposure to phenols on children's respiratory health, integrating both prenatal and postnatal exposures and intra-breath oscillometry parameters. Additionally, some studies focusing on children ≥6 years old showed associations with airway obstruction [16–19] while other studies reported divergent results, showing no association [11, 12, 15] or positive associations [10] with lung function. All these studies were based on single spot urine, or an average of two spot urines collected at two trimesters of pregnancy, which could lead to bias association estimates towards the null hypothesis due to the short half-life of phenols [23]. Two studies also used clustering analyses to identify profiles of exposures to chemicals and to further assess their association with respiratory health in children [13, 14]. However, they considered several families of compounds (e.g. phenols and phthalates) while we focused on phenol compounds, limiting results comparison.

Regarding respiratory diseases up to 3 years, no association was found with profiles of exposure to phenols, which is in line with most previous studies based on children assessing prenatal [9, 10, 21] or postnatal [20, 21] exposure to phenols.

The ExWAS results mostly highlighted associations with phenols considered as categorical variables while profiles of exposure were mostly determined by phenols considered as continuous, which prevents us from making comparisons.

Strengths and weaknesses

Our study has several strengths. The main asset lies in the repeated urine sample collected at different time points the course of pregnancy and infancy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study using exposure data during infancy, a key period for lung development. Using weekly-pooled urine samples (one (infancy) to three (pregnancy) spots of urine per day for 7 days) to estimate exposure limits the attenuation bias of health effect estimates compared to studies relying on a single or few biological samples [24, 38, 39]. In addition, objective lung function parameters were measured using validated methods when children were 2 months and 3 years old [31]. Finally, we performed clustering analyses on all phenol compounds at each time point, thus allowing the complex interactions existing between phenol compounds to be fitted and limiting the number of tests. Moreover, this methodology offers the advantage of minimising the issue of multiple testing, thereby preserving statistical power. While for each lung function parameter the ExWAS analysis requires k tests for k exposures, the clustering analysis combines these k variables into a single variable, thereby reducing the risk for type I errors.

Our study also has limitations. The sample size was relatively limited, especially because some of the children did not perform validated lung function tests, which may have led to underpowered analyses. This prevented us from running an analysis stratified by sex. Although longitudinal lung function trajectories would be relevant to estimate early-life lung function growth, we were unable to do so because of the lack of consistent measurements at 2 months and 3 years. Additionally, we did not consider exposure to phthalates, because these compounds were not assessed in our population at 2 months. Then, despite our efforts to adjust for potential confounders in the statistical models, residual confounding resulting from unmeasured confounders or imprecisely measured variables cannot be entirely ruled out. After further adjusting the association between prenatal exposure to phenols and lung function at 3 years on bronchiolitis in the first 2 years, we obtained very similar results. This indicates that our findings do not support a major role for respiratory infections in the effects of phenols on lung function. However, this hypothesis needs to be tested in further studies. Finally, the use of cluster analysis did not enable us to study dose-response relationships.

Conclusion

Our study, the first based on exposure to synthetic phenol compounds assessed repeatedly during pregnancy and infancy, showed globally consistent associations between high exposure to a mixture of phenols in early life, in particular to parabens, and impaired lung function at age 3 years. Our results provide further evidence of the deleterious health effects of exposure to phenols early in life and support the need to tighten regulations on phenol compounds.
