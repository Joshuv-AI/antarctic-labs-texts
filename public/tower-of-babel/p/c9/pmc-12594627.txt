Regulatory roles of LncRNAs in colorectal cancer immune evasion: current concepts and future perspectives
Authors: YuHan Wang, YiShan Li, Peng Huang, Chun Li, ShengJin Tu, Hua Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health challenge, ranking as the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality due to late-stage diagnosis and limited effective treatments. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as critical regulators of immune responses and potential therapeutic targets in CRC. LncRNAs modulate immune checkpoint pathways, influencing genes like programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), and contributing to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Additionally, lncRNAs are implicated in the regulation of immune cells and immunological pathways, affecting the immune responses. With advancements in genomic technologies, the identification and functional characterization of lncRNAs are crucial for developing new therapeutic strategies. Their ability to serve as biomarkers for immunotherapy responses and their potential to enhance existing treatments underscore the need for continued research into lncRNA biology. A comprehensive understanding of lncRNA-mediated mechanisms is vital for improving outcomes and extending the benefits of immunotherapy for patients with CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03793-5.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a widespread and highly aggressive form of cancer, currently ranking as the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. One of the significant challenges in managing CRC is the insufficient availability of effective screening and diagnostic tools [2, 3]. As a result, a substantial number of patients are diagnosed only at advanced stages of the disease when treatment options are limited [4–6]. Despite substantial advancements in the treatment of CRC, patient outcomes continue to be unsatisfactory due to challenges such as recurrence, metastasis, and drug resistance [7, 8]. This often contributes to a poor prognosis, with research indicating that the globally five-year survival rate for individuals diagnosed with advanced CRC ranges between approximately 5%ཞ10% [9, 10]. This highlights the urgent need for developing new biomarkers and therapeutic strategies to improve the clinical outcomes of CRC patients.

Research on noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) is marked by uncertainty and ongoing debates, primarily stemming from the traditional view that RNA primarily functions as a mediator between genes and proteins [11–13]. NcRNAs, such as small nucleolar RNAs, were often perceived as having only supportive roles. However, the exploration of the human genome in the early 21st century led to a significant shift in this understanding, prompting the discovery of numerous microRNAs, long non coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs) [14, 15]. Comprehensive transcriptomic analyses revealed that a substantial portion of both animal and plant genomes was actively transcribed into longer RNA molecules, many of which exhibit little to no protein-coding potential [14]. LncRNAs are a notable subset of these molecules, typically defined as being over 200 nucleotides in length [16]. Initially, the molecular biology community expressed skepticism regarding these noncoding RNAs, considering them potential transcriptional noise or “junk” RNA due to their generally low sequence conservation [17]. However, ongoing research has demonstrated that lncRNAs serve as crucial regulatory molecules, playing significant roles in various biological processes [18–20]. LncRNAs can function as sponges for miRNAs, thereby modulating miRNA-mediated regulation of genetic information [21, 22]. Additionally, lncRNAs can impact protein function and downstream signaling pathways through direct interactions with proteins [19, 23]. Additionally, these genes typically exhibit transcription patterns that closely resemble those of protein-coding genes [24]. Previous studies have indicated that transcription factors, DNA methylation, and histone modifications can regulate the expression of lncRNAs at the transcriptional level, while RNA modifications, RNA-binding proteins, and miRNA can influence lncRNA expression at the post-transcriptional level [25]. These functions endow lncRNAs with potent regulatory capabilities, allowing them to be widely involved in the onset and progression of various human diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, and metabolic diseases [26, 27].

An increasing body of evidence indicates that lncRNAs play essential regulatory roles in various types of tumors, indicating that lncRNAs have emerged as important therapeutic targets for malignant diseases [28, 29]. Recent studies suggest that lncRNAs are vital regulators in the initiation and progression of CRC, affecting numerous biological processes including tumor growth, metastasis, immune regulation, remodeling the tumor microenvironment (TME), and therapeutic resistance, suggesting that lncRNAs have emerged as promising therapeutic targets for combatting CRC [30, 31]. This review aims to provide a thorough overview of the functional regulatory mechanisms of lncRNA in CRC, focusing specifically on their roles in immune regulation to facilitate the ongoing advancement of research in CRC immunotherapy.

Characteristics and functions of LncRNAs in human cancers

The ncRNA category comprises a highly diverse assortment that can be further categorized into small ncRNA and lncRNA genes [15]. Most lncRNAs are transcribed by POL II (RNA polymerase II, a multi-protein complex) and exhibit a high degree of tissue specificity [32]. Many lncRNAs resemble canonical messenger RNAs in that they are spliced and carry poly(A) tails. In contrast, a subset of lncRNAs lacks polyadenylation and/or the canonical 7-methylguanosine (m7G) cap, arises from RNA polymerase I or III promoters, or is generated by processing of precursor transcripts [32]. These molecules often harbor intronic and repetitive sequences, and for many of them, biological functions remain uncharacterized [32]. Generally, lncRNAs are encoded within the genome either as standalone entities or overlapping with genes in either the sense or antisense direction [33]. LncRNAs are classified into several categories, including sense, antisense, bidirectional, intronic, intergenic, and enhancer-associated, depending on their positional relationship to protein-coding genes [34].

LncRNAs that localize in the nucleus are capable of interacting with a range of molecules, such as DNA, RNA, and proteins [35]. This interaction plays a crucial role in regulating chromosomal structure and function [35]. Additionally, lncRNAs can influence gene transcription in both a cis- and trans- manner, affecting various processes including mRNA splicing, stability, and translation [36]. Once localized in the cytoplasm, lncRNAs primarily engage in post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression [37]. They can influence mRNA translation by sponging miRNAs and participate in the regulation of intracellular signaling pathways [14, 38]. LncRNAs that are specifically localized to certain organelles may also play roles in regulating the functions and metabolic processes of these organelles, such as oxidative reactions and homeostatic balance in mitochondria (Fig. 1) [14].

In recent years, emerging evidence has revealed that lncRNA play a significant role in the progression and development of human tumors [39]. LncRNAs can act as molecular scaffolds, bringing together proteins and other RNA molecules to form complexes that regulate gene expression and chromatin structure [40]. Moreover, lncRNAs also interact with transcriptional factors that contribute to disease development [41]. In many types of cancer, specific lncRNAs are aberrantly expressed, contributing to oncogenic pathways. For instance, LOC100507424 modulated the expression of forkhead box protein M1 (FOXM1) by interacting with E2F Transcription Factor 1 (E2F1). In vivo experiments demonstrated that LOC100507424 facilitated tumor growth in a preclinical model of pancreatic cancer [42]. Moreover, the lncRNA-E2F1 interaction significantly promoted the tumor growth, metastasis and therapeutic resistance in cervical cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer and lung cancer [43–46]. Additionally, lncRNAs are also implicated in modulating the tumor microenvironment and influencing immune responses against tumors [47, 48]. For example, a reduction in the lncRNAs NEAT1 and MALAT1 was noted from the lung cancer mouse models, which affected NK cell functionality and indicated a substantial role of the lncRNA-regulated immune system in tumor growth [49]. Their unique expression patterns make lncRNAs potential biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and prognosis, as well as promising therapeutic targets [50]. As research continues to uncover the diverse functions of lncRNAs in cancer biology, they might offer new insights into tumor mechanisms and lead to novel treatment strategies.

Landscape of CRC immunotherapy

Immunotherapy has become a key treatment modality for CRC, offering new hope for patients, particularly in cases where traditional therapies have failed [51]. The most prominent immunotherapy approaches include immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which target pathways such as programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), thereby enhancing T-cell activity against tumor cells [52]. However, the implementation of immunotherapy in CRC faces significant challenges. Notably, only a subset of patients—especially those with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) tumors—tend to respond favorably to current ICIs [53]. This limited efficacy in the majority of microsatellite stable CRC cases, highlighting a critical gap in treatment options [54]. Another obstacle is the immunosuppressive TME often present in CRC, which can hinder the effectiveness of immune responses [55]. Factors such as regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and various cytokines contribute to this immunosuppressive milieu, impairing treatment outcomes [56]. Moreover, resistance mechanisms can develop, diminishing the long-term benefits of immunotherapy [18]. Thus, ongoing research aims to identify biomarkers that predict response, as well as to develop combination therapies that target both the immune system and the tumor cells more effectively. Addressing these challenges is essential for expanding the benefits of immunotherapy to a broader range of CRC patients and improving overall survival rates.

Mechanisms of LncRNAs in CRC immune evasion

LncRNAs have emerged as significant players in cancer, particularly in the context of immune evasion [47, 57, 58]. These RNA molecules can regulate various biological processes, including gene expression, cell signaling, and immune response modulation, ultimately influencing tumor behavior and the effectiveness of immunotherapy [59–61]. In the realm of immune evasion, lncRNAs can contribute to the development of an immunosuppressive TME by modulating the expression of key immune checkpoint molecules and cytokines [62, 63]. For example, certain lncRNAs have been implicated in promoting the expression of PD-L1 and CTLA-4, which are crucial for T-cell inhibition, thereby facilitating tumor cells’ ability to escape immune detection [64]. By affecting the infiltration and activation of immune cells, lncRNAs can hinder the overall immune response to cancer, contributing to tumor growth and metastasis [65]. Therefore, targeting specific lncRNAs could enhance the effectiveness of existing immunotherapies by reversing immune evasion mechanisms and reactivating T-cell responses against cancer. The clinical significance of lncRNAs lies not only in their role in immune regulation but also in their potential as therapeutic targets [66]. However, the role of lncRNAs in immune evasion of CRC remains unclear. Continued research into lncRNA function and their interactions with the immune system could lead to novel strategies for overcoming immune evasion in CRC, improving patient outcomes, and extending the benefits of immunotherapy to a wider patient population (Table 1).

Immune checkpoint modulation by LncRNAs

ICIs represent a groundbreaking class of therapies that amplify the immune system’s ability to target and eliminate tumors [52]. These agents function primarily by inhibiting specific proteins that restrict T-cell activation and activity. The most notable targets include PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 [52]. Cancer cells often take advantage of these pathways to escape detection by the immune system. By increasing the expression of checkpoint molecules like PD-L1, tumors can hinder the function of immune cells, especially cytotoxic T cells [67]. This leads to the formation of an immune-tolerant microenvironment, which reduces the immune system’s capacity to recognize and destroy cancer cells. Consequently, this evasion promotes tumor growth and metastasis while lowering resistance to therapeutic interventions [68].

Recent studies have indicated that certain lncRNAs have significant roles in cancer immunology, particularly in processes such as regulation of immune checkpoint molecules [69]. Despite the identification of numerous immune-related lncRNAs, their specific functions and underlying mechanisms are still not fully understood. LncRNAs have the ability to directly interact with target proteins, thereby contributing to immune evasion (Fig. 2). For example, Ni et al. have identified a novel mechanism in which lncRNA small Nucleolar RNA Host Gene 29 (SNHG29) interacted with Yes-associated protein (YAP) to maintaining its nuclear localization and inhibit ubiquitination-mediated protein degradation, thereby enhancing the transcriptional activity of YAP [70]. YAP is a downstream component of the Hippo pathway that regulates tumor progression. Additionally, YAP plays a role in tumor immune suppression by promoting the expression of PD-L1 [71]. Thus, the SNHG29-YAP interaction ultimately led to increased expression of PD-L1, which suppresses T-cell activity and contributes to the immune evasion [70]. Furthermore, one recent study has revealed that lncRNA 60967.1 played an essential role in the regulation of TME within CRC. LncRNA 60967.1 could reduce tumor growth and metastasis via increasing expression of PLCD4 and upregulating concentrations of all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA). Moreover, lncRNA 60967.1 contributed to the expression of IFN-γ within the TME and reduction of PD-1 levels, suggesting the potential inhibition of immune exhaustion [72].

MiRNAs are a type of non-coding RNAs that are approximately 23 nucleotides in length, which specifically bind to the 3’ untranslated region of mRNAs, leading to their destabilization and the inhibition of protein translation [73]. The competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) mechanism suggests that RNAs containing miRNA-binding sites can compete with mRNAs for miRNA binding, thereby creating a co-regulatory network that operates at the post-transcriptional level [74]. Emerging evidence has indicated that lncRNAs can function as ceRNAs to modulate miRNAs, thus implicating in the regulation of tumorigenesis, metastasis and immune regulation [75, 76]. For example, Chen et al. revealed that silencing lncRNA SNHG16 led to decreased cell growth, migration, and immune evasion in CRC cells, effects that were counteracted by the introduction of a miR-324-3p inhibitor and the overexpression of ETS-like transcription factor 4 (ELK4). Mechanistically, SNHG16 served as a ceRNA that increased ELK4 expression by sequestering miR-324-3p, which in turn promoted the transcription of PD-L1, consequently altering immune responses and aiding in the evasion of tumor immunity [77]. Furthermore, lncRNA PROX1 antisense RNA 1 (PROX1-AS1) functioned as a molecular sponge for miR-520d, leading to the upregulation of PD-L1 expression. By targeting miR-520d/PD-L1 axis, PROX1-AS1 promoted immune evasion, thus contributing to the tumor growth and metastasis [78]. In line with this, lncRNA SNHG4 was shown to regulate MET expression via directly sponging miR-144-3p, thereby activating PD-1/PD-L1 pathway and mitigating immune escape [79]. Notably, lncRNA KCNQ1OT1 was found to be significantly elevated in exosomes derived from CRC cells. The tumor-promoting role of KCNQ1OT1 was operated via the autocrine effects of exosomes released by CRC cells. This mechanism involved the miR-30a-5p/ ubiquitin-specific peptidase 22 (USP22) pathway, which regulated the ubiquitination of PD-L1 and suppressed the response of CD8+ T cells, thereby promoting immune escape [80]. Additionally, several lncRNAs have been found to modulate PD-L1 expression, thus facilitating immune evasion in CRC. LncRNAs such as LINC01088, HCG18 and MIR17HG functioned as molecular sponges for multiple miRNAs including miR-548b-5p, miR-20b-5p and miR-17-5p to inhibit their ability to downregulate PD-L1 expression. By stabilizing PD-L1 mRNA, these lncRNAs enhanced its expression, enabling cancer cells to escape immune surveillance by inhibiting T-cell activation [81–83]. Importantly, several lncRNAs were reported to regulate expression of CTLA-4 and other immune-regulated genes, which contributed to immune escape in CRC. SNHG14, which exhibited downregulated expression in CRC, and was involved in the mechanisms of immune evasion [84]. It interacted with miR-200a-3p/ PCOLCE2 axis, leading to the upregulation of immune checkpoint genes including CTLA-4, thereby promoting tumor immune evasion [84]. MIR4435-2HG, which sponged miR-500a-3p, thus upregulate the expression of PDCD1, CD274, and CTLA-4, which promoted immune evasion and enhanced tumor growth [85]. These findings underscored that lncRNA as potential therapeutic targets for CRC therapy; however, additional research is necessary to confirm its clinical relevance.

Modulation of innate and adaptive immune cells

Emerging evidence has highlighted the critical role of the innate immune response in tumor development. Macrophages, the essential players in innate immunity, play a significant role in shaping the TME, where they exert diverse effects related to tumorigenesis [86]. Macrophages are a diverse population of highly adaptable cells within the TME, distinguished by their plasticity and variability [87]. The presence of macrophages or macrophage-related cytokines and chemokines in human solid tumors is often linked to unfavorable prognoses [88]. Furthermore, TAMs can influence the TME through interactions with other cell types. Macrophages can be classified into two distinct phenotypes: the pro-inflammatory M1 type and the anti-inflammatory M2 type [89]. M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory substances that initiate inflammation and promote anti-tumor responses, including TNF-α, IL-12, IL-23, and iNOS [90]. In contrast, M2 macrophages produce anti-inflammatory cytokines that support immunosuppressive and pro-tumor functions, such as TGF-β, IL-10, and Arg-1 [91]. Generally, TAMs display M2-like characteristics, and their contribution to the pathogenesis of CRC remains to be fully understood [92]. Recently, lncRNAs have been found to influence the polarization of macrophages toward M1 or M2 phenotypes, thus impacting immune responses within the TME of CRC [93]. Liu et al. demonstrated that the interaction between lncRNA RP11-417E7.1 and high mobility group A1 (HMGA1) facilitated the transcription of the neighboring gene thrombospondin 2 (THBS2) by creating chromatin loops that connect its promoter and enhancer regions [94]. This mechanism activated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby enhancing metastasis in CRC. Moreover, exosomes secreted by CRC cells transported THBS2 to macrophages, leading to M2 polarization of these macrophages, which sustained a microenvironment conducive to metastasis [94]. In line with this, lncRNA HLA-F-AS1 bound competitively to miR-375, leading to an inverse regulation of its expression. Elevated levels of HLA-F-AS1 result in decreased miR-375 expression, which causes an increase in PFN1 levels in both CRC cells and CRC-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), further promoting the M2 polarization of macrophages and establishing a permissive TME [95]. LINC01615 played an essential role in the M2 polarization of TAMs. Mechanistically, LINC01615 could be transferred from cancer cells to TAMs through exosomes. Within exosomes, LINC01615 functioned as a scaffold to facilitate the interaction between RBMX and EZH2, ultimately promoting M2 polarization of TAMs, which in turn accelerated the immune escape and development of CRC [96]. Exosomal lncRNA BANCR promoted the polarization of M2 macrophages by activating the RhoA/Rock pathway through the recruitment of IGF2BP2. Blocking the RhoA/Rock pathway reversed the M2 polarization of TAMs induced by exosomal BANCR, therefore establishing a suppressive TME and promoting CRC progression [97]. LncRNA NEAT1, sourced from M2-EVs, acted as a ceRNA by interacting with miR-204-5p, resulting in the upregulation of RRS1 expression. These processes fostered immune escape and promoted CRC progression [98]. Additionally, lncRNAs could interact with M1, thus impacting CRC TME. Teng et al. revealed that exosomes originating from M1 macrophages efficiently transported lncRNA CASC19 to cancer cells, thus interacting with miR-410-3p and impacting the TGF-β signaling pathway, which inhibited CRC progression [99]. Yang et al. revealed that lncRNA NBR2 was downregulated in CRC cells, while miR-19a was overexpressed, correlating with elevated M2 polarization in TAMs. Conversely, a decrease in miR-19a levels promoted M1 polarization, activated AMPK, inhibited the HIF-1α and AKT/mTOR signaling pathways, and enhanced the antitumor properties in TAMs with overexpressed NBR2, effects that can be reversed by introducing a miR-19a mimic [100] (Fig. 3).

Natural killer (NK) cells are essential for tumor immune surveillance, with studies showing that reduced NK activity is linked to higher cancer susceptibility and metastasis [101]. They recognize and respond rapidly to malignant cells using various germline-encoded surface receptors, and upon activation, release cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzymes to kill tumor cells [102]. Moreover, NK cells are significant producers of chemokines and cytokines like interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), which are crucial for modulating adaptive immune responses [103]. The lncRNA SNHG10 derived from CRC cells inhibited the survival and cytotoxic effectiveness of NK cells by upregulating INHBC expression, which offers essential evidence that exosomal lncRNAs play a role in immune evasion through NK cell suppression and suggest a potential therapeutic strategy for CRC [104].

In recent years, EVs have emerged as a promising avenue for cancer therapy. Among them, exosomes—cell-derived nanovesicles that possess properties such as immunogenicity and molecular transfer—present innovative opportunities for cancer immunotherapy [105]. Fang et al. demonstrated that lncRNA PWAR6 accumulated in exosomes derived from cancer-associated fibroblasts. This accumulation inhibited the degradation of NF-E2 p45-related factor 2 (NRF2) by interacting with Keap1, leading to an increase in SLC38A2 expression. Consequently, this reduced the availability of glutamine and hampered the immunological functions of NK cells. These mechanisms collectively enhanced immune evasion and facilitated the metastasis of colorectal cancer cells to the liver [106]. LncRNA ELFN1-AS1 supported the assembly of the GCN5/SND1 complex, which increased H3K9ac enrichment at the GDF15 promoter, subsequently enhancing its transcription. Enhanced GDF15 downregulated NKG2D and GZMB expression through the JNK signaling pathway in NK cells, ultimately impaired NK cell cytotoxicity and contributed to the immune evasion of CRC cells [107]. These findings emphasize its promise as a therapeutic target; however, additional research is required to confirm its clinical relevance.

Key signaling pathways influenced by LncRNAs

LncRNAs play an essential role in regulating TME of CRC by modulating multiple pathways [60]. The Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling pathway serves as a crucial intracellular mechanism for fundamental biological processes, such as modulating immune responses [60]. LncRNA TUSC7 modulated oxidative stress levels and inhibited M2 polarization of peritoneal macrophages in CRC by targeting miR-23b, accompanied by the activation of STAT3/STAT6 signaling pathway [108]. LncRNA ALMS1-IT1 was found to activate the phosphorylation of the STAT3, further negatively modulating ferroptosis and promoting immune evasion in CRC [109]. In addition to STAT signaling pathway, lncRNAs are also known to influence other vital pathways related to immune evasion. For instance, the lncRNA p50-associated COX-2 extragenic RNA (PACER), located upstream of the COX-2 gene, enhanced COX-2 expression via the NF-κB signaling pathway. PACER interacted directly with the p50 subunit of NF-κB, facilitating the activation of the COX-2 promoter by the p65 subunit and leading to increased COX-2 levels. This rise in COX-2 not only inhibited lymphocyte activity but also accelerated the production of PGE2, which further suppressed the release of factors that promote immune responses [110].

Therapeutic delivery strategies of LncRNAs in CRC immunotherapy

Simultaneously, the involvement of lncRNAs in cancer immunotherapy has been increasingly clarified, with research emphasizing their immunogenic roles in various tumor types, such as melanoma, breast cancer, lung cancer, and liver cancer [111, 112]. In the context of CRC, studies have shed light on the function of lncRNAs in immunotherapy, revealing how they facilitate immune evasion and contribute to the development of drug resistance. A prominent example of this is lncRNA MIR155HG, which promoted oxaliplatin resistance through inducing an immunosuppressive TME [113]. Mechanistic studies have revealed that MIR155HG induced M2 macrophage polarization via modulating miR-650/ANXA2 pathway [113]. Silencing of MIR155HG additionally suppressed M2 macrophage polarization, suggesting the role of immune-suppressive pathway in oxaliplatin resistance [113].

Additionally, studies also suggested that lncRNAs could have a positive influence on immunotherapy for CRC. For instance, simvastatin reduced PD-L1 expression and enhanced anti-tumor immunity by downregulating lncRNA SNHG29. Mechanistically, SNHG29 interacted with YAP, preventing its phosphorylation and subsequent ubiquitination-mediated degradation [70]. This interaction facilitated the transcriptional downregulation of PD-L1, which highlighted lncRNA SNHG29 as a crucial molecular mediator linking simvastatin to PD-L1-related immune evasion and suggests it as a potential therapeutic target for CRC immunotherapy [70]. Furthermore, mechanistic investigations revealed that lncRNA 60967.1 overexpression demonstrated a markedly improved anti-tumor response of anti–PD-1 therapy, evidenced by substantial decreases in tumor growth of CRC murine models [72].

Moreover, lncRNA SNHG4, HCG18, LINC01088 and PROX1-AS1 also mediated immune evasion via PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, which might potent therapeutic targets for CRC immunotherapy [79, 82, 83]. Although there have been encouraging advancements in lncRNA-targeted therapies for cancer treatment. Various approaches have been devised to target ncRNA and modulate their expression or function, including antisense oligonucleotides (ASO), RNA interference, and CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing methodologies [114, 115]. Numerous antisense oligonucleotides have received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for treating various conditions [116]. Several therapies utilizing RNAse H-dependent mechanisms or siRNA/RNA interference to reduce the expression levels of target transcripts are currently being tested in clinical trials [116]. However, few studies have investigated the therapeutic strategies by targeting lncRNAs in the preclinical settings of CRC. Investigating alternative nanoparticles could significantly reduce the costs associated with lncRNA drug development. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are frequently used as carriers in gene therapy clinical trials and have successfully delivered ASOs targeting mRNA [117, 118]. The success of mRNA-containing LNP vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic further highlights the potential of LNPs for efficient drug delivery [119]. Given the structural similarities between lncRNAs and mRNAs, lncRNA-loaded LNPs may represent a viable clinical option, offering significant therapeutic benefits. A recent study identified VPS9D1-AS1 as a crucial lncRNA in CRC, associated with sensitivity to ICIs. By utilizing a LNP delivery system, ASOs effectively inhibited VPS9D1-AS1 in mouse models of CRC, leading to significant reductions in tumor growth and metastasis [120]. The LNP strategy facilitated remodeling TME by promoting the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and dendritic cells, thereby enhancing ICI efficacy and indicating promising advances in CRC immunotherapy [120]. The results showed that ASO-mediated lncRNA-targeted therapy significantly decreased tumor volumes in mouse models and improved survival rates, confirming its therapeutic potential.

Conclusions and perspectives

CRC remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, primarily diagnosed at advanced stages largely due to subtle symptoms and the absence of reliable biomarkers [121]. As researchers delve into the molecular underpinnings of CRC, identifying lncRNAs as regulators of immune checkpoint gene expression has emerged as a promising avenue for developing innovative therapeutic strategies. These strategies aim to enhance the efficacy of CRC immunotherapy, a field that holds great potential in harnessing the immune system to combat cancer.

Current methods for inhibiting oncogenic lncRNAs include gene knockout, RNA interference (RNAi), and ASOs [122, 123]. While there has been notable progress in research surrounding these methods, the development of lncRNA-based gene therapy faces several significant hurdles. Firstly, many lncRNAs remain poorly characterized within the scientific community. Although various databases have been created to predict their potential functions and explore the mechanisms by which they operate, the number of annotated lncRNAs remains quite limited [124]. Furthermore, many lncRNAs identified through high-throughput sequencing techniques are still awaiting detailed annotation [124]. There is an urgent need for a comprehensive database that functions in tandem with cutting-edge sequencing technologies [125]. This integration is crucial to enhance our understanding of tumor-specific lncRNAs, which could ultimately lead to the identification of novel therapeutic targets. Secondly, the rate of preclinical and clinical development for lncRNA-based therapies is noticeably sluggish. As functional molecules that display limited evolutionary conservation across species, lncRNAs complicate the evaluation of off-target toxicities and therapeutic efficacy in experimental models [126]. A potential solution to these challenges lies in identifying structural conservation for orthologue detection. Although these orthologues may not possess identical sequences, they frequently exhibit similar three-dimensional structures, which can aid in predicting their biological roles. Additionally, recent advancements in human organoid models, which mimic the TME more accurately than traditional cell lines, provide promising avenues for expediting clinical development in lncRNA research [127]. These models offer a more nuanced platform for studying lncRNA functions in a setting that resembles in vivo conditions, ultimately guiding therapeutic discovery [127]. Finally, an efficient delivery system for lncRNAs is crucial for the success of effective cancer therapies. The development of various delivery vectors for lncRNAs, including LNPs, exosomes, and virus-like particles (VLPs), has transformed the therapeutic landscape [128, 129]. LNPs provide several advantages including resistance to nucleases, avoiding renal clearance, and improving cellular uptake [130]. Exosomes, as natural endogenous carriers, exhibit low toxicity, lack of immunogenicity, and favorable permeability, making them particularly attractive for clinical applications [131]. VLPs are self-assembling nanocarriers that can generate a robust immune response without containing viral nucleic acids, demonstrating effective tissue targeting and delivery capabilities [132]. However, the high costs associated with membrane engineering raise significant barriers to large-scale production, impeding the widespread application of these advanced delivery systems [133]. Additionally, most lncRNAs are context‑ and tissue‑dependent and resist simple tumor‑suppressor/oncogene labels [134]. Falling RNA‑seq costs and advancing mapping technologies will enable broader functional annotation across organs and other ncRNA classes [135]. Clinically, the main bottleneck is incomplete insight into their roles and downstream networks, though platforms to design lncRNA modulators now support robust preclinical and early clinical testing [136]. Overall, the field is early, but its therapeutic promise is rapidly consolidating and is likely to be leveraged soon in precision oncology.

Beyond their oncogenic roles, lncRNAs play a critical role in modulating immune responses and mechanisms of immune evasion in CRC. They can influence the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, thereby shaping the tumor microenvironment and contributing to the immune evasion exhibited by CRC cells. By regulating these immune checkpoint pathways, lncRNAs have the potential to enhance the immunosuppressive characteristics of tumors, making it increasingly difficult for the immune system to mount an effective attack against cancerous cells [137]. The interplay between lncRNAs and the immune system suggests that targeting specific lncRNAs may enhance the efficacy of existing immunotherapies [137]. This could be achieved by reversing immune suppression and restoring the immune system’s intrinsic ability to recognize and destroy tumor cells. Such strategies may significantly improve the accuracy and effectiveness of CRC immunotherapy while ultimately leading to better patient outcomes. Furthermore, the expression of PD-L1, a widely recognized biomarker for predicting sensitivity to immunotherapy, is intricately regulated by various lncRNAs [138]. However, it is crucial to note that elevated levels of PD-L1 expression do not universally guarantee better outcomes with ICI therapy due to the complexity of tumor biology [139]. Despite these challenges, lncRNAs continue to hold a pivotal role in advancing CRC immunotherapy. By translating these mechanistic insights into coherent therapeutic strategies, we can significantly improve immune-mediated control of tumors and enhance prognoses for patients suffering from CRC. The continued research into lncRNA functions and their implications in cancer therapy contributes immensely to the future landscape of oncology treatment, paving the way for more tailored and effective strategies against this devastating disease.
