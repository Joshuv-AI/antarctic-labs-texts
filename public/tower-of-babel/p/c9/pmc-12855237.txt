Safer oxygenation through flexible optical scopes during airway management: an in vitro study
Authors: Alexandre Garioud, Michael Seltz Kristensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeOxygen insufflation through the working channel of flexible optical scopes improves oxygenation and visibility during tracheal intubation but potentially causes barotrauma when not pressure-restricted. Pressure limited to 30–40 cm H2O improves safety. We sought to conduct an in vitro study to develop a setup that delivers oxygen at desired pressures while maintaining sufficient flows to achieve the documented benefits.MethodsIn the present in vitro study, we constructed a setup with equipment readily available in anesthesia locations, allowing regulation of the pressure at which oxygen is delivered at the distal end of the flexible optical scope. Pressure was regulated via the adjustable pressure-limiting valve and measured by immersion in water. We identified which combinations of set flow from the anesthesia machine and set pressure resulted in distal pressures of 10, 15, 30, and 40 cm H2O, respectively. We measured the resulting flows.ResultsIn flexible scopes with a channel diameter ≥ 2 mm, we obtained clinically relevant flows > 3 L·min−1 at pressure of 30–40 cm H2O.ConclusionsOxygen insufflation through a flexible optical scope at a targeted distal pressure is possible with ubiquitous anesthesia equipment. Scopes with a suction-/working-channel diameter ≥ 2 mm delivered clinically relevant flows > 3 L·min−1 at a pressure of 30–40 cm H2O. Thinner scopes delivered lower flows. These findings will guide future studies and the implementation of this setup in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12630-025-03059-2.

Tracheal intubation guided by a flexible optical scope, previously referred to as a “fibrescope,”1 is a cornerstone in the management of the anticipated difficult airway.1 The procedure is still regarded as one of the most challenging in anesthesiology, and acquiring and maintaining experience in this domain is of paramount importance.1 When performing tracheal intubation guided by a flexible optical scope, loss of anatomical orientation from blurring of the lens caused by secretions, hemorrhage, or vomit may produce major challenges that can result in unsuccessful airway management. These challenges may be combined with oxygen desaturation, making the situation even more challenging.2 Established adjuncts, such as nasal high-flow oxygenation, antisialogogues, and suction, may remain insufficient to resolve these challenges.2 To help clear the lens, prevent the accumulation of liquids, and maintain normoxia, oxygen insufflation via the suction channel of the flexible optical scope has been used since the 1980s.3–9 Recent clinical studies have confirmed improved visibility and oxygenation when using oxygen flows of 3–7 L·min−1.10 Nevertheless, in the same review,10 the unsafe nature of this method in its current form was also exposed. Oxygen insufflation without pressure limitation combined with accidental misplacement of the tip of the scope may result in major and fatal complications from barotrauma.10 Even when regulated by a flow meter, oxygen delivery from the hospital’s central network or cylinder will only cease when a pressure far beyond an estimated safe pressure is reached. This can result in gastric rupture and pneumothorax, which are the most frequently reported iatrogenic injuries.10 If the pressure can be kept < 30 cm H2O, the risk of these severe complications is greatly reduced. If the pressure can be kept < 40 cm H2O, the risk remains low, and applying this pressure for short periods is justified in the case of blurring of the lens leading to difficulty in advancing the scope during the management of a difficult airway.10 We sought to conduct an in vitro study to develop a setup assembled with equipment readily available in most anesthesia locations that allows regulation of the pressure at which oxygen is delivered at the distal end of the flexible optical scope at acceptable pressures and at clinically sufficient flows that maintain the documented benefits of the method. Clinicians may use the results to guide the use of safer oxygen insufflation via the suction or working channel during tracheal intubation with a flexible optical scope.

Methods

In the present in vitro study, we developed an apparatus that allowed us to set a specific maximal pressure at the proximal end of the flexible optical scope when insufflating oxygen through either the suction channel or the working channel of the scope (Table 1). Subsequently, we measured the resulting pressures and flows of the oxygen that were delivered at the distal end of the flexible optical scopes (Tables 2 and 3). Description of the setup

To control the pressure delivered by the setup, oxygen is delivered to the flexible optical scope by connecting it to the circle system of an anesthesia machine. This allows users to preset a desired maximum pressure that can be delivered to the flexible scope by manually turning the wheel of the APL valve to the targeted pressure. The user then interconnects the circle system and the flexible optical scope either via the suction channel or the working channel. Figure 3 and ESM eVideo 2 show the adjustment of flow to obtain the targeted safe pressures at the distal end of the flexible optical scopes. In preliminary tests, a high-flow setting from the anesthesia machine resulted in pressures at the distal end of the scope that were slightly above the maximum pressure set on the APL valve. We therefore aimed to determine which flow settings on the anesthesia machine would deliver the same pressure at the distal end of the scopes as the pressure set on APL valve. We targeted pressures of 40, 30, 15, and 10 cm H2O. The APL valve was set to the targeted pressure, and the oxygen flow from the anesthetic machine was initially set to maximum (18 L·min−1). The manual spontaneous breathing mode on the anesthesia machine was activated, and thus, oxygen flowed through the scope. The distal end was gradually submerged into a water-filled transparent tube with centimeter markings indicating the depth from the water surface. Oxygen flow was visualized from the bubbles exiting the distal end of the scope until the flow of oxygen ceased (ESM eVideo 2).12 The depth at which oxygen flow stopped in the water recipient thus corresponded to the pressure delivered by the setup at its distal end in cm H2O. The flow delivered from the anesthetic machine was then reduced until the pressure delivered at the distal end was identical to the pressure setting of the APL valve. We thus identified the maximal flow setting from the anesthesia machine at which the obtained pressure at the distal end of the scope was equal to the APL valve settings at 10, 15, 30, and 40 cm H2O, respectively (Tables 2 and 3).Fig. 3Illustration of the automatic flow stop when adequate pressure is reached.The adjustable pressure-limiting valve was set to 30 cm H2O. Results

Tables 2 and 3 present the pressure and flow measurements from oxygen delivered through the suction and working channel of the flexible optical scope, respectively. When applying a flow of 18 L·min−1, we observed pressures at the distal end of the scope 5 cm H2O above the APL valve setting. Activation of the oxygen flush for 5 sec raised the distal pressure 10 cm H2O above the APL valve setting. We subsequently decreased the flow from the anesthesia machine in 1-L increments until the pressure measured at the distal end of the scope was identical to the pressure set on the APL valve (Tables 2 and 3). The measured oxygen flows at the distal end of the scope increased with the working channel diameter and the increasing pressure setting. When applying pressures of 30 cm H2O and 40 cm H2O, the highest flows were measured during oxygen insufflation through the working channel in the nondisposable scopes from Olympus. The opposite was observed in the disposable scopes from Ambu, where the suction channel allowed the highest flows. The results were mixed when applying pressures of 10–15 cm H2O. The highest flow was recorded from oxygen insufflation through the working channel of the Olympus BF-1T180 EVIS EXERA II (6.25 L·min−1) and the lowest from the suction channel of the Ambu aScope 4 Broncho slim (0.16 L·min−1). We observed no difference between each of the three measurements of pressure and flow, and no difference was observed when disposable flexible scopes from Ambu were switched to a new identical scope (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

In the present in vitro study, we found that, when insufflating oxygen was delivered at a pressure of 30 cm H2O through either the working channel or the suction channel of flexible optical scopes, oxygen flows of 3.3–5.6 L·min−1 at the distal end were obtained in three of the five scopes tested (Ambu aScope 4 Broncho [regular and large] and Olympus BF-P180 EVIS EXERA II). When increasing the pressure to a maximum of 40 cm H2O using the same scopes, the flows increased to 4.0–6.3 L·min−1. The 2 scopes with the smallest inner diameter (Olympus LF-V VISERA and Ambu aScope 4 Broncho slim) failed to deliver clinically sufficient flows, i.e., flows > 3 L·min−1.10 The narrowest scope tested that delivered a sufficient flow had an inner diameter of 2.0 mm (Ambu aScope 4 Broncho regular). The working channel and suction channel each have their benefits and drawbacks. The working channel allows continuous flow without button activation, and suctioning remains possible without changing tube placement. The suction channel method has less cumbersome piping and generates slightly higher flows. A better sealing around the suction channel is believed to cause this flow difference.

Previously, oxygen flow rates of 3–7 L·min−1 during airway management have been reported to result in superior oxygenation, secretion clearance, lens defogging, spread of local anesthetic topicalization, and better conditions for clearing the lens.10 The flows obtained from the abovementioned scopes using the setup presented in this study are thus within the range of flows reported to result in significantly superior conditions for fibreoptic intubation.

Importantly, when adequately adjusting the fresh gas flow and APL setting on the anesthesia machine, the setup can deliver oxygen at pressures that minimize the risk of barotrauma. We recommend against increasing fresh gas flows to levels greater than those listed in Tables 2 and 3, and against oxygen flush valve activation. Both may cause pressure augmentation to levels greater than the originally selected APL settings. It was recently concluded that delivering a pressure of ≤ 30 cm H2O at the distal end of the flexible optical scope may greatly reduce risks of severe side effects from barotrauma.10 This latter conclusion, in combination with the reported beneficial effects and the findings from the present study, suggests that the application of oxygen insufflation via the working channel with a pressure kept ≤ 30 cm H2O may be recommended whenever there is a risk of encountering fogging, secretions, or blood that may interfere with visibility during tracheal intubation guided by a flexible optical scope. When the delivered pressure is increased up to 40 cm H2O for short periods, the flow through the working channel is further increased (Tables 2 and 3). The risk of serious side effects remains low but increases compared with lower pressures.10 This combination could be justified in clinical situations where achieving tracheal intubation guided by the flexible scope might otherwise fail.10

The setup achieved replicable pressure and flow measurements. Nevertheless, it was only tested on one anesthesia machine brand. The presented methodology can be used by anyone to test the pressures obtained for the specific brands of anesthesia machine and flexible scopes that are available in the reader’s department.

Conclusions

In this in vitro study, we report that oxygen insufflation through a flexible optical scope at a targeted pressure is possible. Thinner scopes may fail to deliver clinically sufficient flows. These findings will guide future studies and the implementation of this setup in clinical practice.
