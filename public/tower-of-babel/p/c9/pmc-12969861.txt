Association of Blood Eosinophil Counts with Expiratory Airflow Limitation: A Multi-Ethnicity Observational Study
Authors: Jianbo Xue, Yukun He, Xiaojun Ma, Yixin Zhang, Wenyi Yu, Xinqian Ma, Feifei Yang, Shuming Guo, Mengtong Jin, Ran Li, Wentao Ni, Zhancheng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWhile elevated blood eosinophil counts (BECs) are established biomarkers in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, their impact on lung function in general populations remains less defined. This multi-ethnicity study examined BEC-lung function associations across diverse populations and employed Mendelian randomization (MR) to explore causal mechanisms.Patients and methodsIntegrated cross-sectional analyses involved three cohorts: Hongtong (n=13,868), NHANES (n=11,833), UK Biobank (UKB; n=428,610). Longitudinal assessment used UKB data (n=38,532). Multivariable regression assessed BEC associations with FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC, and PEF. Linear mixed-effects models estimated longitudinal changes by baseline BEC. Two-sample MR used European/Asian genetic instruments. Subgroup analyses assessed age, sex, disease status, and smoking effects.ResultsBECs consistently showed inverse associations with FEV1 across cohorts, strongest in UKB (266.22 mL and 171.95 mL FEV1 reduction per 1×109 cells/L BEC increase in males and females, respectively). Associations with FVC, FEV1/FVC, and PEF were most pronounced in UKB, whereas significant associations were absent for FVC in Hongtong and NHANES males. Longitudinally, higher baseline BECs predicted accelerated lung function decline, especially in males. Subgroup analyses revealed intensified negative associations in smokers and individuals >45 years. Importantly, these inverse associations persisted in sensitivity analyses excluding individuals with respiratory diseases. MR showed significant association in all traits in Europeans (P<0.05) but only for FEV1/FVC in Asians.ConclusionBECs show a significant and independent association with lung function impairment at the population level. MR analyses provide supportive evidence for a potential causal role, identifying distinct patterns of airway obstruction in Asian populations versus broader lung function deficits in Europeans. Variations by sex, ancestry, age, and smoking underscore demographic considerations in eosinophil-related respiratory health. These findings suggest the potential utility of monitoring BECs in asymptomatic individuals and warrant further investigation into personalized prevention strategies.

Introduction

Lung function serves as a pivotal biomarker of respiratory health, encapsulating the integrated physiological capacity of both airways and pulmonary parenchyma. While its gradual decline is an established hallmark of aging and a predictor of population-wide morbidity, emerging evidence underscores that accelerated deterioration often stems from subclinical inflammatory processes rather than overt disease manifestations alone.1–3 Among inflammatory mediators, eosinophils-classically linked to parasitic immunity and allergic responses-have gained recognition as accessible markers of systemic and respiratory inflammation.4,5 Although their clinical significance in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is well-characterized,6–8 recent studies suggest eosinophils may be associated with lung function trajectories, even in individuals without chronic respiratory conditions.9,10 Notably, elevated blood eosinophil counts (BECs) have been associated with reduced forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and elevated fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) across both healthy and diseased populations, independent of smoking status or baseline lung function.3,7,10–12

Despite these advances, critical knowledge gaps persist. First, existing cohort studies have predominantly examined homogenous populations (eg, US or South Korean), limiting the generalizability of findings to other ethnic groups and environmental contexts. Second, the causal relationship between BECs and lung function decline remains undefined due to the inherent confounding in observational designs. Third, the assessment of pulmonary function has often been narrowly focused on FEV1, overlooking dynamic measures such as peak expiratory flow (PEF) or forced vital capacity (FVC), and potential heterogeneity across sex and age subgroups.

To directly address these gaps, this study adopts a comprehensive analytical approach for the multi-cohort observational analyses. We leveraged data from three distinct cohorts: the Hongtong County cohort (representing a rural East Asian population), the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, representing a diverse US population), and the UK Biobank (UKB, a large-scale European cohort). Then we conducted independent, parallel analyses within each cohort, which allows us to test the robustness of eosinophil-lung function associations across diverse genetic backgrounds and environmental exposures.

Furthermore, to overcome the limitations of observational inference regarding causality, we employed Mendelian Randomization (MR) to assess potential causal directionality. MR has emerged as a pivotal tool in public health research, enabling the inference of causal relationships between exposures—such as blood eosinophil counts—and health outcomes like lung function by using genetic variants as instrumental variables, which helps mitigate biases from confounding and reverse causation inherent in traditional observational epidemiology.13 By integrating cross-ethnicity epidemiological data with genetic causal inference, this study aims to clarify the role of eosinophils in pulmonary function decline and inform targeted preventive strategies across diverse demographics.

Methods

Data Source and Study Design

To investigate chronic respiratory symptoms and diseases in rural Chinese populations, we conducted a comprehensive survey in Hongtong County, Shanxi Province, using a cluster sampling approach from April 2021 to July 2024. Participants ≥18 years, without recent fever/acute respiratory illness/hospitalization were enrolled, and inability to complete surveys (due to advanced age, or poor compliance, et al), or questionable data quality (including excessive missing data, key demographic information missing, inattentive responding, or implausible outliers) were excluded. Ultimately, 269 out of 325 villages (83% coverage) were covered and 26,480 participants were enrolled. We finished the survey covering (1) demographic information, specifically age and gender, was obtained directly from participants’ Resident Identity Cards using electronic readers; (2) symptoms, past medical history and exposure history (smoking/dust/drink) was independently completed by a self-administered online questionnaire; (3) anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, heart rate, and blood pressure were performed on-site by trained staff; (4) peripheral blood samples were collected for complete blood count tests; and (5) pulmonary function was assessed via standard spirometry, recording parameters such as FVC, FEV1, and FEV1/FVC. This survey was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Linfen Central Hospital, China (Approval No. 2021-1-1).

In addition to Hongtong County cohort, we integrated data from another two sources: the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2012,14 and UK Biobank.15 NHANES, conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), represents a cross-sectional survey employing multistage probability sampling to assess US population health.14 For this analysis, we extracted data encompassing demographics, comorbidities, history of smoking and alcohol exposure, peripheral blood cell count-related laboratory results, and pulmonary function outcomes from the NHANES datasets. The NHANES was approved by the Institutional Review Board of CDC/NCHS. Also, our study used the data from the UK Biobank (Application ID 688295), a population-based prospective cohort that recruited >500,000 participants aged 40–69 years across 22 assessment centers in the UK between 2006 and 2010. The cohort employed standardized protocols to collect comprehensive baseline data, including spirometry (following ATS/ERS guidelines using Vitalograph devices), physical examinations, questionnaires, and blood test.15 The data at baseline and the follow-up spirometry results from 2014 assessment cycle were used in this study. We extracted data encompassing the same variables as those covered in the NHANES. We restricted the longitudinal analysis to participants who had valid lung function measurements and covariate data at both the baseline and the repeat assessment visits (N = 38,532). The UK Biobank has approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee.

After excluding participants with hematological malignancies or missing data (BEC, or lung functions), final samples included 13,868 (Hongtong), 11,833 (NHANES), and 428,610 (UK BioBank) participants (Figure 1A). Measurement of Lung Function and BECs

Lung function assessment protocols varied by cohort: Hongtong cohort utilized portable Sunmi V2 devices (Guangzhou Hongxiang Medical Technology Co., Ltd. Respiratory Home) (2–3 repeats, highest values kept).16 NHANES used Ohio 822/827 spirometers (grades A–C retained per ATS/ERS standards);17 UK Biobank employed Vitalograph Pneumotrac 6800 devices (two acceptable maneuvers, ≤5% FEV1/FVC variability); Participants who used an inhaler within an hour prior to spirometry testing were excluded.18

BECs were measured using Sysmex XN-10[B3] analyzers (Hongtong cohort), and Beckman Coulter HMX (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA) (NHANES/UK Biobank), reporting both percentage and absolute count. The normal reference ranges in Hongtong cohort are 0.02–0.52 * 109 cells/L for absolute count and 0.4%–8.0% for percentage, based on the industry standards published by Chinese government.19 The determination of normal reference values for eosinophils in NHANES and the UK Biobank is based on the guidelines set by the American Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute CLSI (EP28-A3c).20 0.50 * 109 cells/L for absolute count and 5.0% for percentage was defined as hypereosinophilia.21

Statistical Analysis

Normality of variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (normally distributed) or median (IQR) (non-normally distributed), and compared using ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared by χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable linear regression was used to examine associations between BECs and lung function, adjusted for age, race, smoking/drinking status, body mass index (BMI), and comorbidities. Subgroup analyses were stratified by respiratory disease status, age (>45 years), and smoking status. Multivariable linear regression analysis was adjusted for age, race, smoking, or drinking status, BMI, and underlying diseases (coronary heart disease, stroke, angina, congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, COPD, chronic bronchitis, asthma, and emphysema). Besides, smooth curve fitting was performed to investigate the nonlinear associations between BECs and lung functions. Linear mixed effects model (LMM) was performed based on the UK Biobank repeat-measured data, to identify predictors of BECs, with the fixed effect for year (duration of the observation period) and a random effect for individuals to accommodate repeated measurements. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, http://www.R-project.org) and the Free Statistics analysis platform (version 2.0, Beijing, China). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Mendelian Randomization Analysis

We conducted a comprehensive two-sample MR analysis to investigate the causal relationship between BECs and lung function parameters (FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC ratio, and PEF). Our MR analysis was based on three core assumptions for causal inference: (1) the selected genetic variants are strongly associated with BECs (our exposure of interest); (2) there are no unmeasured confounders affecting both the genetic variants and lung function outcomes; and (3) the genetic variants influence lung function exclusively through their effects on BECs (horizontal pleiotropy is absent) (Figure 1B).

Instrumental variables (IVs) were selected from recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of European and Asian populations. Exposure data were derived from: Biobank Japan (East Asian)22 and an European-ancestry meta-analysis (n=474,237);23 while outcome data incorporated spirometry metrics from Taiwan Biobank,24 UK Biobank,25 and another European meta-analysis (n=475,645)26 (Supplementary Table S1). Exposure and outcome data derived from different cohorts with no disease condition excluded; these independent data sources minimized overlap. Stringent criteria including genome-wide significance (p < 5×e−8), linkage disequilibrium clumping (R2<0.001 within 10,000 kb), and F-statistics >10 were applied to ensure strong instruments.27 To assess the robustness of the MR findings and detect potential horizontal pleiotropy, we performed sensitivity analyses using MR-Egger regression, the Weighted Median method, and the MR-PRESSO. The GWAS for both exposures and outcomes typically adjusted for key covariates such as age, sex, and genotyping batch; however, as we relied on publicly available summary statistics, the set of covariates was determined by the original GWAS studies. As our study involved independent MR analyses performed within each dataset separately, we did not enforce consistency requirements or make adjustments for baseline characteristics and covariates across the datasets. All items with key values missing were excluded. Absolute risk translation and bidirectional MR was not applicable as all outcomes were continuous lung function parameters. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. This analysis was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Linfen Central Hospital, China (Approval No. 2021-1-1). This study was conducted and reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) guideline (https://www.strobe-mr.org/).

Lung function parameters demonstrated consistent gradients across cohorts. NHANES participants exhibited the highest median values for FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC ratio, and PEF. Relationships of BECs with Lung Function in Cross-Sectional Study

Cross-sectional analyses demonstrated varying degrees of inverse associations between BECs and lung function parameters across the three cohorts. While UK Biobank showed robust associations, effects in Hongtong and NHANES were more modest and inconsistent. When considering the whole sample, elevated BECs were associated with a lower FEV1 and FEV1/FVC in all cohorts in the adjusted model (Supplementary Table S2). When stratified with gender, multivariable-adjusted models revealed that each 1×109 cells/L increase in BECs corresponded to FEV1 reductions of 266.22 mL (95% CI: 245.6–286.84) in UK Biobank males and 171.95 mL (157.54–186.36) in females. In contrast, attenuated effects were observed in NHANES males (β=−137.31, P=0.013) and Hongtong (males: β=−143.41, P=0.026; females: β=−110.62, P=0.011) (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S3). FEV1/FVC ratios showed comparable negative associations, though Hongtong females became non-significant after adjustment. Regarding FVC, associations were significant in UK Biobank and NHANES females but did not reach statistical significance in males from the Hongtong or NHANES cohorts. PEF demonstrated the most pronounced reductions (>800 mL/s) in UK Biobank and Hongtong males (both P<0.05). The effects of BEC on decreasing lung functions were enhanced among males than females in UK Biobank and Hongtong cohorts. Smoothing curve analyses revealed nonlinear negative association between FEV1 and BECs in Hongtong cohort, with sex-specific patterns for FVC in males and FEV1/FVC in females (Figure 2). NHANES exhibited consistent nonlinear negative associations for all lung function parameters. Longitudinal Results from the UK BioBank Cohort

Our longitudinal analyses were restricted to a subset of 38,532 UK Biobank participants who completed both baseline and follow-up assessments, representing a “survivor” sub-cohort with complete data. In this group, elevated baseline BECs were significantly associated with declines in male follow-up lung function, showing adjusted reductions of −207.42 mL (95% CI: −415.13 to −271.12) for FEV1, −173.47 mL (−256.44 to −90.51) for FVC, −2.14% (−3.02 to −1.26) for FEV1/FVC ratio, and −345.29 mL/s (−566.79 to −123.78) for PEF (Table 3). While in females, significant BEC-associated declines were observed for all parameters except PEF. After stratifying by gender, the LMM showed that higher BECs were significantly associated with a faster decline in FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC during the observation. The association was stronger in males than in females. Similarly, the association between BECs and change in PEF in females was not statistically significant (Supplementary Table S4).

Subgroup Analysis

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we performed comprehensive subgroup analyses stratified by age, disease status, and smoking exposure, which revealed significant variations in the associations between BECs and lung function parameters. Notably, while the UK Biobank cohort maintained consistent negative associations between BECs and all four lung function parameters (FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC ratio, and PEF) across subgroups, we observed age-dependent attenuation of these associations in NHANES males >45 years following covariate adjustment (P>0.05, Figure S1). Intriguingly, the Hongtong cohort demonstrated strengthened BEC-FEV1/FVC associations specifically in males >45 years, suggesting potential age–sex interactions. Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with respiratory diseases revealed persistent BEC-lung function associations in UK Biobank but loss of significance for both FEV1 and FEV1/FVC in Hongtong males, suggesting disease-mediated modulation in this group (Figure S2). Smoking-stratified analyses showed robust negative correlations in across all parameters in UK Biobank smokers or non-smokers (Figures S3 and 4). For non-smokers, smoking intensifies the negative correlations between BECs and lung function parameters in UK Biobank. While Hongtong smoking males exhibited selective FEV1 and FEV1/FVC reductions (Figure S3). These findings collectively demonstrate that BEC-lung function relationships are dynamically modified by age, underlying respiratory conditions, and smoking exposure, with important geographic and gender variations that underscore the need for population-specific evaluation of eosinophil-related pulmonary impairment.

MR Analyses of BECs and Lung Function

Our MR analysis incorporated 39 SNPs as IVs for assessing BEC associations with FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC in the Asian population. For European populations, we included 427, 431, 419, and 429 SNPs in analyses of BEC associations with FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC, and PEF, respectively (Figure 1B). In the European population, the IVW method revealed robust causal associations between genetically predicted higher BECs and impairment across all assessed lung function parameters. Specifically, elevated BECs were causally linked to reduced FEV1 (β=−0.047, P=4.22×10−8), FVC (β=−0.026, P=0.002), FEV1/FVC ratio (β=−0.056, P=4.39×10−10), and PEF (β=−0.042, P=4.04×10−7). These findings were corroborated by the Weighted Median method, which yielded consistent effect estimates (P<0.001 for all traits except FVC). Although Cochran’s Q test indicated significant heterogeneity among IVs (P<0.05), the MR-Egger intercept term did not detect significant horizontal pleiotropy for most traits (Intercept P>0.05), reinforcing the validity of the causal inference (Figure 3 and Supplementary Table S5). In contrast, the Asian population exhibited a distinct, trait-specific causal pattern. We identified a significant causal effect of higher BECs on airflow obstruction, as evidenced by a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio (β=−0.045, P=0.025). This association was robust in the Weighted Median analysis (β=−0.068, P=0.017) and showed no evidence of heterogeneity (PCochran’s Q = 0.453) or horizontal pleiotropy (PMR-Egger Intercept = 0.828). However, no significant causal associations were observed for lung volumes, including FEV1 (β=−0.006, P=0.703) and FVC (β=0.013, P=0.530). Sensitivity analyses using MR-PRESSO detected no significant outliers driving the FEV1/FVC association in Asians (P=0.472). Collectively, these MR results provide strong evidence that eosinophils are a causal driver of broad lung function decline in Europeans, whereas in Asians, their causal impact appears specifically localized to airway obstruction pathways.

Discussion

Our study provided compelling evidence linking elevated BECs to accelerated lung function decline across diverse populations. These findings significantly extended current understanding of eosinophil-mediated pulmonary function decline in general populations, unlike previous studies that focused primarily on clinical populations, single race or, limited lung function measures.6,9,12 While observational data establish strong correlations, our Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis provides supportive evidence for a potential causal link between BECs and lung function impairment. Rather than immediate therapeutic prescriptions, these results suggest the potential utility of BECs as a readily accessible marker for respiratory risk assessment in the general population.

We observed consistent negative associations between BECs and all four major lung function parameters (FEV1, and FEV1/FVC) in the natural US, European, and Chinese cohorts through observational study. However, heterogeneity was noted regarding FVC, which showed no significant correlation in the Chinese cohort or NHANES males (Table 2). This aligns with the mixed results in earlier literature. Hancox et al reported that higher BECs predicted FEV1 decline in both asthmatic and non-asthmatic adults of New Zealand/European race.8 Similar associations were observed between BECs and FEV1, FEV1/vital capacity in European cohorts.28 But in previous reported NHANES III study, there was no association between BEC and FEV1, FVC.29 Wu et al described the independent negative associations between eosinophils and FEV1, FVC in the NHANES 2011–2012 and Dongfeng-Tongji cohort from China.30 These inconsistencies likely reflect distinct population characteristics, such as environmental exposures or variations in spirometry protocols, underscoring the necessity of our multi-cohort design to distinguish universal associations from population-specific effects. Our longitudinal analysis of the UK Biobank further confirmed that higher baseline BECs are associated with a steeper trajectory of lung function decline (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S6), consistent with findings in other international cohorts. A key finding of our study is the divergence in causal patterns between ethnicities revealed by MR analysis. While MR in Europeans indicated that elevated BECs causally impair overall lung volumes (FEV1, FVC, and PEF), the causal effect in the Asian population was specifically restricted to the FEV1/FVC ratio (Figure 3). Thus, in the Asian population, eosinophil-mediated inflammation appears to be related to airway obstruction rather than restrictive defects. This finding aligns with the MR-PRESSO sensitivity analysis, which confirmed the robustness of the FEV1/FVC association in Asians (P=0.472 for global test), ruling out pleiotropic bias. Previous review summarized a wide range of proposed explanations for the observed population differences,33 including inherent, anthropometric, and environmental differences. Multiple large-scale genetic studies demonstrate significant ethnic differences in lung function determinants. Annah et al identified novel locis which are specific for European, African, Asian ancestry, and found that some locis have different effects in different races. For example, rs7899503 in JMJD1C was significantly associated with FEV1 in the meta-analysis (β = 21.16 mL, P = 8.70 × 10−14), but European showed weaker effects than Asian (β: 17.35 vs 28.29 mL).34 These findings underscore a critical consideration for both clinical and basic research on pulmonary function: ethnic/racial differences must be systematically accounted for in study design and interpretation.

When stratified by age, participants in the older age group had more significant declines in lung functions. Aging exerts profound effects on pulmonary physiology through mechanisms including reduced chest wall compliance and small airway dysfunction.35–37 The intensified association in older adults suggests that eosinophilic inflammation may interact with age-related structural remodeling and immunosenescence, potentially accelerating functional loss in lungs with diminished repair capacity.38

While our study did not measure specific inflammatory biomarkers, existing literature provides a plausible mechanistic framework for our findings. Increasing studies revealed that eosinophils contributed to a variety of roles in both health and disease.39–42 Based on comprehensive analysis, the reasons for airflow limitation caused by eosinophils may include the following aspects: 1) direct epithelial injury via cytotoxic granule proteins (major basic protein/major basic protein/eosinophil derived neurotoxin) that disrupt mucosal barriers and expose sensory nerves;43 2) sustained inflammation and mucus hypersecretion through autocrine loops of IL-5/IL-13, and chemokine networks (eg, CCL6/15/23-CCR1) that recruit additional eosinophils;44,45 and 3) structural remodeling mediated by TGF-β-induced fibroblast activation (subepithelial fibrosis).46 However, in the absence of direct biomarker data (eg, IgE, FeNO) in our cohorts, these specific pathways remain hypothetical. Our results primarily establish the epidemiological link, suggesting the possibility that systemic eosinophilia serves as a potential driver of subclinical respiratory impairment.

Several limitations should be considered. First, while we adjusted for key sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as specific environmental exposures or detailed atopy markers cannot be ruled out due to data non-availability in some cohorts. However, excluding participants with known respiratory diseases partially mitigates confounding by medication use. Second, the lack of direct inflammatory biomarkers (eg, IgE, FeNO) in some cohorts limits our ability to dissect specific biological mechanisms or distinguish atopic form non-atopic phenotypes. Thus, our findings should be interpreted as associations rather than definitive mechanistic proof. Third, although MR helps infer causality, the result remains a possibility. Finally, our Asian population sample was relatively small, and the longitudinal data was lacked, warranting confirmation in larger studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that elevated blood eosinophil counts (BECs) are independently associated with lung function impairment across diverse populations. Mendelian randomization analyses provide supportive evidence for a potential causal role, identifying distinct patterns of airway obstruction in Asian populations versus broader lung function deficits in Europeans. These findings extend current understanding of eosinophil-associated respiratory health, while the observed heterogeneity across cohorts highlights the importance of accounting for demographic and genetic contexts. Future research is warranted to elucidate the biological mechanisms driving these population differences and to evaluate whether monitoring BECs in asymptomatic individuals offers clinical utility for early risk stratification.
