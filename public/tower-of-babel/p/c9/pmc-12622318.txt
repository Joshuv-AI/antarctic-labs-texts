Innate immuno-response to nanoparticle uptake in liver and spleen mimics pathogen infection
Authors: Bertha A Brodin, Giovanni M Saladino, Hans M Hertz, Marie Arsenian-Henriksson, Muhammet S Toprak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSystemically administered nanoparticles (NPs) designed for biomedical applications are retained in liver and spleen where they become rapidly phagocyted by tissue macrophages leading to inflammation.MethodsTo gain insight into the NP-immune cell interaction in liver spleen and lungs, we followed the distribution of molybdenum nanoparticles (MoNPs) in vivo by X-Ray Fluorescence Imaging (XRF) and examined the NP-macrophage interaction and physiological response in these organs.ResultsXRF imaging showed that intravenously administered MoNPs transiently accumulate in lungs, liver, and spleen. This leads to increments in the number of Kupffer cells (KC), natural killer (NK) cells, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Macrophage phenotype switched from pro- to an anti-inflammatory. In parallel genes with immunoregulatory and cytoprotective functions were expressed to maintain homeostasis. Nanoparticle uptake in spleen was operated by CD169/Siglec1 splenic macrophages indicating initiation of a secondary immune response. Silica coating reduced nanoparticle toxicity.ConclusionThe innate immunoresponse to NP uptake in liver and spleen is similar to viral or bacterial infections in these organs. A possible secondary immunoresponse to NPs can be primed in spleen aided by CD169/Siglec1 splenic macrophages. Silica coating of metal NPs tunes down this response.KEYWORDS: Innate immunoresponse, liver immunology, spleen immunology, nanotoxicity, metal nanoparticles

Introduction

Nanoparticles (NPs) designed for diagnostics and as drug carriers can be chemically tailored to improve the likelihood of reaching the target tissues, however once in circulation they get opsonized by blood plasma components and inevitably will enter the liver through the portal vein. Depending on the physicochemical properties, such as size, surface composition, charge, and delivery route, NPs can be further distributed in other organs including, lung, spleen, kidney, bone, and soft tissues [1–3]. The liver however plays a major role [4].

The liver is a vital organ in the frontline of the body’s immune response to bacteria, viruses, and toxic molecules. It is strategically positioned in the body, flooded by the major portal vein and artery, and organized in a tissue structure that maximizes the contact of immune cells with pathogens and toxins. It efficiently filters the blood and constitutes a major homeostasis and immune organ where nutrients and self-molecules are tolerated while pathogens and toxic material, including NPs, are rapidly intercepted and cleared from the body [5]. Through the portal vein, the liver is functionally connected to the spleen: the major secondary lymphoid organ in the body. The spleen has a unique tissue structure arranged in two major compartments: the red pulp with a microvascular structure that functions as a mechanical filter for the blood-borne pathogens and molecules, aged erythrocytes and platelets, circulating drugs and nanomedicines [6], and the white pulp, rich in lymphoid cells organized in B and T-cell follicles [7]. Many leukocytes with innate functions, including neutrophils, monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), gamma delta (γδ) T cells, and macrophages reside in the red pulp [8]. In murine spleen but not in humans, the white and red pulp boundary is well delineated by an area rich in memory B-cells and macrophages called the marginal zone.

The first-in-line innate immune response in the liver is carried-out by its permanent and large macrophage armament, the Kupffer cells (KCs). These unique macrophages are intimately involved in the body response to infections, intoxication, and hepatic injury, since they get constantly exposed to gut-derived pathogens, bacterial endotoxins, and other toxins that may enter the liver. Kupffer cells recognize “danger” signals on the surface of infectious microorganisms referred to as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and danger signals released from injured cells in non-infections conditions: damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [9].

Expectedly, systemically administered nanomedicines will be recognized as “danger” molecules (nanoparticle associated molecular pattern, NAMPs) by KCs and by other tissue macrophages [10–14] resulting in cytokine release, tissue inflammation, and oxidative stress [5,15], eliciting the innate immune response.

Besides the liver, NPs are also retained in the spleen and their uptake is operated by splenic macrophages localized in the marginal zone [16,17]. These macrophages intercept cargo through CD169/sialoadhesin/Sialec1, an adhesion molecule that recognizes sialylated glycans on the surface of bacteria [18]. This interaction is important for establishing a local immune response against pathogens, and toxic molecules. Although the spleen has been neglected as a vital organ, it has an important connection with the liver in modulating immunological functions [6].

The innate immune response plays a central role in the distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity of administered bio-nanomaterials. The present study follows this response in liver, spleen, and lungs after NP administration across time and unveils the similarity of this response to pathogen infections in these organs and a new role for splenic macrophages in boosting an adaptative immunoresponse to NP exposure.

Materials and methods

Nanoparticle synthesis

Molybdenum oxide (MoO2) NPs (Mo-NPs) were synthesized with a solvothermal method and subsequently coated with a dye-doped (Cy5.5) silica (SiO2) shell to obtain MoSi-NPs as previously reported [19]. Briefly, ammonium heptamolybdate – AHM (0.356 g) was dissolved in Milli-Q water (56 mL) and ethanol-EtOH (24 mL) in a Teflon holder, into which polyvinylpyrrolidone-PVP (1.189 g), was added under stirring. The Teflon holder was then enclosed in a stainless-steel autoclave and heated to 180°C maintaining there for 18 h. The mixture was centrifuged and washed with DI water after cooling down to room temperature. The black precipitate (MoO2 NPs) was finally collected, re-dispersed in DI water, and stored at 4°C until further use. The fluorescent dye Cy5.5 was integrated into silica through a one-pot method [20]. Briefly, Cy5.5-NHS (1 mg), Et3N (0.2 μL), DMSO (50 μL), and APTES (0.3 μL) were added and stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The final product, Cy5.5-APTES, was stored at 4°C until further use without any further purification. The silica coating was performed via a modified Stober process, in which TEOS (10 μL) was added dropwise into a mixture of EtOH and DI water (EtOH:H2O 3.75:1), MoO2 NPs (2.85 mg). The dispersion was stirred for 30 min, then ethanolamine-EA (200 μL), was added dropwise to initiate the reaction, immediately followed by the addition of Cy5.5-APTES (1 μL). The mixture was stirred for 2 h at room temperature in a dark environment. The product was centrifuged and washed with EtOH and DI water. The final material, silica shell – MoO2 core NPs, was dispersed in DI water and used for the subsequent steps. The NP size and other physicochemical characteristics are presented in Figure 1.

Macrophage-NP live imaging

The LRRK2 knocked out murine macrophage cell line SC-6004 (ATCC® TIB-71™) created from the parental line RAW264.7 was used to study NP-macrophage interaction in vitro.

Cells were plated in chamber slides at a concentration of 20 000 cells/well and incubated overnight to allow the cells attach to the chamber slide surface. The cells were then incubated with the NPs for 10 minutes, 6 h, and 24 h. Prior to live imaging, the excess of NPs were washed out with PBS and the cells were then incubated with NucBluetm Live Ready Probes and CellMask green actin tracking stains to visualize the nucleus and actin filaments by live imaging using an EVOS 5000 Imaging System (Thermofisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Animal studies

Fully immunocompetent seven-week-old BALB/c female mice were obtained from Janvier Labs (Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France) and kept under controlled temperature (21 ± 1°C) and humidity (55 ± 5%) conditions. Mice (8 per group) were injected intravenously with 100 μL isotonic suspension containing either Mo-NPs or MoSi-NPs both NPs at a concentration of 20 mg Mo/kg. Uninjected mice (n = 4) were used as controls.

The mice receiving MoSi-NPs were imaged 1 h, 24 h, and 1 week after NPs administration for whole body tracking of nanoparticle distribution. The mice were terminated after 24 hr (n = 4) or 1 week (n = 4) XRF imaging session and the major organs were collected for immunohistochemistry and molecular analysis.

In vivo X-ray fluorescence projection imaging

In vivo XRF projection images were acquired using in house built liquid jet X-ray fluorescence imaging system as previously described [21]. Whole body images were acquired from NP injected, anesthetized, and immobilized mice at 1 h, 24 h, and 1 week post NP injection. Of the eight NP-injected mice, four mice were euthanized at 24 h and the other 4 mice one week after NP injection, and their liver, spleen and lungs were collected after euthanasia for further examinations.

Histochemistry and Immunofluorescence

The organs collected were fixed in PBS buffered 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, pH 7) for 24 h, and thereafter transferred into 70% ethanol, before paraffin embedding (FFPE) and sectioning. All tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated before staining and subjected to antigen retrieval using citrate buffer pH 5.5 if stained with antibodies. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed using an automized standard protocol. The H&E-stained sections were scanned for digital morphological examination using the Q-Path web platform (Cambridge, UK).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of tissue macrophages and NK cells was performed using the following antibodies: rat monoclonal antibody F4-80 (clone BM8, Invitrogen, Waltham, MS, USA) at 1:50 dilution; rabbit anti CD163 antibody (EPR19518 Abcam Cambridge, UK) at 1:200 dilution; rabbit anti CD107a/LAMP1 antibody EPR21026 (Abcam Cambridge, UK) 1:200 dilution; and rabbit anti sialoadhesin/CD169 antibody (EPR27102-11) 1:100 dilution. The sections were incubated with the antibodies overnight. After antibody removal, and three consecutive washes with Tris-Borate Saline solution (TBS), the sections were incubated with either Alexa-488, Alexa-555 or Alexa 350 conjugated secondary antibodies. Thereafter, the tissue sections were washed three times with TBS and tissue autofluorescence reduced using the Tissue Autofluorescence Quenching Kit- ReadyProbes™ (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) before mounting in Prolong-Gold (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Immunofluorescence images were obtained using a Nikon CrEST spin confocal microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Gene expression studies

Representative fresh liver, spleen, and lung tissue were collected (prior formalin fixation) in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and cryopreserved for RNA isolation and quantitative gene expression. RNA was isolated from fresh tissue biopsies using the RNA isolation kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to manufacturer instructions. The quality and concentration of the RNA were evaluated using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies Santa Clara, CA, USA), prior to cDNA synthesis (AzuraQuantTM, Azura Genomics, Raynham, MA, USA). The expression of 29 genes associated with inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis was evaluated (in triplicates) in liver, spleen, and lungs from control mice, Mo-NP injected, and MoSi-NP injected mice, by quantitative RT-PCR using AzuraView GreenFast TM qPCR reagents (Azura Genomics, Raynham, MA, USA) and a custom-made PCR array (RealTimePrimers, Elkin Park, PA, USA). Gene expression was estimated using the Livak method as follows: First, ΔCT values were calculated by normalizing CT values for each gene to the average CT of three housekeeping genes (ß2 microglobulin, GAPDH, and ß-actin). ΔΔCT was calculated by normalizing the ΔCT values of the target gene (NP exposed sample) in relation to ΔCT values of the control (untreated sample). Quantitative gene expression was plotted as log2 fold change in accordance with the Livak method.

Results

We synthesized MoNPs with bimodal XRF and immunofluorescence imaging properties according to our previously protocol [22]. These particles are composed of a molybdenum core (MoNP), an element that emits fluorescence upon X-ray excitation; enabling in vivo XRF imaging; and a silica layer embedded with the fluorophore Cy5.5 (here referred as MoSiNPs) permitting the intracellular tracking of the particles in tissue sections by optical fluorescent microscopy [19,23] (Figure 1(A)). MoNPs and MoSiNPs were synthesized accordingly, and administered intravenously into immunocompetent mice. NP body distribution and clearance were imaged using XRF imaging, and the retention organs were excised post-mortem for gene expression and NP-cell interaction studies (Figure 1(B) and (C)).

We first exposed the murine macrophage cell line SC-6004 to MoSiNPs in vitro and followed the NP-macrophage interaction for 6 hours and 24 hours. The nanoparticles were internalized and gradually accumulated in the cytoplasm of the macrophages as shown in (Figure S1).

KCs represent 70% of the immune cell population in the liver [25]. Immunofluorescence staining using the F4/80 antibody showed a gradual increment in the number of Kupffer cells from 24 hours to one week (Figure 2(B), and S3). These macrophages expressed the hemoglobin scavenger receptor CD163 characteristic of macrophages responding to inflammation also known as M2 macrophages [26]. The number of CD163 positive macrophages increased in parallel to F4/80+ Kupffer cells (Figure 2(B), in orange) however, CD163+ macrophages were mostly observed surrounding the endothelial lining of portal veins at 24 hours and spread in the liver parenchyma one week after NP administration.

We then quantified the expression of 29 genes associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis (Table S1). MoNP uptake in liver induced acute oxidative stress and inflammation reaching a peak at 24 h and ending after one week in parallel to NP clearance from this organ (Figure 2(C)). The generation of oxidative stress was evident by the expression of the nuclear factor-erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), sulfiredoxin (Srxs1), nitric oxide (NO) synthases Nos1 and Nos2, and thioredoxins Trdx1 and Trdx2. The Nrf2 transcription factor is a master regulator of genes encoding several xenobiotic metabolizing enzymes activated in response to oxidative stress [27]. Nos1 and Nos2 induce NO synthesis and are important for hepatocyte function that protects the liver during sepsis and ischemia [28]. Thioredoxin is part of the disulfide reductase system in the liver [29], and Srxn1 is an antioxidant enzyme, important both for intermediate liver metabolism and detoxification.

Increments in the expression of genes encoding inflammatory cytokines such as Interleukin 1b (Il1b), tumor necrosis factor (Tnf), the heme oxigenase 1 (Hmox1) and the protease activated receptor 1 (Par1) were also evident but not statistical significant (Figure 2(C)). Tnf and Il1b are proinflammatory cytokines with central roles in tissue inflammation [30,31]Hmox1 is involved in erythrocyte turnover and bilirubin formation and is an essential component of the cytoprotective mechanism in stressed livers [32]. The protease activated receptor Par1, is a component of the coagulation cascade and drive profibrogenic events in hepatic stellate cells that can lead to hepatic fibrosis [33]. Inflammation and oxidative stress likely induced partial cell damage as observed by an increment in the expression of genes involved in DNA repair such as the Xrcc gene, and in apoptosis such as Atr, Atm, Bax, and Bcl2 (Figure 2(C)). These results are in accordance to our previous findings of an acute and transient liver stress response following the administration of ruthenium NPs [23].

Effects of NP retention on the physiology and innate immunoresponse in spleen

Despite not to the same extent as in liver, NPs are often retained in the spleen, the largest secondary lymphoid organ, functionally linked to the liver. The spleen removes timeworn red blood cells, and exerts important immunoregulatory functions to ensure pathogen eradication [6].

Consistently, circulating MoNPs were also retained in spleen (Figure 3(A)). The histological examination of tissue sections presented in Figure 3(B) shows the characteristic histological organization of the normal spleen consisting of white pulp (wp) with a central arteriole (a) and a highly organized B and T lymphocytes follicles and sheaths. Surrounding the wp areas is the red pulp (rp) in which splenic macrophages reside, and the red blood cells are eliminated while recycling iron [6]. In murine spleen, the interface between the white pulp and the red pulp is primarily composed of myeloid cells and referred to as the marginal zone. The spleen from mice exposed to MoNP displayed pronounced marginal zones (white arrows) and increased lymphoproliferative areas in the red pulp (yellow arrows) containing lymphocytes and mononuclear cells (inset, yellow arrows) indicative of extramedullary hematopoiesis (Figure 3(B)). Immunofluorescence staining showed increments in the F4/80 positive macrophage population spread on the red pulp and accumulated in the marginal zone as observed in HE stainings. These macrophages also expressed CD169/sialoadhesin that disitinguish the splenic marginal methalophilic macrophages, a unique macrophage population expressed in secondary lymphoid organs and involved in virus recognition (Figures 3(B) and S4).

MoNP retention in spleen induced transient oxidative stress and inflammation as determined by gene expression studies, however at lower fold levels than in the liver (Figures 3(C) and 5(B)).

Effects of NP retention on the physiology and innate immunoresponse in the lung

Besides liver and spleen, MoNP particles were detected by XRF imaging in lungs, an organ that with its branching structure and large alveolar epithelia provides an extensive surface for gas exchange and lung homeostasis. The pulmonary vasculature is constructed with a highly specialized endothelium that acts as a tight barrier, filters blood, and controls the interstitial flux of proteins and other molecules.

MoNPs transiently accumulated in lungs 1 hour after particle administration clearing gradually out after 24 hours as shown in Figure 4(A). In contrast to liver and spleen, oxidative stress and inflammation tend to increase with time. Although gene expression was evaluated in the lungs from two mice per time point, we observed increments of Gstm2, Scara2, Par1, and Hmox1 one week after NP administration (Figure 4(B)). The morphology of the lung tissue 24 hours after MoNP administration showed accumulation of inflammatory cells such as macrophages in the perialveolar areas (Figure 4(C), yellow arrows) as well as localized areas of microhaemorrhage (black arrow) characteristic of an active lung inflammatory process. Consistently, increments in the F4/80+ macrophage population were evident in the lungs 24 hours after NP administration. NP distribution and toxicity in relation to nanoparticle surface-coating

Surface coating of nanoparticles can be used to tune toxicity, colloidal stability, tissue targeting, and particle clearance [36]. Aiming to reduce MoNP toxicity, we coated the particles with silica doped with the fluorophore Cy5.5 (MoSiNPs). The incorporation of the fluorophore enabled the study of NP-host cell interactions in tissue sections using confocal immunofluorescent microscopy. MoSiNPs were intravenously injected to mice (n = 8) and their distribution followed by whole-body XRF imaging at 1 h, 24 h, and after 1 week. While Mo-NPs distributed in lungs, liver, and spleen, the silica coated particles evaded lung accumulation but were retained in liver and spleen (Figure 5(A)). Silica coating significantly reduced the toxicity of MoNPs in liver and spleen as determined by a decreased expression of oxidative stress, inflammation, and DNA damage-associated genes. It was also evident that this response to NP retention was superior in liver than in spleen (Figure 5(B)).

NP-macrophage interaction in liver and spleen

Liver and spleen sections were stained with antibodies recognizing the F4/80 antigen, the hemoglobin scavenger receptor CD163 expressed in macrophages with an alternative activated/M2 phenotype [26]; the adhesion molecule CD169/Sialoadhesin present in a subset of macrophages from secondary lymphoid organs [37]; and the CD107a/LAMP1, a marker for activated natural killer (NK) cells [38]

Nanoparticle detection in liver was restricted to the cytoplasm of CD163+ CD169+ macrophages indicating that NP uptake by Kupffer cells induces a switch from pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory/protective phenotype also referred to as M2 phenotype. Kupffer cells co-expressed F4/80 and CD169/sialoadhesin, a receptor involved in antigen presentation and crosslinks the innate with the adaptative immune response during viral infections [39]. In addition to Kupffer cells, increments in a LAMP1/CD107+ cell population with no NP intake were observed in the liver indicating the recruitment/activation of NK cells in response to NP retention (Figure 5(C)).

In spleen, MoSiNPs were localized within the cytoplasmic compartment of F4/80+ macrophages of the red pulp and accumulated in a subset of F4/80+ CD169+ macrophages at the marginal zone (Figure 5(C) low panel, white signals) indicating a possible antigen presentation process and subsequent activation of the adaptative immunoresponse.

Discussion

It is known that contrast agents-metal NP, such as gold and superparamagnetic iron oxide NPs, are retained in liver and spleen when administered in vivo. This sequestration is due to NP uptake by organ resident macrophages, resulting in liver inflammation and tissue damage [40–42]. NP phagocytosis by tissue macrophages has been demonstrated in liver and spleen tissue sections by confocal microscopy [13,17]. The present research adds new insights into the macrophage reprograming in liver and spleen coordinated with the expression of genes to resolve nanoparticle uptake and maintain body homeostasis, all together resembling the response to pathogen infection.

Using bimodal nanoparticles composed of a molybdenum core compatible for X-ray fluorescence imaging, and a silica shell in which a fluorophore has been covalently attached enabling optical fluorescence, we could follow NP biodistribution in living mice by whole body imaging and trace the NP-macrophage interaction in liver, spleen, and lung using confocal fluorescent microscopy. We further followed the evolution of NP macrophage interaction in these organs over a week period demonstrating that the response to NP uptake in liver and spleen mimics viral and/or bacterial infections in these organs.

Tissue macrophages such as the Kupffer cells in liver, the splenic macrophages and the alveolar macrophages of the lung are central in the innate immune response to NP uptake; in particular, the vast armament of Kupffer cells is central for clearing out pathogens and other hazardous molecules that may enter the blood stream, simultaneously balancing to keep body homeostasis [43].

We show that Mo-NP retention in the liver goes in parallel with increments in the KC population, likely due to de novo macrophage recruitment since in spite that Kupffer cells can divide locally in adult liver [44], under liver injury conditions, macrophages from the peritoneal cavity rapidly infiltrate the injury site increasing the macrophage pool to resolve inflammation [5]. Also, in conditions where macrophages are damaged or eradicated, circulating monocytes repopulate the liver and mature to acquire the properties of the KCs [44,45]. This homeostasis process is very important for example in the case of acute liver failure (ALF) and acute on chronic liver failure (ACLF) where the failure to resolve liver insult leads to systemic inflammation, immunosuppression, susceptibility to infections, and ultimately multi-organ failure [46,47].

The retention of MoNPs in liver induced an orderly immunological response that is similar to other hepatic inflammatory conditions [46,48,49], progressing in three phases [46]. In the initiation phase, KCs become activated after damage/pathogen associated molecular patterns (DAMPs/PAMPs) recognition (in this case NP-associated molecular pattern recognition NAMPs) and initiate a pro-inflammatory response. The second phase is propagation, in which monocytes are recruited to the liver and differentiated into inflammatory macrophages in parallel to the recruitment of other effector cells such as iNK cells, with an associated systemic release of cytokines and chemokines and hepatic tissue damage. A final resolution/tissue-repair phase in response to anti-inflammatory cytokines/mediators in which macrophages undergo functional reprogramming toward a pro-restorative (M2) phenotype, homeostasis, and tissue recovery. Consistently with the resolution/tissue repair phase, we showed that KCs reprogrammed toward a CD163+ phenotype following MoNPs phagocytosis. In parallel, genes encoding proteins with anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective functions such as the sulfiredoxin1 (Srxs1) and the heme oxigenase 1 (Hmox1) genes were up-regulated. The hemoglobin scavenger receptor expressed CD163 that is a major change in the macrophage switch from pro-inflammatory to immunoregulatory and cytoprotective type [50]. High CD163 expression on macrophages is characteristic of tissues responding to inflammation wound healing or tissue repair [26]. A soluble form of CD163 (sCD163) is normally excreted from tissue macrophages into the blood circulation, and its concentration increases during viral infections, and chronic or acute liver inflammation [51]. Indeed, sCD163 could be used as a plasma biomarker to monitor NP-induced liver inflammation.

Interestingly, tissue macrophages recruited in response to NP uptake co-expressed the CD169/Sialoadhesin/Siglec, a membrane protein expressed in myeloid cells from spleen, liver and lungs. Sialoadhesin recognizes sialilated glycans present in neutrophils, NK cells, subsets of T cells and in viruses, and plays an important role at the interphase between pathogen phagocytosis and subsequent antigen presentation to T-cell subsets [37]. This function is particularly featured during several viral infections such as during human immunodeficient virus (HIV) Zika virus, Dengue virus, and SARS-CoV-2 [39] infections. CD169 is particularly expressed in a subpopulation of splenic macrophages located at the marginal zone, the marginal metalophilic macrophages (MMM) [52]. Similarly, the retention of MoNP in spleen-induced accumulation of CD169 positive macrophages in the marginal zone, likely in cross talk with T cell subsets and establishing an acquired immune response to the particles. Supporting the role of CD169/Sialoadhesin/Siclec in nanoparticle recognition by splenic macrophages, it has been recently shown that carbon nanotubes are recognized by the aromatic residues of Siglec 14 [53].

The accumulation of NPs in lungs resulted in late inflammation with a subsequent formation of microhemorrage. Consistently, genes involved in heme metabolism such as the protease activated receptor Par1 and the heme cleaving enzyme Hmox1 were up-regulated in this organ. PARs mediate the interplay between coagulation, inflammation, and fibrosis by promoting endothelial-epithelial barrier disruption, inflammatory cell recruitment, and collagen deposition in response to lung injury, while Hmox1 is involved in the induction of ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death characterized by oxidative stress and redox imbalance.

The endothelium of the pulmonary vasculature forms at tight barrier to control the extravasation of proteins, and interstitial fluid homeostasis. It also serves to filter the blood, removing microemboli and other particulate matter. Infection and inflammation compromise the normal barrier homeostasis, resulting in increased permeability, protein and cellular extravasation, edema formation, and ultimately acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Long-term NP accumulation in the pulmonary vasculature may potentially cause similar damage. This toxicity can be tuned by modifying nanoparticle surface coating of the particles. Surface coating impacts colloidal stability, organ targeting, host immune response, and clearance [36]. Consistently, we showed that the coating of Mo-NPs with a silica shell not only inhibited accumulation in lungs but also reduced the toxic effects in liver and spleen, without affecting NP clearance rate.

Conclusions

NPs entering the liver, spleen, and lungs will be recognized as damage-associated-molecular pattern (DAMPs), and trigger an innate immune response similar to that elicited by other pathogens such as virus and bacteria. This orderly process is characterized by macrophage recruitment, NP phagocytosis by tissue resident macrophages, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Macrophages will then undergo a switch toward a regulatory and anti-inflammatory phenotype to maintain body homeostasis. This response is similar to that induced by pathogen infection in these organs. Nanoparticles can also be retained in spleen a major secondary immune organ, were they are processed by marginal metallophylic macrophages indicating potential boosting of an acquired immunoresponse to the NPs. Organ inflammation and oxidative stress caused NP uptake can be bypassed by biocompatible NP surface coating. On the other hand repetitive NP exposure in liver can lead to chronic inflammatory pathologies.
