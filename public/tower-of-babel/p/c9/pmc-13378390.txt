High resolution spatial profiling of the hematopoietic landscape of the murine lung
Authors: Nicholas J Skvir, Alexandra B Ysasi, Veronica Jarzabek, Anthony K Yeung, Jiaji G Chen, Ruben Dries, George J Murphy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOur current understanding of blood cell development stems primarily from adult bone marrow and the fetal liver. However, emerging evidence highlights the lung as a previously underappreciated residence for hematopoietic cells. Spatial in situ transcriptomics enables accurate mapping of cell identities and interactions within intact tissue, providing insights not accessible by dissociated single-cell profiling. Here, we present a high-resolution spatial transcriptomic atlas of the healthy adult murine lung, emphasizing the hemato-endothelial landscape. As a case study, we developed a workflow to identify and curate rare, often multinucleated, megakaryocytes, by binning spatial enrichment of canonical markers, expert curation, and correction for segmentation artifacts. We characterized the spatial neighborhoods of megakaryocytes within vascular, stromal, and immune microenvironments. We demonstrate the utility of this dataset for hypothesis-driven signaling studies by examining ligand-receptor interactions across canonical pathways. This resource defines the lung-blood niche and advances our understanding of the organ-specific properties of blood cells.Keywords: spatial transcriptomics, MERFISH, murine lung, megakaryocytes, hematopoiesis

Introduction

Hematopoiesis is a spatially and temporally dynamic process that has classically been ascribed to the bone marrow, which serves as the principal reservoir for hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and their downstream lineages. However, accumulating evidence demonstrates that hematopoietic activity extends well beyond the marrow and spleen, with the lung emerging as an important and previously underappreciated site of blood cell production and immune regulation. Recent discoveries have revealed that the murine lung harbors a surprisingly rich hematopoietic landscape, including resident populations of megakaryocytes (MKs), neutrophils, monocytes, dendritic cells, and innate lymphoid cells that together contribute to pulmonary immune surveillance and systemic hematopoietic function.1,2,3,4,5

Seminal intravital imaging and transgenesis studies have demonstrated that the lung microvasculature is a site of platelet biogenesis, with significant number of platelets generated from MKs actively residing in and trafficking through the pulmonary circulation, particularly following insult or injury.1,4,6 More recently, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has broadened this view by cataloging the heterogeneity of hematopoietic cells within the lung parenchyma, revealing context-dependent adaptations during infection, inflammation, and tissue repair.7,8,9,10 These studies underscore the concept that the lung is not only a passive filter for circulating cells but also a dynamic hematopoietic niche capable of shaping systemic immune and platelet homeostasis.11

Despite these advances, a critical limitation has been the ability to capture the spatial organization of hematopoietic cells within the highly complex lung microenvironment. Traditional approaches such as flow cytometry and scRNA-seq provide powerful single-cell resolution but require dissociated tissues, thereby eliminating information about tissue architecture, cellular neighborhoods, and cell-cell communication. Histologic methods can provide some spatial context but lack the multiplexing capacity required to interrogate diverse cell populations simultaneously. Consequently, our understanding of how hematopoietic cells are positioned in situ within the lung (relative to the vasculature, epithelium, and stromal compartments) remains incomplete.

Spatial transcriptomics technologies have recently transformed this landscape by enabling gene expression profiling while preserving tissue architecture.12,13 Among these, multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization (MERFISH) provides the highest detection sensitivity and available resolution, allowing single-molecule detection of hundreds to thousands of transcripts in intact tissue sections.14,15 When coupled with customized probe panels and advanced computational tools, MERFISH enables unprecedented opportunities to dissect the composition, spatial distribution, and functional interactions of rare and morphologically heterogeneous hematopoietic cells in complex tissues such as the lung.16

Here, we apply MERFISH through the Vizgen MERSCOPE platform to generate the highest resolution spatial transcriptomic map of the murine lung hematopoietic niche to date. We designed a custom 500-gene probe set that captures the broad cellular diversity of the pulmonary environment, with focused enrichment for hematopoietic subsets and immunoregulatory pathways. Importantly, we developed stringent analytic pipelines to identify and characterize lung-resident MKs, a rare and morphologically complex population that plays a central role in platelet generation and local immunoregulation. By integrating high-content imaging, custom segmentation approaches, and nearest-neighbor analyses, we delineate the spatial architecture and molecular signaling networks of the lung MK niche with unprecedented resolution. Together, these data establish a foundational resource for understanding the spatial dynamics of pulmonary hematopoiesis and provide a framework for investigating how this niche is remodeled in health and disease.

Results

High resolution overview of the hematopoietic, endothelial, and pulmonary landscapes of the adult murine lung

To establish a high-resolution reference map of the healthy murine lung, we generated the highest possible spatial transcriptomic resolution map of intact lung tissues by using MERFISH14,17 through the MERSCOPE platform. To accomplish this, we referenced previously published literature3,18 and scRNA datasets5,19 to guide the development of a custom 500 gene MERFISH probe set designed to maximally cover the complex lung niche composition and inherent biological variability. This included markers for different cell types and subtypes (i.e., pulmonary, hematopoietic/immune, endothelial, etc.) and further enrichment for genes involved in immunoregulatory processes, ligand-receptor (L/R) signaling, and developmental pathways. We profiled two independent tissue blocks with this approach; in total, 184,911 cells were captured across both samples (67,872 in sample A and 117,039 in sample B). Following quality control and reference-based annotation, the dataset resolved into 38 discrete cell types spanning a broad range of compartments of the adult lung parenchyma (Figure 1A). These included epithelial populations such as alveolar type 1 and 2 cells (AT1/AT2), ciliated, and Sox9+ epithelial cells; endothelial subsets including CAP1/EPC, CAP2 (“aerocyte” capillary), arterial endothelial cells (AEC), venous endothelial cells (VEC), and lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC); mesenchymal lineages including alveolar fibroblasts (AF1, AF2), secondary crest myofibroblasts (SCMF), and mesothelial cells; and a diverse array of immune and hematopoietic populations, including macrophages (alveolar and interstitial), dendritic cells (maDC), lymphocytes (ILC, T cells, B cells, natural killer [NK] cells, Tregs), erythroid cells, and MK/platelets. Canonical definitions and marker genes for each of these populations are provided by the LungMAP CellRef.9 This global view revealed detailed architecture of the adult lung, including conducting airways, alveolar regions, and interstitial niches, as well as clearly resolved mesothelium and lymphatic vasculature. Within conducting airways, ciliated epithelial cells were clearly resolved, surrounding the airway lumen (Figure 1B). In the adjacent interstitium, immune cell aggregates could be detected, illustrating the ability of MERSCOPE to localize hematopoietic populations with high spatial fidelity (Figure 1B, red arrow). Together, these results demonstrate that MERSCOPE, combined with a targeted transcript panel, can resolve both broad tissue architecture and rare hematopoietic niches in situ. This dataset thus serves as a valuable reference framework for interrogating murine lung biology in situ at the highest level of resolution.

Single cell deconstruction of MERFISH spatial data identifying 38 individual cell types

To classify the transcriptionally distinct populations within our dataset, we first applied standard Giotto20 workflows. Principal-component analysis (PCA) followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embedding and Leiden clustering yielded ∼17–19 clusters across both samples. While many peripheral clusters could be annotated based on canonical marker expression, a substantial fraction of cells fell into a large, central cluster, suggesting that Leiden clustering alone was insufficient to resolve the full diversity of cell types seen in the lung (Figure S2). To improve confidence in cell annotations, we leveraged the LungMAP murine single-cell Guo et al.9 (CellRef), which contains approximately 40 well-curated cell types from healthy mouse lung. We used Harmony21 integration to create a shared embedding space between the single-cell reference and spatial data followed by label transfer of annotated CellRef data to cell identities in the spatial data (Figure 2A). Using this refined approach, the MERFISH dataset resolved into nearly 40 discrete cell types spanning epithelial (AT1, AT2, ciliated, Sox9+), endothelial (CAP1/EPC, CAP2, arterial, venous, lymphatic), fibroblast/mesenchymal (AF1, AF2, SCMF, mesothelial), immune (myeloid, lymphoid), and hematopoietic (MK/platelet, erythroid) lineages (Figure 2B). Altogether, this CellRef-informed approach substantially improved the granularity of cell annotations compared to unsupervised clustering and was used as the foundation for all downstream analyses.

High resolution spatial profiling of the lung and MK niche

MKs are rare but biologically significant hematopoietic cells previously reported to reside in the lung.2,4,22 Because of their scarcity, large size, and transcriptional similarity to platelets, MKs could not be cleanly resolved using the initial default clustering approach. Hence, we developed a more explicit and semi-automatic pipeline to identify MKs with high confidence. First, the spatial transcriptomics data were rasterized using a “hex-binning” approach across our two datasets (detailed in the STAR Methods) with the goal to identify tiled regions showing enrichment of MKs based on six canonical MK marker genes (Gp1ba, Itga2b, Mpl, Pf4, Tubb1, Vwf). These enriched regions were then prioritized as potential candidate MK locations (Figure 3A) and further confirmed in the following manner. First, 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and cell boundary staining was used to identify characteristic MK morphology, including large, multinucleated cells (Figures 3B and 3C). Then, transcript overlays were used to visualize marker co-expression and provide final confirmation of MK identity. To correct for initial segmentation errors due to large, multinucleated, single MKs being split into multiple polygons, we manually corrected these cell boundaries where necessary, and used these corrected cell polygons for downstream analysis in the Giotto analysis framework (Figure 3D). This workflow enabled us to conservatively identify approximately eight genuine MKs across both samples, providing a foundation for downstream neighborhood analyses of the MK niche within the lung, while also underscoring the potential complexity in analysis of cell types with unique morphologies. Thus, we next examined the spatial context of MKs within the lung. Using our reference-based cell type labeling, we constructed a spatial network of all cells across both samples and performed proximity enrichment analysis, quantifying whether cell type pairs are globally over or under-represented as spatial neighbors relative to a randomized null distribution. Firstly, pairwise enrichment plots (Figure 4A) were generated for the whole lung, as well as corresponding heatmaps and cell proximity networks (Figure S3). Preliminary global neighborhood enrichment of the top 25 enriched and bottom 25 depleted cell type pairings identified both expected and unique relationships between lung cell types. To characterize this landscape, we quantified enrichment patterns across all 741 possible cell-cell interactions from our 38 cell types. Homotypic interactions were strongly overrepresented among significant spatial enrichments (35 enriched homotypic pairs out of 38 possible pairs, 92%) compared to heterotypic interactions (157/703, 22%; Fisher’s exact test, odds ratio, OR = 40.35, p < 2.2 × 10−16), indicating pronounced cell-type-specific clustering. These patterns likely reflect conserved tissue organization and lineage clustering. Additional enriched proximities included ciliated-deuterosomal pairs and classical dendritic cell subsets (cDC1-cDC2), consistent with developmental and functional links among these cell types, as well as structural associations such as AF2-mesothelial. In contrast, the most depleted interactions often involved secretory or ciliated epithelial cells paired with unrelated lineages (e.g., lymphatic endothelial, NK cells, AT1, etc.), underscoring the compartmentalization of airway-associated epithelia away from vascular, immune, and hematopoietic niches. Together, these findings reinforce expected features of lung organization while also highlighting less well-characterized associations. For example, enrichment of AF and mesothelial cells reflects a known connection, specifically in lung injury and disease.23,24 Similarly, proximity of dendritic cells to innate lymphoid cells and interstitial macrophages supports immune-immune cross-talk within interstitial niches. While some of these interactions align with known developmental or functional relationships (e.g., ciliated-deuterosomal, cDC1-cDC2), others may represent underappreciated aspects of stromal and immune organization revealed by spatial profiling. Looking more specifically at significant hematopoietic interactions (Figures S5 and S6), hematopoietic-hematopoietic interactions were also significantly enriched relative to all other interaction classes (60/136, 44% vs. 132/605, 22%; OR = 2.82, p = 2.6 × 10−7), consistent with preferential immune cell colocalization. Within hematopoietic interactions specifically, homotypic interactions predominated (15/16, 94%) over heterotypic hematopoietic interactions (45/120, 38%; OR = 24.5, p = 1.6 × 10−5), suggesting structured immune cell clustering rather than broad intermixing across hematopoietic lineages. For visualization purposes, minimum interaction thresholds (>5 interactions expected and observed) were applied to bar plots; however, statistical analyses were performed across all tested interaction pairs.

Next, to examine the local niches of our validated MKs specifically, we looked at the subset of cell proximity interactions in which they were involved. From this list of interactions, we generated bar plots showing proportions of cells in close proximity to MKs, binned by radial distance from centroids (Figures 4B and 4C). We did this at two levels, using both broad and more specific cell identities from our earlier reference-based labeling (see STAR Methods). At 10–20 μm, a mosaic of cell types emerged, including alveolar epithelium (AT1/AT2), capillary endothelial cells (CAP1/EPC, CAP2), and lymphocytes, with smaller contributions from fibroblasts, granulocytes, and mesenchymal populations. Beyond 20 μm, endothelial representation increased further, consistent with vascular association, while airway epithelium and myeloid cells appeared at low-to-moderate levels. CAP1/EPC progressively becomes the largest non-MK/platelet component, while AF1 remains a steady, medium-sized fraction; additional minor slivers (e.g., AM, cDC1, Sox9+ epithelium) appear without overtly displacing other types. Notably, AT1/AT2 together remain represented at all distances, indicating that MKs are consistently positioned within alveolar parenchyma—near, but not exclusively abutting, the epithelial surface. Taken together, these observations motivate a model in which pulmonary MKs are supported by overlapping endothelial, epithelial, stromal, and immune cues. Specifically, the persistent alveolar epithelial contribution suggests epithelial-derived factors may regulate MK maturation or platelet release, while endothelial enrichment is compatible with vascular positioning and potential platelet egress. Stromal and immune neighbors may provide additional trophic or regulatory inputs.

Examination of L/R interactions within the lung

To further examine modes of cellular communication within this dataset, we interrogated L/R signaling pathways represented in the custom panel. Using Giotto’s spatCellCellcom functionality to evaluate L/R co-expression among cells in spatial proximity, we generated a table of putative L/R interactions across all annotated cell types (Table S2). From this, we visualized a representative set of interactions in a dot plot (Figure 5A). Dot size corresponds to empirical significance, while color indicates relative fold-change enrichment compared to randomized null distributions. This analysis recapitulated several well-established signaling axes, including angiogenesis (Vegfa-Flt1/Kdr), chemokine signaling (Ccl2-Ccr2), bone morphogenic protein (BMP) pathways (Bmp4-Bmpr2), matrix-integrin interactions (Col1a1-Itga1), apoptosis (Fas-FasL), and interferon response (Ifna-Ifnar1).

To validate these statistical enrichments in situ, we visualized selected L/R pairs directly within the MERSCOPE images (Figures 5B–5E). Bmp4-Bmpr2 signaling showed broad receptor expression across epithelial, endothelial, and mural (pericyte, vascuar smooth muscle (VSMC)) compartments, with sparse stromal and epithelial ligand distribution suggesting locally constrained activation. Vegfa-Flt1 interactions involved both autocrine and paracrine configurations, with ligands and receptors co-localized in alveolar epithelium (predominantly AT2 and AT1/AT2 cells) and fibroblasts, with complementary expression at epithelial-endothelial interfaces. Vegfa expression was additionally detected in airway epithelial populations including ciliated and deuterosomal cells, consistent with a broader role for VEGF signaling in airway epithelial homeostasis beyond its canonical angiogenic function25 (Table S2). Col1a1-Itga1 highlighted stromal fibroblasts and immune cells as key collagen sources, with receptors enriched in epithelial and vascular mural cells, consistent with matrix-integrin signaling across stromal-epithelial and stromal-vascular boundaries. Together, these examples underscore the ability of spatial transcriptomics to resolve cross-compartment communication in the lung with both statistical and spatial fidelity. While not intended as a comprehensive atlas of lung signaling, these analyses demonstrate how this dataset can be mined to interrogate diverse pathways of interest.

Discussion

In this study, we present a high-resolution spatial transcriptomic map of the adult murine lung, generated using Vizgen MERSCOPE with a custom 500-gene panel. By combining two independent tissue blocks, we assembled a dataset of ∼185,000 cells spanning epithelial, endothelial, mesenchymal, immune, and hematopoietic compartments. Using reference-based annotation, nearly 40 discrete cell types were confidently identified. Our findings extend prior work that increasingly recognizes the lung as more than a passive site of gas exchange, highlighting instead its role as an active hematopoietic niche with contributions to platelet biogenesis and immune regulation. Previous studies have shown that MKs and their progenitors can traffic to the lung vasculature, where they contribute to circulating platelet pools and engage with endothelial and immune neighbors. By adding spatial resolution, our study refines this view, identifying specialized microenvironments in the alveolar parenchyma where MKs reside. These neighborhoods were layered: MKs were embedded within a background of alveolar epithelium, neighbored closely by capillary endothelium, and consistently accompanied by fibroblast/mesenchymal populations and low-level immune surveillance. This organization underscores that pulmonary MKs are supported by overlapping epithelial, vascular, stromal, and immune cues, echoing but also extending niche dynamics described in the bone marrow.

Beyond individual findings, the dataset itself represents a community resource. Prior scRNA-seq atlases, such as LungMAP, have provided invaluable reference frameworks for murine lung biology, but lack spatial information. By adding high-resolution transcript localization, this MERSCOPE dataset complements those resources and enables researchers to interrogate cell types, neighborhoods, and signaling pathways directly within their tissue context. We envision this dataset as a high-fidelity spatial map that others can mine to address diverse questions about lung organization and function. Clinically, this spatial framework provides context for understanding how pulmonary vascular injury, inflammation, or fibrosis might perturb hematopoietic dynamics. For example, disruption of endothelial or stromal support could alter platelet production, while immune remodeling of the interstitium could affect MK functionality and platelet release. More broadly, our dataset establishes a foundation for probing L/R networks at the lung-blood interface, opening avenues to identify therapeutic pathways that could be targeted to modulate thrombosis, immunity, or repair in cardiopulmonary disease. At the methodological level, this work illustrates strategies that can be generalized to other spatial studies. Hex-binning proved an effective strategy for locating rare cells when clustering alone was insufficient; projection of an external single-cell reference (LungMAP) substantially improved cell type annotations; and L/R analysis demonstrated how targeted panels can yield curated pathway-level insights, validated directly in situ. Importantly, the utility of such analyses depends critically on the thoughtful selection of ligands, receptors, and downstream effectors included in the gene panel. Inclusion of pathway-relevant genes, spanning not only L/R pairs but also intracellular effectors and transcriptional targets, maximizes the biological interpretability of spatially resolved signaling inference. Together, these approaches highlight how spatial transcriptomics bridges histological context with transcript-level detail, enabling both robust reference mapping and focused biological case studies.

Limitations of the study

The use of a targeted 500-gene panel, while providing the highest detection sensitivity and designed for breadth across lung and hematopoietic biology, necessarily restricts the scope of detectable signals relative to unbiased transcriptome-wide approaches. Label transfer-based annotation of highly transcriptionally similar cell populations may also be inherently limited by the resolution of the gene panel, thus occasional misclassification between similar populations cannot be excluded. For example, the observed spatial association between airway smooth muscle (ASMCs) and SCMFs in our proximity enrichment analysis may reflect, at least in part, an artifact where airway-associated myofibroblasts can be called as SCMFs due to their shared marker gene profiles, given that these populations occupy distinct niches within the lung. The small number of MKs identified (eight in this instance) limits statistical power for neighborhood comparisons, and our reliance on manual validation introduces subjectivity, though consensus review helped mitigate this. Additionally, while MERFISH immunostaining is technically feasible on some platforms, and would be informative for follow-up studies, it was not performed here in order to focus on achieving the best transcriptional data quality and to minimize additional experimental complexity in our early adoption of this technology. Furthermore, the tissue sections profiled represent transverse sections of the caudal right lung lobe, which, due to the maximum tissue area constraints of the MERSCOPE platform, precluded the use of sections capturing multiple lobes simultaneously. As a result, proximal airway domains are not represented in the current dataset, and differences in cell type distribution across lung regions and lobes (including potential variation in MK localization) cannot be excluded. Follow-up studies profiling multiple lobes and axial levels would provide a more comprehensive spatial atlas of the murine lung. Finally, the dataset represents healthy adult murine lung tissue and does not address variation across developmental or disease states as a stand-alone resource.

In conclusion, we provide both a high-resolution spatial reference of the murine lung and a case study of MK niche analysis. By demonstrating how curated workflows can resolve rare cell types and uncover spatially organized interactions, this work underscores the potential of MERSCOPE and related technologies to advance our understanding of tissue organization.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

Two male C57BL/6J mice aged 8–12 weeks were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664). Animals were housed under standard specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. Animals were naive to prior experimental procedures. All animal procedures were approved by the Boston University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) under protocol number PROTO201800151 and conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations. Only male mice were used in this study, therefore the influence of sex on results was not assessed.

Method details

Tissue isolation and processing

Mice were euthanized via isoflurane overdose followed by transcardial perfusion to flush blood from the pulmonary system. Lungs were inflated through the trachea using a 1:1 mixture of OCT and PBS. Lung lobes were bluntly dissected at the bronchi, flash frozen in OCT, and stored at −80°C. Cryosectioning and slide preparation was performed in accordance with Vizgen tissue preparation guidelines. Briefly, tissue blocks were equilibrated to −20°C for 30 min, then 10 μm sections were mounted on Vizgen MERSCOPE slides (Vizgen, Cat. 20400106) and re-frozen at −20°C for 30 min. Slides were then incubated in pre-warmed 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) fixation buffer (prepared using 32% PFA, 10× PBS and nuclease-free water) for 1 h at 37°C, washed 3× in 1× PBS (prepared using nuclease-free water), then incubated at 4°C overnight in 70% ethanol to permeabilize the tissue. Autofluorescence was quenched using a MERSCOPE photo bleacher (Vizgen, Cat. 10100003) for at least 3 h. Cell boundaries were stained using the MERSCOPE Cell Boundary Stain Kit (Vizgen, Cat. 10400118). Slides were then incubated with a custom gene panel mix at 37°C for 36–48 h according to the Vizgen Encoding Probe Hybridization Protocol. Gel embedding and clearing of the hybridized tissue was performed according to Vizgen Sample Prep Kit (Vizgen, Cat. 10400012) guidelines. Sample RNA quality was validated by extracting RNA from approximately 50 μm of sectioned tissue using an RNA 6000 Pico Kit (Agilent Technologies, Cat. 5067-1513) and analyzing with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Samples used for MERSCOPE analysis had a minimum RNA integrity number (RIN) of 8. Sample quality was further validated using the MERSCOPE Sample Verification Kit (Vizgen, Cat. 10400008) with probes targeting the mouse housekeeping gene Eef2 in a rapid imaging protocol in laser channels 561 and 647.

Panel design

The gene panel was designed for the identification of mouse lung and airway cells with an emphasis on functional characterization of epithelial and innate immune populations. A preliminary panel of roughly 700 genes was generated from several published single-cell datasets5,19 using known markers for major cell types in the lung and airways allowing us to map the lung landscape, including epithelial cells (basal cells, ciliated cells, secretory cells, type I and II alveolar cells), endothelial cells, fibroblasts, immune cells (monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils, lymphocytes), as well as megakaryocytes, for which marker selection was further informed by prior transcriptional characterization of lung-resident megakaryocyte populations from our group.3 We included genes for growth factors and receptors, cytokines and chemokines, cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, macrophage activation, phagocytosis, and endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition. We included key genes for major cell signaling pathways known to be involved in lung/airway injury and the regulation of pulmonary stem cell fate including: Notch, Hippo, Wnt, BMP, FGF, PDGF, and VEGF pathways. Genes related to extracellular matrix, cell adhesion and matrix proteases were included as these are known to be critical for regulating lung/airway maintenance and repair. Highly abundant genes were excluded from the panel to avoid optical crowding, with individual gene abundances limited to <700 fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM), as estimated using Vizgen’s panel design portal, which calculates gene abundances using data from publications and public repositories. Genes with fewer than 50 target regions were excluded to ensure high target specificity. The panel was further refined by excluding functionally overlapping genes and limiting the 500-gene panel to a total panel abundance of <9000 FPKM, in accordance with Vizgen’s default panel design guidelines to minimize optical crowding. This custom panel of genes can be found in its entirety in Table S1.

Software and computational environment

All computational analyses were performed utilizing the Giotto spatial transcriptomics software suite20 (v4.2.1) in RStudio Server (R v4.4.0, RStudio v2024.04.2–764). Analyses were run on a high-performance computing cluster environment at Boston University (BU SCC), and all parameters, intermediate objects, and scripts logged to ensure reproducibility. Additional packages loaded included gcc (v12.2.0), gdal (v3.6.4), geos (v3.11.1), hdf5 (v1.8.21), java (v11.0.4), proj (v9.2.0), and sqlite3 (v3.37.2). Scripts used to perform different stages of analysis can be found at https://github.com/nskvir/MERSCOPE-Lung-Profiling (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20454186). An interactive viewer for this dataset implemented with Vitessce26 can be accessed at https://nskvir.github.io/MERSCOPE-Lung-Profiling/.

Data processing and quality control

Both raw spatial outputs from Vizgen MERSCOPE, including per-transcript coordinate tables, cell segmentation boundaries, and z stack mosaic images (DAPI, Cellbound, PolyT) were loaded into Giotto. Counts were generated by point-in-polygon overlap per z stack (calculateOverlap/overlapToMatrix) and summed into an aggregate layer (aggregateStacks) prior to analysis, which was set as the active spat unit. Quality control filtering was performed with filterGiotto after total expression was examined for each sample (Figure S1), requiring genes to be detected in at least five cells (feat_det_in_min_cells = 5), cells to contain at least five detected features (min_det_feats_per_cell = 5), and a minimum expression threshold of 1 count per feature. Following filtering, 10,496 cells were removed out of 184,911 total (5.7%), with no genes removed from the 500-gene panel, yielding a final dataset of 174,415 cells. In the post-filtering dataset, the median number of genes detected per cell was 84 (mean 86, range 5–294), with median raw transcript counts of 125 per cell in Sample A and 188 per cell in Sample B. Normalization was performed with normalizeGiotto, applying library size normalization and scaling counts to a factor of 1,000 per cell. These thresholds were selected to balance stringency with retention of rare cell populations, given the targeted nature of the 500-gene panel.

Dimensionality reduction and clustering

Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA), and top principal components were retained based on scree plot visualization for downstream analysis. UMAP was applied to the reduced PCA space to generate two-dimensional embeddings for visualization purposes. Unsupervised clustering was carried out with the Leiden algorithm (doLeidenCluster) using Giotto defaults, and cluster-specific marker genes were identified with the standard Giotto pipeline. Although this approach yielded multiple interpretable clusters for a majority of cell types across our samples, there remained some ambiguity among several large, central clusters. To improve confidence and validate our cell type annotations, we utilized a reference dataset (the LungMAP murine single-cell reference)9, containing approximately 40 annotated cell types, onto the MERSCOPE UMAP embeddings using newly implemented Giotto functionality. More specifically, features were intersected between the single cell and spatial data to make a joined object, which was then re-processed (filterGiotto, normalizeGiotto, runPCA and runUMAP). Lastly, Harmony integration21 was performed (runGiottoHarmony) followed by label transfer (labelTransfer), and re-addition of the cell metadata (addCellMetadata). The labelTransfer function assigns cell type annotations via KNN similarity voting in the Harmony-integrated joint embedding space, identifying the k nearest scRNA-seq reference cells for each spatial cell and assigning labels by majority vote, along with a transfer probability score reflecting annotation confidence. This projection enabled final re-labeling of cells according to their proximity to reference-defined clusters, as opposed to de-novo reclustering of the integrated dataset, yielding a higher-confidence annotation set that was used for subsequent analyses.

Megakaryocyte identification and curation

Because megakaryocytes (MKs) are both transcriptionally similar to platelets and morphologically atypical (large, often multinucleated), they posed challenges for automated identification and subsequent segmentation in the dataset. To overcome this, we applied a two-step strategy. First, we implemented a stepwise approach to identify potential regions with MKs. More specifically, we used Giotto’s tessellate function to overlay hexagonal bins across each sample, with “shape_size” values set between 50 and 100 and calculated enrichment scores for each bin based on six canonical MK marker genes (Gp1ba, Itga2b, Mpl, Pf4, Tubb1, Vwf), using two complementary scoring approaches: a rescaled sum score, in which each marker gene’s expression was individually rescaled to a 0–1 range and summed per bin to reward co-expression of multiple markers, and a scaled mean score, in which the mean of library-size normalized, log-transformed, and z-scored expression values across all six markers was calculated per bin to capture overall signature elevation relative to the tissue-wide mean. Candidate regions were systematically screened and prioritized where both scoring approaches were positive. These regions were then examined visually by experts to discern MKs from platelet clusters, based on morphology (DAPI and cell boundary stains) in tandem with robust expression and co-localization of our six marker-gene transcriptional overlays. Secondly, to correct for segmentation errors (where single MKs were often split into multiple polygons, thus identifying them as separate cells), we re-assessed cell boundaries of each putative MK. Polygons corresponding to each curated MK were merged within the Giotto object, utilizing their individual polygon IDs, new centroids were calculated, and the corrected polygons were reinserted into the Giotto object. Lastly, boundaries were redrawn using the draw function from the Terra package to ensure proper calculation of transcriptional overlaps for each cell. This ensured a one-to-one correspondence between curated MKs and polygon objects, necessary especially for downstream neighborhood enrichment analyses of rare cell types (see results). While elongated or atypical MK extensions were occasionally observed, our conservative approach excluded these from MK classification unless they satisfied both morphological criteria and co-expression of canonical MK markers.

Proximity enrichment analysis

Utilizing the createSpatialNetwork and cellProximityEnrichment functions within the Giotto framework, we constructed a spatial Delaunay network of all cells across a merged object comprised of both samples together in order to perform proximity enrichment analysis. Globally, pairwise enrichment scores were calculated for every cell type combination relative to a randomized null distribution. Enrichment and depletion scores were calculated as the ratio of observed to expected interaction frequencies, where expected frequencies were derived from 1,000 permutation simulations in which cell type labels were randomly reshuffled across all cells in the spatial network while preserving network topology. P-values were calculated empirically and corrected for multiple testing using FDR adjustment. For focus on curated MKs specifically, neighborhood composition was quantified at both broad and specific levels in radial bins extending outward in 10 μm increments to determine relative cell-type proportions of neighbors at varying distances. The “specific” level corresponds to the full complement of cell type annotations assigned via LungMAP CellRef label transfer (annotated as “celltype_level2”), while the broad level represents a manually curated mapping of these cell types into major biological compartments (Airway Epithelium, Alveolar Epithelium, Endothelial, Fibroblast, Myeloid, Lymphocyte, Granulocyte, and Mesenchymal), informed by the hierarchical structure of the LungMAP CellRef. The complete broad category mapping is documented in the provided analysis scripts. After defining these levels, a KNN network was constructed using the createSpatialKNNnetwork function. Next, by using extracted metadata, cell connections with our megakaryocytes were binned by distance, with counts and relative proportions used to construct our bar plots.

Ligand-receptor analysis

To visualize potential intercellular signaling, we used Giotto’s spatCellCellcom function to calculate ligand-receptor (L/R) enrichment scores across the annotated lung cell types. A curated set of L/R pairs was derived from our custom 500-gene panel, which included genes spanning angiogenesis, BMP signaling, chemokine/chemotaxis, apoptosis, extracellular matrix, growth factors, and interferon pathways. The analysis was run with 1,000 permutations to generate null distributions for comparison. Enrichment was quantified as the fold change between observed versus randomized L/R co-localization, with significance assessed by empirical p-values from the permutations. To generate our dot plots summarizing enrichment across L/R pairs, 11 pairs were manually selected as a representative set of canonical signaling axes spanning BMP, VEGF, chemokine, ECM-integrin, apoptosis, and interferon pathways represented in our custom gene panel, rather than based on a strict statistical threshold alone. Representative cell-cell interaction pairs were then selected from the top ∼50 entries in the interactions table, sorted by adjusted p-value and log2 fold change relative to the permuted null distribution (for complete interactions table, see Table S2).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the Giotto spatial transcriptomics software suite (v4.2.1) in R (v4.4.0). Statistical details including exact n values and test results are reported in the Results section and figure legends where applicable.

Quality control and filtering

Following aggregation of transcript counts across z-stacks, quality control filtering was applied requiring genes to be detected in at least five cells, cells to contain at least five detected features, and a minimum expression threshold of 1 count per feature. These thresholds yielded a final dataset of 174,415 cells from an initial 184,911 (10,496 cells removed, 5.7%). QC distributions for total expression for both samples are visualized in Figure S1.

Cell proximity enrichment

Enrichment and depletion scores were calculated as the ratio of observed to expected interaction frequencies across 1,000 permutation simulations in which cell type labels were randomly reshuffled while preserving network topology. P-values were derived empirically and corrected for multiple testing using FDR adjustment. A minimum threshold of 5 observed and 5 expected interactions was applied for visualization. Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare proportions of enriched homotypic versus heterotypic interactions; results are reported as odds ratios with associated p-values in the Results section.

Ligand-receptor analysis

Enrichment was quantified as the fold change of observed versus randomized L/R co-localization across 1,000 permutation simulations, with significance assessed by empirical p-values corrected for multiple testing. The complete interactions table is provided in Table S2.

Megakaryocyte curation

Eight megakaryocytes were identified and curated across both samples (8 MKs total). Given the small number of curated MKs, neighborhood analyses are descriptive rather than inferential, and no formal statistical comparisons were performed on MK-specific neighborhood compositions.
