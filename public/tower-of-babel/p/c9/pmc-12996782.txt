Dual-mode in situ monitoring of CO2 conversion via pH indicator and UV-vis spectroscopy
Authors: Qixian Xie, Bowen Guo, Yichen Zhang, Tianze Yang, Chenfang Cui, Zhengliang Liu, Honglin Yao, Baili Li, Wurong Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe integration of the KHCO3-fed bipolar membrane (BPM) electrolyzer with potassium hydroxide (KOH)-based carbon dioxide (CO2) capture enables directly in situ conversion of captured CO2 into valuable carbonaceous products, eliminating costly CO2 recovery and compression steps. Even this integrated BPM system assembly holds promise; the critical lack of CO2 adsorption intermediate species tracking necessitates the development of a monitoring platform. This work pioneers a novel dual-modal strategy employing simultaneous indicator-based sensing with in situ ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy, achieving real-time visualization of triphasic dynamics during integrated CO2 capture-regeneration-conversion. Within this synergistic process involving Ar-saturated KHCO3 decomposition and electrochemical reduction, an etched indium (In) catalyst achieves 50% formate Faradaic efficiency (FE) at −1.1 V vs. RHE (reversible hydrogen electrode). The synergistic application of these complementary in situ diagnostics unlocks fundamental mechanistic insights into integrated CO2 capture and conversion systems, establishing them as critical enablers for advancing efficient and scalable electrochemical CO2 valorization.

Introduction

As the development of using renewable energy fully displaces traditional fossil fuels across power generation, transportation, and other sectors, the carbon dioxide (CO2) capture from point sources or direct air capturing, followed by sequestration or utilization, remains essential for mitigating climate change.1,2 Aqueous solutions with polyethylene glycol (PEG)/superbase, monoethanolamine (MEA), or potassium hydroxide (KOH) are widely employed to absorb the low-concentration CO2, which forms carbon-containing products.3,4,5,6 Prakash et al. studied the CO2 capture capacity based on various aqueous amine solutions by bubbling CO2 and converted the in situ-captured CO2 to formate via Ru-PNP and Fe-PNP pincer complexes.7 This process is followed by a regeneration process to concentrate the captured CO2 and restore the solution for reuse in subsequent capture cycles,8 which is a promising field that reduces the energy and cost penalties associated with traditional carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS).9 The integrated CO2 capture and utilization (ICCU) strategy addresses atmospheric CO2 accumulation, which avoids the need for energy-intensive desorption. Utilizing an “activated” form of captured CO2 (e.g., carbamates, carbonates, and bicarbonates) is more reactive than gaseous CO2 and enables reactions under milder conditions.10 Owing to the restricted solubility and sluggish diffusion of CO2 in aqueous environments,11 the productivity for converting CO2 into CO, formate (HCOO−), ethylene, and alcohols in low-temperature electrolyzers utilizing aqueous solutions is intrinsically diminished.12 Duan et al. reported an acid-tolerant Bi-based MOF (metal organic framework) operating in an acidic environment (pH 2) to provide formate guided by a Pourbaix diagram, which suppresses hydrogen evolution (HER) and minimizes carbonate formation.13

Traditional CO2 electrolyzers suffer from low carbon utilization efficiency due to irreversible CO2 loss through carbonate formation and crossover.14 To circumvent this fundamental issue, new electrolyzers were designed for utilizing a liquid bicarbonate solution as the feedstock.15,16,17 This approach bypasses the energy-intensive step of regenerating gaseous CO2 and enables the direct conversion of HCO3− ions, effectively creating a pathway for in situ CO2 generation and subsequent reduction within the cell. However, the very complexity that enables their performance also obscures direct, spatially resolved observation of critical internal states, particularly the dynamic evolution of local pH across different compartments. To bridge this gap, we employ a simplified H-cell system with bipolar membranes (BPMs) as a model platform. In the CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) system, BPMs serve as an alternative electrochemical cell separator for electrochemical CO2 reduction cell.18,19,20 Comprising cation exchange layer (CEL), a water dissociation catalyst, and an anion exchange layer (AEL), BPMs can be used for blocking CO2 crossover and converting (bi)carbonate back to CO2.21 Even though commercial BPMs inhibit CO2 crossover, the acidic CEL degrades CO2RR selectivity at the cathode,22 which also results in the problem of exsolution of CO2 from the bicarbonate solution.14 To mitigate cathode acidification, the bicarbonate buffering catholyte can be introduced to maintain a local pH >12 in the reaction environment.23,24,25 While BPM-based CO2 reduction systems effectively prevent crossover of cathode and anode products, only a fraction of recycled CO2 is converted per pass.20,26 The low single-pass conversion in gaseous CO2-fed BPM electrolyzers is due to the need for stoichiometric excess CO2 supply and the formation of HCO3− and CO32− species in the electrolyte. To mitigate these issues in the acidic cathodic environment, employing highly efficient catalysts combined with KOH-mediated CO2 capture and CO2 conversion to potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3) solution effectively enhances the Faradaic efficiency (FE) of carbonaceous products.27,28 By integrating CO2 capture, CO2RR, and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) processes, we design a triple reactor for splitting KHCO3 solutions into CO2 via BPM, which are typically generated by CO2 or simulated flue gas absorption into 1 M KOH solution. Within this configuration, the etched indium (In) catalyst achieves a FEformate of above 50% at −1.1 V vs. RHE. However, this approach currently lacks the transient intermediate species involved, thus limiting a fundamental understanding of the process kinetics and CO2 behavior under operational conditions.

Addressing the lack of real-time monitoring techniques for tracking dynamic intermediates in the KOH-mediated CO2 capture to KHCO3 formation and subsequent KHCO3 decomposition to CO2/K2CO3 reaction pathways within BPM systems, this study proposes a novel dual in situ monitoring strategy. This approach will enable real-time tracking of pH dynamics and carbonate/bicarbonate speciation, which are critical to optimizing performance and stability in integrated CO2 capture and conversion systems and can be incorporated into in situ ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) pH monitoring within membrane electrode assembly-based electrolyzers.29 This methodology overcomes the inherent limitations of conventional offline analyses in capturing transient intermediates, provides direct experimental evidence for carbonate-mediated CO2 cycling mechanisms, and significantly enhances reaction pathway resolution analysis in BPM electrolysis systems.

Results and discussion

Crystal structure, morphology characterization, and electrochemical performance

A triple electrolyzer facilitates CO2 fixation and in situ-directed electrochemical conversion. Within this configuration, a conventional H-cell enables the in situ conversion process, wherein the carbonate produced by fixing CO2 with a base is decomposed, and the CO2 generated in the electrochemical system is directly converted into valuable carbonaceous products (CαHβOγ). The detailed reaction process is shown in Figure 1A; the BPM within the electrolyzer dissociates water into H+ and OH− ions. These ions migrate directionally under the applied potential, where H+ moves toward the cathode compartment and OH− migrates toward the anode compartment, thus enabling the requisite pH control for efficient carbonate regeneration and cathodic CO2 reduction.30 In the cathode compartment, the H+ reacted with HCO3− to provide CO2, which can be subsequently in situ converted into CαHβOγ under cathodic potential. In the anode compartment, the OER process proceeds under 1 M KOH on the surface of the Pt anode. To identify the carbon sources in HCO3− and the resulting products, an isotopic labeling experiment was first conducted. An etched In electrode was tested at −1.1 V vs. RHE under 0.1 M NaH13CO3 electrolyte. From the result of gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) curve, the m/z peak at 29 and 44.95 can be ascribed to 13CO and 13CO2, respectively,31,32 proving that the carbon atom was derived from H13CO3− (Figure S1). The 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy results revealed the presence of both 13C-labeled formate and unlabeled formate (Figure S2), indicating that one portion of the carbon source originated from NaH13CO3, while the other portion was likely derived from dissolved CO2 during the experiment.33 To further validate the system’s behavior, tests under 1 M KHCO3 (Ar saturated) at different potential are conducted. The current density exhibits significant different between the 1 M KHCO3 and 1 M KHCO3 (Ar saturated), as shown in Figures 1B and S3. The current density measurements are obtained via chronoamperometry, which reveals that 1 M KHCO3 (Ar saturated) yields comparatively lower current densities. To demonstrate the aforementioned processes, a laboratory H-cell with In-based catalysts and Pt catalyst was employed as an electrochemical system. The In electrode has been reported as one of the best electrocatalysts for formate production in electrochemical CO2 reduction.34,35,36 As shown in Figure S4, the In wire electrode with a diameter of ∼800 μm exhibits a smooth surface as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Elemental mapping analysis further illustrates the spatial distribution of In and oxygen (O) species within the electrode structure, with distinct color coding facilitating comprehensive material characterization. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern reveals the polycrystalline crystal structure of the pristine In catalyst (Figure S5), which is consistent with In (PDF#05–0642). To further enhance the performance of In-based catalysts, metallic In wire electrodes were subjected to alkaline etching for nanostructuring, which successfully constructed nanocube electrodes. SEM analysis reveals that the etched catalyst surface is decorated with nanocube particles ranging from ∼50 to 200 nm in size (Figure S6). Moreover, the elemental mapping of SEM indicated a significant increased oxygen content on the etching In surface. Combined with XRD pattern analysis (Figure S5), it was revealed that the nanocube layer formed on the surface corresponds to In(OH)3.

By comparing the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of two In-based samples in Ar-saturated 1 M KHCO3, the etched In electrocatalyst demonstrated superior polarization performance than the pristine In electrocatalyst beyond −1.0 V vs. RHE, which may be associated with the ECO2RR process (Figure 2B). At moderate overpotentials, etched In electrocatalyst selectively stabilizes the formate pathway via its intermediate adsorption mechanism.37 The etched In electrocatalyst surface likely forms nanostructures (Figures 2A and S6), which enhance CO2 adsorption and activation. Therefore, the electrochemical reduction of CO2 to formate on etched In-based catalysts was systematically investigated under 1 M Ar-saturated KHCO3 electrolyte and applied potentials (−1.0 to −1.4 V vs. RHE), as shown in Figure 2C. Electrochemical test results reveal a pronounced dependence of FEformate on both electrolyte concentration and cathodic potential, with competing HER dominating. Here, the gaseous products of the etched In-based catalyst were tested via gas chromatography (GC). The gaseous products were primarily H2 with minor amounts of CO (Figure S7), which was generally consistent with the types of gaseous products reported in the literature.38,39 All the reaction processes are performed in an electrochemical H-cell; etching In-based catalysts show the in situ KHCO3 converting to formate. FEformate was quantified via NMR spectroscopy, with calibration standards shown in Figure S8. The FEformate in a 1 M KHCO3 system is greater than 50% within the voltage of −1.1 V to vs. RHE (Figure 2C), which is consistent with previous reports of the iCO2-BPM system for formate production.16 As the potential increases, the FEformate declines progressively, reaching <30% at −1.3 V vs. RHE, indicative of HER dominance. According to GC measurements, at a potential of −1.1 V vs. RHE (the cell voltage is −2.53 V, Figure S9), the amount of CO2 produced is 26066.47 ppm. Through calculation, the amount of CO2 is determined to be 4.09 × 10−5 mol. Under this potential, the amount of CO produced is 2.69 × 10−6 mol, while the charge passed at this potential theoretically yields 1.36 × 10−4 mol of CO2. The utilization conversion rate for gaseous products is 3.21%, and the energy efficiency (EE) of this system was 48.20% (Table S1). To further verify the stability of the system, the etched In electrocatalyst was subjected to a stability in a 1 M KHCO3 electrolyte at −1.1 V vs. RHE, with 1 mL of solution sampled every 12 h for analysis. The results showed that the FEformate remained above 50% within the first 36 h but progressively decreased as the reaction continued (Figure 2D). Dual-mode in situ monitoring properties

A series of laboratory triple decarbonation H-cells was constructed to validate the proposed electrochemical scheme. Here, the left chamber (chamber L) was used for CO2 or CO2(15wt %)/N2(85wt %) adsorption and the obtained KHCO3 solution storage, which was separated from the cathode chamber (middle chamber, chamber M) via impermeable and gas-tight plastic membrane (Figure S10). To further broaden its application, the KHCO3 obtained via CO2 or CO2(15wt %)/N2(85wt %) adsorption could be connected to the cathode chamber (chamber M) of the electrochemical cell via a peristaltic pump. Figures 3A–3H show the time-lapse images of a reactor assembled with etched In electrodes. An electrolyte consisting of 1 M KOH in distilled water was introduced in the chamber L as the CO2 capture medium. This alkaline electrolyte facilitates the chemisorption of CO2 through hydroxide carbonate equilibria, with phenolphthalein (0.1–0.2 wt %) added as a spectrophotometric pH indicator to monitor localized alkalinity shifts. The experimental setup enabled in situ visualization of electrochemical processes, including pH gradient evolution under controlled operational conditions. After adding phenolphthalein indicator to 1 M KOH, the solution appears light pink, as shown in chamber L of Figure 3A. As CO2 was continuously introduced into the system, the color of electrolyte progressively shifted from pink to rose red (Figures 3A–3C), accompanied by a noticeable deepening of the hue. This color evolution aligns precisely with the trend observed in the standard solutions of KOH and K2CO3 (Figures S11A and S11C). These consistent color changes demonstrate that KOH within the system effectively captures CO2, with gradual formation of K2CO3. With increasing CO2 input, the electrolyte color faded from rosy red to pale pink and ultimately became colorless and transparent (Figures 3C–3E), matching the mixed K2CO3 and KHCO3 standard solution (Figures S11B and S11C). At 50 sccm CO2 flow, the conversion process of KOH adsorption to KHCO3 persisted for ∼30 min. In situ UV-vis spectra quantitatively monitored the chromogenic transitions during CO2 capture by 1 M KOH supplemented with phenolphthalein (Figure 3I), which was performed in a photoelectrochemical cell with detachable membrane (Figure S12). The UV-vis spectra clearly showed the continuous introduction of CO2, with the absorbance (Abs) value at 552 nm progressively increasing from an initial value of 0.0317–0.117 during the initial 4 min. By 14 min, the Abs value had increased significantly to 2.114. This rise in Abs agrees with the visual observation of the electrolyte turning rose red, confirming that KOH adsorbs CO2 and converts to K2CO3. The Abs value would drop from 16 min with the amount of CO2 increasing; by 28 min, it would drop from 2.067 to 0.0029, confirming that the K2CO3 absorbs more CO2 and converts to KHCO3 (Figure 3I).Figure 3Integrated monitoring of electrochemical CO2 capture and KHCO3 decomposition in a triple decarbonation H-cell(A–H) Time-lapse images (10 h electrolysis, −1.2 V vs. RHE) showing progressive pH indicator dye color changes reflecting electrochemical pH shifts: (A) 0 min: initial system.(B and C) 3–10 min: upon CO2 input, the left absorption chamber changes from pink to rose red, and a blue color develops in the right chamber’s starch-potassium iodide solution.(D and E) 24–29 min: the left absorption chamber changes from rose red to transparent.(F–H) 180–600 min: the middle chamber changed from yellow brown hue to blue with the KHCO3 decomposition.(I) In situ UV-vis spectra of the CO2 absorption chamber (left chamber, L chamber), tracking species evolution during CO2 dissolution.(J) In situ UV-vis spectra of the KHCO3 conversion chamber (middle chamber, M chamber), monitoring KHCO3 concentration and decomposition products.

Thymol blue could serve as a pH indicator for the KHCO3 electrolyte undergoing in situ CO2 generation via proton (H+) flux from BPM.40 Initially, the pristine KHCO3 solution exhibited a characteristic yellow brown hue (Figure 3F). Progressive electrolysis induced chromogenic shifts: after 3 h, the electrolyte transitioned to blue (Figure 3F), intensifying to dark blue at 5–6 h and stabilizing as light blue after 10 h (Figures 3G and 3H). This color change is consistent with the trend observed in the KHCO3-K2CO3 standard system (Figure S13), confirming that KHCO3 reacts with the cathode layer of the BPM membrane to produce H+, forming CO2, K2CO3, and H2O, which is in accordance with the mechanism shown in Figure 1A. In situ UV-vis spectra was employed to observe the decomposition of KHCO3 producing CO2 (Figure 3J), which clearly showed the continuous decomposition of KHCO3, with the Abs value at 338 nm progressively decreasing from an initial value of 0.568 to 0.185 during the 10 h test process. The Abs value at 435 nm decreases from 0.564 to 0.182 at 10 h test, which corresponds to the color changing from brown to blue. Till 5–6 h test, the color of the electrolyte in the middle chamber is dark blue, corresponding with the highest Abs value at 596 nm, attributing to the blue absorption. The image and in situ UV-vis spectra of the triple chambers display the CO2 capture and utilization (Figure 3). In the right chamber (chamber R), the starch-potassium iodide indicator causes the solution to gradually turn blue upon applying a positive potential, confirming the occurrence of oxidation reactions (Figures 3A–3H). Additionally, an ex situ experiment was conducted to evaluate thymol blue as an indicator. Comparative testing of the pristine 1 M KHCO3 electrolyte and the electrolyte after 10 h of reaction revealed a color transition from yellow brown hue to blue, which was generally consistent with that of the in situ experiments, confirming that thymol blue can be considered electrochemically stable during the 10 h test period (Figure S14). Consequently, the ex situ experiment implies that the local pH shift, primarily associated with the consumption of KHCO3 and regeneration of K2CO3, was the main driver of the color change, rather than being solely attributable to the concurrent OH− production. Furthermore, a 60 h stability test was conducted to monitor the cathode electrolyte. The pristine KHCO3 solution containing thymol blue exhibited a color evolution from yellow to brown, then to blue within the first 12 h, and this color subsequently faded to light blue and eventually became transparent after 60 h (Figure S15). The Abs value at 435 nm decreases from 0.296 to nearly zero during 60 h test, which corresponds to the color changing from brown to blue to transparent (Figure S16). After 12 h test, the color of the electrolyte in the middle chamber is blue, corresponding with the Abs value of 0.358 at 596 nm, attributing to the blue absorption. Up to 60 h test, the Abs value at 596 nm is 0.0105, consistent with the electrolyte becoming virtually transparent (Figure S15F).

Simulated flue gas CO2 capture and conversion

A coupled electrochemical system was developed for formate production via in situ KHCO3 conversion using BPM electrodialysis, integrated with CO2 or simulated flue gas capture through KOH carbonation. Aqueous KOH solution was saturated with the CO2 from simulated flue gas to yield equimolar KHCO3 electrolytes, enabling direct electrochemical reduction to formate on etched In catalysts (Figure 4A). To further verify the stability of this BPM system, the etched In electrocatalyst was subjected to a stability test in a 15% CO2, 85% N2-saturated 1 M KOH electrolyte at −1.1 V vs. RHE, with 2 mL of solution sampled every 12 h for analysis. The results showed that the FEformate remained above 30% at 24 h but progressively decreased as the reaction continued (Figure 4B). This study integrates KOH-mediated CO2 capture with KHCO3-fed BPM electrolysis for the direct in situ electrochemical conversion of captured CO2 from simulated flue gas to formate, achieving FE (∼30%) using an etched In catalyst at −1.1 V vs. RHE. It validates a novel dual-modal in situ monitoring strategy that provides a method for transient intermediate species within CO2-integrated capture, regeneration, and conversion systems. The in situ monitoring strategy provides real-time mechanistic insights into the triphasic CO2 capture (KOH-K2CO3-KHCO3, visualized via phenolphthalein color fading), regeneration of KHCO3 to K2CO3, and CO2 (visualized via thymol blue color shift to blue) within an operating BPM electrolyzer. This approach enables quantitative, time-resolved tracking of key species kinetics (e.g., sequential K2CO3 formation/consumption monitored at 552 nm, KHCO3 decomposition dynamics tracked via absorbance changes at 338 and 596 nm), offering direct experimental validation of carbonate-mediated mechanisms and significantly enhancing the resolution of reaction pathway analysis. This work not only presents an integrated CO2-to-formate process leveraging BPM electrolysis and KOH capture but also more significantly introduces a transformative dual-modal in situ monitoring strategy for electrochemical CO2 valorization technologies.

Limitations of the study

Our work demonstrates an in situ CO2 conversion system based on a BPM electrochemical H-cell, incorporating in situ UV-vis spectroscopy and online indicator monitoring for CO2 or simulated flue gas capture. This study primarily employs an H-cell for visual demonstration, and the investigation of this system has not been extended to its application in industrial-scale membrane electrode assembly cells. Furthermore, more advanced in situ techniques are required to conduct deeper investigations into the intermediate during the CO2 capture and conversion reaction processes.

Method details

Preparation of the etched in electrocatalysts

The pristine In wire (∼800 μm) was chosen to synthesis the nano In electrocatalysts, which was subjected to 1 M KOH solution to etch for nano structuring with 6h under room temperature. Here, the pristine In wire was washed sequentially via CH3CH2OH, deionized water, dilute H2SO4 and deionized water then transformed into the KOH solution.

Catalyst characterization

The morphology of as-prepared In based samples was characterized by a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS Sigma 360) operating at 20 kV in the secondary electron mode. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on Rigaku Ultima IV instrument with Cu Ka radiation (45 kV, 40 mA, k = 1.5418 Å), recorded with 2θ ranging from 10° to 90°, 10°/min.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical measurements were carried out at room temperature in a two-chamber H-cell and a triple-chamber H-cell using an electrochemical workstation (Ivium, V34541, and Potentiostat WHHD-2), under −1.0 V to −1.4 V vs. RHE. Here, the commercial Pt and the etched In electrodes were chosen as anode and cathode.
