Association Between Allergy, Asthma, and Cancer: The Japan Public Health Center‐Based Prospective Study
Authors: Mayo Hirabayashi, Manami Inoue, Taiki Yamaji, Motoki Iwasaki, Shoichiro Tsunage, Norie Sawada, The JPHC Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeveral epidemiological studies have explored the potential associations between allergy, asthma, and cancer incidence, but the findings remain inconclusive. This study investigates the association between a history of allergy, asthma, and cancer risk in a Japanese population. Data were obtained from the Japan Public Health Center‐based Prospective Study (JPHC Study). Participants reported in a self‐administered questionnaire whether they had been diagnosed by a physician for allergy, asthma, or both (hereafter referred to as “allergy and/or asthma”). They were followed until December 2013. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for overall and site‐specific cancers for allergy, asthma, and combined allergy and/or asthma. Subgroup analyses were performed by smoking status. Among participants, 17,679 cancer cases were identified, and 6.5% reported a history of allergy and/or asthma. A borderline association was observed between allergy and/or asthma and thyroid cancer (HR 1.54, 95% CI 1.00–2.35). Subgroup analysis revealed that men with a history of smoking and allergy and/or asthma had an increased risk of colorectal cancer (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.03–1.63), while never‐smoking women with allergy and/or asthma showed an increased risk of cervical cancer (HR 1.98, 95% CI 1.01–3.86). These findings showed no overall association of allergy, asthma, or their combined exposure with cancer risk in a Japanese population, warranting further investigation.

Introduction

The global prevalence of cancer has increased in recent decades. The number of incident cases of cancer, 18.1 million recorded in 2018, increased to 19.3 million in 2020 [1, 2]. Preventative efforts have included the search for possible associations between cancer onset and different chronic diseases. Among these, allergy and asthma are of particular interest due to their prevalence and potential influence on cancer development.

Allergy and asthma are chronic conditions that affect millions worldwide. Allergy, a hypersensitive immune response to environmental or internal triggers, affects approximately 30% of the world's population [3]. Asthma, a chronic airway inflammatory disease, is estimated to affect over 400 million people globally in 2025 [4]. While these conditions are distinct, they share overlapping immune mechanisms, including chronic inflammation and immune activity, which may play a role in cancer onset.

Whether allergy and asthma promote or protect against cancer remains a subject of debate. On the one hand, the hypersensitive immune response associated with these conditions may enhance immune surveillance, detecting and removing deregulated or damaged cells before carcinogenesis and lowering cancer risk [5]. On the other hand, chronic inflammation, a condition associated with allergy and asthma, may contribute to cancer development by creating a pro‐tumorigenic environment, inducing oxidative stress, and promoting mutations in tumour suppressor genes or impairing DNA repair mechanisms [6, 7]. Findings from previous epidemiological studies are inconclusive: While some reported an inverse association between allergy and cancers of the colorectum [8], pancreas [9], stomach [8], oesophagus [8], liver [8], and glioma [10, 11], others found an increased risk for cancers of the skin [12], breast [13, 14], lung [15], and thyroid [16]. Furthermore, cigarette smoking, an established cancer risk factor, has been shown to modulate systemic inflammation and immune pathways and may interact with allergic immune responses to influence oncogenesis [17, 18, 19].

Most studies on the association between allergy, asthma, and cancer have been conducted in Caucasian populations. Studies in East Asian populations are limited despite the potential differences in environmental and lifestyle factors that could influence cancer risk. Here, we aimed to investigate the association between allergy, asthma, and their combined exposure and cancer in a Japanese population using a large‐scale prospective cohort.

Methods

Study Design

The Japan Public Health Center‐based Prospective (JPHC) Study is an ongoing prospective cohort study initiated in the 1990s to investigate the associations between lifestyle factors and disease onset. The study is described in detail elsewhere [20]. The JPHC Study consists of two cohorts: Cohort I, established in 1990, and Cohort II in 1993. The study enrolled over 140,000 participants aged 40 to 69 years at the baseline survey from 11 public health centre (PHC) areas in Japan.

Study Population

We included those who responded to a self‐administered baseline questionnaire on life‐related factors between 1990 and 1993. Figure 1 shows a flow diagram of study participant selection. We excluded participants from Katsushika, Tokyo, because of the lack of information on cancer incidence. We also excluded those who died or moved out of the study area before the baseline questionnaire (n = 113), non‐Japanese nationals (52), those with an incorrect birthdate (416), duplicates [12], non‐responders (26,741), and those with a history of cancer (2,294), leaving 103,718 (49,446 men, 54,252 women) participants for analysis.

Participant flow chart.

Exposure and Covariates

A self‐administered questionnaire on lifestyle, including data on smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, anthropometry, and medical history, was administered at baseline. For medical history, participants were asked if they had been diagnosed by a doctor with any of the following: Diabetes, stroke, hypertension, myocardial infarction or angina pectoris, asthma, bronchitis, allergy, chronic kidney disease, cystitis, chronic liver disease, liver cirrhosis, gastroduodenitis, or gallstones. Based on the responses, participants were classified as having allergy or asthma if they reported a physician diagnosis of allergy, asthma, or both (from here on referred to as allergy and/or asthma).

Follow‐Up and Identification of Cancer Cases

Person‐years were calculated from the date of the baseline survey to the date of cancer diagnosis, death, moving out from the study area, or the end of 2013 (except for Osaka: 2012), whichever came first. The residential registry confirmed study participants' residence and survival status. Cancer incidence was identified through active patient notification from major local hospitals in the study PHC area and population‐based cancer registry records. When necessary, the information was supplemented with death certificates. Cancer was coded based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) for Oncology, 3rd edition. Cancer sites were selected based on previous studies [21, 22], in addition to major cancer sites: Oral cavity, pharynx, and larynx (C00–14.9); oesophagus (C15.0–15.9); stomach (C16.0–16.9); colorectal (colon: C18.0–18.9); rectum (C19.0—C20.9); liver (C22.0); gallbladder (C23.9); pancreas (C25.0—C25.9); lung (C33.9, C34.0—C34.9); skin (C44.0—C44.9, C51.0—C51.9, C60.0—C60.9, C63.2); breast (C50–50.9); cervix (C53.0—C53.9); prostate (C61.9); kidney (C64.9); glioma (C71.0—C71.9, histology 9380, 9382, 9400, 9431,9444); and thyroid (C73.9). For participants diagnosed with multiple cancers during the follow‐up period, the analysis was restricted to the first incident cancer.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed separately for allergy, asthma, and the combined allergy and/or asthma exposure. The combined allergy and/or asthma was included in the analysis because allergy and asthma share overlapping immunological and inflammatory pathways. Given the limited number of cases for several cancer sites, site‐specific and smoking‐stratified analyses focused on the combined allergy and/or asthma exposure. The combined characteristic differences among study participants based on their allergy or asthma history were compared using analysis of variance, the Kruskal–Wallis test, or the chi‐squared (χ2) test. To estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), we used multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models, with participants who reported no history of allergy or asthma as the reference group. The models were adjusted a priori based on variables identified in previous literature as potential confounders [20, 23, 24]. Included confounders were body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), smoking status (never, former, < 20 cigarettes/day, ≥ 20 cigarettes/day), alcohol consumption (never, < 150, 150–299, 300–449, ≥ 450 g/week), physical activity (continuous, assessed as total daily metabolic equivalent tasks [MET]‐hours/day, based on questionnaire‐derived activity frequency and duration), and history of diabetes mellitus. The proportional hazards assumption for the models was evaluated and confirmed using Schoenfeld residuals. All models were stratified by PHC, gender (in the overall analysis), and age. p‐values for linear trends were calculated by treating the categorical variables as continuous.

Given the possible association between cigarette smoking, allergy and/or asthma, and cancer onset, we conducted a subgroup analysis for those with information on smoking status, which combined a history of allergy and/or asthma and smoking status. The study participants were divided into four groups based on their allergy and/or asthma and cigarette smoking status: Never smokers without allergy and/or asthma; never smokers with allergy and/or asthma; ever smokers without allergy and/or asthma; and ever smokers with allergy and/or asthma. We calculated the p for interaction (p‐int) between smoking status and history of allergy and/or asthma.

We applied a Bonferroni correction to account for multiple testing across site‐specific cancers. The adjusted threshold (p < 0.003) was calculated by dividing the conventional alpha (α)‐level (p < 0.05) by the number of cancer sites examined (n = 15).

All analyses were performed using Stata 16.0 (StataCorp LP).

Results

During an average of 17.9 years of follow‐up, there were 17,679 incident cases of cancer (10,843 men, 6836 women). Table 1 shows the study subjects' baseline characteristics by self‐reported allergy and/or asthma. For men, there was no apparent difference in age or BMI based on self‐reported history of allergy and/or asthma. Men who reported a history of allergy and/or asthma had a lower prevalence of current smoking, alcohol consumption, and history of diabetes mellitus. For women, compared to those who did not report any history of allergy and/or asthma, those who reported a history of allergy and/or asthma were younger, had a lower BMI, and were also more likely to be smokers and to drink more.

Among ever‐smokers with a history of allergy and/or asthma, the risk of colorectal cancer increased (HR 1.31; 95% CI 1.06–1.63; p‐int 0.03) compared to ever‐smokers without allergy or asthma, as did the risk of rectal cancer (HR 1.48; 95% CI 1.04–1.09; p‐int 0.10) (Table 4). When separated by gender, men with a history of smoking showed an increased risk for colorectal cancer (HR 1.30; 95% CI 1.03–1.63; p‐int 0.08), while the risk for rectal cancer did not (HR 1.42; 95% CI 0.98–2.05; p‐int 0.16). For women, we observed an increased risk for cervical cancer among never‐smokers who reported a history of allergy or asthma (HR 1.98; 95% CI 1.01–3.86; p‐int 0.22), whereas risk remained statistically insignificant for all other cancer sites. Discussion

In this study, we aimed to determine the associations between allergy, asthma, and cancer risk among a Japanese population. We observed no significant association between self‐reported history of allergy, asthma, or their combined exposure and cancer risk, including site‐specific cancer. A history of allergy and/or asthma was associated with a higher risk of colorectal cancer in ever‐smoking men as well as cervical cancer in never‐smoking women. These findings suggest a complex relationship between allergy, asthma, and cancer, warranting further exploration to better understand the potential mechanisms and public health implications.

Previous studies have found both positive and negative associations between allergy and cancer. While some studies [25, 26] reported decreased risk for overall cancer, others [13] found increased risk or no association [27, 28]. Here, we observed no association between a history of allergy, asthma, or a combination of the two with overall cancer risk.

When analysed by site, we observed a slightly increased risk of thyroid cancer among individuals with a self‐reported history of allergy and/or asthma. A pooled analysis of case–control studies from Europe, North America, and Asia found a positive association between allergy and medullary thyroid cancer (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.01–4.74) [16]. One proposed hypothesis is that the repeated injury and repair process of the chronic inflammation associated with allergy could lead to a skewing of the T‐lymphocyte response from T‐helper 1 (Th1), which is involved in tumour suppression and eradication, to Th2, which is associated with allergy and infection [29]. Long‐term skewing toward Th2 is associated with immune suppression and reduced tumour surveillance. Th2 polarisation may increase levels of cytokines such as interleukin (IL)‐4, −5, and −13 while suppressing interferon‐ɣ, IL‐12, and −18, which play a role in tumour suppression [30]. The thyroid gland is also affected by hypersensitivity reactions of the immune system [31]. A recent meta‐analysis [32] showed an increased risk of thyroid cancer in individuals with Hashimoto's thyroiditis (RR 1.49, 95% CI 1.42–1.57), an autoimmune condition characterised by chronic inflammation of the thyroid gland [33]. This raises the possibility that chronic inflammation of the thyroid gland, potentially resulting from conditions such as allergy, may represent a prerequisite condition for thyroid carcinogenesis. While the authors of the pooled analysis did not provide any possible mechanism, they hypothesised that vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) released by thyroid cancer—known for its immune‐regulatory function that suppresses local inflammation—could contribute to cutaneous or respiratory systems resembling allergic conditions, potentially leading to misdiagnosis. These findings underscore the complex interplay between immune dysregulation, chronic inflammation, and thyroid cancer risk. A deeper understanding of this relationship could provide new insights into the relationship between allergy and thyroid cancer.

We observed an association between the combination of asthma and allergy and the risk of colorectal cancer among ever smokers when stratified by smoking status. While no study to date has directly examined the interaction between allergy and colorectal cancer with consideration to smoking status, previous studies have investigated these associations separately. Tobacco smoking is a well‐established risk factor for colorectal cancer [34, 35], while several studies have reported an inverse association between allergy and colorectal cancer [8, 36, 37, 38]. The modifying effect of smoking could be explained by its role in altering immune responses and inflammatory pathways. Smoking is known to induce chronic systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which are known to contribute to carcinogenesis [39, 40]. Interestingly, a previous Japanese study [41] found that higher C‐reactive protein (CRP) levels were associated with colorectal cancer risk, and a meta‐analysis reported an association between CRP level and colorectal cancer, which appeared to be stronger for men [42]. CRP is often used as a marker of chronic low‐grade inflammation [43], which may elevate during an allergic reaction. It is possible that the pro‐inflammatory effects of smoking, combined with allergy‐related immune activation, could be activating inflammatory signalling pathways and promoting colorectal cancer development.

Among women who never smoked with a history of allergy and/or asthma, we observed an elevated risk of cervical cancer. A previous case–control study found an inverse association between cervical cancer and a history of hives (odds ratio (OR) 0.63) but no association with asthma or hay fever [44]. A British cohort study reported increased cervical cancer risk with childhood non‐atopic eczema (OR 3.27; 95% CI 1.95–5.49), but the association was null for hay fever (1.04; 0.50–2.17) [45]. One possible mechanism for our observed increase in cervical cancer risk among never‐smoking women involves sex hormones. Asthma and allergies during adolescence are up to three times more common in women than in men [46, 47]. Oestrogen can augment humoral immune responses and activate mast cells, which release histamine and inflammatory factors that could shift the immune balance toward Th2 pathways [48, 49, 50]. However, given that this is an observational study, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

The strengths of this study include its prospective population‐based study design and recruitment of participants from the general population. Given its long follow‐up period, low loss to follow‐up, and use of population‐based cancer registries to identify cancer cases, we believe we were able to identify sufficient cancer incident cases. We also used cancer incidence as an endpoint instead of death, which allowed us to directly measure the risk of cancer associated with a history of allergy. This is the first study to examine the association between allergy, asthma, and cancer risk among the Japanese general population.

The study also has several limitations. Although the questionnaire specifically asked about physician‐diagnosed conditions, the responses were self‐reported, and the possibility of misclassification cannot be ruled out. We lacked details on allergy subtypes (e.g., hay fever, skin allergy, and food allergy), symptom severity, duration, and age at first diagnosis. We also lacked data on treatments, such as antihistamines or corticosteroids, which have anti‐inflammatory properties that may alter cancer risk. Furthermore, those who suffered from conditions such as eczema and atopic dermatitis, or who had not had their condition diagnosed by a medical doctor, most likely answered “no” in the baseline questionnaire, which could have skewed our findings. While we did consider the possibility of surveillance bias, in a Japanese healthcare system, preventative cancer screening is widely available and offered at relatively low cost. We therefore believe it is unlikely to have significantly skewed the detection of cancer incidence among those with allergy or asthma. Lastly, we could not calculate risk for certain cancer sites, such as glioma, due to the limited number of cases.

In summary, no overall association was observed between allergy, asthma, or their combined exposure and cancer risk. More studies are needed to understand the association between allergy, asthma, and the oncogenesis process in an Asian setting.
