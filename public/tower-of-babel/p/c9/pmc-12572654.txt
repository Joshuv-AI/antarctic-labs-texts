Modeling and analysis of fascioliasis disease with Katugampola fractional derivative: a memory-incorporated epidemiological approach
Authors: Rishi Kumar Pandey, Kottakkaran Sooppy Nisar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFascioliasis, a zoonotic disease, involves complex transmission across humans, animals, snails, and the environment. This study presents a fractional-order model using the Caputo-Katugampola derivative to capture memory effects in disease dynamics. The model, a system of nine fractional differential equations, is solved using the Chebyshev spectral method, with convergence ensured through rigorous analysis. We prove the existence and uniqueness of solutions via the Banach fixed-point theorem. Stability analysis derives the basic reproduction number , assessing disease-free and endemic equilibria. Sensitivity analysis identifies key parameters influencing transmission, informing control strategies. Numerical simulations illustrate the time evolution of all compartments, providing insights into fascioliasis dynamics. This framework enhances understanding of zoonotic disease epidemiology and demonstrates the utility of fractional calculus in modeling memory-dependent systems, offering a robust tool for studying infectious diseases.Keywords: Fascioliasis, Katugampola Fractional Derivative, Chebyshev Spectral Method, Epidemiological Modeling, Memory EffectsSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Applied mathematics

Introduction

Fractional calculus has emerged as a powerful framework in epidemiological modeling, addressing key limitations of classical integer-order approaches by incorporating memory effects and hereditary properties into disease transmission dynamics1. The non-local nature of fractional derivatives provides a more realistic representation of biological systems, capturing long-range dependencies and anomalous diffusion phenomena that characterize disease spread2,3. Recent methodological advances demonstrate how fractional-order operators account for time-dependent transmission rates and historical dependencies that influence epidemic trajectories, offering significant improvements over conventional models that assume Markovian processes4,5. The Caputo fractional derivative, in particular, has become instrumental in analyzing disease progression dynamics due to its well-defined initial conditions, while generalized Caputo-type operators have enabled the development of novel epidemic models with enhanced biological fidelity6–8. Empirical applications have validated the framework’s effectiveness across diverse pathogens, including Zika virus models that accurately reproduced crossover behavior in Colombian outbreak data and SARS-CoV-2 models that quantified memory effects in transmission dynamics9,10. Comparative studies of COVID-19 models have consistently demonstrated superior data-fitting capabilities of fractional approaches, with HIV modeling revealing complex viral dynamics that integer-order systems fail to capture11–13. Mathematical models using fractional calculus, have become essential for understanding and managing the spread of infectious diseases. Fractional-order models are effective because they capture the memory and non-local effects inherent in biological systems. This approach has been applied to a wide range of diseases, including the modeling and analysis of Cholera dynamics14 and a comprehensive analysis of the SARS-CoV-2 virus with reinfection15. Similarly, fractional differential equations have been used to create a mathematical model for Monkeypox disease transmission16. Other studies have focused on specific aspects of disease control, such as the dynamical analysis of HIV/AIDS, which incorporates optimal control strategies17. Researchers have also used nonlinear fractional-order SEIRS models with Crowley-Martin functional responses and saturated treatment to provide a comprehensive analysis of disease dynamics18. These works collectively highlight the significant role of mathematical modeling in public health.

The framework’s versatility extends to tuberculosis, hepatitis B, dengue, and conjunctivitis modeling, where fractional operators provide more accurate incidence projections by accounting for latency periods and heterogeneous mixing patterns3,19–22. While computational complexity and operator selection remain active research areas, emerging synergies with machine learning techniques such as physics-informed neural networks for solving fractional equations-are addressing these challenges while opening new methodological frontiers5,23. The interdisciplinary impact of fractional epidemiological modeling continues to grow, informing public health interventions through improved prediction of awareness campaign efficacy and offering theoretical insights that span viscoelastic materials, robotic control systems, and oncology applications24–28.

Memory effects play a fundamental role in disease transmission dynamics, where the progression of epidemics inherently depends on the historical interactions and exposure durations of infected individuals29. The non-Markovian nature of real-world disease spread necessitates mathematical frameworks that can capture these hereditary properties, making fractional calculus an indispensable tool for modeling such memory-dependent processes30. Different fractional operators incorporate memory through distinct mathematical formulations, each offering unique advantages for epidemiological applications31. The Caputo derivative remains prominent in ecological modeling due to its compatibility with classical initial conditions, with recent extensions introducing adaptive memory lengths that dynamically adjust to local transmission patterns2,32,33. This flexibility has proven particularly valuable in tuberculosis modeling, where Caputo-based formulations successfully integrate therapeutic interventions and awareness campaigns34. While the Riemann-Liouville operator maintains theoretical importance in fractional calculus, its practical limitations in handling real epidemiological data have increasingly favored alternative approaches35,36. The Atangana-Baleanu (ABC) derivative has emerged as a powerful alternative, employing Mittag-Leffler kernels to model non-singular memory effects in systems ranging from plant disease dynamics to Ebola transmission patterns37–39. Comparative studies of waiting time distributions reveal how ABC operators produce fundamentally different decay characteristics than classical fractional approaches, with significant implications for modeling viscoelastic biological processes35. The Caputo-Fabrizio derivative offers another non-singular kernel approach, demonstrating particular utility in modeling heat transfer phenomena relevant to disease transmission in fluid environments40. For computational implementations, the Grünwald–Letnikov formulation provides essential discretization schemes, enabling numerical solutions that preserve memory effects in simulated outbreaks41. These diverse fractional operators have collectively advanced the modeling of numerous pathogens, from tuberculosis dynamics with treatment delays to COVID-19 transmission with memory-dependent contact rates9,30,42. The framework has successfully captured complex interactions in hepatitis B/C co-infections, Ebola outbreak patterns, and malaria transmission dynamics through vegetation changes43–45. Emerging computational challenges persist, particularly regarding the non-local nature of fractional operators, prompting development of adaptive memory methods that balance accuracy with computational feasibility46. Recent interdisciplinary innovations are extending these concepts, such as fractional chain rule implementations in neural network architectures for outbreak prediction, signaling new frontiers in data-driven epidemiological modeling47.

The Katugampola fractional derivative represents a significant advancement in fractional calculus by generalizing classical operators while preserving essential differential properties often lost in other fractional formulations48,49. Its mathematical structure elegantly unifies the Riemann-Liouville and Hadamard derivatives through a parameterized framework, enabling continuous transitions between different fractional behaviors49,50. The Katugampola fractional derivative’s generalized two-parameter framework provides superior modeling capabilities for fascioliasis dynamics compared to Caputo-Fabrizio and Atangana-Baleanu derivatives, particularly in capturing complex memory effects and host-environment interactions51–53. While existing studies have successfully applied Caputo derivatives to fascioliasis modeling53, the Katugampola operator’s flexible structure offers enhanced adaptability for representing the disease’s nonlinear transmission pathways51,52. Further comparative studies are needed to fully quantify its advantages over other fractional approaches in epidemiological applications51,53. Unlike many fractional operators that abandon fundamental calculus rules, the Katugampola derivative maintains rigorous adherence to linearity, product, quotient, and chain rules—properties critical for analytical manipulations and physical interpretations48,54. This operational fidelity extends to power rule applications and higher-order generalizations, with emerging research exploring commutative and inverse properties that broaden its theoretical utility51,55. The derivative‘s versatility is further demonstrated through the Hilfer-Katugampola formulation, which creates an encompassing framework that interpolates between multiple established fractional operators including Caputo, Hadamard, and Riemann-Liouville variants56. Applied mathematics has leveraged these properties to solve complex fractional differential equations, particularly in financial mathematics where the operator captures memory effects in Black-Scholes models and in fluid dynamics through fractional Navier-Stokes formulations57,58. Numerical analysis of Katugampola-type systems has progressed through spectral methods employing Chebyshev polynomials, while stability analysis benefits from adapted Laplace transform techniques52,59,60. The operator has proven particularly effective in modeling viscoelastic materials where it simultaneously captures memory-dependent stress relaxation and time-varying viscosity, outperforming classical fractional models in certain polymer systems60. Environmental applications demonstrate its capability in modeling anomalous atmospheric diffusion processes, with recent work successfully identifying fractional orders from CO2 concentration data61,62. Comparative studies with alternative fractional operators reveal the Katugampola derivative’s unique advantages in problems requiring both non-locality and preservation of classical calculus rules, though its increased parametric flexibility introduces computational complexities that demand specialized numerical approaches63,64. The derivative’s physical interpretation continues to evolve, with ongoing research clarifying its relationship to underlying system dynamics across different application domains65,66. This balance between mathematical generality and operational practicality has established the Katugampola derivative as a transformative tool in contemporary fractional calculus research67.

Compartmental models have become indispensable for analyzing the complex transmission dynamics of fascioliasis, integrating multi-host interactions between humans, livestock, and intermediate snail hosts within environmentally mediated transmission cycles68,69. Human populations are typically structured using SIR-based frameworks, though contemporary models increasingly incorporate asymptomatic carriers and immune states to reflect the full spectrum of clinical manifestations and potential partial immunity70–72. The veterinary dimension introduces additional complexity, with cattle and sheep serving as critical reservoir hosts whose infection dynamics often require age-structured modeling approaches to account for differential susceptibility and transmission potential across life stages73,74.

Snail population modeling presents unique challenges, necessitating exposed (E) compartments to capture prepatent infection periods and detailed mortality functions reflecting parasite-induced fitness costs75,76. The inclusion of environmental drivers has become increasingly sophisticated, with temperature-dependent functions modulating both snail reproduction rates and parasite development timing, while hydrological models incorporate rainfall patterns to predict habitat suitability for intermediate hosts77,78. Recent advances have coupled these biological components with land use data through geographic information systems, enabling spatially explicit predictions of transmission hotspots under changing agricultural practices79,80. The mathematical toolbox for fascioliasis modeling has expanded beyond classical ODE approaches to include agent-based simulations that capture individual host movement patterns and network models that represent heterogeneous contact structures81,82. These frameworks have yielded critical epidemiological insights, particularly in estimating climate change impacts on disease distribution through shifting snail habitat ranges and accelerated parasite development rates22,83. Control strategy evaluations benefit from model-derived estimates of key parameters such as the basic reproduction number (), which inform targeted interventions ranging from anthelmintic administration to biological snail control53,84. Emerging challenges include the need to integrate within-host parasite dynamics with population-level transmission models, particularly regarding drug resistance evolution in Fasciola populations, while the growing One Health paradigm demands more unified frameworks that simultaneously address human, veterinary, and environmental health dimensions85–87. The field continues to evolve through incorporation of high-resolution environmental data, molecular epidemiology insights, and advanced computational methods that collectively enhance predictive accuracy for this climate-sensitive zoonosis.

Environmental mediation of transmission has been increasingly quantified through temperature-dependent functions affecting parasite development rates and habitat suitability models that incorporate hydrological patterns and land use data77,79. Spectral methods utilizing Chebyshev polynomials have emerged as a powerful tool for solving nonlinear fractional differential equations (FDEs), offering superior accuracy and exponential convergence rates for smooth problems89,90. The global approximation properties of Chebyshev polynomials, when combined with collocation frameworks, enable efficient transformation of FDEs into algebraic systems91,92. Recent methodological advances include the development of fractional Chebyshev differentiation matrices (FCDMs) that enhance numerical stability through recurrence relations at Chebyshev-Gauss-Lobatto points93. These methods have demonstrated remarkable versatility across diverse applications, from solving multi-order fractional delay differential equations with irrational delays90 to modeling financial systems through fractional Black-Scholes equations94. The approach has proven particularly effective for nonlinear fractional integro-differential equations, where Chebyshev series enable significant matrix dimension reduction95, and for epidemiological modeling through operational matrix approaches96. However, challenges persist regarding solution non-smoothness and computational costs, prompting research into adaptive refinement techniques and hybrid spectral-finite element approaches97,98. The development of preconditioning strategies shows promise for improving the conditioning of resulting algebraic systems, potentially expanding the method’s applicability to more complex fractional systems99. These ongoing refinements continue to solidify spectral methods’ position as a cornerstone of fractional calculus computations.

Despite these advances, previous models often overlooked key features relevant to fascioliasis, such as multi-host interactions, control strategies like drug treatment and livestock management, and the role of environmental contamination with delayed snail dynamics. Our study introduces a Katugampola fractional-order epidemiological framework that explicitly integrates memory, host-environment feedback, and time-delay effects, thereby distinguishing itself from earlier integer-order and fractional models. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first attempt to capture the memory-driven epidemiology of fascioliasis using the Katugampola derivative. We have developed and analyzes a Katugampola fractional fascioliasis disease model with a 9-variable compartmental structure, applying the Caputo-Katugampola fractional derivative alongside the Chebyshev spectral method to capture the intricate transmission dynamics of fascioliasis across human, animal, and snail populations, integrating environmental interactions and memory effects. The study explores the influence of memory effects on long-term disease dynamics, evaluates the stability properties and basic reproduction number to inform disease control strategies, and confirms the convergence of the Chebyshev spectral method in fractional epidemiological modeling. The key highlights of this work are threefold: (i) we construct a compartmental model that captures the multi-host transmission cycle of fascioliasis with Katugampola fractional derivatives; (ii) we derive analytical properties including positivity, boundedness, and equilibrium analysis under memory-driven dynamics; and (iii) we implement numerical simulations using collocation-based techniques to examine how memory and nonlocal effects influence disease persistence and control strategies.
