Synergistic nitrogen and endohedral MoCl5 doping for ultrahigh-conductivity carbon nanotube fibers
Authors: Tongzhao Sun, Jiankun Huang, Bowen Yu, Yanyan Zhao, Xiaocang Han, Zijian Wang, Xinshi Zhang, Xiangyang Li, Xiaoxu Zhao, Jinhui Yang, Lixing Kang, Yuanlong Shao, Muqiang Jian, Jin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lightweight and highly conductive carbon nanotube fibers (CNTFs) are attractive for flexible electronics, yet their performance remains constrained by inefficient charge transport. Here we report a synergistic doping strategy that integrates in-plane nitrogen doping with endohedral molybdenum pentachloride (MoCl5) incorporation to produce CNTFs with exceptional electrical properties and environmental durability. Nitrogen doping creates sidewall defect sites that promote MoCl5 encapsulation, yielding a strong charge-transfer effect and markedly increased carrier density. The resulting fibers achieve a high specific electrical conductivity of 14166 S m2 kg−1 and a current carrying capacity of 1241 A mm−2, surpassing copper by 115% and 28%, respectively. The CNTFs also exhibit high flexibility and environmental stability, retaining performance under thermal, mechanical, and solvent stresses. When woven into textiles, they deliver an electromagnetic shielding effectiveness of 92.7 dB (8.2-12.4 GHz). This work establishes a scalable doping approach for fabricating ultrahigh-conductivity CNTFs for advanced flexible electronics.

Introduction

Fibers with high flexibility, electrical conductivity, and current-carrying capacity are highly desired for the development of portable electronics and smart textiles1–6. Conventional metallic wires, such as copper (Cu) and aluminum (Al), have long been used as electrical conductors due to their excellent electrical conductivity. However, their high density, limited flexibility, and susceptibility to oxidation and corrosion in harsh environments significantly constrain their applicability in emerging lightweight and flexible electronic technologies. These limitations highlight the urgent need for alternative conductive materials that combine high electrical performance with mechanical robustness and environmental stability. Carbon nanotube fibers (CNTFs), formed by assembling individual carbon nanotubes (CNTs) into uniaxially aligned architectures, have garnered significant attention as a lightweight and mechanically robust alternative to conventional metallic wires7–12. These fibers are expected to translate the extraordinary properties of their nanoscale building blocks to the macroscale, offering low density, high mechanical strength and flexibility, excellent electrical and thermal conductivities, and robust environmental stability. However, despite these advantages, the electrical conductivity and specific electrical conductivity of CNTFs remain inferior to those of Cu (~60 MS m−1 and 6590 S m2 kg−1)13 and are significantly lower than those of individual CNTs (100 MS m−1)14. This performance gap arises from structural limitations in fiber assembly and the inherently low carrier density. As a result, charge transport within CNTFs remains inefficient, leading to increased Joule heating, greater energy dissipation, and reduced current-carrying capacity, thereby limiting their widespread utility in high-performance electrical and energy applications.

To address these challenges, various strategies have been explored to enhance the electrical conductivity of CNTFs through modifying their hierarchical structures and optimizing their compositions. Posttreatment approaches, such as solution-assisted wet-stretching15–18 and mechanical densification19–24, have been used to reorganize the hierarchical structures of CNTFs produced via direct aerogel spinning or vertically aligned array spinning. These approaches result in significant improvements in nanotube alignment, packing density, and intertube interactions, thereby reducing contact resistance between individual CNTs and enhancing electrical performance. However, despite these advancements, the electrical conductivity of the resulting fibers remains an order of magnitude lower than that of Cu. In contrast, CNTFs fabricated through liquid crystal spinning exhibit well-aligned and densely packed structures, achieving electrical conductivities in the range of 1–12 MS m⁻1 25–33. Additionally, an alternative approach involves metallizing CNTFs to fabricate CNT/metal core-shell fibers, which integrate the lightweight properties and high current-carrying capacity of CNTFs with the remarkable electrical conductivity of metals34–39. However, weak interfacial interaction between the CNTFs and the metal layer, coupled with limited long-term stability, poses significant challenges to the performance and applications of these hybrid fibers. Doping carbon-based fibers using acids40,41, halogens25,42–44, or metal halides45, has emerged as a promising strategy to increase the carrier density and effectively enhance the fiber conductivity. Doped fibers exhibit the electrical and specific electrical conductivities that rival those of metals, while maintaining the low density of pristine CNTFs (PCNTFs). However, there is still plenty of room for improving the electrical properties of CNTFs, and challenges such as the instability of dopants and associated performance degradation also continue to hinder the practical applications of these fibers46. Therefore, addressing these limitations is pivotal to the development of CNTFs with high electrical conductivity, ultrahigh specific conductivity, and remarkable performance stability, paving the way for their integration into advanced technological applications.

In this work, we develop a doping engineering strategy to fabricate CNTFs co-doped with in-plane nitrogen and endohedral molybdenum pentachloride (MoCl5), achieving significant enhancements in electrical performance. In-plane nitrogen doping introduces sidewall defects on the nanotubes, modestly improving electrical conductivity and creating sites for MoCl5 adsorption and encapsulation within the nanotube inner cavities. This synergistic approach yields CNTFs with a high specific electrical conductivity of 14166 ± 48 S m2 kg−1 and an excellent current-carrying capacity of 1241.38 ± 94.34 A mm−2, exhibiting 115% and 28% improvements compared to Cu, respectively. The doped fibers also exhibit high tensile strength, flexibility, and robust environmental stability, maintaining their performance under diverse operational conditions. These properties make them particularly well-suited for lightweight conductive wires and high-performance electromagnetic interference shielding textiles. This work highlights an approach to fabricate highly conductive CNTFs, offering a versatile and scalable pathway for their integration into next-generation electronic and textile applications.

Results

Fabrication and structural characterization of CNTFs

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, we developed a doping engineering strategy that integrated in-plane nitrogen doping with endohedral MoCl5 doping to enhance the electrical performance and stability of CNTFs. PCNTFs, produced via wet spinning, were sequentially treated with inductively coupled plasma (ICP) in the oxygen/argon and nitrogen/argon atmospheres to create sidewall defects and enable nitrogen doping, resulting in nitrogen-doped CNTFs (N-CNTFs). N-CNTFs were then subjected to a two-zone vapor transport process to achieve nitrogen and MoCl5 co-doping CNTFs (N/MoCl5-CNTFs). In this process, N-CNTFs and dopants were placed at the two opposite ends of a vacuum-sealed glass tube. Upon heating, MoCl5 sublimated and diffused toward the fiber zone, where it was absorbed and encapsulated within the nanotube inner cavities. The successful incorporation of MoCl5 into N/MoCl5-CNTFs was demonstrated through radial cross-section analysis using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM). TEM imaging (Fig. 1b) reveals that dark MoCl5 particles distribute within the nanotubes. This observation is further supported by Z-contrast HAADF-STEM imaging (Fig. 1c), which shows that particles with brighter contrast are clearly observed within the nanotube inner cavities. This brighter contrast is attributed to MoCl5’s higher atomic number (Z) compared to carbon47–49. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mappings confirm these findings, revealing a uniform spatial distribution of nitrogen (N) throughout the fiber, indicative of effective in-plane nitrogen doping. Simultaneously, preferentially localized molybdenum (Mo) and chlorine (Cl) within the nanotube channels corroborate the endohedral incorporation of MoCl5. As a result, the resulting N/MoCl5-CNTFs exhibit excellent electrical performance, with a high electrical conductivity of 27.34 ± 0.09 MS m−1, a significant specific electrical conductivity of 14166 ± 48 S m2 kg−1, and a current-carrying capacity of 1241.38 ± 94.34 A mm−2. We continuously produced monofilament and multifilament PCNTFs via wet spinning from a liquid crystalline solution of CNTs in chlorosulfonic acid (CSA) (Supplementary Fig. 1)27. The as-received CNTs were purified to reduce impurities and improve crystallinity. The purified CNTs exhibited a low oxygen content, and the average aspect ratio was subsequently estimated to be approximately 4300 using a capillary thinning rheometric method50,51 (Supplementary Fig. 2). The high-purity and high-crystallinity CNTs enable their dispersion in CSA, forming well-defined liquid crystals. PCNTFs were then obtained by extruding the spin dope through 1- or 20-hole nozzles into an acetone coagulation bath, followed by collection onto a rotating drum. The as-spun PCNTFs exhibit smooth surfaces and highly aligned, densely packed structures, as characterized by surface and cross-sectional images (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). Sequential treatments with oxygen/argon plasma and nitrogen/argon plasma were applied to introduce sidewall defects, enabling the in-plane nitrogen doping. Optimization of nitrogen/argon plasma exposure reveals that N-CNTFs retain a well-ordered structure after plasma treatments (Supplementary Fig. 3d–f), and a 5-min treatment results in the highest tensile strength and electrical conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 3g, h). In parallel, MoCl5-doped CNTFs (MoCl5-CNTFs) were prepared by exposing PCNTFs to a two-zone vapor transport process. A treatment duration of 48 h is found to optimize electrical conductivity without altering the structural integrity of fibers (Supplementary Fig. 4). Finally, N-CNTFs were further processed via the above two-zone vapor transport process to produce N/MoCl5-CNTFs, combining in-plane nitrogen doping with endohedral MoCl5 incorporation (Fig. 2a, b). We further reversed the doping sequence, where MoCl5 doping was performed initially, followed by nitrogen plasma treatment, producing CNTFs with opposite doping sequences (MoCl5/N-CNTFs). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, these fibers exhibit a significantly reduced electrical conductivity compared to both MoCl5-CNTFs and N/MoCl5-CNTFs. This reduction is likely due to the thermal effects associated with the plasma treatment, which may cause partial dopant desorption52. To assess the microstructure of these fibers, wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) measurements were performed to evaluate the nanotube orientation within fibers. The nearly unchanged azimuthal scan curves and Herman’s orientation factor values derived from WAXS patterns for PCNTFs, N-CNTFs, MoCl5-CNTFs, and N/MoCl5-CNTFs confirm their highly ordered structures (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating that the doping processes do not compromise the structural integrity of these fibers. We performed radial cross-sectional elemental analysis, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Raman spectroscopy to confirm the successful doping of the fibers. Elemental mappings demonstrate the homogeneous distribution of N and Mo throughout the fibers (Fig. 2d–f), originating from nitrogen plasma and MoCl5 doping, respectively. XPS was employed to investigate the chemical composition and bonding states of the fibers (Fig. 2g). For N-CNTFs, the N 1s spectrum confirms the successful incorporation of N, with pyridinic N identified as the predominant bonding configuration (Supplementary Fig. 7a and Table 1), known to induce p-type doping in CNTFs53. For N/MoCl5-CNTFs, the XPS spectrum shows the characteristic peaks corresponding to C 1s, Mo 3d, Cl 2p, N 1s, and O 1s. High-resolution Mo 3d spectrum of N/MoCl5-CNTFs exhibits four peaks at 231.8, 233.1, 234.9, and 236.3 eV, assigned to Mo(IV) 3d5/2, Mo(IV) 3d3/2, Mo(V) 3d5/2, and Mo(V) 3d3/2 states, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7b)54. The Mo4+ state likely arises from partial Cl liberation during the MoCl5 intercalation process55. Additionally, the C 1s spectrum of PCNTFs, centered at 284.5 eV, shifts by 0.4 eV toward lower binding energy upon doping (Supplementary Fig. 7c). This shift reveals electronic charge transfer from CNTs to the encapsulated material56–59. Raman spectroscopy using a 532 nm excitation wavelength provided further structural and electronic insights. The IG/ID value of N-CNTFs (46.51 ± 0.30), where ID and IG represent the D-band and G-band intensities of CNTFs, respectively, shows a slight decrease compared to that of PCNTFs (49.75 ± 0.39) (Supplementary Fig. 7d, and Table 2), consistent with the introduction of minor sidewall defects during nitrogen plasma treatment60. Similarly, MoCl5-CNTFs and N/MoCl5-CNTFs show IG/ID values of 48.54 ± 0.43 and 45.25 ± 0.33, respectively, indicating that the structural integrity of the nanotube lattice is largely preserved following both doping processes61, thereby supporting efficient charge transport performance. Additionally, Raman spectra of N-CNTFs, MoCl5-CNTFs, and N/MoCl5-CNTFs reveal progressive blue shifts of the G-band compared to PCNTFs, with the largest shift of about 19 cm−1 observed in N/MoCl5-CNTFs (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Table 3). These shifts are attributed to doping-induced charge transfer, wherein electron donation from CNTs to N and MoCl5 results in strong p-type doping and corresponding modifications to the CNTs electronic structure62,63.

To further investigate the transition in electrical behavior, we employed Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) to measure the surface potentials of the fibers (Supplementary Fig. 8). The surface potentials of N-CNTFs, MoCl5-CNTFs, and N/MoCl5-CNTFs are progressively higher than that of PCNTFs, corresponding to an increase in work function from 4.45 ± 0.08 eV to 4.56 ± 0.03, 4.63 ± 0.02, and 4.87 ± 0.03 eV, respectively (Fig. 2i). This increase in the work function is attributed to p-type doping effect of N and MoCl5, which induces a downshift in the Fermi level (EF) of CNTs57. These observations are further supported by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Simulations reveal that MoCl5 acts as a strong electron acceptor, with Bader charge analysis showing electron transfer of 0.85 e− per encapsulated molecule and 0.55 e− per adsorbed molecule (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Band structure analysis further shows that nitrogen doping lowers the EF, indicative of hole doping64. Subsequent MoCl5 doping leads to a further downshift in the EF (Supplementary Fig. 9c–f), reinforcing the increasingly p-type character of the system. These electronic modulations are consistent with both our spectroscopic and KPFM measurements (Fig. 2h, i). This exponential dependence highlights how progressive EF shifts lead to substantial increases in carrier density40,58,65. We further performed Hall effect measurements using a van der Pauw configuration on CNT films fabricated under the same doping conditions as the fibers. Although the van der Pauw method is not ideally suited for highly anisotropic materials, it still offers qualitative insights into intrinsic transport trends45, rather than quantitative values representative of the high-conductivity fibers. As expected, carrier mobility decreases upon doping due to enhanced carrier scattering in N-CNT and N/MoCl5-CNT films compared to PCNT films. In contrast, carrier densities for N-CNT, MoCl5-CNT, and N/MoCl5-CNT films increase substantially relative to PCNT films (Supplementary Table 4), consistent with the doping-induced enhancement in carrier density. In accordance with the Drude model, which defines that electrical conductivity is proportional to the product of mobility and carrier density, these results explain how even with a modest decrease in mobility, the marked rise in carrier density results in significantly improved electrical conductivity. Taken together, the high electrical conductivity of the fibers results from the synergistic effects of preserved structural integrity supporting adequate carrier mobility, and significantly elevated carrier density enabled by effective doping.

Mechanical and electrical performance of CNTFs

The ordered structures and increased carrier density significantly enhance the electrical conductivity and current-carrying capacity of the fibers. Electrical conductivity (σ) was measured using a four-point probe method over a fixed fiber length of 80 mm (Supplementary Fig. 10). To ensure reliable electrical contact, conductive silver paste was applied at both the current and voltage electrodes. A constant current was supplied through the outer electrodes, and the resulting voltage drop was measured across the inner electrodes. The average diameter of each fiber, obtained from the lateral SEM measurements (Supplementary Fig. 11), was used to calculate the fiber cross-sectional area applied in both mechanical and electrical property analyses. For each fiber type, five independent measurements were performed using multiple samples to determine the corresponding mechanical and electrical properties (Supplementary Tables 5–8). Notably, N/MoCl5-CNTFs obtained with a doping duration of 48 h exhibit an optimal composition, delivering an exceptional electrical conductivity of 27.34 ± 0.09 MS m−1, which is 3.20, 2.73, and 1.58 times that of PCNTFs, N-CNTFs, and MoCl5-CNTFs, respectively (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 12a, b, and Table 9). To evaluate the influence of fiber length on electrical conductivity, we further measured the raw resistance of N/MoCl5-CNTFs over extended lengths (100–200 mm). A strong linear relationship between resistance and fiber length is observed (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d), confirming the structural uniformity of the fibers. To evaluate the effectiveness of MoCl5 relative to other dopants, we prepared N/I2-CNTFs, N/CuI-CNTFs, and N/FeCl3-CNTFs using I2, CuI, and FeCl3 as dopants, respectively, via a similar two-zone vapor transport method. The electrical conductivity of N/I2-CNTFs reaches 16.89 ± 0.06 MS m−1, while N/CuI-CNTFs and N/FeCl3-CNTFs exhibit slightly lower conductivities of 25.97 ± 0.09 and 26.87 ± 0.08 MS m−1, respectively, compared to N/MoCl5-CNTFs (Supplementary Fig. 13). This trend suggests MoCl5 induces stronger p-type doping and more significant modulation of the CNTs electronic structure through charge transfer interactions, resulting in the highest electrical conductivity. These results highlight MoCl5 as the most effective dopant among those tested. N/MoCl5-CNTFs achieve a notable specific electrical conductivity of 14166 ± 48 S m2 kg−1 (or 13746 ± 47 S m2 kg−1 based on the linear density method; see Supplementary Table 9), exceeding those of gold, zinc, silver, and Cu by factors of 4.60, 4.49, 1.19, and 1.15, respectively, and is comparable to that of aluminum66 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 10). Additionally, N/MoCl5-CNTFs exhibit a high tensile strength of 2.49 ± 0.14 GPa and a specific tensile strength of 1.26 ± 0.07 N tex−1, significantly surpassing those of conventional metals. The current-carrying capacity is defined as the maximum current density sustained by a freestanding fiber under ambient air conditions. It is calculated by dividing the maximum current measured prior to failure by the fiber’s cross-sectional area. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 14c, the current density initially increases linearly with voltage until a sharp drop occurs, indicating fiber failure due to Joule heating30,36. Based on infrared imaging, the estimated failure temperature is approximately 630 °C (Supplementary Fig. 14d, e). N/MoCl5-CNTFs show a high current-carrying capacity of 1241.38 ± 94.34 A mm−2, yielding increases of 262, 217, and 63% compared with PCNTFs, N-CNTFs, and MoCl5-CNTFs, respectively (Fig. 3d). The specific current-carrying capacity of N/MoCl5-CNTFs is 6.43 × 104 A cm g−1, which is 5.87 times higher than that of commercial Cu wires67. A comparative analysis of the current-carrying capacity and electrical conductivity of N/MoCl5-CNTFs with those of previously reported CNTFs, buckypapers, metal/CNT composites, and Cu wires highlights the extraordinary combination of high current-carrying capacity and excellent electrical conductivity (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 11). We further investigated the temperature-dependent relative resistance of CNTFs and commercial Cu wires (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 15a). The electrical resistance of fibers at 300 K (R0) and other temperatures (RT) was compared, revealing that the minimum resistance of CNTFs appears near 33 K. Below this temperature, the resistance of CNTFs increases with decreasing temperature, and the plot of ln R versus T−1/4 shows a high linearity (Supplementary Fig. 15b), consistent with Mott’s variable-range hopping transport41. Above this temperature, the resistance of CNTFs increases with increasing temperature due to the phonon scattering (Supplementary Fig. 15c), showing metallic behavior. Additionally, the temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR) increases with the degree of doping. In contrast, Cu wires exhibit a continuous decrease in resistance as the temperature decreases. The TCR values for N/MoCl5-CNTFs and Cu wires within the 100–400 K range show that N/MoCl5-CNTFs possess a lower value than Cu.

We further compared the environmental stability of the doped fibers. While MoCl5-CNTFs exhibit a notable decline in the electrical conductivity over time, N/I2-CNTFs, N/CuI-CNTFs, N/FeCl3-CNTFs, and N/MoCl5-CNTFs maintain more significantly stable electrical conductivities over 50 days under ambient conditions (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 16a). Among these, N/MoCl5-CNTFs demonstrate the smallest change, indicating superior long-term stability. Additionally, N/MoCl5-CNTFs exhibit minimal changes in electrical conductivity after prolonged exposure to various solvents, in contrast to the marked decrease observed in MoCl5-CNTFs (Supplementary Fig. 16b). We also assessed the performance of N/MoCl5-CNTFs under cyclic thermal and mechanical stresses. The electrical conductivity of N/MoCl5-CNTFs decreases by only 21% upon heating to 200 °C and exhibits a minimal resistance increase of 1.2% following repeated thermal cycling (Fig. 4b). Moreover, the electrical resistance of N/MoCl5-CNTFs remains nearly unchanged, with only a 1% variation after 2000 bending cycles (Fig. 4c), underscoring the robust flexibility and durability of the fibers. To investigate the origin of the exceptional environmental stability observed in N/MoCl5-CNTFs, we performed additional HAADF-STEM and EDS analyses on the radial cross-sections of MoCl5-CNTFs and N/MoCl5-CNTFs. In MoCl5-CNTFs, bright contrast associated with MoCl5 predominantly locates in interstitial spaces between adjacent CNTs (Fig. 4d). Corresponding EDS mapping further confirms Mo and Cl signals mainly localize outside the nanotube interiors (Fig. 4e–h), in sharp contrast to the N/MoCl5-CNTFs, where MoCl5 is confined within the inner cavities of the nanotubes (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 17). This confirms that nitrogen doping promotes the endohedral incorporation of MoCl5. Conductive wires and electromagnetic shielding applications of CNTFs

The exceptional electrical performance and environmental stability of N/MoCl5-CNTFs make them highly suitable for applications as lightweight conductive wires and electrothermal fibers. Multifilament N/MoCl5-CNTFs act as a lightweight conductor capable of illuminating four bulbs (Fig. 5a). Additionally, the N/MoCl5-CNTF tow with a thickness of about 71 μm exhibits excellent electrothermal properties, making them promising candidates for high-performance fibrous heaters. To evaluate their electrothermal performance, an infrared thermal camera was used to monitor the temperature distribution and measure the saturated temperature under different applied voltages. At applied voltages of 0.5, 1.5, and 3 V, the saturated temperatures of fibrous heaters increase from 39, 211, to 397 °C, respectively (Fig. 5b, c), demonstrating high electrothermal efficiency. The fibrous heater also exhibits a rapid electrothermal response, with a heating rate of 1254 °C s–1 at a low voltage of 3 V, which is comparable with the cooling rate and outperforms most existing fibrous heaters68,69. The substantial electrical conductivity of N/MoCl5-CNTFs makes them promising candidates for electromagnetic interference shielding effectiveness (EMI SE). Leveraging the excellent mechanical flexibility and high tensile strength of as-obtained fibers, plain-woven textiles were successfully fabricated using a machine loom, with polyester fibers as the warp and PCNTFs or N/MoCl5-CNTFs as the weft (Supplementary Fig. 18). As shown in Fig. 5d, the EMI SE performance of PCNTFs- and N/MoCl5-CNTFs-based textiles was measured across the frequency range of 8.2–12.4 GHz (X-band). The EMI SE of N/MoCl5-CNTFs-based textiles is ~81.1 dB, significantly higher than that of PCNTFs-based textiles (50.9 dB). Furthermore, the EMI SE performance of double-layer N/MoCl5-CNTFs-based textiles reaches ~92.7 dB. Figure 5e compares the total EMI SE (SET), absorption EMI SE (SEA), and reflection EMI SE (SER) of different textiles. It is evident that SER and SEA significantly contribute to the overall SET, and increases in the electrical conductivity and textile thickness result in an improvement in SET70. To further elucidate the EMI shielding mechanism, we compared the power efficiency of absorption (A), reflection (R), and transmission (T) of the textiles. Notably, the power efficiency of R of all textiles is significantly higher than A and T at the frequency of 8.2–12.4 GHz (Supplementary Fig. 19). In particular, the power efficiency of R for N/MoCl5-CNTFs-based textiles is 0.966, higher than those of A (0.034) and T (8.04 × 10–7), suggesting the EMI SE performance is primarily attributed to reflection rather than absorption71,72. Figure 5f illustrates the EMI SE mechanism of textiles. Most incident electromagnetic waves are immediately reflected due to the impedance mismatch between the highly conductive N/MoCl5-CNTFs and free space. The residual electromagnetic waves are then efficiently attenuated by conductance loss and multiple internal reflections. Finally, after the multiple back-and-forth reflections within the dense fiber structure, the remaining electromagnetic waves are fully absorbed, followed by the minimal transmission73,74. Owing to the high EMI SE performance, N/MoCl5-CNTFs-based textiles can serve as an effective shielding barrier, capable of disabling the wireless phone charging (Fig. 5g–i). As a result, the woven N/MoCl5-CNTFs-based textiles show great potential for use in personal protection for EMI shielding fields.

Discussion

In summary, we demonstrate a doping engineering strategy for the fabrication of CNTFs co-doped with in-plane nitrogen and endohedral MoCl5. The incorporation of in-plane nitrogen doping creates sidewall defects on the nanotubes, facilitating the adsorption and encapsulation of MoCl5 within their inner cavities. This synergistic interaction enhances carrier density, leading to significant enhancements in the electrical conductivity and environmental stability of fibers. The resulting fibers exhibit significant electrical conductivity, current-carrying capacity, high tensile strength, flexibility, strong EMI shielding capacity, and robust resistance to various solvents, cyclic thermal, and mechanical stresses. Additionally, the strategy demonstrates broad generality and dopant compatibility, as evidenced by similarly high electrical conductivity and robust environmental stability achieved with I2, CuI, and FeCl3 dopants. This strategy provides a versatile and scalable platform for producing lightweight and highly conductive CNTFs, with promising applications in lightweight conductive wires and advanced functional textiles.

Methods

Materials and chemicals

Molybdenum pentachloride (MoCl5), molybdenum oxide (MoO3), chlorosulfuric acid (CSA, 99.5%), and acetone (>99.5%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used as received without further purification unless otherwise specified.

Purification of CNTs

The CNTs used in this study were purchased from Cnano Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China) and subsequently subjected to a purification process. The as-received CNTs were first dispersed in 30% H2O2 (1 mg mL−1) and stirred for 24 h, followed by vacuum filtration. The recovered CNTs were then dispersed in 12 M HCl (5 mg mL−1) and stirred for 12 h to remove residual metal catalysts. After neutralization with deionized water, the CNTs were frozen in liquid nitrogen and dried overnight via freeze-drying.

Preparation of wet-spun PCNTFs

PCNTFs were prepared via a wet spinning process based on our previously reported protocol27, with a key modification to improve alignment and fiber quality. Briefly, a liquid crystal spinning dope containing 0.8–1.5 wt% CNTs was prepared by dispersing CNTs into CSA. The dope was extruded through 1-, 20-, or 100-hole nozzles with an inner diameter of 0.1–0.31 mm into an acetone coagulation bath. To enhance the initial alignment of CNTs during extrusion, we designed the nozzles with a narrower convergent angle of approximately 10°. This adjustment improved flow stability and promoted more uniform orientation of CNTs along the fiber axis during coagulation and subsequent stretching. The spinning rates (0.01–0.2 mL min–1) and winding rates (2–3 m min–1) were carefully controlled to achieve a draw ratio above 1.6, facilitating the formation of as-spun fibers. The resulting fibers were further washed with distilled water and dried at 170 °C for 24 h.

Preparation of N-CNTFs

PCNTFs were placed inside a quartz reaction tube, positioned approximately 4 cm from the induction coil of a radio-frequency power supply (VERG-1000, Zhongshan K-Mate General Electronics Co., Ltd.). The fibers were dried at 100 °C under vacuum (~1 mbar) for 24 h to eliminate residual moisture. The dried fibers were then subjected to ICP treatment at a frequency of 13.56 MHz and a power of 50 W for 2 min in an O2/Ar (50/150 sccm) atmosphere. This treatment generated sidewall defects on the CNTs, enhancing the availability of nitrogen doping sites. Subsequently, the fibers were exposed to an N2/Ar plasma (50/150 sccm) under a background pressure of ~1 mbar, using the frequency of 13.56 MHz and power of 50 W, for 2–15 min to introduce nitrogen atoms into the nanotube structure, yielding the N-CNTFs.

Preparation of MoCl5-CNTFs

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 20, PCNTFs and a mixture of MoCl5 and MoO3 in a mass ratio of 1:0.6 were placed separately inside a glass tube. The tube was dried at 100 °C under vacuum (~1 mbar) for 24 h to remove residual moisture, then flame-sealed, and annealed at 300 °C for 9–72 h in a furnace with a heating/cooling rate of 10 °C min−1. After cooling to room temperature, the fibers were rinsed with acetone to remove residual dopants and dried at 60 °C to obtain the final MoCl5-CNTFs.

Preparation of N/MoCl5-CNTFs

N/MoCl5-CNTFs were prepared using the same two-zone vapor transport method. N-CNTFs and a mixture of MoCl5 and MoO3 in a mass ratio of 1:0.6 were loaded separately inside a glass tube. Following vacuum drying at 100 °C for 24 h (~1 mbar), the tube was flame-sealed and annealed at 300 °C for 9–72 h in a furnace. The resulting fibers were then washed with acetone and dried at 60 °C to yield N/MoCl5-CNTFs.

Preparation of N/I2-CNTFs, N/CuI-CNTFs, and N/FeCl3-CNTFs

For comparison, N-CNTFs and each dopant (I2, CuI, and FeCl3) were placed separately inside glass tubes, and dried at 100 °C under vacuum (~1 mbar) for 24 h to remove residual moisture. Subsequently, the flame-sealed glass tubes were annealed in a furnace at 200 °C for I2, 750 °C for CuI, and 360 °C for FeCl3, respectively. After cooling to room temperature, the fibers were removed, rinsed with acetone to eliminate adsorbed dopants, and dried at 60 °C to obtain N/I2-CNTFs, N/CuI-CNTFs, and N/FeCl3-CNTFs.

Measurement of the diameter of the fibers

The average diameter of each fiber was obtained from the lateral dimensions of the fibers’ SEM (FEI Quattro S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, operated at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV) images, based on thirty measurements for each sample. SEM images confirm that the fiber diameter is uniform along the axial direction. To further verify measurement consistency, the effective diameters were determined from cross-sectional area measurements. Cross-sections were prepared using FIB (Helios G4 UX, Thermo Fisher Scientific) cutting and subsequently characterized by SEM. The fibers exhibit an approximately circular cross-sectional morphology. Cross-sectional areas were quantified from the SEM images using ImageJ software (v1.54g). For each condition, at least three independently prepared fibers were analyzed to ensure statistical reliability. The effective diameters obtained from cross-sectional analysis agree well with those derived from the lateral SEM measurements under the assumption of circular geometry (Supplementary Table 9), confirming the reproducibility and low variability of the results. Accordingly, the average diameter of each fiber obtained from the lateral SEM measurements was used to calculate the cross-sectional area employed in both electrical conductivity and tensile strength analyses.
