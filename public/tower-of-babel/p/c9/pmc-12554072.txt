Dog deworming by smart collar: a 24-month field evaluation from a One Health prospective, randomized, double masked trial
Authors: Shijie Yang, Yuancheng Yang, Xuenian Xu, Chenqing Sun, Xiaojuan Zhang, Fan Li, Jianhong Hu, Baolin Zhong, Liying Wang, Ying Wang, Shuai Han, Chuizhao Xue, Xu Wang, Baixue Liu, Chun Fang, Guoxiang Kui, Zonglin Shen, Yu Feng, Shizhu Li, Ning Xiao, Yuhua Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEchinococcosis constitutes a major zoonotic parasitic disease with profound public health and socioeconomic implications. Dog deworming remains a cornerstone intervention endorsed by World Health Organization. This study comparatively assessed automated vs. manual praziquantel (PZQ) bait delivery systems for dog echinococcosis control.MethodsA prospective, randomized, double-blinded field trial employing a One Health framework was conducted in Tianzhu Zangzu Zizhixian, China—a cystic echinococcosis endemic region. Township-level cluster randomization allocated dogs to smart collar deworming group (SCDG) with monthly automated PZQ delivery and manual deworming group (MDG) with conventional bait administration. Intervention assignment remained masked between groups. Laboratory personnel were blinded during fecal antigen analysis using enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) evaluated efficacy via odds ratios (ORs) over 24 months, SPSS software (version 27.0) was used for data processing. Metrics included parasitological outcomes, deworming frequency, collar deployment and recovery rates.ResultsFrom June 2021 to July 2023, 1920 dogs (800 from SCDG, 1120 from MDG) were enrolled with owner consent. Among the 5119 fecal samples analyzed (2320 from SCDG, 2799 from MDG), 33 were antigen-positive (8 from SCDG, 25 from MDG). Baseline positivity showed no intergroup difference (SCDG: 0.8 % [6/792] vs. MDG: 1.2 % [13/1099], P = 0.36). At 24 months, SCDG achieved 0 % positivity (0/661) while MDG's was 0.6 % (5/789). Smart collars conferred significant protection (OR = 0.432, 95 % confidence interval [CI]: 0.194–0.959, P < 0.0001)—equivalent to 56.8 % infection risk reduction. At 12 months, the recycling rate and integrity rate of smart collars were 83.3 % (666/800) and 74.3 % (495/666), respectively; and the proportion of dogs dewormed 6–12 times per year was 86.7 % (577/666). At 24 months, the recycling rate and integrity rate of smart collars were up to 93.6 % (749/800, χ2 = 42.106, P < 0.001) and 94.1 % (705/749, χ2 = 107.269, P < 0.001), respectively. During this period, 91.6 % (1202/1312) of dogs received deworming 6–12 times per year. In 2023, a total of 647 smart collars were distributed, with the recycling rate and integrity rate of 99.8 % (646/647) and 96.4 % (623/646), respectively. The proportion of dogs dewormed 6–12 times per year increased to 96.6 % (625/646, χ2 = 34.969, P < 0.001).ConclusionsField deployment of smart collars proves operationally viable, sustainably enhancing deworming frequency while reducing dog infection rates more effectively than manual methods. The 56.8 % protective effect advancement mitigates environmental egg contamination, thereby lowering zoonotic transmission risk.Keywords: Dog deworming, Smart collar, Echinococcosis, One Health, Field evaluation, Randomized, Double masked trial

Introduction

Echinococcosis represents a zoonotic parasitic infection transmissible between humans and animals [1]. The two primary forms were recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as neglected tropical diseases [2], which are cystic echinococcosis (CE) resulting from Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato, and alveolar echinococcosis (AE) caused by Echinococcus multilocularis. CE imposes a considerable global health burden due to its extensive distribution and high regional prevalence. Conversely, AE exhibits greater virulence and mortality [[3], [4], [5]]. Within China's northwestern territories, CE is endemic across 370 counties spanning nine provincial divisions, with AE concurrently present in 115 of these counties [6,7].

Field epidemiology consistently identifies dogs as the principal definitive hosts for both E. granulosus and E. multilocularis [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Robust evidence confirms that routine canine anthelmintic treatment markedly reduces Echinococcus spp. transmission [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Since 2006, China has implemented nationwide monthly dog deworming using praziquantel (PZQ) to curb transmission. This initiative achieved substantial progress, reflected in the decline of fecal Echinococcus antigen positivity in dogs from 4.25 % (2012) to 0.44 % (2022) [7]. Nevertheless, sustaining monthly manual deworming for all dogs faces significant obstacles, including veterinary workforce shortages, logistical difficulties in reaching owned dogs, and prohibitive costs, particularly in remote, resource-limited regions. Surveys illustrate these challenges: in Xinjiang Uyghur Zizhiqu (2015), 36.8 % of the owners failed to administer baits [17]; in Xiji County (Ningxia Huizu Zizhiqu), only 68.9 % of household dogs received 6–12 treatments annually, with township coverage varying widely (24 %–84 %) [18]; in Gansu Province and Qinghai Province, merely 21.7 % and 47.5 % of owners adhered to monthly schedules [19,20]. Practically, accurate data on deworming coverage and frequency remain difficult to ascertain. Consequently, inadequate deworming contributes to persistent Echinococcus spp. prevalence in dogs: 0.40 % (Sichuan Province), 1.47 % (Yunnan Province), 0.74 % (Xizang Zizhiqu), 0.44 % (Gansu Province), 0.85 % (Qinghai Province), 0.23 % (Xinjiang Uyghur Zizhiqu), and 0.36 % (Ningxia Huizu Zizhiqu) [7]. This contrasts sharply with the WHO-recommended target of <0.01 % canine echinococcosis prevalence for national elimination of human infection [21].

Since 2016, the National Institute of Parasitic Diseases, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC) has developed a remote management system (RMS) based on Internet of Things (IoT) technology, integrated with smart collars dispensing PZQ baits to enhance deworming coverage and frequency [22]. A 6-month field trial in Kangma County, Xizang Zizhiqu, demonstrated increased coverage (57.7 %–85.7 %) and monthly deworming rates (40.3 %–91.4 %) [23]. A subsequent one-year randomized controlled trial (RCT) in Hezuo City (Gansu Province) and Seni District (Xizang Zizhiqu) achieved near-complete elimination of fecal Echinococcus antigen (reduced to zero) and an automated bait delivery rate of approximately 87.8 % using these collars [24]. Current research objectives now focus on evaluating the two-year field efficacy and PZQ bait delivery performance of smart collars vs. manual administration through prospective RCTs.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective, randomized, double-blinded, manual deworming-controlled field trial evaluated smart collars vs. conventional methods for administering PZQ baits to dogs. Conducted from June 2021 to June 2023 across 8 of 19 townships in Tianzhu Zangzu Zizhixian, Gansu Province, China (hereinafter referred to as Tianzhu County), the study assessed the efficacy of smart collars in enhancing deworming frequency and preventing Echinococcus infection. Sub-studies included owner questionnaires in the manual deworming group (MDG) and smart collar integrity assessments in the smart collar deworming group (SCDG). Backup dogs were recruited to mitigate attrition-related losses. Though extended to a third year due to sustained collar functionality, those results remain unreported pending follow-up completion. Ethical approval was granted by the National Institute of Parasitic Diseases, China CDC (Ethical Review Committee No. 2021019; Appendix 1). The trial profile is outlined in Fig. 1.

Trial profile.

Study area

Site selection criteria included: (1) representativeness of Qinghai–Xizang Plateau echinococcosis-endemic regions; (2) exclusive cystic echinococcosis prevalence (mitigating bias from fox/wildlife transmission); (3) well-documented dog populations; (4) controlled baselines with homogeneous town-level canine infection rates, and (5) committed local personnel (county CDC staff and township/village deworming teams). Tianzhu County (36°31′–37°55′N, 102°07′–103°46′E) occupies 7149.8 km2 on the northeastern Qinghai–Xizang Plateau in central Gansu Province, China. Characterized by high-altitude terrain (2040–4874 m above sea level), the region experiences a subarctic climate (mean annual temperature: 0.3 °C; precipitation: 397.4 mm). Administratively divided into 19 townships encompassing 178 villages, it sustains a population density of about 24 persons/km2 (total population: 200,700 across 66,040 households) (Fig. S1). Tianzhu County met the selection criteria, as a cystic echinococcosis endemic zone [15]. Epidemiological surveillance revealed a 0.22 % human echinococcosis prevalence in residents ≥12 years (1094 CE cases), canine fecal antigen positivity of 1.44 % in 6462 owned dogs and 1.17 % in 305 stray dogs, and livestock infection rates of 1.46 % (sheep) and 6 % (cattle) [15].

Randomization and sample size

Eight townships underwent cluster randomization via SPSS software (version 27.0) using permuted blocks. Four townships were assigned to SCDG and four to MDG (Fig. 1). Sample size estimation employed Walters’ normal approximation [24] for a two-group parallel design, assuming 1.2 % baseline fecal antigen positivity (MDG preliminary survey), target SCDG prevalence of 0.01 % at 24 months, α = 0.05, and power = 80 %. This yielded 666 dogs (balanced). Accounting for 20 % (SCDG) and 50 % (MDG) attrition, 1920 dogs (800 in SCDG, 1120 in MDG) were enrolled.

Targeted dogs

All township-registered dogs were screened. Inclusion criteria: (1) residence in selected townships, (2) owner consent, (3) clinical health. Exclusion criteria: (1) age <1 year or weight <4 kg, (2) pregnancy, (3) unsuitable collar fit, (4) owner refusal, (5) adverse collar reaction, (6) morbidity or age >12 years. Baseline distribution and characteristics of the target dogs in 82 villages of 8 townships in the two groups are summarized in Appendix 2A.

Blinding procedures

Cluster randomization assigned townships to SCDG or MDG. In SCDG, County CDC staff obtained door-to-door consent from listed dog owners for smart-collar deworming. Inter-group blinding was strictly maintained: SCDG and MDG personnel operated in physically segregated teams with distinct communications using non-identifiable codes (e.g., “Group A/B”), preventing cross-protocol awareness. Within groups, village-level implementation was intentionally unblinded: village veterinarians openly executed their assigned intervention to ensure fidelity but received no information about the other group's procedures. Village vets collected fecal samples labeled with local IDs. Before laboratory transfer, an independent coordinator replaced all identifiers with randomized alphanumeric codes and destroyed the linkage table. Laboratory technicians processed samples in randomized batches using standardized procedures, recording results against masked codes only. Statisticians analyzed de-identified data, maintaining blinding until final comparison.

Remote management system (RMS)

A hierarchical RMS (version 3.0.1) developed by our team, which is maintained and upgraded by Shanghai Yier Information Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) [22], was deployed at Tianzhu County CDC in June 2021, integrating epidemiological data from selected townships across three categories: static, optional, and dynamic records [22]. The six-tier structure (national to village levels) assigned township/veterinarians to affix collars and input data, with county administrators overseeing RMS operations.

Interventions

In SCDG, targeted dogs received smart collars that automatically delivered PZQ baits [[23], [24], [25]] (Fig. 2A and B). Collars (12 baits each) were fitted per sampling lists, with RMS enabling real-time deworming monitoring and scheduled release. Non-collared dogs (ineligible individuals and eligible dogs exceeding sample size requirements) received conventional manual deworming (like MDG). In MDG, conventional manual monthly deworming was implemented, and medication was conducted via door-to-door delivery of PZQ chewable tablets by village veterinarians during scheduled visits. Deworming frequency in the SCDG was assessed by enumerating residual PZQ tablets in the smart collars, while in MDG, assessment required cross-verification of village veterinarian deworming logs against dog owner questionnaire records. Both groups received state-approved PZQ tablets (100 mg/tablet) that masked PZQ's bitterness, administered via weight-based dosing: one tablet/month for dogs of 5–20 kg; two tablets/month for dogs >20 kg. Dogs' weight distribution was summarized in Appendix 2B. Health education, lamb vaccination, and stray dog management were also implemented in the two groups.

MDG questionnaire

Tianzhu CDC technicians conducted surveys (May 2022 and June 2023) assessing owner knowledge, focusing on bait delivery methodology (“Who” and “How”) and deworming frequency. Linguistically validated (Chinese/Tibetan) questionnaires used unique IDs and were administered bilingually.

Collar retrieval and reaffixing

Each collar (12-bait capacity) released baits monthly. After 12 months (May 2022), SCDG collars were collected (Fig. 2C and D) and shipped to Shanghai for evaluation. Assessments included: bait compartment integrity, power status, residual baits, and functionality. Collars were categorized as: (1) fully functional, (2) repairable, or (3) irreparable. Categories (1) and (2) were reloaded with 12 baits; category (3) and unretrieved collars were replaced. Functional reloaded/replacement collars were reaffixed by June 2022 for 12 additional months. Final retrieval occurred post-24th bait release (May 2023).

Fecal sample collection and analysis

Village veterinarians collected samples at three timepoints (the first time was set as baseline) in the two groups as indicated in Fig. 1. Samples were bagged, labeled, transported to Tianzhu CDC, stored at −80 °C (≥7 days), and then transferred to store at −20 °C until analysis [26]. Canine Fecal Echinococcus Antigen Detection Test Kit (Shenzhen Combined Biotech; sensitivity 95.45 %, specificity 96.97 %; batch 20200801) was used to detect Echinococcus antigen.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes: (1) odds ratios (ORs) (smart vs. manual deworming); (2) 24-month fecal antigen positivity. Secondary outcomes: (1) collar recycling and integrity rates; (2) proportion of dogs dewormed 6–12 times (per 12 or 24 months).

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel 2019 software was used for data management and SPSS software (version 27.0) was used to perform analyses. Categorical variables presented as frequencies/percentages; χ2 tests compared inter-group differences. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) [27] were used to analyze longitudinal ORs with 95 % confidence interval (CI) for deworming efficacy, while accounting for missing fecal data. GEE database and variable assignments were elaborated in Appendix 3 and 4). Two-tailed tests were used to determine statistical significance, with a threshold of P < 0.05.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the field trial had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report. All authors, including the corresponding author had full access to all the data in the study and had final responsibility for the decision to submit for publication.

Results

Dog enrollment and baseline characteristics

Cluster randomization encompassed 19 townships housing 6542 registered dogs. From eight selected townships, 3210 dogs were screened, 1290 were excluded (Fig. 1). Within the SCDG cohort, 60.3 % (882/1462) met eligibility criteria, yet only 54.7 % were enrolled. Exclusions comprised sufficient sample attainment (5.6 %), age <1 year or weight <4 kg (12.9 %), and oversized physique (10.9 %). Ultimately, 1920 dogs underwent cluster randomization: 800 to SCDG and 1120 to MDG (Fig. 1). Baseline measurements revealed a mean weight of (10.4 ± 4.1) kg (n = 1516; range: 4–20 kg), mean dental age of (4.8 ± 2.4) years (n = 1901; range: 1–12 years), and sex distribution of 650 females and 1267 males (n = 1917) (Appendix 2).

Echinococcus antigen positivity outcomes

A total of 5189 fecal samples were collected (2349 from SCDG; 2840 from MDG), with 5119 analyzed (2320 from SCDG; 2799 from MDG). Baseline Echinococcus antigen positivity showed no significant inter-group difference (SCDG: 0.8 %; MDG: 1.2 %; χ2 = 0.837, P = 0.36). At 12 and 24 months, Echinococcus antigen positivity in SCDG declined to 0.3 % (2/654) and 0 % (0/661), respectively. GEE detected no significant temporal trend (Wald χ2 = 1.239, P = 0.266). All 213 samples from newly recruited SCDG dogs were tested negative. Echinococcus antigen positivity in MDG decreased to 0.8 % (7/911) and 0.6 % (5/789) at 12 and 24 months, respectively (Table 1), with no statistical significance (Wald χ2 = 1.747, P = 0.418).

GEE model analysis

GEE indicated no significant “group-by-time” interaction effect (χ2 = 0.253, P = 0.615). Time effect analysis revealed no significant differential infection risk across timepoints overall (Wald χ2 = 5.028, P = 0.083), though SCDG exhibited a faster decline. Group effect analysis demonstrated significant superiority for SCDG (Wald χ2 = 4.316, P = 0.038), with smart collars conferring a protective OR of 0.432 (95 % CI: 0.194–0.959). This translates to a 56.8 % reduction in infection risk vs. manual deworming. Conversely, manual deworming carried a 2.325-fold higher infection risk compared to smart collars (Table 2).

Smart collar performance metrics

All 800 SCDG dogs received functional smart collars in June 2021 (Fig. 2A and B). Initial observations confirmed bait suitability and canine acceptance (Appendix 5 video). At the 12-month interim (May 2022), 666 collars were retrieved (recycling rate: 83.3 %). Functional assessment showed that 495 (74.3 %) collars were fully operational (integrity rate), 152 (22.8 %) were with minor damage, and 19 (2.9 %) were irreparably compromised. By the 24-month endpoint (June 2023), both recycling and integrity rates increased significantly compared to those in 2022 (93.6 % and 94.1 %, respectively; P < 0.0001). Sub-analysis of the 647 collars reaffixed in 2022 demonstrated near-complete recovery (recycling rate: 99.8 % [646/647]; integrity rate: 96.4 % [623/646]) (Fig. 1, Table 3, Table 4).

Deworming frequency and efficacy

Analysis of deworming frequency revealed significantly higher proportions of dogs in SCDG receiving 12, 11, and 10 treatments annually (53.7 %, 9.2 %, and 8.0 %, respectively) compared to those in MDG (31.9 %, 5.8 %, and 6.9 %, respectively) at the 12-month mark (Table 5, Fig. 3A). By the 24-month endpoint, SCDG demonstrated substantial increases across all measured frequencies (12, 11, 10, 9, 8, and 7 times/year: 41.3 %, 12.9 %, 10.2 %, 10.1 %, 8.5 %, and 7.2 %, respectively) compared to MDG (27.6 %, 5.9 %, 6.6 %, 7.0 %, 5.3 %, and 4.8 %, respectively) (P < 0.001; Table 5, Fig. 3B). This progression indicates continuous utilization of the smart collar for a duration of 24 months leads to a sustained increase in the benefits associated with higher deworming frequency. Furthermore, among collars reused in 2023 (n = 646), 96.7 % (625/646; χ2 = 34.969, P < 0.001) achieved 6–12 treatments annually, maintaining efficacy comparable to that of 2022 (Fig. 3C). In comparison with SCDG, MDG consistently exhibited lower proportions of dogs receiving >6 treatments/year without significant inter-annual variation (Table 5, Fig. 3C).

Discussion

As a preventable neglected zoonosis prioritized by WHO, echinococcosis control requires integrated strategies combining canine deworming, livestock management, and public health interventions, which can accelerate progress by 5–10 years [12]. While localized successes exist in confined settings [12], China's vast western endemic zones—characterized by expansive grasslands, complex ecologies, and high infection pressure—present distinct epidemiological challenges unmatched in scale or severity elsewhere. This context underscores the urgent need for innovative tools like automated deworming collars to achieve China's national goal of controlling human echinococcosis across 370 counties by 2030 [28].

China mandates monthly canine deworming (100 % coverage), exceeding WHO recommendations (4–8 treatments/year; >90 % coverage) [21]. Our study demonstrates that smart collars significantly outperform conventional manual monthly deworming (conducted via door-to-door delivery of PZQ chewable tablets by village veterinarians during scheduled visits) in sustaining high-frequency deworming. While absolute monthly adherence (12 treatments/year) reached only 41.3 % in SCDG (vs. 27.6 % in MDG), the critical metric of ≥6 treatments/year rose to 91.6 % in SCDG at 24 months—a 36.7 % absolute increase over MDG (P < 0.001). This aligns with but substantially extends prior short-term evaluations: whereas earlier pilot studies [22,23] reported 6-month compliance improvements, our 24-month data confirm sustained frequency enhancement, addressing a key limitation of transient interventions.

Device applicability remains constrained, with only 60.3 % (882/1462) of dogs deemed collar-suitable. Primary exclusions involved physiological factors: immature size/weight (12.9 %), oversized physique (10.9 %), and adverse reactions (4.7 %)—highlighting needs for ergonomic redesign and size diversification. Nevertheless, functional performance was robust: estimated deworming coverage reached 96.8 % at 12 months and 99.3 % at 24 months among deployed collars. Initial suboptimal recovery (83.3 %) and integrity (74.3 %) rates at 12 months—primarily due to collar loss (91.8 % of attrition)—improved significantly (P < 0.0001) to 93.6 % and 94.1 %, respectively, at 24 months through management refinements. This trajectory suggests scalability with iterative device optimization.

Initial 12-month collar retrieval (83.3 %) and functional integrity (74.3 %) metrics proved suboptimal. Attrition analysis identified two primary failure modes: inaccessible owner-dog pairs during surveillance visits (8.2 %) and physical collar disappearance (91.8 %). Implementation of enhanced device management protocols and performance optimization enabled successful re-deployment of 800 collars—comprising 647 refurbished units and 153 replacements for severely damaged or lost devices. Concurrently, 134 previously excluded dogs underwent re-screening: 42 attained eligibility through maturation, while 92 met inclusion criteria unchanged from baseline. These interventions collectively elevated 24-month performance to 93.6 % retrieval and 94.1 % integrity rates (P < 0.0001). Notably, SCDG maintained stable attrition (16.8 % at 12 months; 16.9 % at 24 months), contrasting sharply with MDG's progressive attrition increase (17.5 %–28.9 %).

SCDG achieved progressive Echinococcus antigen reduction, declining to 0.3 % at 12 months and 0.0 % at 24 months. This mirrors elimination timelines reported in smaller studies: 6 months in Xizang Zizhiqu [23], 12 months in Gansu Province, Xizang Zizhiqu and Qinghai Province [24,25]. Crucially, GEE analysis quantified smart collars’ protective superiority: a 56.8 % reduction in infection risk (OR = 0.432; 95 % CI: 0.194–0.959) compared to manual deworming. This effect size falls within the range of prior regional observations (27.0 %–81.8 % risk reduction) [24,25], but represents the first longitudinal validation under controlled trial conditions. Notably, the protective effect intensified over time despite comparable baseline positivity—suggesting cumulative benefits of sustained high-frequency deworming.

Several study limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our findings. Firstly, due to the performance of the smart collar itself and the limitation of the sample size, the deworming coverage rate of the smart collar in four townships of SCDM is only 54.7 % (800/1462), it inevitably interfered with the actual deworming effect. Secondly, the loss to follow-up rate of 16.8 % and 28.9 % in SCDG and MDG, respectively, at the end of 24 months impacted the outcome of GEE. Thirdly, while SCDG deworming frequency was objectively verified through residual tablet counts, MDG adherence relied on unverified self-reports from veterinarian logs and owner questionnaires, leading to potential overestimation. Fourthly, the evaluation period of 24 months is still relatively short, and the sample size of 800 collars is still not large enough. A more objective evaluation of the effect still requires long-term and large-scale on-site collar application. Fifthly, as village deworming workers have multiple responsibilities, their regular meetings inevitably involve discussions of the study intervention, leading to unblinding and bias. Lastly, the inclusion of cost-effectiveness analysis in future agendas is imperative for further assessing the potential application of smart collars in real-world scenarios.

In conclusion, this trial confirms that smart collar technology is operationally feasible in challenging field settings, driving significant improvements in deworming frequency sustainability. Crucially, it provides the highest-quality evidence to date that automated delivery confers superior and progressive protection against canine Echinococcus infection—reducing environmental contamination and zoonotic spillover risk. Future iterations addressing ergonomic limitations and economic barriers could transform echinococcosis control paradigms in endemic regions globally.
