Abiotic synthesis of RNase-resistant phosphodiester and pyrophosphate-linked polymers via thermodynamically controlled wet-dry cycling
Authors: Povilas Simonis, Hannah V Rheinstädter, Viktoras Masevičius, Ralph E Pudritz, Katherine E Bujold, Maikel C Rheinstädter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the abiotic synthesis of structurally modified, RNase-resistant oligonucleotides with phosphodiester backbones from non-activated mononucleotides of adenosine monophosphate (AMP) and uridine monophosphate (UMP), and pyrophosphate linked D-ribose-5’-phosphate polymers under simulated wet-dry cycling conditions. These polymers were generated in a dedicated simulation chamber, under well controlled and reproducible thermodynamic conditions. The polymers lead to diffuse bands in electrophoresis gels using fluorescence detection with SYBR Gold at an excitation wavelength of 365 nm, a key spectral signature that allows us to distinguish them from standard nucleic acids. We show that these polymers are stable against enzymatic degradation by RNase 1, DNase 1, and DBR1, however partially degrade in alkaline hydrolysis. Evidence for polymerization was obtained in UV-vis and IR spectra. SAXS suggests aggregates in solution of approximately 120 nucleotide and ribose phosphate units. 31P NMR provided evidence for the formation of a phosphodiester backbone in cycled AMP/UMP mixtures, and the formation of a pyrophosphate backbone when cycling ribose phosphate. This suggests that these abiotically formed polymers are not perfect copies of canonical RNA; they were likely structurally modified (e.g., with missing bases), yet they are stable enough to persist and form distinct bands on a gel. These findings demonstrate that thermodynamic control within an environmental simulation chamber can drive the formation of complex, enzyme-resistant biopolymers without the use of activating agents, offering a robust model for exploring localized prebiotic synthesis pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56548-9.Keywords: Oligonucleotides, Ribose polymers, Abiotic synthesis, Nucleic acids, Wet-dry cycles, Prebiotic chemistry, Environmental simulatorSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Chemistry

Introduction

A major question related to the emergence of life is how RNA polymers first formed under prebiotic conditions, in the absence of metabolism1,2. The polymerization of nucleotides in an aqueous solution is endergonic, making this critical first step towards living systems thermodynamically unfavorable3,4. Moreover, newly formed RNA strands would have been highly susceptible to hydrolysis, precluding the accumulation of biogenic polymers in solution5–7. Thus, the mechanism by which RNA polymers may have formed in aqueous prebiotic environments is unknown8,9. Overcoming this aqueous condensation problem requires shifting our perspective from a single, global prebiotic ocean to recognizing the early Earth as a complex mosaic of highly localized physicochemical regimes. Resolving the thermodynamic barriers of endergonic polymerization relies heavily on the specific environmental and physical forces inherent to these local niches10,11.

The thermodynamic barriers to polymer formation have been overcome experimentally by utilizing chemically activated mononucleotides12,13. Pioneering work conducted by Orgel and colleagues achieved non-enzymatic RNA polymerization using imidazole-activated mononucleotides14,15,65. In this mechanism, aromatic heterocycles such as imidazole act as leaving groups that are important in driving non-enzymatic extensions of RNA templates at significant rates16. Huang and Ferris further demonstrated that polymerization can be enhanced by concentrating the activated mononucleotide derivatives on montmorillonite clay surfaces17,18. These activated nucleotides have greater reaction rates but would likely not have been readily available in the prebiotic environment2,19. Thus, there may have been a mechanism to kinetically trap newly formed polymers and promote phosphodiester bond formation without the use of activated nucleotides 20,21,27.

Fluctuating conditions on early Earth, such as repeated dehydration-hydration cycles may have provided the energy required to facilitate this process22,23. Kinetic simulations developed by Ross and Deamer demonstrate that non-enzymatic polymerization of non-activated nucleotides is favorable in highly concentrated solutions that undergo cycles of hydration and dehydration24–26. In the dry phase, monomers that are close to each other can form bonds while the ensuing wet phase allows for diffusion and repositioning of the molecules27,28. Molecular crowding also enhances associations between molecules, providing an opportunity for favorable interactions such as molecular complementarity29,30. Oligomers are susceptible to hydrolysis in the wet phase but are then allowed to combine and form even longer polymers in the dehydration phase31,32. Rather than yielding pure, canonical RNA strands, these fluctuating cyclic environments act as a dynamic environmental filter that drives messy chemistry. Such localized, non-equilibrium regimes impose selective physical pressures on complex precursor mixtures, favoring the accumulation of robust, highly heterogeneous, and degradation-resistant structures out of chaotic monomer pools33,34.

These cycling conditions would have been commonplace on early Earth, arising from volcanic activity, seasonal variation, and temperature changes between day and night23,35. Volcanic hydrothermal fields likely contained collections of ocean and rainwater that continuously evaporated and refilled36,37. Thermal cycling in these ponds would have significantly increased solute concentrations and consequently increased the likelihood of bond formation between individual monomers27,32. This effect has been observed experimentally using multiple different conditions and substrates38,39. For instance, glycine oligopeptides were readily produced in fluctuating wet-dry cycles in a simulated prebiotic environment containing clay and water40,41. Lipid-assisted and salt-promoted synthesis of RNA polymers have also been demonstrated under similar conditions23,42–44. One study reported that RNA-like polymers up to 100 units in length emerged from a mixture of non-activated nucleoside 5’-monophosphates and phospholipids after multiple thermal cycles44. Phospholipids, which may have played a role in forming the first protocells, enhanced bond formation by concentrating the reactants and encapsulating the products in lipid vesicles44–48,66. Despite inspiring initial results, there are not many instances of follow-up studies replicating them, quantifying and characterizing the products27,49. Song, Simonis, Deamer and Zare have recently shown that uridine and adenosine monophosphate form oligomers up to 53 nucleotides long in cycles of wetting and drying by mass spectrometry and nanopore sequencing50,51. However, the low reported yields of RNA polymers from such experiments can complicate the detection and quantification of the products6,52,53.

In this study, we used a custom-built environmental simulation chamber to model a highly specific, localized prebiotic regime: an acidic wet-dry cycle. We reliably and quantitatively simulated this niche using unbuffered monomer solutions starting at pH 2 to 3, operating under a nitrogen atmosphere illuminated by white light, and subjected to repeated thermal cycling between 10 °C and 98 °C. To isolate the effects of thermodynamic cycling from simple dehydration within this specific regime, we contrasted our multi-cycle protocol against a single dry-down control. We demonstrate that under these harsh, acidic conditions, a single dehydration event leads strictly to template damage and degradation; it is exclusively through the environmental filtering of repeated wet-dry cycling that this regime acts as a kinetic trap, fostering polymerization. Our goal was to determine what polymers of adenosine monophosphate (AMP) and uridine monophosphate (UMP) mononucleotides might form under these conditions, and to characterize their properties using various biochemical approaches and NMR spectroscopy. We found that wet-dry cycling of AMP and UMP mononucleotides produces heterogeneous, short-chain, oligonucleotides with phosphodiester bonds but significant structural modifications, conferring enzyme resistance. Conversely, wet-dry cycling of D-ribose-5’-phosphate yielded polymers of ribose linked by pyrophosphate backbones, formed through a disproportionation reaction, which also demonstrate enzymatic resistance, indicating that these molecules deviate from the single-stranded, biological RNA structure, although they share structural analogy with pyrophosphate-linked nucleic acids such as poly(ADP-ribose) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH/NAD+). This shows that monomeric building blocks like AMP, UMP, and ribose-5’-phosphate can form polymers under early Earth-like conditions, potentially revealing pathways for prebiotic synthesis and suggesting how these molecules could have persisted before biological systems emerged.

Beyond their implications for the origins of life, the ability to synthesize enzyme-resistant oligonucleotides from simple precursors presents potential opportunities for biotechnology and medicine. Specifically, the generation of phosphodiester- and pyrophosphate-linked polymers that naturally evade standard degradation pathways offers a potential platform for the development of novel, stable nucleic acid therapeutics. Furthermore, our approach utilizes precise thermodynamic control within an environmental simulation chamber to drive these condensation reactions, establishing a framework for sustainable, green chemistry that mimics natural energy cycles rather than relying on hazardous activating agents or organic solvents.

Materials and methods

Our experimental strategy was designed to first synthesize polymers from non-activated precursors, such as AMP, UMP, and D-ribose-5’-phosphate, under simulated prebiotic conditions using wet-dry cycles. These experiments were conducted in a custom-built simulation chamber, which allows for precise control over environmental parameters such as temperature, pressure, atmospheres, radiation, and humidity.

Following synthesis, our primary method for detection and size separation was agarose gel electrophoresis. The resulting polymers were visualized using the fluorescent nucleic acid stain SYBR Gold. A key aspect of our analysis involved multi-wavelength imaging of the gels, which allowed us to differentiate the abiotic products from standard nucleic acids based on their distinct fluorescence and absorbance profiles.

To elucidate the chemical nature and properties of these polymers, we then employed a suite of characterization techniques. The stability of the products was tested against both chemical degradation (alkaline and acid hydrolysis) and enzymatic digestion using RNase 1, DNase 1, and the debranching enzyme DBR1. We used 31P NMR spectroscopy to elucidate the chemical structure of the polymer backbones, which provided evidence for either phosphodiester or pyrophosphate linkages. Ancillary techniques for further structural analysis included UV-vis and infrared spectroscopy, and Small-Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS) to determine the size of the resulting aggregates. Finally, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry was used to identify the formation of reaction byproducts to validate the pathway for the formation of ribose phosphate polymers.

The simulator chamber

Wet-dry cycles were conducted within a custom-built environmental chamber (Angstrom Engineering, Hamilton, Ontario, Canada), shown in Fig. 1a. The simulator allows for precise, simultaneous control of a wide range of environmental parameters, including temperature (−30 °C to 130 °C), relative humidity (0 – 100% RH), radiation (185 – 1,000 nm, from UV to far-infrared), pressure (0.1 – 760 Torr), and gas composition. The simulator is also equipped with both light bulbs (200 W/m2) for UVC (180 – 280 nm) and UVB radiation (280 – 315 nm) and LED arrays (300 W/m2) capable of generating UVA (315 – 400 nm), visible, infrared, and far-infrared radiation. The different components are shown in Fig. 1b. Up to four different gas species can be introduced into the chamber via gas lines to create specific atmospheric compositions. Wet/dry cycles are mimicked by cycling the relative humidity. This is managed precisely by splitting the incoming gas flow: one fraction is bubbled through a water reservoir for humidification, creating “wet” gas saturated with water, while the remaining “dry” gas flows directly into the chamber. All parameters are computer-controlled through the Aeres integrated recipe-driven software platform. This system allows for the creation of customized recipes that mimic cyclic changes in all parameters. These capabilities were developed to enable a wide range of experiments on prebiotic chemistry.

Wet-dry cycling conditions

Solutions of RNA, AMP, UMP, AMP/UMP mixtures and ribose phosphate along with controls, were prepared and dried in 96-well plates before placement in the simulator. All simulations were conducted at ambient pressure under a nitrogen atmosphere illuminated by white light created by LEDs at 30 W/m2. Fig. 2a shows the simulator’s interior at different simulation stages. Fig. 2b diagrams the corresponding protocol. Samples are initially equilibrated at 98 °C to ensure complete drying. The temperature is then lowered to 10 °C, while simultaneously increasing the humidity setpoint to 75% RH. The wet-cold cycle is achieved by first increasing humidity during system cool-down. When the bottom plate temperature drops below the dew point, water vapor condenses onto the samples. Humidity then briefly rises during the subsequent heating step. Next, the system is heated back to 98 °C. These steps are repeated up to 30 times.

Wet-dry cycling protocol. (a) The 96-well plate inside the chamber during dry (left), condensing (middle), and wet (right) phases. (b) Flowchart of the experimental recipe. (c) Temperature profiles for the chamber (orange) and sample platen (green) over 20 cycles. (d) Relative humidity (RH) profile over 20 cycles.

The resulting temperature profile is shown in Fig. 2c. While the chamber temperature oscillates between 10 and 95 °C, the platen reaches temperatures of near 100 °C and near 0 °C, respectively. The humidity profile (Fig. 2d) begins at approximately 15% RH, rises to around 60%, and ultimately reaches 100% RH. Sample hydration is achieved by saturating the simulator’s nitrogen atmosphere and then cooling the platen and samples below the dew point. Water is evaporated during the subsequent heating step. This procedure enables rapid, reproducible cycling and complex simulation profiles. Samples were exposed to 30 W/m2 of white light during simulations. The simulator can also incorporate different light spectra including bright/dark cycles and UV radiation although these capabilities were not used in our experiments. In this study, we ran wet-dry simulations that involved between 20 and 30 cycles, taking between 4 and 6 days. To ensure reproducibility, all wet-dry cycling simulations and subsequent gel electrophoresis analyses were performed in three independent chamber runs. The gel images presented throughout this manuscript are representative of these independent replicates.

Samples exposed to a single dry-down on a hot plate at 95 °C were used as a control to validate the significance of wet-dry cycling. Dry films were dissolved in 50 µL of ultrapure water for further analysis. Contamination is prevented by using RNA-free reagents and supplies, and autoclaving equipment before usage.

Reagents and materials

Solutions of adenosine-5′-monophosphate (AMP) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and uridine-5′-monophosphate (UMP) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were prepared at room temperature in endotoxin-free ultrapure water (EMD Millipore, USA). Due to poor solubility, AMP was dissolved using ultrasound in a water bath. After filtration through an Acrodisc MS syringe filter with 0.2 µm, 25 mm polytetrafluoroethylene membrane (Cytiva, USA), the concentration of mononucleotides was evaluated by measuring absorption with Nanophotometer NP80 (IMPLEN, Germany) and applying respective extinction coefficients. The final concentrations of nucleotides were adjusted to 10 mM. An Orion Star A221 benchtop pH meter with Orion 9810BM micro pH electrode (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to measure the pH of all solutions used in this study: AMP (pH 3), UMP (pH 2). Templates were HPLC-purified DNA 20-mers with free 5’ ends containing thymine (dT20) or adenine (dA20) bases in nuclease-free water (Metabion, Germany). Before the wet-dry cycling experiment, 50 µL of solutions of the different mixtures were loaded into a ClearLine sterile tissue culture 96-well plate (Scientific Laboratory Supplies, United Kingdom). The microRNA Marker (S1328S, 21-mer, New England Biolabs, USA), 20 ng/µL was used as reference in the gels. The 21-mer is a commercial, proprietary size marker and its specific sequence is not disclosed by the supplier; it serves only as a size and migration reference.

Solutions of RNA from baker’s yeast (Sigma-Aldrich, R6750) in endotoxin-free ultrapure water were prepared as control at a concentration of 0.2 mM. The RNA used is a heterogeneous mixture of naturally occurring yeast RNA molecules and does not possess a single, defined sequence. Solutions of D-ribose 5’-phosphate disodium salt dihydrate (Sigma-Aldrich) and ribose (Supelco) at concentrations of 10 mM and 20 mM were prepared in endotoxin free ultrapure water. The exact composition of all samples used for wet-dry cycling experiments is listed in Supplementary Table 1. pH values have been measured before and after 30 cycles and are listed in Supplementary Table 2. We note that while the pH of RNA, ribose phosphate and ribose solutions are close to a neutral pH of 7, the pH of the AMP and UMP nucleotide solutions is significantly lower, between 2.3 and 3.1. The solutions were not buffered, and simulations were run at these low pH values.

Ethanol precipitation

20 µL of 3 M NaOAc (Sigma-Aldrich, anhydrous) was added to 200 µL of sample and mixed. 550 µL of ice-cold 100% ethanol (Commercial Alcohols, Brampton, ON, Canada) was added, mixed well and incubated overnight at −20 °C. The tube was then centrifuged at 19,000 × g for 60 minutes at 4 °C and the supernatant was decanted. 1 mL of ice-cold 70% ethanol was added and centrifuged again at 19,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. Supernatant was decanted and pellet was dried under a stream of dry nitrogen for 5 minutes before resuspending in 50 µL of ultrapure water.

Alkaline and acid hydrolysis protocols

Three solutions were prepared: A: 1 M NaOH, 4 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), B: 1 M HCl, C: 1 M NaCl, 4 mM EDTA. The primary role of EDTA in the assays was to bind to and sequester divalent metal cations, such as magnesium () and calcium (). 50 µL of sample was mixed with 10 µL of Solution A and incubated at 60 °C for 4 hours. Then, 10 µL of Solution B and 30 µL of water were added and 20 µL were transferred into the gel. For the control samples: 10 µL of control sample were mixed with 2 µL of solution C and 8 µL of ultrapure water, resulting in a similar ion concentration (~100 mM) between samples and controls. This protocol resulted in a final concentration of 0.2 M NaOH. A 1 M NaOH assay was also prepared using a starting concentration of 6 M NaOH, neutralizing with 6 M HCl and using 6 M NaCl as control.

For acid hydrolysis, samples were incubated with 1 M and 6 M HCl, respectively, and neutralized with NaOH following the above protocol.

Desalting columns

Cycled solutions were filtered using Glen Gel-Pak 1.0 mL Desalting Columns, (Glen Research, USA) prior to the spectroscopic measurements to remove unreacted mononucleotides and orthophosphate. To prepare the columns for use, the liquid storage content was allowed to drain completely by gravity and was disposed of as waste. Next, 1.0 mL of the appropriate solvent (e.g. H2O, D2O) was added to the column and allowed to drain completely. This rinsing step is repeated a total of three times to ensure the apparatus has been properly conditioned, with all effluent being discarded as waste. Next, 1.0 mL of our sample solution (or a 1.0 mL mixture of sample solution and solvent if it is required to meet volumetric needs) was added and allowed to fully saturate the column, discarding the effluent as waste. Lastly, 500 μL of our desired solvent was added to the column and collected as the desalted product. This step is repeated two additional times, with each 500 μL portion being collected into the final 1.5 mL sample (which was concentrated if needed).

RNase 1, DNase 1 and DBR1 assays

We used RNase 1 (Ribonuclease I from E. coli, Thermo Fisher, Vilnius, Lithuania), DNase 1 (Thermo Fisher, Vilnius, Lithuania) and RNA debranching enzyme DBR1 (human, Prosepc, Ness Ziona, Israel). We note that unlike the pyrimidine-specific RNase A family, RNase 1 is a non-specific endonuclease that cleaves the phosphodiester bonds of all four canonical ribonucleotides (A, C, G, and U). We further used HEPES (Bioshop, Burlington ON, Canada), Trizma hydrochloride solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (anhydrous, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for some of the assays.

RNase 1: A 0.4 M HEPES solution with an initial pH of 5.2 was brought to pH 8.2 through the addition of NaOH. 5 µL of this solution was added to 10 µL of the target solution. 5 µL of RNase 1 solution was added, vortexed and incubated for 2 hours at 37 °C. Control samples were mixed with 10 µL of the HEPES buffer.

DNase 1: 18 µL of target solution was mixed with 2 µL of the 10× DNase buffer solution included by the supplier, and 2 µL of DNase. Control samples contained 18 µL target solution and 2 µL DNase buffer.

DBR1: 18 µL of target solution was mixed with 2 µL of 10× 10 mM TrisHCl + 0.15 M NaCl buffer, as recommended by the supplier, and 2 µL of DBR1 solution. Control samples contained 18 µL target solution and 2 µL DBR1 buffer.

Qubit analysis

The concentration of short RNA products was determined by employing a Qubit microRNA assay kit (Thermofisher Scientific, USA) with a Qubit 4 Fluorimeter (Thermofisher Scientific, USA). 20 µL of sample volume was mixed with 180 µL of prepared RNA quantification assay in Qubit Assay Tubes (Thermofisher Scientific, USA). The mixture was kept in the dark for around 2 minutes before measuring the fluorescence.

Gel electrophoresis and imaging

Electrophoresis was performed using the E-Gel™ Power Snap Plus Electrophoresis Device (Thermo Fisher Scientific). While denaturing polyacrylamide gels were tested during initial optimizations, they yielded substantially poorer resolution for the cycled samples. The complex, aggregated, and highly heterogeneous nature of the abiotic mixtures likely prevented efficient migration into the polyacrylamide matrix. Consequently, the specialized pre-cast agarose E-gel system was selected as the optimal matrix for visualizing these specific products. Samples (20 µL, equal to 200 nmol for AMP/UMP and ribose phosphate) were injected into each well of a pre-cast E-Gel™ EX 4% agarose gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The gels were run for 15 minutes using the E-Gel™ Power Snap Plus Electrophoresis Systems for E-Gel™ EX gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gels were pre-stained with SYBR gold, and no further staining was performed. SYBR Gold is a fluorescent dye that binds to nucleic acids, especially double-stranded RNA or nucleic acids showing defined structures. It has a fluorescence maximum at 495 nm and a secondary excitation peak in the UV range from 300–365 nm54. This bufferless system provides a self-contained and reproducible environment for separation. Immediately after electrophoresis, the gels were removed from the device and placed into the Azure c400 gel imaging system (Azure Biosystems, United States) for analysis. Fluorescence measurements were performed using excitation wavelengths of 302 nm, 365 nm, 460 nm (Cy2), 472 nm (epifluorescence), 526 nm (Cy3), and 628 nm (Cy5). The resulting gel images were loaded into imageJ/Fiji and Minimum/Maximum displayed values of the Tiff files were set to 55,000/65,535 for the images shown in this manuscript such that the displayed gel intensities can be compared between gels, no other manipulations were applied. Densitometric lane profiles were created by integrating the different lanes in imageJ/Fiji and fitting and subtracting a suitable background using a custom Python script. The densitometric lane profiles are shown next to the gels with the signals associated with the AMP/UMP and ribose phosphate polymers shaded.

Small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)

Small-Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS) was employed to characterize the size and shape of the resulting aggregates. X-ray diffraction measurements were performed using CuK X-rays (λ=1.5418 Å) generated by a RIGAKU SmartLab rotating anode instrument operated at 9 kW. The focusing multi-layer optics provided a high intensity circular beam with a diameter of 200 μm and an angular divergence of 0.008 rad with monochromatic X-ray intensities of 108 counts/mm2/s. The instrument is equipped with a Rigaku HyPix-3000 2-dimensional single-photon semiconductor detector with pixels of size 100 μm2. 20 μl of sample solution was filled into a 1 mm X-ray glass capillary with a wall thickness of 0.01 mm (Charlessupper, USA).

UV-Vis spectroscopy

Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy was used to monitor changes in molar absorptivity, such as the hypochromic effect, which would indicate base-stacking interactions and the formation of ordered secondary structures within the oligomers. An Implen NanoPhotometer NP80 UV/VIS spectrophotometer was used. Droplet measurements were done using the nanodrop port, typically using 1 μL of sample. Spectra were measured over a range of 200 to 900 nm, with a wavelength accuracy of ±0.75 nm. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

Infrared (IR) spectroscopy was used to detect the formation of new covalent bonds, such as C-O-C or P-O-C linkages, which would indicate polymerization after wet-dry cycling. Infrared spectra were recorded using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5 FT-IR spectrometer. For each measurement, 2 µL of the sample was deposited directly onto the instrument’s diamond crystal stage. The spectra were acquired over a range of 4000-400 cm−1 with a resolution of 1 cm−1. To ensure reproducibility, all measurements were performed in triplicate.

31P NMR spectroscopy

31P NMR spectroscopy was used to identify the chemical nature of the polymer backbones. Spectra were acquired on a Bruker AV 600 spectrometer located at the McMaster University NMR facility, operating at a 31P frequency of 242.9 MHz. Samples were prepared by dissolving the final, concentrated material in deuterium oxide (D₂O) and were analyzed in 5 mm NMR tubes. For each sample, a total of 128 scans were accumulated to achieve a sufficient signal-to-noise ratio. 1,000 scans were collected for the cycled AMP/UMP and ribose phosphate solutions to increase the signal-to-noise ratio.

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry

Mass spectra were acquired using a Bruker neofleX™ MALDI-TOF/TOF mass spectrometer, located at the BioInterfaces Institute at McMaster University. Samples (uncycled and cycled D-ribose-5’-phosphate at 20 mM) were prepared using the dried-droplet method. A 0.5 M solution of 2’,6’-dihydroxyacetophenone (DHAP) in methanol was supplemented with 25% saturated ammonium citrate and used as the matrix. 1 µL of the sample was mixed with 1 µL of the matrix solution directly on the MALDI target plate and allowed to air-dry at room temperature. The instrument was operated in reflectron positive ion mode, and spectra were acquired over a mass-to-charge (m/z) range of 100-4000. Each spectrum was an average of 2,000 laser shots from random positions within the sample spot to ensure representative data.

Results

Synthesis of prebiotic polymers

To evaluate the formation of abiotic polymers, mixtures of non-activated mononucleotides (AMP and UMP) and D-ribose-5’-phosphate were subjected to thermodynamically controlled wet-dry cycling within the environmental simulation chamber. Samples underwent 20 to 30 repeated cycles of hydration and dehydration under acidic, unbuffered conditions (pH ~2 to 3) at temperatures fluctuating between 10 and 98 °C. The resulting reaction products were subsequently characterized using multi-wavelength fluorescence, enzymatic assays, and spectroscopic techniques to determine their structural and chemical properties.

Multi-wavelength electrophoresis analyses

Imaging the gel with samples that underwent 20 wet-dry cycles revealed that the fluorescence patterns are highly dependent on the excitation wavelength (Fig. 3). Shown are wavelength of 365 nm, 472 nm, and 628 nm, all available wavelengths are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. A comparison of all starting materials and controls after 20 wet-dry cycles is shown in Supplementary Figure S2. Unlike the commercial 21-mer RNA reference standard, which consistently migrated as a sharp, well-developed band, the abiotically synthesized products appeared as broad, somewhat diffuse bands or smears. This electrophoretic profile is an expected physical reflection of the high structural heterogeneity and polydispersity of the uncontrolled thermodynamic synthesis (as later confirmed by SAXS), rather than a technical limitation of the gel. Furthermore, high residual salt concentrations from the unpurified cycled mixtures occasionally caused minor migration artifacts compared to desalted controls.

Multi-wavelength fluorescence imaging of products after 20 wet-dry cycles. The same gel, pre-stained with SYBR Gold, was imaged at excitation wavelengths of (a) 365 nm, (b) 472 nm, (c) 628 nm.

Excitation at 472 nm, near one of the dye’s known excitation peaks, resulted in fluorescence from all markers, including ssRNA (lanes M, 1), ssDNA (lanes 8, 9), and dsDNA (lane 10), and was most selective for these intact nucleic acid markers, leading to high intensity signals. In contrast, excitation at 365 nm was found to be highly selective for the wet-dry cycling products (e.g., lane 4), which fluoresced brightly at this wavelength while the standard markers remained faint. This wavelength selectivity suggests a non-canonical nature of the polymers. The cycled mixtures also yielded specific bands that were visible under 628 nm excitation. The exact chemical nature and structural state of the species responsible for this 628 nm signal is likely the result of broad-spectrum autofluorescence coming from the complex, brown prebiotic polymers formed during the intense thermal cycling. This multi-wavelength approach therefore allows for the differentiation of standard nucleic acids (strongest at 472 nm), abiotic polymers (strongest at 365 nm), and non-enzymatic browning (visible at 628 nm). To validate that repeated thermodynamic cycling is the essential driver of this polymerization, we analyzed control samples subjected to only a single dry-down event. No polymerization was observed in gels of AMP/UMP mixtures after a single dry-down cycle (SupplementaryFigure S3), confirming that simple dehydration is insufficient to drive polymer synthesis.

The fluorescence at an excitation wavelength of 365 nm that we observe is likely related to a structurally modified nature of the abiotically synthesized oligomers. SYBR Gold is a fluorescent dye that binds to nucleic acids. While it has a secondary excitation peak in the UV range (~300-365 nm), its fluorescence is most intense when it intercalates in well-structured nucleic acids (like double-stranded RNA) and is excited near its primary maximum of ~495 nm54. The gels show that standard RNA and DNA markers fluoresce brightly when excited at 472 nm (close to the 495 nm peak). Our data provides evidence that the synthesized AMP/UMP polymers do not behave like canonical RNA. Potential defects in the polymer structure can directly affect how the SYBR Gold dye can bind to it. The primary, high-efficiency binding mode for SYBR Gold is intercalation, where the dye slips between the stacked base pairs of a stable double-helix55. Because the abiotically formed polymers lack a uniform canonical structure, potentially due to missing bases, alternative branching linkages, or highly irregular conformations, they likely cannot form the regular, stable helical structures required for efficient standard intercalation6. This forces the dye into an alternative, less common binding mode, such as electrostatic interaction with the exposed phosphate backbone56. We speculate that the non-intercalative binding to the damaged polymer backbone has different electronic properties, and this alternative binding mode is more efficiently excited by the secondary UV wavelength (365 nm) than by the primary visible light wavelength (~495 nm).

Polymerization yield is highest for pure mononucleotides and ribose phosphate

We then tested the efficiency of various starting materials in producing oligonucleotides after 30 wet-dry cycles (Fig. 4). Polymerization occurred in the pure AMP/UMP mixture (lane 4) and the D-ribose-5’-phosphate solutions (lanes 5 and 6). The highest yield was found in the pure AMP/UMP mixture. We analyzed this mixture using a Qubit microRNA assay, confirming a concentration of ~1.5 µg/mL. Yields for ribose phosphate at 10 mM and 20 mM were determined to be ~0.3 µg/mL and ~1.25 µg/mL, respectively. To determine if pre-existing polymers enhance this process, we included control mixtures containing standard RNA (lanes 2 and 3); comparing the corresponding band intensities revealed that the presence of RNA does not exert a templating effect or increase the overall yield. Furthermore, to validate the necessity of the phosphate group in the starting material, we cycled pure ribose and ribose mixed with phosphoric acid as negative controls (lanes 7-10). No polymerization was observed in these unphosphorylated controls, confirming that covalent attachment of the phosphate group to the monomer prior to cycling is strictly required for polymer formation.

AMP/UMP polymers are degraded by alkaline hydrolysis

The stability of AMP/UMP polymers, control RNA, and ribose phosphate polymers against alkaline hydrolysis was tested using NaOH at concentrations of 0.2 M in the gels in Fig. 5. Samples and control samples were prepared as described in the Materials and Methods Section. Using this protocol, the salt concentration in control and hydrolyzed samples were comparable, though the samples are diluted to about 50% as compared to the controls. The gel in Fig. 5 shows that while AMP/UMP and ribose phosphate bands slightly decrease in intensity (Fig. 5a), RNA completely degraded 0.2 M NaOH (Fig. 5b). At 1 M NaOH (included as Supplementary Figure S4), the RNA band disappeared while the bands for AMP/UMP and ribose phosphate were still visible, though significantly decreased in intensity.

AMP/UMP and ribose phosphate polymers are not degraded by RNase 1, DNase 1, and DBR1

To test the integrity and nature of the synthesized polymer backbones, we used a suite of highly specific enzymes to distinguish canonical structures from noncanonical or alternative polymers that may have formed through this prebiotic synthesis.

Ribonuclease 1 (RNase 1), a member of the RNase A superfamily, is an endonuclease that is fundamental to RNA degradation and turnover in many biological systems. Its catalytic mechanism is highly specific, relying on the presence of a 2’-hydroxyl group on the ribose sugar, which acts as an internal nucleophile to attack the adjacent phosphorus atom. This reaction cleaves the canonical 3’-5’ phosphodiester bond on the 3’ side of pyrimidine residues (cytosine or uracil). Therefore, resistance to RNase 1 provides evidence that the polymers lack a fully accessible, canonical single-stranded RNA backbone. We note that this resistance alone does not establish a specific alternative linkage pattern or a uniquely stable RNA analogue; it indicates that the synthesized products, whether due to missing bases, a mixture of alternative linkages, or highly compacted, heterogeneous conformations, are sufficiently non-canonical or sterically hindered to evade specific enzymatic cleavage.

We also employed Deoxyribonuclease I (DNase 1), an endonuclease that catalyzes the cleavage of phosphodiester bonds in the backbone of DNA. Unlike RNase 1, its mechanism does not depend on a 2’-hydroxyl group, and it acts on both single- and double-stranded DNA. In this study, DNase 1 serves as a negative control. It cannot degrade the polymer, due to its ribose backbone (as opposed to deoxyribose in DNA), thereby distinguishing the synthesized products from DNA and validating the specificity of any observed degradation by other enzymes.

To probe specific non-canonical linkages such as phosphate linkages between positions other than 3’ and 5’, we used the RNA Lariat Debranching Enzyme 1 (DBR1). In eukaryotes, DBR1’s primary function is to resolve the lariat structures that are intermediates of pre-mRNA splicing. These lariats are defined by a unique 2’-5’ phosphodiester bond that forms a three-way branch point (linking the 5’ and 3’ ends of the intron to a 2’-5’ bond on an adenosine) at the adenosine branch point. DBR1 is a highly specific metallophosphoesterase that catalyzes the hydrolysis of this 2’-5’-bond, linearizing the intron for subsequent degradation. Its specificity makes it a probe for testing the presence of specific 2’-5’ linkages in branched structures. However, because DBR1 is a highly specialized debranching enzyme, resistance to DBR1 does not definitively exclude the presence of 2’-5’ phosphodiester bonds; rather, it indicates that the specific lariat-like branched architectures recognized by the enzyme are either absent or sterically inaccessible within the heterogeneous polymer mixture. Fig. 6 provides a schematic of the specific modes of action for these enzymes.

Schematic of enzymatic and chemical cleavage sites in RNA. Alkaline hydrolysis and RNase 1 target the phosphodiester linkage via the 2’ hydroxyl group. This mechanism is effective on both the canonical 3’-5’ and the non-canonical 2’-5’ linkages, as long as a free 2’-OH group is available to initiate the cleavage. DNase 1 also targets the 3’-5’phosphodiester linkage. DBR1 specifically cleaves 2’-5’ phosphodiester bonds found in RNA lariats.

31P NMR shows formation of phosphodiester bonds in cycled AMP/UMP and formation of pyrophosphate in cycled ribose phosphate

To gain more definitive insight into the chemical environments around the phosphorus atoms incorporated in the cycled polymers, 31P NMR was conducted. Based on the chemical shifts observed, the identity of the functional groups in these polymers can be determined. This technique is particularly useful as it directly probes the phosphorus atoms, allowing for unambiguous identification of the backbone linkages, a level of detail not achievable with the other methods employed57. More specifically, this technique can be used to distinguish phosphate esters from pyrophosphate and polyphosphates, providing insight as to the types of linkages present in the cycled polymers. AMP/UMP and ribose phosphate 10 mM solutions underwent cycling. The dried films were rehydrated with 3.3 mL D2O , respectively, and desalted to remove unreacted AMP/UMP and ribose phosphate. The samples were then concentrated to ~0.6 mL and the final concentration of the nucleic acid material for AMP/UMP was determined using a UV-vis nanodrop to be 0.9 mg/mL. This modified sample was submitted for 31P NMR analysis using a Bruker AV 600 (1,000 scans).

The spectra of various molecules and mixtures, as well as their cycled products are shown in Fig. 9. In Fig. 9a, the control RNA sample shows a distinct peak at approximately −0.85 ppm, which falls in the expected chemical shift range for phosphodiester bonds (−5 to 0 ppm). The uncycled AMP/UMP mixture (blue traces) and the uncycled ribose phosphate solution (red traces) both show sharp, intense peaks between 1 and 4 ppm (specifically at 1.25 ppm, 1.96 ppm, 3.60 ppm, and 4.10 ppm), characteristic of phosphate monoesters (0 to 10 ppm) and orthophosphates. 40 mM phosphoric acid in D2O was included in the scans as reference and the corresponding peak was detected at -0.0217 ppm.

31P NMR highlights the formation of phosphate and pyrophosphate-linked polymers. (a) Overlay of all samples, showing chemical shift regions for phosphate monoesters, phosphodiesters, and pyrophosphates. (b) Spectra of RNA, uncycled AMP/UMP, and cycled AMP/UMP, highlighting the new phosphodiester peaks between −0.21 and 0.73 ppm. (c) Spectra of RNA, uncycled ribose phosphate, and cycled ribose phosphate, highlighting the new pyrophosphate peak at -6.31 ppm.

After wet-dry cycling, the spectrum for the AMP/UMP sample in Fig. 9b shows a significant change (blue traces). The 31P NMR data for the cycled AMP/UMP sample provides evidence for the formation of a heterogeneous mixture of phosphodiester-linked oligonucleotides. More specifically, the new signals, appearing in a broad range from −0.21 ppm to +0.73 ppm, fall within the established chemical shift window for phosphodiester bonds, which is typically centered around 0 ppm. In a uniform polymer, a single, sharp peak would be expected, whereas the observed broadness indicates a population of polymers with varying lengths, conformations, and linkage isomers.

This observation is consistent with the established understanding that the chemical environment of the phosphorus atom significantly influences its NMR signal57. The canonical 3’-5’ phosphodiester linkage found in natural RNA typically resonates slightly upfield (more negative ppm values), as exemplified by the control RNA peak at -0.85 ppm. In contrast, non-canonical 2’-5’ phosphodiester linkages are consistently reported to produce signals that are shifted downfield to more positive ppm values. For instance, studies on dinucleoside monophosphates have shown that the signal for a 2’-5’ linkage in ApA (adenylyl-(2’→5’)-adenosine) appears approximately 0.5 ppm downfield from its 3’-5’ counterpart58. This downfield shift of 2’-5’ linkages has been a critical diagnostic tool in studying everything from prebiotic polymerization to the biological role of 2’-5’-oligoadenylate synthetase (OAS) enzymes59. Therefore, the presence of multiple peaks in the positive region of the spectrum (+0.31 ppm, +0.44 ppm) supports the conclusion that the abiotic synthesis produced a mixed population of oligomers containing both canonical 3’-5’ and non-canonical 2’-5’ phosphodiester bonds.

A new, prominent peak appears in the cycled ribose phosphate sample in Fig. 9c (green traces) at −6.31 ppm60. This chemical shift falls directly within the characteristic range for pyrophosphate bonds (−10 to −5 ppm). At the same time, there is a complete absence of signals in the phosphodiester region. This result indicates that the ribose phosphate monomers did not form phosphodiester bonds but instead polymerized to create a backbone linked by pyrophosphate linkages. This polymer may share structural analogy to poly(ADP-ribose), a ribose-based polymer involved in post-translational modifications of proteins.

In summary, 31P NMR analysis provides evidence for two distinct polymerization pathways: the formation of phosphodiester bonds when nucleobases are present (AMP/UMP) and the formation of a pyrophosphate backbone when only the sugar-phosphate unit is used (ribose phosphate).

Furthermore, UV-vis spectroscopy of the desalted reaction products confirmed the retention and characteristic absorbance profiles of the nucleobases in the case of AMP/UMP and evidence for stacking in the case of ribose phosphate within these synthesized polymers (see Supplementary Information and Supplementary Figure S7).

Infrared spectra of cycled AMP/UMP do not show evidence bond formation while cycled ribose phosphate spectra show evidence for bond formation

IR spectra are shown in Fig. 10a. The analysis focused on comparing the spectra of the starting materials (AMP/UMP and ribose phosphate) with the spectra of the products after cycling.

Fourier-Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectra. (a) Raw spectra of starting materials (AMP/UMP, ribose phosphate) and their cycled products, with an RNA control. (b) Difference spectra (cycled - uncycled) highlighting the formation of new bonds in the ribose phosphate sample at 977 cm−1 and 1107 cm−1.

The most significant result was observed in the ribose phosphate sample. The difference spectra in Fig. 10b, calculated by subtracting the spectrum of the uncycled material from the cycled material, reveals two distinct new bands at approximately 977 cm−1 and 1107 cm−1. The frequencies of these new bands are consistent with the formation of new P-O-P (pyrophosphate) bonds, providing evidence for the formation of pyrophosphate during the cycling of ribose phosphate61,62.

To provide supportive evidence for the assignment of P-O-P bond formation in the ribose phosphate polymer, semi-empirical quantum chemistry calculations were performed using the IRCalc tool (Swiss Federal Institute of Technology Lausanne (EPFL)). We simulated the structure of a model pyrophosphate-linked ribose dimer to predict the characteristic vibrational frequencies corresponding to the P-O-P backbone. The calculated characteristic frequencies compatible with the pyrophosphate backbone structure include: 1158.7 cm−1, 1121.3 cm−1, 971.5 cm−1, 937.3 cm−1, 885.6 cm−1, and 868.1 cm−1. The computed frequencies are compatible with the features observed in the FT-IR spectrum in Fig. 10b, in particular the strong features at approximately 977 cm-1 and 1107 cm-1. We note that the FT-IR signals in the cycled samples are likely broadened because of the expected heterogeneity and physical state of the abiotically synthesized materials. For the ribose phosphate products, different pyrophosphate linkage sites (e.g., 5’-5’, 5’-1’) are possible. Each unique linkage or structural conformation has a slightly different vibrational energy, resulting in a superposition of closely spaced vibrational peaks, which the instrument records as a single, broadened band.

In contrast, the difference spectrum for the AMP/UMP sample was largely flat, with no significant new bands appearing in the 1000–1500 cm−1 range. While the formation of pyrophosphate linkages in the ribose phosphate sample produced a clear signal, the phosphodiester bonds formed in the AMP/UMP sample were likely too low in yield to be distinguished from the background signal of the abundant precursor molecules.

SAXS shows the presence of aggregates in AMP/UMP and ribose phosphate solutions

Fig. 11 shows SAXS curves for cycled and non-cycled AMP/UMP and cycled and non-cycled ribose phosphate. The characteristic feature in these curves is a signal at 0.015 Å−1 for AMP/UMP in parts (a) and (b), and at 0.01 Å−1 for ribose phosphate in parts (c) and (d). To determine the characteristic size (d-spacing) the relationship derived from Bragg’s Law is used: d = 2π/q. Given a peak position (q) of 0.015 Å−1, the calculation yields a characteristic distance of approximately 420 Å for AMP/UMP, and about 630 Å for ribose phosphate. This distance represents a preferred correlation length or repeating structure within the sample, related to self-assembly or aggregation in the solution. This peak position was determined by fits of a Lorentzian peak profile to the data and are plotted as solid lines in Figs. 11b and d. The width of the correlation peaks was fitted to 0.015 Å−1 for AMP/UMP and 0.013 Å-1 for ribose phosphate.

Small-Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS) curves. (a) Cycled vs. uncycled AMP/UMP. (b) Difference curve for AMP/UMP showing increased scattering from aggregates after cycling. (c) Cycled vs. uncycled ribose phosphate. (d) Difference curve for ribose phosphate showing decreased scattering after cycling. Solid lines in b) and d) are fits to a Lorentzian peak profile.

As shown in Fig. 11, an additional signal in the scattering data appears for AMP/UMP after cycling, while for ribose phosphate, there is a significant decrease in this signal when cycled. The contrasting SAXS results likely arise from the different chemical natures of the polymers: the nucleobase-containing AMP/UMP polymers aggregate after formation, while the cycling process breaks down pre-existing aggregates of ribose phosphate as it forms new, less-aggregated polymers.

To estimate the number of AMP and UMP molecules in an aggregate, it can be assumed that the effective linear length contributed by each AMP or UMP molecule due to stacking or a similar regular linear arrangement is approximately 3.4 Å. Given this, the correlation length (420 Å) divided by the assumed distance per stacked monomer (3.4 Å) yields a characteristic scale corresponding to approximately 120 molecules. We emphasize that this metric reflects an aggregate or correlation scale rather than a direct measurement of a single, continuous covalent chain length. The scattering signal likely arises from non-covalent linear aggregates or complex assemblies held together by a combination of base stacking, hydrogen bonding, and covalent linkages. Based on the proposed structure below, one can assume that the stacking distance of the ribose phosphate polymers is in the order of 5 Å, increased by the distance from the central phosphorus (P) atom to each of the surrounding oxygen (O) atoms, which results in polymer clusters of 126 units (630 Å/5 Å), comparable to the size of the AMP/UMP chains. Both structures show a relatively broad distribution of sizes, which is consistent with the heterogeneous nature of an abiotic polymerization process based on their polydispersity based on the width of the scattering peak of about 0.87 for AMP/UMP (0.013 Å /0.015 Å) and about 0.67 for ribose phosphate (0.01 Å/0.015 Å).

The calculated polydispersity values indicate that both samples are highly polydisperse, with the AMP/UMP polymers showing a PDI of approximately 0.87 and the ribose phosphate polymers showing a PDI of approximately 0.67. This high degree of non-uniformity is expected from an uncontrolled, abiotic synthesis process, which would naturally produce a very broad and heterogeneous distribution of different-sized aggregates rather than particles of a single size. Furthermore, the results show that the AMP/UMP polymer aggregates are significantly more polydisperse than the ribose phosphate aggregates. This suggests that the aggregation mechanism for the AMP/UMP polymers, which is likely driven by a combination of base stacking and hydrogen bonding, results in a more varied and complex range of aggregate sizes compared to the simpler self-assembly of the ribose phosphate polymers.

Discussion

The formation of biopolymers like RNA and peptides from their constituent monomers in a dilute aqueous environment is fundamentally hindered by a significant thermodynamic barrier. This condensation reaction, which releases a water molecule for each bond formed, is endergonic () and antientropic (), as it combines multiple disordered molecules into a single, more ordered polymer. Consequently, the equilibrium in bulk water heavily favors the reverse reaction, hydrolysis, leading to a prevalence of short, non-functional oligomers, often termed the Flory Length Problem. However, specific prebiotic environments, such as evaporating droplets or surfaces undergoing wet-dry cycles, can invert this unfavorable thermodynamic landscape. This process transcends simple concentration effects described by Le Châtelier’s principle; instead, it constitutes a form of physical activation. As water evaporates, the increase in the surface-area-to-volume ratio forces monomers into a two-dimensional plane, pre-organizing them and mitigating the primary entropic penalty of the reaction. This molecular crowding, coupled with the sharply reduced chemical activity of water in the nearly-dry state, collectively shifts the reaction’s Gibbs free energy to become exergonic (). This mechanism stands in contrast to chemical activation, which relies on high-energy activating agents (e.g., phosphorimidazolides) to provide the chemical impetus for bond formation.

Each component of the simulation cycle (e.g., the number of repeats, the temperatures, and the humidity levels) thus serves a chemical purpose. As water evaporates, the dissolved precursor molecules (AMP, UMP, or ribose phosphate) are forced into close proximity, significantly increasing their concentration. Heat is essential to overcome kinetic barriers towards product formation by providing the necessary energy to drive these condensation reactions forward, allowing the monomers to link together and form polymers. The wet phase is essential for allowing the newly formed polymers and any unreacted monomers to dissolve and diffuse again. This allows them to mix and randomize for the next round of polymerization in the subsequent dry phase. Without this step, the molecules would be locked in place, and further chain elongation would be impossible. While some polymers might break down (hydrolyze) during the wet phase, the repeated dehydration steps ensure that the net effect over many cycles is the gradual accumulation and elongation of stable polymer chains. The choice of 20–30 cycles is a practical duration for a laboratory experiment (4–6 days) that is sufficient to demonstrate significant polymer formation.

The products resulting from the wet-dry cycling of adenosine monophosphate (AMP) and uridine monophosphate (UMP) can in general be characterized as heterogeneous, short-chain oligonucleotides with significant structural modifications. 31P NMR analysis confirms that these polymers are linked by phosphodiester bonds, and SAXS data indicates the presence of assemblies with a correlation scale corresponding to aggregates of approximately 120 nucleotide units. Furthermore, these polymers were readily precipitated from solution using standard ethanol precipitation protocols, concentrating the product (Supplementary Figure S7). However, they differ from canonical RNA as they are resistant to degradation by RNase 1, stable against acid hydrolysis (Supplementary Figure S9). While RNase 1 and alkaline hydrolysis readily cleave the canonical 3’-5’ phosphodiester backbone of RNA, the resistance of these polymers suggests a modified or inaccessible structure. Furthermore, while the precise microstructures remain unresolved, their resistance to both the 3’-5’ targeting RNase 1 and the specialized 2’-5’ debranching enzyme DBR1 suggests that the overall heterogeneity of the products protects them from specific enzymatic degradation. The complex, non-canonical architectures, potentially involving unusual conformations, missing bases, or significant steric hindrance, appear to render the backbone linkages inaccessible to these highly specialized biological probes. This combined resistance highlights that the polymers obtained from wet-dry cycling are stable. This stability persists across a wide pH range (pH 3–11).

The products from wet-dry cycling D-ribose-5’-phosphate are likely to be polymers of ribose linked by a pyrophosphate backbone. This structure is proposed from the convergence of data: the gel electrophoresis confirms the formation of a large, anionic material (the smear), the SAXS data indicates aggregates of approximately 126 units, and the 31P NMR data shows pyrophosphate signals (−6.31 ppm) and a lack of phosphodiester signals. Combined, this is evidence for polymerization of ribose units linked by pyrophosphate bonds. These polymers do not form directly from D-ribose-5’-phosphate; a disproportionation reaction is proposed where two D-ribose-5’-phosphate molecules form D-ribose-5’-diphosphate and free D-ribose, with the polymer then forming from these new species. These ribose phosphate polymers, like AMP/UMP polymers, are resistant to DNase and the debranching enzyme DBR1.

The data, therefore, point toward two distinct and parallel polymerization pathways, as summarized schematically in Fig. 12. The first pathway involves the direct polymerization of mononucleotides. When AMP and UMP are cycled, they form oligomers with phosphodiester bonds that are the hallmark of canonical RNA (Fig. 12a). In contrast, the second pathway, which begins with only D-ribose-5’-phosphate, proceeds through a different mechanism. Our results support a disproportionation reaction where two D-ribose-5’-phosphate molecules are converted into D-ribose-5’-diphosphate and free D-ribose. These products then polymerize to create a polymer linked by a pyrophosphate backbone (Fig. 12b). This model accounts for the appearance of strong pyrophosphate signals in the 31P NMR spectra. Supplementary Figure S10 shows MALDI-TOF mass spectra that suggest the presence of free ribose in the cycled material.

Proposed pathways for polymerization. (a) AMP and UMP polymerize via 3’-5’ and 2’-5’ phosphodiester bonds. (b) D-Ribose-5’-phosphate undergoes disproportionation to form ribose and ribose diphosphate, which then polymerize, forming pyrophosphate linkages (c).

This proposed structure for the wet-dry cycling of D-ribose-5’-phosphate shows analogy with poly(ADP-ribose) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH/NAD+) both of which consist of ribose nucleosides connected with pyrophosphate linkages, either as a polymer or a dinucleotide. These results suggest that a larger diversity of nucleic acid polymers can have formed and subsisted under prebiotic conditions. The main structural difference between our ribose polymers and RNA are the absence of nucleobases at the anomeric position of ribose (position 1’) and the replacement of the phosphate linkages with pyrophosphate. The absence of the nucleobases in ribose may have influenced how these monomers come together during wet-dry cycling to promote the formation of the pyrophosphate-linked polymer observed for ribose-5’-phosphate, a hypothesis that is supported by SAXS analysis when compared to AMP/UMP mixtures. Ribose and AMP/UMP also differ in their reactivity at the anomeric site. Ribose is present in a more reactive hemiacetal form, while AMP/UMP, are present as more stable N-glycosides, which may influence the final structure of the polymers observed. It will be important to determine how this position influences product formation, whether glycosidic linkages also form and if nucleobases can be incorporated during polymerization.

Overall, the ribose-5’-phosphate experiments achieved the polymerization of two components in nucleic acid polymers, in this case ribose, which is connected with pyrophosphate linkages. The anomeric position in ribose is expected to be susceptible to nucleophiles (e.g., nucleobases), especially if it is activated with a phosphate to act as a leaving group. In future work, we will determine if the anomeric position is accessible for nucleobase installation and the conditions for these reactions. Exploring derivatization with nucleobases, especially adenine, could provide a plausible prebiotic pathway to the synthesis of poly(ADP-ribose).

These findings are potentially relevant for origins of life research because they demonstrate that relatively simple starting materials like AMP, UMP, and ribose-5’-phosphate can form polymers under conditions simulating early Earth (wet-dry cycling). These conditions would have been prevalent on volcanic islands and small land masses as the first continents began to form, creating natural cycles of hydration and dehydration. Specifically, our experimental parameters closely align with freshwater hot-spring settings, where acid-catalyzed polymerization at water-film or droplet surfaces provides a potent mechanism for chain elongation, as recently modeled by Song et al50. Our simulation chamber provides a controlled physical realization of this specific geological niche.

The enzymatic resistance of these products suggests that multiple types of robust, RNA-related or phosphate-linked polymers could have co-existed. The formation of oligomers with phosphodiester backbones provides a direct, abiotic route to structures chemically related to those central to the RNA world hypothesis. Equally important is the discovery that ribose phosphate alone forms polymers with a different pyrophosphate backbone; this suggests that a variety of alternative structural backbones could have formed alongside nucleobase-containing polymers. However, the exact microstructure, replication capacity, and ultimate biological relevance of these heterogeneous materials remain open questions. The phosphodiester-linked polymers were found to be resistant to RNase 1, the enzyme that specifically digests RNA. This contrasts with recent findings by63, who reported ribonuclease sensitivity for certain wet-dry products formed from AMP and UMP. Our observed resistance suggests that the specific acidic, multi-cycle thermal regime utilized in our simulation chamber yields a highly heterogeneous, non-canonical architecture, such as a mixed population of 2’-5’ and 3’-5’ linkages or missing bases, that is distinctly less accessible to standard nucleases than products formed under other cycling conditions. The formation of a ribophosphate backbone with missing nucleobases under wet-dry cycling conditions was recently confirmed by6. They are highly heterogeneous, structurally modified polymers, yet stable enough to persist and form distinct bands on a gel. Ultimately, our acidic, high-temperature cycling represents just one productive window in a broader landscape of plausible prebiotic environments. For instance, efficient nonenzymatic RNA-forming chemistry can also emerge in drastically different regimes, such as the low-salt alkaline conditions utilizing 2’,3’-cyclic phosphates recently demonstrated by64. Together, these varied findings reinforce the view that the early Earth offered a mosaic of distinct local environments, each capable of driving unique chemical evolution and biopolymer synthesis.

While the primary focus of this work remains the prebiotic emergence of biopolymers, the unique chemical properties of these abiotically synthesized materials open intriguing, albeit highly speculative, avenues for future research. For instance, the inherent resistance of these proto-RNA backbones to rapid degradation by specific nucleases (RNase 1, DNase 1, and DBR1) raises the possibility that such structural heterogeneity might eventually be explored for translational applications. If these abiotic synthesis pathways can be further understood and controlled, they could speculatively offer a framework for generating stable scaffolding molecules or aptamer-like structures, potentially reducing the reliance on the organic solvents and protecting groups required in standard phosphoramidite chemistry. A critical next step in this progression will be the incorporation of cytidine and guanosine mononucleotides into the cycling regime. Expanding this abiotic synthesis to include all four canonical bases will provide insights into how complex base compositions influence the efficiency of nonenzymatic polymerization and the ultimate structural stability of the resulting diverse copolymers.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrated that thermodynamic control within a specific, localized prebiotic regime, an unbuffered, highly acidic wet-dry cycle (pH ~2 to 3), provides a physical mechanism for the abiotic synthesis of polymers from simple, non-activated precursors. Rather than yielding uniform, canonical sequences, our results highlight the power of “messy chemistry” driven by environmental filtering. We revealed two distinct and parallel polymerization pathways within this harsh regime: cycling a mixture of AMP and UMP generated highly heterogeneous, polydisperse oligonucleotides with biologically relevant phosphodiester backbones, whereas cycling D-ribose-5’-phosphate yielded robust polymers with a non-biological pyrophosphate backbone likely via a disproportionation reaction. Both polymer types, while susceptible to alkaline hydrolysis, exhibit stability against specific enzymatic degradation by RNase 1, DNase 1, and the debranching enzyme DBR1. This resistance confirms that the severe physical pressures of this acidic thermal regime select for highly modified, non-canonical architectures, such as damaged or missing nucleobases, capable of persisting where standard canonical RNA would degrade. Ultimately, these findings reinforce the modern paradigm of the early Earth not as a single global ocean, but as a diverse mosaic of highly localized physicochemical niches, each capable of uniquely driving complex chemical evolution and the synthesis of structurally robust biopolymers.
