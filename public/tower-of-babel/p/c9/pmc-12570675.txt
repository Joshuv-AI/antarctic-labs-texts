Green and solid-state approaches: dyes mitigation efficiency of two trimetallic alloys in aquatic environment
Authors: Faiz Rabbani, Hafza Nosheen Anwar, Huzaifa Shahid, Faisal Faiz, M. Asim Farid, M. Atif, G. M. Shah, Hafiz Faiq Bakhat, Ahson J. Shaikh, M. Mubeen, M. Akram, Adnan Arshad, Hakkı Akdeniz, Zeki Erden, Çağdaş Can Toprak, Ayman El Sabagh, Ibrahim Al-Ashkar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The escalating ecological pollution, particularly from organic colorants, is having a devastating impact on aquatic ecosystems, with dire consequences for marine life such as discharge of polluted water from various industries may reduce the biodiversity in water. Two novel trimetallic alloys expressed as CoNiCuOx (TMAs) and ZnNiCuOx (TMAg) were successfully synthesized via non-solvated and green methods respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis was employed to investigate the surface morphology and pore size distribution within the synthesized matrix. Vibrational analysis was conducted to confirm the presence of metal oxide vibrational bands and adsorbed water molecules on the synthesized materials. Additionally, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out to determine the structural characteristics and crystallinity of both TMAs and TMAg. The TMAs material demonstrated exceptional efficiency in removing various dyes such as 71% for Ponceau S at 45 °C, 81% for Amaranth at a dose of 8 mg/L, 67% for Trypan Blue after 25 min of UV exposure, 93% for Congo Red at 45 °C, 100% for Evan Blue at a dose of 8 mg/L. In contrast, the green-synthesized material showed notable removal efficiencies such as 45% for Congo Red after 25 min of UV exposure, 72% for Brilliant Green at a dose of 8 mg/L, 25% for Orange-1 at a material dose of 8 mg/L, 20% for Methyl Yellow after 25 min of UV exposure. The mitigation process was investigated under various experimental conditions, including the impact of material dosage, UV light exposure, contact time between the dye and material, and temperature effects on the reaction medium. The results showed that the non-solvated method outperformed the green approach in terms of efficiency for removing organic dyes.

Introduction

A number of materials such as nano metallic oxides [1] and nanocomposites were studied for the mitigation of these emerging pollutants [2]. Metal oxides are acting as good catalysts and excellent sensors because of their greater surface area and high reactivity. In recent years. the biological methods were used to prepare different materials having different forms, sizes, compositions, and physicochemical qualities. A new self-scrubbing nanofiltration film for the efficient decoloration of the cationic dye MB [3] from colorful wastewater. Scientists prioritize using plants for green synthesis of metal oxides due to the abundant volume of biomass. Various plant components with distinct qualities including seeds, leaves, roots, barks, and fruits are being used for production [4]. Plant extracts have advantages over other biological systems on the basis of inexpensive, nontoxic, rapid and production scale [5]. Various components of plant extract like proteins and carbohydrates that function to reduce metals and synthesize of Metal oxides. The plant leave extract is combined with a salt solutions comprising cobalt, nickel, copper, and zinc at room temperature. The quality, stabilization, quantity generated, and yield rate of the metal oxides are influenced by several variables i.e. pH, temperature, contact time, concentration of metal salt and nature of components present in plant. The metal oxide reduction in plants occurs more quickly than that in bacteria and fungi because they require an extensive incubation period due to the availability of water-soluble phytochemicals. Recently the ternary metal oxide nanoparticles (NiCuCoOx) were synthesized using hydrothermal method and were used and were applied as capacitors [6]. Similarly ternary ZnO-NiO-CuO prepared using co-precipitation method and used as a photoanode [7] and ammonia sensing purpose [8].

The indiscriminate release of hazardous synthetic dyes [9] into aquatic environments has become a pressing environmental concern, posing significant threats to ecosystems, human health, and the economy. Water resources are severely contaminated on account of fast industrial development and growing population [10]. The textile, paper, and leather industries are among the primary contributors to dye pollution, with millions of tons of dye-containing wastewater discharged annually. Various organic/inorganic industrial effluents pose a serious threat to the biosphere and should be appropriately treated before being released into the environment [11]. More than half (54%) of the colorants discharge currently in the biosphere across the world from the textile area. The discharge can be divided into four sectors such as dyeing sector (21%), dye production (7%), tannery and paint (8%), paper and pulp (10%) industries [12]. The recalcitrant nature of these dyes, combined with their potential to cause mutagenic and carcinogenic effects, necessitates the development of innovative and sustainable remediation strategies. Various chemicals were being added during dying procedure in order to enhance the adsorption rate between the color and the fibers. Once the finished product is ready, some of these chemicals and dyes become part of it due to their high stability and toxicity to withstand degradation even in harsh conditions [13]. Water pollution by dyes is a severe disposal issue because dyes are highly visible in water and undesirable [14]. In 2018, WHO reported that approximately 844 million persons may have a lack of safe water drinking facility globally [15] .

More than 100,000 tons of dyes are reportedly produced annually, and approximately 10% of these dyes are released into the environment, endangering aquatic life and endangering human health. Benzene, anthraquinone, naphthalene, and other compounds are used as raw materials in the dye production process. These compounds typically chelate with metals or salts to produce dye wastewater that contains acids, salts, alkalis, halogen, dyes, hydrocarbons, amine, nitro, and related intermediates. Additionally, some colors can break down into heavy metals including mercury, cadmium, and chromium as well as carcinogenic aromatic amines and dyes containing cyanide, pyridine and phenol [16].

Discharged non-biodegradable textile water can typically disrupt vital components by obstructing light from reaching aquatic ecosystems, which affects the growth and reproduction of bacteria that breakdown dyes. Serious ecological impacts emerge from this, including altered aquatic environments and lessened photosynthesis to aquatic plants. Due to uncontrolled liberate of mineral components like nitrites and phosphates, this disrupted the process of photosynthesis in aqua plants and caused the sensation of eutrophication such as biotic and abiotic stresses which leads to imbalance the production of reactive oxygen demand etc.

This also resulted in durable risks of diligence, byproducts of chlorination, accumulation inside organisms and chromium’s mutagenicity and carcinogenicity [14, 17]. Industrial effluents may change physical, biological and chemical characteristics of aquatic environment by altering turbidity, noise, odor, pH and temperature. These changes in water’s quality can also result in health issues which make it hazardous and increase risk of allergies, skin irritations, dermatitis, cancer and genetic abnormalities in people [17]. Many aquatic disorders, including mucous skin, tear off nasal septum and austere respiratory tract generally are caused by dyes present in water [18].

Traditional dye removal methods, such as chemical coagulation, adsorption, and biological treatment, often suffer from limitations including high operational costs, secondary pollution, and reduced efficiency. In contrast, green and solid-state approaches have emerged as promising alternatives, leveraging the unique properties of nanostructured materials to achieve enhanced dye mitigation efficiency. Currently reported methods to remove dyes categorized into three main categories chemical, biological and physical treatment methods. Conventional chemical methods of dye removal are ultraviolet irradiation, advanced oxidation process, fenton reaction, electrochemical destruction, photochemical, oxidation and ozonation [12]. The applications of bacteria, fungi and algae species result the biological methods and the use of adsorption materials including agricultural, natural and industrial species result the physical methods.

Based on nature of nanoparticles, bimetallic and trimetallic oxides are more preferred as they have unique properties and structure [19]. Transition polymetallic oxides are more attracted due to higher catalytic activity and more efficient in many applications such as heterogenous nanocatalyst during various transformations [20]. Nano researchers and scientists are working to decrease the size and morphology of these polymetallic materials as dimers, trimers and alloys due to their novel properties such as optical, magnetic, electrical and catalytic ones [21].

In current studies, the non-solvated reactions of Co-Ni-Cu-Cl system led to Co-Ni-Cu-Ox (TMAs) by solid state approach and Zn-Ni-Cu-Cl system led to Zn-Ni-Cu-Ox (TMAg) using green method.

[22], as eco-friendly catalysts for the degradation of synthetic dyes in aquatic environments. This study demonstrates the efficacy of TMAs and TMAg in mitigating a diverse range of dyes and colorants, with TMAs targeting five specific dyes and TMAg applied to the removal of five distinct colorants. Moreover, Optimization process during solid state synthesis using different concentration of metal chlorides and roasting at different temperatures for its purification is another its uniqueness. Notably, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive investigation to explore the simultaneous mitigation of such a large number of dyes and colorants using these materials. By harnessing the synergistic effects of these trimetallic alloys, this research aimed to develop a novel, efficient, and sustainable dye removal technology that could be easily integrated into existing wastewater treatment infrastructure.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The metal halides such as Copper chloride, CuCl2 (Purity > 98%), nickel chloride, NiCl2 (Purity > 98%), cobalt chloride, CoCl2 (Purity > 98%) and zinc chloride, ZnCl2 (Purity > 98%) were used during experiment. The organic dyes including congo red (CR), brilliant green (BG), orange-1 (O-1), trypan blue (TB), ponceau S (PS), amaranth (AR), methylene blue [22], evan blue (EB) and methyl yellow [7] were purchased from sigma aldrich and were used without any refinement.

Solid state synthesis

The non-solvated reactions of Co-Ni-Cu-Cl system led to TMAs by solid state approach [23]. The material taken was CuCl2 (0.315 g, 134.45 mmol), CoCl2 (0.30 g, 129.83 mmol) and NiCl2 (0.38 g, 162.2 mmol) with non-stoichiometric ratio of anhydrous chlorides of cobalt, nickel and copper. The mixture was ground using an agate pestle and mortar, then transferred to a 10 ml crucible and heated in a furnace and ramped to 530 ˚C for 24 h. The resulting material was further annealed to room temperature. After synthesis, TMAs was washed with acetone, dried in an oven for 30 min, and then characterized. Subsequently, TMAs was utilized for the effective removal of colorants [24]. To ensure the purity of TMAs, experiments were conducted at different temperature ranges (450–530 ˚C) as reported for the synthesis of high entropy oxides [25]. Additionally, non-stoichiometric ratios of anhydrous metal chlorides were employed, as shown in Table 1, to optimize the synthesis process. This study builds upon previous research, which successfully synthesized nano copper antimony oxychlorides [26] and various phases of cobalt and nickel selenium oxychlorides [27, 28] using a non-solvated approach.

Green synthesis

In this study, three extracts (Citrus medica- Lemon, Aloe barbadensis miller-Aloe Vera and Allium cepa-Onion) were used for the synthesis of inorganic moiety (TMAg). The leave of each plant (100 g) was separately washed, dried and cut into small pieces followed by heating at 80 °C for 2 h in 200 ml distilled water. The extract of Lemon, Onion and Aloe vera was filtered and mixed with 1 g of anhydrous ZnCl2, NiCl2 and CuCl2, respectively and stirred for 5 min using magnetic stirrer at 150 rpm. The mixtures were combined in a beaker and stirred on magnetic stirrer for an hour. The mixture was centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 10 min. The upper portion of mixture was separated from solid residue in a beaker which was further heated at 100 °C to make thick paste. The dark brown paste was collected in ceramic crucible which was roasted in muffle furnace for 2 h at 350 °C to obtain TMAg material which was used further for characterization [29]. Characterization

Synthesized TMAs and TMAg materials were characterized using SEM Micrograph connected with Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX) S3700N (HITACHI), Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrometer, 6700 P (Thermo-Nicolet), powder XRD, PANalytical X’Pert Powder (Philips) and UV-Visible Spectrophotometer, Lambda 25 (Perkin Elmer).

TMAs was used for mitigation of CR, AR, TB, EB and PS while TMAg was applied to remove CR, BG, O-1, TB and MY on their λmax. Efficacy of both materials was evaluated at different experimental conditions such as TMA dose (2–8 mg), irradiation time (10–50 min), reaction temperature (25–45 ˚C) and reaction time (5–25 min). The absorbance was monitored by UV/Vis Spectrophotometer and removal efficiency (R%) of TMAs and TMAg was calculated using Equation (1) [30].

Study was conducted to assess TMAs dosage effect on the removal of five dyes: EB, AR, CR, TB, and PS. For this purpose, 20 ml 2 ppm of each dye solution was taken in 5 beakers considering as five experiment sets. 1st set of dye solution was considered as standard while 2nd, 3rd, 4th and 5th sets were blended with 2, 4, 6, and 8 mg of TMAs, respectively, stirred continuously for 10 min and centrifuged to separate TMAs suspension from dye solution. Absorbance of dye solutions were recorded at their λmax and compared with standard solution. Same method was applied for the removal efficiency of TMAg at its various dosages.

UV irradiation-induced removal of EB, AR, CR, TB, and PS was studied using TMAs. This procedure involved managing 10 ml 2 ppm solution of each dye in seven beakers, which were regarded as seven sets of tests. 1st set of dye solution was a standard solution without UV exposure while 2nd set of dye solution was considered as standard solution with UV exposure for 40 min. All the remaining sets of solution were mixed with 2 mg of TMAs separately and exposed under UV light for different duration (10 to 50 min). After UV exposure, each set of dye solution was centrifuged. Absorbance of each set of dye solution was recorded at their λmax and compared to both of their standards (with and without UV exposure). Similarly, removal efficiency of TMAg was measured at its various UV exposure times.

An effect of temperature and contact time on removal process with both TMAs and TMAg were measured by varying temperature and contact time of reaction mixtures.

Results and discussion

Prepared materials were characterized using different characterization techniques.

Morphology

SEM analysis was performed to examine the surface of synthesized materials. Data of TMAg was recorded in 5–500 μm resolution (Fig. 2 - upper) while data of TMAs, was recorded in 0.5–5 μm resolution (Fig. 2 - lower). The material exhibited a porous structure with pore sizes ranging from 142 to 360 nm (Fig. 2, upper), potentially enhancing its interaction efficiency with various colorants. Particle surfaces in matrix appeared smooth, wrinkled and spherical (Fig. 2 - lower). Synthesized materials exhibited particle sizes ranging from 80 to 434 nm, spanning the nano to macro scale, which is comparable to reported sizes for nickel/cobalt particles [31]. Results revealed that inherent polarity of synthesized materials led to the aggregation of particles into larger units (aggregates), which further united into agglomerates [32]. This two-step assembly process yielded diverse morphologies, including triangular, tetrahedral, and pentagonal structures. Furthermore, the material’s surface was found to be uneven and rough, likely due to the extreme temperatures employed during the synthesis process [33]. EDX analysis confirmed the presence of Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn within the material.

FTIR

The vibrational bands in synthesized products were studied using FTIR analysis. FTIR patterns of TMAs were found in Fig. 3 which showed well-defined peaks in range of 411.71 cm− 1 to 1050.33 cm− 1. These peaks were co-related with stretching and bending modes of M-O which gave information about the presence of cobalt, nickel and copper in synthesized materials. The result indicated that water molecules were not adsorbed on TMAs surface, due to absence of O-H vibrational band in matrix.

FTIR patterns of TMAg (Fig. 3) clearly indicated vibrational bands of metal oxides as indicated by peak at 1558 cm− 1 [34]. Peak at 1584 cm− 1 has also been reported for metallic oxides [35]. It also showed no organic part in the matrix. The band at 3390 cm− 1 is attributed to the water moisture present on the surface of the synthesized product [36, 37]. At 1638 cm− 1 the vibration due to bending mode of water has been reported in literature [38]. The surface properties of both synthesized materials are not matched due to the presence of water moisture on the surface of TMAg which leads to attract more dye molecules more strongly than that of TMAs.

Removal of organic dyes

Two inorganic dye removal agents, TMAs and TMAg were utilized to remove dyes from various dye solutions (2–10 mg/l). The removal procedures were monitored at λmax values for all corresponding dyes, i.e. 620 nm for EB, 515 nm for PS, 523 nm for AR, 497 nm for CR, 580 nm for TB, 486 nm for O-1, 624 nm for BG, 580 nm for TB and 403 nm for MY using UV-Vis spectrophotometer. The study was matched with photo-degradation of Rhodamine B as reported in literature [23]. The absorbance patterns showed that initial dye concentration was highest at zero time, in the absence of material. As increasing amounts of trimetallic materials were added, dye concentration progressively decreased. Absorbance measurements indicated a decrease in dye removal rate over time. Discoloration trend was found as CR > AR > TB > EB > PS using TMAs and BG ˃ CR ˃ O-1 ˃MY ˃ TB using TMAg.

Optimization of dye removal

Effect of dose

Removal trend of different dyes at dose effect of TMAs (8 mg/20mL) has been found as EB (100%) [45], Am (81%) [46, 47], CR (60%) [48], TB (50%) [49] and PS (43%) [50] (Fig. 6) while the dose effect of TMAg (8 mg//20mL) has been found as BG (72%) [51], O-1 (25%), CR (14%), MY (12%) [52] and TB (5%) [53] (Fig. 7). TB removal results demonstrated that TMAs exhibited higher efficiency than TMAg in removing dyes, indicating a stronger dose-response effect. This can be attributed to the increased number of active sites and reactive surface area with higher material doses, leading to enhanced dye removal efficiency. Similar results were reported in [54] for MB removal using copper oxide nano-composites.

Effect of UV irradiation

When mixtures of various dyes and removal agents were exposed to UV light, dye removal trends were observed as follows: TB (67%), EB (33%) [45], CR (20.68%) [24], PS (8%) and Am (not significant) with TMAs (Fig. 6), and CR (45%), BG (51%), MY (20%), O-1 (14%) and TB (4.5%) with TMAg (Fig. 7). Similar trend was reported [22] for MB degradation using date pulp waste based zinc oxide NPs.

Effect of temperature

Temperature plays a crucial role in determining yield, stability, shape, and size of metal oxides synthesized via green methods. Elevated temperatures accelerate nucleation and enhance reaction rates [55]. Temperature of reaction mixture varied in 25–45˚C range.

Removal %age of dyes was found as CR (93.33%), EB (30%), Am (67%), PS (71%) and TB (12.3%) with TMAs (Fig. 6), and CR (15%), O-1(12%), TB (2.6%), BG and MY (not significant) with TMAg (Fig. 7). It is obvious that as heating temperature rises from 25 to 45 °C, removal capacity increases. This suggests that dye adsorption onto materials is endothermic, indicating that higher temperatures favor dye removal [56]. Maximum removal of MB was found to be 92%.

Effect of contact time

Dye removal efficiency was evaluated based on contact time between dye solution and removal agents. Results showed following removal percentages: EB (53%), TB (50%), PS (42.9%), Am (7%), and CR (2.5%) when using TMAs (Fig. 6). Dye removal on contact with TMAg was found as CR (8%), BG (47%), O-1 (12%) and MY (17%) and TB (2.6%) [57] (Fig. 7). This study is further confirmed by Pakzad [58] where CR degradation was maximum at highest time interval (88%).

On comparison of removal efficiency, TMAs is more efficient at higher temperature for CR removal (93.33% at 45 ˚C) than at other experimental conditions i.e. at 8mgL− 1 dose (60%), at UV exposure (20.68%), contact time (2.5%) while TMAg is more efficient under UV exposure for CR mitigation, 45% at 25 min UV exposure than at other experimental conditions i.e. 14% at dose 8mgL− 1, 15% at 45 ˚C, 8% at contact time of 30 min.

It has been observed that TB has been removed more effectively by TMAs at various conditions i.e. 50% in dose effect, 67% under UV exposure, 12.3% at 45 ˚C, 50% at 30 min contact time than that of TMAg at various experimental factors i.e. 5% in dose effect, 4.5% under UV exposure, 2.6% at 45˚C, 2.6% at 30 min contact time (Figs. 6 and 7).

TMAs consistently outperformed TMAg as a dye removal agent under all experimental conditions, demonstrating its potential as a superior option for optimizing dye removal process. This might be due to high roasting temperature of TMAs during its synthesis using solid state method. At high temperature, reaction between metal ions proved to be more fruitful which enhanced its surface and binding activity. This may be due to non-hydrated surface of TMAs as shown in Fig. 3 (black) whereas TMAg has adsorbed water molecules on its surface as shown in Fig. 3 (blue). These results also indicated that non-solvated approach is more efficient and effective than green approach. An optimization of mitigation showed that dose effect is more dominant factor, followed by temperature factor, UV exposure and then least one is contact time (Fig. 6).

Proposed mechanism for dye removal

Synthesized TMAs and TMAg were used to degrade organic dyes in wastewater. Combination of metals creates a positive hole [59], that forces water molecules to form hydroxyl radicals. Organic colorants, upon adhering to the surface of prepared samples, break down into smaller molecules, through oxidation. Dye mitigation process is initiated through physical adsorption onto prepared TMA surface. As the process progresses, liberate of electrons from NiCuZnOx creates positive holes in metal framework, facilitating adsorption of dyes. The prepared TMAs, comprising Ni, Co, Cu and Zn atoms, absorb light and generate electrons, resulting in the formation of positive holes within their structures. The positive holes generated in TMA structure facilitate water absorption, leading to the formation of hydroxyl radicals (·OH). These hydroxyl radicals react with organic dyes, ultimately oxidizing them into carbon dioxide and water. Concurrently, conduction band species combine with molecular oxygen to produce oxygen radicals, which then combine with water to form hydroxyl radicals, which oxidize dyes to produce their stable oxides. Synergistic action of valance band and conduction band species enables efficient disintegration of dye molecules, thereby facilitating mitigation process. Conclusion

This study investigated treatment of industrial colored wastewater using two micro-sized materials synthesized via non-solvated and green methods. TMAg pores in range of 142–360 nm enhanced its efficiency for interaction. FTIR analysis revealed a prominent peak at 1558 cm⁻¹, attributed to metal oxide vibrational bands in TMAg. Additionally, crystallite size of TMAg ranged from 16.74 to 45.09 μm. The dye removal trends using TMAs were: CR > AR > TB > EB > PS, whereas TMAg exhibited the trend: BG > CR > O-1 > MY > TB.

On comparison of removal efficiency, TMAs proved efficiency at higher temperature for CR removal (93.33% at 45 ˚C) than at other experimental conditions i.e. at 8mgL− 1 dose (60%), at UV exposure (20.68%), contact time (2.5%) while TMAg proved to be more effective under UV exposure for CR mitigation, 45% at 25 min UV exposure than at other experimental conditions i.e. 14% at dose 8mgL− 1, 15% at 45 ˚C, 8% at contact time for 30 min. It is proved that TB is being removed more effectively by TMAs at various conditions i.e. 50% at dose effect, 67% under UV exposure, 12.3% at 45 ˚C, 50% at 30 min than that of TMAg at various experimental factors i.e. 5% at dose effect, 4.5% under UV exposure, 2.6% at 45˚C, 2.6% at contact time 30 min.

TMAs has proved itself the most efficient removal agent for EB (100% at 8 mgL− 1 dose) than TMAg at all experimental conditions during the optimization of removal process. In future, more mixed metal alloys may be applied for removal of organic pollutants from polluted aqueous bodies.
