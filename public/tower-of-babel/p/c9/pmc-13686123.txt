High‐Fat Diet Exacerbates Neuropathology in a Transgenic Mouse Model of Multiple System Atrophy
Authors: Marie‐Laure Arotcarena, Anna Delamarre, Miguel Lopez‐Cuina, Hugo Martin, Elio Balpe, Xavier Fioramonti, Erwan Bezard, Wassilios G Meissner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare and devastating neurodegenerative disorder. Accumulating clinical and preclinical evidence suggests that diabetes and insulin resistance may adversely influence MSA pathophysiology.ObjectiveWe investigated the potential association between diabetes, impaired glucose homeostasis, and MSA neuropathology in rodents.MethodsWe subjected the PLP‐SYN (proteolipid promoter) transgenic mouse model of MSA to either a standard chow diet or a high‐fat diet (HFD) for 4 months to induce diet‐associated metabolic alterations. Metabolic, neuropathological, and behavioral parameters were subsequently evaluated at multiple time points.ResultsPLP‐SYN mice fed a HFD exhibited a more pronounced diabetic phenotype, characterized by aggravated peripheral glucose dysregulation and insulin resistance, compared with wild‐type mice on the same diet. Moreover, 4 months of HFD feeding aggravated MSA‐related neuropathology, as evidenced by increased α‐synuclein accumulation and enhanced dopaminergic neurodegeneration, accompanied by accelerated impairment of fine motor function.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings indicate an association between dysregulated glucose metabolism and MSA neuropathology. Our results further support the potential of modulating glucose metabolism to slow disease progression in MSA and provide additional rationale for exploring whether antidiabetic agents could provide therapeutic benefits. © 2026 The Author(s). Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare, adult‐onset, sporadic neurodegenerative disease characterized by varying combinations of parkinsonism, cerebellar impairment, and autonomic dysfunction. 1 , 2 The neuropathological hallmark is the presence of α‐synuclein (αSyn)‐positive intracytoplasmic inclusions within oligodendrocytes, termed “glial cytoplasmic inclusions,” which accumulate throughout the central nervous system. The disease follows a relentlessly progressive course, with a median survival of 6 to 9 years. 2 Although some symptomatic therapies are available, no disease‐modifying treatment has yet been established to slow disease progression.

Only a limited number of factors have been linked to the onset and prognosis of MSA. 1 , 2 Among these, type‐2 diabetes has been associated with poorer outcomes in one MSA cohort study, suggesting a possible connection between dysregulated glucose metabolism and MSA. 3 Similar associations have been reported for other neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson's disease (PD) and Alzheimer's disease. 4 Moreover, cross‐sectional studies have indicated a higher prevalence of diabetes and antidiabetic use among MSA patients. 5 , 6

Abnormal glucose metabolism includes insulin resistance, defined as a diminished responsiveness of target tissues to insulin and insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1) signaling. Indeed, several studies suggest altered insulin signaling in MSA patients, including elevated serum concentrations of insulin and IGF‐1. 7 , 8 Particularly, increased serum IGF‐1 levels have been correlated with both symptom severity and disease duration. 7 In line with these observations, we previously demonstrated evidence of central insulin resistance in postmortem brain tissue from MSA patients, reflected by increased levels of insulin receptor substrate‐1 (IRS‐1) phosphorylated at serine 312 (IRS‐1pS312) in neurons and oligodendrocytes of the putamen. 9 Consistently, elevated levels of IRS‐1pS307, the murine orthologue of IRS‐1pS312, were detected in transgenic mice expressing human αSyn in oligodendrocytes under the control of the proteolipid promoter (PLP‐SYN), a well‐established transgenic mouse model of MSA. 9 , 10 In this model, treatment with exendin‐4, a glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1RA) approved for the treatment of diabetes, reduced neuronal loss, oligodendroglial αSyn burden, and central insulin resistance. 9

Therefore, we investigated the potential relationship between metabolic disorders, impaired insulin signaling, and MSA‐related pathology. To this end, we assessed the extent of metabolic and neuropathological alterations in the well‐characterized PLP‐SYN transgenic mouse model of MSA 11 after exposure to a high‐fat diet (HFD) to induce diet‐associated metabolic alterations (Fig. 1A).

Patients and Methods

Animals

Homozygous transgenic PLP‐SYN male mice (MGI:3604008) overexpressing human αSyn under PLP 10 and background‐, age‐, and sex‐matched nontransgenic C57Bl/6J mice were used in this study (wild‐type [WT] mice, bred in house). They were bred and housed in a temperature‐controlled room under a 12‐ h light–dark cycle, with free access to food and water. To model metabolic alterations with weight gain and insulin resistance, mice were fed a HFD (45 kcal% fat, 35 kcal% carbohydrates, D12451, Research Diet, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) or a standard diet (SD: 8.4 kcal% fat, 72.4 kcal% carbohydrates, A04, Research Diet) for 16 weeks, starting at 2 months of age, as previously described. 12 , 13 WT‐SD: N = 11, WT‐HFD: N = 10, PLP‐SD: N = 7, and PLP‐HFD: N = 18.

Experiments were performed in accordance with the European Union directive of September 22, 2010 (2010/63/EU) on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. The Institutional Animal Care and Ethical Committee of Bordeaux University (CE50, France) approved the experiments authorized by the ministry under reference APAFIS#20775‐201904251 5421633 version7.

Metabolic Tolerance Tests

Mice were placed in a quiet and stress‐free room in individual boxes and starved for 6 hours (8:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m.) with free access to water before the experiment. Intraperitoneal glucose administration (1 g/kg) for the glucose tolerance test (GTT) and intraperitoneal insulin administration (human recombinant insulin: 0.75 IU/kg) for the insulin tolerance test (ITT) were performed at t0. Glucose concentrations were measured in a tail vein blood drop using a handheld whole‐blood glucose meter (glucometer) and corresponding glucose test strips (Accu‐Chek Performa) at t0, t0 + 15, t0 + 30, t0 + 45, t0 + 60, t0 + 90, and t0 + 120 minutes. At the end of the experiment, each mouse was placed back in the home cage with free access to food. Seven days separated the GTT and ITT. Fasting glycemia corresponds to the t0 time point of the GTT. The reported area under the curve (AUC) reflects glucose levels above this common reference value and includes both differences in baseline glycemia and the subsequent glucose excursion after glucose/insulin administration.

Open‐Field Test After Intranasal Insulin Challenge

Mice were handled and habituated daily to intranasal delivery injections 1 week before testing. The day of the experiment, mice were fasted for 18 hours before intranasal delivery of human recombinant insulin (7 μg/μL insulin, diluted in NaCl 0.9%; Sigma‐Aldrich, Saint‐Quentin‐Fallavier, France) or vehicle (NaCl 0.9%) (5 μL/nare) to reach a final dose of 2 IU of insulin. 14 Mice were returned to their home cages and tested 60 minutes after intranasal administration. Mice were placed in the open‐field arena, and videos were recorded for 10 minutes with a camera using EthoVision XT, version 15, software (Noldus Information Technology, Paris, France). Next, each mouse was removed and placed back in the home cage. The time spent in the center was recorded.

Beam Task Experiment

Motor coordination and balance were assessed using a version of the traversing beam task as previously described. 15 The total number was counted on videos for steps, slips, and errors. A slip through or outside of the grid was considered an error when the limb sliped below the grid surface (halfway down). 15

Tissue Collection

Animals were killed after 4 months on the diet. The left hemisphere of the brains, the upper lobe of the liver, and the epididymal fat of each animal were immediately frozen by immersion in a cold isopentane bath at −60°C for 1 minute and stored at −80°C for biochemical investigation. The right hemisphere of the brains of each animal was postfixed for 3 days in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C, cryoprotected in gradient 20% sucrose in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) before being frozen by immersion in a cold isopentane bath (−60°C) for at least 1 minute, and stored immediately at −80°C until sectioning using a cryostat for histochemical analysis.

Histopathological Analysis

To assess the integrity of the nigrostriatal pathway, tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) immunohistochemistry was performed on substantia nigra and striatal sections. Briefly, sections (50 μm) from three representative levels of the striatum (anterior, medial, and posterior) and serial sections (1/4) corresponding to the whole substantia nigra were incubated with a rabbit monoclonal antibody raised against TH (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, EP1532Y, ab137869, 1:5000). αSyn pathology was assessed with a rabbit antibody raised against human αSyn (Abcam, MJFR1, ab138501, 1:5000) and against pS129‐phosphorylated αSyn (Abcam, EP1536Y, ab51253, 1:5000) immunostaining. Briefly, selected sections of one rostro‐caudal level of substantia nigra and striatum were specifically identified and incubated in the same well to allow direct comparison of immunostaining intensity. For pretreatment with proteinase K, sections were incubated first with proteinase K (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA, AM2542) at 10 μg/mL in PBS before long sequential washes in distilled water and then in PBS. Inflammatory processes in the substantia nigra and the striatum were measured using glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)/S‐100 (DAKO, Santa Clara, California, USA, Z0334/Abcam, ab7852) and ionized calcium‐binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1, Wako, 019‐19741) immunohistochemistry.

Sections were incubated overnight at room temperature with the aforementioned antibodies. The following day, revelation was performed with anti‐species peroxidase EnVision system (DAKO), followed by 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine incubation. Sections were then mounted on gelatinized slides, dehydrated, counterstained with 0.1% cresyl violet solution if necessary, and coverslipped until further analysis. Sections were scanned in a high‐resolution scanner (PanScan, 3D Histech) at 20× magnification. Surface quantifications were estimated using either a deep‐learning detection or a threshold detection macro on Visiopharm software. SlideViewer and Fiji software were used to analyze microglial morphology using fractal dimension quantification, a validated technique for assessing microglial activation based on cell skeletonization, as we previously reported. 16 Three images per region and per animal were analyzed (63× for substantia nigra, n = 23–102 cells per animal; 40× for striatum, n = 7–102 cells per animal). All analyses were performed by a researcher blinded to the experiments.

Principal Component Analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in Python (Python software foundation version 3.9.7) and its scientific stacks—scipy, numpy, and matplotlib—using data described in Dataset S2. Correlations between variables listed in Dataset S1 were assessed using Pearson's correlation analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). All values are expressed as the mean and the standard error of the mean. Each dot in the scatterplot represents one individual. In all analyses, statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Details of the statistical analysis are presented in Dataset S3.

Results

Principal Component Analysis Reveals a Unique Cluster of PLP‐HFD Mice

In an initial exploratory analysis, we assessed 27 variables, divided into 2 behavioral, 8 metabolic, 13 postmortem histological, and 4 postmortem biochemical variables, associated with glucose metabolism, αSyn pathology, neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and motor impairment (Dataset S1). By applying a PCA (Dataset S2), we showed that HFD‐fed PLP‐SYN mice (PLP‐HFD) clustered along PC1 (Fig. 1B), which explained 30.4% of the variance. Interestingly, metabolic variables (body.weight, GTT.AUC.4mo, ITT.AUC.4mo, ITT.AUC.2mo, GTT.AUC.2mo, fat.weight, fasting glycemia), αSyn‐dependent variables (Surface.MJFR1.SN, Surface.MJFR1.STR, Psyn.PK.resist.SN), and those associated with neurodegeneration (Intensity.TH.SN, Surface.TH.SN, Number.TH.SN) represented the best contributors to PC1. These results suggested that the PLP‐HFD cohort presents a unique phenotype associated with specific metabolic and neuropathological features.

PLP‐HFD Mice Exhibit Impaired Peripheral Glucose Tolerance

During the in vivo phase, we assessed multiple metabolic variables at different time points (Fig. 1A). These included body weight, epididymal fat weight, fasting glycemia, and glycemic responses to glucose administration during a GTT or to insulin administration during an ITT.

As expected, both WT‐HFD and PLP‐HFD mice exhibited higher body weight compared with SD‐fed WT (WT‐SD) and PLP‐SYN (PLP‐SD) mice (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, the average body weight of PLP‐HFD mice was significantly higher than that of WT‐HFD mice after 4 months of diet (Fig. 2A). After 4 months of diet, PLP‐HFD mice also exhibited higher fasting glycemia than PLP‐SD and WT‐SD mice (Fig. 2B; Fig. S1A). Moreover, PLP‐HFD mice exhibited a higher epididymal fat mass (Fig. S1B), a well‐established and reliable indicator of adiposity. 17

Finally, we assessed the 2‐hour glycemic response of fasting mice to an intraperitoneal glucose administration (1 g/kg) using a GTT at T0, T0 + 2 months, and T0 + 4 months. At T0, right before the diet, there was no significant difference between WT and PLP‐SYN mice, suggesting that both cohorts present similar tolerance to glucose administration at basal levels (Fig. S1C). At T0 + 2mo and T0 + 4mo, we observed higher blood glucose levels during the GTT in WT‐HFD and PLP‐HFD mice compared to their littermate SD mice (Fig. 2C,D; Fig. S1D). In addition, PLP‐HFD mice exhibited a greater glucose intolerance compared to WT‐HFD mice after 4 months of diet (Fig. 2C,D), with a similar trend already observed at T0 + 2 months (Fig. S1D).

Altogether, these data indicate that HFD mice show impaired glucose metabolism, with PLP‐HFD mice exhibiting the most pronounced metabolic dysfunction compared to all other groups.

PLP‐HFD Mice Exhibit Peripheral Insulin Resistance and Impaired Brain Insulin Sensitivity

To assess whether PLP‐HFD mice exhibit peripheral insulin resistance in addition to an abnormal glucose tolerance, we evaluated the 2‐hour glycemic response of fasting mice to an intraperitoneal insulin injection (0.75 U/kg) during an ITT at T0 + 2 months and T0 + 4 months. At T0 + 2 months, only the PLP‐HFD cohort presented a less pronounced decrease in blood glucose in response to insulin administration compared to the WT‐SD and PLP‐SD groups (Fig. S1E). At T0 + 4 months, as expected, both the WT‐HFD and the PLP‐HFD exhibited higher blood glucose levels after insulin administration compared to their SD littermates (Fig. 2E,F). Interestingly, the PLP‐HFD cohort exhibited a trend for a greater insulin intolerance compared to WT‐HFD mice, suggesting that PLP‐SYN mice were more insulin‐resistant than WT mice (Fig. 2E,F).

To assess brain insulin sensitivity in the four experimental groups, we evaluated anxiety in the open‐field test in response to an acute intranasal insulin challenge. 14 Briefly, we performed an intranasal administration of insulin or saline solution 1 h before an open‐field test in 16‐h fasted animals and measured the time spent in the central area (Fig. S2A). We observed a trend in the WT‐SD group toward decreased time spent in the center, suggesting that, as expected, 14 insulin exerts anxiolytic properties. In contrast, there was no change in the time spent in the central area in all other groups tested (Fig. S2A), suggesting that PLP‐SD and PLP‐HFD mice present brain insulin resistance.

After termination, we assessed a biochemical marker of insulin resistance by analyzing the ratio of IRS‐1pSer307 to total IRS‐1 levels in the peripheral tissue (liver, Fig. S2B) and different brain areas (frontal cortex, striatum, and substantia nigra, Fig. S2C). We did not observe any significant difference in IRS‐1pSer307 to total IRS‐1 levels in the liver between the four experimental groups (Fig. S2B). In the brain, we also did not observe any significant difference in the IRS‐1pSer307 to total IRS‐1 ratio in the frontal cortex and the striatum between the experimental groups (Fig. S2C). However, in the substantia nigra, we observed increased levels of IRS‐1pSer307/total IRS1 in PLP mice compared to WT mice, suggesting insulin resistance in this region of interest. However, no difference was observed between PLP‐SD and PLP‐HFD mice.

Altogether, these data suggest that HFD‐fed mice exhibit peripheral insulin resistance and a moderate impairment in brain insulin sensitivity, in addition to abnormal glucose tolerance, which was more pronounced in PLP‐HFD mice.

PLP‐HFD Mice Present Increased α‐Synuclein Pathology

To determine the consequences of dysregulation of glucose metabolism on MSA pathology, we examined different markers of αSyn pathology in the postmortem brains from WT and PLP‐SYN mice fed a SD or HFD for 4 months. As the PLP‐SYN mice specifically overexpress the human SNCA gene under an oligodendroglial promoter (PLP promoter), we first aimed to evaluate the percentage of human αSyn‐positive oligodendrocytes in both the substantia nigra and the striatum (Fig. 3A,B). We observed no significant difference in total human αSyn protein levels between PLP‐SD and PLP‐HFD mice in either structure. We then moved to the analysis of a well‐known indirect readout of pathological αSyn, that is, serine 129‐phosphorylated αSyn (pS129‐αSyn), in both the substantia nigra and the striatum (Fig. 3C,E). Interestingly, we found that the PLP‐HFD cohort exhibited significantly higher amounts of pS129‐αSyn in the substantia nigra (Fig. 3C) and a trend toward an increase in the striatum (Fig. 3E). Submitting the nigral sections to proteinase K treatment enables the quantification of aggregated, that is, pathological pS129‐αSyn (Fig. 3D) by removing the soluble fraction. PLP‐HFD mice exhibited higher levels of PK‐resistant pS129‐αSyn compared to PLP‐SD in the substantia nigra (Fig. 3D).

Altogether, these data point to a higher pathological αSyn burden in PLP‐HFD mice.

Neurodegeneration and Fine Motor Impairment Are Exacerbated in PLP‐HFD Mice

Finally, we wondered whether dysregulation of glucose metabolism could contribute to the second major hallmark of MSA, the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc). Therefore, we performed a histochemical analysis of the nigrostriatal dopaminergic pathway in postmortem brains from WT and PLP‐SYN mice fed an SD or HFD for 4 months. We first quantified TH‐positive neurons in the SNpc (Fig. 4A,B). PLP‐HFD mice exhibited a strong trend toward dopaminergic neuron loss in the SNpc compared with PLP‐SD mice (Fig. 4A,B). PLP‐HFD mice also exhibited a significantly decreased amount of TH‐positive staining in the SNpc (Fig. 4C). However, PLP‐HFD mice did not present any dopaminergic fiber loss in the striatum, compared with any other experimental group (Fig. 4D,E), consistent with the notion that substantial TH‐positive neuron loss is required to detect corresponding TH fiber loss in the striatum. 18

Due to the key role of neuroinflammation in MSA‐related neurodegeneration, we assessed microglial and astroglial activation by quantifying the area as well as the state of ramification (fractal dimension) occupied by Iba1‐positive microglia (Fig. S3A,B) and GFAP‐positive astrocytes (Fig. S3C,D) in the substantia nigra and striatum of WT and PLP‐SYN mice fed a SD or HFD for 4 months. No significant differences were detected between WT and PLP‐SYN mice, or between SD and HFD mice, for either microglial or astroglial content in these regions (Fig. S3). These findings suggest that the exacerbated neurodegeneration observed in PLP‐HFD mice occurred independently of changes in microglial or astroglial immunoreactivity.

Finally, to evaluate fine motor skills, we performed a balance beam walking test on mice at T0 + 4 months, immediately prior to animal termination. This behavioral test was used to measure subtle deficits in motor skills and balance in trained mice. As expected for this age (6 months), 11 PLP‐SD mice did not present significant fine motor deficits (Fig. 4F). In contrast, PLP‐HFD mice exhibited a significantly higher number of errors in the beam task compared to WT‐HFD mice (Fig. 4F), suggesting that HFD aggravated and accelerated motor impairment in PLP‐SYN mice.

Altogether, these data demonstrate that HFD exacerbates dopaminergic neuron loss and aggravates motor deficits in transgenic PLP‐SYN mice, without affecting microglial or astroglial immunoreactivity.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated a potential link between diet‐induced metabolic alterations and MSA‐like pathology in a transgenic mouse model of MSA. An initial exploratory PCA, incorporating 27 variables related to glucose metabolism, αSyn pathology, neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and motor impairment, identified a distinct alteration profile in PLP‐HFD mice. This unique signature was primarily driven by abnormalities in peripheral glucose metabolism and markers of neurodegeneration.

PLP‐HFD mice exhibited greater impairments in peripheral glucose homeostasis and more pronounced insulin resistance compared with WT‐HFD controls, as reflected by increased body weight, hyperglycemia, elevated fat mass, and reduced tolerance to glucose and insulin challenges. These findings suggest that the oligodendroglial α‐synucleinopathy in PLP‐SYN mice directly or indirectly exacerbates the HFD‐induced metabolic disturbances, thereby strengthening a possible pathophysiological link between MSA and metabolic dysfunction.

Beyond dysregulated peripheral glucose metabolism and insulin resistance, and some indication of altered brain insulin sensitivity in the open‐field test, we detected significant differences in IRS‐1pSer307/total IRS‐1 levels—an established marker of insulin resistance—in the substantia nigra although we did not observe any difference between PLP‐WT and PLP‐HFD mice. This is in line with our previous findings, in which elevated IRS‐1pS307 levels were detected in the striatum of PLP‐SYN mice. 9 The issue of central insulin resistance in MSA remains critically important. Beyond disrupting downstream insulin signaling pathways, impaired insulin sensitivity may compromise neuronal glucose uptake, which is particularly detrimental due to the exceptionally high energy demands of the brain. 19 , 20 This is especially relevant for metabolically vulnerable regions such as the substantia nigra, a structure severely affected in MSA. 21 Further studies are needed to determine the extent to which HFD influences central insulin resistance in the context of MSA.

Neuropathologically, PLP‐HFD mice exhibited more severe alterations, including elevated levels of pS129‐αSyn and increased dopaminergic neuronal loss, accompanied by worsening fine motor impairment. Particularly, HFD not only aggravated neuropathology but also accelerated the clinical phenotype, as motor deficits were not anticipated at 6 months of age based on previous observations. 11 These findings are consistent with reports from preclinical models of PD, in which HFD‐induced obesity and insulin resistance exacerbate dopaminergic neurodegeneration and motor dysfunction. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain how dysregulated glucose metabolism and insulin resistance may potentiate dopaminergic neuronal loss, including mitochondrial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, impaired neurite outgrowth, synaptic deficits, and a heightened pro‐inflammatory response. 22 , 25 , 26 , 27 Although we did not detect evidence of neuroinflammatory reactivity based on the parameters assessed in this study, the evaluation of cytokine profiles would be necessary to provide a more comprehensive characterization of the inflammatory status. Moreover, insulin resistance has been shown to disrupt dopaminergic signaling by increasing dopamine turnover in the dorsal striatum, largely through enhanced monoamine oxidase–mediated degradation. 26 In addition, recent in vivo evidence indicates that HFD‐induced peripheral insulin resistance can increase S129 phosphorylation of αSyn, consistent with our observations. This increase in pS129‐αSyn burden has been associated with elevated expression of polo‐like kinase 2, a kinase implicated in αSyn phosphorylation. 28 Further mechanistic investigations are warranted to determine how impaired glucose metabolism contributes to the exacerbation of MSA neuropathology.

As limitations, we acknowledge that the control diet was not ingredient matched to the HFD; therefore, in addition to differences in fat content, variations in vitamins, minerals, and fiber may have contributed to the observed effects and should be considered when interpreting the results. Moreover, only male mice were included in this study; future investigations incorporating female cohorts will be necessary to assess potential sex‐dependent effects.

Similar to observations in patients with PD, 29 our findings support clinical translation to determine whether targeting glucose metabolism in MSA patients with insulin resistance or overt diabetes may help attenuate disease progression. More broadly, our results support the exploration of antidiabetic agents as potential disease‐modifying therapies for MSA. In this context, we previously demonstrated the therapeutic potential of exenatide in the PLP‐SYN mouse model, a first‐generation GLP‐1RA approved for the treatment of diabetes. 9 Exenatide reduced brain markers of insulin resistance, preserved dopaminergic neurons, and decreased striatal monomeric αSyn levels. Furthermore, a phase 2, randomized, single‐center, open‐label clinical trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of exenatide in a small cohort of patients with MSA (25 receiving weekly exenatide in addition to standard care and 25 receiving standard care alone). 30 After 48 weeks, the total Unified MSA Rating Scale score (primary endpoint) worsened by 6.1 points in the exenatide group compared with 13.3 points in the standard care group (P = 0.0003). Although the single‐center, open‐label design limits definitive conclusions, these encouraging findings warrant further exploration in a larger, multicenter, randomized, placebo‐controlled trial.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates an association between impaired glucose metabolism and neuropathological alterations in a transgenic mouse model of MSA. Our findings further support the potential of targeting metabolic dysfunction as a strategy to slow disease progression in MSA and provide additional rationale for exploring whether antidiabetic agents could offer therapeutic benefits.
