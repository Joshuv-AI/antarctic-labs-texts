Comparison of postoperative outcomes related to ejaculatory function and post-micturition dribbling between bulbospongiosus muscle sparing and non-sparing anastomotic urethroplasty techniques
Authors: Rodolfo Brilhante de Farias, Francisco E Martins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe bulbospongiosus muscle is located in perineum and acts on the propulsion of urine on micturition and sperm on ejaculation. The aim of this study was to compare the outcomes of excision and primary anastomotic urethroplasty (EPA) with and without bulbospongiosus muscle sparing related to ejaculatory function and post-micturition dribbling postoperatively. In the methods, this was a prospective interventional study. Seventy patients with bulbar urethral stricture who underwent EPA in a single institution were included in the present study after having signed the informed consent form. Patients were divided in a single-blind and random manner into 2 groups: 35 underwent bulbospongiosus muscle sparing technique and 35 non-muscle sparing. Preoperative and postoperative (after 3–15 months) ejaculatory function was assessed using the 4 ejaculatory questions of Male Sexual Health Questionnaire Short Form (MSHQ-EjD-SF) and post-micturition dribbling by patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) questionnaire for urethral stricture surgery. In the results, in the preoperative evaluation, mean score of MSHQ-EjD-SF showed no statistically significant difference between the 2 groups compared (p = 0.87) and no patient included in this study had post-micturition dribbling. The mean age of patients was 61.47 years (± 10.41). The mean length of urethral stricture was 1.97 cm (± 0.27). The mean follow-up was 18.7 months (± 2.91). The overall surgical success rate in this study was 95.7% (67/70). There was a decrease in mean score of MSHQ-EjD-SF 3 to 15 months after the urethroplasty compared to the preoperative period in the 2 groups of patients studied. The mean decrease in MSHQ-EjD-SF score was of 0.59 (± 0.10) in patients with bulbospongiosus muscle sparing and 0.67 (± 0.12) in patients without sparing. However, this decrease did not show any statistically significant difference within each group (p = 0.52 for the muscle-sparing group and p = 0.55 for the non-muscle-sparing group) nor between the 2 groups compared (p = 0.72). There was also no statistically significant difference between the groups regarding post-void dribbling, with 1 patient in the sparing group and 2 in the non-sparing group reporting this complication (p = 0.64). In conclusion, the alternative technique of bulbospongiosus muscle sparing in EPA urethroplasty does not seem to improve the outcomes related to ejaculatory function and post-micturition dribbling postoperatively. This suggests that the division of bulbospongiosus muscle in midline in the standard non-muscle-sparing technique would not lead to significant damage to muscle innervation and vascularization than in the alternative muscle-sparing technique.Keywords: Urethral stricture, Surgery (urethroplasty), Postoperative complications, Ejaculation, Lower urinary tract symptoms, Organ sparing treatmentsSubject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Urology

Introduction

Urethral stricture is a complex disease worldwide and that poses many challenges for its treatment. Treatment usually includes surgery, and depending on the characteristics of each case, there is a wide variety of surgical techniques that can be used. The main objective of these types of surgical treatment is to obtain the highest success rate represented by adequate urethral patency and normal urination, and with minimal stricture recurrence rate that require complementary surgical treatments. However, in more recent years, in addition to the concern with achieving the highest possible success rate, there has also been a growing interest in reconciling a high success rate of urethral patency with the reduction of complications that could compromise patients’ quality of life. Therefore, surgical techniques have been created and adapted to preserve as much as possible the tissues addressed during the surgeries, with the aim of maximum preservation of the vascularization, nerves, external urinary sphincter, among other important anatomic structures, thus also leading to a lower number of complications and better functional results in terms of erectile and urinary functions. Another of these structures that has been considered for preservation is the bulbospongiosus muscle. The bulbospongiosus muscle is a paired muscle that covers the bulb of the urethra1 and, through rhythmic contractions, this muscle is responsible for the expulsion of semen and urine, especially the last drop2. In the standard surgical technique for treating bulbar urethral stricture, there is a median longitudinal division of the bulbospongiosus muscle to expose the bulbar urethra. An alternative technique was created, in which this median longitudinal division of the bulbospongiosus muscle is avoided; the muscle is simply released from the urethra and pulled downward to expose the bulbar urethra, with the intention of completely preserving the bulbospongiosus muscle and, therefore, assessing whether this latter technique would result in fewer ejaculatory disorders and less post-void dribbling. The objective of this study was to compare the results of these 2 surgical techniques for treating bulbar urethral stricture: the standard one in which the median longitudinal division of the bulbospongiosus muscle is performed, and an alternative one in which the bulbospongiosus muscle is preserved and is not split in half.

Methods

After all necessary ethical approvals by the Human Research Ethics Committee , the study began to be conducted at a single institution. All methods and experiments of the present study were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This was a prospective interventional study and it was single-blind, meaning only the patients included in the study were blinded to their group. The surgical team, however, needed to be aware of each patient’s group in order to perform the specific type of surgical technique planned for each group. The surgeries for the patients included in this study began to be performed in the year 2018 and continued until the year 2022. The inclusion criteria consisted of male patients with bulbar urethral stricture who were candidates for surgical treatment through Anastomotic Urethroplasty—Excision and Primary Anastomosis (EPA)—who understood, agreed to and signed the informed consent form. The informed consent form was obtained from all the patients included in this study and/or their legal guardian(s). All urethroplasty surgeries in this study were performed by a single surgeon. This type of anastomotic urethroplasty called EPA is already a well-known technique and includes a complete transection of the urethra, excision of all spongiofibrosis (the whole stricture) and primary anastomosis of the healthy urethral ends without fibrosis. Eligible patients underwent surgery alternately in each group. In the first eligible patient, we began with the standard technique without preservation of the bulbospongiosus muscle, and the next eligible patient was operated on using the alternative technique with preservation of the bulbospongiosus muscle. So this continued in an alternating manner between the two groups until the end of the study. The total sample of the seventy patients included in this study was divided in a single-blind and random manner into 2 groups: − 35 patients underwent the standard technique in which there was a median longitudinal division of the bulbospongiosus muscle (dissection of the bulbospongiosus muscle and its division in the midline using scissors, it was not used electrocautery for that, “cold dissection and incision” only used) to expose the bulbar urethra, with its subsequent reconstitution with complete closure through continuous suture with 3 − 0 Vycril after performing the urethral anastomosis. A second group of 35 patients underwent an alternative technique, in which the bulbospongiosus muscle was not divided in the midline, but after being released from the bulbar urethra and pulled downwards to obtain urethral exposure, then the muscle was lifted again and sutured back into its original position covering the urethra after the urethral anastomosis. To assess ejaculatory function, patients were asked to complete the self-administered short form of the Male Sexual Health Questionnaire (MSHQ-EjD Short Form)3. It contains four questions related to ejaculatory function3. Patients in both groups were also asked to complete the patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) questionnaire for urethral stricture surgery4 to assess post-void dribbling. The pre- and postoperative (3 to 15 months after surgery) results of both questionnaires were analyzed and compared.

For the statistical analysis, data were checked, entered, and analysed using MedCalc Software (version 20). Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation for quantitative variables, and number and/or percentage for qualitative variables. We used the unpaired student t test and chi-square test to analyse data when appropriate. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant and confidence interval was 95%.

Results

In the preoperative evaluation, the mean MSHQ-EjD-SF score did not show a statistically significant difference between the 2 groups compared (p = 0.87) and no patient included in this study presented post-void dribbling.

The mean age of the patients was 61.47 years (± 10.41). The mean length of the urethral strictures was 1.97 cm (± 0.27). The mean follow-up was 18.7 months (± 2.91). The overall surgical success rate in this study was 95.7% (67/70), with two cases of recurrence from the standard technique group and one from the bulbospongiosus muscle preservation group. All these 3 cases of recurrence were additionally successfully treated by endoscopic procedure (simple urethral dilatation).

There was a decrease in the mean MSHQ-EjD-SF score 3 to 15 months after urethroplasty compared to the preoperative period in the 2 groups of patients studied. The mean decrease in the MSHQ-EjD-SF score was 0.59 (± 0.10) in patients with preservation of the bulbospongiosus muscle and 0.67 (± 0.12) in patients without preservation. However, this decrease did not present a statistically significant difference within each group (p = 0.52 for the group with muscle preservation and p = 0.55 for the group without muscle preservation) nor between the 2 groups compared (p = 0.72). There was also no statistically significant difference between the groups regarding post-void dribbling, with 1 patient in the sparing group and 2 in the non-sparing group reporting this complication (p = 0.64). Regarding the surgical techniques, all anastomotic urethroplasties (EPA) could be performed satisfactorily, without complications, with the technique of preservation of the bulbospongiosus muscle presenting a greater difficulty than the standard technique for total exposure of the area of ​​the most proximal bulbar urethral stricture cases. However, this difficulty did not prevent the performance of the bulbospongiosum muscle preservation approach. On the other hand, the standard technique of non-preservation of the bulbospongiosus muscle required some additional time for the reconstitution of the bulbospongiosus muscle including closure of its median longitudinal division.

Discussion

In bulbar urethroplasty surgeries, complications such as ejaculatory dysfunction and post-void dribbling remain as potential surgical concerns. Modifications of preservation techniques have been proposed in recent years with the aim of reducing these complications and, consequently, improving patients’ quality of life, in addition to achieving a high urethroplasty success rate. For example, in 2008, Barbagli et al.5 described a modification of the standard urethroplasty technique with preservation of muscles and nerves to reduce the occurrence of these complications. The difference was that Barbagli et al.5 described the preservation technique performing only substitution urethroplasties with an onlay buccal mucosa graft, while in this study only anastomotic urethroplasties (EPA) were performed. This difference in the use of the substitution technique with a buccal mucosa graft is important in the assessment of the possibility of graft sacculation, which may increase the risk of post-void dribbling and semen sequestration compared with the anastomotic technique (EPA) without the use of a graft or flap. Another study evaluating the topic by Elkady et al.6 from 2018 showed results comparable to those of Barbagli et al.5 regarding post-void dribbling and semen sequestration. This study by Elkady et al.6 also used substitution urethroplasty using buccal mucosa graft as in the study by Barbagli et al.5. So, unlike the findings of the present study, these 2 previously cited studies by Barbagli et al.5 and Elkady et al.6, which both used substitution urethroplasty techniques, showed better results related to ejaculatory function and lower risk of post-void dribbling with the use of the bulbospongiosus muscle preservation technique. However, on the other hand, the study by Fredrick et al.7 from 2017, which used an anastomotic urethroplasty technique like this study, showed results compatible with the present study, that is, there was no statistically significant difference between urethroplasties with and without preservation of the bulbospongiosus muscle in relation to ejaculatory function and the incidence of post-void dribbling. The advantages of this study compared to Fredrick’s study7 were a significantly larger sample of patients, the assessment in relation to post-voiding dribbling using an already validated questionnaire: patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) questionnaire for urethral stricture surgery, in addition to a longer follow-up period. What could explain this absence of difference between one urethroplasty technique and another found in the present and also in Fredrick’s study7 would be the anatomy of the bulbospongiosus muscle, in which most of the innervation and vascularization would come from a lateral and more proximal position and would therefore not be as affected in the median longitudinal division of the muscle generally performed in the standard technique. Subsequently, the reconstitution with the closure of this median longitudinal division would be able to recover the muscle well enough for the maintenance of adequate function without major significant complications. Therefore, in the author’s opinion, the bulbospongiosum muscle preservation technique should be performed whenever possible, thereby avoiding the median longitudinal division of the bulbospongiosus muscle. However, given the findings of this study and of Fredrick et al.7, if this implies significant difficulties in adequate exposure of the area of urethral stricture, especially in the most proximal bulbar urethra, we will not insist on preservation and would divide the bulbospongiosus muscle to allow adequate access without difficulty and consequently provide an adequate anastomosis and correction of the urethral stricture. We would also suggest that the bulbospongiosus muscle sparing surgical technique should be beneficial in the scenario of reoperation (redo) cases (patients who already had previous bulbar urethroplasty) in order to avoid further injure of the anatomy and function of the bulbar urethra, although no studies and consequently no robust evidence exists in the literature. As reported in the studies by Barbagli et al.5 and Elkady et al.6, substitution urethroplasty techniques using a buccal mucosa graft can produce different results. Therefore, this fact of not comparing different urethroplasty techniques (such as anastomotic and substitution urethroplasties) to evaluate the postoperative results was a limitation of this study, and thus, we recommend expanding the research with the comparison between different urethroplasty techniques, specifically EPA and substitution urethroplasties using buccal mucosa graft. Another limitation of the present study was that it could not have been double-blind because the surgical team needed to know which group each patient belonged to in order to perform the specific type of surgical technique planned for each group.

Conclusion

The alternative technique of bulbospongiosus muscle preservation in anastomotic bulbar urethroplasty (EPA) does not appear to improve outcomes related to ejaculatory function and post-void dribbling in the postoperative period. This suggests that the midline division of the bulbospongiosus muscle in the standard non-muscle-sparing technique would not lead to significantly more damage to muscle innervation and blood supply than in the alternative muscle-sparing technique. In our opinion, the muscle-sparing technique can be used whenever it does not add additional significant difficulty to the surgery.
