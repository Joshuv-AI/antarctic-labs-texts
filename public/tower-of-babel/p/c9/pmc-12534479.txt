miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p can be used in a novel serum prognostic panel for cervical cancer
Authors: Shuping Li, Huiying Li, Yan Li, Ying Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Due to cervical cancer poor prognosis, discovering prognostic biomarkers is urgently needed. Numerous publications indicated the presence of stable microRNAs (miRNAs) in human serum. This study aimed to identify a specific panel of serum miRNAs that could be used as reliable biomarkers for cervical cancer prognosis evaluation. The candidate miRNAs were measured using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction in serum and tissue samples from patients with either favorable (n = 62) or unfavorable (n = 65) prognosis. These miRNAs were correlated with clinical parameters and patients’ survival. Functional assays assessed the role of candidate miRNAs in cervical cancer cells. Receiver operating characteristic curves assessed the candidate miRNAs prediction value for cervical cancer prognosis. Compared to the good prognosis group, plasma miR-378 and miR-20a were upregulated, and miR-520a-3p was downregulated in the poor prognosis group. miR-378 and miR-20a were identified as independent risk factors (P = 0.044 and 0.018), both of which promoted C33a cell proliferation, invasion, and migration while suppressing apoptosis. In contrast, miR-520a-3p acted as a protective factor for cervical cancer prognosis (P = 0.018), exhibiting opposing effects on malignant behaviors. The combination of the three miRNAs showed the best prognostic predictive potential for cervical cancer, with an area under curve of 0.907. The serum miRNA biomarker panel (miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p) could be used as a non-invasive and accurate prognostic predictor of cervical cancer. This panel could be a promising tool in clinical therapeutic strategies to aid in the timely prediction and management of cervical cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20338-6.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors in the female reproductive system1. According to the Global Cancer Statistics Report2, there were approximately 662,000 new cases of cervical cancer worldwide in 2022, resulting in 348,189 deaths, accounting for 3.3% and 3.6% of the total number of cancer incidence and mortality, respectively3. Radical hysterectomy, radiotherapy, traditional chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy have achieved some progress in treating patients with cervical cancer. However, follow-up data have indicated that low response and high recurrence rates remain refractory, with a 5-year survival rate of merely 17%4. Although previous studies have identified several risk factors associated with cervical cancer recurrence and prognosis, such as age, ethnicity, pathological type, clinical stage, lymph node metastasis, tumor size, and treatment received5, there remains ongoing debate regarding the impact of certain factors. For example, findings related to age, pathological type, and tumor differentiation have been inconsistent across studies6. Therefore, it is necessary to explore new biomarkers to predict the risk, assess the prognosis, develop effective treatment strategies, and improve the survival rate of patients with cervical cancer.

The identification of microRNAs (miRNAs) has unveiled a new frontier in cancer research, offering remarkable potential for the development of miRNA-based biomarkers capable of regulating downstream genes and signaling pathways7. Accumulating data suggest that miRNAs are involved in the occurrence and development of various malignancies8. The main role of miRNAs in carcinogenesis is currently considered to be related to growth, differentiation, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis by negatively regulating target mRNA molecules in their 3’-untranslated region9. The miRNA expression patterns vary significantly among various tumor types and stages, therefore, the miRNA expression profiles can serve as promising non-invasive diagnostic markers. Furthermore, miRNA profiling is more advantageous than mRNA profiling as it can detect more stable and reliable biomarkers10. Due to the numerous functions of miRNAs in cancer development, their dysregulation might be linked to cancer progression. Many articles on miRNA biomarkers available for cancer prognosis have been published. For instance, miR-497 has been identified as a potential prognostic indicator in hepatocellular carcinoma11. The level of circulating miR-155 and miR-21 is upregulated in the presence of precursor lesions in the pancreatic tissue and was associated with histological progression characteristics, indicating that it might serve as a prognostic biomarker for pancreatic cancer12,13. Therefore, miRNAs are promising prognostic and predictive biomarkers for many malignancies. Recent advances have demonstrated that not only tumor suppressors and oncogenes but also miRNAs play a crucial role in the development and progression of cervical cancer. Previous research has identified numerous cancer-specific miRNAs for the early detection of cervical cancer, studies exploring the relationship between cervical cancer prognosis and circulating miRNAs remain limited14.

The objective of this study was to identify differentially expressed miRNAs in serum and tissue samples from patients with favorable or unfavorable prognosis and to explore their contributions to cervical cancer progression. Additionally, we aimed to assess their predictive value as novel biomarkers to better monitor the prognosis of patients with cervical cancer.

Results

Screening and confirmation of candidate MiRNAs

We evaluated the profiles of differentially expressed miRNAs during the occurrence, development, and in relation to the prognosis of cervical cancer using GEO2R (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r) to identify dysregulated miRNAs. A literature review was used to refine the list of candidate miRNAs15–19. The tissue levels of eight selected miRNAs were assessed in a subset of 62 patients with a good prognosis and 65 patients with a poor prognosis. As shown in Fig. 1a, the levels of miR-378 (0.028 ± 0.004 vs. 0.048 ± 0.009), miR-20a (0.017 ± 0.003 vs. 0.050 ± 0.008), miR-520a-3p (0.676 ± 0.091 vs. 0.093 ± 0.045), and miR-155-5p (0.303 ± 0.046 vs. 0.132 ± 0.031) differed significantly between patients with a good and a poor prognosis. Compared to the good prognosis group, the serum levels of miR-378 (0.165 ± 0.223 vs. 0.287 ± 0.376) and miR-20a (0.091 ± 0.129 vs. 0.154 ± 0.213) were significantly upregulated in the poor prognosis group, and that of miR-520a-3p (0.701 ± 0.335 vs. 0.457 ± 0.375) was significantly downregulated (Fig. 1b). Moreover, compared to the healthy control group, the serum levels of miR-378 (0.080 ± 0.064 vs. 0.165 ± 0.223) and miR-20a (0.039 ± 0.027 vs. 0.091 ± 0.129) were significantly upregulated in the good prognosis group, and that of miR-520a-3p (0.895 ± 0.373 vs. 0.701 ± 0.335) was significantly downregulated (see Supplementary Fig. 2). The expression levels of miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p in human cervical epithelial cells (CRL2614) and cervical carcinoma cell lines (SiHa, C33a, HeLa, CasKi, MS751, ME180, and AV3) were assessed. Compared to CRL2614 cells, miR-378 (0.019 ± 0.005 vs. 0.037 ± 0.014) and miR-20a (0.024 ± 0.005 vs. 0.043 ± 0.012) were remarkably upregulated, and miR-520a-3p (0.326 ± 0.086 vs. 0.119 ± 0.034) was significantly downregulated in C33a cells (Fig. 1c). Based on these findings, we selected the C33a cell line (which endogenously expresses high levels of miR-378 and miR-20a but low levels of miR-520a-3p) as the experimental model for subsequent studies. Our results showed that miR-378 and miR-20a were upregulated, and miR-520a-3p was downregulated in tissues and serum of cervical cancer patients with a poor prognosis. Our subsequent research focused on these three candidate miRNAs.

The effect of miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p on malignant phenotypes in C33a cells

To evaluate the importance of three candidate miRNAs in cervical cancer progression, we first examined their expression efficiency after treatment with mimics or inhibitors of candidate miRNAs. As shown in Fig. 2a, compared with the mimic control group, miR-378 mimic significantly upregulated miR-378 expression (0.079 ± 0.018 vs. 0.031 ± 0.005); miR-20a mimic markedly increased miR-20a levels (0.072 ± 0.012 vs. 0.040 ± 0.006); miR-520a-3p mimic substantially elevated miR-520a-3p expression (0.671 ± 0.132 vs. 0.144 ± 0.065). Compared with the inhibitor control group, miR-378 inhibitor significantly downregulated miR-378 expression (0.007 ± 0.004 vs. 0.028 ± 0.005); miR-20a inhibitor substantially reduced miR-20a levels (0.009 ± 0.003 vs. 0.039 ± 0.005); miR-520a-3p inhibitor dramatically suppressed miR-520a-3p expression (0.031 ± 0.014 vs. 0.146 ± 0.062). Functional experimental results demonstrated that the viability of C33a cells treated with miR-378 (73.447 ± 12.726 vs. 43.632 ± 7.272) or miR-20a (63.448 ± 9.090 vs. 38.178 ± 3.636) mimics was significantly higher than in the corresponding mimics control treatments, while the viability after treatment with miR-378 (24.816 ± 5.454 vs. 38.178 ± 7.272) or miR-20a inhibitors (20.907 ± 6.454 vs. 41.723 ± 4.457) was significantly lower than in the corresponding inhibitor control treatments (P < 0.01). The viability of C33a cells in the miR-520a-3p mimic (20.726 ± 6.454 vs. 42.637 ± 10.908) and inhibitor groups (68.902 ± 13.544 vs. 45.268 ± 8.577) showed the reverse outcomes to those of miR-378 and miR-20a (P < 0.01). The Transwell and scratch assays indicated that compared with the corresponding mimics control treatment, the miR-378 (125.375 ± 17.251 vs. 101.319 ± 2.87, 71.736 ± 10.003 vs. 51.444 ± 5.287) and miR-20a mimic (145.600 ± 17.700 vs. 101.775 ± 2.85, 72.450 ± 10.003 vs. 48.586 ± 5.287) treatments significantly increased the number of invasive cells and enhanced the cell migration distance, and the opposite was noted in the inhibitor groups (48.550 ± 10.710 vs. 99.319 ± 3.12, 43.125 ± 5.900 vs. 61.700 ± 6.950, 18.441 ± 6.716 vs. 100.018 ± 2.719, 33.582 ± 5.287 vs. 45.299 ± 5.289, all P < 0.01). miR-520a-3p mimic (49.875 ± 13.900 vs. 100.025 ± 6.450, 53.873 ± 6.945 vs. 69.592 ± 10.003) and inhibitor treatments (203.200 ± 26.650 vs. 102.011 ± 5.450, 80.024 ± 12.147 vs. 44.870 ± 6.716) showed the reverse outcomes to those of miR-378 and miR-20a (P < 0.01; Figs. 2c and d). Finally, the cell apoptosis rates in the miR-378 (1.728 ± 0.814 vs. 3.984 ± 1.719) or miR-20a mimic (0.811 ± 0.803 vs. 3.568 ± 1.525) treatments were significantly lower than in the corresponding mimic control treatments (P < 0.01), and the apoptosis rates in the miR-378 (23.205 ± 5.355 vs. 3.927 ± 1.126) and miR-20a inhibitor (25.233 ± 6.311 vs. 4.841 ± 1.782) groups were significantly higher than in the corresponding inhibitor control groups (P < 0.01; Fig. 2e). Treatment with miR-520a-3p mimics (22.420 ± 4.284 vs. 4.288 ± 2.341) and inhibitors (1.427 ± 0.711 vs. 3.971 ± 1.425) showed the reverse outcomes to those of miR-378 and miR-20a (P < 0.01).

The correlation between miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p expression and clinicopathologic parameters

We assessed whether miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p could pinpoint specific clinicopathologic parameter signatures related to cervical cancer prognosis. Tables 1, 2 and 3 present the association between miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p levels and the clinicopathologic characteristics of patients with cervical cancer. Chi-squared tests indicated that miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p correlated significantly with the TNM stage, differentiation grade, depth of invasion, and metastasis. These findings indicated that higher miR-378 and miR-20a and lower miR-520a-3p were associated with TNM stage Ⅱa, low differentiation, depth of invasion ≥ 2/3, and lymph node metastasis. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis demonstrated that differentiation [odds ratio (OR), 2.224; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.087–4.552], lymph node metastases [odds ratio (OR), 2.215; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.089–4.506], miR-378 [odds ratio (OR), 2.085; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.019–4.264], miR-20a (OR, 2.368; 95% CI, 1.162–4.827), and miR-520a-3p (OR, 0.423; 95% CI, 0.207–0.862) were independent predictors of cervical cancer prognosis (Table 4). Our results suggested that miR-520a-3p had a significant protective effect against cervical cancer, whereas miR-378 and miR-20a promoted it.

The prognostic value of miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p in cervical cancer

To further investigate the potential utility of the three candidate miRNAs as serum prognostic biomarkers for cervical cancer, we performed survival analysis in association with miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p in the good and poor prognosis groups. Lower serum miR-378 and miR-20a and higher serum miR-520a-3p were associated with a higher 3-year PFS rate (Fig. 3a), indicating that they were closely related to cervical cancer prognosis. Correlation analysis with the tumor markers showed that miR-378 and miR-20a were positively associated with SCC-Ag (r = 0.687 and 0.677; P < 0.001) and CEA (r = 0.671 and 0.667; P < 0.001), while miR-520a-3p was negatively associated with SCC-Ag (r = − 0.692, P < 0.001) and CEA (r = − 0.655, P < 0.001; Fig. 3b). ROC analysis for the prognostic value of the three miRNAs resulted in the following AUC values: 0.616 for miR-378 (95% CI, 0.526–0.701), 0.612 for miR-20a (95% CI, 0.521–0.697), and 0.717 for miR-520a-3p (95% CI, 0.631–0.794; Fig. 3c; Table 5). It was evident from the ROC curve analysis that a combination of the three miRNAs exhibited the best prognostic potential with an AUC of 0.907 (95% CI, 0.842–0.951), specificity of 89.2%, and sensitivity of 90.3%. The Youden index was used to calculate the optimal cut-off values of the three miRNAs for differentiating between good and poor prognoses.

Discussion

Cervical cancer, one of the most commonly diagnosed cancers, is associated with an unfavorable prognosis. Novel, highly sensitive, and specific biomarkers are crucial for this disease20. Circulating miRNAs could serve as non-invasive diagnostic and/or prognostic biomarkers for cervical cancer by delivering important clinical information throughout the disease process21. The present study found that the assessment of circulating miRNAs could be a reliable strategy for evaluating the prognosis of cervical cancer. The advantages of circulating miRNAs as prognostic biomarkers are in their stability, reproducibility, and consistency, and in overcoming the disadvantage of tissue-specific miRNAs, which are detectable only in tissues22. We performed a comprehensive analysis of the large-scale miRNA expression and clinical data in cervical cancer from the GEO2R database, successfully identifying the differentially expressed miR-378, miR-20a, miR-520a-3p, and miR-155-5p between cervical cancer tissues of patients with good prognosis and those with poor prognosis. Further assessment found that miR-378 and miR-20a were significantly upregulated, and miR-520a-3p was notably downregulated in the serum of patients with a poor prognosis.

These aberrant miRNAs are important in driving tumorigenesis and tumor progression by affecting crucial cellular processes such as cell viability, angiogenesis, apoptosis, and metastasis23. It has been reported that miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p have pleiotropic effects in various cancer types. For example, miR-378 functions as an oncogenic miRNA in some cancers, including cervical24, luminal breast25, and non-small cell lung26 cancers, whereas it functions as a tumor suppressor in colon cancer27, oral squamous cell carcinoma28, and glioblastoma multiforme29. Functional assays indicated that enhanced miR-378 expression stimulated the development of hepatic inflammation and fibrosis by mediating the NF-κB/TNFα axis30. miRNA-378 promotes the migration and invasion of cervical cancer by targeting the autophagy-related protein 1231. miR-20a is an oncogenic miRNA in prostate32, colorectal33, and cervical34 cancers, while it acts as a tumor suppressor in breast cancer35, endometrial cancer36, and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma37. The depletion of miR-20a inhibited the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells and promoted apoptosis by targeting Foxj233. Analysis of miRNAs in cancer pathways found that miR-520a-3p provided a crucial node in cancer occurrence and development. miR-520a-3p is a prominent tumor suppressor, overexpression of miR-520a-3p inhibited cell proliferation in acute myeloid leukemia and induced apoptosis by inhibiting MUC1 expression38. miR-520a-3p inhibits invasion and metastasis by targeting the NF-B signaling pathway in non-small cell lung cancer39. miR-520a-3p also suppresses the proliferation of cervical carcinoma cells by targeting CXC motif chemokine Ligand 840. Consistent with previous studies, our study indicated that miR-378 and miR-20a mimics promoted C33a cell proliferation, invasion, and migration and reduced the cell apoptosis rate, while miR-520a-3p mimics did the opposite. Notably, identical experimental outcomes were reproduced in HeLa cells (see Supplementary Fig. 1). Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis demonstrated that differentiation, lymph node metastases, miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p were independent prognostic predictors for cervical cancer. miR-520a-3p has a significant protective effect against cervical cancer, whereas miR-378 and miR-20a increase the risk for poor prognosis. Our results demonstrated that miR-378 and miR-20a were circulating oncogenic biomarkers, and miR-520a-3p was a circulating tumor suppressor in cervical cancer.

A panel consisting of miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p as prognostic biomarkers in cervical cancer could improve patient management through timely intervention and therapy decisions-making. This study showed that high expression levels of miR-378 and miR-20a and low expression levels of miR-520a-3p predicted poor PFS for patients with cervical cancer. A previous study demonstrated that miR-378 was associated with a shorter disease-free survival in triple-negative breast cancer and was related to a poor prognosis41. The expression of miR-378 is upregulated in ovarian cancer, and high miR-378 was associated with poor PFS42. Similarly, recent studies have shown that miR-20a plays an important role in cancer pathogenesis, promoting the initiation and progression of the carcinogenesis processes. The dysregulation of miR-20a expression was associated with gastrointestinal cancer initiation, metastasis, and poor prognosis43. A Kaplan-Meier plotter database indicated that high hsa-miR-20a expression was associated with a low overall survival rate in patients with endometrial cancer44. Emerging evidence indicated that miR-520a-3p was downregulated in epithelial ovarian cancer tissues and cell lines, acted as a tumor suppressor during the carcinogenesis process, and was a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target45. The confirmation of specific miRNAs featuring in cervical cancer brings great hope for their potential application as diagnostic and/or prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Further studies will elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms and facilitate the development of innovative methods in cervical cancer diagnosis and treatment.

ROC analysis demonstrated that serum levels of miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p had a prognostic value for cervical cancer, suggesting that these miRNAs hold promise as potential non-invasive diagnostic and/or prognostic biomarkers. Our findings indicated that high miR-378 and miR-20a, and low miR-520a-3p were associated with TNM stage Ⅱa, low differentiation, depth of invasion ≥ 2/3, and lymph node metastasis. Survival analysis revealed that the serum levels of miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p were correlated with cervical cancer prognosis. Pearson’s correlation analysis indicated that miR-378 and miR-20a were positively associated with SCC-Ag and CEA, while miR-520a-3p was negatively associated with them, suggesting their potential prognostic value. The combination of three miRNAs exhibited the best prognostic potential with an AUC of 0.907, specificity of 89.2%, and sensitivity of 90.3%. Meanwhile, the Youden index was used to calculate the optimal cut-off values of the three miRNAs for differentiating between good and poor prognoses. Circulating miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p could become attractive, minimally invasive biomarkers, providing real-time diagnostic, prognostic, and treatment response information. Circulating miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p cannot replace the gold standards but could serve as powerful, non-invasive auxiliary prognostic tools for cervical cancer.

Conclusion

The findings of this study support the potential utility of miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p as promising prognostic biomarkers of cervical cancer. However, further large-cohort validation studies in various populations are needed to verify the prognostic accuracy and clinical significance of these miRNAs. Functional studies are required to explore the molecular mechanisms through which these miRNAs regulate cervical cancer pathogenesis and elucidate their potential as treatment targets.

Materials and methods

Participants and sampling

Women who underwent primary tumor resection at the Hongqi Hospital Affiliated with Mudanjiang Medical University were recruited for this study from January 2019 to January 2021. Preoperative serum specimens and postoperative tumor tissues were obtained from 127 patients with cervical cancer, 62 with a good prognosis, and 65 patients with a poor prognosis. The inclusion criteria were: (1) cervical cancer as the first primary tumor; (2) cervical cancer diagnosed by preoperative biopsy or postoperative histopathology; (3) complete clinicopathological, demographic, and follow-up information. The exclusion criteria were: (1) underwent clinical therapy before collecting the serum samples; (2) had confirmed distant metastasis before surgery; (3) had other malignant tumors; (4) incomplete clinical data or interrupted follow-up. This study followed the ethical standards stipulated in the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and the Hongqi Hospital Affiliated with Mudanjiang Medical University approved this study (No. 2024028), and the patients signed informed consent before sample collection.

Follow-up

The included patients were followed up regularly for three years at 3-month intervals from the first day after surgery. Follow-up was terminated at the time of disease progression, recurrence, metastasis, or cervical cancer-related death. The follow-up information was collected through a medical history review and telephone interview until January 31, 2024. The following information was collected: (1) basic information, such as age, tumor diameter, TNM stage, differentiation grade, depth of invasion, metastasis, pathological type, and myometrial invasion; (2) follow-up information, including disease progression, time of recurrence or metastasis, location of metastasis, time of occurrence of a second or additional primary malignant tumor, time and cause of death, and last follow-up time. Patients with a stable postoperative condition, no recurrence, no metastasis, or no death were defined as having a good prognosis. Patients who experience disease progression, recurrence, metastasis, or cervical cancer-related death were defined as having a poor prognosis.

Grouping

We used the optimal cut-off values derived from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to divide the patients into high (≥ 0.132) and low (< 0.132) miR-378, high (≥ 0.075) and low (< 0.075) miR-20a, and high (≥ 0.810) and low (< 0.810) miR-520a-3p groups.

Cell culture

The human cervical epithelial cell line (CRL2614) and cervical carcinoma cell lines (SiHa, C33a, HeLa, CasKi, MS751, ME180, and AV3) were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection. The cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and 100 IU/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. All experiments were conducted with early-passage cells (passages 3–10).

Validation of MiRNAs by reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Tissues and serum samples were transferred to sterile tubes, and total RNA was extracted immediately using TRIzol reagent (Sigma Aldrich) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA yield and purity were measured by NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific), using samples with an A260/A280 absorption ratio greater than 1.8. The isolated RNA was reverse-transcribed using the Primescript Reverse Transcription kit and miScript SYBR Green PCR kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). Using U6 as an internal reference gene, the levels of miR-378, miR-20a, miR-95-3p, miR-330-3p, miR-520a-3p, miR-155-5p, miR-651-3p, and miR-331-3p in the tissue and serum samples were measured. The following reaction procedure was used: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min. The relative amounts of the miRNAs were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences for the miRNAs and U6 are listed in Table 6.

miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p lentivirus construction and transfection

The lentivirus gene transfer vectors carrying the miR-378 (Gene ID: 100314258), miR-20a (Gene ID: 777935), and miR-520a-3p precursor (Gene ID: 574467) and encoding green fluorescence protein (GFP) were constructed by GenePharma Corporation (Shanghai, China). Synthetic miRNAs mimics, mimic controls, inhibitors, and inhibitor controls for miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p were purchased from GenePharma. The vector transfections were conducted using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded in six-well plates and grown to 70 ~ 80% confluency. The cells were transfected and then cultured in a complete medium for 4 h. The final miRNA mimic concentration was 50 nM.

Cell viability assay

The cell viability was assessed by the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Laboratories, Inc.). Briefly, after undergoing the relevant treatment for 48 h, cells (5 × 103 cells/mL) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated for another 48 h. Subsequently, 10 µL CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek Instrument Inc.).

Cell invasion assay

The invasion of cells was assessed using Transwell chambers (6-well plates, 8-µm pore size; Corning Incorporated). The chambers were pre-coated by placing 100 µL Matrigel (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) into them. After undergoing the relevant treatment for 48 h, starved cells (5 × 105 cells/mL) were added into the upper chamber, while 500 µL of DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS was added into the lower chamber. After 36 h, the invasive cells were fixed with 70% methanol for 30 min at ambient temperature and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The number of invading cells was counted under a light microscope (magnification, ×200; Olympus Corporation).

Cell migration assay

The migratory ability of C33a cells was evaluated using the wound-healing assays. After undergoing the relevant treatment for 48 h, the C33a cells were grown to 80%–90% confluency in 6-well plates. The cell monolayer was scratched in a straight line (time point 0). The scratch width was photographed under a light microscope (magnification, ×200; Olympus Corporation) at time 0 and after 48 h.

Cell apoptosis assay

The C33a cell apoptosis was measured using the Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). After undergoing the relevant treatment for 48 h, cells (5 × 103 cells/mL) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated for another 48 h. C33a cells (5 × 105 cells/mL) were harvested and incubated with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of PI for 20 min in the dark at ambient temperature. The rate of apoptotic cells was calculated using a FacsCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and the data were analyzed using CellQuest, Version 3.3 (BD Biosciences) software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative results are expressed as means ± standard deviations and were compared by unpaired Student’s t-test (two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (three or more groups). ROC curve analysis and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) were used to assess the prognostic predictive value of miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p for patients with cervical cancer. The optimal cut-off values were determined from the ROC curves using Youden’s index. The Kaplan-Meier curves were used to analyze the patient’s progression free survival (PFS). Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression were used to identify independent risk factors associated with cervical cancer prognosis. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between miR-378, miR-20a, and miR-520a-3p and squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC-Ag) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
