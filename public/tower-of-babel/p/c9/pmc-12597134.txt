A quantitative and qualitative assessment of differential privacy’s ability to support collaborative research using a real-world data analysis
Authors: David P Gieser, Ashna R Arya, Rebecca Lee Smith, John A Vozenilek, Vishwanath Raman, Jonathan A Handler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSharing clinical data for research that is both collaborative and privacy-preserving remains a challenge. Differential privacy (DP) offers a solution by introducing noise to query results. Using the PrivateSQL DP platform, this study assesses the resulting utility of differentially private data at different levels of aggregation through analyses of COVID-19 pandemic associations with new cancer diagnosis counts (NCCs).Materials and MethodsData from a multi-hospital system for adult (18–90 years) encounters from 2019-2021 with apparently new cancer diagnoses were extracted, then queried using standard SQL (“original”) and DP, each with 1-week and 4-week aggregations. Analyses on the 4 datasets included NCCs by year and multivariate regression models of associations between COVID-19 positivity rates (by county) and change in NCCs between pre- and post-COVID-19 start.ResultsNCCs dropped in 2020, rebounding in 2021. This same pattern was demonstrated in the 4-week, but not the 1-week, DP dataset. Confidence intervals were substantially narrower in regressions using original datasets compared to those using DP datasets, and narrower in DP dataset regressions using 4-week rather than 1-week aggregation. Post-hoc sensitivity analyses found significant associations with 2 variables of interest on the original datasets (though these have methodologic limitations), but not the DP datasets.DiscussionDP reduces analytic accuracy to protect data privacy, but aggregation mitigated this tradeoff. Strategies for using DP in healthcare research and potential opportunities to enhance the DP platform were identified.ConclusionDP platform enhancements for hypothesis-driven medical studies may expand DP’s ability to support fruitful, cross-institutional research collaborations.Keywords: differential privacy, cancer, COVID-19, collaborative research

Introduction

Clinical research commonly requires the use of clinical data. Researchers may wish to collaborate with or across healthcare systems to execute their research. However, health systems may be reluctant to share data with researchers due to the need to protect confidential information and patient privacy. Several solutions have been used to address this problem, such as aggregation, data use agreements, deidentification, synthetic data, k-anonymity, federated models, and other solutions.1–3 Although aggregated results are commonly treated as de-identified,3–5 it may be possible in some instances to re-identify individual patients from aggregate results.

Differential privacy (DP) provides another mechanism to protect confidentiality and privacy by adding noise to returned results of (typically aggregate) queries.6 Based on the number of queries and magnitude of allowed re-identification risk (aka “privacy budget”), DP identifies the “noise” to be added to results by sampling from a suitable probability distribution parameterized by query sensitivity and per-query privacy budget. “Query sensitivity” is determined by the aggregate functions used in the query and the width of the allowed values of non-categorical, numeric variables returned by the query through aggregate functions.7

Typically, DP “noisy result ranges,” which correspond to the width of the noise sampling distribution, will be wider for smaller datasets and narrower for larger datasets relative to the true statistic. For each returned row, a randomly selected number from the DP noise distribution is added to the result before it is returned to the user. The difference between the returned results and the actual results may vary, which can affect the usability of the resulting data, but the likelihood of leaking private information can be mathematically very small with an appropriately configured privacy budget.8,9 Commonly, DP results composed of many aggregated data points will be closer to the actual value than results composed of fewer data points.10

The COVID-19 pandemic had a significant impact on both healthcare accessibility and willingness of the public to engage with the healthcare system.11,12 We sought to assess the use of DP in a study of real-world data, in this case, to assess whether associations exist between the COVID-19 pandemic and the number of new cancer diagnoses at a health system. The primary aim of this work was to quantitatively describe differences between results from the same study performed with and without DP, at different levels of aggregation and with different types of analyses. The secondary aim was to qualitatively identify potential opportunities to enhance the applicability and utility of DP in real-world clinical research.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by OSF Research Administration and the University of Illinois College of Medicine Peoria Institutional Review Board.

This was a retrospective, exploratory study of data from a US Midwest, multi-hospital healthcare system. Included encounters were with adult patients (18-90 years), were between 2019 and 2021, had an encounter-associated home address in Illinois mappable to geographic metadata (eg, county, RUCA code13) had characteristics felt consistent with an encounter in which a new cancer may be diagnosed, and had a diagnosis of cancer in either their problem list or as an encounter diagnosis having characteristics suggesting it as new. Data from certain care locations acquired during the study period were excluded. A patient could be in the dataset more than once if given multiple new ICD-10 cancer diagnoses during the included period. These data were joined with publicly available datasets to create the queries used to generate the study datasets.14,15

This study used Oasis Labs’ PrivateSQL,16 a DP platform based on the Chorus DP library that provides epsilon-DP.7 By using a “query rewriting” function that adds noise to returned results using the Laplace mechanism,17,18 all computation on private data happens locally, never leaving the source database. This project’s PrivateSQL output was assessed as de-identified using the HIPAA expert determination method.

Python software scripts executed the queries and generated the datasets. Datasets were processed to generate demographic summary counts in R 4.3.0 using tidyverse19 and regression models were fit using the glm function. Aggregation by year analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel.

To explore the effect of aggregation on results, queries generated datasets at 2 levels of aggregation (by week and by 4-week intervals). Both standard SQL and DP queries were run on PostgreSQL for the 2 levels of aggregation, resulting in 4 datasets: 1-week grouped original dataset (“O1”), 4-week grouped original dataset (“O4”), 1-week grouped DP dataset (“DP1”), and 4-week grouped dataset (“DP4”).

Datasets consisted of the count of encounters with new cancer diagnoses (as defined above) for each unique combination of the following values (ensuring the dataset also contained rows with 0 counts): home county; home address RUCA code group (categorized by values 1-3 and 4-10); age in years (categorized as 18-29, 30-49, 50-79, and 80+); health insurance category (grouped as Medicare or Medicaid, commercial, self-pay, and other); calendar year; either week number or the 4-week interval number of the year. MMWR week number20 was used for rows aggregated by week, and MMWR weeks 1-4 comprise interval #1, 5-8 comprise #2, etc. was used for rows aggregated by 4-weeks.

The datasets included the above data elements and the COVID-19 positivity rate for the relevant county and interval (count of new reported COVID-19 cases during the period divided by the county’s estimated 2020 population). Positivity rate was the independent variable of interest for the primary analysis regressions, intended to represent the “gestalt” COVID-19 burden citizens might perceive in their community (potentially affecting their willingness and/or ability to engage with the health system).

To explore the use of DP for different types of analysis, 2 types of primary analyses were performed on each dataset: descriptive statistical analyses using counts summed by year (“yearly counts”), and hypothesis testing analyses using multivariate regressions. It was presumed that yearly counts would be less affected by random DP noise than regressions due to greater aggregation with the yearly counts, and that the 4-week aggregated dataset results would be less affected by DP noise than the corresponding 1-week results. In addition to the sum of counts by year, count averages and standard deviations were calculated to determine coefficients of variation (CV). Any interval not completely included in all 3 study years was excluded to equalize the number of days comprising each year’s counts.

Multivariate regression models were built with the dependent variable being the difference between case counts prior May 2020 (defined as pre-COVID-19) and case counts in the corresponding time interval after May 2020 (post-COVID-19 start, or “post-COVID-19”); a positive value relates to fewer cases post-COVID, while a negative value relates to more cases post-COVID-19. The primary analysis’ independent variable of interest was the COVID-19 positivity rate (null hypothesis of no association between positivity rate and the count of cancer cases during an interval), with RUCA code group, age in years group, and insurance group included in the model as potential confounders.

In sum, this study was designed for analyses at 4 levels of aggregation (least to most): (1) multivariate regression on the 1-week aggregated dataset, (2) multivariate regression on the 4-week aggregated datasets, (3) aggregates by year on the 1-week aggregated datasets, and (4) aggregates by year on the 4-week aggregated datasets.

DP requires data hosts to set per-query and total privacy budgets. Greater noise is added with lower per-query budgets, increasing privacy but diminishing potential result validity. After consultation with the PrivateSQL team, the total budget was set to 2, similar to or less than some other published budgets for sensitive data.21–24 It also is double what might actually have been used had the primary purpose of this work not been to study DP itself, since 2 datasets were generated instead of one for that purpose. With that said, researchers might generate datasets at multiple granularities if the impact of added noise on result accuracy cannot be adequately determined or addressed in advance. Initial test queries were run with the per-query budget set extremely low to consume minimal total budget (0.001 or less). Then the per-query budget was adjusted up with the intent of consuming virtually all of the remaining budget, split equally among the 2 DP queries.

Qualitative results

Privacy budget-related

Determining appropriate per-query and total privacy budgets proved challenging and required expert consultation. Allocating excess budget for unanticipated needs decreases the accuracy of results for known needs. However, additional budget may be required if new needs or errors are discovered after the budget has been consumed. Adding budget adds privacy risk, and the platform had minimal ability to guide budget decision-making. Therefore, greater upfront planning was required, and some follow-up exploration could not be pursued.

“Test runs” of the DP queries were desired, but each DP query consumes budget. PrivateSQL provides synthetic data for query testing without consuming budget, but the investigators felt that DP queries on the actual data would provide the most reliable test. This was accomplished by setting a very low per-query budget for tests, then adjusting it upward for the final queries. Only the system administrator could make these budget changes. Though not burdensome for this study, this effort could be substantial at scale.

Configuration- and SQL-related

The DP platform provides a rich set of configurations that must be properly configured to protect privacy. Some configurations proved more complex. For example, each categorical field used in a SQL GROUP BY clause must have all its possible values defined in a “domain set.” This can be time-consuming, and the platform may return incomplete results if the domain sets are incomplete. The platform team recommends providing all possible values of a field, even if they are not present in the dataset (so private information cannot be inferred by the absence of data). Therefore, a query to select all unique values of a field in the source dataset may not always suffice.

DP configuration also requires setting “clipping boundaries” for non-categorical, numeric variables. Anything outside the range is given the value at the relevant clipping boundary. PrivateSQL auto-generates 5% and 95% clipping boundaries based on a field’s data distribution, and that can be overridden (eg, if outliers are important). However, when the value is set too widely, DP will add unnecessary noise; setting it too narrowly increases the amount of data that is clipped (changed to the minimum or maximum boundary value), leading to information loss.

Using the domain sets, PrivateSQL automatically adds left outer joins to the submitted query so that every possible combination of values among all categorical fields used in a query’s GROUP BY clauses is processed. This expands the size of the dataset, which can lead to long query times, query timeouts, and very large result sets. Depending on the query and domain sets, a dataset that would ordinarily return only a few rows could be expanded to millions or more rows. Although a SQL HAVING clause can filter out the undesired rows, a very large interim dataset may still be produced, affecting compute costs and query times. This study’s analysis required nearly every combination of the GROUP BY field values anyway (to generate the “zero count” rows), so PrivateSQL’s dataset expansion largely mirrored the expansion already performed by the submitted query. The impact of PrivateSQL’s row expansion may differ for other studies.

DP mainly supports aggregate queries (eg, returning counts, averages, etc.), and PrivateSQL’s platform had additional limitations in the aggregations and SQL syntax it could support at the time of study execution, and some SQL operations require special handling, such as CASE statements.

The original plan was for 2 investigator teams (original data and DP teams) to work more fully independently. PrivateSQL had a rudimentary synthetic data approach to facilitate query development (each executed query generates a different set of results). However, developing and validating the complex queries without access to the original data seemed difficult or impossible. Therefore, the queries were developed by the original data team. The Python scripts to run those queries and store the results were developed separately by each team. The R scripts were originally developed by the team biostatistician with access to only PrivateSQL’s synthetically generated data. The original data team then made minor modifications (eg, fix bugs, fix runtime errors, make minor enhancements).

Discussion

As others have shown,26,27 this study found that new cancer diagnosis counts at a multi-hospital healthcare system substantially dropped in 2020, nearly returning to 2019 levels in 2021. This general pattern was also observed with DP4 results. A very different pattern was observed with DP1 results, presumably because of the lesser aggregation and relatively greater ratio of noise to value in each row.7,28 The impact of noise at different levels of aggregation was demonstrated in the CVs of the yearly counts, for which DP4 results were substantially lower than those of DP1 (Figure 1).

Figure 1 and Table 1 results utilized counts generated by aggregating data from queries that had already performed some aggregation. Table 1 counts used more granular post-query aggregations than those of Figure 1. If Figure 1 and Table 1 counts had instead been generated by additional queries that performed all required aggregations and calculations within the query, and if the same per-query budget had been used, the magnitude of the added noise relative to each value returned by DP queries would have been much smaller, likely yielding results much closer to those of the original datasets. The per-query privacy budget could have been substantially lowered for those additional queries and still likely yielded results more closely adhering to those from the original datasets. If the DP4 query had been the only DP query performed for the regressions, nearly half the budget would have remained to run those additional queries.

Although some of the multivariable regressions found significant associations for variables of interest with the original data but not with the corresponding DP results, the reverse did not occur. DP’s addition of noise would make false conclusions of non-significance (Type 2 errors) seem more likely than false conclusions of significance (Type 1 errors). That may be preferable, since Type 1 errors are generally considered more concerning.29 Still, researchers seek to avoid either error type. Figure 2 and Table 2 demonstrate that the expected increased probability of Type 2 error with DP may be mitigated with greater dataset aggregation. Therefore, in the presence of a larger budget, a stronger effect, and/or with a less aggregated analysis (eg, using regions larger than county), it seems possible that the DP data analysis accuracy could approach that of the original data.

This study used epsilon-DP, or “pure” DP, the strongest form of DP.30 Zero-Concentrated Differential Privacy (zCDP), an “approximate” DP formulation that may mitigate some of the issues in generating domain sets, is now supported by PrivateSQL. zCDP was not available at the time of this study, and new research evaluating the privacy-sparing performance and resulting utility of approximate DP formulations in comparison to epsilon-DP for hypothesis-testing in the healthcare domain may be needed. This study’s findings may prove useful in informing the design and execution of that work.

The use of DP in clinical research is at a relatively early stage. A 2021 scoping review found only 1 of the 54 articles meeting inclusion criteria had “hypothesis testing” as its analytic purpose.9 A recent PubMed using the search ‘“Differential Privacy” “regression”’ returned only 13 results,31 and one searching “‘Differential Privacy’ ‘hypothesis’” returned only 6 results.32 Although a 2023 review noted a “surge in development of novel algorithms for DP medical analysis,”17 this assertion was not cited and we could not find a description of a search strategy or counts of search results to enable a quantitative measure of the magnitude of this surge. Most of the cited work related to medical data analysis seemed related to the use of DP for genomic data, machine learning, and/or data sharing in medical research. The use of DP for hypothesis testing in medical research may still be very limited, and our study adds to this early body of literature.

Although some training and education is needed to work with the DP platform, it greatly reduced the effort that otherwise would have been required for this study. Without this type of tooling, DP may be out of reach for most healthcare researchers.

DP inherently limits the types of analyses that can be performed, and it is more difficult to validate queries and analytics when data access is limited by differential privacy. However, some challenges in this work seemed related to the DP platform’s newness rather than to DP itself.

We identified several DP platform enhancements that, if feasible, may further address the needs of healthcare research:

A method to readily quantify the re-identification risk between DP using a particular set of budget and clipping boundary settings compared to the re-identification risk for other common scenarios (eg, use of HIPAA Safe Harbor de-identification) to facilitate configuration decision-making.

While the focus of the study was to explore the use of DP, and the analysis of COVID-19 and cancer counts was mainly intended to provide a vehicle for that exploration, some findings seemed notable. Between the year and the positivity rate, the year seemed to more consistently reflect an association of COVID-19 with cancer diagnosis counts, since (1) the yearly cancer counts demonstrated a substantial drop in 2020; (2) when year was added to the regression, results showed that 2020 was associated with a significant difference from 2019 cancer counts, while 2021 was not; and (3) the positivity rate became significant in all regressions for which the 2021 data was removed. Unexpectedly, a higher positivity rate was associated with a lesser, (not greater) difference in pre- vs post-COVID-19 cancer counts. This could be related to differing positivity rates at the time of scheduling the appointment vs the date of the appointment itself. An inverse relationship might be found between new cancer diagnoses and positivity rate if appointment scheduling is higher during a positivity rate nadir prior to a spike, with the appointments occurring during the ensuing spike, and vice versa. The associations in this study are limited by its retrospective, single health system nature using complex queries of a complicated dataset. For example, new cancer cases to the health system may not have been new for the patient, some data may have been inaccurately captured or incorrectly queried, some cases may have been “rule outs” not confirmed on follow-up, and others would have been missed because the diagnosis was made at another health system or because the cancer diagnosis was captured in a part of the EHR not queried for the dataset. There may also have been unmeasured covariates affecting results. Therefore, the preliminary COVID-19 associations found in this study may best be considered potentially fruitful areas of future research, especially since a recent PubMed search did not return a study similar to this one.35

Limitations

The assessment of DP was limited by the use of a single research topic applied to specific datasets using a particular methodology on a single platform. Different research topics, datasets, methodologies, budgets, configurations, and technologies might have had different results. With that said, this work adds to the early knowledge base on the use of DP for medical research, and provides real-world supporting data and lessons learned to inform future research and guide improvements in the state-of-the-art.

Conclusion

Differential privacy is a promising technology for reducing barriers to cross-institutional collaboration and the sharing of private data. DP seems a good fit for use cases requiring relatively simple aggregate queries of curated datasets, especially where the count of entities comprising each returned row is large. New tools, DP budgeting and querying “playbooks” for healthcare research (some of which were described here), and newer algorithms for performing regressions in a DP context may significantly expand the use cases to which DP can be fruitfully applied. A dedicated platform can significantly reduce the training, knowledge, and effort required for using DP in research. Several potential platform features were identified that may further reduce the effort required to use the DP platform in healthcare research and increase the breadth of potential use cases appropriate for the platform.
