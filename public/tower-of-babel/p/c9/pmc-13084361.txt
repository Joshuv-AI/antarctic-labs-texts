Bayesian Mendelian randomization methods for index trait bias correction in subsequent trait genome-wide association studies
Authors: Nimish Adhikari, Kathryn L. Lunetta, David R. Gagnon, Gina M. Peloso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIndex trait bias (also called index event bias) can occur in genetic studies due to conditioning on incident trait, which can bias genetic associations with subsequent traits. We propose the use of two Bayesian Mendelian randomization (MR) methods (Bayesian weighted MR [BWMR] and MR-HORSE) to correct index trait bias in genome-wide association studies (GWASs) of subsequent traits. We compare these Bayesian MR methods to previously proposed methods for index trait bias through a simulation study. We observe that BWMR has similar type I error compared to using an inverse variance weighted MR, weighted median MR, and Dudbridge but has an inflated type I error compared to SlopeHunter. MR-HORSE and SlopeHunter have similar type I errors for smaller correlations between incident and subsequent traits; however, MR-HORSE and SlopeHunter have better controlled type I error for a large negative correlation and large positive correlation, respectively. All methods have comparable power across correlations between incident and subsequent traits. We applied the methods to a GWAS of subsequent acute ischemic stroke (AIS) or 3-point major adverse cardiovascular event after an incident AIS event in the Million Veteran Program and for fasting insulin adjusted for body mass index, and we observed slight differences in the results between the correction methods. We observed that a single index trait bias correction method is not optimal across all scenarios; therefore, applying multiple methods and checking for consistency between the estimates could provide an approach to determine the presence of and correction for index trait bias.

Introduction

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) test for associations between an outcome and individual genetic markers across the genome. They are typically performed with the outcome being a quantitative trait or disease status. However, most current drugs focus on secondary prevention of disease.1,2 For GWASs to provide more robust evidence for potential therapeutic targets, it would be beneficial to align GWAS outcomes with the outcomes of typical clinical trials. GWASs of subsequent traits could be used to identify risk factors for disease progression in those who already have an incident trait and may lead to identification of novel therapeutic targets. However, subsequent trait studies are usually conditioned on incident status, which makes associations vulnerable to index trait bias.3,4,5

Index trait bias is a type of collider bias that is induced when conditioning on an outcome or another trait. If an outcome of interest has multiple risk factors, then conditioning on disease status or a risk factor can cause independent risk factors to become correlated, inducing spurious relationships between the outcome of interest and risk factors.3 For subsequent trait studies, the shared confounders between incident and subsequent traits act as risk factors associated with both outcomes, and selection of subjects by disease status can induce spurious or biased estimates of effect of a genotype on a subsequent trait (Figure 1). A famous example of this kind of bias is called the obesity paradox, where among people with cardiovascular diseases, those with higher body mass index (BMI) tend to have longer survival, which may occur due to lower shared risk factors among people with higher BMI who have coronary heart disease6; however, some have claimed that this may not be fully due to only incident-trait bias.7Figure 1Directed acyclic graph showing index trait biasHere, X is the incident trait, Y is the subsequent trait, G is the SNP, and U is a shared confounder between incident and subsequent trait. Current methods to account for index trait bias include adaptation of Mendelian randomization (MR) methods (Inverse variance weighted [MR-IVW]8 and weighted median [MR-WMED]),9 an adaptation of MR-EGGER10 outlined in Dudbridge et al.,3 which is referred to here as the Dudbridge method, as well as a Gaussian mixed-model clustering-based method called SlopeHunter.11 We propose two additional Bayesian MR methods: Bayesian weighted MR (BWMR) and MR with a horseshoe prior (MR-HORSE). These Bayesian methods assign a prior to the bias terms and provide robustness to some assumptions in correcting for index trait bias. BWMR has not been previously applied for index trait bias correction, but MR-HORSE has been used previously in simulations mimicking chronic kidney disease (CKD) outcomes.12 Here, we perform extensive simulations to compare these methods for index trait bias in GWASs. Additionally, we compare the use of these methods on publicly available GWASs of BMI from the GIANT consortium13 with fasting insulin (FI) adjusted for BMI from Mahajan et al.14 as well as acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and subsequent AIS or 3-point major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) after an incident AIS in the Million Veteran Program (MVP).

Subjects and methods

We first define our incident and subsequent traits, where incident trait is represented by X, subsequent trait is represented by Y, the single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) is represented by G, and U is a compound measure of polygenic and non-polygenic confounders, as defined by Dudbridge et al.3 We assume a linear relationship between incident and subsequent traits, without loss of generality,3 with any given SNP and set of confounders.(Equation 1)X=βGXG+βUXU+ϵX(Equation 2)Y=βGYG+βUYU+βXYX+ϵY.Here, βGX represents the effect of the SNP G on incident trait, and βGY represents the effect of the SNP on the subsequent trait controlling for the incident trait, ϵX and ϵY are errors for X and Y, respectively, Var(ϵX) = σX2, and Var(ϵY) = σY2. In practice, confounders may not be observed so we can estimate our effect of interest, which is the effect of subsequent trait conditional on the incident trait and the SNP.(Equation 3)E[Y|G,X]=βGY′G+βXY′XβGY′ is the biased effect of the SNP on subsequent traits and will be the estimand for any associations between SNP and subsequent traits.3 We assume without loss of generality that E(X) = E(Y) = E(ϵX) = E(ϵY) = 0; that G, U, ϵX, ϵY, are pairwise uncorrelated; and that the models have no interactions between X and Y.

Dudbridge et al.3 showed that using an asymptotic least-squares solution of Equations 1 and 2 that(Equation 4)βGY′=βGY+bβGX.

Dudbridge et al.,3 alongside all the methods discussed below, assume that b is approximately constant across all SNPs, since variation explained by individual SNPs is small in relation to overall genetic variation. Equation 4 then can be used to obtain the bias correction factor b, which can be used to obtain the unbiased estimate for βGY. MR methods

MR15,16 is a method used to identify causal effect of a risk factor on an outcome using instrumental variables (IVs), which are SNPs. MR is akin to a randomized controlled trial. Genotypes are randomly assigned at each generation, and the genotypes, which are unrelated to confounding factors, can be used as IVs, which can help eliminate the possibility of reverse causality.

MR methods focus on estimating the causal effect of an exposure X and an outcome Y, both with unknown confounders. For MR the following three criteria of valid IVs should be satisfied:1.Relevance: the IVs are associated with exposure2.Independence: the IVs are independent of the confounders3.Restriction: the IVs only affect the outcome Y through exposure X

MR methods have been used to account for index trait bias because of the similarities of the causal structure and the quantities to be measured for index trait bias.8 From Figure 1 and Equation 4, we see that the structure of index trait bias fits into the MR framework. MR methods have been used to estimate this bias; however, there are some important observations to consider when applying MR for index trait bias. Even though we leverage the framework of the MR methods, we do not infer a causal relationship of incident trait with subsequent trait. Instead, we assume that the effect estimated leveraging the MR framework is the biasing factor.8 Subsequent trait studies transform the exposure-outcome framework of traditional MR into focusing on incident-subsequent traits, where the effect of interest is the true effect on subsequent trait (βGY) instead of the causal effect of the exposure on the outcome. We still assume that all IVs are associated with the exposure—in other words, the incident trait—but we can include a larger set of non-valid IVs since the direct effect of SNP on outcome is not considered a nuisance parameter here, and non-zero values of βGY are expected.17 However, using a larger set of SNPs can mean that the weak incident effects can bias our estimate,3 so appropriate methods are needed to account for this bias.

We focus on applying MR-IVW and the MR-WMED9 approaches to index trait bias. Equations 1, 2, and 3 constitute the basis of MR methods, but for each SNP we also assume that the effect estimates are normally distributed:(Equation 5)βˆGX∼N(βGX,σX2)(Equation 6)β′ˆGY∼N(βGY′=βGY+bβGX,σY2).Here, the bias correction factor can be thought of as the SNP-subsequent trait effect through only the SNP-incident trait-subsequent trait pathway, whereas βGY is the direct effect of G on Y (which includes both pleiotropic and direct effect only on subsequent trait).

For the MR-IVW approach, we use ratio estimators for each SNP G, which are defined as:(Equation 7)bj=βˆGY′βˆGX(Equation 8)Var(bj)=βˆGXσY2.

The ratio estimate is a consistent estimate of b. Assuming no measurement error (σX2=0) and the direct effect is 0 (βGY = 0), for any N SNPs the IVW estimate is given by:(Equation 9)bIVW=∑j=1Nwjbjwj,wj=βˆGXσY2.In a traditional MR approach, βGY would be assumed to be 0, and if all MR assumptions are valid, then this estimate is a consistent estimator. However, in using these methods for index trait bias, we assume some violation of the restriction assumption and some non-zero βGY effect.9

For the MR-WMED approach, let S = {bˆ1,…,bˆN} be the set of all ratio estimators as defined in Equation 4. Then, we definesj=∑j=1kwj.

The weighted median estimator bWMED is the median of the distribution having bˆj as the pj=100∗(sj−wj2) percentile. For any other percentile values, we can extrapolate linearly between neighboring ratio estimates. The weighted median method is more robust to changes in assumptions. If the assumptions are valid for over 50% of the SNPs, then the estimator is consistent.

Like MR-IVW methods, the SNPs used in MR-WMED should follow the IV assumptions for traditional MR methods, where we use a set of independent SNPs through linkage disequilibrium clumping, which are associated with the incident trait using some arbitrary p value thresholding. However, we assume some violation of the restriction assumption and some non-zero βGY effect since these methods require some non-cases for the model to correctly weight the effects according to the model.8 These methods are not fully robust to violations of IV assumptions, and if there exist genetic correlations, then the variance of the ratio estimator will be underestimated, which will overestimate the weights, and bias will be overestimated.8

SlopeHunter

Dudbridge et al.3 calculated the bias correction b using an adaptation of MR-EGGER18 regression to progression studies, where the estimates can be approximated by the linear regression of βGY′ on βGX:βˆGY′=bˆ0∗+bˆ∗βˆGX.

The estimate bˆ∗ can be biased toward zero due to the weak incident trait effect described in a separate section below. After adjusting for regression dilution, we obtain the bias-corrected estimate:βˆGY=βˆGY′−bˆβˆGX,with standard error:σGY2+bˆ2σGX2+βˆGX2var(bˆ)+σGX2var(bˆ).

The subsequent effect estimates and standard error for all methods discussed in this paper, including MR-HORSE and BWMR, are estimated using these formulas.

SlopeHunter11 is a model-based clustering extension to the Dudbridge, where we assume that the SNPs can belong to one of four mutually exclusive clusters: (1) SNPs affecting incident trait, (2) SNPs affecting incident and subsequent trait, (3) SNPs affecting only subsequent traits, and (4) SNPs affecting either incident or subsequent trait. A Gaussian mixed-model clustering method is then used to estimate the bias correction b, which is then applied to all SNPs. SlopeHunter also requires the zero modal residual assumption (ZEMRA) to account for the fact that most of the SNP effects influence the subsequent trait through incident trait (Table 1). A threshold is used to limit SNPs that are associated with the first two clusters, and the bivariate Gaussian mixture model shown below is then used to identify the correction factor using the expectation-maximization algorithm, with standard errors calculated using bootstrap.(βˆGXβˆGY′)∼π1∗N((00),(sX2b1sX2b1sX2b12sX2))+(1−π1∗)N((00),(sX2b1sX2+σXYb1sX2+σXYb12sX2+σY2+2b1σXY)).Here, π1∗ represents the probability that the SNP G belongs to the first cluster, and sX is the estimated standard error for βˆGX.

Bayesian MR methods

The next set of methods use a Bayesian prior to estimate the bias factor for index trait bias. We will use a non-informative weighted prior with BWMR19 and a horseshoe prior called MR-HORSE.20 MR-HORSE has been recently used to account for bias in disease progression studies12; however, the simulation modeling choices were specific to a study of CKD. Here, we provide a more general simulation framework to establish performance of the method and its comparison against other index trait bias methods.

BWMR

For a BWMR,19 we still assume normally distributed effect estimates as in Equations 5 and 6, but we also assume that(Equation 10)βGY∼N(0,τ2),where τ2 is an unknown parameter to be estimated from the data. Then, combining Equations 1, 2, and 4, we can see thatE[Y|X,G]=βGYG+bβGXG+βUYU+βXYX+ϵY.

Here, as long as the direct effect βGY satisfies Equation 10, and βGY is independent for all the SNPs, we can relax the exclusion assumption for MR (IVs only affect the outcome Y through exposure X) since the non-zero variance τ2 accounts for the influence of weak direct effects of G on Y. This method is robust to instrument invalidity but still requires the assumption that instrument strength is independent of direct effect (called the InSIDE assumption19). Hence, combining Equations 5, 6, and 10 we obtain:(Equation 11)βˆGX|βGX∼N(βGX,σX2)β′ˆGY|βGY∼N(bβGX,σY2+τ2).Here, a prior can be assigned to βGX and a non-informative prior on b:βGX∼N(0,σ2),b∼N(0,σ02)

To deal with the robustness of Equation 10 to direct effects, we assign a weighting criterion to βGY, where wG = 0, if βGY deviates from the model in Equation 10 and wG = 1 otherwise. Hence, we also assume thatwG|π1∼Bernoulli(π1),π1∼Beta(a0,1).π1 is a hyperparameter that controls the weight for each SNP wG, which can be controlled by specifying a0 to account for the proportion of IVs that do not completely follow Equation 10, which are the SNPs that have a significantly larger than zero effect on subsequent trait Y. The algorithm for inference is described in the paper by Zhao et al.19 and is also provided in a downloadable format on GitHub and provided in web resources.

In practice we use a p value threshold to limit inclusion to SNPs only associated with the incident trait; however, there may be outliers in those SNPs where β′ˆGY is not zero. The weighting scheme used in BWMR should account for those weak effects and should also correct for the outliers in β′ˆGY where it is non-zero; however, in the presence of many outliers, the estimates will still be biased.

MR-HORSE

For MR-HORSE,20 we still assume normally distributed effect estimates as in Equations 8 and 9, and we can assign a joint normal prior on (βGX, βGY):(βGXβGY)∼N((μ0),(sX2ρGsXϕGτρGsXϕGτϕG2τ2)).

Then, the following weakly informative priors are used:μ∼N0,1,sX2∼N+0,1,b∼N0,1,where N+ is the half-normal distribution, bounded below at zero. Here, βGX represents the effect of the SNP G on incident trait, and βGY represents the effect of the SNP on the subsequent trait. This method also requires selection of SNPs by their effect on the incident trait. If the SNP G is a valid instrument, then the true value of βGY is zero, otherwise, the SNP has a direct unbiased effect and is accounted for by some non-zero βGY. The correlation between βGX and βGY for SNP G is modeled by the ρG parameter. In situations where we are working with mostly uncorrelated traits, we can use a parametrization for ρG = 2rG-1 where rG∼Beta(10,10), which would place most of the mass near zero.20 However, different priors can be used to account for other correlation structures between incident and subsequent trait.

For the final two parameters in the model, we use the priors:ϕG∼Cauchy+0,1,τ∼Cauchy+0,1where Cauchy+(0,1) is the half-Cauchy distribution. Under this parametrization, the marginal distribution of βGY is the horseshoe prior, which is a shrinkage prior that will shrink some of the βGY toward zero, while non-zero βGY will escape shrinkage, and the horseshoe prior is less likely to underestimate the value of non-zero βGY. The algorithm for inference is described in the paper by Grant and Burgess,20 and the function to run this algorithm is provided in a downloadable format on GitHub and provided in web resources.

MR-HORSE has a parameter to account for correlation between incident and subsequent traits; hence, it should be more robust to different values of correlation between incident and subsequent traits. Like BWMR, there is also some thresholding used to limit SNPs significant for incident trait, and the horseshoe prior on βGY accounts for specific outlier SNPs.

Correction for weak incident effect

The set of SNPs used to calculate bias using MR methods, which are adapted to subsequent trait studies, do not need to follow the MR restriction criterion, since the parameter of βGY is no longer a nuisance parameter but is required to be non-zero for better inference of the bias. However, using a larger set of SNPs means that a larger number of random errors are introduced into our inferences, which can bias the estimate toward the null, which in MR is called weak instrument bias and is equivalent to regression dilution.21,22 This bias occurs due to a range of weak incident effects, and we can use a bias correction method for a single predictor as described in Cai et al.17:b∗=bvar(βˆGX)varˆ(βGX).Here, b∗ is the bias-corrected estimate, and b is the original estimate. The Hedges-Olkin estimator23 provides a good estimate for the variance of βGX:varˆ(βGX)=var(βˆGX)−E(σGX2).

Simulations

We simulated data for 10,000 independent SNPs for 20,000 unrelated individuals. Both incident and subsequent traits were simulated as quantitative traits without loss of generality. We simulated the SNPs to follow the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium using a uniform distribution with a minor allele frequency in the range 0–0.49. The effect estimates were generated using normal distributions, and the overall heritability of both incident and subsequent trait was set to 0.5. Three scenarios were considered for the simulation. In the first scenario, the proportion of variance for the incident trait affected by the SNPs in G1 is 0.15. In the second scenario the proportion of variance for incident trait affected by the SNPs in G1 is 0.25, and for the third scenario this proportion of variance explained by G1 SNPs is 0.35. The simulation setup mirrors that used in SlopeHunter.11 A shared confounder with variance 0.4 was simulated for incident and subsequent traits for the main analyses, and an error term with variance 0.1 was added to each outcome. For SNPs that affect both incident and subsequent traits, the effect estimates were randomly generated using a multivariable normal distribution with zero mean, with genetic correlations between incident and subsequent traits, which took values of −0.9, −0.5, 0, 0.5, and 0.9. We assume for the purpose of the simulation that the expectations of incident and subsequent SNP effects are zero. A p value threshold of 0.001 was used to limit SNPs for both SlopeHunter and the MR methods.

Once the data were generated, association analysis for incident traits and subsequent traits were performed using a linear regression model using PLINK.24 Summary statistics obtained from these genetic analyses were used to apply the index-bias-correction methods, and the number of SNPs that were classified as significant, using 0.05 as the significance level, for incident trait only, subsequent trait only, both incident and subsequent trait, and neither trait was stored. A total of 1,000 simulations were performed, and type I error at alpha = 0.05 for the subsequent trait was calculated for the G1 cluster SNPs and G1+G4 cluster SNPs, and power was calculated using SNPs associated with subsequent traits (G2+G3).

We applied SlopeHunter, MR-EGGER, MR-IVW, MR-WMED, BWMR, and MR-HORSE to the summary statistics obtained from the simulated GWAS results and generated average type I error and power for the subsequent trait results across the 1,000 simulations.

We performed further secondary simulation scenarios where we increased the p value threshold for SNPs to be used for all the methods, using thresholds of 0.01, 0.1, and 0.2. We also simulated a scenario where a larger proportion of SNPs affected both incident and subsequent traits (9%), as well as a smaller proportion of SNPs affecting only incident traits (1%).

BMI and BMI-adjusted FI

We applied the same methods compared in the simulation to public GWAS results using the exome chip for FI (MIM: 606035) adjusted for BMI (MIM: 606641),14 to identify effects on FI through an independent path through BMI. The summary statistics for BMI13 in European (EUR) samples was downloaded from the GIANT consortium website, and 24,133 SNPs were harmonized between the two GWAS results. The SNPs were pruned using the 1000 Genomes EUR reference panel, with an R2 threshold of 0.1 and 250 SNP windows, which resulted in 5,606 SNPs for estimating the index trait correction factor b. A p value threshold of 0.001 was also used to limit the SNPs for SlopeHunter and MR analyses, and a genome-wide significance threshold of 5 × 10−8 was used to identify significant SNPs associated with FI, adjusting for BMI. The results obtained from SlopeHunter, BWMR, and MR-Horse were then compared.

MVP example

The index trait bias methods were applied to GWAS results from the MVP for incident AIS and subsequent AIS or 3-point MACE, which is a composite measure that includes myocardial infarction, AIS, and death.25 The goal of this analysis was to estimate the effect of SNPs on subsequent AIS and MACE accounting for index trait bias. Incident stroke was defined as any diagnosis of AIS or transient ischemic attack (TIA) using hospital-linked data. People who experienced their initial stroke more than 1 year prior to recruitment were excluded from our stroke phenotype. Specific International Classification of Diseases codes used for the MVP can be found in Elmore et al.25 Subsequent AIS/TIA was defined as any secondary diagnosis of AIS/TIA at least 90 days after the incident diagnosis, to avoid recoding of the primary outcome, and would be considered outcomes after the acute phase of an incident AIS/TIA. Ancestry-specific as well as across ancestry-fixed effects meta-analyzed GWASs were performed.

Each index trait bias correction method was applied to these results for both subsequent AIS and MACE, using a p value threshold of 0.001 for the selection of SNPs for the correction factor b. A genome-wide significance threshold of 5 × 10−8 was used to identify significant SNPs, and the results obtained from SlopeHunter, BWMR, and MR-Horse were compared.

Results

Overall, our simulation results show that the Bayesian MR methods can eliminate or minimize index trait bias in certain scenarios and can provide comparable performance to existing methods in other scenarios, as we outline in more detail below. BWMR provides comparable performance with respect to other MR methods, and MR-HORSE outperforms all the other methods in a set of scenarios where its assumptions are satisfied. Results have been provided for scenarios where the proportion of variance in outcome affected by the incident-only SNPs are 0.15 and 0.35 only; the results for 0.25 are similar and provided in Table S1.

Type I error

We define overall type I error as the ratio of all SNPs significant at a 0.05 level for the subsequent trait in groups G1 and G4 divided by the total number SNPs in G1 and G4. Across all SNPs, the mean type I error across 1,000 simulations for the unadjusted, SlopeHunter, Dudbridge, and MR methods are in the range of 0.03–0.07, with SlopeHunter and MR-HORSE in the lower range (Tables S2–S4).

Since the SNPs that directly affect the incident trait but not the subsequent trait are susceptible to index trait bias, we calculate the type I error for all SNPs significant at a 0.05 level in G1 divided by the total number of SNPs in G1 for the subsequent trait analysis. All the following results will focus on this type I error (Tables 2 and 3; Figure S1). Among the SNPs affecting the incident trait only (G1), the unadjusted estimate has inflated type I error. The type I error for SlopeHunter, BWMR, and MR-HORSE vary across simulation scenarios for correlations of −0.9 and 0.9; SlopeHunter has a type I error in the range of 0.045–0.279; BWMR has errors in the range of 0.049–0.471; and MR-HORSE has a type I error of 0.034–0.359, where the confidence intervals (CIs) for the type I error contain 0.05 dependent on the proportion of variance in outcome dictated by SNPs in G1. Discussion

For genetic studies of a subsequent trait, conditioning on the incident trait can introduce index trait bias due to shared risk factors between the incident and subsequent traits. We have introduced the use of two Bayesian MR methods to account for that bias and have compared those methods with other MR and non-MR methods through simulations. MR methods in this scenario do not infer causality since we assume that there is no causal effect between incident and subsequent trait, and the MR framework is leveraged to identify a biasing factor. By the nature of the phenotypes, for standard MR methods the data will be such that the MR assumptions are broken. Since our effect of interest is the effect of SNPs on the subsequent trait, we include SNPs in the MR calculation that have non-zero effects on both incident and subsequent traits, inducing horizontal pleiotropy by design.17

MR-HORSE provides performance comparable to that of SlopeHunter when the correlations between incident and subsequent traits are 0, −0.5, and 0.5, and it also has type I errors closer to 0.05 than SlopeHunter under larger negative genetic correlations, without a substantial loss in power. SlopeHunter outperforms all other methods in the presence of large positive correlations, where type I error is near the nominal level across simulation scenarios.

Depending on the correlations between incident and subsequent traits, and the proportion of variance in the incident trait explained by the SNPs in G1, we observe that the BWMR method is comparable in terms of both type I error and power to other MR methods and the Dudbridge approach, and that the weighted median method seems to have type I error closer to 0.05 and comparable power under low positive and negative genetic correlations compared with all the other MR methods except MR-HORSE. BWMR is not robust to violating the InSIDE assumption, but it may be robust to slight variations in correlation due to the τ parameter; however, when the correlation is large it provides an inflated type I error like the other MR methods.

We also observe that SlopeHunter and MR-HORSE both have 0.05 in the CIs for the simulation for genetic correlations of −0.5, 0, and 0.5. Both MR-HORSE and SlopeHunter do not require the InSIDE assumption. MR-HORSE uses the parameter ρG to quantify correlation between the incident and subsequent traits for each SNP. SlopeHunter assumes a correlation parameter in its likelihood for mixed-model clustering, which makes these methods more robust to different values of genetic correlations in the simulations, except for large positive and large negative correlations. SlopeHunter still requires the ZEMRA assumption, which governs the fact that most SNPs still affect outcome through incidence.

All the methods require some p value threshold parameter, with larger values allowing more SNPs, which inflates type I error for SlopeHunter and MR-HORSE in different scenarios. For high-positive or high-negative correlations between incident and subsequent traits, the proportion of variance affected by incident-only SNPs seems to indicate the change in type I error: negative correlations with small variance proportions led to type I errors closer to 0.05 for MR-HORSE, and larger proportion and large correlations led to type I errors closer to 0.05 for SlopeHunter. To find an appropriate value for the threshold, users should perform analyses at different thresholds to determine sensitivity to this parameter in the data.11

Power cannot be deemed accurate for scenarios where type I error is inflated. Incident causal SNPs are the main contributors of index trait bias since conditioning on incident trait provides a pathway to cause a biased estimate for the SNP-subsequent trait effect. Therefore, to account for the bias, our main concern is the type I error, and methods should look to minimize type I error first. Even still, we observe that the average power is slightly lower for all correction methods compared with the unadjusted method’s power across all simulation scenarios despite larger type I errors in most cases across the methods. There seems to be a slight dip in power for extreme correlations for both SlopeHunter and MR-HORSE, but the trends for the power seem to be similar for all methods.

BWMR has not been previously used to assess collider bias, but MR-HORSE has recently been used to assess and correct index trait bias.12 The simulation scenarios and use case in Donovan et al. are to assess kidney function decline in CKD cases, and the simulation modeling and data generation were done to mimic their kidney function study design by simulating a binary incidence phenotype and a smaller number of SNPs. They also used a different prior for MR-HORSE where ρG = 2rG-1 and rG∼Beta(3,1), which puts a weighting on higher positive correlation and is informed by their knowledge of CKD. We mimic the simulation scenarios presented in SlopeHunter11 and Dudbridge et al.,3 which follow a more general structure with fewer assumptions about the incident and subsequent event data-generating mechanism. The type I error for the simulation in Donovan et al.12 is less than 0.05, but the type I error is assessed for SNPs in groups G1 and G4, for which we have type I error closer to 0.05 (Tables S2–S4); this may be due to the larger number of SNPs in our primary simulation. We also make use of a threshold and regression dilution correction in MR methods to mimic real use case scenarios and apply them to both quantitative and binary phenotypes in our examples. MR-HORSE is comparable to SlopeHunter with respect to the various simulation scenarios tested here; however, MR-HORSE can leverage prior knowledge of traits to choose appropriate priors, which can influence the type I error rates.

Assigning a prior to the bias b parameter means treating it as a random variable. Previous methods assume that each SNP contributes the same amount of bias, and the bias factor b is a constant. However, the nature of index trait bias is such that depending on the relationship of any SNP with the shared unmeasured confounders, the bias can vary. Bayesian methods that assign a prior to the bias assume that the bias is not constant and randomly distributed (the normal distribution in BWMR and with the horseshoe distribution in MR-HORSE). This prior, in conjunction with the data at hand, is used to obtain a reasonable estimate for the random variable of bias, and as our priors become more accurate, so will the estimate of the bias approach the “true” bias. Currently, we are using non-informative priors, but appropriate priors can be used in conjunction with likelihoods that account for correlations and SNP causal clusters to obtain better estimates.

In our real data examples, we observe that the actual beta values seem not to change much by the correction factors; however, there was a difference in passing a genome-wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10−8) for both SNPs in the FI-adjusted BMI results and the three SNPs in the ancestry-specific GWAS for subsequent AIS and MACE in the MVP. The correction was also applied to SNPs with non-significant p values—none of them became significant after correction, which led us to believe that there was minimal evidence of index trait bias in the sample. For both these results, the SlopeHunter estimates have a larger difference, with the unadjusted beta estimated compared with MR methods, but the difference is not drastic. For the MVP results, all the effect estimates remain close to the unadjusted effect, and while some of the p values differ in significance among the methods, they all are close to the genome-wide significance threshold. This leads us to conclude minimal evidence of index trait bias affecting our outcome in this population for subsequent AIS and MACE. For the FI adjusting for BMI results, there is a difference in the significance of the results shown by MR-HORSE and the other methods, which could indicate index trait bias, and the data should be examined for genetic correlations between incident and subsequent trait as well as consider confounders that could affect both incident and subsequent trait that could contribute to these differences. However, it is important to note that there was minimal evidence of index trait bias in empirical examples, which limits our ability to demonstrate differential method performance in practice.

Bayesian MR methods have certain advantages with reference to other models. MR-HORSE and BWMR can use a larger threshold for p values since we want some effect of the SNP-subsequent trait to remain. These methods could be extended with different priors and likelihoods to more accurately portray the index trait bias framework, adapting methods like MR-MIX,26 which uses a mixture model parameterization for SNPs with appropriate Bayesian priors.

A challenge in subsequent trait studies is identification and quantification of index trait bias. Our comparison of methods indicated that there does not seem to be one method that appropriately accounts for index trait bias consistently across all scenarios. These methods are largely dependent on the genetic correlation between incident and subsequent trait and the proportion of variance in the outcome influenced by SNPs associated with incident trait. Large positive and large negative genetic correlations seem to inflate type I error and decrease power, but there seems to be some consistency among SlopeHunter and MR-HORSE for low-negative to low-positive genetic correlations without too much decrease in power. Hence for these scenarios, using and comparing results across bias-correction methods can provide some evidence of the presence of index trait bias. In the practical application of these methods for genetic analysis, we recommend obtaining an estimate of genetic correlation between incident and subsequent traits, applying several index trait bias-correction methods, and determining the similarity or differences in the corrected estimates. Large differences between corrected estimates, alongside the genetic correlation estimate could be an indication of index trait bias in the sample. Based on our findings in the simulation scenarios, if a sample is assumed to have index event bias, SlopeHunter and MR-HORSE methods would have more accurate estimates under moderate to zero correlation. Bayesian methods provide an alternate method to correct for index trait bias that may be more robust to the breaking of certain assumptions.
