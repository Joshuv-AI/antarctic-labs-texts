Prolonged ventricular fibrillation treated with nifekalant in fulminant myocarditis following late-onset coronavirus disease 2019
Authors: Satoko Ojima, Takuro Kubozono, Takeko Kawabata, Shin Kawasoe, Satoshi Yamaguchi, Yuito Enokida, Satoshi Mukai, Daisuke Kanda, Mitsuru Ohishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFulminant myocarditis (FM) is a life-threatening inflammatory cardiac disease that can rapidly progress to cardiogenic shock or fatal arrhythmias. Delayed-onset FM following coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection is rare and cases complicated by sustained ventricular fibrillation (VF) are exceptional.A previously healthy man in his late teens developed dyspnoea and complete atrioventricular block after COVID-19 infection. Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 30%. Despite veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and Impella CP support, he developed persistent VF lasting for five days, refractory to amiodarone and repeated defibrillation. Continuous intravenous infusion of nifekalant hydrochloride terminated VF and converted to sinus rhythm. Endomyocardial biopsy demonstrated CD68+ macrophage-predominant inflammation, consistent with COVID-19-associated myocarditis.This case suggests that nifekalant may be a valuable therapeutic option for managing life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias while preserving myocardial contractility in FM following COVID-19 infection.

Background

Fulminant myocarditis (FM) is a severe cardiac inflammatory disease characterised by a sudden onset and rapid clinical deterioration. It often leads to cardiogenic shock or fatal arrhythmias within days to weeks. Although recent advances in treatment, such as mechanical circulatory support, have significantly improved outcomes, FM remains potentially fatal, particularly when complicated by refractory ventricular arrhythmias. Persistent refractory arrhythmias can prevent myocardial recovery due to progressive necrosis, thereby further worsening the prognosis.

To date, there have been no published reports of sustained, life-threatening arrhythmias in delayed-onset FM following coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). In this case, ventricular fibrillation (VF) was refractory to amiodarone but was successfully terminated with nifekalant, suggesting its potential as an alternative treatment for cases unresponsive to conventional antiarrhythmic agents.

Case presentation

A man in his late teens initially presented with fever and tested positive for COVID-19 2 weeks prior. He developed dyspnoea at rest and sought care at a referring hospital. At the hospital, electrocardiography (ECG) demonstrated a complete atrioventricular (AV) block with a heart rate of 40 beats per minute, and echocardiography revealed a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 30%. Therefore, he was transferred to our hospital for further evaluation.

ECG at our facility demonstrated diffuse ST-T segment elevation in addition to complete AV block. Physical examination revealed a blood pressure of 106/63 mm Hg, normal body temperature, oxygen saturation within normal limits on room air and normal heart sounds without murmurs or bilateral rales.

Five minutes after arrival at our hospital, he experienced a generalised seizure associated with loss of consciousness. ECG monitoring revealed a 10 s pause during the episode. His blood pressure dropped to 69/44 mm Hg. He was immediately transferred to the cardiac catheterisation laboratory for initiation of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) and insertion of an Impella CP device.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed circumferential pericardial effusion around the right ventricle, diffuse myocardial oedema and myocardial hypertrophy. Further evaluation of myocardial injury biomarkers showed markedly elevated levels of high-sensitivity troponin I (32 716.3 ng/L; normal <34.2 ng/L), creatine phosphokinase (773 U/L) and creatine kinase-MB isoenzyme (76 U/L). Emergency coronary angiography revealed no evidence of coronary artery stenosis. Endomyocardial biopsy demonstrated scattered interstitial inflammatory cell infiltrates and focal myocyte injury, consistent with acute myocarditis. Immunohistochemical staining showed a predominance of CD68+ macrophages with relatively fewer CD3+ T-lymphocytes (figure 1).

Paired sera collected before and after the onset of illness showed increased antibody titres to echovirus 9 and 16. A nasopharyngeal swab tested negative for severe acute respiratory syndrome-coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) by reverse transcription PCR. Furthermore, the SARS-CoV-2 IgG antibody index was 0.89 (normal <1.40), while the SARS-CoV-2 spike IgG antibody level was substantially elevated at 14 500 U/mL (normal <50.0 AU/mL).

Differential diagnosis

The initial differential diagnoses included lymphocytic myocarditis unrelated to COVID-19, acute coronary syndrome, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, giant cell myocarditis and severe coronary vasospasm. Acute coronary syndrome and vasospasm were excluded by emergency coronary angiography, which revealed no stenosis or spasm. Takotsubo cardiomyopathy was unlikely because echocardiography revealed diffuse hypokinesis rather than regional wall motion abnormalities and myocardial biopsy showed inflammatory cell infiltration. Giant cell and lymphocytic myocarditis were also less likely as biopsy demonstrated CD68+macrophage predominance without multinucleated giant cells or CD3+ T-lymphocyte dominance. Collectively, these findings supported the diagnosis of FM related to COVID-19 infection.

Treatment

Given the patient’s worsening vital signs, VA-ECMO was promptly initiated. Despite the establishment of circulatory support with VA-ECMO, the patient subsequently developed asystole, as evidenced by the ECG. In addition, an Impella CP device was inserted to facilitate left ventricular unloading. The patient was endotracheally intubated and placed on mechanical ventilation for respiratory support.

On admission to the intensive care unit, the patient progressed from asystole to VF. Following intravenous administration of magnesium, VF transiently converted to ventricular tachycardia (VT), at which point an intravenous infusion of amiodarone was initiated. The patient briefly developed VT, but soon reverted to VF. Initial VA-ECMO support with a flow of 3.4 L/min and Impella CP support at 1 L/min maintained adequate cerebral and right upper limb perfusion. Venous oxygen saturation remained above 75%. However, the Impella CP alone was insufficient to maintain systemic perfusion during sustained VF. Early VA-ECMO weaning was considered unlikely, as end-organ perfusion remained dependent on ECMO support. Despite repeated defibrillation attempts, the sinus rhythm could not be restored. On the fifth day of persistent VF, a continuous infusion of nifekalant was initiated. Following this, defibrillation successfully converted the rhythm to sinus (figure 2). Thereafter, his cardiac function gradually improved, and he was weaned from VA-ECMO on the seventh day of hospitalisation and from the Impella CP on the tenth day. TTE performed 2 days after weaning from the Impella CP demonstrated recovery from dyskinesis, with normalisation of LVEF (68%). Left ventricular thickness improved to the normal range (interventricular septal thickness, 11.5 mm; posterior wall thickness, 12.6 mm) (figure 3). The patient was extubated from the ventilator on the thirteenth day and was discharged on hospital day 39.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient’s ejection fraction returned to normal following the restoration of sinus rhythm. However, normalisation of high-sensitivity troponin I levels (31.9 ng/L) required 20 days. Moreover, although the patient continued to exhibit first-degree AV block attributable to myocarditis, there was no progression to higher-degree block requiring permanent pacemaker implantation. Therefore, device implantation was avoided.

Discussion

Previous studies have reported various forms of myocardial involvement in COVID-19. Echocardiography in patients with COVID-19 has revealed myocardial dysfunction in up to 40% of cases, including both left and right ventricular systolic dysfunction.1 Moreover, classical myocarditis was identified in 7.2% of 277 cardiac autopsy cases among patients with COVID-19.2 FM complicated by VF has rarely been reported in the literature. Cases of COVID-19–associated myocarditis complicated by VF have been described.3 However, reports of FM with prolonged VF lasting several days remain extremely limited. While the incidence of myocarditis in COVID-19 is not negligible, the percentage of patients with delayed-onset myocarditis or FM following COVID-19 has not yet been clarified.

In the present case, the patient tested negative for SARS-CoV-2 by PCR. The anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG antibody index was within the normal range, whereas spike-specific IgG levels were markedly elevated. This serologic pattern typically suggests either prior natural infection or post-vaccination immune response. As the patient had not received any COVID-19 vaccines, the elevated spike IgG levels were considered indicative of prior infection. SARS-CoV-2 IgG titers generally peak after the second vaccine dose and decline to approximately one-third of their peak values within 3 months.4 In this case, the patient presented 6 months after his second vaccine dose, with a spike IgG level of 14 500 U/mL, significantly higher than expected for a post-vaccination response. Therefore, we attributed the antibody profile to prior natural infection. Furthermore, analysis by the National Institute of Infectious Diseases, Japan, found no detectable virus RNA in the myocardial tissue.

Myocardial biopsy with immunohistochemical staining revealed a significant predominance of CD68+ macrophages over CD3+ T lymphocytes. This immunophenotype contrasts with typical lymphocytic myocarditis, in which CD3+ cells are predominant, and is more consistent with COVID-19-associated myocarditis.5

Moreover, few reports have described sustained VF in COVID-19-associated myocarditis. In our case, VF persisted for 5 days despite prompt defibrillation and administration of amiodarone. The patient’s serum potassium remained above 4.5 mmol/L, excluding hypokalaemia. Persistent VF prevented weaning from ECPELLA (a combination of VA-ECMO and Impella) and raised concerns about myocardial injury due to repeated defibrillation.

Consequently, continuous infusion of nifekalant hydrochloride was administered. Nifekalant is a pure class III antiarrhythmic agent that selectively blocks the rapid component of the delayed rectifier potassium current (IKr) and has minimal negative inotropic effects.6 Nifekalant has also been shown to lower the defibrillation threshold compared with amiodarone, which has β-blocking properties. In the present case, severe myocardial inflammation and cytokine-mediated electrical instability may have contributed to the persistence of VF. In this context, cytokine-mediated myocardial dysfunction may contribute to VT/VF storms, and inflammatory cytokines have been reported to be associated with electrical storms.7 The proposed mechanism involves inflammatory cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor, interleukin-1 and interleukin-6, which can alter the function of gap junctions, outward potassium currents, and L-type Ca2+ channels in atrial and ventricular myocytes.8 These findings may help explain the effectiveness of nifekalant in terminating refractory ventricular fibrillation.

Furthermore, inflammatory cytokines increase reactive oxygen species and deplete ATP levels. Such inflammatory effects may involve multiple potassium currents, including IKr, thereby contributing to electrical instability. The process suppresses K+ channel activity and prolongs the action potential duration. As a result, early afterdepolarisations and delayed afterdepolarisations may develop, ultimately leading to VF.9 These electrophysiological disturbances may contribute to the persistence of ventricular fibrillation in severe myocarditis. In the present case, lidocaine was not selected because the arrhythmia was considered to reflect myocarditis-related electrical instability rather than an ischaemia-related ventricular arrhythmia. β-blocker therapy was also avoided because the patient had severe cardiogenic shock requiring VA-ECMO and Impella support, and additional haemodynamic deterioration was a concern.

Nifekalant further prolongs the action potential duration and refractory period by inhibiting IKr, thereby prolonging the QT interval and terminating VF. The prolonged refractory period resulting from K+ current inhibition may have prevented the re-induction of fibrillation after defibrillation shocks. In our case, these mechanisms likely contributed to the successful termination of VF. Patient’s perspective

I suddenly became short of breath and lost consciousness before arriving at the hospital. Later, I was told that my heart had stopped and that I had been supported by life-sustaining equipment for several days, which was a real shock to me. My family also told me that my condition had been extremely critical, and I am deeply grateful to the medical team for their prompt and dedicated care. I have realised how important it is to respond quickly when feeling unwell, and I would like to tell others in a similar situation to go to the hospital and get checked as soon as possible.
