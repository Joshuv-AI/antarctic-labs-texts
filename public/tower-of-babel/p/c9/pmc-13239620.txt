Balancing Risk and Hope: Insights Into Pregnancy Outcomes in Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension
Authors: Teresa Segura de la Cal, Carmen Pérez‐Olivares, Raquel Luna‐López, Irene Martín de Miguel, Alejandro Cruz‐Utrilla, Olga Villar, Fernando Sarnago, Pedro Muñoz, Marina Santiago Sánchez, María Jesús López Gude, Maite Velázquez, Laura Forcén, Juan F. Delgado, Fernando Arribas Ynsaurriaga, Pilar Escribano‐Subias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPregnancy poses substantial risks for women with Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension (PAH). This study aimed to evaluate maternal and neonatal outcomes in a contemporary PAH cohort under modern management, specifically investigating whether vasoreactive status warrants distinct clinical recommendations. We conducted a retrospective, longitudinal cohort study of PAH pregnancies managed at our institution between February 2010 and December 2023, comparing clinical characteristics, maternal complications, long‐term outcomes, and neonatal parameters between vasoreactive and non‐vasoreactive patients. Among 29 patients, 8 underwent pregnancy termination, and 21 proceeded with the pregnancy; 7 (33.3%) of whom were vasoreactive. Non‐vasoreactive patients experienced significantly higher rates of major complications (35.7% vs. 0%, p = 0.039) and minor complications (92.9% vs. 0%, p < 0.001). Complications in the non‐vasoreactive subgroup included prostacyclin initiation (78.6%), inotropic support (35.7%), mechanical circulatory support (14.3%), and one death (7.14%). All neonates born to non‐vasoreactive patients were delivered preterm and showed a trend toward higher complication rates (84.6% vs. 42.9%, p = 0.054). Over a median 5‐year follow‐up, two non‐vasoreactive patients died, whereas none of the vasoreactive patients lost their vasoreactive profile. In conclusion, despite advances in management, pregnancy in PAH remains associated with substantial maternal and neonatal morbidity. Vasoreactive patients, however, exhibit a distinctly favorable prognosis for both mother and offspring, which suggest the need for tailored recommendations for this subgroup.

Pregnancy, labor, and the postpartum period impose substantial physiological demands that can be difficult for women with Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension (PAH) to tolerate. The pregnancy‐induced hemodynamic adaptations necessary to meet the fetus's metabolic demands begin early. These changes include a progressive decline in peripheral vascular resistance, activation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system to expand blood volume, and increases in heart rate and stroke volume. Collectively, these adaptations lead to a 30%–50% increase in cardiac output (CO) above pre‐pregnancy levels by the 20th week of gestation [1, 2, 3, 4].

In PAH, the rigidity of the pulmonary arterial system prevents the vasodilation necessary to accommodate this increased CO. This can lead to a progressive rise in right ventricular afterload with severe consequences [2, 5]. Additionally, labor and the early postpartum period bring pronounced hemodynamic stress. Vaginal delivery poses significant risks due to uterine contractions and the resulting increase in CO caused by blood return from the contracting uterus and the elevated heart rates. For this reason, cesarean section (C‐section) is generally recommended for most PAH patients, particularly those with severe disease [2, 6, 7]. Although C‐section offers better control over delivery hemodynamics, it also carries an increased risk of blood loss and obstetric hemorrhage. The postpartum period introduces further challenges, including decompression of the inferior vena cava and uterine autotransfusion, both of which exacerbate the cardiovascular burden [2, 5]. Management of PAH during pregnancy is also complicated by the teratogenic potential of most PAH‐specific therapies, which significantly limits treatment options [8, 9].

Historically, the prohibitively high maternal morbidity and mortality associated with pregnancy in PAH led major guidelines to advocate for early termination of pregnancy (ETP) [8, 10, 11]. Recent years have brought forth publications with conflicting data regarding pregnancy‐associated risks. Some groups have suggested a reduction in the complication rate and reported promising outcomes in select patients. This has prompted proposals for a less rigid approach, especially for women with lower pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) or pulmonary pressures [10, 12, 13, 14]. Despite this evolving perspective, no specific recommendations are currently made that differentiate PAH subgroups. In particular, the management of patients who respond to calcium channel blockers (CCB responders) remains a critical gap. This subgroup may enjoy a less complicated pregnancy, consistent with the overall favorable clinical course of their disease [15, 16, 17].

This study aimed to evaluate maternal and neonatal outcomes in a contemporary cohort of PAH patients and to determine whether vasoreactive patients warrant individualized counseling and management.

Methods

Study Population

Patients diagnosed with PAH before or during pregnancy, per international guidelines prior to 2022 (mean pulmonary artery pressure ≥ 25 mmHg, PVR > 3 Wood units, and pulmonary artery wedge pressure ≤ 15 mmHg), were included. Diagnosis required right heart catheterization (RHC), except in post‐tricuspid Eisenmenger patients. Pregnancy was confirmed by an obstetric team. Patients with segmental pulmonary hypertension or single‐ventricle physiology were excluded.

All participants were enrolled in the REHAP registry (Spanish Registry of PAH) after obtaining informed consent. For patients with pre‐existing PAH, baseline characteristics were recorded from the most recent evaluation prior to pregnancy. In newly diagnosed cases, baseline data corresponded to the initial diagnostic evaluation during pregnancy.

A detailed description of the pregnancy management protocol is provided in Supporting Information S1.

Definitions

Definition of vasoreactive patients or responders to CCB was established according to Sitbon criteria.

Cardiac complications during pregnancy were divided into major and minor events. Major adverse events included maternal death, cardiogenic shock requiring mechanical circulatory support (MCS), or inotropic support. Minor events were defined as hospitalizations for cardiac issues (e.g., heart failure, arrhythmia, or syncope), initiation of prostacyclin therapy (systemic prostacyclins or iloprost), or oxygen therapy. Maternal death was defined as occurring within 42 days of ETP or delivery, in accordance with international definitions. Neonatal complications included the need for inotropic support, mechanical ventilation, or neonatal death within 28 days.

Regarding termination of pregnancy, ETP was defined as the patient‐decided, planned interruption of pregnancy occurring before Week 12. Importantly, patients who required an urgent C‐section during the pregnancy course due to non‐viable fetal status were not considered ETP, but were instead grouped with those who decided to proceed with the pregnancy.

Follow‐Up

All patients were followed until the end of the study period or until the occurrence of death or lung transplantation.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Categorical variables were analyzed using frequencies, percentages, and χ² tests. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata Version 14.0 (StataCorp, Texas, USA).

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this research. However, we recognize the significant value of patient involvement in enhancing research quality. From this point onwards, we plan to actively engage participants and the public in the dissemination of our study results, ensuring that this process is appropriately managed and medically supervised.

Results

Between February 2010 and December 2023, 29 patients with PAH were evaluated at our center during pregnancy. Twenty‐four patients were already diagnosed with the disease, while 5 suffered a debut of PAH during pregnancy; none of these new cases were responders to CCB. Most of the patients with a previous PAH diagnosis were first evaluated in our center during the first trimester, although 2 patients were referred at Weeks 26 and 34, after having discontinued treatment and follow‐up on their own initiative. The Pregnancy Course

The overall event rate was 61.9%; minor events occurred in 61.9% and major events in 23.8%. There were no complications in the vasoreactive group. Considering only the subgroup of non‐vasoreactive patients (14 patients; 66.7%), the event rate was 92.9%. Figure 2 shows complications during pregnancy in our global cohort.

The figure illustrates the complications encountered during pregnancy. Notably, inotropic support was required in five patients. One patient with PAH associated with connective tissue disease (PAH‐CTD), initially diagnosed with severe PAH at 12 weeks, required a curettage while on ECMO. Another patient with hereditary pulmonary veno‐occlusive disease (PVOD) and low PVR developed pulmonary edema. An urgent cesarean section was deemed necessary and performed while the patient was on ECMO, but she ultimately passed away at 22 weeks. The remaining three patients required inotropic support toward the end of their pregnancies. Two of these patients presented late to our center: one with Eisenmenger syndrome and one with portopulmonary hypertension. The third patient, a heritable PAH case (due to BMPR2 mutation), had been closely monitored at our institution throughout her pregnancy.

Regarding major complications among the 14 non‐vasoreactive patients, 5 (35.7%) required ionotropic support, and 2 (14.3%) were assisted with MCS: one VA‐ECMO for a patient first diagnosed with severe PAH associated with connective tissue disease (CTD) at week 12, and one VV‐ECMO for respiratory failure in a heritable pulmonary veno‐occlusive disease (PVOD) patient (confirmed by the presence of a bi‐allelic variant in EIF2AK4) with low PVR. The patient with PAH‐CTD was the only one among the 5 newly diagnosed patients presenting with major complications. Both patients assisted with MCS underwent obstetric surgery while on ECMO (a curettage for the patient with PAH‐CTD at Week 13; and C‐section in the patient with PVOD at Week 22). The patient with PAH‐CTD could be discharged alive after a long hospital stay; while the PVOD patient died hours after the C‐section was performed. With respect to ionotropic requirements, the remaining 3 patients accounted for one PAH‐CTD, one Eisenmenger patient, and one HPAH. Maternal mortality was estimated at 4.8% for the whole cohort; whereas for the cohort of non‐responders this figure rose up to 7.14%. In terms of minor complications in the non‐vasoreactive subgroup: 5 patients (35.7%) received oxygen therapy, 10 (71.4%) were admitted for cardiac reasons, and 11 patients (78.6%) were started on the prostacyclin pathway (9 on epoprostenol and 2 on iloprost). Regarding the newly diagnosed subgroup, four patients required prostacyclins (three epoprostenol and one iloprost), all as add‐on therapy to PDE5 inhibitors. Six of the 11 patients started on the prostacyclin pathway were discharged on dual oral combination therapy once endothelin receptor antagonists could be restarted; however, 5 could not be weaned off and were discharged on prostacyclins as a new medication. There were significant differences depending on the maternal vasoreactive profile. Responders to CCB managed to achieve a longer gestational age (median 37 weeks, vs. 32 weeks; p < 0.001) and, accordingly, their offspring presented with better birth weight (median 2840 g vs. 1820 g; p < 0.001). None of the newborns from the vasoreactive cohort presented an Apgar score below 7 at 1st minute, while this scenario was encountered in 5 newborns from the non‐responders (38.5%, p < 0.05). Besides this, although there was a trend towards fewer neonatal complications in those newborns from the responder cohort, this did not reach statistical significance.

The Long‐Term Follow‐Up

Over a median follow‐up period of 5 years (IQR 1.2–8.5 years), 2 patients died (9.5% of the whole cohort and 14.3% of the non‐responders), both non‐responders. From the vasoreactive cohort, none of the 7 patients lost their response to CCB.

Discussion

This study presents comprehensive data from a tertiary referral center about the management and outcomes of pregnancy among women with PAH in the current era. The results can be summarized as follows: (1) morbidity and mortality of pregnancy in patients with PAH remain high despite aggressive management, with nearly 62% of the cohort having a complication during pregnancy, and more than a third of them being severe (nearly 24% of the cohort had major complications); (2) on the contrary, vasoreactive patients exhibit a benign course of pregnancy; (3) preterm delivery is almost universal among non‐vasoreactive patients; hence complications in the new‐born are not uncommon.

Overall mortality in our cohort of 21 pregnancies accounted for 4.8%, which appears to be slightly lower than previously reported [14, 18, 19, 20, 21]. In accordance with previous guidelines, it is our common practice to extensively counsel female patients of reproductive age on maternal and fetal risks. We therefore emphasize the importance of effective contraception and, should pregnancy occur, recommend ETP. We deviate from this strict recommendation for vasoreactive patients, where, based on our experience, we have traditionally adopted a more neutral approach [8]. From our cohort of pregnant PAH patients, despite the aforementioned counseling, 72.4% chose to proceed. In the ongoing pregnancy scenario, our protocol mandates a close follow‐up plan and favors the early initiation of prostacyclins (mostly intravenous epoprostenol) at the slightest sign of deterioration. This is done to minimize the risk of major complications and aim for a longer, safer gestational age. With this management plan, 11 patients (52.3%) were started on prostacyclins during the pregnancy (excluding the C‐section setting), with 5 of them requiring maintenance of the parenteral treatment at discharge. Despite this strategy, 5 patients (23.8%) still presented major adverse events, of whom 2 (9.5%) required mechanical circulatory support, and one patient (4.8%) died.

As pulmonary hypertension physicians, we are aware of the variety of underlying pathologies and their specific associated conditions that fall under the category of group 1 PAH; yet, it is assumed that they share a common physiology and treatment strategies in terms of the use of the specific PAH therapy. For this reason, we excluded published studies lacking hemodynamic data or those that included a patient population beyond Group 1 when comparing our results [13, 18, 22, 23, 24]. With regards to the ROPAC registry, it reported a maternal mortality rate of 9% in 45 patients between 2013 and 2018, although some of these patients had chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension or lung diseases [19]. Duarte et al. evaluated 12 pregnancies, predominantly PAH associated with congenital heart disease (PAH‐CHD), and reported a higher mortality rate of 16.7%, though it is noteworthy that three of these patients did not receive PAH therapy, likely due to the study period (1999–2009) [21]. Regarding PAH‐CHD, Liu et al. recently published a 5.8% mortality in their cohort of 260 PAH‐CHD patients between 2010 and 2019; however, their proportion of Eisenmenger patients was superior, and this subgroup presented the highest mortality [14]. Importantly, a recent meta‐analysis evaluating 217 pregnant PAH patients (63.6% CHD‐PAH, 21.5% idiopathic PAH) between 2008 and 2018 showed an overall mortality of 12%, being higher in the IPAH group [20]. We believe that the conflicting data regarding subgroups with higher complication rates reinforces the unpredictable nature of pregnancy in the PAH setting.

Nevertheless, it seems reasonable to expect a lower likelihood of complications in the vasoreactive population. Hence, we analyzed vasoreactive and non‐vasoreactive patients separately, because combining them might lead to recommendations that are not applicable to all patients. Recently, Kamp et al. reported positive outcomes, sparking a debate on whether a new era of safer pregnancy for reproductive‐age PAH patients had begun [12, 25, 26]. Based on their findings, they suggest that pregnancy might be safe in patients with low PVR (< 6.25 WU); however, since 10 out of 17 pregnancies in their cohort were in vasoreactive patients, this conclusion may not be universally applicable. Our data support caution regarding PVR alone. In our cohort, only 3 of the patients with PVR < 6 WU were non‐responders, yet they suffered serious complications: (1) the patient with PVOD who died of pulmonary edema, (2) a patient with postoperative CHD‐PAH required iloprost starting at Week 11, and (3) a patient with IPAH started on iv epoprostenol at Week 26. Although prostacyclins could be discontinued in these 2 patients after delivery, we do not believe there is enough evidence to reassure female PAH patients regarding pregnancy solely based on PVR, and they should be aware of the likelihood of these complications to make an informed decision. At the same time, this study from Kamp et al., along with ours, demonstrates how vasoreactive patients are able to complete their pregnancy uneventfully and to deliver at term. Two main conclusions can be drawn from these results: (1) vasoreactive PAH females may merit specific and less restrictive counseling; (2) the actual rate of pregnancy‐related complications for non‐vasoreactive cohort is significantly higher (in our 14 non‐vasoreactive cohort: 78.6% prostacyclin use, 35.7% inotropic support, 14.3% MCS, 7.1% mortality).

The prognosis for the newborn differed sharply between vasoreactive and non‐vasoreactive patients. While previous undifferentiated studies reported high preterm delivery rates (ranging from 49% to 83.7%) [14, 19, 20], in our cohort, prematurity was nearly universal in the non‐vasoreactive group (with 46% delivered before Week 31), but the exception was in the vasoreactive group (only 1/7). Although neonatal complications are multifactorial, they typically correlate with gestational age. Accordingly, while overall complication rates did not reach statistical significance, there were no deaths in the vasoreactive offspring. Furthermore, if non‐invasive ventilatory support was required, it was needed only for 1 day, contrasting with a trend toward a longer time on ventilatory support in the non‐vasoreactive offspring.

In terms of long‐term follow‐up, it has been postulated that pregnancy may accelerate PAH progression and/or precipitate a loss of response to CCB [27]. Based on our results, there is not enough data to claim that pregnancy worsens long‐term prognosis. However, the observed high event rate and the finding that nearly half of the patients who started prostacyclins could not be weaned off suggest a potential link to long‐term deterioration. In contrast, we found a highly reassuring result for the vasoreactive group: none of the CCB responders in our cohort lost their vasoreactive response during follow‐up. This reinforces the hypothesis that pregnancy is likely a safe option for this specific subgroup.

Limitations

The retrospective nature of this study represents a primary limitation. A further concern is the possibility of referral bias, as our data were collected exclusively from a single tertiary center with specialized expertise in the management of PAH during pregnancy. Finally, the small sample size prevented us from assessing the impact of other crucial risk factors on pregnancy outcomes, including PAH etiology, late PAH diagnosis, or late referral during the gestational period.

Conclusions

Pregnancy continues to be a high‐risk scenario for most PAH patients. However, the subgroup of responders to CCB deserves special attention because of their better and mostly uneventful prognosis, both for the mother and the offspring; therefore, we advocate for a less restrictive approach in these vasoreactive patients. Multicentre collaboration and further research are key to both verifying these conclusions and identifying additional prognostic markers.
