Superselective Transcatheter Embolization of a Small-bowel Arteriovenous Malformation Using N-butyl Cyanoacrylate-lipiodol: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Nona Fujimoto, Masamichi Koganemaru, Asako Kuhara, Tomoko Kugiyama, Shohei Mizushima, Miyuki Sawano, Keiichi Ishibashi, Masaru Morita, Kozo Tsuruta, Takumi Kawaguchi, Shuichi Tanoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 79-year-old woman with massive hematochezia after endoscopic hemostasis was diagnosed with a localized ileal arteriovenous malformation supplied by a single vasa recta from the ileal branch of the ileocolic artery, classified as Cho classification type IIIa. Superselective embolization using a triple coaxial technique and a 1:7 n-butyl cyanoacrylate-lipiodol mixture achieved complete occlusion of the feeding artery and nidus while preserving bowel perfusion. No ischemic complications or recurrent bleeding occurred during an 18-month follow-up period. This case suggests that superselective n-butyl-2 cyanoacrylate embolization is an effective, curative, and minimally invasive treatment for small bowel arteriovenous malformations, supported by a review of previously reported cases.Keywords: small-bowel arteriovenous malformation, transcatheter embolization, n-butyl cyanoacrylate, Cho classification type IIIa

Introduction

Vascular lesions of the gastrointestinal tract include arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) and arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs). Both are rare clinical entities characterized by an abnormal shunt between arteries and veins without an intervening capillary bed [1]. AVMs are predominantly congenital in origin and are characterized by the formation of a plexus of vessels called a nidus [2, 3]. In contrast, AVFs are typically acquired, often following surgery or trauma, and involve a direct communication between an artery and a vein [4-6]. We encountered a case of a small bowel AVM (Cho classification type IIIa) presenting with massive hematochezia, which was successfully treated with transcatheter arterial embolization. To the best of our knowledge, there is limited literature reporting the successful treatment using NBCA as a monotherapy for this condition, making our case a potentially unique contribution. Herein, we report this case along with a review of the literature regarding small bowel AVMs successfully treated by interventional procedures.

Case Report

Ethics committee approval was not required for this type of study. Informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report and any accompanying images.

A 79-year-old woman was referred to our hospital for the diagnosis and treatment of gastrointestinal bleeding presenting as multiple episodes of hematochezia over an 8-month period. Her medical history included aortic and mitral valve replacement 20 years prior and long-term warfarin therapy, as managed by her cardiologist. She had no history of inflammatory bowel disease, hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia, or small bowel surgery.

On admission, the patient was hemodynamically stable but anemic. Laboratory tests revealed a hemoglobin level of 7.9 g/dL, platelet count of 171 × 103/μL, total bilirubin of 0.5 mg/dL, albumin of 2.3 g/dL, and an international normalized ratio of 1.00. Warfarin had been temporarily discontinued during a prior evaluation at another institution. Initial transfusions had been completed before admission, and no signs of infection or organ dysfunction were noted. Multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT revealed intense early enhancement in the arterial phase (Figure 1), with subsequent opacification of the draining vein in the early portal phase, suggesting a vascular malformation. Double-balloon enteroscopy revealed abnormally dilated vascular lesions in the ileal mucosa corresponding to the CT enhancement sites. Endoscopic clipping achieved temporary hemostasis, but hematochezia recurred and persisted. Consequently, angiography was performed for diagnosis and treatment.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography in the arterial phase revealing an abnormal early enhanced area of the ileum (arrows).

Superior mesenteric artery angiography was performed under local anesthesia via the right femoral artery using a 4-Fr catheter (Shepherd Hook, Medikit, Tokyo, Japan) through a 4-Fr sheath. The lesion observed on CT was confirmed to be an AVM, which featured dilated vessels, irregular staining in the distal single ileal branch of the ileocolic artery with single vasa recta, nidus, and early venous drainage (Figure 2). Further angiography demonstrated an approximately 9-mm nidus with rapid venous drainage, with an early draining vein visualized within 1 second after contrast injection using a triple coaxial technique to facilitate detailed diagnosis and treatment planning. Because the nidus comprised numerous feeding arteries from single vasa recta and draining veins, all of which were of small caliber, it was classified as Cho classification type IIIa. A 2.9/2.9-Fr steerable large microcatheter (Leonis Mova; SB-kawasumi Laboratories, Kanagawa, Japan) was advanced through the 4-Fr catheter using a 0.016-inch guidewire (Meister, ASAHI INTECC). A localized AVM supplied by a single ileal branch of the ileocolic artery was visualized. A 1.3/1.8-Fr microcatheter (Carnelian MARVEL; Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was then advanced through the larger microcatheter using a 0.010-inch guidewire (ASAHI CHIKAI X 010, Asahi Intecc) (Figure 3). The NBCA-lipiodol mixture was prepared at a ratio of 1:7 using the two-syringe pumping method and was warmed to approximately 37°C prior to injection to achieve optimal viscosity. Water steam was used for warming. Subsequently, an NBCA-lipiodol mixture (Lipiodol Ultra Fluid, Guerbet, Roissy, France) was injected in a non-wedged position to allow flow-directed delivery to embolize from the feeding artery to the venous side of the shunt (Figure 4). A total volume of 0.6 mL of the mixture was delivered under fluoroscopic guidance. Consequently, the AVM was completely occluded while preserving adjacent arterial flow (Figure 5).

Postembolization, the patient reported no abdominal pain, nausea, or fever. Abdominal examinations were unremarkable, laboratory values remained stable, and no additional transfusions were required. Oral intake resumed on post-procedure day 1, and the patient was subsequently discharged in good condition. Following embolization, the patient had no recurrent hematochezia and an uneventful clinical course, and small bowel endoscopy was therefore not performed. Warfarin was restarted after multidisciplinary discussion. During the 18-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with stable hemoglobin levels and normal inflammatory markers. No recurrence of melena, ischemic complications, or bowel dysfunction was observed, and the treatment was considered a clinical success.

Discussion

Table 1 summarizes previously reported cases of small bowel AVMs located distal to the ligament of Treitz that were successfully treated solely by interventional procedures [7-10]. Cases clearly identified as AVFs were excluded from this table. The diagnostic criteria for AVF in this study were defined as cases characterized by a simple arteriovenous shunt without the presence of a nidus or nidus-like network. Including our case, the cohort comprised one woman and four men, with ages ranging from 25 to 79 years (mean age, 60 years). The most common presenting symptom was hematochezia, with one case exhibiting portal hypertension. For the radical cure of AVMs, complete occlusion of the shunt is essential, and determining a treatment strategy based on the Cho [11] or Yakes [12] classifications is considered effective. In particular, the Cho classification facilitates the organization of shunt structures, which helps in determining the treatment strategy―whether to use a transarterial or transvenous approach―and serves as an indicator for the clinical endpoint of embolization. Among the literature reviewed, only two cases, including ours, explicitly mentioned the Cho classification. One of these, reported by Shigeyama et al. [10], involved a Cho classification type II lesion; they achieved shunt occlusion via a transvenous approach by embolizing the dominant outflow vein using ethanolamine oleate and microcoils. Giurazza et al. [9] reported the complete disappearance of a shunt in a Yakes classification type IIIa lesion using a liquid embolic agent, precipitating hydrophobic injectable liquid (PHIL). Although Yakes classification type IIIa roughly corresponds to Cho classification type II, PHIL is non-adhesive, which reduces the risk of the microcatheter becoming trapped in the embolic agent. This advantage allows for slow, prolonged infusion, which likely enabled treatment via a transarterial approach in their case. In other reports [7, 8], there was no mention of the Cho or Yakes classifications, nor was there a description of how the treatment strategy was determined. In those cases, a transarterial approach was employed, and shunt occlusion was achieved using either a combination of NBCA and coils or coils alone. To the best of our knowledge, our case appears to be among the few to demonstrate successful shunt occlusion using NBCA alone for a small bowel AVM.

Cho classification type IIIa is characterized by the presence of numerous shunts between non-dilated, fine, blush-like arterioles and venules [11]. For this type, a transarterial approach alone is effective, and effective blood flow occlusion can be achieved by distributing a liquid embolic agent from the feeding artery to the venous side of the shunt [11]. In such cases, proximal embolization of the feeding artery is ineffective and must be avoided, as it complicates future access. Generally, the polymerization time of NBCA in vivo is shorter than that in vitro; therefore, an NBCA-lipiodol mixture with a slightly higher dilution than initially anticipated is often required [13]. This phenomenon is attributed to the accelerated polymerization rate of NBCA at body temperature compared to room temperature. Furthermore, it is thought that the abundance of anions upon contact with the endothelium in areas of vascular branching, sharp tortuosity, or stenosis also contributes to this acceleration [13]. Therefore, when embolizing a nidus consisting of a cluster of fine vessels, a higher dilution ratio is considered necessary.

In the present case, several factors were taken into account: the microcatheter tip was not sufficiently close to the nidus during NBCA administration, and both the blood flow volume and velocity in the feeding artery were likely reduced due to its small caliber and the presence of the catheter itself. Consequently, the NBCA-lipiodol mixture was set at a relatively high dilution ratio of 1:7. Additionally, to further reduce viscosity and improve peripheral delivery, the mixture was heated to approximately 37°C before performing embolization using the continuous column technique. The embolization was successfully completed within the targeted area, resulting in immediate disappearance of the shunt and rapid resolution of hematochezia. Compared to previously reported cases, no recurrence of bleeding has been observed over a relatively long follow-up period. In cases of AVMs with a small-caliber, highly tortuous nidus such as this one, a high dilution ratio of 1:7 and pre-warming may be effective in achieving complete shunt occlusion by enhancing the peripheral delivery of the NBCA-lipiodol mixture. Furthermore, the high dilution of NBCA may offer the additional advantage of reducing the frequency of procedural complications, such as microcatheter adhesion.

In contrast, small bowel AVFs involve a direct communication between an artery and a vein, forming a high-flow fistula. Consequently, they are more likely to cause not only gastrointestinal bleeding but also portal hypertension or steal phenomena [4-6]. These lesions correspond to type I in both the Cho and Yakes classifications [11, 12]. As with AVMs, either a transarterial or transportal approach is selected for the interventional closure of an AVF shunt. For shunt occlusion, since the target vessel diameter is relatively large, coils, vascular plugs, or a combination of both are frequently employed, with favorable therapeutic outcomes reported in the literature [4-6].

The triple coaxial technique is an effective technique when more selective catheterization is required [14]; however, its use has not been previously reported for small bowel AVMs. In cases of lower gastrointestinal bleeding, such as the present case, it is suggested that complications such as post-embolic intestinal ischemia can be avoided by limiting the embolization of the vasa recta to three or fewer vessels. Therefore, highly selective catheterization is mandated [14, 15]. Since the vasa recta of the small bowel are small in caliber and numerous, identifying the culprit vessel can be challenging. High-flow microcatheters allow for the injection of a sufficient volume of contrast medium during angiography; thus, the triple coaxial technique is also considered useful for identifying the culprit vessel. Furthermore, the small bowel can exhibit intense peristalsis [15]. When inserting a small-diameter microcatheter into the target vessel, the triple coaxial technique offers the additional advantage of providing stable backup support.

As a limitation of this report, a direct comparison using the Cho classification is difficult, as most previous reports of transcatheter embolization for gastrointestinal bleeding did not employ the Cho or Yakes classification systems.

In conclusion, based on this case report, superselective transcatheter embolization with NBCA is possibly a safe and effective therapeutic modality for small bowel AVM. Our experience, supported by a review of the literature, indicates that for Cho classification type IIIa lesions involving small-caliber feeding arteries, meticulous titration of the NBCA concentration, specifically the 1:7 NBCA-lipiodol ratio employed in this case, facilitates controlled delivery and ensures optimal penetration of the embolic agent to the venous component of the nidus.
