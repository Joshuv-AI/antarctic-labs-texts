Inferior‐Lead Lambda (λ) Wave Revealing Critical Right Coronary Artery Stenosis: A Case Report
Authors: Jian Xiong, Djandan Tadum Arthur Vithran, Jingjing Chai, Zheng Cao, Zhixiao Wang, Xiaoyong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLambda (λ) waves are uncommon ECG markers of malignant arrhythmias, usually associated with channelopathies or transient ischemia. We report a 34‐year‐old man with recurrent syncope preceded by subxiphoid chest pain and diaphoresis. Admission ECG showed λ‐like morphology in the inferior leads with dynamic ST changes. Laboratory tests and echocardiography were normal, while Holter monitoring demonstrated persistent ST‐segment depression. Coronary angiography revealed diffuse right coronary artery disease with a critical 95% stenosis, treated with drug‐eluting stent placement. ECG abnormalities resolved after PCI, and syncope did not return. Serial angiography over 3.5 years confirmed sustained stent patency.Keywords: case report, lambda wave, percutaneous coronary intervention, right coronary artery stenosis, syncope

Introduction

Electrocardiographic repolarization abnormalities have long been recognized as vital warning signs of life‐threatening cardiac events, including malignant arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death (Tse and Yan 2017; Arteyeva 2020). Among these, the lambda (λ) wave is particularly rare and underrecognized. This distinctive morphology, which resembles the Greek letter λ, is characterized by a notched terminal portion of the QRS complex, a steep down‐sloping ST segment, and an inverted T wave (Cipriani et al. 2021; Andreou 2022). Although uncommon, the λ wave has been observed in association with syncope, ventricular fibrillation, and sudden cardiac death, underscoring its significance as a potential high‐risk electrocardiographic marker (Cipriani et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2020).

Historically, λ waves were often classified within the spectrum of channelopathies, particularly Brugada syndrome and other J‐wave syndromes. This interpretation gained traction following studies identifying mutations in the SCN5A sodium channel gene, which linked abnormal repolarization morphologies with primary electrical disease (Antzelevitch and Yan 2015; Tokioka et al. 2014; Achmad et al. 2023). Patients with these disorders typically exhibit dynamic ECG patterns that can be unmasked by fever, drugs, or autonomic influences, and they remain at high risk for malignant arrhythmias. Such observations reinforced the perception of the λ wave as a primarily genetic and arrhythmogenic phenomenon.

More recently, however, accumulating clinical evidence has highlighted the role of ischemia in generating λ‐like patterns. Case series and observational studies have documented λ morphologies in patients with acute myocardial infarction, coronary vasospasm, and myocardial bridging (Cipriani et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2020). Mechanistic studies suggest that ischemia amplifies J waves and increases transmural dispersion of repolarization, thereby predisposing to phase II re‐entry and malignant ventricular arrhythmias (Andreou 2022; Haïssaguerre et al. 2019; Rudy 2021; Tse and Yan 2017). These findings have shifted the understanding of the λ wave from being exclusively linked to genetic channelopathies to a broader marker of electrical instability, encompassing both inherited and acquired substrates.

Despite its potential importance, the λ wave remains poorly characterized in contemporary practice. The majority of published cases involve acute coronary syndromes or dynamic vasospastic events, while only a small number have described fixed obstructive coronary artery disease as the underlying cause (Cipriani et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2020). Moreover, reports of syncope as the primary clinical manifestation associated with a λ wave are exceedingly rare. This diagnostic gap can contribute to delays in recognition and treatment, as clinicians may attribute the morphology to benign or genetic causes while overlooking potentially reversible ischemia (Kamakura et al. 2022; Tikkanen et al. 2012).

Here, we describe the case of a 34‐year‐old man with recurrent syncope and a λ wave pattern in the inferior leads, ultimately found to be due to a critical right coronary artery stenosis. Both the electrocardiographic abnormalities and clinical symptoms resolved completely following percutaneous coronary intervention, with durable stability during long‐term follow‐up. This case expands current knowledge on ischemia‐induced λ waves and emphasizes the importance of considering underlying coronary artery disease when encountering this rare but high‐risk ECG phenotype.

Case Presentation

Patient Information

A 34‐year‐old man with a 5‐year history of hypertension (maximum recorded 180/110 mmHg) presented with recurrent syncope. He had achieved temporary control with antihypertensive therapy but discontinued medication after 3 months, and blood pressure monitoring was not continued thereafter. At the time of admission, ambulatory blood pressure was within the normal range. The patient denied smoking, alcohol use, other comorbidities, or any relevant family history of premature cardiovascular disease or sudden cardiac death. He had no known allergies and no previous cardiovascular interventions.

Primary Concerns and Symptoms

The first syncopal episode occurred on 23 May 2020, during a morning walk. He developed a sudden urge to defecate, followed by localized subxiphoid chest discomfort described as a coin‐sized dull ache. Symptoms progressively intensified, with dizziness, visual blackout, limb weakness, and transient loss of consciousness accompanied by urinary incontinence but without fecal incontinence, convulsions, or frothing at the mouth. He regained consciousness spontaneously and reported severe headache, diaphoresis, chest tightness, and generalized fatigue, which improved after several minutes of rest. Over the following 2 weeks, three additional syncopal events occurred, two in the early morning (between 06:00 and 08:00) and one in the afternoon. Each episode was preceded by focal xiphoid pain, diaphoresis, headache, and visual blackout, followed by loss of consciousness. Between events, he remained asymptomatic and performed daily activities without limitation.

Clinical Findings

On admission to our institution, the patient's vital signs were stable, and his physical examination was unremarkable. No cardiac murmurs, abnormal heart sounds, or neurological deficits were detected. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were otherwise regular.

Diagnostic Assessment

Electrocardiography obtained during a syncopal event showed a λ wave pattern in the inferior leads (II, III, aVF), characterized by a notched terminal QRS complex, a steep down‐sloping ST segment, and inverted T waves (Figure 1). On admission, ECG demonstrated horizontal ST‐segment depression (0.075 mV) in leads V4–V6 (Figure 1). During a chest pain episode on 11 June 2020, repeat ECG showed horizontal ST depression in leads II, III, and aVF, reciprocal ST elevation in aVR, and down‐sloping ST depression of 0.3 mV in leads V3–V4 (Figure 1). Ten minutes after pain resolution, the ECG reverted to the admission pattern (Figure 1).

Laboratory investigations, including BNP, thyroid function, electrolytes, coagulation profile, hepatic and renal function, lipid panel, and complete blood count, were all within reference ranges. Chest, brain, and adrenal CT scans were unremarkable. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed mild concentric left ventricular hypertrophy with a wall thickness of 11 mm and a preserved ejection fraction of 63%. Twenty‐four‐hour Holter monitoring demonstrated sinus rhythm with a minimum heart rate of 43 bpm (recorded at 00:05), a maximum heart rate of 106 bpm (recorded at 06:58), and an average heart rate of 68 bpm. Twenty‐one isolated supraventricular premature beats and three paired beats were recorded. Persistent ST‐segment depression (0.1–0.2 mV) was observed in leads II, III, aVF, and V3–V6. Heart rate variability was normal.

Differential diagnoses considered included primary electrical disorders (Brugada syndrome, early repolarization, or other channelopathies), coronary vasospasm, myocardial bridging, and fixed obstructive coronary artery disease.

Therapeutic Intervention

On 12 June 2020, coronary angiography revealed diffuse atherosclerotic plaque throughout the right coronary artery with irregular intima and a critical 95% stenosis at the second bend (Figure 1). A 4.0 × 24 mm EXCEL drug‐eluting stent was implanted. Post‐procedure ECG showed complete normalization of ST–T segments and resolution of the λ wave morphology (Figure 1). The patient was discharged on dual antiplatelet therapy and standard secondary prevention medication.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

The patient remained stable post‐PCI, with no recurrent episodes of syncope or chest pain. Follow‐up coronary angiography performed in April 2021, July 2022, and January 2024 confirmed stent patency with no new lesions (Figure 1). Serial ECGs demonstrated persistent normalization without recurrence of the λ wave pattern. The patient continues to be asymptomatic with preserved functional capacity and tolerance of medical therapy.

Discussion

Lambda (λ) waves are rare but clinically meaningful electrocardiographic (ECG) patterns. Their recognition is essential because they have been associated with malignant ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, yet their pathophysiological origins remain heterogeneous. In this report, a young patient presented with recurrent syncope and an inferior‐lead λ wave pattern. The underlying cause was a fixed critical stenosis of the right coronary artery (RCA), and both symptoms and ECG abnormalities resolved after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). The case highlights the importance of not attributing λ waves exclusively to channelopathies and underlines the role of ischemia as a potential substrate.

Strengths and Limitations

The major strength of this case is the comprehensive documentation across different clinical phases. Serial ECGs captured the λ wave during syncope, dynamic ischemic ST changes during chest pain, and complete resolution after revascularization. Invasive coronary angiography provided definitive anatomical correlation, and follow‐up angiograms at 10 months, 2 years, and 3.5 years confirmed durable patency. More importantly, the patient remained symptom‐free for over 3 years, reinforcing the causal relationship between ischemia and the λ wave pattern.

Limitations include the absence of genetic testing for SCN5A or other ion channel mutations, which would have definitively excluded a channelopathy. Electrophysiological testing was not performed, so latent arrhythmogenic susceptibility cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, as a single case report, the findings cannot be generalized to all presentations of λ waves. Nevertheless, the strength of the temporal and therapeutic association supports an ischemia‐driven mechanism in this patient.

Review of the Medical Literature

The λ wave was initially described in young men with syncope and sudden nocturnal death, and was often linked to Brugada syndrome and SCN5A mutations (Antzelevitch and Yan 2015; Tokioka et al. 2014; Achmad et al. 2023; Guo et al. 2021). This led to its early classification as a marker of channelopathy‐associated electrical instability.

More recent studies, however, have emphasized ischemia as an alternative mechanism. Kukla et al. (2008) reported three patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) who exhibited λ‐shaped ST‐segment elevation and subsequently developed ventricular fibrillation. Sato et al. (2012) provided mechanistic evidence that ischemia accentuates J waves by amplifying transmural dispersion of repolarization, facilitating phase II re‐entry and malignant arrhythmias. Haïssaguerre et al. (2019) further clarified that inferolateral J‐wave syndromes may arise from either depolarization or repolarization abnormalities, with ischemia as a critical precipitant. More recent reports have also highlighted λ morphologies in coronary vasospasm, myocardial bridging, and acute coronary syndromes (Kim et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2018).

These cases highlight diverse mechanisms, including acute coronary occlusion, vasospasm, drug‐induced abnormalities, and Takotsubo cardiomyopathy. This review highlights that λ waves represent a spectrum of disorders rather than a uniform entity. Some are ischemia‐driven and resolve with PCI or vasodilator therapy; others are channelopathic and require ICD implantation. The heterogeneity underscores the importance of a structured diagnostic workup, integrating coronary evaluation with consideration of genetic testing when angiography is routine.

Rationale for Conclusions

In the present case, several factors support ischemia as the mechanism. First, the λ morphology was only present during ischemic episodes and disappeared following RCA stenting. Second, a multi‐year follow‐up with repeat angiography confirmed durable patency and absence of recurrence, excluding transient spasm as the primary driver. Third, the absence of family history, routine laboratory tests, and lack of recurrent arrhythmia after PCI make channelopathy less likely. Together, these findings provide strong evidence that fixed coronary stenosis can underlie the λ wave pattern. This conclusion complements existing literature, where most reported ischemic cases involved AMI or vasospasm, and emphasizes that chronic coronary artery disease should also be considered.

Clinical Implications

Clinicians should view the λ wave as a red flag ECG marker. When identified, the initial priority is to exclude ischemia through early coronary angiography. If obstructive disease is confirmed, revascularization can abolish the arrhythmogenic substrate and prevent life‐threatening events. In patients with no angiographic stenosis, vasospasm testing and therapy with calcium channel blockers should be considered. When neither stenosis nor vasospasm is evident and malignant arrhythmias occur, channelopathy should be suspected and managed with genetic evaluation and ICD implantation as appropriate. Ultimately, management must be individualized, but the unifying principle is that λ waves always demand urgent and thorough investigation.

Conclusion

Lambda (λ) waves are uncommon but clinically significant ECG abnormalities. They should not be dismissed as benign or attributed solely to channelopathies. In this case, the λ pattern was a marker of critical RCA stenosis and resolved after PCI, illustrating that ischemia may be a key underlying mechanism. The presence of a λ wave warrants immediate evaluation, as timely diagnosis and intervention can be lifesaving.
