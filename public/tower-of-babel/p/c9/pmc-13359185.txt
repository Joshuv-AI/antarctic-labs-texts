“Fifth‐day fits” revisited: A literature review of benign idiopathic neonatal seizures and comparison with KCNQ2‐ and KCNQ3‐associated benign familial epilepsy syndromes
Authors: Varun Kannan, Aishwarya V Pareek, Abhijit R Das, Charles T Gay, James J Riviello
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBenign idiopathic neonatal seizures (BINS), colloquially referred to as the “fifth‐day fits,” is a clinical neonatal epilepsy syndrome associated with early, spontaneous resolution of seizures and favorable developmental outcome. Although this disease entity was first described over four decades ago, the etiopathogenesis remains unknown, and it is unclear if the syndrome represents a single, cohesive disorder or a common manifestation of various unrelated neonatal neurological disturbances. As such, there are no standardized approaches to diagnostic workup and management. Benign familial neonatal seizures (BFNS) is a well‐characterized genetic syndrome associated with KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 pathogenic variants, which also manifests clinically with self‐resolving seizures in the neonatal period. While it remains unclear if there is any shared pathogenesis between these two disorders, the exceedingly similar phenotypic presentations and natural history raise the question of whether consensus management approaches used in genetic BFNS can also be applied to BINS. Here, we present a topical and historical review of BINS and BFNS literature and propose specific treatment recommendations based on extrapolation of limited existing clinical data.Keywords: benign neonatal seizures, channelopathies

Introduction

Among all pediatric age groups, neonates carry the highest risk of seizure occurrence. 1 The relatively low seizure threshold observed in the neonatal period may at least partially be explained by paradoxical excitatory GABAergic signaling, which eventually normalizes to typical inhibitory function within the first few weeks of life. 2 While the majority of neonatal seizures are provoked by obvious acquired brain insults, including perinatal asphyxia, ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, electrolyte and metabolic derangements, and meningoencephalitis, unprovoked seizures are also observed in this age group. 3 , 4 In the pregenomic era, the etiologies of neonatal epilepsies with unprovoked seizures were unknown, though certain familial cases were presumed to be secondary to inherited disorders. 5 With the advent of “next‐generation” molecular diagnostic testing, however, confirmation of both inherited and de novo genetic diseases with neonatal seizure onset has increased substantially. 6 Of particular interest are the “channelopathies,” characterized by genetic alteration of neuronal ion channel structure, function, and/or expression, resulting in pathologic spontaneous neuronal firing and seizures. 7 Hundreds of channel genes are now implicated in neonatal‐onset epilepsy syndromes with a wide spectrum of disease severity, ranging from spontaneously resolving seizure disorders with normal development to intractable, lifelong epilepsies with devastating neurodevelopmental consequences.

Despite modern advances in genetic diagnostics, we continue to observe cases of unprovoked seizures in otherwise healthy term neonates with nondiagnostic genetic testing results. These cases are reminiscent of a well‐recognized but poorly understood “benign” neonatal seizure syndrome that was first reported decades ago, referred to most commonly as the “fifth‐day fits” (we will instead use the term “benign idiopathic neonatal seizures,” or “BINS”). This clinical syndromic diagnosis is codified in epilepsy literature, but aside from a flurry of publications in the years following its initial description, there has essentially been no further progress in elucidating its etiology or consensus recommendations regarding optimal clinical management. 8 , 9 Curiously, the clinical presentation, natural history, and prognosis of fifth‐day fits very closely resemble those of a far better characterized familial channelopathy disorder—BFNS secondary to variants in KCNQ2 or KCNQ3. 10 This observation raises several important questions:

To answer these questions, we present a historical review of the literature regarding both BINS and BFNS. We subsequently offer specific management recommendations for BINS, extrapolated from limited existing clinical data from BFNS studies.

Emergence of the “fifth‐day fits”

Seemingly unexplained seizures in healthy newborns were first described in Australia in the late 1970s 11 (Figure 1). These seizures occurred in term neonates with no known perinatal birth trauma, asphyxia, or encephalopathy and appeared to peak in onset between days three and seven of life, hence the term “fifth‐day fits.” In 1981, a French group also noted a similar pattern of early onset unprovoked seizures, with many of the children having a characteristic interictal electroencephalogram (EEG) background pattern of sharp theta waves, coined theta pointu alternant. 12 Consistent natural history among all patients was spontaneous resolution of seizures within days to weeks of onset and normal neurodevelopmental outcome.

Sporadic “epidemics” of this syndrome were also reported during this decade in other countries with variability in prevalence over time, sparking inquiries regarding infectious or other environmental triggers. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Goldberg et al. postulated that zinc deficiency induced by widespread introduction of cow milk‐based formulas in neonatal units could be contributory, though this hypothesis was never validated in subsequent studies. 13 Herrmann et al. documented >90% seropositivity for rotavirus infection in a similar fifth‐day fit cohort, which was much higher when compared with healthy neonates and neonates with other serious illnesses without seizure. 14 Of note, a recent increase in presumed fifth‐day fits cases in South Korea prompted an investigation, which confirmed high correlation with rotavirus infection and associated radiographic leukoencephalopathy, suggesting that the data from the Herrmann et al. study may have reflected undiagnosed infectious encephalopathy in the premagnetic resonance imaging era. 15

The typical presentation is the abrupt onset of focal motor (unilateral or alternating‐laterality clonic) or apneic seizure, which can occur in clusters and even progress to status epilepticus before spontaneous resolution within two to three days. After this acute seizure period, there is a complete cessation of spontaneous seizure activity, and long‐term neurodevelopment is unaffected. Ictal EEG shows rhythmic epileptiform discharges most prominently in temporal or Rolandic regions. The interictal theta pointu alternant pattern is reported in over half of the cases, though other patterns, including multifocal discharges, excessive discontinuity, and even normal electrographic background, are possible. While acute treatment of status epilepticus is generally indicated, there are no consensus recommendations for the necessity of prophylactic treatment, choice of medication, or duration of treatment.

Benign familial neonatal seizures and neonatal potassium channelopathies

Around the same time as the initial recognition of sporadic self‐resolving neonatal seizures, reports emerged in the literature describing a likely familial benign neonatal seizure syndrome 16 (Figure 1). Inheritance appeared to follow an autosomal dominant pattern, with most children's seizures resolving during infancy. 17 Further study of affected families identified two associated genetic susceptibility loci, the majority in chromosome 8 and a lesser but still significant proportion in chromosome 20. 18 , 19 These were later confirmed to localize to the genes KCNQ2 and KCNQ3, which, respectively, encode the voltage‐gated potassium ion channels Kv7.2 and Kv7.3. 20

Electrophysiology studies have since established the essential roles that these channels play in maintaining physiologic neuronal resting membrane potentials, and disruption of normal channel function alters neuronal excitability, resulting in lowered seizure threshold. 10 The mechanism of spontaneous resolution of seizures in most neonates with BNFS remains unknown but could be related to changes in potassium channel expression patterns over the first days of life or normalization of initial paradoxical GABAergic function as previously discussed. 2 , 21 In addition to BFNS, variants in KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 have been associated with a wide phenotypic spectrum of neonatal epilepsy syndromes, with growing genotype–phenotype correlational data. 22 BFNS appears to represent the mildest form of KCNQ2‐ and KCNQ3‐associated epilepsy, with other children developing persistent seizures into later childhood and developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) with intractable epilepsy in the most severe cases (Figure 2). The degree to which variants affect channel structure and function appears to correspond with phenotypic severity; BFNS‐associated variants induce mild haploinsufficiency, while severe DEE phenotypes are associated with more deleterious missense alterations that interfere with normal channel gating, charge transfer, and/or receptor expression and trafficking. 22

A model for categorization of neonatal‐onset epilepsies, acknowledging the similar phenotype and favorable neurodevelopmental outcome of benign idiopathic neonatal seizures (BINS) and benign familial neonatal seizures (BFNS), and the wide overall spectrum of KCNQ2/KCNQ3 disorders spanning BFNS and developmental and epileptic encephalopathy phenotypes.

While the majority of KCNQ2‐ and KCNQ3‐associated neonatal seizure cases reported are familial and autosomal dominantly inherited, de novo variants in neonates with initial presentation consistent with BINS have been reported. 23 , 24 The significant phenotypic overall between confirmed de novo “BFNS” and cases of BINS with negative genetic testing for known Mendelian epilepsy disorders (including potassium channelopathies) raises the question of whether there is some common pathogenic genetic disruption in channel expression or function that we cannot detect with current molecular genetic diagnostic techniques. There is increasing recognition that deep intronic variants and tissue‐specific mosaic variants can cause epilepsy and evade detection via targeted and broad‐spectrum sequencing. 25 , 26 , 27 Future studies could consider alternative diagnostic approaches, such as quantitative RNA sequencing, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cell‐free DNA testing, or minimally invasive sampling of olfactory mucosal neurons in suspected genetic neonatal epilepsies with nondiagnostic initial DNA sequencing results. 27 Alternatively, self‐resolving neonatal seizures may be a common clinical manifestation of multiple genetic and acquired environmental (perinatal, nutritional, and infectious) insults, which disrupt the delicate electrophysiological balance of the vulnerable neonatal brain, and therefore may not represent a single disease entity.

Clinical approach to the diagnosis of self‐limited neonatal seizures

While the pathophysiology of BINS remains unknown, what is well established in the literature and clinical practice is that the syndrome is a diagnosis of exclusion. 8 , 9 Provoking brain injuries (such as hypoxic‐ischemic encephalopathy, vascular insults, meningoencephalitis, and toxic/metabolic derangements) and various identifiable etiologies of neonatal epilepsies (structural/anatomic, genetic, metabolic) collectively remain more prevalent than truly idiopathic BINS and therefore must be evaluated for thoroughly. 4 , 28 Standardized approaches to neonatal onset seizures recommend imaging (magnetic resonance imaging of the brain ideally), infectious studies, CSF analysis, serum electrolytes, and metabolic markers, and there is a growing impetus toward early molecular genetic testing (both targeted panels and broad‐spectrum arrays such as whole‐exome and whole‐genome sequencing) for suspected genetic epilepsies. 29 , 30 , 31

Although imaging and initial laboratory evaluation can quickly identify and rule out most provoking causes of neonatal seizures, genetic testing for neonatal epilepsies and DEE can take anywhere from days to several weeks. Despite the inherent delay in diagnostic confirmation, there are clinical and electrographic features of neonatal seizure presentations that can aid in risk stratifying between likely benign/self‐limited seizures and more severe genetic epilepsies/DEE. Santarone et al. reported that the day of seizure onset appears to have predictive value for etiology, with seizure occurring on the first day of life correlating with severe insults (hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy, inborn errors of metabolism, brain malformations) and genetic DEE. 32 In contrast, onset of seizures in “self‐limited” neonatal seizure syndromes clustered between 1 and 10 days of life; perinatal stroke and meningoencephalitis‐associated seizure onset also overlapped with this range, but these etiologies are readily diagnosed with imaging and laboratory evaluation. The day of seizure onset also appears to have prognostic value, with onset on the first day of life correlating with significantly worse neurodevelopmental outcome. 33

Seizure semiology and EEG features can also be helpful in determining etiology. Unilateral tonic motor seizures are highly suggestive of channelopathies, including benign potassium channel disorders. 34 However, the presence of generalized onset or mixed generalized/focal onset seizures strongly correlates with the more severe DEE disorders. 35 Cornet et al. compared EEG data from neonates with early‐onset genetic epilepsy (including both DEE and BFNS) with data from neonates with acute provoked seizures (hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy and stroke) and concluded that while tonic focal motor seizures are prevalent in both DEE and BFNS, the presence of severe EEG background abnormalities (such as burst‐suppression pattern) can be used to distinguish DEE from BFNS. 36 Furthermore, these severe EEG background findings are associated with adverse outcomes. 37 In summary, electroclinical features of neonatal seizures that are suggestive of potentially self‐limited syndromes and favorable outcomes include seizure onset beyond the first day of life, absence of generalized seizures, and normal or only mildly abnormal interictal EEG background (Table 1).

Molecular genetic testing remains inherently expensive, and it is reasonable for clinicians to weigh the potential diagnostic and therapeutic yield of genetic testing against prohibitive costs in the context of self‐limited seizure disorders (especially in resource‐limited settings). If genetic testing is deemed appropriate, the choice of testing approach also requires careful consideration. Despite the low cost, traditional microarray‐based tests have low diagnostic yield in nonsyndromic epilepsy cases. 38 Targeted epilepsy panels likely represent the most cost‐effective initial step, with subsequent escalation to more expensive (but highest yield) exome/genome sequencing if such panels are nondiagnostic. 38

Pharmacologic management of self‐limited neonatal seizures

Aside from spontaneous resolution and favorable developmental outcome, another consistent clinical feature of BFNS and KCNQ2‐/KCNQ3‐related epilepsies is marked favorable response to sodium channel–blocking antiseizure medications (ASMs), such as carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine. 39 The precise mechanism for this response is unclear, but co‐localization of voltage‐gated potassium and voltage‐gated sodium channels is validated in in vitro studies and may play a role. 40 To date, no studies have examined the efficacy and safety profiles of ASMs in the treatment of BINS. While phenobarbital continues to be regarded as among the most efficacious ASMs for neonatal seizures, there is robust emerging evidence regarding the adverse neurotoxic and potentially detrimental developmental effects of long‐term use. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 Furthermore, systematic review data suggests that sodium channel blockers are superior to both phenobarbital and levetiracetam in achieving seizure freedom in the KCNQ2‐related epilepsies. 39 Sands et al. reported a series of 19 neonates with KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 BFNS and found that 17 (88%) had complete cessation of seizure activity within hours of starting low‐dose oxcarbazepine or carbamazepine, with no adverse effects and normal long‐term developmental follow‐up. 45 For these reasons, we propose that in cases of neonatal seizures with benign imaging, CSF parameters, and absence of the concerning clinical/electrographic features previously discussed, oxcarbazepine is a reasonable first‐line prophylactic ASM while awaiting genetic confirmation of the presence of potential de novo KCNQ2 or KCNQ3 variants. Phenobarbital should be reserved for neonates with severe neonatal epilepsies and/or provoked seizures from acquired neonatal brain insults to minimize unnecessary neurotoxicity and optimize disease‐specific pharmacologic therapy (Figure 3).

The decision to initiate any form of prophylactic treatment for self‐limited neonatal seizure disorders is itself not unambiguous. We generally argue in favor of ASM maintenance therapy to reduce the distressing effects of recurrent seizures (especially apneic seizures) on families and to prevent progression to status epilepticus. In our experience, however, some parents of children with BFNS deferred medication treatment for their children due to the known benign natural history of the disorder within their own family. Ultimately, a shared decision‐making model with family input should be used in these treatment discussions. Duration of treatment remains another important unanswered question. Given the low probability of seizure recurrence beyond the first few weeks of life, empiric weaning at some point in early infancy is reasonable with appropriate precautions for hypothetical seizure recurrence (especially while awaiting genetic testing results to further inform prognosis). Alternatively, repeat EEG with confirmation of normal interictal EEG background beyond the neonatal period could be used as a marker for safe weaning potential.

Conclusions

The clinical syndrome of spontaneously resolving neonatal seizures associated with favorable neurodevelopment remains incompletely understood. While a significant proportion of such cases are ultimately confirmed to be secondary to benign channelopathies, there remain sporadic cases with yet‐undetermined etiology, and sadly our understanding of this disorder has not advanced substantially in the decades since the initial reports. Both of the self‐limited neonatal seizure syndromes discussed here remain purely diagnoses of exclusion, and diligent evaluation for provoking brain insults and/or electroclinical features of potentially severe neonatal epilepsy syndromes is essential in all cases of neonatal‐onset seizures. Once concerning etiologies are ruled out, however, it is reasonable to treat suspected BINS versus BFNS neonates with a more targeted, and potentially less harmful, management approach (sodium channel blockers in lieu of neurotoxic traditional drugs). Further investigation into the etiology and optimal clinical management of BINS is clearly needed. For the time being, the phenomenon of fifth‐day fits remains a curious conundrum for pediatricians and neurologists.
