Electrostatic-driven dehydration of ions in nanoporous membranes
Authors: Kairui Liu, Razi Epsztein, Shihong Lin, Lie Liu, Jiuhui Qu, Meng Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Surface charge critically affects ion-selective membrane performance, particularly in separating ions with similar size and charge, the key challenge in water treatment. Herein, we investigate the permeation of alkali chlorides (LiCl, KCl, and CsCl) through steric hindrance–free nanoporous membranes with tunable surface charge densities. Supported by molecular dynamics simulations, we confirm that electrostatic effects promote the dehydration of Cl−, the counterions to the membrane charge, at the positively charged membrane surface. This dehydration leads to a great tendency of Cl− to absorb to the membrane surface and be retained, compromises Cl− partitioning, and impedes salt cotransport. For negatively charged membranes, Cs+ with its lower hydration energy undergoes greater electrostatic-driven dehydration and partition hindrance than K+, resulting in selective KCl transport. Our findings provide both theoretical and experimental proofs of ionic dehydration and transport impediments driven by electrostatic interactions at charged membrane surfaces, presenting an in-depth perspective for designing ion-selective membranes to separate similar ions based on charge effects.

INTRODUCTION

The demand for ion-selective membranes with perfect selectivity to ions such as lithium has escalated in recent years (1, 2). Their rational application is vital for addressing water safety, resource recovery, and energy conservation—the global challenges in the water-energy nexus (3, 4). The pore structures of these membranes play a key role in achieving ion selectivity (5–7). For instance, polyamide nanofiltration membranes with a pore size of ~6 Å preferentially allow the permeation of monovalent ions, which have smaller hydrated radii, over multivalent ions (8). Beyond size exclusion, the Donnan effect—a charge-based phenomenon—enables the membrane to differentiate between ions of similar size but differing valence (9). In the case of sulfate and chloride anions, which have comparable hydrated radii, an intrinsically negatively charged polyamide membrane induces stronger electrostatic repulsion toward sulfate, thereby enhancing selective separation (10, 11).

Membrane charge plays a pivotal role in facilitating the selective transport of monovalent ions with comparable hydrated radii (12–14), a feature increasingly relevant to addressing challenges in the field. This selectivity may arise from ion-specific interactions between the membrane surface and the ions being transported (15). Specifically, the charged surface exerts varying degrees of electrostatic attraction and repulsion to ions with different charge densities during transport (16, 17). Research has shown that steric hindrance–free, negatively charged graphene nanoporous membranes exhibit selective transport capacity among alkali cations with subtly different properties (18). However, the subtle differences in the properties of ions sometimes fail to fully account for the selectivity induced by the surface charge effects (19–21). The mechanisms underlying the unusual differences in the kinetic and thermodynamic behaviors of ions at the charged membrane surface remain poorly understood.

The charged membrane surface also dictates the partitioning behavior of ions entering the membrane pore from the bulk (22, 23). Transition state theory (TST) for transmembrane permeation offers a viable framework for understanding ion behaviors in membranes (24, 25), explaining ion permeation in terms of activation energy (enthalpy related) and the pre-exponential factor (entropy related) as described by the Arrhenius equation (26). While TST studies have partially addressed the role of dehydration (i.e., shedding the surrounded water molecules) in enabling ions to fit into the confined membrane pore during partitioning (2, 27, 28), they have not fully explored the contributions of membrane surface charge and related factors to ion partitioning. The lack of a systematic approach for customizing membrane surface charge complicates the isolation and understanding of surface charge effects. Consequently, the precise role of membrane charge in mediating ion selectivity, particularly with respect to the transport behaviors and activation energies of ions with similar properties, remains unclear. Therefore, a focused investigation into the effect of membrane charge on ion transport is pressingly needed.

In this study, we engineered multilayers of Al2O3 nanoparticles on a porous substrate to develop steric hindrance–free membranes with customized surface charge densities (SCDs) via atomic layer deposition (ALD). Three alkali cations—Li+, K+, and Cs+—were selected as model ions because of their identical charges and minimal variations in hydrated size, enabling an investigation of their transport through the membranes. Pore sizes were controlled to above 5 nm to minimize steric hindrance effects on ion transport. Ionic permeability, activation energies, and partition energies were analyzed within the TST framework to systematically examine the migration behaviors of the alkali cations and corresponding anion (Cl−) through membranes with different SCDs. The effect of membrane SCDs on the hydration state of transport ions was evaluated by means of both experiments and simulations. Our findings reveal that electrostatic-driven dehydration of counterions, relative to membrane charge, plays a crucial role in membrane ion selectivity.

RESULTS

Fabrication of steric hindrance–free nanoporous membranes on AAO substrates

The ALD technique was used to design the steric hindrance–free nanoporous membranes on anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) substrates to explore the influence of membrane surface charge on selective ion transport. Specifically, heated trimethylaluminum (TMA) steam and H2O were successively introduced in one deposition cycle to deposit the amorphous Al2O3 layer on AAO membranes (fig. S1), as shown in Fig. 1A (29). As the number of cycles increases, the deposited Al2O3 forms a hexagonal pore wall around the AAO membrane pores and gradually covers the pores (Fig. 1B) (30). AAO membranes with a 20-nm pore diameter that underwent 50, 100, and 150 ALD cycles of Al2O3 (Fig. 1C and fig. S2) and pristine AAO membranes with 20-, 50-, and 80-nm pore diameters (Fig. 1D), named as ALD50@AAO-20, ALD100@AAO-20, and ALD150@AAO-20 and AAO-20, AAO-50, and AAO-80, respectively, were prepared. Under the optimized conditions, the deposited Al2O3 primarily accumulates on the substrate surface, with minimal penetration into the pore structure (<1% of the substrate thickness), as shown in fig. S3. Fitting by the x-ray reflection curves, we verify the high precision and controllability of the ALD technique (Fig. 1E and table S1). When the number of deposition cycles reaches 150, however, the membrane pores become fully blocked, preventing the passage of ions through the membrane (fig. S4). In contrast, other ALD membranes exhibit pore sizes exceeding 5 nm (Fig. 1F). Notably, the deposited Al2O3 imparts amphoteric characteristics to the ALD membranes, exhibiting an isoelectric point of about 8.5 (Fig. 1G). This dissociation feature is comparable to that of the pristine AAO substrates, albeit with a higher surface-charged capacity (31, 32). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy confirmed the shift of the O peaks to lower binding energy in the membranes, from AAO-80 to ALD100@AAO-20, indicative of increased charged capacity on the surface of those membranes (33), as shown in Fig. 2A. Teorell-Meyer-Sievers theory was further used to assess the SCD of the membrane (34, 35) and the transport number for ions in the membrane by determining the membrane potential ( ΔΦmbr ). This potential is composed of the Donnan potential ( ΔΦdon ) at the membrane surface and the diffusion potential ( ΔΦdif ) within the membrane (Fig. 2B and notes S1 and S2). Figure 2C presents an example of the potential test for 0.1 M potassium chloride (KCl) transport across various membranes at pH 7 to calculate the SCDs of the nanoporous membranes. When the solution pH falls below the isoelectric point of the membrane, the membrane surface becomes positively charged because of the protonation of Al2O3, which can lead to the repulsion of cations. As pH decreases, protonation intensifies, thereby increasing SCDs, while an extreme pH environment may cause the blockage of membrane pores (fig. S5). Thus, we adjusted the solution pH to 6, 7, and 8 to tune the SCDs of fabricated membranes within the range of 2.1 to 56.6 mC/m2 and determine the ion transport numbers through those membranes (table S2 and fig. S6). Notice that the SCDs of ALD50@AAO-20 and ALD100@AAO-20 are similar at the corresponding pH despite having different pore sizes, as each cycle of ALD covers previous deposition.

SCD-mediated transmembrane transport properties of alkali cations in nanoporous membranes

To examine the effect of membrane surface charge on the transmembrane permeation of ions, the transport properties of alkali cations through the prepared membranes were assessed by measuring ionic fluxes for single-salt solutions of lithium chloride (LiCl), KCl, and cesium chloride (CsCl) using a concentration-driven diffusion experiment (note S3). The relationship between the ionic flux and SCDs of different membranes is summarized in Fig. 2D. Fluxes for the tested ion pairs (LiCl, KCl, and CsCl) decline with increasing SCDs as well as decreasing pore sizes, exhibiting varying transport rates, which are dependent on the hydrated radius and hydration free energy of the cations. Given that all three cations share the same anion (Cl−) in the solution, the differences in ion flux primarily stem from the cations, with a consistent transport order of Cs+ > K+ > Li+ observed across the membranes. However, although the flux trend of the ions remains constant, the ion selectivity of the membrane does not exhibit a monotonic trend. The K+/Cs+–to–Li+ selectivity in prepared membranes displays a “volcano-like” trend, characterized by an initial increase, plateaus, and a subsequent decline as a function of SCD (Fig. 2D). Given the negligible differences in Debye length under these experimental conditions (note S4), the observed trend underscores that the influence of membrane charge on the transport of similar ions is different. No significant differences in ion selectivity were observed between ALD50@AAO-20 and ALD100@AAO-20. These membranes have similar charge densities but differ in pore sizes. This observation suggests that changes in pore size alone may have a minor impact on the membrane ion selectivity when the pore is larger than the hydrated ion and that the SCD is the major factor influencing the transport. For clarity, three SCDs, 2.1 mC/m2 (AAO-80 at pH 8), 22.7 mC/m2 (AAO-20 at pH 7), and 56.6 mC/m2 (ALD100@AAO-20 at pH 6), were selected as representative SCDs, recorded as (+)SCD #1, (+)SCD #2, and (+)SCD #3 as shown in Fig. 2E. Electro-driven ion transport in these membranes confirmed a trend consistent with the diffusion experiments (note S5 and figs. S7 and S8).

Electrostatic interaction between the ion and the membrane surface influences the transport behavior of ions

The magnitude of the effective activation energy for ion transmembrane permeation depends on the extent of hindrance exerted during ion partitioning into the pores and intrapore diffusion (25). To elucidate the observed difference in the transport behavior of alkali cations across the membranes on the molecular level, we first investigated the effective activation energies ( Ea ) for the ion pairs (LiCl, KCl, and CsCl) in concentration-driven diffusion experiments (note S6). Ea for the transport of ion pairs in membranes with (+)SCD #1, (+)SCD #2, and (+)SCD #3 were determined using a linearized form of an Arrhenius-type equation (Fig. 3C and fig. S9). Differences in the Ea for various ion pairs across membranes with three SCDs exhibit trends consistent with their selectivity within these membranes (Fig. 3D). Specifically, the largest disparity in effective activation energies among the three ion pairs occurs in the membrane with (+)SCD #2, aligning with its highest ion selectivity. This suggests that factors directly associated with Ea , such as ion dehydration and electrostatic interactions between ions and the charged membranes, dominate the ion transmembrane transport as well as the membrane ion selectivity in the studied membranes (36). In contrast, the factors related to the pre-exponential factor ( Bc ) of the Arrhenius equation, such as the freedom of motion of the transport ions, are not the main influencing factors of membrane ion selectivity owing to the large pore size of the studied membranes (note S6).

Next, we explored the partition energy ( ΔEP ) for ion pairs because of its strong correlation with the electrostatic interactions between the membrane surface and ions based on the Van’t Hoff framework (Fig. 3C and fig. S10). According to previous work (Fig. 3A and note S6), a quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) was used to determine the ΔEP for ion pairs in membranes with representative SCDs (fig. S11) (28, 37). Nanoporous structures were directly fabricated on the chip, rather than attached, considering the poor interfacial compatibility between the AAO membrane and the chip. In situ fabrication ensured that the pore size and porosity of fabricated nanoporous structures matched those of the previously studied membranes (table S3), achieved via anodizing, secondary etching, and deposition techniques, as shown in Fig. 3B and fig. S12. Given the identical fabrication process, the SCDs of the three nanopore structures fabricated in situ are considered identical to the previously steric hindrance–free membranes with (+)SCD #1, (+)SCD #2, and (+)SCD #3. The calculated Van’t Hoff energies of partition for transport ion pairs are also associated with ion dehydration and electrostatic interactions. Significant variations in the energy were evident for ion pairs partitioning into the membranes with different SCDs (Fig. 3D). Specifically, membranes with (+)SCD #1 exhibited negligible partition energy for ion pairs. In contrast, membranes with (+)SCD #2 displayed a positive partition energy solely for LiCl, whereas membranes with (+)SCD #3 showed a positive partition energy for all ions. The observed difference indicated that the partitioning stage plays an important role during ion transmembrane transport, as shown in figs. S13 and S14.

At (+)SCD #1, the membrane surface minimally influences ions, permitting unhindered partitioning of ion pairs into the membrane and resulting in an almost zero partition energy (Fig. 3D) (6). With increasing SCD, electrostatic forces promote cation repulsion and anion attraction (38). Considering the similar characteristics between the hydrated Cl− and K+ (39), the partition energy of KCl tends to be closer to zero. Conversely, for LiCl, the high charge density coupled with the high hydration enthalpy of Li+ yields positive partition energy for the ion pairs (table S4) (40). The difference in energy awakened by electrostatic attraction and repulsion initiates a tunable process of ion partitioning at the surface of the membrane, ultimately influencing the transmembrane ion behaviors (41). However, as the SCD increases further to (+)SCD #3, all ion pairs face additional energy costs when partitioning into the membrane (Fig. 3D). This shift in partition energy from negative to positive for KCl and CsCl confirms that the Donnan exclusion picture remains valid even when charge localization is primarily at the membrane surface. This shift leads to a convergence of partition energy across different ion pairs and reduces the differences in transport behavior for alkali cations. However, whether the increase in surface charge leads to a sign reversal in the partition energy, indicating a change in the spontaneity of ion partitioning, remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

The charged membrane surface hinders counterions through their electrostatic-driven dehydration and impedes salt partitioning

In a previous study, it was shown that counterions in the solution can partially neutralize membrane surface charges (16). Specifically, counterions may adsorb to the membrane surface by replacing their hydration shell with oppositely charged surface groups, thereby affecting the membrane’s charge (42). This behavior, in turn, is bound to have a certain impact on the partitioning of ions themselves. Unlike metal electrodes, where charge originates from free electrons, membrane charge depends on the dissociation of surface functional groups. This results in a closer proximity of counterions [i.e., (H2O)nCl−] to the Helmholtz plane in the electric double layer on the membrane surface (43). Upon ions reaching the Helmholtz plane, their coordination water may be stripped away and reoriented toward the charged membrane surface rather than anchoring in the hydrated layer of ions (44). This reorientation can induce partial dehydration or deformation of hydrated ions within the electric double layer, with the intensity varying depending on the sequence and manner of ion population in the Helmholtz plane. In membranes with subnanometer pores, permeation ions may shed their surrounding water molecules in the hydration shell to some extent to fit in the confined membrane pores, leading to increased enthalpic barriers (45). Unlike ionic dehydration mediated by steric hindrance, which is energetically unfavorable, electrostatic-driven dehydration results in a reduction of the Gibbs free energy of the system (13). This mechanism represents a further energy compensation of the hydrated ion by the membrane surface charge, thereby promoting its stabilization in a lower energy state. Notably, such compensation may negatively affect ion partitioning. It is well known that specific binding sites for ions within the pores can promote ion partitioning into the membranes but impede diffusion (46), resulting in faster transmembrane transport for weakly bound ions, that is, the well-known trade-off between ion partition and diffusion (47). When the charged groups that can induce electrostatic-driven dehydration of counterions are predominantly located on the membrane surface, counterions may also be more likely to be hindered by the membrane surface rather than partitioned into the pores. It is well known that the partition of ions is described by the equilibrium constant K, quantifying the concentration ratio between ions in the membrane and the solution at thermodynamic equilibrium. For counterions, adsorption onto the membrane surface is required as an initial step, followed by entry into the membrane pore to complete their partition. Therefore, the partition of counterions is contingent upon the equilibrium constants associated with these two successive processes (defined as K1 and K2). While dense groupings on the membrane surface may activate the energy compensation that facilitates counterion absorption (electrostatic attraction can also do that) (48), the extensive membrane surface area introduces a huge penalty of too many stable binding sites for adsorbed counterions to enter the pore (15). As a result, the counterions fall into the “trap” of compensation on the membrane surface. Because of Donnan equilibrium, the hindered counterions, in turn, impede the cotransport of co-ions (49, 50) and are reflected in the increased partition energy.

Molecular dynamics simulations validate the electrostatic-driven dehydration phenomena of counterions at the membrane surface under experimental conditions (Fig. 4A and note S7). Specific SCDs, (+)SCD #1 to (+)SCD #3, (+)SCD #4 (115.5 mC/m2), and (+)SCD #5 (160.0 mC/m2), were selected to analyze ion behaviors on the membrane surface with varying SCDs. Considering that cations hardly influence the dehydration of anions, a KCl solution serves as the case study. Counterions Cl− accumulate within the Helmholtz plane located ~5 Å from the membrane surface, migrating progressively toward the inner Helmholtz plane with increasing SCDs (fig. S15). As the SCD increases, there is a notable augmentation in the likelihood of hydrated counterions losing the coordinating water molecules and being hindered by the charged membrane surface. We then calculated the radial distribution function (RDF) of water molecules bound to Cl− and K+ at the surface of membranes with different (+)SCDs to further understand the behavior of ions (fig. S16). At (+)SCD #3, Cl− on the membrane surface loses an average of 0.49 coordinating water molecules in the first hydration layer compared to 0.14 at (+)SCD #2 (Fig. 4B). The further dehydration of Cl− at the membrane surface confirms the increased partition energy of ion pairs of about 2.5 kcal/mol, as shown in Fig. 3D. When the SCD increases to 160.0 mC/m2, the loss of coordination water for Cl− rises to 1.26, while the hydration number of K+ remains largely unaffected.

To further confirm the influence of electrostatic-driven dehydration on the transport behavior of ions, we altered the surface charge of the membranes from positive to negative by adjusting the solution pH to 9. Consequently, the counterions associated with the membrane changed to three alkali cations instead of Cl−. SCDs of AAO-80, AAO-20, and ALD100@AAO-20 under solution pH were determined, recorded as (−)SCD #1 (−2.5 mC/m2), (−)SCD #2 (−22.3 mC/m2), and (−)SCD #3 (−52.9 mC/m2), as shown in Fig. 5A. Molecular dynamics simulations supported the electrostatic-driven dehydration of cations at the highly negatively charged membrane surface with (−)SCD #3, as shown in Fig. 5B. As we expected, cations with different hydration energies exhibit varying tendencies to dehydrate and be hindered by the charged membrane surface (Fig. 5C) (51). Ions having low hydration energies, such as Cs+, are preferentially dehydrated at the highly negatively charged membrane surface, losing about 0.8 coordination water molecules. In contrast, strongly hydrated cations, such as Li+, can directly partition into the membrane in a fully hydrated state, with almost no loss of coordination water. This suggests that Li+ may have stronger partitioning than Cs+ in the membrane with (−)SCD #3. Correspondingly, no cation dehydration occurs at the surface of a positively charged membrane with a charge density of 52.9 mC/m2 (fig. S17). Concentration-driven diffusion experiments for single-salt solutions LiCl, KCl, and CsCl in membranes with (−)SCD #1 to (−)SCD #3 support the proposed mechanisms (Fig. 5D). More negative SCDs decrease the fluxes for the tested ion pairs (LiCl, KCl, and CsCl). The observed selectivity of K+ and Cs+ toward Li+ in these membranes displayed a “volcano-like” trend relative to the SCD of the membrane (fig. S18), which the electrostatic-driven dehydration of cations can explain. In the membrane with (−)SCD #3, the ion flux of KCl exceeds that of CsCl, suggesting the faster transmembrane transport of K+ compared to Cs+, confirming that counterions with varying hydration energies encounter different levels of partitioning hindrance at the membrane surface with high SCDs. For ions with lower hydration energy, electrostatic-driven dehydration facilitates their preferential accumulation and hindrance at the membrane surface. As a result, ions with lower hydration energy demonstrate higher partition energy compared to ions with higher hydration energy, as shown in Fig. 5E. The partition energies for the transport of LiCl, KCl, and CsCl in membranes with (−)SCD #3 further support the conclusion (fig. S19). The observed inversion of ion selectivity may suggest that electrostatic-driven dehydration of counterions influences even the rate-limiting step during transmembrane ion transport. Nonetheless, only ions with markedly different hydration energies can surpass the inherent selectivity of diffusion (fig. S20). Last, we investigated the impact of electrostatic-driven dehydration of counterions on ion transmembrane transport within mixed-salt solutions (fig. S21). As the difference in ion transport behavior is less distinct in mixed-salt solutions compared to single-salt solutions, the data imply that coexisting ions may shield the membrane charge, thereby reducing its ion separation capability. Still, the electrostatic-driven dehydration can influence ion behavior in multicomponent electrolyte systems, as evidenced by the highest ion selectivity observed at (±)SCD #2.

DISCUSSION

A comprehensive understanding of the impact of membrane surface charge on ion transport is essential for advancing ion-specific selectivity in membrane technologies. This study elucidates how ion transport behavior in steric hindrance–free nanoporous membranes varies with SCDs. Our findings highlight the critical role of electrostatic interactions, mediated by membrane surface charge, in the dehydration of counterions, thereby notably affecting ion partitioning. We also demonstrate that the hydration energy of different counterions leads to distinct interactions with the membrane surface, as illustrated in Fig. 5E. Specifically, Cs+, with its softer hydration shell and lower hydration enthalpy, is more readily dehydrated and hindered in its partitioning at the negatively charged membrane surface compared to Li+. These mechanisms are applicable to other charged membranes, such as NF and graphene-based membranes (table S5). These enhance our understanding of atypical ion transport behaviors and propose a potential framework for integrating electrostatic-driven dehydration into existing theories of ion transport across membranes.

The pre-exponential factors in the Arrhenius equation for the activation energy of salt permeation and the Van’t Hoff equation for energy change in salt partition are related to the entropy of the respective processes. In our steric hindrance–free membranes, salts exhibiting higher partition energy have larger pre-exponential factors, reflecting greater entropy-related freedom of motion, as shown in table S6. This finding suggests that the enthalpy-entropy compensation theory, previously applied to salt-rejecting membranes, can be extended to membranes with larger pore sizes (36, 52). Variations in ion “stickiness” or translational freedom on the membrane surface may explain the observed differences in entropy (53). A comprehensive application of TST is expected to further deepen our understanding of electrostatic-driven ion dehydration. In addition, the Eyring equation, which integrates kinetic theory and thermodynamics, can be used to analyze the free energy associated with ion permeation, offering further insights into the entropy and enthalpy energy barriers.

The electrostatic-driven dehydration of ions at the membrane surface is intertwined with other factors. When the membrane’s SCD reaches a threshold that induces strong dehydration of counterions, the partitioning of weakly hydrated ions into steric hindrance–free nanoporous membranes is preferentially restricted. In contrast, strongly hydrated ions remain largely unaffected by steric hindrance, even in the absence of dehydration. However, ion behavior can differ substantially in practical applications involving ion-selective membranes with subnanometer pores. Regardless of electrostatic effects, ions must undergo sufficient dehydration to fit the confined pores. Therefore, electrostatic-driven ion dehydration at the membrane surface adds complexity to ion partitioning, with transmembrane transport governed by the interplay between electrostatic- and steric-driven dehydration. Furthermore, the presence of specific coordination functional groups on the membrane surface may also influence ion behavior during transmembrane transport.

Enhancing membrane ion selectivity necessitates precise optimization of both pore size and membrane charge for specific applications (54). For instance, raising the membrane’s SCD may promote the selective separation of ions disadvantaged by dehydration. However, this is constrained by the limited number of charged groups available on the membrane surface. Furthermore, increasing the SCD can reduce the ion flux (55), necessitating a balance between these factors to optimize membrane performance for practical applications (56, 57). The selective recovery of valuable ions, such as Li+, from brines is of substantial practical interest. Augmenting the SCD of a negatively charged membrane with subnanometer pores may reduce Na+/Li+ selectivity by promoting the electrostatic-driven dehydration and partitioning impediment of Na+, thus enhancing the recovery of the target ions. Furthermore, effective ion separation based on charge effects requires membranes with uniform, precisely engineered pores and narrow pore size distributions or strategic combinations of membranes with different pore characteristics. If reduced ion flux is acceptable, optimizing target ion selectivity can be achieved through electro- or concentration-driven filtration processes (58, 59).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

AAO substrates (pore diameters of 20, 50, and 80 nm according to the statistical calculation from the top surface images) were purchased from Hefei Pu-Yuan Nano Technology Co., Ltd. (China). LiCl (≥99%), NaCl (sodium chloride; ≥99.5%), and KCl (≥99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. RbCl (rubidium chloride; ≥99.5%) and CsCl (≥99.9%) were purchased from Meryer. All solutions were prepared with Milli-Q water (18.2 megohms·cm). The solution pH was adjusted with 0.1 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) and alkali metal hydroxide (XOH), where X represents the corresponding alkali metal ion.

Al2O3 ALD

A Beneq ALD reactor (TFS200-106, Finland) was used to deposit Al2O3 on the AAO substrates. Before deposition, the AAO substrates were ultrasonically cleaned in ultrapure water and ethanol to ensure surface cleaning. One cycle deposition of a single atomic Al2O3 layer in the reaction chamber goes through the following steps: (i) pulse infusing heated TMA steam in 0.15 s to react and form Al(CH3)(1-2)* on the AAO surface and release gaseous CH4, (ii) purging the reaction chamber using the carrier gas nitrogen (N2) in 2 s to remove the unreacted TMA and CH4, (iii) pulse infusing H2O in 0.15 s to transform Al(CH3)(1-2)* to Al(OH)(1-2)* and release gaseous CH4, and (iv) purging the reaction chamber using the carrier gas nitrogen (N2) again in 2 s to remove the unreacted H2O and CH4 (29, 60). The ALD process was conducted at a chamber temperature of 200°C with a nitrogen purge flow rate of 0.2 liters/min, repeated 50 to 150 times for the cycle to obtain the Al2O3 ALD membrane for the experiment.

Material characterization

The scanning electron microscope GEMINISEM 500 (Zeiss, China) was used for the morphology characterization of AAO membranes before and after ALD modification, and ImageJ was used to calculate the average diameter of the prepared pore combined with the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (averaged from more than three measurements). Specifically, SEM images were first converted to an 8-bit grayscale format, after which the threshold was adjusted to identify the positions and dimensions of membrane pores accurately. Subsequently, numerical analysis software was used to statistically calculate the porosity and pore diameters (fig. S22). The thickness of the Al2O3 ALD layer was characterized by film x-ray reflection at room temperature using a D8 Discover instrument (Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. The x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy instrument ESCALAB XI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) was used to characterize the binding energy and elemental composition of elements Al and O for the prepared membranes. The zeta potential was characterized by Delsa Nano C (Beckman Colter, US).

In situ fabrication of nanoporous membranes on the QCM chip

The in situ fabrication of nanoporous membranes on the QCM chip (area of 1.13 cm2) followed the sputtering, anodizing, etching, and deposition steps. Considering the consumption of aluminum in the electrochemical polishing process, 10-μm Al was sputtered on a bare QCM sensor with a 5-MHz fundamental frequency by using dc magnetron sputtering. After polishing in the ethanol-perchloric acid solution (4:1) for 4 min at 5°C and 15 V as for the anode, the sensor was anodized in 0.3 M oxalic acid with a 25-V applied potential. The pore size after primary oxidation is about 20 nm. To obtain the membrane structure with an average pore size of 80 nm on the sensor, the sensor was immersed in 5 wt % phosphoric acid for 60 min for secondary etching.
