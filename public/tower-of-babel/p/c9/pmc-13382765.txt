Association between regular hypnotic use and age-related macular degeneration
Authors: Jianqi Chen, Shu Tu, Yingting Zhu, Zhidong Li, Xuhao Chen, Xinyue Shen, Shuifeng Deng, Yehong Zhuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have confirmed the presence of benzodiazepine receptors in the retina, and exploratory research has suggested that the use of hypnotics may be linked to changes in retinal function and an increased risk of maculopathy. This indicates that regular use of hypnotic medications is likely associated with age-related macular degeneration (AMD). However, the precise relationship remains largely unclear. In this study, we aimed to investigate this association in the UK Biobank.MethodsRegular use of hypnotics, including benzodiazepines or Z-meds (non-benzodiazepine hypnotics such as zolpidem and zaleplon) were assessed at baseline. Nearest-neighbor propensity score matchings (PSM) were implemented to control for potential confounding variables. Cox proportional hazards regression with robust variance estimation was utilized to calculate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, systemic, and lifestyle variables. Sensitivity analyses were performed using traditional Cox proportional hazards regression on the original cohort, with additional subgroup analyses stratified by age group and sex.ResultsFollowing the PSM procedure, 1899 benzodiazepine users and 7557 non-users were selected from the benzodiazepines cohort, and 321 Z-meds users and 1284 non-users were selected from the Z-meds cohort. A total of 262 participants in the benzodiazepines cohort developed incident AMD during follow-up after PSM, compared with 49 participants in the Z-meds cohort. Upon adjusting for covariates, the prolonged use of Z-meds was associated with an elevated AMD risk (HR: 2.15, 95% CI: 1.23−3.74, P=0.007), whereas no significant association was observed for the regular use of benzodiazepines (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.73−1.30, P=0.854). Sensitivity analyses demonstrated consistent associations. Subgroup analyses also indicated consistent associations across various categories.ConclusionsProlonged use of Z-meds may elevate the risk of developing AMD. Given the increasing prescription of Z-meds for the treatment of insomnia, these findings underscore the necessity of considering potential ocular risks and requiring more frequent monitoring.

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) represents the primary cause of vision impairment among older adults, affecting approximately 20%−30% of individuals aged 75 years and older in developed nations.1 This condition involves the degeneration of the macula, a critical component for acute central vision, color discrimination, and detailed visual tasks. Consequently, AMD considerably impedes daily activities, rendering tasks that require clear vision more challenging. With global cases anticipated to reach 288 million by 2040 due to aging populations and increased life expectancies, the burden of AMD is projected to rise significantly.2, 3, 4

AMD is a multifactorial condition characterized by the accumulation of drusen between the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and Bruch's membrane, resulting from RPE dysfunction, as well as choroidal neovascularization and geographic atrophy.5 Research has established a correlation between inadequate sleep duration and the AMD, supported by evidence from cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, and Mendelian randomization analyses.6,7 Insomnia treatment frequently involves the use of hypnotic medications.8 Data from Public Health England reveal that approximately 2% of adults in England were prescribed Z-meds and 3% were prescribed benzodiazepines, which are the two most prevalent hypnotics in clinical practice.9 Currently, there is a growing concern regarding the misuse, dependency, and safety of these hypnotic agents. Although effective in addressing sleep disturbances, hypnotics have been associated with an elevated risk of health issues, such as cardiovascular diseases and dementia.10,11

Previous investigations have identified the presence of benzodiazepine receptors in the retina, predominantly located in the inner plexiform layer, as well as in the inner nuclear and ganglion cell layers.12 Additionally, high concentrations of non-specific benzodiazepine binding sites have also been detected in the retinal pigment epithelium and retinal vascular structures.12 These receptors potentiate the effects of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), a principal inhibitory neurotransmitter, by enhancing GABA receptor sensitivity. Specific hypnotic agents, such as benzodiazepines, have been reported to be associated with the development of maculopathy.13 This evidence suggests that the use of hypnotics may be linked to alterations in the retina and potentially increase the risk of retinal diseases, particularly AMD. However, to date, no large-scale cohort study has investigated the association between regular hypnotic use and AMD. In this study, we utilized data from the UK Biobank to examine the association between AMD and the use of hypnotics, including benzodiazepines and Z-meds.

Methods

Study design and sample

The UK Biobank constitutes a comprehensive cohort study that has enrolled over 500000 participants across various assessment centers in England, Scotland, and Wales. It systematically collects an extensive array of data, encompassing sociodemographic information, health behaviors, medical histories, physical measurements, and biological specimens. The study has obtained ethical approval from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (Reference: 21/NW/0157), in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent has been obtained from all participants.

Assessment of use of hypnotic agents

The use of hypnotic agents was evaluated at baseline. Only regular prescription medications were documented, while short-term medications were excluded. Hypnotic agents were further classified into Z-meds (including zolpidem and zaleplon) and benzodiazepines (including lorazepam, alprazolam, diazepam, triazolam, clonazepam, flunitrazepam).11 Participants using benzodiazepines were excluded from the analysis investigating the association between Z-meds and AMD. Similarly, participants using Z-meds were excluded from the analysis investigating the association between benzodiazepines and AMD. Assessment of AMD

In this study, the primary outcome of interest was AMD, identified using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 code (H35.3) and ICD-9 code (3625). The follow-up period extended from the completion of the baseline assessment by participants until the first occurrence of AMD, death, or censoring.

Assessment of covariates

In the analysis of the association between regular hypnotic use and AMD, a range of covariates were considered. These encompassed demographic variables (age, sex, and ethnicity), socioeconomic indicators (Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI)), systemic factors (body mass index (BMI)), lifestyle variables (smoking status (never, former, or current), drinking status (never, former, or current), healthy diet score, and sleep score patterns), and comorbidity (diabetes, hypertension, depression, anxiety, dementia, and epilepsy). The TDI is an established indicator of socioeconomic status in UK populations, with higher scores indicating a lower socioeconomic status.14 The healthy diet score was derived from seven components outlined in the most recent U.S. Dietary Guidelines, with higher scores reflecting a healthier diet.15 Based on established methodologies, five sleep-related factors (chronotype, sleep duration, insomnia, snoring, and daytime sleepiness) were combined to construct a healthy sleep score, with each low-risk behavior assigned 1 point, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 5, where higher scores indicate healthier sleep patterns.16 The sleep score pattern was categorized as healthy (≥4), intermediate (2–3), or poor (≤1) according to the total score.16 The covariates included in this study exhibited low rates of missing data, with all covariates being less than 5%. To address the issue of missing covariates, we employed multiple imputation using the mice package in R, conducting five iterations as the default procedure.

Statistical analysis

To balance the baseline characteristics of hypnotic users and non-users, a nearest-neighbor propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted to account for potential confounders between hypnotic use and AMD. Participants utilizing hypnotic agents were designated as the exposure group. Given the relatively small number of Z-med users, we selected a 1:4 nearest-neighbor matching ratio to maximize statistical efficiency and improve estimate precision. The standardized mean difference (SMD) was utilized to assess the distribution of baseline characteristics between the groups and to detect any imbalances.17 An absolute SMD value exceeding 0.2 indicates an imbalance, whereas an SMD of 0.2 or less denotes adequate balance, with values below 0.1 indicating excellent balance.17 All covariates were considered in the PSM process, employing a caliper size of 0.2.18

Covariates were evaluated both before and after PSM. Variables following a normal distribution, as determined by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, were reported as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the t-test. Variables not adhering to a normal distribution were presented as median (interquartile range (IQR)) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages) and compared using the Chi-square (χ2) test.

To evaluate the relationship between the use of hypnotics and the risk of AMD, cox proportional hazards regression with robust variance estimation were employed. This approach was selected due to the use of PSM to balance covariates and mitigate confounding bias. PSM excludes unmatched samples, thereby reducing the sample size and introducing correlation within matched pairs. Cox proportional hazards regression with robust variance estimation was employed to account for these issues. Three models were developed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 included adjustments for age, sex, and ethnicity; and Model 3 incorporated further adjustments for all covariates. Additionally, sensitivity analysis was also performed, using traditional Cox proportional hazards regression based on the original cohort data (Fig. 1). Subgroup analyses for Z-meds and benzodiazepines were conducted stratified by age and sex to explore potential interaction effects, with all other covariates included in the fully adjusted models.

Data analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.0) (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-sided. To account for the two primary exposure comparisons (benzodiazepines and Z-meds), a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of P<0.025 (0.05/2) was applied to the analyses. For subgroup analyses, results were interpreted in an exploratory manner using conventional two-sided P values.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the UKB cohort

Among the 500912 participants in the UKB benzodiazepines cohort, 1904 were identified as regular users of benzodiazepine agents, while 499008 were non-users. Similarly, within the 499329 participants in the UKB Z-meds cohort, 321 were regular users of Z-meds agents, and 499008 were non-users. Regular users of both benzodiazepines and Z-meds were predominantly older, female, and of lower socioeconomic status, with higher rates of obesity and current smoking, but were less likely to consume alcohol. They also had a higher prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, depression, anxiety, and dementia; benzodiazepine users were additionally more likely to report poor sleep and have a diagnosis of epilepsy (Table 1, Table 2). Association between hypnotic use and AMD

In the analysis of benzodiazepine use, no significant association with AMD was observed when compared to non-users. In the initial unadjusted model, the hazard ratio (HR) was 0.94 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.70−1.26, P=0.667). After adjusting for demographic variables in model 2, the HR slightly decreased to 0.95 (95% CI: 0.71−1.28, P=0.750). Further comprehensive adjustments in model 3 yielded similar results, with an HR of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.73−1.30, P=0.854) (Table 3). Conversely, the use of Z-meds was associated with an increased risk of AMD compared to non-users. In the initial unadjusted model, the HR was 2.02 (95% CI: 1.16−3.52, P=0.014). Upon adjusting for demographic variables in model 2, the HR was slightly adjusted to 2.04 (95% CI: 1.17−3.56, P=0.012). Further comprehensive adjustments in model 3 produced comparable results, with an HR of 2.15 (95% CI: 1.23−3.74, P=0.007) (Table 3). The Kaplan–Meier curves in Fig. 2 illustrate the association between benzodiazepine and Z-med use and the incidence of AMD after PSM. The log-rank test showed that Z-med users had a significantly higher incidence of AMD compared with non-users (HR: 2.02, 95% CI: 1.11−3.67, log-rank P=0.019). Discussion

In this cohort study, we identified the varied effects of different categories of hypnotics on AMD. Overall, the use of benzodiazepines did not elevate the risk of AMD. However, evidence has raised safety concerns regarding the use of Z-meds in relation to AMD.

The pathways through which hypnotic agents might influence the risk of AMD are likely to be complex and multifactorial. Activation of GABA-A receptors is known to induce relaxation of arterial smooth muscle, leading to vasodilation and a subsequent reduction in blood pressure.19 Such vascular effects could theoretically influence ocular perfusion. Previous epidemiologic studies, including the Beaver Dam Eye Study, have reported associations between vasodilator use and incident AMD20; however, whether GABA-A–mediated vascular changes translate into clinically meaningful effects on AMD risk remains uncertain. Additionally, GABA-A receptor–coupled anion channels regulate chloride influx and neuronal excitability. Under conditions of excitotoxic imbalance, altered GABAergic signaling may influence neuronal vulnerability, potentially contributing to retinal neurodegeneration, including AMD.21 Although oxidative stress is a central component of AMD pathogenesis, there is currently no direct evidence demonstrating that GABA-A–related effects are mediated specifically through oxidative stress mechanisms.22 Rather, GABAergic alterations may interact with excitotoxic and inflammatory pathways, which themselves are closely intertwined with oxidative stress in the degenerative cascade.22

The observed differences between benzodiazepines and Z-meds may reflect their distinct pharmacologic profiles at the GABA-A receptor, although this interpretation remains speculative. Z-meds demonstrate a high selectivity for the α1 subunit of the GABA-A receptor, which primarily mediates sedative effects. This targeted interaction not only enhances the specificity of their action and potentially raises the risk of AMD, but also significantly reduces the likelihood of tolerance development, thereby potentially maintaining the receptor's effects during regular use.23 In contrast, benzodiazepines interact with a broader range of receptors and, with regular use, are associated with GABA-A receptor down-regulation and tolerance, potentially reducing their risk effect.24 However, there is no direct evidence linking these pharmacodynamic differences to AMD development at present, and further mechanistic studies are needed.

While both classes of drugs may cause side effects, benzodiazepines are associated with a greater risk of systemic complications. These include cardiovascular conditions such as coronary artery disease, heart failure, and increased cardiovascular mortality.11 Such adverse outcomes may be mediated by tolerance-related activation of the sympathetic nervous system, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.11 Additionally, benzodiazepines may lead to sleep apnea syndrome, rebound insomnia, withdrawal symptoms, and the development of tolerance and potential misuse.25 In contrast to benzodiazepines, Z-meds tend to preserve the natural structure of sleep and promote sleep patterns that closely resemble those of normal physiological rest.26 Z-meds have demonstrated superior efficacy in enhancing patients' sense of restfulness upon waking, improving daytime functioning, and increasing total sleep duration.26 They are associated with a reduced risk of adverse effects, particularly concerning daytime drowsiness, tolerance, dependence, and addiction.26 In addition, Z-meds are considered to pose fewer safety concerns for older adults.26 In recent decades, Z-meds have increasingly emerged as the preferred treatment for managing insomnia, with prescription rates progressively rising in clinical settings.26 However, our findings indicate that regular use of Z-meds is significantly associated with an elevated risk of AMD. This suggests that individuals taking these medications may require more frequent monitoring.27 Accordingly, closer ophthalmic monitoring, potentially including modalities such as color fundus photography or optical coherence tomography, may be considered in such populations.27

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first investigation into the relationship between hypnotic agents and AMD within such a large cohort. The study possesses several notable strengths that enhance its validity. Firstly, the study utilized PSM to simulate a randomized controlled trial in the comparison of treatment and control groups.28 This approach effectively reduced the influence of confounding variables, thereby facilitating a more precise evaluation of the impact of hypnotic agents on AMD. Secondly, the study utilized data from the UK Biobank, a large-scale and ongoing cohort study. Through a longitudinal cohort approach, this study effectively evaluated different hypnotic agents and their longitudinal association with AMD, providing crucial insights into which agent warrants further investigation.

However, this study has some limitations. First, exposure to hypnotic medications was assessed only at baseline based on self-reported regular prescription use. The UK Biobank does not provide information on duration of prior use, cumulative dose, adherence, or changes in medication use during follow-up. Therefore, we were unable to evaluate time-varying exposure or cumulative effects. Misclassification of exposure is also possible. However, such misclassification is likely to be non-differential with respect to incident AMD and would generally bias the estimated associations toward the null. As a result, the observed association between Z-med use and AMD risk may represent a conservative estimate. Future studies incorporating time-varying exposure data are warranted to further clarify this relationship. Second, AMD was identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes from linked healthcare records without detailed ophthalmologic examination data or disease staging information. As a result, early or subclinical cases may have been under-detected. In addition, differential detection bias cannot be completely excluded, as individuals using hypnotic medications may have more frequent healthcare encounters. Nevertheless, AMD diagnosis in the UK typically requires specialist ophthalmologic assessment rather than routine primary care evaluation. Although we adjusted for relevant comorbidities, residual bias related to healthcare utilization may remain.29 Third, we did not implement participant selection based on baseline sleep conditions, but included sleep conditions as a covariate to encompass individuals exhibiting both favorable and unfavorable responses to hypnotics, thereby minimizing selection bias. Nonetheless, this strategy may have introduced variability in the baseline sleep quality. Fourth, although we considered a series of covariates and utilized PSM to balance the baseline characteristics, residual confounding may still persist. Furthermore, the results are derived from the UK Biobank cohort, which is known to be affected by healthy volunteer selection bias.29 Participants tend to be healthier and more health-conscious than the general population, and the cohort has limited ethnic diversity. In addition, prescribing patterns and healthcare utilization within the UK setting may differ from other healthcare systems, which may limit the generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, our findings suggest differential associations between Z-meds and benzodiazepines in relation to AMD risk. While no significant association was observed for benzodiazepine use, Z-med use was associated with an increased risk of incident AMD in this cohort. Given the observational nature of the study and the potential for residual confounding, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Further prospective and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the underlying relationship and its clinical relevance.
