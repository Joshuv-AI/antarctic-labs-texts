Repurposing rosmarinic acid as an anti-colorectal cancer agent through bolstering T cell anti-tumor immunity by enhancing activation of MEK1-mediated TCR signaling
Authors: Zhengchun Kang, Xu Li, Xiuzhu Ma, Feihu Yan, Zhen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractActivation of the T cell receptor (TCR) complex is fundamental to initiating adaptive immune responses, particularly in CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes that mediate anti-tumor immunity. However, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment often impairs TCR signaling, limiting the efficacy of T cell-based cancer immunotherapies. Here, we report the identification of rosmarinic acid (RA), a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound, as a potent small molecule enhancer of TCR signaling. RA significantly augments IL-2 and IFN-γ production, promotes T cell proliferation, and enhances cytotoxicity of CD8⁺ T cells in vitro. Mechanistically, RA directly binds to MEK1, a key kinase in the MAPK/ERK pathway, with high affinity, leading to reduced MEK1 phosphorylation and downstream signaling activation. Transcriptomic and metabolic profiling of RA-treated CD8⁺ T cells revealed upregulation of genes associated with TCR signaling, calcium flux, PPAR signaling, and oxidative phosphorylation, indicating a broad remodeling of T cell effector function and metabolism. In vivo, RA treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth, increased tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) frequency, and improved survival in MC38 tumor-bearing mice. These effects were abrogated upon CD8⁺ T cell depletion, confirming their central role. Furthermore, RA synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy and enhanced the efficacy of adoptively transferred OT-I T cells in colorectal tumor-bearing hosts. Collectively, our findings reveal RA as a novel immunomodulatory agent that boosts CD8⁺ T cell responses via MEK1-mediated TCR signaling enhancement, providing a promising strategy for drug repurposing in cancer immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04366-3.Keywords: Rosmarinic acid, T cell receptor, MEK1, Cancer immunotherapy, Drug repurposing

Introduction

In recent years, substantial progress has been made in developing immunotherapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing anti-tumor immunity. Among immune cells, CD8+ T cells are central to mediate effective anti-tumor responses [1]. However, the function is often compromised in the tumor microenvironment (TME), where tumors exploit various mechanisms to evade immune detection. Cancer immunotherapy aims to counteract these suppressive effects and restore T cell activity [2–4]. The goal of cancer immunotherapy is to counteract this suppression by restoring or amplifying the CD8+ T cells functions within the tumor setting [5, 6]. The CD8+ T cells activation relies heavily on T cell receptor (TCR) signaling initiation and propagation, which occurs when TCRs engage with specific antigenic ligands—molecules or compounds that trigger an immune response [7, 8]. Previous research has shown that antigenic stimulation is a critical factor in regulating TCR activation, which can be quantitatively assessed through the production of interleukin-2 (IL-2) [9–11]. However, pharmacologic strategies to safely and consistently enhance TCR signaling remain limited.

Given the high costs and long timelines of de novo drug development, drug repurposing has become an attractive strategy in cancer therapy [12]. The MAPK (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase) pathway operates downstream of the TCR, loss of key components such as MAP3K2 or MAPK1/3 diminishes IL-2 production and blunts T cell activation. Although MEK is a central kinase within this cascade, its precise contribution to T cell effector function and differentiation remains incompletely defined [13]. Despite the role of MEK in T cell functions and differentiation [14], many cancer patients may have already developed an initial harbor pre-existing T cell response for a long time before discerning the disease diagnosis, these T cell populations are probably crucial factor in designing effective immunotherapies. Furthermore, MEK/ERK signaling supports TCR-driven activation and IL-2 production, several studies indicate that rosmarinic acid (RA) can suppress proximal TCR–MAPK signaling in T cells (e.g., PLCγ1/Itk/Lck interference) and dampen CD8⁺ T cell responses under certain conditions [15–17]. These data suggest that RA’s impact on the MAPK axis is dose and context dependent, particularly with the state of T cell and microenvironment.

RA is well-known for its anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor properties, yet its role in T cell function remains underexplored [18, 19]. Most previous studies have focused on the direct tumoricidal effects of small molecules, with little attention to their potential immunomodulatory roles. In this study, we performed a screen of natural product libraries and identified rosmarinic acid (RA) as a compound that significantly enhances T cell activation and CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor immunity. RA is well-known for its anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor properties, yet its role in T cell function remains underexplored.

RA suppresses the tumor growth and increases TILs infiltration in MC38 mouse model

To investigate the therapeutic potential of RA in vivo, we administered RA or vehicle to MC38 tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice. RA treatment led to a marked reduction in tumor volume and weight compared to vehicle mice (Fig. 1a–d). We profiled tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) by flow cytometry. Within live CD45⁺ leukocytes, RA treatment significantly increased the frequency of CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 1e), accompanied by a parallel rise in the CD4⁺ T cell compartment when normalized to tumor mass (Fig. 1f). Consistently, the absolute number of CD8⁺ T cells per gram of tumor was markedly elevated in RA-treated mice (Fig. 1g), indicating a broad expansion of effector T cells. Furthermore, intracellular cytokine staining plots show a pronounced increase in IFN-γ–producing CD8⁺ T cells in tumors from RA-treated mice (Fig. 1h). A higher proportion of IFN-γ⁺ cells among CD8⁺ T cells in tumors from RA-treated mice (Fig. 1i), together with an increase in their abundance when normalized to tumor weight (Fig. 1j). Representative flow plots demonstrate an expansion of granzyme B (GzmB)–expressing CD8⁺ T cells with RA (Fig. 1k). This was reflected by a significant rise in the percentage of GzmB⁺ cells among CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 1l) and an increase in the absolute number of CD8⁺GzmB⁺ cells per gram of tumor (Fig. 1m). Collectively, these data indicate that RA not only increases intratumoral T cell infiltration (CD4⁺ and CD8⁺) but also enhances the cytotoxic differentiation and functional competence of CD8⁺ T cells within the tumor microenvironment.

Because production of effector cytokines (including IL-2) and proliferation are hallmarks of T cell activation, we purified CD8⁺ T cells from wild-type mice and found that RA exposure significantly increased IFN-γ and TNF-α production (Fig. 2c–f). In addition, we found that pretreatment with RA significantly promotes the IL-2 production in protein and mRNA level (Fig. 2, g and h). And q-PCR analysis showed that RA promotes the Ifng mRNA level in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2 i). When we labeled the CD8+ T cells with CFSE after pretreated with RA, the data showed that RA promotes the CFSE dilution (Fig. 2 j), suggesting that RA could enhance the T cell proliferation. Given that robust T cell proliferation and cytokine production require substantial protein synthesis and metabolic upregulation, we investigated the mTORC1 signaling axis, a central hub linking TCR stimulation to anabolic metabolism. The 70-kDa ribosomal protein S6 kinase (p70S6K) is a direct downstream substrate of mTORC1; once phosphorylated, p70S6K phosphorylates the ribosomal protein S6, thereby initiating efficient translation of mRNAs essential for cell growth and cell cycle progression. To determine if RA modulates this critical pathway, we performed western blot analysis. The data showed that RA treatment significantly promotes the phosphorylation of both p70S6K and its substrate S6 (Fig. 2, k and l). Altogether, these data demonstrated that RA could remodel TCR downstream signaling.

RA remodels the CD8⁺ T cell transcriptome and highlights TCR pathways

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying RA-mediated T cell potentiation, we performed RNA-seq analysis on CD8+ T cells treated with RA versus vehicle. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct transcriptional profiles between the two groups, indicating a profound remodeling of the cellular state (Fig. 4a). Differential expression analysis identified 2,374 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 1,236 upregulated and 1,138 downregulated in the RA-treated group (Fig. 4b–d). We focused on upregulated genes associated with effector function and signaling regulation. As shown in the heatmap (Fig. 4e), RA treatment significantly induced the expression of genes linked to T cell activation (e.g., Il18, Ccr6), survival, and signaling adaptation. Trav15n-2, Ccr6, and Il18 referred to orthogonal transcript-level checks and in silico pathway/context interrogation, whereas Mapk8ip1 was the only candidate that we advanced to protein and functional follow-up. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the "T cell receptor signaling pathway" and "Calcium signaling pathway" were among the top enriched pathways (Fig. 4f). This was further corroborated by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), which showed a robust positive enrichment for TCR and calcium signaling signatures in RA-treated cells (Fig. 4g), suggesting that RA modulates core activation networks.

Crucially, our analysis reveals a distinct metabolic reprogramming signature. The chord diagram analysis demonstrates a significant association between upregulated metabolic genes, such as Fabp1 (fatty acid-binding protein 1), Hmgcs1 (involved in ketone body synthesis), and the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 4h). Given that the PPAR signaling pathway serves as a primary regulator of fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and mitochondrial biogenesis, these findings suggest that RA directs T cell metabolism toward a memory-associated bioenergetic profile. We validated these transcriptomic findings via qPCR (Fig. 4i). Consistent with RNA-seq data, significant upregulation of Mapk8ip1, Tyro3, Arg2, Scgb1a1, and Drc1 was observed. In particular, the upregulation of Mapk8ip1 (JIP1), a scaffolding protein that spatially organizes the JNK/MAPK module, supports our hypothesis that RA induces an adaptive remodeling of MAPK pathway architecture to optimize signal transduction. Tyro3, an upstream receptor tyrosine kinase, may contribute to sustaining activation thresholds. Additionally, Arg2 plays a critical role in modulating mitochondrial arginine metabolism and redox balance, aligning with the observed metabolic shift. To phenotypically confirm this metabolic reprogramming, we evaluated mitochondrial mass using confocal immunofluorescence microscopy to ensure subcellular resolution. Representative images (Fig. 4j) revealed a marked increase in mitochondrial content in RA-treated CD8+ T cells compared to the vehicle control. Quantitative analysis of single-cell fluorescence intensity confirmed a statistically significant elevation in mitochondrial mass (Fig. 4k). Collectively, these transcriptomic and imaging data indicate that RA orchestrates extensive transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming, characterized by an optimized TCR/MAPK signaling architecture and enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, thereby supporting robust effector function.

RA directly binds to and inhibits the phosphorylation of MEK1

The transcriptomic data indicating that the MAPK pathway is a key downstream target of RA in CD8+ T cells, we sought to validate this interaction at the protein level. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that RA treatment significantly attenuated the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 (p-ERK) in CD8+ T cells compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 5a and b). This led us to hypothesize that RA might directly target MEK1, the specific upstream kinase of ERK. To test this, we first evaluated the inhibitory potency of RA in a cellular context using MC38 cells, which exhibit high basal MAPK activity. RA inhibited MEK1-dependent signaling with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 0.074 nM, which was comparable to, and even more potent than, the specific MEK inhibitor Trametinib (IC50 = 0.358 nM) (Fig. 5c). Consistent with this, time-course Western blot analysis in CD8+ T cells confirmed that RA treatment significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of both MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 following anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation (Fig. 5d and e).

To confirm a direct physical interaction, we performed Microscale Thermophoresis (MST) using recombinant human MEK1 protein. The binding isotherm demonstrated that RA binds directly to MEK1 with high affinity, exhibiting a dissociation constant (Kd) of 1.474 nM (Fig. 5f). Finally, to rigorously validate MEK1 as the functional target, we utilized MEK1-deficient CD8+ T cells. We first confirmed the efficient knockout of MEK1 protein by Western blot (Fig. 5, k and l). In biochemical assays, MEK1 deficiency abolished the ability of RA to further suppress ERK phosphorylation, confirming that RA acts specifically through MEK1 (Fig. 5i and j). Functionally, MEK1-deficient CD8+ T cells exhibited a "hyper-activated" phenotype with spontaneously elevated IFN-γ production, mimicking the effect of RA treatment in wild-type cells (Fig. 5, g and h). Importantly, RA treatment failed to induce a further significant increase in IFN-γ in MEK1-knockout cells (Fig. 5h), demonstrating that the immunomodulatory effects of RA are strictly MEK1-dependent. Collectively, these data support a model where RA directly binds to MEK1 and fine-tunes MAPK signaling, thereby preventing over-stimulation and optimizing T cell effector function for superior anti-tumor immunity (Fig. 5m).

Discussion

Our study reveals a previously unrecognized immunoregulatory role for rosmarinic acid (RA), a natural phenolic compound, in enhancing CD8+ T cell anti-tumor immunity by targeting MEK1 and modulating TCR-mediated signaling. While RA has been widely studied for its anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidative, and tumoricidal effects in cancer cells [20, 21], its immunological functions—particularly within the adaptive T cell compartment—remain poorly understood. Here, we demonstrate that RA not only enhances CD8+ T cell activation and proliferation but also reprograms their transcriptional and metabolic states to favor a robust effector phenotype.

Our in vivo data in the MC38 tumor model showed that RA significantly reduced tumor burden and prolonged survival, effects that were dependent on the presence of CD8+ T cells. RA-treated tumors harbored increased infiltration of IFN-γ+ and Granzyme B+CD8+ T cells, indicative of enhanced cytotoxicity. The adoptive transfer of RA-primed OT-I T cells further confirmed that RA acts directly on CD8+ T cells to enhance their anti-tumor efficacy. These findings are consistent with prior reports demonstrating that small molecules can recondition tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) to overcome the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [22, 23]. Mechanistically, we identified MEK1 as a direct molecular target of RA using microscale thermophoresis (MST), which revealed a strong binding affinity (Kd = 1.474 nM). Functional studies confirmed that RA modulates TCR signaling by attenuating MEK1 and ERK phosphorylation, which paradoxically promotes T cell activation. This finding is aligned with recent evidence suggesting that partial inhibition of the MAPK pathway can improve T cell persistence and memory formation [24, 25]. Notably, MEK1 knockdown abrogated RA-induced enhancement of T cell function, providing causal evidence for MEK1 dependency. While our study identifies MEK1 as a direct target of RA and confirms that MEK1-deficient T cells recapitulate the RA-induced enhancement of IFN-γ production, our focus remains on this pivotal effector cytokine to establish signaling dependency. We acknowledge that a comprehensive functional analysis of MEK1-deficient CD8⁺ T cells including detailed assessment of proliferation kinetics (e.g., CFSE dilution), cytotoxicity, and the full spectrum of cytokine secretion (e.g., IL-2, TNF-α) was not conducted in this investigation. Future studies will aim to systematically delineate the broad phenotypic consequences resulting from MEK1 ablation and their precise overlap with RA-mediated immunomodulation.

Several studies report that RA suppresses T cell responses—inducing apoptosis of activated T cells from rheumatoid arthritis patients and inhibiting proximal TCR signaling via PLCγ1/Itk and Lck, thereby reducing Ca2⁺/NFAT activation [15, 17, 26]. In contrast to these prior contexts, in the tumor setting we find that RA enhances CD8⁺ T cell effector function and metabolic fitness, likely through the context-dependent factors outlined below. First, dose–exposure window: prior work typically used 30–50 μM for 16–48 h, conditions that promote mitochondrial stress and collapse proximal Ca2⁺ signaling, whereas our lower/intermittent exposures tune MEK1–ERK kinetics and enhance distal outputs (e.g., S6/mTORC1, effector programs) without closing the proximal “gate.” Second, cell state: Earlier studies examined already-activated/autoimmune CD4⁺ T cells with low apoptotic thresholds; by contrast, anti-tumor CD8⁺ T cells under chronic antigen benefit from moderated MAPK activity that preserves metabolic fitness and effector–memory balance. Third, tissue context: in tumors, RA acts across compartments (T cells, myeloid cells, cancer cells), and the net effect can favor immunity. Together, our MEK1-dependent data support a signal-tuning model rather than blanket inhibition, explaining the disparate literature and suggesting that calibrated, time-limited MAPK modulation may complement immunotherapy.

Although the Ras-Raf-MEK1/2-ERK1/2 cascade represents the classical downstream pathway of TCR signaling, facilitating early CD8⁺ T cell activation by driving AP-1-dependent transcription and synergizing with the NFAT/NF-κB program, our data reveal that RA treatment leads to reduced phosphorylation levels of MEK1/2 and ERK1/2, while concurrently enhancing the functionality of CD8⁺ T cells [27]. This apparent paradox aligns with an emerging perspective that ERK signaling acts as a decoder of signal strength and duration downstream of the TCR, and that the amplitude/kinetics of MAPK can shape the fate decisions of CD8⁺ T cells (e.g., effector versus memory-like states), rather than merely amplifying T cell activation in a monotonic manner [28]. Under conditions of intense or sustained stimulation, it has been reported that partial inhibition of the MEK-ERK signaling pathway can protect tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells from exhaustion/dysfunction associated with chronic activation and enhance anti-tumor immunity, particularly when combined with checkpoint blockade therapy.

The synergistic effect of RA with PD-1 blockade highlights its translational potential. Despite the success of checkpoint inhibitors, a significant proportion of patients fail to respond due to T cell exhaustion or insufficient priming [29, 30]. RA-mediated enhancement of TCR signaling may provide a strategy to boost T cell responsiveness in this context. Our findings thus align with recent studies demonstrating that modulating TCR-proximal pathways can reinvigorate T cell responses and sensitize tumors to immunotherapy [31, 32].

Fabp1 (L-FABP) is a cytosolic lipid chaperone that exhibits high affinity for long-chain fatty acids and related lipid mediators. It has been demonstrated to directly interact with nuclear PPARα, supporting its role in facilitating the delivery of lipid ligands to PPARs and promoting PPAR-dependent transcriptional programs involved in fatty acid transport and β-oxidation [33]. Activation of fatty acid oxidation mediated by peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling in CD8⁺ T cells promote mitochondrial fitness and expands the population of tumor-reactive CD8⁺ T cells, thereby enhancing responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy [34]. Importantly, tissue-resident lymphocytes can exhibit organ-specific patterns of FABP isoform usage that adapt to local lipid availability, suggesting that FABP dependence is not limited to FABP4/5 but may also involve other isoforms in the tissue niche [35]. In this context, the observed upregulation of Fabp1 in RA-treated CD8⁺ T cells align with KEGG-enriched PPAR signaling and supports the interpretation of RA-induced activation of the lipid metabolism/PPAR-FAO module. Coupled with increased p-p70S6K/p-S6 levels and enhanced effector readouts, these findings are consistent with the role of RA in promoting a metabolic adaptation program, which may contribute to sustaining the activation and effector functions of CD8⁺ T cells.

While our results are promising, several limitations warrant further exploration. First, the precise binding interface between RA and MEK1 remains to be structurally defined. Future studies employing crystallography or NMR could elucidate the interaction landscape and inform the design of more potent analogs. Second, RA metabolism generates various derivatives, some of which may retain or even exceed the bioactivity of the parent compound [36]. Investigating these metabolites could uncover additional immunomodulatory agents. Third, although our study focused on colorectal cancer, the effects of RA in other tumor models, particularly poorly immunogenic cancers require validation. Moreover, it is unknown whether RA modulates other immune cell types, such as dendritic cells or myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which may contribute to its overall anti-tumor effects [37]. While our MEK1 genetic data establish that RA-dependent modulation of the MEK–ERK axis is required for the observed IFN-γ phenotype, we did not perform a comprehensive functional mapping of MEK1-KO CD8⁺ T cells (e.g., CFSE-based proliferation, IL-2/TNF-α polyfunctionality, Granzyme B expression, and direct cytotoxicity assays). Given that ERK/AP-1 signaling contributes to these activation outputs, these endpoints represent important directions for future work to define the full overlap between MEK1 perturbation and RA-mediated immunomodulation [38].

In conclusion, this study identifies RA as a potent immune-enhancing small molecule that directly binds to MEK1 to reprogram CD8+ T cell activation, metabolism, and function. These findings suggest that RA may serve as an adjuvant for existing immunotherapies or as a stand-alone agent for enhancing T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. Our work provides a strong preclinical rationale for the repurposing of RA in cancer immunotherapy and sets the stage for further translational and clinical investigation.

Methods

Cell culture

Primary CD8⁺ T cells were isolated from OT-I transgenic or wild-type C57BL/6 mice using magnetic bead-based negative selection (Miltenyi Biotec). Cells were cultured in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1 mM non-essential amino acids, 2 mM L-glutamine, 0.05 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin. MC38 and MC38-OVA murine colon carcinoma cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 20 mM HEPES. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator and tested negative for mycoplasma.

Murine models

C57BL/6 and OT-I mice (6–8 weeks, male, SPF grade) were obtained from SLAC Laboratory (Shanghai) and housed under standard conditions with 12-h light/dark cycles. For subcutaneous tumor models, 5 × 105 MC38 or MC38-OVA cells were injected into the right flank. Tumor volumes were measured every 2 days and calculated as: volume = (length × width2)/2. RA (10 mg/kg) or vehicle (DMSO) was administered intraperitoneally on Day 7 post-tumor inoculation (when tumors became palpable), and was administered every 3 days. For CD8+ depletion, 200 µg anti-CD8α (clone 2.43, Bio X Cell) was injected i.p. on days 2, 5, 8, and every third day thereafter. Anti-PD-1 (clone RMP1-14, 200 µg/mouse) was administered on days 0, 3, 7, and 11. Mice were sacrificed when tumors exceeded 2 cm3 or showed signs of distress. All animal procedures were approved by the IACUC of Naval Medical University.

Western blot analysis of TCR signal

CD8⁺ T cells were pretreated with RA (10 µM) or vehicle for 2 h, followed by stimulation with anti-CD3ε (1 µg/mL) and anti-CD28 (1 µg/mL) for various durations (0–30 min). Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime). Proteins were resolved via SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Primary antibodies included anti-p-MEK1/2, anti-p-ERK1/2, p-S6 (Ser235/236), and β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology). Detection was performed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and ECL (Thermo Fisher). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ.

Real-time quantitative PCR (q-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured CD8+ T cells using Fastagen RNA isolation kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were lysed in RNA lysis buffer, and samples were treated with DNase I to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by NanoDrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher), and integrity was verified via agarose gel electrophoresis. First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed using Bimake RT Master Mix, employing a combination of oligo(dT) and random primers to ensure broad transcript coverage.

Quantitative PCR was conducted using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Each reaction contained 10 ng of cDNA, 10 μL SYBR mix, and 200 nM gene-specific primers in a total volume of 20 μL. Thermocycling parameters were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s. Melt curve analysis was performed to confirm product specificity. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with mouse β-actin serving as the endogenous normalization control. Primers used include: Il2 (F: 5′-GCGGCATGTTCTGGATTTGACTC-3′, R: 5′-CCACCACAGTTGCTGACTCATC-3′); Ifng (F: 5′-ATGAACGCTACACACTGCATC-3′, R: 5′-CCATCCTTTTGCCAGTTCCTC-3′); and β-actin (F: 5′-GACCTGACTGACTACCTCATGAAGAT-3′, R: 5′-GTCACACTTCATGATGGAGTTGAAGG-3′). All samples were run in technical triplicates.

FACS analysis

Naïve CD8+ T cells were isolated from spleens and lymph nodes using a CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) with negative selection, followed by purity confirmation (> 95%) via flow cytometry. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and exposed to either RA (10 μM) or vehicle control (0.1% DMSO) for 2 h. After treatment, cells were harvested, washed with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS + 2% FBS).

For surface staining, cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD8, CD25, CD69, and CD44 (BioLegend) for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. After washing, cells were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde. For intracellular cytokine detection, cells were restimulated with PMA (50 ng/mL) and ionomycin (1 μg/mL) in the presence of Brefeldin A (5 μg/mL) for 4 h. Cells were then fixed, permeabilized with Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD Biosciences), and stained for IFN-γ, TNF-α, and Granzyme B. Acquisition was performed on a BD LSRFortessa X-20 cytometer, and data were analyzed with FlowJo v10. Gating strategies excluded doublets and dead cells, with > 30,000 events recorded per sample.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To measure cytokine secretion, CD8+ T cells were treated with RA or vehicle as above and cultured in 96-well flat-bottom plates (2 × 10^5 cells/well) for 4 h. Supernatants were collected and centrifuged to remove debris. Mouse IL-2 concentrations were determined using a commercial ELISA kit (R&D Systems) per the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 100 μL of each sample or standard was added to IL-2 antibody-coated wells and incubated for 2 h. After washing, biotinylated detection antibody and streptavidin-HRP were sequentially added, followed by TMB substrate. Absorbance at 450 nm was read using a SpectraMax i3 plate reader (Molecular Devices). Cytokine levels were calculated based on standard curves and normalized to cell number.

Confocal immunofluorescence imaging

CD8+ T cells were cultured with vehicle, RA (10 μM), or αCD3/αCD28 (1 μg/mL each) stimulation for 2 h, then incubated with 100 nM MitoTracker Deep Red FM (Invitrogen) at 37°C for 30 min. Cells were washed, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and mounted onto slides using DAPI-containing ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher). Images were acquired using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope at 63 × magnification under identical exposure settings. Mean intensity/area ≥ 10 cells per group from 3 independent experiments were analyzed.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST)

To evaluate direct RA-MEK1 binding, purified recombinant His-tagged mouse MEK1 protein (Sino Biological) was fluorescently labeled using the RED-tris-NTA dye (NanoTemper Technologies). Serial dilutions of RA (0.1 nM–100 μM) were incubated with constant MEK1 concentration in MST buffer (PBS, 0.05% Tween-20, 0.1% BSA) at room temperature for 30 min. MST measurements were performed using the Monolith NT.115 instrument with standard capillaries and 40% MST power. Binding curves were fitted using MO. Affinity Analysis software, and dissociation constants (Kd) were derived from nonlinear regression.

RNA-seq

CD8+ T cells treated with RA or vehicle for 4 h were collected in TRIzol reagent, and total RNA was extracted per the standard protocol. RNA quality was assessed by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer; samples with RIN > 8.0 were used. Library preparation was performed using Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Kit, followed by sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to obtain 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic and aligned to the mouse genome (GRCm38) with STAR. Gene expression levels were calculated as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression was assessed with DESeq2, using fold change > 1.5 and adjusted p < 0.05 as significance thresholds. Enrichment analyses for KEGG pathways and GO terms were performed using the ClusterProfiler R package. RNA-seq samples consisted of purified splenic CD8⁺ T cells from individual wild-type C57BL/6 mice (no pooling), activated with plate-bound anti-CD3 and soluble anti-CD28 in the presence of RA or vehicle and collected within the short activation window; three biological replicates per condition (n = 3) were processed into separate libraries. The sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number PRJNA1363090.

CellTrace CFSE analysis of T cell proliferation

CD8+ T cells were labeled with 5 μM CellTrace CFSE (Invitrogen) in serum-free RPMI 1640 for 10 min at 37°C, followed by quenching with ice-cold medium and extensive washing. Labeled cells were seeded into 24-well plates and treated with RA (10 μM) or vehicle, followed by stimulation with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies (1 μg/mL each) for 72 h. Cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry for CFSE dilution, reflecting cell division. Proliferation index was calculated using FlowJo’s proliferation platform.

FACS analysis with T cell-mediated cytotoxicity

OT-I CD8+ T cells were pretreated with RA (10 μM) or vehicle for 2 h and then co-cultured with MC38-OVA target cells at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 1:4 in 96-well plates. No exogenous anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies were added, as the MC38-OVA cells presenting the specific ovalbumin peptide (SIINFEKL) provided the antigen-specific stimulation for the OT-I T cells. After 24 h, cells were stained with anti-CD8, anti-CD44, and viability dye (Zombie Aqua Fixable Viability Kit). In some experiments, intracellular staining for IFN-γ and TNF-α was performed. Cytotoxicity was quantified by assessing the viability of target cells and the expression of cytolytic markers in T cells. Data were collected on a BD LSRFortessa and analyzed using FlowJo.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated MEK1 knockout in CD8⁺ T cells

The Cas9 RNP complex targeting MEK1 (Map2k1) was constructed by pre-complexing guide RNA with recombinant Cas9 at a 1:1 molar ratio. For each condition, 2 million cells were transfected using the Lonza 4D-Nucleofector™ System with P3 Solution under the pulse code DN-100. The transfected cells were immediately transferred into pre-warmed culture medium and maintained with appropriate cytokine support. MEK1 knockout efficiency was evaluated via western blot analysis at 72 h post-transfection, followed by protein analysis at designated time points.

Intracellular cytokine analysis in T cells

Following RA or vehicle treatment for 4 h, CD8+ T cells were stimulated with PMA (50 ng/mL) and ionomycin (1 μg/mL) in the presence of brefeldin A for 4 h. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against IFN-γ and TNF-α (eBioscience). Flow cytometric analysis was conducted to determine the frequency and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of cytokine producing cells. Data were normalized to the DMSO control.

Flow cytometry of tumor-infiltrating T cells (TILs)

Tumors were excised and mechanically dissociated using the gentle MACS dissociator (Miltenyi) in enzyme cocktail (collagenase D and DNase I). Cell suspensions were filtered through 70 μm strainers, and red blood cells were lysed. TILs were stained with antibodies against CD45, CD3, CD8, CD4, CD44, CD69, and CD62L to assess phenotype and activation status. Viability dyes excluded dead cells. Intracellular cytokines were assessed as described above. All data were acquired using BD LSRFortessa and analyzed with FlowJo.

In vivo mouse models

For subcutaneous tumor models, C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, female) were injected with 5 × 105 MC38 cells into the right flank to establish syngeneic skin melanoma. For orthotopic peritoneal colon cancer models, the same number of MC38 cells was injected intraperitoneally. Mice were randomized into control and treatment groups (n = 5–10 per group) and administered Rosmarinus acid (RA) at 10 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection every three days. Control mice received an equal volume of vehicle (0.1% DMSO in PBS). Tumor volume was measured every other day using digital calipers and calculated as (length × width2)/2. Mice were monitored for survival, tumor ulceration, and signs of distress per institutional IACUC guidelines. At endpoint or upon euthanasia, tumors were excised for immune phenotyping. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were isolated from digested tumor tissues using enzymatic dissociation (collagenase IV and DNase I) and density gradient centrifugation. Isolated cells were stained for T cell markers and analyzed by flow cytometry to assess activation status (CD44, CD62L, CD69), cytokine production (IFN-γ, TNF-α), and memory subsets.

In vivo experiments with CD8 depletion or RA combined with anti-PD-1 therapy

To assess the role of CD8⁺ T cells in RA-mediated tumor suppression, mice were treated with anti-CD8α monoclonal antibody (clone 2.43; 200 µg/mouse, i.p.) on days 2, 5, and 8 after tumor inoculation and then every third day until sacrifice. For combination immunotherapy experiments, RA (10 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered alongside anti-PD-1 antibody (clone RMP1-14; 200 µg/mouse, i.p.) on days 0, 3, 7, and 11. Tumor growth was tracked over time, and overall survival was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis. The efficacy of combinatorial treatment was assessed by comparing tumor progression and survival curves across control, RA-only, PD-1-only, and RA + PD-1 groups.

In vivo experiments with T cell transfer

To evaluate the functional enhancement of T cells by RA in vivo, purified OT-I CD8⁺ T cells were treated ex vivo with RA (10 µM) or vehicle for 2 h, washed, and intravenously injected (1 × 10⁶ cells/mouse) into C57BL/6 mice bearing established MC38-OVA tumors. Tumor size was monitored over time, and survival was recorded. On day 14 post-transfer, TILs were analyzed by flow cytometry to evaluate the persistence, expansion (CD45.1⁺), activation (CD69, CD44), and memory phenotype (CD62L expression) of transferred OT-I T cells. In some experiments, intracellular cytokine staining was performed after ex vivo restimulation with OVA peptide to assess functional reactivity.
