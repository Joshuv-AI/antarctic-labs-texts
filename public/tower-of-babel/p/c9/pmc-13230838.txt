Structural response of RC beam-column joints reinforced with smart alloys and polyvinyl alcohol fibers
Authors: Ali S. Alqarni, Mohammad J. Alshannag, Mahmoud M. Higazey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study introduces an innovative experimental approach that combines a hybrid system of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) fiber–reinforced composites and shape memory alloys (SMA) to enhance the seismic performance of exterior reinforced concrete (RC) beam–column joints (BCJs). The proposed technique leverages the excellent self-centering capability of SMA together with the excellent energy dissipation characteristics of steel and PVA fibers to improve joint behavior without compromising load capacity. Four one-third–scale RC-BCJ specimens were cast and tested under quasi-static reversed cyclic loading. The results showed that both the PVA fiber scheme and the proposed hybrid system exhibited reduced residual displacements, higher self-centering capacity, greater energy dissipation, improved load and displacement capacities, smaller crack widths, and slower stiffness degradation compared with the control specimen. Furthermore, a finite element (FE) model was developed in ABAQUS to simulate the hysteretic response of the tested joints, and the numerical results showed good agreement with the experimental findings.

Introduction

In seismic areas, moderate to severe earthquakes can cause permanent inelastic deformations at the beam-column joints due to the yielding of steel reinforcement at the critical regions and reaching higher stress levels. These residual deformations are not fully recoverable with conventional repair methods or standard design practices. Consequently, this can result in reduced structural ductility and post-yield performance, as well as diminished serviceability and increased maintenance requirements. Moreover, the presence of significant post-earthquake residual drifts can lead to expensive repairs and potentially necessitate the demolition and reconstruction of entire buildings1. For instance, after the earthquake sequence in New Zealand from 2010 to 2011, approximately 25% of buildings in Christchurch’s central business district were no longer vertical2, and 60% of reinforced concrete buildings with three or more stories were demolished3 due to being deemed “uneconomic to repair”. Another example is the 1995 Kobe earthquake in Japan, which caused almost 100 bridge columns to show significant permanent deformation, necessitating their demolition and replacement, leading to considerable economic losses4. Moreover, extensive damage to reinforced concrete buildings, resulting in the demolition of numerous structures, has been observed in various other earthquakes around the world. This includes the 1985 Mexico City earthquake, the 1994 Northridge earthquake in the United States, the 2009 L’Aquila earthquake in Italy, the 2010 Maule earthquake in Chile, and the deadliest earthquake that struck Turkey and Syria in 20235–9. Therefore, it is important to develop smart design alternatives and construction practices that can minimize the permanent damage induced in the structural systems due to seismic excitations without compromising their energy dissipation and load capacities.

Among the methods explored to reduce the impact of permanent (i.e., residual) deformations and improve post-earthquake conditions in structures is the application of smart materials and systems, like Shape Memory Alloys (SMA)10–12. Due to their unique self-centering and superelastic characteristics, SMA can be seen as an ideal solution for minimizing maintenance needs of concrete structures located in active seismic areas13–23. The superelastic effect of SMA lies in its capability to recover the initial shape after loading and unloading process with minimal residual strain. Several SMA are used in the industry including NiTi, Cu-based and Fe-based with various properties and applications24–26. However, superelastic NiTi are the most commonly SMA used in concrete structural applications due to its superior properties including the strain recovery up to 8%, high strength and corrosion resistance. Over the past twenty-five years, the cost of Ni–Ti has decreased substantially, falling from over USD 1,000 per kilogram in 1999 to approximately USD 150 per kilogram in 200927, and to below USD 60 per kilogram today. Although it remains more expensive than conventional construction materials, the use of SMA reinforcement provides notable advantages. While it may raise initial construction costs, it can significantly reduce both direct and indirect seismic losses compared to traditional reinforcement methods. Xiang et al.20 conducted a comparative assessment of the initial construction costs and post-earthquake repair costs for RC bridges reinforced with conventional steel bars and SMA bars. For the prototype bridge examined in their study, the use of pure SMA reinforcement led to a 20% increase in total construction cost compared to the steel reinforcement. However, this higher upfront investment was associated with a substantial reduction in seismic risk, as the annual seismic losses for earthquakes with a 2,475-year return period were reduced by 81%. Limited experimental studies exist in the literature on the effect of SMA on the behavior of beam-column joints (BCJs) subjected to lateral cyclic loading. Most of the studies published on the effectiveness of SMA in improving the self-centering ability of reinforced concrete (RC) connections are numerical in nature. This could be due to the high cost associated with their manufacturing. Youssef et al.27 conducted tests on two large-scale exterior beam-column joint specimens to explore the potential of using superelastic SMA as reinforcement to decrease the residual displacement of the joint during cyclic loading. Their results demonstrated that SMA reinforced beam-column joint recovered most of its post-yield residual deformation whereas steel joint experienced large residual drifts. Moreover, they reported that the joints reinforced with SMA bars dissipated up to 37% lower energy compared to steel reinforced concrete beam column joints.

Oudah et al.28 introduced a single-slotted beam technique for self-centering reinforced concrete exterior beam-column joints using SMA bars. This approach allows for the relocation of the plastic hinge away from the column face by vertically slotting the beam at a specified distance from it. Test results demonstrated the system’s effectiveness in reducing the pinching shear effect and minimizing joint deformation. Additionally, the joint reinforced with SMA bars dissipated about 50% lower energy compared to joint reinforced with steel bars. Azariani et al.29 tested exterior BCJ with different concrete strengths and transverse reinforcements. Their test results revealed that the joints reinforced with SMA exhibited a reduction in energy dissipation by 60% and 53% for joints designed with concrete strengths of 45 MPa and 30 MPa respectively, compared to steel reinforced joints. Abdulridha et al.30 tested large scale RC shear walls reinforced with SMA bars, and subjected to reversed cyclic loadings. Their results showed that the SMA bars increased the self-centering of shear wall by 92% compared to only 31% for control specimen, while the shear wall reinforced with SMA dissipated 40% lesser energy compared to control specimen. Abdulridha et al.31 tested also RC beams strengthened with SMA bars, under reversed cyclic loadings. They reported that the beams reinforced with SMA showed 86% self-centering compared to 26% for control specimen. Furthermore, the beam reinforced with SMA dissipated 54% lower energy compared to control RC beam. Further details on the most important applications of SMA in civil engineering structures during the last two decades can be found in a recent publication by Alshannag et al.32.

Alqarni et al.33 developed finite element models using ABAQUS to analyze the cyclic response of exterior steel I beam-hollow column connections reinforced with welded SMA bolts and seat angles. Their numerical findings showed a reduction in residual drift by up to 94% and a twofold increase in self-centering for specimens strengthened with SMA plates and angles compared to control connections. Similarly, Higazey et al.34 proposed a nonlinear finite element (FE) model to assess the performance of reinforced concrete beam-column joints enhanced with shape memory alloys (SMA) and steel rebars. Their results demonstrated the effectiveness of combining SMA bars with steel rebars to improve the seismic performance of the joints. Under cyclic loading, conventional steel reinforcement dissipates energy primarily through plastic yielding, producing wide hysteresis loops and high energy absorption capacity; however, this mechanism also leads to permanent deformations and residual drifts. In contrast, superelastic SMA bars exhibit comparatively narrower hysteresis loops and lower energy dissipation, as reported in the literature, but their inherent self-centering capability enables near-complete strain recovery even after large deformations. This study introduces an innovative approach to improving the seismic performance of exterior reinforced concrete beam–column joints subjected to reversed cyclic loading. The proposed technique leverages the complementary advantages of both materials by integrating the superelastic, self-centering behavior of SMA bars with the energy dissipation capacity of conventional steel reinforcement, thereby enhancing the resilience and extending the service life of concrete structures exposed to seismic actions.

Four one-third scale RC-BCJ specimens were cast and tested; one seismically designed using conventional steel rebars, and two reinforced with SMA in combination with steel rebars, and one with PVA fiber mortar cast in the critical region of the joint. The test results were expressed in terms of self-centering, energy dissipation, displacement capacity, load capacity, and stiffness degradation. Moreover, a 3D finite element model was also performed for simulating the load-displacement response of the tested specimens. The reason behind adding PVA fibers to concrete in this study was to enhance the post-cracking ductility of the RC-BCJs, thereby compensating for the reduction in energy dissipation associated with the use of SMA bars. In addition, the inclusion of PVA fibers contributes significantly to improving the durability of the concrete matrix by enhancing crack resistance, reducing permeability, and promoting long-term mechanical stability. These improvements stem mainly from the fibers’ ability to bridge microcracks, inhibit crack propagation, and effectively control the width and distribution of macrocracks. It is important to note that, temperature effects were not considered in this investigation, and the findings reflect typical ambient structural conditions.

The present investigation fills in the gap in research by developing a hybrid system consisting of steel and SMA bars for enhancing the self-centering capability of the RC-BCJ without significant reduction in its energy dissipation (i.e. ductility). The novelty of the proposed system relies on combining the superior self-centering properties of SMA bars with the outstanding energy dissipation characteristics of steel bars and PVA fibers for enhancing the seismic performance of the RC-BCJs without compromising their load-carrying capacity. Unlike previous studies, where improvements in self-centering were often accompanied by considerable losses in energy dissipation, the proposed system effectively balances both self-centering ability and energy dissipation. Moreover, this study introduces, for the first time in the literature, an experimental hybrid system combining SMA and steel bars at the beam–column interface of the joint (i.e., the plastic hinge region) subjected to cyclic loading. Previous studies reported in the literature, including one conducted by the present authors, were primarily numerical and relied on monotonic loading to assess the reliability of the proposed hybrid system.

Experimental program

The details of test specimens, material properties, and test procedures are presented in the following subsections.

Test specimens

The structural model considered in this investigation represents a seven-story reinforced concrete building designed according to the Saudi Building Code (SBC 301 − 24)35. The moment-resisting frame building is assumed to be located in a high seismic region in Saudi Arabia. An exterior beam–column joint located between the mid-height of the first and second stories was selected for the experimental investigation carried out in this paper, as shown in Fig. 1. To meet the laboratory constraints, a reduced-scale specimen was designed at one-third of the actual size, and the loading applied during testing was scaled accordingly. Four one-third scale joint specimens were prepared to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed techniques in enhancing the seismic performance of BCJ specimens strengthened with SMA bars. The geometric properties and reinforcements details of the tested specimens are shown in Fig. 2; Table 1 respectively.

The first specimen (BCJ-CSD) designated as the control specimen was well-seismically designed using steel bars only, whereas the second specimen (BCJ-SMA) was similar to the control specimen with the SMA bars placed in the plastic hinge region of the beam. The third specimen (BCJ-PVA) was identical to the second specimen except that the PVA fiber mortar was placed in the critical region of the joint and extended to a distance of 275 mm and 200 mm in the beam and upper and lower columns, respectively. replacing the concrete cast in the critical region of the joint. The fourth specimen (BCJ-HYB) was designed to have hybrid steel-SMA reinforcement such that one 8-mm steel bar and two 10-mm SMA bars placed in the critical region of the beam section. The SMA bars had a length of 400 mm and were threaded at both ends up to a distance of 100 mm to ensure full bonding with threaded mechanical couplers, as shown in Fig. 3. The reinforcement details and wood forms of the specimens are presented in Fig. 4. Following the casting of concrete, the specimens were cured with wet burlap for 28 days before testing. The BCJ specimens were all cast with the same concrete strength grade.

Material properties

The SMA bars used in the BCJ specimens were manufactured from NiTi with a strain recovery and austenite finish temperature (Af) of up to 6% and 0 °C (± 5), respectively. The chemical composition of NiTi bars is presented in Table 2. Figure 5a shows the experimental cyclic tensile behavior of the SMA bar up to 6% strain at room temperature. The ultimate strength and strain of SMA bars were 852 MPa and 12%, respectively. Table 3 summarizes the mechanical properties of the SMA bars and identifies the transformation start and finish stresses utilized in this study based on the parameters defined in Fig. 6. Unlike conventional steel reinforcement, which yields and undergoes permanent plastic deformation, the SMA bars exhibited a stress–strain response with a transformation plateau (Fig. 5a). This plateau results from stress-induced phase transformation between austenite and martensite, enabling large strains without significant stress increase. Upon unloading, the bars recovered up to 6% strain due to superelasticity, whereas conventional steel shows minimal recovery beyond the elastic range. This behavior highlights the potential of SMA bars to reduce residual deformation and enhance self-centering under cyclic loading. The modulus of elasticity of steel was 205 GPa, while the yield and ultimate strengths were 566 MPa and 713 MPa, respectively, as shown in Fig. 5b.

The properties of the PVA fibers used in the critical region of BCJ-PVA specimen are presented in Table 4. The concrete mixture proportions and PVA fiber mortar are also presented in Tables 5 and 6, respectively. The results of the compressive and splitting tensile strengths of the concrete and PVA mortar are presented in Table 7.

Test setup and instrumentation

All BCJ specimens were tested under reversed cyclic loading using an electrical actuator with 500 kN capacity shown in the schematic diagram of the test setup illustrated in Fig. 7. One vertical LVDTs was placed underneath the tip of the beam to measure the lateral displacement; two horizontal LVDTs were placed at the top and bottom face of the column along the beam to measure the major crack width, and two diagonal LVDTs were placed at the face of the joint to measure the shear deformations, as shown in Fig. 7. Four strain gauges were also pasted on the longitudinal reinforcement at the face of the column to measure the strains in steel bars. An electric pump was used to control the displacement of the hydraulic jack. A displacement rate of 0.5 mm/s was used for testing the joint specimens investigated. All the LVDTs and strain gauges were connected to a data acquisition system to record the load versus displacement/strains until failure of the specimen. The data logger recorded the values each second, which were then transferred to a computer to plot the required graphs.

A displacement-control test protocol was performed using an electric pump in accordance with the cyclic loading strategy, as shown in Fig. 8. The loading started at 1% drift and increased incrementally by 0.5% drift until reaching 6.5% drift. Three full cycles were performed for each drift before proceeding to the next cyclic drift. All specimens were tested up to failure. The drift increment of 0.5% was selected to ensure accurate capture of the nonlinear response and was also consistent with the operational resolution and practical limitations of the loading control system used in the laboratory. Despite the importance of applying an external axial compressive load on the column during the test, no external axial load was applied on the column of the joint specimens tested in this investigation, in order to avoid the enhancement in the joint load capacity as reported by many researchers36–39, and to meet some practical constraints existing in the laboratory. However, a previous study by the authors34 investigated axial load effects at three levels, 15%, 30%, and 60% of column capacity, on SMA-reinforced BCJ with keeping all other parameters constant. The findings demonstrated that increasing the axial load to 60% of the column capacity enhanced the joint load capacity by 14%, increased stiffness by up to 32%, and improved energy dissipation by 28% compared to specimens tested without axial load.

Load-displacement hysteretic response

The hysteretic load-displacement responses of the BCJ-CSD vs. BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA vs. BCJ-HYB, and BCJ-PVA vs. BCJ-HYB specimens are presented in Fig. 9. The use of threaded SMA bars in BCJ-SMA specimen not only resulted in improved self-centering but also enabled the BCJ to withstand a peak load and maximum lateral displacement of 37.8 kN and 40 mm (5% drift), respectively, compared to 24.9 kN and 28 mm (3.5% drift) for the BCJ-CSD. The BCJ-CSD and BCJ-SMA specimens exhibited different load-displacement responses due to the differences in the mechanical properties of the reinforcing bars (steel vs. SMA). While the BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens showed similar load-displacement responses, the use of PVA fibers (BCJ-PVA) increased the maximum lateral displacement to 52 mm (6.5% drift) compared to 40 mm (5% drift) for the BCJ-SMA. The enhancement in the maximum displacement capacity of the BCJ-PVA specimen consequently caused the area under the load–displacement curve to increase resulting in a superior energy dissipating BCJ. The hybrid SMA-steel reinforcement in the BCJ-HYB specimen resulted in improved hysteresis loop (energy dissipation) without compromising the stiffness and maximum load capacity compared to the BCJ-SMA specimen. In general, SMA bars appeared to be very effective in achieving larger displacement capacity values of 43% (5.0% drift), 86% (6.5% drift) and 86% (6.5% drift) for the BCJ-PVA, BCJ-SMA and BCJ-HYB specimens, respectively, compared to the control BCJ-CSD specimen. These significant improvements are due to the higher ultimate tensile strength, and threaded surface of NiTi SMA bars relative to the conventional steel rebars. This could have in turn enabled the specimens to take full advantage of the superior properties of SMA bars and achieve higher lateral loads and displacements capacities compared to the control BCJ reinforced with steel.

It is worth mentioning that some variations exist between the positive and negative loads in the hysteresis responses of BCJ specimens reinforced with SMA bars. For instance, the BCJ-SMA specimen reached a maximum load of 37.8 kN in the positive direction, compared with − 29.55 kN in the negative direction, giving a positive-to-negative peak load ratio of 1.28. Similar trends were seen for the BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens, with ratios of 1.45 and 1.44, respectively, while the control specimen showed a smaller difference, with a ratio of 1.19. These variations agreed with the results reported in the literature31. This observation is in close alignment with the findings of Oudah28, who reported a comparable positive-to-negative peak load ratio of 1.5, further validating the systematic nature of this hysteretic asymmetry. Recent studies40–43 have indicated that, unlike steel, SMA bars exhibit distinctly asymmetric stress-strain curves when subjected to tension-compression cycles, a behavior attributed to the inherent properties of NiTi materials. This pronounced tension-compression asymmetry is likely due to the strong crystallographic texture formed during the commercial production of polycrystalline NiTi40. The compressive responses are generally characterized by a smaller recoverable strain, a higher critical transformation stress, and a steeper slope in the stress plateau43. Moreover, the mechanical properties of the SMA shows some variations in the stress-strain response in tension than compression.

Failure mode and crack patterns

The crack patterns and the crack widths of the specimens tested at a 3.5% drift ratio and at the maximum drift ratio are shown in Figs. 10 and 11 respectively. The major crack widths presented in this investigation were determined from the difference between the readings of two horizontal LVDTs positioned at the top and bottom faces of the column along the beam direction. The recorded values during testing are presented in Fig. 11. In addition, the fine crack widths were measured using a crack microscope with an accuracy of 0.01 mm. The failure of the specimens was characterized by the fracture of the beam’s main reinforcement, in the plastic hinge region away from the face column. The fracture of the steel bar in the BCJ-CSD specimen occurred at 3.5% drift, whereas the SMA bars were capable of undergoing larger drift ratios up to 5% and 6.5% drifts for the BCJ-SMA and BCJ-PVA specimens, respectively. Unlike the other BCJ specimens where the fracture of the longitudinal reinforcement occurred, the test was stopped for the BCJ-HYB specimen after achieving a drift ratio of 6.5% to avoid LVDTs damage. Moreover, the 8-mm diameter steel bar fractured while the two 10-mm dimeter SMA bars remained intact after the test was ceased. It is worth mentioning in this investigation that all SMA bars used in this study had a smooth surface finish. The threaded portions were used solely to ensure proper anchorage by securely fastening the bars to the couplers. A detailed post-test examination of the BCJ-SMA and the BCJ-HYB revealed no evidence of bond failure. Instead, failure was governed by the rupture of the reinforcement.

At a 1% drift ratio, flexural cracks were observed in the beam at a distance of 0 mm, 100 mm and 200 mm away from the column face in BCJ-CSD and BCJ-PVA specimens. Similar flexural cracks in the beam were noticed in BCJ-SMA specimen at a further distance of 0 mm, 300 mm and 400 mm with a crack width of 0.3 mm. Unlike the other BCJ specimens, the flexural cracks were limited to a distance of 0 mm and 250 mm from the column face. The width of the major cracks at 1% drift for all specimens were less than 0.2 mm. A few minor diagonal cracks were seen in the joint region of the BCJ specimens with a crack width of 0.1 mm, which remained unchanged until the end of test.

At a 2% drift ratio, the initial beam flexural cracks of the BCJ-CSD extended in length and width. Additionally, a new flexural crack occurred at 450 mm away from the column face, and a diagonal fine crack was noticed at the joint region. The flexural cracks experienced at a 1% drift ratio for the BCJ-SMA and BCJ-HYB specimens, were expanded further with no signs of new additional cracks being formed elsewhere. However, the BCJ-PVA specimen experienced additional parallel flexural fine cracks at approximately 400 and 500 mm from the column face at 1.5% and 2% drifts, respectively. At 2% drift, the width of the major cracks for the BCJ-CSD, BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA, and BCJ-HYB specimens were approximately 0.8 mm, 0.4 mm, 0.5 mm and 0.5 mm, respectively. New fine diagonal cracks in the joint region were also observed for the BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens with a crack width of 0.1 mm.

At a 3% drift ratio, the beam flexural cracks of the BCJ-CSD at 0 mm, 200 mm, and 450 mm away from the column face were extended further in length and no new cracks were noticed. While the flexural cracks observed earlier for the BCJ-SMA and BCJ-PVA specimen were increased in length and size, two new diagonal cracks were formed in the joint region and a new flexural crack was recorded at 500 mm away from the column face. At 3% drift, the width of the major cracks for the BCJ-CSD, BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA, and BCJ-HYB specimens were approximately 4.1 mm, 0.8 mm, 0.9 mm and 1.1 mm, respectively. The BCJ-HYB experienced a new flexural crack in the beam at distance of 400 mm away from the column face and showed an increase in the length and width of the flexural cracks observed previously. A few minor diagonal cracks were also noticed in the joint region of the BCJ-SMA, BCJ-HYB, and BCJ-PVA specimens with a crack width of 0.1 mm.

At a 3.5% drift ratio, the main flexural crack at the column face for the BCJ-CSD increased considerably in length and size showing some initial signs of failure, as shown in Fig. 10a. This was also evident from the horizontal displacement recorded by the LVDT at the top of beam, as shown in Fig. 11. At 3.5% drift, the width of the major cracks for the BCJ-CSD, BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA, and BCJ-HYB specimens were approximately 4.1 mm, 0.8 mm, 0.9 mm and 1.1 mm, respectively. However, the BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens were still intact and the crack propagation across the depth and width of these specimens were less by 17% to 23% compared to the BCJ-CSD specimen. A new flexural crack was formed at a distance of 500 mm away from the column face for the BCJ-PVA, while the previously observed flexural cracks of this specimen and the BCJ-SMA and BCJ-HYB specimens were increasing in length and width. Furthermore, new minor diagonal cracks were also perceived in the joint region of the BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens with a crack width of 0.1 mm.

The BCJ-CSD specimen failed in flexure after reaching a 3.5% drift ratio along the major flexural crack discussed earlier due to the fracture of the longitudinal steel bars. However, no new flexural cracks were observed in the BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB, and the existing cracks extended at higher drift ratios, especially those flexural cracks at the face of the column. The cracks of BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens were more in number and evenly distributed between the beam and joint than the other specimens, which enabled them to resist a higher drift ratio of up to 6.5%, as shown in Fig. 10b. The widths of the major cracks at the failure of the BCJ-CSD, BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA, and BCJ-HYB specimens were approximately 4.1 mm, 1.4 mm, 1.7 mm and 3.2 mm, respectively, as shown in Fig. 11. The BCJ-SMA and BCJ-PVA specimens failed in flexure due to fracture of longitudinal reinforcement at a drift ratio of 5% and 6.5%, respectively, while the test was stopped for the BCJ-HYB specimen after reaching a drift ratio of 6.5%. At the maximum drift ratio of the BCJ-HYB specimen, the concrete cover at the top and bottom of the beam close to the column face spalled off, as shown in Fig. 10b.

Load capacity

The BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens showed 52%, 63%, and 55% higher load capacity, respectively, than the BCJ-CSD specimen, as shown in Fig. 12a. The specimens that were reinforced with steel rebars only (BCJ-CSD) or had hybrid reinforcement (BCJ-HYB) reached the peak load capacity before achieving the maximum drift value. Following the peak load a rapid drop in the load capacity of these specimens was observed in the subsequent drift values. The BCJ-CSD and BCJ-HYB specimens achieved the maximum load at 3% and 4.5% drift ratios, respectively, and then the load capacity sharply decreased up to the failure point after 3.5% and 6.5% drift ratios. Nevertheless, the specimens reinforced with SMA bars only in the plastic hinge regions, such as the BCJ-SMA and BCJ-PVA specimens, reached the peak load capacity and failed at the maximum drift values of 5% and 6.5%, respectively. This was due to the inherent differences in the stress-strain curves of steel and SMA bars.

Residual displacement and self-centering factor

Residual displacement refers to the permanent displacement of a structure resulting from inelastic deformations caused by seismic events44. The permanent deformation of joints is an important norm for determining the serviceability of the buildings and the post-earthquake maintenance requirements. The allowable residual displacement limit proposed by Kawashima et al.10 was 1% drift. This limit was selected because reinforced concrete columns with a drift larger than 1.75% had to be demolished and rebuilt after the Kobe earthquake. The residual displacement versus corresponding drift level curves of the specimens are presented in Fig. 12b. The residual displacement (drift) of the BCJ-CSD specimen at a 3.5% drift level was equal to 13.24 mm (1.66%). This value exceeded the allowable residual drift and was very close to the recommend value for demolition as opposed to retrofitting due to economic reasons. The specimen BCJ-CSD reached a residual displacement of 8 mm, which corresponds to a residual drift of 1%, at an applied drift of 2.8%, while BCJ-HYB reached the same value of residual drift at an applied drift of 4.7%. Nevertheless, BCJ-SMA and BCJ-PVA performed better and failed prior to reaching a residual drift of 1%. At a 3.5% drift level, the residual displacement (drift) of BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens was 2.48 mm (0.31%), 3.48 mm (0.44%), and 4.70 mm (0.59%), respectively. This showed that SMA bars were effective in minimizing the residual drift below the allowable limit, compared to steel rebars. Even at higher drift level of 5%, the residual displacement (drift) of the BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens was 5.00 mm (0.63%), 4.44 mm (0.56%), and 9.60 mm (1.20%), respectively. This signifies the considerable ability of the superelasticity of the SMA material to achieve larger drift capacity without compromising the BCJ performance. The self-centering factors of the specimens tested were also determined in accordance with Eq. 1 presented by Shajil et al.45. Figure 13 shows that BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA, and BCJ-HYB specimens resulted in improved self-centering by 88%, 89%, and 76%, respectively, while the BCJ-CSD specimen had 53% self-centering. This could be attributed to the superelastic property of SMA which provided the BCJ with the self-centering capability.

Energy dissipation

Cumulative energy dissipation was determined by adding the areas enclosed by the load-displacement envelopes over consecutive cycles until the conclusion of the test46. The curves showing cumulative energy dissipation in relation to the corresponding drift ratio are illustrated in Figs. 12c and 12. The BCJ-CSD specimen showed the highest cumulative energy dissipation at a 3.5% drift ratio. This was attributed to the larger hysteretic loop of steel compared to SMA. However, the BCJ-CSD specimen suffered extensive cracking in the plastic hinge region at a 3.5% drift ratio, as shown in Fig. 10a, which led to dissipating a higher amount of energy. Unlike the BCJ-CSD specimen, the BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA, and BCJ-HYB specimens reinforced with SMA in the plastic hinge region suffered moderate cracking at the same drift level, as shown in Figs. 10a, b and c, respectively. The use of PVA fibers (BCJ-PVA) or hybrid reinforcement (BCJ-HYB) at a 3.5% drift ratio appeared to improve the energy dissipation by 23% and 35%, respectively, compared to the BCJ-SMA specimen. Figure 13b shows that the BCJ specimens reinforced with SMA bars, as opposed to steel bars, were capable of undergoing higher drift ratios ranging from 5% to 6.5% resulting in larger hysteresis response, and thus higher energy dissipation. The BCJ-HYB specimens had the highest cumulative energy dissipation at the maximum drift ratio. The energy dissipation at the maximum drift ratio of the BCJ-HYB specimen was 1.5, 2.9 and 3.0 times larger than the BCJ-PVA, BCJ-SMA, and BCJ-CSD specimens, respectively. Meanwhile, there was a slight increase in energy dissipation of 4% in the BCJ-SMA specimen compared to the BCJ-CSD specimen. The use of PVA fibers or hybrid SMA-steel reinforcement appeared to be effective solutions for designing BCJ specimens with excellent self-centering and energy dissipation characteristics, as shown in Table 8.

Stiffness

The stiffness in each half cycle was determined using a line drawn between the maximum positive and negative drift points following the procedure described by Shannag et al.47. The stiffness values versus the corresponding drift ratios for each specimen tested are summarized in Fig. 12d. The results showed that the BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB showed 22%, 37%, and 19% lower stiffness values at 1% drift respectively, compared to the BCJ-CSD. This was attributed to the lower elastic modulus of the SMA compared to the steel. Although the BCJ-CSD specimen achieved the maximum initial stiffness relative to the other specimens, its stiffness degradation rate was substantially high. The specimens reinforced with SMA bars and PVA fiber mortar, (BCJ-PVA, and BCJ-SMA), in the critical region of the joint exhibited the lowest rate in stiffness degradation among the specimens tested, as shown in Fig. 15(d). This could be due to the superelastic properties of SMA bars, and the ability of the PVA fibers to arrest the microcracks at initial stages of loading. Additionally, at a 3.5% drift ratio, the stiffness of the BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens were 1.12, 1.00 and 1.16 kN/mm respectively compared to 0.68 kN/mm in the BCJ-CSD specimen. The significant reduction in stiffness and strength of BCJ-CSD specimen compared to other specimens, could be due to the large plastic deformations and wider flexural cracks experienced by the control specimen, as shown in Fig. 10a.

The strains measured in the top tensile reinforcing steel and SMA bars at the plastic hinge region for the BCJ-CSD, BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA, and BCJ-HYB specimens, and SMA bar for BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens are shown in Fig. 14. The BCJ-CSD specimen suffered high residual strains, as shown in Fig. 14a, whereas BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens experienced minor residual strains, as shown in Figs. 14b, c, and d, respectively. Unlike the BCJ-CSD specimen, the BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB specimens recorded larger strains as a result of undergoing larger drift ratios. The maximum and residual measured strains in the tensile longitudinal reinforcement of the tested BCJ specimens are summarized in Table 8. It can be noticed that the residual strains in the SMA bars for the BCJ-SMA and BCJ-PVA, and BCJ-HYB specimens were 75%, 78% and 52% respectively less than the residual strain in the steel bars for the BCJ-CSD specimen. The residual strain in the hybrid SMA-steel reinforcement for the BCJ-HYB specimens was higher than the residual strain in the reinforcement of BCJ-SMA and BCJ-PVA. This could be due to the large plastic deformations and thus lesser residual strains experienced by the steel bar compared to the SMA bars.

Finite element modeling

Finite element models were developed in ABAQUS48, and validated with the experimental results of this study, as discussed in the following subsections.

Material models

The materials were modeled based on the experimental mechanical properties presented in Sect. 2.2. In ABAQUS48, the SMA elements were defined using the newly developed superelasticity material model, which allows direct representation of austenite–martensite transformation behavior under loading, as illustrated in Fig. 15a. The constitutive relationships proposed by Auricchio and Taylor work17,18, Shrestha and Hao25, and Fugazza49 were used as a basis for defining the superelasticity of SMA in ABAQUS48. The mechanical characteristics of SMA used in the FE models were based on the experimental cyclic load test, as presented in Fig. 5a. The elastic-plastic model was used to define the steel reinforcement following the bi-linear stress-strain curve50–52, as shown in Fig. 15b. The modulus of elasticity of steel rebars was 205 GPa; the Poisson’s ratio was 0.3 while the steel density was 7800 kg/m353,54. The inelastic behavior of concrete was modeled using the concrete damage plasticity (CDP) model implemented. The constitutive formulation of CDP model effectively represents the nonlinear compressive and tensile plasticity of concrete, as shown in Fig. 15c. The mechanical properties of the concrete mixtures presented in Table 7 were used to model the concrete following the model code55 and using the CDP generator software developed by Elkady56. The plasticity parameters of concrete are presented in Table 9. The density and Poisson’s ratio of concrete was taken as 2400 kg/m3 and 0.2, respectively57. Figure 15d presents the typical idealized tensile stress-strain response for PVA composite material58.

Elements and interactions

The concrete element and steel bars of the BCJ specimens were modeled using an 8-node solid element (C3D8R) and two-node truss elements (T3D2), respectively. The coupler tying steel to SMA was simulated using tie contact. Perfect bond between reinforcement and concrete was assumed, and thus, the interaction was modeled as embedded region interaction. The discretized mesh used for validating the experimental test results of the BCJ specimens is shown in Fig. 16a. The optimal mesh size, determined through sensitivity analysis to satisfy accuracy requirements while minimizing computational time, was found to be 20, resulting in a total of 9,522 elements for the concrete and steel reinforcement.

Loading and boundary conditions

The BCJ specimens were subjected to a monotonic load at the tip of the beam in this study, since the pinching effect could not be addressed in ABAQUS48 for simulating the performance of reinforced concrete subassemblages under cyclic loading. However, limitations associated with modeling reinforced concrete joints under cyclic loading in ABAQUS48 have been reported in the literature59–61. The adopted monotonic analysis still offers meaningful and reliable assessment of the joint performance. The boundary conditions of the experimental study were simulated by assuming pin supports at column ends, as shown in Fig. 16b. Additionally, the reactions at the supports were calculated by assigning reference points during the test.

FE crack progression

The finite element tension damage contours obtained from the FE models are compared with the experimentally observed crack patterns for all tested specimens, as shown in Fig. 17. In both numerical and experimental results, the highest damage concentration occurred in the beam plastic hinge region adjacent to the column face, consistent with the primary flexural cracking zones observed during testing. The FE model successfully captured the initiation and propagation of cracks along the beam length and their gradual penetration into the joint region as the drift ratio increased. Additionally, based on the FE modeling, the variation in damage intensity among specimens aligns with the experimental findings, where BCJ-CSD exhibited the most severe and localized damage, while BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA, and BCJ-HYB showed more distributed cracking and reduced damage intensity. These correlations demonstrate that the proposed FE model provides a reliable prediction of the flexural damage development and failure modes for different reinforcement configurations.

Plastic hinge lengths were calculated using equations proposed in the literature (Table 10), resulting in values ranging from 0.57 d to 1.57 d, where d is the effective beam depth. The SMA bar length was then selected to balance performance and efficiency. The SMA reinforcement must be sufficiently long to prevent yielding of the remaining steel bars coupled with the SMA bars within the plastic hinge region; however, unnecessary extension of the SMA bars would increase material cost without providing additional structural benefit. Numerical results showed that a 400 mm SMA bar in the plastic hinge region effectively controlled steel yielding while providing comparable stress distribution, as illustrated in Fig. 19. However, it should be clarified that 300 mm of the SMA bar is located within the beam to fully cover the calculated plastic hinge region, while the remaining 100 mm extends into the column. This extension was intentionally provided to shift the stress concentration that may develop at the mechanical coupler between the SMA and the steel reinforcement away from the beam–column interface. Since the maximum bending moment occurs at the column face, placing the coupler directly at this critical section could lead to localized overstressing. The effect of SMA bar length was extensively investigated in a previous parametric study conducted by the authors34.

Prediction of load-displacement response

The comparison between the average experimental and numerical values of the peak load for the tested BCJ specimens is illustrated in Fig. 18. It was observed that the numerical values were closely matching the experimental results with a ratio of numerical to average experimental peak load ranging from 1 to 6%. Alam et al.62 developed finite element models to predict the load-displacement response of the experimental work performed by Youssef et al.27. Alam et al. results showed that the numerical values were lower than the experimental value by 8%. Halahla et al.53, Nahar et al.63 and Higazey et al.34 also predicted numerically the load-displacement response of the experimental work conducted by Youssef et al.27. Their numerical results were 6%, 5% and 4% respectively, higher than the experimental values reported by Youssef et al. Oudah64 indicated that the numerical 3D finite element models predicted well the experimental results of the study28 with variations between numerical and experiential results ranging from 1 to 14%.

Determining the appropriate length of SMA bars in the plastic hinge region is still one of the most difficult aspects of determining the inelastic response of the BCJ. The plastic hinge lengths were calculated following the equations proposed in the literature, as shown in Table 10. It can be observed that the values varied between 0.57d and 1.57d, where d is the effective depth of the beam. The stresses in the reinforcing bars of the BCJ-CSD reached yielding and the plasticity spread out in the plastic hinge region of the beam, as shown in Fig. 19a. Figure 19b compares the stress in reinforcements for the tested specimens BCJ-CSD, BCJ-SMA, BCJ-PVA and BCJ-HYB. The numerical results showed that using SMA bar with a length of 400 mm in the specimens prevented the yielding in the coupled steel rebars and showed comparable stress distribution response with the BCJ-SMA. The higher stresses observed in the BCJ-PVA compared to normal concrete may be attributed to crack bridging, delayed crack propagation, lesser stress distribution, and enhanced bond strength. The lower stresses reported in the BCJ-HYB’s low SMA bar were due to the higher reinforcement ratio compared to the other BCJ, resulting in the steel rebar bearing most of the stresses.

Conclusions

The present study suggests effective methods utilizing SMA bars and PVA fiber composites to improve the seismic performance of typical exterior reinforced concrete beam-column joints (BCJ) in multi-story buildings located in a high seismic zone in Saudi Arabia. The key findings of this investigation are summarized as follows:

The proposed hybrid systems which combines SMA-steel reinforcement or SMA-PVA fibers may serve as an effective solution for the design of exterior RC-BCJ in multi-story buildings situated in high to very high seismic regions, offering excellent self-centering abilities and significant energy dissipation without compromising load capacity.The BCJ specimens reinforced with the PVA fiber composites and the hybrid technique proposed in this study, displayed the following unique characteristics compared to the control BCJ specimen (BCJ-CSD), when tested under cyclic loading.
