Background

Coronary artery anomalies (CAAs) are a broad group of anatomical abnormalities characterized by either an abnormal origin, course, or termination of any of the three major epicardial arteries of the heart and are present in < 1% of the population [1]. These abnormalities may go unnoticed during a person’s lifetime and be incidental findings; however, they can occasionally present acutely or chronically as angina, myocardial infarction, cardiac arrhythmias, or even sudden cardiac death [2]. In patients with any type of suspected abnormality, studies such as invasive coronary angiography (ICA) or cardiac computed tomography angiography (CCTA) should be performed to rule out these abnormalities [1]. The approach will depend on the patient’s presentation and any complications that may arise.

Case presentation

History of presentation and past medical history

A 65-year-old woman with angina pectoris, particularly with moderate to vigorous physical activity, and medical history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia and obesity. Baseline vitals prior to CCTA include heart rate of 55 beats/min and blood pressure of 160/70 mmHg. The patient’s prior studies included the following: Electrocardiogram (ECG): sinus rhythm at 60 bpm with normal axis (30°), QRS duration of 80 ms, corrected QT interval of 400 ms, normal R-wave progression in V1-V6, and a single isolated premature ventricular complex. Echocardiogram: preserved global systolic function, segmental abnormality of the septum, type 2 diastolic dysfunction with normal pressures, without evidence of valvular disease, pericardial effusion or pulmonary hypertension. Ergometry (treadmill test): stress induced ST-segment depression at 03:50 min. Coronary angiography: revealed congenital absence of the left main coronary artery (LMCA), with separate and eccentric ostia for the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and left circumflex artery (LCx) from the left coronary sinus without signs of significant atherosclerotic lesions.

Management

The patient was referred to a scheduled CCTA. During the CCTA, the patient experienced a transient cardiac arrest secondary to ventricular fibrillation (VF), which required immediate electrical cardioversion, resulting in the return of spontaneous circulation. Notably, the LAD originated from a separate ostium from the left coronary sinus with an interarterial course of approximately 29 mm between the ascending aorta and the pulmonary trunk (Figs. 1 and 2). Upon admission to the ward, the patient’s vital signs were heart rate of 92 beats/min, a blood pressure of 140/120 mmHg, a breath rate of 18 breaths/min and an oxygen saturation of 98% at room air.

Due to chronic ischemic disease caused by the congenital anomaly, the patient underwent on-pump coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) with a left internal mammary artery-to-left anterior descending artery (LIMA-LAD) and exposure and mobilization of the LAD along its interarterial course, where it was ligated with an hemoclip to prevent competitive flow from the anomalous native vessel prior to LIMA-LAD bypass and first septal branch.

Outcome and follow-up

The postoperative course was uneventful; the patient recovered with no major complications and was discharged from the hospital 12 days after the surgery.

Discussion

The prevalence of an absent LMCA with separate origins of LAD and LCx as reported in the literature reviewed is < 1% [3]. While many such anomalies are benign, an interarterial course – as seen in this case – is categorized as high-risk due to the potential for “slit-like” compression between the aorta and pulmonary artery during physical exertion, an acute takeoff angle or and intramural course [2]. In this patient, LAD originates from a separate ostium from the left coronary sinus in the absence of a LMCA and follows an interarterial course between the aorta and the pulmonary artery, placing the vessel within a dynamically compressible anatomical corridor. During states of increased hemodynamic stress - such as physical exertion, sympathetic activation, or even rapid contrast administration during CCTA - both the aortic root and pulmonary trunk undergo systolic expansion due to elevated intraluminal pressure and vascular distensibility. This simultaneous radial enlargement reduces the interarterial space available for the anomalous LAD producing a functional external compression of its proximal segment which is frequently intramural or semi-intramural within the aortic wall in such anomalies. The resulting phenomenon can be conceptualized as a “scissor effect,” in which the pulsatile walls of the aorta and pulmonary artery approximate during systole, transiently narrowing the coronary lumen and impairing coronary blood flow. Acute increases in preload, afterload, and heart rate—whether exercise-induced or procedure-related—can therefore exacerbate this dynamic obstruction, precipitating critical myocardial ischemia and malignant ventricular arrhythmias, such as the ventricular fibrillation that led to cardiac arrest in this case [2]. This mechanical compression explains the patient’s positive stress test and subsequent ventricular arrhythmia [4].

Although anatomically classified as a high-risk coronary anomaly, this patient exhibited only long-standing exercise intolerance and ventricular extrasystoles, without a major cardiovascular event until the age of 65. This clinical course supports the concept that potentially lethal coronary anomalies may follow a highly heterogeneous trajectory [5–7]. The chronic limitation to exertion suggests the presence of recurrent low-grade myocardial ischemia rather than extensive acute ischemic episodes. Prolonged exposure to sublethal ischemia may induce ischemic preconditioning and partial myocardial adaptation, reduce the likelihood of transmural infarction while not eliminating susceptibility to malignant ventricular arrhythmias under intense physiological stress [8–10]. In addition, the patient’s symptoms likely promoted behavioral self-limitation of physical activity, maintaining myocardial oxygen demand below the threshold required to produce complete dynamic luminal collapse. This adaptive reduction in exertional stress represents a plausible explanation for the absence of sudden cardiac death at earlier stages of life, consistent with prior observations that some anomalous coronary courses remain clinically silent or mildly symptomatic for decades [6–7]. Age-related vascular changes may also have contributed to the delayed presentation. Progressive stiffening of the aortic root and pulmonary trunk reduces arterial distensibility and could paradoxically attenuate the magnitude of dynamic interarterial compression for many years, maintaining coronary flow within a subcritical range. Over time, however, the interaction of additional factors—such as hypertension, possible atherosclerotic changes, or distal coronary remodeling—may shift this equilibrium toward more severe ischemia and electrical instability [6]. Finally, although collateral vessels were not documented or seen in this patient, the potential development of partial collateral circulation from the circumflex or other coronary territories may have provided supplementary perfusion to the anterior wall during episodes of transient narrowing of the interarterial segment. Such compensation could explain the absence of prior extensive infarction and the patient’s survival into older age with a predominantly chronic symptomatic profile rather than a catastrophic presentation [10]. Taken together, this case illustrates how an anatomically high-risk coronary anomaly may behave clinically as chronically symptomatic rather than truly incidental, and how the occurrence of ventricular fibrillation during coronary CT angiography likely represents the moment at which the anatomical substrate crossed the threshold into unacceptable risk, thereby justifying definitive surgical management in accordance with contemporary guideline-based risk stratification [6].

Currently, there are several imaging techniques for the diagnosis of coronary artery abnormalities. The American College of Cardiology (ACC) and the American Heart Association (AHA) 2025 Adult Congenital Heart Disease Management Guidelines recommend CCTA or cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) to evaluate an anomalous coronary artery. The current gold standard technique for studying CAAs is multidetector CCTA, which allows for detailed visualization of high-risk anatomical features because of its superior spatial resolution [1]. New imaging technology is undoubtedly essential for detecting more cases that may have previously gone undetected by other studies.

When detecting CAA, the age, symptoms, vascular anatomy, and the presence of ventricular ischemia or ventricular arrhythmias should be considered to determine the appropriate treatment. This patient presents with a high-risk variant that manifests in myocardial ischemia, leading to potentially lethal arrhythmias due to the interarterial course of the LAD. All of this supports the decision to perform coronary artery bypass grafting as a definitive treatment, which is recommended by the ACC/AHA 2025 guidelines [1]. This restores normal blood flow to the affected area of myocardial tissue. The average age of diagnosis of congenital coronary anomalies according to the literature is 50 to 70 years and mostly incidentally [15]. This is a condition that is present from birth and can be diagnosed during the pediatric age as well as in young adults and older adults; therefore, it is important to consider the age of diagnosis for the appropriate treatment of the patient. With a case like this, but in a pediatric or young adult patient, the surgical treatment would be different; Anatomical reimplantation of the abnormal coronary artery into the correct sinus or unroofing (decompression of the intramural tract) is preferred [15]. In young adults, ostium translocation could also be considered. At these ages, coronary revascularization is less preferred due to the risk of flow competition in the functional native artery [15]. In middle-aged adult patients (36–55 years), individualized treatment is preferred. Reimplantation is performed if anatomically viable, and if not, coronary revascularization is performed. In older adult patients, as in the case of this patient. Reimplantation is not recommended as the surgical risk is high due to age and potential atherosclerosis [15]. Coronary revascularization is the safest and most effective option in older adults, as it avoids complex maneuvers and reduces complications. However, when choosing the surgical approach, the anatomical characteristics, symptoms, and the presence of ischemia must also be taken into account.

Conclusions

The low prevalence of coronary artery anomalies, the clinical history and complications presented in this patient make this case important in order not to overlook an etiology such as this as a possible cause of chronic exertional intolerance and other related symptoms, thus providing the patient with a better quality of life and improved long-term survival. When this rare anomaly is detected in the population, clinicians must identify the risk of fatal events and determine appropriate management based on age, symptoms, vascular anatomy, and the presence of arrhythmia.
