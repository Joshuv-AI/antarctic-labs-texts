Revisiting dimensionality in measurement of sense of coherence among rural healthcare workers using network analysis
Authors: Emma L. Cordon, Mark McEvoy, Timothy Skinner, Carina Chan, Angela Crombie, Evan Stanyer, Stephen Begg, Peter Faulkner, Anne McEvoy, Kevin Masman, Laura Bamforth, Xia Li, Pedro Henrique Ribeiro Santiago
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic adversely impacted the mental health and wellbeing of healthcare workers (HCW). Drawn from the theory of salutogenesis, Sense of Coherence (SOC) assesses the extent to which individuals perceive their life experiences as comprehensible, manageable, and meaningful, and is considered an important health promoting resource for HCWs’ resilience and coping under stressful circumstances. However, little is known about its measurement in HCW populations, and the current discrepancy regarding SOC dimensionality continues to limit salutogenic research and application. Previous studies have used factor analytic, Rasch and SEM approaches for assessing SOC dimensionality, which have since been challenged for accuracy. This study applied a novel psychometric methodology, namely Exploratory Graph Analysis (EGA), to revisit the dimensionality of the SOC-13 scale in a regional and rural Australian HCW cohort at 12-month follow-up (n = 649; complete SOC-13 cases n = 597), broadly representative of the Loddon Mallee healthcare workforce demographics. Results support a refined 12-item, unidimensional SOC instrument, achieved by removing one redundant item (SOCQ2) highly overlapping in content with SOCQ3, and demonstrated strong internal consistency and validity related to depression, anxiety, and resilience. Further research is needed to explore dimensionality across populations and to improve missing data treatment in network analyses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40880-1.

Introduction

Sense of coherence (SOC) is a key salutogenic construct described as a coping resource that fosters a positive mindset and resourcefulness, enabling individuals with a strong SOC to navigate stressful situations and environments more effectively1. Importantly, the measurement of SOC demonstrates a strong, positive correlation with mental health in the general population1,2. It has also been previously observed that higher SOC among healthcare workers (HCWs) (e.g. nurses, paramedics, ICU staff) working in stressful healthcare settings has been linked to fewer adverse mental health issues3–5. While strengthening SOC has been identified as beneficial to the promotion of mental health and wellbeing working in stressful contexts (e.g. COVID-19 pandemic), methodological and conceptual challenges continue to limit the use of SOC as a health promoting resource. For instance, the mechanisms that underpin SOC are not fully understood and further research is required to design salutogenic interventions to promote enhanced SOC (comprehensiblity, manageability, meaningfulness)6. Furthermore, there is debate regarding the dimensionality and measurement of SOC, limiting its broader application7.

One of the most frequently used instruments to measure SOC is the SOC-13 scale8. The SOC-13 is a shortened version of the original 29-item Orientation of Life Questionnaire (OLQ) scale developed by Antonovsky9, which has since been translated into more than 30 languages and applied in over 32 countries (10 p.11). To develop the original OLQ from which the SOC-13 scale emerged, Antonovsky8,17 used the mapping-sentence technique drawn from facet theory11. This approach aimed to operationalise the generalised orientation of the SOC construct, with the intention that a wide variety of stimuli were represented among the scale items. This was achieved through the purposeful, theoretically guided design of scale items. Each individual item includes four facets to describe a stimulus, and a fifth, the SOC facet, expressing one of the three dimensions (comprehensibility, manageability, or meaningfulness). Thus, the SOC-13 was derived from the original 29-item OLQ through a deliberate reduction process guided by psychometric properties and the three interrelated components of Antonovsky’s salutogenic theory namely “Comprehensibility” (cognitive component—five items), “Manageability” (behavioural component—four items), and “Meaningfulness” (motivational component—four items), producing a single total score. This highlights the inextricably linked SOC components, making it problematic to derive individual sub-scores for comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness (12 p.731).

Based on the evidence from psychometric studies so far, the SOC-13 reports strong psychometric properties13. Systematic review evidence reports a strong relationship between overall SOC and measures of quality of life and health14, while the SOC-13 is considered to have adequate to excellent internal consistency across various populations with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.70 and 0.9213. However, as emphasised in a future directions paper on the evaluation of SOC-13 psychometric properties, its dimensionality remains a topic of ongoing debate in the salutogenic literature7. There continues to be mixed support15,16 for both the original one-factor SOC-13 structure proposed by Antonovsky12 and various multi-dimensional factor structures13. For example, one-17 and three-factor13,18,19 structures appear most commonly suggested for the SOC-13, yet substantial differences in the item composition for each SOC-13 factor are reported1,13,20. Notably, the study of SOC-13 dimensionality has relied solely on varying traditional factor analytical techniques (e.g. principal-components analysis; confirmatory factor analysis; exploratory factor analysis, structural equation modelling—SEM)13,17,19,21. However, in several situations (e.g., highly intercorrelated dimensions), such techniques have not provided an accurate estimation of dimensionality as have more recent methods such as network analyses22. Modern psychometric approaches such as Rasch modelling (within the item response theory (IRT)—framework) have also been applied to the SOC-13, generally supporting a unidimensional structure while noting occasional item-level misfit23,24. Despite these advances, debate persists. Overall, these polarising views demonstrate the significant limitation for the use and measurement of SOC with the SOC-13 scale, particularly how the instrument has been scored and should be scored in future research, and critically how results should be interpreted.

The present study aims to examine the validity and reliability of the SOC-13 scale and revisit the unresolved dimensionality of the SOC-13 scale in a regional and rural HCW cohort using a cutting-edge psychometric technique, namely Exploratory Graph Analysis (EGA). This rapidly evolving network approach was selected to avoid the limitations associated with factor analytical and or/eigenvalue-based methods (e.g., parallel analysis (PA), minimum average partial procedure (MAP), Kaiser-Guttman rule and the Bayesian information criteria (BIC)), traditionally used in psychology research to estimate the dimensionality of data. These limitations refer to gold-standard factor analytical and/or eigenvalue-based methods such as PA becoming less accurate in certain specific conditions such as when factors were highly correlated in larger sample sizes, in which EGA displays a better model performance25. The literature suggests that the dimensions of the SOC-13 are highly correlated, for which the EGA approach is well suited12. Network analysis offers an effective way to conceptualise and visualise complex psychological mechanisms as a set of inter-related variables26. Additionally, factor analytical procedures usually require several decisions (i.e. which “factor rotation” procedure to use) increasing the “researchers’ degrees of freedom”25. On the other hand, EGA output is displayed graphically in a colour-coded network making it intuitive for interpretation and more accessible to researchers and clinicians without expertise in statistics. Finally, in accordance to the network theory of mental health disorders27, EGA considers behaviours individually (instead of belonging to a “construct”) and for this reason, can provide a much better description (compared to prior factor analytical methods) of the relationship between the three inter-related SOC components (i.e., meaningfulness, manageability, and comprehensibility). We consider this to be a useful approach in the further investigation of protective factors for HCW psychological wellbeing for future pandemic preparedness.

Moreover, the promotion of psychological wellbeing among regional and rural HCW will likely play a pivotal role in their future recruitment and retention. There is evidence suggesting SOC may be an important coping resource among HCW in future pandemic contexts. In accordance with best practice for the development and validation of scales used in health, social, and behavioural research28, the investigation of the SOC-13 scale dimensionality in a sample of regional and rural HCW can be used to inform whether an alternative factor structure is required for this HCW cohort. SOC dimensionality has been previously described only for general and other specific populations12,15,29.

Although the SOC-13 scale has been widely studied, it has not been validated among HCW in rural settings. This represents a critical gap given the relevance of SOC as a construct for resilience and wellbeing in a workforce facing unique challenges such as geographic isolation, workforce shortages and systemic limitations30. These contextual factors may influence the measurement and interpretation of SOC, making psychometric evidence specific to rural settings essential for informing interventions that strengthen coping resources and support future pandemic preparedness. As such, in the current study, we aim to evaluate the SOC-13: (1) item score distributions; (2) item redundancy; (3) dimensionality; (4) criterion validity; and (5) reliability. Specifically, we asked: Using a novel network analysis approach, what is the dimensionality and internal consistency of the SOC-13 scale validated in a cohort of regional/rural HCWs? Based on previous studies, it was hypothesised that the SOC-13 scale would be (a) multidimensional, and (b) have adequate criterion validity (McDonald’s omega > 0.70) for measures of depressive and anxiety symptoms, as two constructs known to be inversely associated with SOC1, and positively associated with the construct of psychological resilience. To the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have employed network psychometrics to evaluate the dimensionality, reliability, and criterion validity of the SOC-13 among HCW in a regional and rural setting.

Method

The study received approval from the Bendigo Health Human Research Ethics Committee (ID: 68,671). All methods were carried out according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent to participate was taken from all participants to participate in the study.

Sample and setting

Data was collected from the Loddon Mallee Healthcare Worker COVID-19 Study (LMHCWCS), a prospective longitudinal cohort study that aims to examine the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on health and wellbeing of rural and regional HCW. Both clinical and non-clinical roles were included to reflect the true composition of the healthcare workforce. Self-administered online questionnaires are conducted at baseline, 12-, and 24-months follow-up over 3 years31. The current study used data from the 12-month follow-up sample (n = 649 participants) during which data was collected between November 2021 and May 2022. The Loddon Mallee region was selected to represent the demographic profile of the broader, Victorian rural and regional healthcare workforce across age, gender, and profession. HCW were drawn from 19 rural and regional health services, five community health centres and the Murray Primary Health Network (Murray PHN)31. Importantly, the follow-up sample was largely representative of the baseline cohort, with only minor differences identified, as confirmed through personal communication with M. McEvoy, 14 December 2023.

As seen in Fig. 1, it is important to note that from the original 1313 baseline participants, only 649 participants completed at least one question from the 12-month follow-up survey (response rate of 49%). Of these, 621 provided a response to at least one item SOC-13 item, and 597 answered all SOC-13 items. All analyses pertaining to the SOC-13 (only included in the 12-month survey) were restricted to complete cases (n = 597) (i.e., participants who responded to all 13 SOC items). Measures

The sense of coherence—13 scale (SOC-13)

The SOC-13 was used for this investigation. The scale consists of 13 items, responses to which are provided on a semantic 7-point Likert scale, where points 1 and 7 indicate extreme feelings about feelings and statements about how one’s life is experienced (e.g. where 1 may be “a source of deep pleasure and satisfaction” and 7 is “a source of pain and boredom”). The SOC scale measures three factors: comprehensibility (five items, e.g. ‘Do you have the feeling that you are in an unfamiliar situation and don’t know what to do?’); manageability (four items, e.g., ‘Has it happened that people whom you counted on disappointed you?’); and meaningfulness (four items; ‘Do you have the feeling that you don’t really care about what goes on around you?’). The questionnaire items scores are summed to create a total score that ranges between 13 and 91 points, a higher score indicating a stronger SOC8,9.

Complementary measures

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; 32) was used to measure depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 is a self-administered questionnaire consisting of nine items measuring the severity of depressive symptoms. Total scores range from 0 and 27, with scores ≥ 10 representing clinically significant depressive symptoms. Furthermore, cut-off points represent thresholds for minimal (1–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19) and severe depression (20–27)32. The PHQ-9 demonstrates high internal consistency (α = 0.89, 0.86) and test–retest reliability (0.84)32.

The Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7; 33), is a 7-item, self-report measure used to screen for Generalized Anxiety Disorder. Patients rate their frequency of symptoms within the last 2 weeks on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), with a possible total score ranging between 0 and 21. Higher scores indicate higher levels of anxiety symptoms. The GAD-7 has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.92) and good test re-test reliability of 0.83 in primary care settings33.

Psychological resilience was measured using the 6-item, self-assessment: the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS; 34). Respondents are presented with six statements (three positively worded items, and three negatively worded items) and are required to indicate their level of agreement/disagreement using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree), to 5 (strongly agree). A possible total score ranging between 6 and 30. Cut-off scores indicate different levels of resilience: ≤ 3.00 (low), and ≥ 4.30 (high)35. The BRS instrument has good internal consistency (α = 0.08–0.91) and test-re-test reliability (0.62–0.69)34.

Network model and estimation

In conducting an EGA it was necessary to estimate a network from our data using the Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM), which models the structure of conditional dependence among multiple random variables (i.e., item Likert-scale responses in the SOC-13 questionnaire)39. In the GGM, nodes represent items and edges represent the structure of conditional dependence (i.e., partial correlations) between items. To calculate the sample size needed for estimating the network model, we used Powerly (version 1.8.6) in R37. For the original SOC-13 scale (13 nodes, density = 0.4, sensitivity = 0.6, power = 0.8), the recommended sample size was approximately n = 1181 participants.

Considering the ordinal nature of the SOC-13 items (i.e., response categories ranged from “very seldom” to “very often”), a polychoric correlation matrix was used as input to estimate the network models40. In accordance with best practices, we reported the polychoric correlation matrix of the (final) network model in Supplementary Fig. 1. As the partial correlations were rarely likely to be zero, a penalised maximum likelihood (ML) estimation, the Graphical Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selector Operator (GLASSO)41, was applied. In the GLASSO, very small partial correlations that are not meaningful are reduced to zero, producing a sparser and more interpretable network, and also diminishing overfitting. The tuning parameter λ was selected based on the minimisation of the Extended Bayesian Information Criteria (EBIC)38, and the hyperparameter γ was set to be 0.5 which is generally a good choice. The network was plotted as a visual representation using the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm42 which plots the nodes according to the strength of their connections (edge weights) and the colour of the edges indicate whether partial correlations are positive or negative, depicted using colour and thickness of connected edges. A parametric bootstrap EGA (2500 replicas) was then applied to the network estimation to evaluate structural consistency, which refers to the proportion of times each EGA-identified dimension was exactly replicated across the bootstrap samples, and item stability, which refers to the proportion of times that each item clustered in their EGA-identified dimension. To conduct the psychometric analysis, we then proceeded to evaluate (1) item response categories, (2) item redundancy, (3) dimensionality, (4) network loadings, (5) reliability, and (6) criterion validity.

Item response categories

Prior to estimating the network model, the first step of analysis was to assess the adequacy of item responses across the SOC-13 scale. This was done by investigating the floor and ceiling effects across all SOC-13 items (i.e., identifying responses rarely used or over-utilised). Floor and ceiling effects are present when the endorsement of any particular response category exceeds 15% of the expected score under a random (uniform) distribution43. Furthermore, we also evaluated whether there were enough responses to any of the response categories44. Response categories with a very small number of responses were collapsed to make an amalgamated response category, and items with strong floor and ceiling effects would be deleted.

Item redundancy

The second step of analysis was to evaluate item redundancy after the initial network estimation was conducted. Item redundancy is established to evaluate whether the items measure the same causal component. A network should be composed of unique causal components45; thus, an individual item must measure a distinct emotion, behaviour, or cognition46. So, in the case of two items being too similar in content, they may be measuring the same causal component (i.e., not unique). Redundancy amongst SOC-13 items was evaluated using the weighted topological overlap (wTO) statistic47 as recommended by Christensen and colleagues48. Furthermore, recent best practice for assessing item redundancy prescribes a single cut-off wTO value of 0.25 when using EBICglasso estimation48. When the wTO statistic indicated potential redundancy, we examined the content of identified items to evaluate whether the items measure the same causal component. In the case where items measure the same causal component but are distinct enough from each other, they can be combined into a sum score or latent variable46. Otherwise, if the items measure the same causal component, but are too similar in content, one of them may be excluded45.

Dimensionality, item stability and network loadings

The third step of the analysis was the evaluation of network communities. In this case, these communities reveal the sub-scales of the SOC-13 questionnaire, such that subscale scores are the summation of item scores belonging to each unique community45. To estimate the number of network communities, the Walktrap community-detection algorithm was employed49. Given the number of dimensions detected in the sample is subject to sampling variation, we also evaluated the stability of the identified communities using an approach called Bootstrap Exploratory Graph Analysis (bootEGA)36. The number of bootstrap samples was set to 2500 to examine the structural consistency of the EGA-identified dimensions. Structural consistency refers to the frequency with which EGA-identified dimensions were exactly recovered across iterative bootstrap samples. Adequate structural consistency is achieved when each dimension is perfectly recovered in more than 75% of the bootstrap samples36. To complement the structural consistency measure, item stability was also evaluated to quantify the robustness of each item’s placement to each dimension. Item stability is the frequency with which each item clustered in their EGA-identified dimension (with values ranging from 0 to 100%)36. Item stability was considered adequate when it was greater than 75%.

After a network community structure was defined, an evaluation of network loadings was performed. Network loadings represent the contribution of each item (node) to a coherent dimension (cluster) within a network, and can range from small (0.0–0.15), moderate (0.16–0.25), or large (0.26–0.35)45. Network loadings are used to identify items that contribute to more than one dimensions (i.e., cross-loadings, > 0.15 in more than one dimension) or items that did not load substantively across any dimension (< 0.16 on all communities)50.

Reliability and criterion validity

The fourth step in the analysis was the evaluation of the SOC-13 scale’s reliability and criterion validity. We employed McDonald’s omega Ω coefficient to assess internal consistency of the SOC-13 scale owing to several advantages over traditionally used Cronbach’s α, such as not relying on the assumption of (1) tau-equivalence, and (2) congeneric model without correlated errors (i.e., models where all items are locally independent as per51). McDonald’s omega Ω values above 0.70 are considered adequate for research purposes as outlined in52.

Given the negative impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on HCW mental health, and the protective influence of psychological resilience53, we assessed criterion validity using Spearman’s correlation coefficients between the final SOC scale and depressive and anxiety symptoms, and resilience scores. Spearman’s was chosen due to non-normal item distributions (skewness −0.31 to −1.28; kurtosis 2.03–3.82). The expectation was that respondents with higher SOC-13 total scores would exhibit lower depressive and anxiety total scores, along with greater psychological resilience.

Results

Sample characteristics

Participant sociodemographic characteristics at the 12-month survey are shown in Table 1. The 12-month survey response sample included 649 HCW who were predominantly older than 40 years, female (83.6%), working in frontline nursing roles, and had completed up to university level education (76%). There were no substantive differences in sociodemographic characteristics between the overall 12-month survey response sample compared with the complete case sample who responded to all 13 items on the SOC instrument (Table 1).

Item response categories

Analysis of the adequacy of the response categories from the SOC-13 demonstrated they were typically left-skewed and did not present any substantive issues with floor or ceiling effects (see Supplementary Figs. 2, 3 and 4 showing half-violin box plots, histograms, and item responses relative to ceiling threshold). Endorsement of several categories such as “very seldom” or “never happened”, was very low. For example, in Item SOCQ4 (“Until now life has had:”), only 4 respondents out of 597 endorsed the “no clear goals or purposes at all” response. As such, infrequent endorsement of the lower Likert point responses (e.g., points 1 and 2) across all SOC-13 items, resulted in combining these categories into a single category, resulting in a 6-point SOC-13 scale.

Item redundancy

We proceeded to evaluate item redundancy. The strongest wTO statistic was observed between Item SOCQ2 (“Has it happened in the past year that you were surprised by the behaviour of people whom you thought you knew well?”) and Item SOCQ3 (“Has it happened that people whom you counted on disappointed you?”) (wTO = 0.482), indicating potential redundancy (see Supplementary Table 2). On examining the “topic” content between the two redundant items, we evaluated that both items measured the same causal component—but SOCQ3 was conceptually broader and more representative of the construct. SOCQ2 was considered narrower and more subjective in wording. Based on this evaluation, SOCQ2 was excluded to reduce redundancy while preserving the conceptual SOC framework.

Twelve-item network

Dimensionality, stability, and network loadings

Following the exclusion of Item SOCQ2, descriptive statistics for the revised SOC-12 scale were calculated (M = 61.6, SD = 12.1, skewness = − 0.37), and the network model was re-estimated. The Walktrap algorithm identified one community displayed in Fig. 2b. Excellent structural consistency was observed for the community (100%). Furthermore, item stability was 100% for all twelve items, that is, the items were all assigned to the same dimension across all bootstrap samples, and the network communities are displayed in Supplementary Fig. 5. Upon inspection of the final 12-item SOC network loadings, seven items displayed large (> 0.25) network loadings on the EGA-identified dimension, and five items displayed moderate (> 0.15) network loadings. The network loadings are displayed in Supplementary Table 3.

Internal consistency and criterion validity

The final SOC-12 scale displayed good internal consistency (Ω = 0.89, 95% CI:0.87,0.90). There were negative and significant correlations between SOC-12 and depressive symptoms (ρ: − 0.72; p < 0.001) and anxiety symptoms (ρ: − 0.70; p < 0.001). Furthermore, there was a significant and positive correlation between SOC-12 and psychological resilience (BRS total score), (ρ: 0.56; p < 0.001). As such, the associations observed between the SOC-12 total scores and depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), anxiety symptoms (GAD-7) and psychological resilience (BRS) were according to theoretical expectations, providing initial evidence for SOC-12 criterion validity.1

Discussion

Enhanced SOC has been associated with greater psychological wellbeing outcomes in stressful contexts, such as HCW exposed to high levels of work-related stress54. It is critical to establish the validity and internal consistency of the SOC scale of interest, and to develop understanding around unresolved research questions regarding its psychometric properties, namely its dimensionality. Knowledge about the dimensionality of SOC scales may help answer a range of new research questions, many of which have practical implications for HCW wellbeing outcomes. However, despite the plausibility of enhancing SOC for the promotion of HCW psychological wellbeing for pandemic preparedness, there are long-running controversies regarding the dimensionality of the main SOC measure (SOC-13). Our study used a new network psychometrics approach, EGA, which addresses limitations of both traditional factor analytic and modern psychometric techniques. EGA can estimate dimensionality better under certain conditions (e.g. highly correlated factors) and detect item redundancy without parametric assumptions. This approach also included steps that examined the sample’s item response distribution and item redundancies. A revised 12-item SOC (SOC-12) scale demonstrated adequate psychometric properties and a unidimensional structure, and is considered ready for use in other rural HCW populations following replication.

This study found that one dimension best described the dimensionality of the revised SOC-12 scale, lending support to Antonovsky’s original global SOC definition8,12. In 1987, Antonovsky developed the original 29-item Orientation of Life Questionnaire (OLQ), from which the shorter 13-item scale (SOC-13) was later derived. In constructing the SOC scale, each item was consciously guided by the facet-theoretical (FT) approach11, drawing on four facets to describe the widest range of stimuli (modality, source, demand, time) and a fifth SOC facet expressing the three SOC components (meaningfulness, comprehensibility, or manageability). As such, operationalising the SOC construct included the three individual dimensions which are considered ‘insolubly interlaced’, suggesting no basis to deriving sub-scores for the three SOC dimensions (12 p.731). However, this outcome is contrary to others who later have found support for a multi-dimensional SOC structure13,55,56. Antonovsky’s work in 1993 and 1996, and Hochwälder’s future perspectives paper in 2019, have therefore emphasised the potential of a revised SOC scale.

Aligned with Antonovsky’s original SOC scale, our study found support for a one-dimensional factor structure after accounting for item redundancy8,17. While supporting a unidimensional structure, redundancy in two items of the SOC-13 may reflect something unique about the rural healthcare context or characteristics of the rural HWC cohort. These findings as in other studies, have highlighted that unaccounted redundancies may change the dimensionality of an instrument57. In response, we recommend an initial step prior to running an EGA, to include both item distribution and item redundancy analyses in future dimensionality studies. An initial analysis was taken in the current study to assess the adequacy of item response distribution of the SOC-13 scale. Due to low response frequencies in the lowest Likert point categories (1 and 2), these were collapsed across all 13 items for our HCW sample, to ensure categories remained meaningful to participants, and to maintain robustness even in smaller studies. Further, this reduced bias in the estimation of the polychoric and partial correlations, as recommended in network psychometric methodology40,58. In terms of measurement precision, it is well-established that collapsing categories can reduce reliability59. However, in our case, collapsing was necessary to prevent bias in network parameters due to very low response frequencies. While this resulted in an even number of responses (six), collapsing additional categories (i.e. 6 and 7) would have led to unnecessary information loss59. Future studies are recommended to evaluate whether a 6-point or 7-point scale is most appropriate in HCW populations.

Steps conducted prior to the EGA analysis for the SOC scale also identified item redundancy (Items SOCQ2 “Has it happened in the past that you were surprised by the behaviour of people whom you thought you knew well?” and SOCQ3 “Has it happened that people whom you counted on disappointed you?”). Item SOCQ2 was excluded based on the content overlap with Item SOCQ3. This finding is consistent with previous psychometric research including those using traditional factor analytical approaches and Rasch modelling, where correlated errors were found between SOCQ2 and SOCQ315,23,60,61. A recent network analysis of the SOC-13 scale conducted by Portoghese et al.56 also suggested the items both ‘appear to measure an ability to understand and manage past experiences where others negatively surprised them’. It is important to bear in mind the possible bias in cross-cultural adaptations of the SOC-13 and variations in sub-populations. The decision to remove Item SOCQ2 was based on the relative subjectivity of its wording, which can be interpreted as negative or positive ‘surprise’. After the removal of Item SOCQ2 in our study, the revised 12-item SOC scale (SOC-12) exhibited adequate psychometric properties, providing an improved and robust measure of SOC for the further investigation into factors promoting wellbeing among rural and regional HCW.

An initial network analysis in our study using the 13-item SOC scale identified three dimensions, whereas after the exclusion of the identified redundant Item SOCQ2, a subsequent EGA indicated that the SOC-12 scale comprised only one dimension, maintaining good psychometric properties compared to the initial SOC-13 scale. This inconsistency is likely due to the item redundancy detected in the early analyses prior to running our study’s second network model, influencing the three-dimension structure within this HCW sample. A striking observation to emerge from the initial SOC-13 scale network estimation is that one entire suggested dimension was composed only of items SOC2 and SOCQ3, subsequently influencing the modified SOC-12 scale’s dimensionality. Furthermore, the network structure estimated for the SOC-13 scale in this study differed from the hypothesised 3-factor structures previously suggested13. Therefore, it is not clear whether it can be assumed that in the absence of this redundancy amongst another population, a true three-factor structure would be defined. In this case, redundancies identified in previous studies may help explain some of the variation evidenced in dimensionality of the SOC-13 scale. By way of illustration, a recent network estimation of the SOC-13 scale conducted by Portoghese and colleagues56 differs from the current study’s findings by identifying a three-dimension solution for the SOC scale. Portoghese et al., 56 conducted their study in a large (n = 5642) sample of Italian adults in which item redundancies were not considered prior to network estimation. Future studies need to further investigate this issue of item redundancy. Redundancies do not contribute to unique information in a network and must be managed to reduce overestimating the dimensionality of a network56.

This research found the modified SOC-12 scale to be a reliable instrument, consistent with earlier SOC-13 studies13. Similarly, the criterion validity of the SOC-12 scale was established, with (a) sense of coherence correlated with constructs related to mental health (e.g., depressive and anxiety symptomology) and (b) psychological resilience as used in prior studies albeit in different populations12,62. The results from the current study indicated that stronger SOC scores were associated with lower depressive and anxiety symptomology, and higher psychological resilience, aligning with theoretical predictions.

The current study provides a comprehensive investigation of the SOC-13 psychometric properties, by conducting steps such as the evaluation of item response category and redundancy, prior to network estimation among a regional and rural HCW cohort during a pandemic context. We investigated the SOC-13 dimensionality using a cutting-edge network analysis within a sample at the 12-month survey time point, including almost 600 complete responses to the SOC-13 scale. Network models have been recently shown to be superior to factor models in identifying the true number of dimensions under certain conditions (e.g. highly correlated factors) when evaluating psychological phenomena (e.g. psychopathology27).

This study provides an additional exploratory step prior to network estimation that improves the reliability and usability of the SOC scale for a HCW population. We collapsed categories to form a 6-point Likert scale that best captured this cohort’s SOC instrument responses and removed a redundant item to develop a revised SOC-12 scale which exhibited good psychometric properties. Both steps could have been affected by selection bias, however the absence of major differences in characteristics between the baseline, 12-month follow-up survey and SOC complete case samples make this unlikely. Using the 12-item SOC scale, our EGA replicated the original one-dimensional factor structure proposed by Antonovsky8,12. This SOC-12 scale will prove useful in generating future hypotheses regarding the association of SOC and HCW psychological wellbeing during times of pandemic-induced stress. Importantly, the validated SOC-12 scale offers practical advantages for strengthening coping capacity and informing policy to enhance HCW wellbeing. Its conceptual and psychometric integrity now make it suitable for guiding interventions that promote resilience and adaptability. Embedding SOC-informed strategies into workforce wellbeing frameworks can optimise resource allocation and support pandemic preparedness planning. Finally, while Portoghese et al., 56 recently compared the dimensionality of the SOC-13 scale estimated from a network analysis with traditional factorial methods in a large convenience sample (n = 5,642) of Italian adults, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that validated an SOC scale using a network psychometric approach for a HCW population.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. One important limitation is that item redundancy may differ across populations, so the overlap identified between SOCQ2 and SOCQ3 may not exist in another population and therefore influence the network structure. Methodological direction in network literature includes options to combine latent variables48, remove one of the redundant items, or include both items46. However, these methods must be attended to on a case-by-case basis of individual questionnaires and their items. A psychological network should comprise unique causal components, that is, each item on the SOC-13 scale under investigation should measure a unique behaviour, cognition, or emotion46. We evaluated redundancy in this study using first and foremost the wTO statistic, followed by content examination of the two identified items to evaluate whether they measured the same topic (i.e. the unique construct that is considered a unique causal component). In this case, we took guidance from de Ron et al., 46 and excluded items (SOCQ2) on the grounds that it was not distinct enough from SOCQ3 topic content. By contrast, a recent network analysis examining the SOC-13 scale combined identified redundant items and estimated them as latent variables56. Arguably, the retention of both items in the final version of the questionnaire (when they are unnecessarily similar), increases the response burden for participants, and the redundancy must subsequently be accounted for in the overestimation of the scale’s reliability. Attending to item redundancy and item responses in future studies would help establish a greater degree of clarity on this matter.

Another potential limitation is sample attrition and reliance on complete-case analysis which may have introduced bias. Of the 649 participants at 12 months, 621 provided a response to at least one item of the SOC-13 questionnaire, with 597 participants answering all thirteen SOC questions. Considering that the proportion of missing values on individual SOC items was about 24%, missingness may have influenced the findings63. However, we believe that substantial selection bias due to missing data is unlikely to be responsible for the network structure observed in this study as there were no major differences in participant characteristics between those who completed the baseline questionnaire and those who completed the 12-month follow-up questionnaire (personal communication M. McEvoy, 6 May 2024) or those who completed all items of the SOC-13 scale. A small proportion of the missingness was due to a sub-group of participants who did not respond to certain questions in the SOC-13 scale, which might have been the result of a specific contextual issue. For example, Antonovsky12 highlights the ‘poorest’ items responded to in a study of elderly participants were those worded around what was being projected “in the next 10 years”. This is deemed unlikely since there was no evidence indicating any single SOC item in our study was answered incorrectly or its wording was misunderstood (i.e. no floor effects were revealed43). We suggest that future studies further investigate the nature of missing data from SOC-scale items in other HCW populations, as non-response of single SOC items or non-response of the entire SOC scale may introduce selection bias that could result in a spurious network structure. Given that no established methods exist for the imputation of missing data in network analysis studies64, multiple imputation methods for missing data in the context of EGA should be developed to allow researchers to explore the impacts of missing data on the network structure and dimensionality of the scale under investigation. Additionally, because data collection occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, participants responses may have been influenced by pandemic-related stressors and workplace conditions. Further, because this study was conducted in one Australian rural region, findings may not generalise to other healthcare contexts, underscoring the need for replication in diverse settings.

Finally, this study might have been affected by lack of statistical power. Using parameters in accordance with the Monte Carlo simulation study37, a larger sample (n > 1181) would be required for optimal estimation of the network model. We believe that this is unlikely to have influenced the final network structure as the original SOC-13 scale, which included the redundant item, initially returned a three-network structure; suggesting that there is sufficient sample size and statistical power to detect a SOC-12 three network structure, if it exists. Importantly, our sample dataset is the largest available for addressing this study’s research question in a cohort of rural HCW, thus representing the best evidence currently available. In addition, as all measures (SOC-13, PHQ-9, and GAD-7) were all self-reported at a single time point, there is potential that common method variance (CMV) inflated the correlations between these constructs. Formal tests (e.g., Harman’s single-factor test) were not feasible due to dataset constraints; therefore, future research should consider multiple measurement approaches or longitudinal designs to better isolate true construct relationships.

This study revisited the dimensionality of the SOC-13 scale using a cutting edge, network psychometric approach. Rigorous measurement of the widely used SOC scale is critical for future salutogenic studies aimed at promoting HCW psychological wellbeing and pandemic preparedness. The results of this investigation using a modified 12-item SOC scale, support a one-dimensional factor structure in a cohort of regional and rural HCWs. Continued efforts are needed in other HCW populations to confirm these findings and examine how item distributions and redundancies influence dimensionality. Statistical methods for imputing missing data would further improve accuracy in network-based estimation of dimensionality. Finally, a future research agenda is needed that is concerned with the imputation of missing data in network analyses for identifying possible sampling bias, alongside efforts to examine cross-cultural validity and longitudinal stability of the SOC-12 network structure.
