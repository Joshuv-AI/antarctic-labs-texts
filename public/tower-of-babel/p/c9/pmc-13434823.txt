Development of a model for design radiation shielding composite aprons using machine learning
Authors: M. R. Alipoor, M. Eshghi, R. Razavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents the development and evaluation of a novel lead-free composite for radiation shielding, designed using an artificial neural network (ANN). The ANN model achieved a high predictive accuracy for the mass attenuation coefficient, with a correlation coefficient of R = 0.997 and a mean absolute percentage error of 0.9–1.8%, closely matching benchmark Geant4 Monte Carlo simulations. The optimized Ba-Gd-I-Sb composite (73.9% Ba, 18.2% Gd, 7.65% I) exhibits superior shielding by exploiting synergistic multi-edge absorption. Key quantitative results show that a 1-mm thickness provides > 10⁵ attenuation at 50 keV, > 95% absorption at 1.0 mm thickness, and a favorable weight-to-performance ratio (1.08 kg for a 0.5 mm shield). The composite’s attenuation of 99% at 80 keV exceeds that of commercial non-lead alternatives (97–98%), while its transmission matches that of lead at lower energies. These findings demonstrate the composite’s high performance and potential as a sustainable, efficient solution for medical and industrial radiation protection.

Introduction

X-rays constitute a foundational technology in modern medical practice, enabling critical non-invasive diagnostic capabilities through diverse applications, including radiography, computed tomography, fluoroscopy, mammography, and bone densitometry1–3. While these technological advancements have markedly decreased the necessity for diagnostic surgical intervention, they concurrently necessitate the implementation of robust radiation shielding protocols. This is essential to safeguard healthcare personnel and the public from exposure, without compromising the diagnostic quality of the imaging procedures4,5. For a considerable period of time, lead (Pb)-based materials have served as conventional solutions in such applications, mainly due to their significant mass density and atomic number6,7. The well-known toxicity of lead, the reduced attenuation in the range of 60–90 keV, the excessive weight in clinical use, and its environmental implications have prompted an international effort to identify effective and safe alternatives8,9. These disadvantages have prompted the search for lead-free alternatives that combine high shielding efficiency with reduced weight and improved biocompatibility. Research has explored a wide range of candidates, including advanced composite architectures. These engineered structures are characterized by their remarkable adaptability and have demonstrated their effectiveness in attenuating ionizing radiation10–14. Such composites are often engineered by incorporating high atomic number elements such as tungsten, bismuth, and thallium into a composite structure15–19. Evaluation of the results of engineered composite structures showed that they can achieve radiation attenuation properties equivalent to, and in some cases superior to, conventional lead-based materials20. This performance is particularly notable in the diagnostic imaging energy spectrum, typically defined as 40–100 keV21,22. However, designing an optimal composite shield is a complex, multi-objective optimization problem. This requires the simultaneous balancing of competing parameters, including radiation attenuation efficiency, desirable structure, weight, cost, and biocompatibility23,24. The vast design space, defined by the choice of matrix, filler type, filler concentration, and microstructural architecture, makes conventional experimental trial-and-error approaches unavoidably time-consuming and costly. To overcome this challenge, a paradigm shift is underway that integrates data-driven methods from machine learning (ML) with materials science. Machine learning models can be trained on datasets that link material composition and structure to shield performance metrics, enabling rapid prediction and optimization of novel composite formulations25–28. This approach accelerates the discovery cycle and allows for the exploration of a wider range of material compositions than experimental methods alone. Emerging research has successfully leveraged machine learning to forecast critical shielding parameters, such as the mass attenuation coefficient and effective atomic number, across a diverse range of composite material architectures29,30. For instance, Bilmez et al.31 demonstrated an ML model to predict the gamma-ray shielding behavior of composites, providing a faster and potentially more accurate alternative to theoretical calculations. This data-driven approach accelerates the discovery of novel material combinations—such as polymer-metal hybrids or graphene-based composites—and optimizes their design, leading to more efficient, lightweight, and high-performance shields32,33. The process involves systematic data generation, feature extraction, and the application of regression or classification models to predict shielding efficacy. This capability not only reduces reliance on costly and time-consuming experimental cycles but also enables the exploration of a vastly larger design space34. As noted by researchers like Benhadjira et al.35 and Boahen et al.36, this shift from rule-based to data-driven methodologies addresses critical gaps in adaptability and material efficiency. However, a discernible gap remains in the application of ANNs for the systematic design of heterogeneous composite structures that exploit strategically aligned elemental absorption edges to maximize attenuation across specific, clinically relevant energy spectra.

This study aims to evaluate the robustness and precision of an ANN-driven machine learning methodology for shielding design by addressing the following questions:

(1) Can an ANN model accurately predict the LAC of complex, multi-element composite structures engineered with intentionally aligned absorption edges?

(2) How do specific elemental absorption features, identified through model interpretability, govern the attenuation performance within the target energy range?

(3) Does this approach facilitate the design of composites with superior attenuation properties without increasing physical thickness, thereby reducing the mass and economic cost of traditional shielding?

By addressing these questions, this study provides a practical tool that replaces weeks of Monte Carlo simulations for shield design with seconds of neural network evaluation, enabling rapid safety scenario planning and retrofit optimization. It empowers material scientists to define performance targets—such as minimum weight or maximum attenuation—and efficiently receive candidate material recipes for synthesis and testing. The validated model has immediate application in the design of next-generation shielding, including compact, lightweight systems for spaceborne detectors, advanced medical radiotherapy units, and portable nuclear power systems. Thus, this work establishes a framework not only for the systematic optimization of advanced shielding materials but also for their accelerated translation from simulation to practical implementation.

Materials and methods

Material selection was governed by the fundamental photoelectric absorption phenomenon, characterized by a pronounced increase in the mass attenuation coefficient at photon energies immediately exceeding an element’s characteristic K, L, and M-shell absorption edges. To maximize attenuation efficacy within the target diagnostic energy spectrum of 80 and 100 kVp, a synergistic design principle was implemented. This approach entailed the strategic identification and combination of elements whose primary K-edge or L-edge energies are closely spaced within this specific range. The proximity of these absorption edges was scrutiny to engineer a broadened, high-attenuation band, thereby compensated for the rapid decline in photoelectric cross-section at energies distal to a singular edge. Initial candidate screening was performed using tabulated data from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) XCOM Photon Cross-Sections Database37. The critical parameters extracted for each element included: atomic number, and the theoretical mass attenuation coefficient the 20 to 100 keV range. This curated dataset serves as the foundational input for the machine learning pipeline. The online database enumerates each constituent element under investigation, its atomic number, its relevant edge energy, and its mass attenuation coefficient at 15 to 100 keV for predicting and modeling the optimal attenuation coefficient. This formulation is not arbitrary, but rather computationally designed based on modeling results to maximize photon attenuation, particularly in the diagnostic and low-energy gamma ray range.

Data generation and preprocessing methodology

The foundational training dataset was constructed utilizing Geant4, a Monte Carlo-based simulation toolkit that accurately represents particle transport through matter. To generate the necessary metrics requisite for evaluating radiation protection efficacy with precision, the transmitted photon fluence was computed employing a G4MultiFunctionalDetector outfitted with an energy-deposit scorer within an air-filled environment, thereby simulating a clinical setting. The phenomenon of photon attenuation was represented by activating all principal electromagnetic interactions—namely, photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering, and pair production—alongside related electron and positron interactions, including bremsstrahlung, ionization, and annihilation, utilizing the G4EMStandardPhysics_option4 physics configuration. A particle range threshold of 10⁻² mm was instituted across all particle types to ensure sufficient spatial resolution for the tracking of secondary particles, which is vital for the precise modeling of energy deposition. To mitigate statistical uncertainty, each simulation incorporated 10⁷ primary photons, adhering to the Monte Carlo principle whereby statistical variance is inversely proportional to the square root of the number of trials (1/√N). As a result, uncertainties in the computed attenuation coefficients were diminished to below 2%, thereby guaranteeing dependable and reproducible outcomes in alignment with established standards in computational radiation physics38,39. The dataset used in this study was determined by the authors by collecting data from a dataset consisting of 105 data points with three input features and one output for different composite ratios. The first input was the X-ray features associated with each energy, the second input was the densities, and the third input was the absorption edge energy of each element, the output of which mapped the attenuation coefficient values ​​corresponding to these features. We statistically analyzed the data using the Describe function. This provided us with important statistical measures such as mean, variance, count, and other data features. We used this information to fit the data to different machine learning models and compare their performance to validate their accuracy. Before training the model, a rigorous preprocessing pipeline is run to ensure data quality and model performance. Some standard data transformation techniques are used to adjust to a more standardized format. First, the natural logarithm of the first input data is taken, which is the energy variable. The logarithm operation suppresses the large difference between the minimum and maximum values ​​of the X-ray intensity, from 15 keV to 100 keV. For the second input, the normalization suppresses the minimum and maximum effects of the density for the linear attenuation coefficient. For the third input, the normalization suppresses the minimum and maximum effects of the absorption edge from 15 keV to 100 keV. With this approach, all incomplete inputs (NaN values) were removed, while the energy and density features were standardized using Standard Scaler normalization. Incomplete entries are the outliers commonly found in radiation studies. With this standardization, an interval is included between the maximum input with the maximum value in the same type of inputs and the minimum input with the minimum value. These steps removed potential biases, normalized the feature scales for faster convergence, and maintained consistent data quality across all samples. After the normalization process, all data points fall between 0 and 1. This process generally makes the training process faster. After the normalization process, the data is fed to the algorithms for training. Machine learning algorithms perform better on processed datasets.

Computational methodology development

To enable scalable and optimized design of composite materials, a dedicated machine learning prediction model was developed. This model was specifically engineered to tackle the central challenge of optimizing radiation protection by learning the intricate relationships between input parameters and mapping them to the desired attenuation function. The modeling framework was constructed using TensorFlow40,41, which provided a robust foundation for building, tuning, and evaluating the machine learning algorithms. The core of this framework is an artificial neural network (ANN). An ANN processes information through a layered, interconnected structure: an input layer that receives the feature data, one or more hidden layers that compute complex, nonlinear relationships, and an output layer that generates the final prediction. Within each hidden layer, inputs are multiplied by specific synaptic weights and then transformed by a nonlinear activation function. This forward pass of information—from input layer, through hidden layers, to the output layer—is illustrated in Fig. 2. The network’s learning objective is to minimize the difference between its predictions and the expected target values. The training process for this artificial neural network is iterative and occurs in two key phases, guided by the backpropagation algorithm (delta rule)42. First, data propagates forward through the network to make a prediction. Then, the error from that prediction is propagated backward, and the algorithm adjusts the internal weights and biases to reduce this error. This cycle repeats until the network’s predictions reach a sufficient level of accuracy. The implemented ANN architecture consists of three fully connected hidden layers with 64, 32, and 16 neurons, respectively, each utilizing ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) activation functions to introduce necessary nonlinearity. This design allows the model to capture the complex physical interactions governing radiation attenuation. To prevent the model from overfitting to the training data, techniques such as early stopping and learning rate reduction were employed. For the optimization process, the model uses a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) approach, specifically the Adam optimizer43. This algorithm dynamically adjusts the learning rate during training, efficiently guiding the weight updates to minimize the loss function. The final configured learning rate was 0.001. To ensure the model’s robustness and ability to generalize, a rigorous validation strategy was adopted. The dataset was initially split, with 80% used for training and 20% held back as a final test set. Additionally, a 5-fold cross-validation protocol was implemented on the training data, ensuring the model’s performance was consistent and not reliant on a single data partition. The model’s effectiveness was quantitatively evaluated on the unseen test set using the mean squared error (MSE) as the primary performance metric. A careful comparison of training and testing metrics was conducted to confirm both high accuracy and a lack of overfitting, confirming the model’s generalizability. Finally, the trained model was deployed to predict LAC values at specific photon energy levels. These predictions were compared against benchmark data generated by the standard Monte Carlo simulation toolkit Geant4. The results demonstrated that the predictive model achieved high accuracy and successfully captured the nonlinear dependencies of LAC on incident photon energy.

Widely adopted in medical physics, radiological safety, and imaging studies, the program’s ability to generate clinically relevant spectral data makes it invaluable for optimizing scan protocols, evaluating exposure levels, and designing protective shielding. The software’s methodology combines semi-empirical modeling based on fundamental physics principles with experimental verification to produce accurate X-ray spectral outputs. Figure 5 depicts the training dynamics of a machine learning model optimizing X-ray attenuation for the Ba–Gd–I–Sb composite, with critical updates to the interpretation based on a provided dynamic error rate determination protocol. Contrary to initial assumptions of near-perfect accuracy, the model exhibits a non-negligible but physically meaningful generalization gap, such that the final error rate shows validation losses remaining significantly higher than training losses. This is not evidence of failure, but rather a signature of the structured physical uncertainty inherent to multi-scale modeling of heterogeneous shielding materials. The error rate reflects the model’s inability to perfectly extrapolate local density fluctuations that are absent in the homogenized forward simulations used for training, such as those based on Geant4 effective-medium approximations. Crucially, the dynamic noise scaling—a reduction in error variance from 0.011 to 0.001 over training—confirms that the model progressively suppresses high-frequency noise while preserving low-frequency physical trends. This variance collapse aligns with the emergence of a robust collective response: the attenuation coefficient becomes increasingly governed by bulk electronic structure factors, including effective atomic number and edge positions, rather than by microscopic disorder. The learning rate reductions, labeled LR₁ through LR₄ at epochs 20, 50, 80, and 100, are applied adaptively. Each reduction is triggered when the relative improvement in validation loss falls below a set threshold. This schedule reflects the hierarchical nature of the physics being learned. During epochs 0–20, the model captures the coarse Z⁴/E³ scaling associated with the dominant photoelectric effect. From epochs 20–50, it resolves the fine structure of edge resonances, particularly the Ba K-edge at 37.44 keV and the Gd edge at 50.23 keV. In epochs 50–80, the model learns the Compton-to-photoelectric crossover and density-dependent electron screening. Finally, during epochs 80–100, it calibrates subtle many-body corrections, such as L-shell contributions and synergistic effects from closely spaced absorption edges in heavy elements. Early stopping at epoch 139—triggered after 10 consecutive epochs without validation loss improvement—is optimal under this dynamic range. Although the training loss continues to decrease slightly beyond epoch 139 (by approximately 0.0001), the validation loss plateaus. This indicates that further optimization would overfit to simulation artifacts—such as discretization errors in energy binning or finite-statistics noise in Monte Carlo tallies—rather than to true physical behavior. The final performance metrics show a 99.1% reduction in training loss and a 98.6% reduction in validation loss. This confirms that the model has captured over 98% of the predictable signal in the dataset—a remarkable achievement given the nonlinearity and multi-scale coupling inherent in X-ray–matter interactions.

Figure 6 presents a parity plot of predicted versus actual linear attenuation coefficients (µ) for the Ba–Gd–I–Sb composite, showing excellent agreement with a correlation coefficient of R = 0.997. This high value reflects that 99.4% of the variance in µ is captured by the model—a strong indicator of fidelity given the deterministic quantum electrodynamic origins of X-ray attenuation. Critically, the data cluster tightly along the y = x line across the full dynamic range (µ ≈ 50–900 cm⁻¹), with no visible systematic bias or edge-related outliers, suggesting the model correctly encodes K-edge physics (e.g., at 33.16 keV for I, 37.44 keV for Ba, 30.49 keV for Sb, and 50.23 keV for Gd) and incorrect density scaling. However, R alone is insufficient to assess scientific utility. Reconstructing from prior metrics (RMSE = 0.796 cm⁻¹) and typical µ values (40–140 cm⁻¹ in diagnostic ranges), the model achieves a Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of approximately 0.9–1.8%. This sub-2% relative error is well below experimental uncertainty (± 1 to 1.5 cm⁻¹) and regulatory tolerances for shielding design, confirming physical reliability. The slight heteroscedasticity is physically expected, as photoelectric dominance at low energies yields larger absolute but smaller relative errors.

Figure 8 shows the element-specific contribution to the mass attenuation coefficient at five photon energies (15, 40, 60, 80, and 100 keV) for Ba, Gd, I, and Sb—revealing the physics-driven hierarchy of elemental efficacy in X-ray shielding. At low energies (15–40 keV), Ba dominates (µ/ρ ≈ 10¹ cm²/g), leveraging its high Z (56) and proximity of its K-edge (37.44 keV) to the operational range; Gd follows closely (Z = 64, K-edge 50.23 keV), with I (Z = 53, K-edge 33.16 keV) contributing significantly due to resonant enhancement just below its edge. The steep drop in all elements of MAC with increasing energy confirms the E⁻³ photoelectric dependence. Crucially, at 15 keV—well below all K-edges—the ordering Ba > Gd > I > Sb reflects pure Z⁴ scaling, validating the dominance of atomic number in the non-resonant range. At higher energies (60–100 keV), Compton scattering begins to increase and the relative advantage of high-Z elements decreases, such that the advantage of Gd over Ba decreases, while Sb (Z = 51) makes a non-negligible contribution (~ 10⁻¹ cm²/g at 100 keV) due to its favorable Z/A ratio and reduced electron binding energy suppression. It is noteworthy that the 73.9% Ba/18.2% Gd/7.65% I formulation is optimal because it maximizes integrated attenuation across the spectrum with Ba providing a bulk low-energy shield, Gd bridging the 40–60 keV gap through its higher K-edge, and I bridging the 30–40 keV region, collectively suppressing the gap between the edges that is a disadvantage of single-element shields.

Figure 13 shows the predicted composite transmittance and absorption percentage as a function of material thickness, providing a direct and highly practical assessment of its protective performance. The transmission drops precipitously with increasing thickness, falling from approximately 60% at 0.1 mm to less than 5% at 1.0 mm. This rapid decline confirms the material’s high attenuation efficiency. The initial steep slope indicates that even a very thin layer provides significant shielding, making this composite suitable for applications where space and weight are critical constraints, such as in wearable protective garments or portable shields. It shows a corresponding rapid increase, rising from approximately 40% absorption at 0.1 mm to over 95% at 1.0 mm. It starts with a steep rise, reflecting the high probability of interaction for low-energy photons in the incident spectrum, and then begins to plateau as the remaining transmitted beam becomes increasingly hardened (composed of higher-energy photons that are less likely to interact). The fact that the two curves are exact inverses of each other (i.e., their sum equals 100% at every point) demonstrates the internal consistency of the model’s predictions. Similarly, the near-complete absorption (> 95%) at 1.0 mm is consistent with the almost flat, near-zero intensity curve for the 1.0 mm material in Fig. 10. The machine learning model’s ability to predict these macroscopic, integrated quantities (transmission and absorption) with such precision validates its predictive power for real-world engineering applications.

Figure 14 presents the predicted composite areal weight as a function of thickness, ranging from 0.25 mm to 1.5 mm. For a fixed area of 60 × 80 cm, the weight scales linearly with thickness, increasing from 0.54 kg at 0.25 mm to 1.08 kg at 0.5 mm and 3.23 kg at 1.5 mm. These data must be interpreted in conjunction with the shielding performance metrics in prior figures. For example, a thickness of 1.5 mm achieves 99.3% radiation protection efficiency (Fig. 13) at a mass of 3.23 kg. Compared with lead (density 11.34 g cm⁻³), which would weigh approximately 8.16 kg at the same 1.5 mm thickness over the same area, the composite provides a substantial weight advantage. Although lead possesses a higher mass attenuation coefficient at certain energies, the composite offers a superior weight‑to‑performance ratio. This makes it a compelling alternative for applications prioritizing mobility and ergonomics, such as protective aprons, thyroid shields, or mobile barriers. Furthermore, the weight data enable calculation of the material’s specific shielding effectiveness—the protection efficiency per unit mass. For example, at 0.5 mm thickness, the material provides 89.2% efficiency (Fig. 13) at a weight of 1.08 kg, yielding a specific efficiency of approximately 82.6% per kg. At 1.5 mm, the efficiency is 99.3% at 3.23 kg, resulting in a specific efficiency of 30.7% per kg. This demonstrates diminishing returns on a per-mass basis, a crucial consideration for optimization. Designers can use this information to select the optimal thickness that provides sufficient protection without imposing an unnecessary weight burden.

Based on the results, the principal limitation of the employed machine learning approach is its inherent lack of interpretability, functioning as a black box that does not explicitly elucidate the causal pathways from material parameters to attenuation properties. To overcome this constraint and enhance the physical transparency of the model, future work will integrate physics-aware metaheuristic programming. This methodology aims to derive explicit, human-readable symbolic expressions that are both unbiased and consistent with the underlying physics. Furthermore, to transition from simulation to practical application, subsequent research must include the physical synthesis of the predicted Ba–Gd–I–Sb composite and the experimental characterization of its mechanical, thermal, and structural properties. Finally, the demonstrated framework exhibits strong potential for generalization to other x-ray energy spectra (therapeutic and industrial ranges) with minimal architectural modifications, requiring only adjustments to the input feature set.

Conclusion

In summation, this research effectively illustrates the power of a synergistic methodology that integrates artificial neural network predictive modeling with physics simulation for the optimization of advanced materials design. The artificial neural network model has demonstrated itself as a highly accurate and dependable instrument for forecasting attenuation properties, achieving a predictive correlation of R = 0.997 and a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 0.9–1.8%, which is below the margin of experimental uncertainty. It accurately replicated the fundamental physics, including characteristic K-edge features, and showed remarkable agreement with Geant4 benchmark simulations.
