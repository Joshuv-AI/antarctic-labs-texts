Development and Validation of the Denver Critical Illness Intubation Entrustment (D-CITE) Tool
Authors: James H. Wykowski, Courtney Donovan, Tristan J. Huie, Melissa New
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundICU intubations are high-risk procedures requiring technical and cognitive skills. Training in ICU intubations varies; no validated tools exist to assess intubation skills for the adult ICU setting. We sought to develop and validate an ICU intubation entrustment tool.Research QuestionIs a novel entrustment-based assessment tool valid to assess adult ICU intubation skill?Study Design and MethodsWe conducted a modified Delphi study consisting of multidisciplinary intubation education experts to identify relevant skills. After development of the tool, we applied the tool in a simulation setting to gather further validity evidence. During the simulation, participants completed 3 intubation scenarios and were evaluated by at least 2 raters. We assessed reliability using Cronbach α and interclass correlation, and conducted exploratory factor analysis and Rasch modeling to assess internal structure validity.ResultsThe modified Delphi study included 19 experts representing 17 institutions and consisted of 2 rounds. The study resulted in the Denver Critical Illness Intubation Entrustment (D-CITE) Tool, containing 10 intubation microskills, a global entrustment assessment, and objective classifications of intubation difficulty. Twenty participants completed the simulation, including 8 beginner participants (0-5 prior intubations), 7 intermediate participants (5-25 prior intubations), and 5 advanced participants (> 100 prior intubations). The D-CITE tool demonstrated high reliability with a Cronbach alpha of 0.97 (95% CI, 0.969-0.981), and an interclass correlation of 0.77 (95% CI, 0.754-0.786). Exploratory factor analysis identified a single factor, signifying that the D-CITE tool evaluates intubation competency as a single construct. Rasch modeling demonstrated appropriate model fit (infit mean square ± SD, 0.99 ± 0.05; outfit mean square ± SD, 0.96 ± 0.06).InterpretationWe created a novel intubation entrustment tool and gathered validity evidence to support its use for evaluating trainee progress toward competence in a simulated environment. Future studies will assess validity for use during real-world intubations.

Take-Home Points

Study Question: Is a newly created entrustment tool valid for assessing fellows’ ICU intubation skills in a simulated environment?

Results: We created the Denver Critical Illness Intubation Entrustment (D-CITE) tool using a modified Delphi study; in the simulated environment, the D-CITE tool demonstrated appropriate validity for use to assess fellows’ ICU intubation skills in the simulated environment.

Interpretation: Our results show that the D-CITE tool is appropriate for use to assess fellows’ ICU intubation skills during simulation, with future studies planned in the clinical environment.

Intubation is 1 of the most performed procedures in the US. Emergency intubations are high-risk procedures, with 40% of ICU intubations resulting in hypotension, hypoxemia, or cardiac arrest.1,2 Intubation training is variable among pulmonary and critical care medicine (PCCM) fellowship programs, depending on local culture, service line agreements surrounding intubation practices, and faculty comfort with intubations.3,4 Regardless of the method or volume of training, fellowship-trained intensivists are typically deemed capable of intubating independently when credentialed by a hospital. This assumption of competence may be flawed. A prior study of neonatal intubation found that only 45% of neonatal critical care fellows achieved competence before graduating training.5 Additionally, progress toward competence was variable, with some fellows achieving competency in 8 attempts, whereas others required > 40.5

Gaining competence in ICU intubations requires mastering both technical and cognitive skills.1 Many studies of adult intubation training and assessment focus on outcomes such as first-pass success, motion capture analysis of intubation technique, or time to intubation.6, 7, 8 Specifically, a prior retrospective analysis used operator growth curves modeled using first-pass success to identify a threshold for competence, and found most trainees plateaued between 30 and 40 intubations.9 Although these variables are important measures of intubation technique, they do not capture the complexity and variation in intubation difficulty (eg, anatomic distortion, periintubation shock) relevant to intubation of critically ill adults.10,11 Other studies have sought to create evaluation tools to systematically determine if and when trainees gain these skills. However, all the tools that currently exist were either validated for use outside of the ICU, in neonatal populations, or lack any validity evidence.11, 12, 13, 14 Additionally, these tools primarily rely on checklist methods of assessment, which fail to assess the continuum of skill development, and may have lower interitem reliability relative to tools using rating scales.15

Entrustment is an emerging form of evaluation and feedback that may more reliably assess trainees’ progression toward independent practice. When completing an entrustment tool, clinical supervisors make decisions about how much supervision a trainee requires based on clinical observations.16,17 The anchors used in entrustment tools reflect real-world decisions made by supervisors (ie, “I need to be present in the room just in case,” “I need to provide ongoing coaching”) and consequently may be more effective tools for formative and summative feedback.18 We sought to develop a novel adult ICU intubation entrustment tool using a modified Delphi process and to validate this tool as a method to track trainees’ intubation skill development in a simulated environment.

Study Design and Methods

Messick’s unified validity framework19 guided our approach to study design. We planned the Delphi study to generate content validity for the tool, and our simulated validity study to generate validity for the other 4 domains of Messick’s framework. This study was reviewed by the Colorado Multi-Institutional Review Board and granted expedited review (COMIRB No. 24-2327).

Entrustment Tool Generation and Delphi Process

We began generating our tool with a literature search of prior intubation tools. We sought to identify tools used for adult critical care, but also analogous tools used in anesthesia and pediatric critical care settings. We then conducted a modified Delphi process. We used 3 criteria for expertise: leadership of a fellowship or institution’s airway curriculum, serving as faculty in a national airway management course, or prior publications in the field of airway management education.

The full protocol of our Delphi process is available (e-Appendix 1). We began our first Delphi round by adapting an intubation checklist published by the Association of Pulmonary Critical Care Medicine Program Directors.20 We sought feedback on 3 components of the tool: intubation characteristics used to subcategorize ratings (eg, anatomically difficult airway), intubation scenario microskills, and other markers of intubation success (eg, first-pass success). In round 1, experts could vote to include, exclude, or modify existing components, and suggest new components to be voted on in subsequent rounds. Suggested components were edited and consolidated as appropriate for clarity. In round 2, participants voted on the components suggested in the previous round and finalized wording for previously included components. For both rounds, we set a consensus threshold of 70%. Voting took place using the electronic Qualtrics platform. The content of each voting round was reviewed by 3 coauthors and 1 pulmonary/critical care physician not participating in the Delphi study for clarity before dissemination. We adapted entrustment anchors from the Ontario Bronchoscopy Assessment Tool, and existing entrustment tool commonly used in pulmonary/critical care fellowship training.21

Simulation Validation

We designed our validation study with an interpretation-use argument of assessing for fellows’ progress toward competence based on skill demonstrated during simulated intubations.22 The interpretation-use argument is a framework for how data gathered during validation studies should be used for real-world decision-making. This ensures that the design of validation studies aligns with the intended use of the tool being validated. To gather further validity evidence for our tool, we conducted 2 simulation sessions. We created 3 scenarios (e-Appendix 2) designed to mimic common real-world ICU intubations: status epilepticus, upper gastrointestinal bleed requiring intubation for endoscopy, and progressive respiratory failure due to pneumonia. The scenarios were reviewed and edited by a group of 4 experts in airway management education. Participants completed simulations using an airway manikin, and had the opportunity to select from a variety of equipment, including video and direct laryngoscopy blades, endotracheal tube sizes, and stylets.

We calculated an estimated sample size needed by multiplying the number of components assessed by the tool by 10, a commonly used standard in the field of psychometrics.23,24 Simulation participants included PCCM fellows and internal medicine residents. We chose first- and third-year PCCM fellows to participate in the first session to reflect a range of prior experience. As the session occurred during the second half of the academic year, first-year fellows had all completed at least 25 intubations, and third-year fellows had all completed > 100 intubations. For a second session, we recruited internal medicine residents interested in pursuing PCCM fellowship to mimic the experience level of novice fellows just beginning their fellowship.

Participants completed each scenario while being observed and rated by at least 2 faculty members. Participants were instructed to verbalize all steps of the procedure including any contingency planning or observations made. Intubation characteristics (eg, anatomic difficulty, physiological difficulty) were included in the tool to subcategorize intubation attempts, but were not assessed by raters during this simulation given the scenarios were standardized for all participants. Faculty noted Cormack-Lehane view grade achieved and the number of attempts needed for successful intubation. After completing each scenario, participants received 2 to 3 minutes of feedback but did not view the entrustment tool. After the session, observing faculty completed informal interviews and a brief survey about the tool (e-Appendix 2).

Statistical Analysis

We began analysis in SPSS with descriptive statistics (IBM). We assessed reliability with Cronbach alpha and intraclass correlations.25 We tested between-group differences for each microskill and the global assessment using analyses of variance, and within-group differences for each scenario. We evaluated the association of first-pass success with overall scores using paired t tests.

We conducted exploratory factor analysis using principal component analysis to examine dimensionality. We used the Bartlett test of sphericity and a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure to determine appropriateness for factor analysis.26,27 Factors were retained with eigenvalues > 1.0, whereas items were retained on factors if they were > 0.40.28

We then used Rasch modeling using Winsteps software (Winsteps) to confirm dimensionality and assess the item and person performance. Rasch modeling is a tool to analyze how well components of an evaluation describe participant ability. In an ideal fit Rasch model with 10 test items, the highest ability participant would answer all correctly, with the next strongest participant answering 9 of 10 answers correctly, missing only the most difficult, and so on and so forth. Fit statistics confirm that the data fit the model as evaluated by mean square (MNSQ) infit and outfit values between 0.5 and 1.5. In Rasch modeling, reliability is measured by computing how people and items spread across the measure, called separation. Person separation explores the ability of items to identity levels of the measure across people on a less-to-more continuum, whereas item separation shows the ability of people to distinguish items.29 A separation of 2.0 is minimum standard for reliability, with higher levels of separation indicating higher reliability.

Although entrustment scales are typically evaluated as ordinal scales, Rasch models transform ordinal rating into a continuous scale using logits representing the probability of responding in a category based on person ability.18,29 To ensure an even distribution across the theorized entrustment levels, we examined scale category probability curves and evaluated MNSQ infit values.30 To further assess scale validity, we calculated the Andrich threshold and category step structure for each position on the 5-point entrustment scale.

Modified Delphi Process

Sixteen experts participated in our modified Delphi process, including 7 trained in pulmonary/critical care, 3 trained in anesthesiology, and 6 trained in emergency medicine and critical care. Collectively, the experts represented 14 institutions. The modified Delphi process consisted of 2 rounds, with 94% participation in each round (e-Appendix 3). Simulation Results

Of the 20 participants, 5 were third-year fellows (> 100 intubations each), 7 were first-year fellows (25-35 intubations each), and 8 were residents (0-5 intubations each). All participants chose video laryngoscopy to complete their intubations. Of all intubation attempts, 138 of 150 (92%) achieved first-pass success, and 93 of 150 (62%) achieved a Cormack-Lehane grade 1 view. There were statistically significant increases in scores on each intubation microskill and the global assessment with increases in training (Table 2). Within each level of training, there were no statistically significant differences in scores across the 3 scenarios. Faculty Evaluation

Ten faculty participated in our simulation sessions, representing 4 Denver area academic hospitals. Interrater reliability, assessed via mean interclass correlation, across all items was 0.77 (95% CI, 0.754-0.786); this was 0.87 for the Global Assessment. Six of 10 faculty completed the evaluation survey, all of whom either strongly agreed or agreed that (1) they understood what the tool was asking, (2) the tool would help them give formative feedback, (3) they were able to easily complete the tool on their phones, and (4) they would have time to complete the tool in the clinical environment. Interviews with faculty demonstrated a challenge in understanding how to rate skills that the faculty member was unable to observe; we revised our faculty instructions for future iterations based on this feedback.

Exploratory Factor Analysis

Our data set demonstrated suitability for factor analysis (Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin, 0.949; Bartlett test of sphericity, < 0.001). Exploratory faculty analysis demonstrated a single factor measuring the construct of ICU intubation ability, with an eigenvalue of 8.25 explaining 82.5% of variance in scores. All intubation microskills loaded onto the single factor of intubation competency, with exploratory factor analysis loading of > 0.4 for all microskills. (Table 3).

Discussion

We used a robust process to develop and validate the D-CITE Tool, an assessment of critical care fellows’ intubation competency. The development of our tool included a thorough review of literature to evaluate prior tools. We sought to expand the scope of prior intubation tools in 2 ways: (1) including the situational and hemodynamic factors that frequently complicate ICU intubations, and (2) using a rating scale that captured the spectrum of skill development rather than a binary checklist. To capture the complexity of ICU intubations, we recruited a broad pool of airway management experts from 14 institutions for our Delphi study, including those with backgrounds in anesthesia, emergency medicine, and critical care. We then completed a preliminary validation study in a simulated environment, which demonstrated that the D-CITE tool has appropriate validity evidence for use during simulation.

The key in any validation study is establishing the intended use of the tool for which you are gathering validity evidence, known as the interpretation-use argument.22 We approached validity using an interpretation-use argument of assessing fellows’ progress toward intubation competency in a simulated environment. Our validation study demonstrated consistent content validity and reliability. Despite the complex combination of skills required during an ICU intubation, factor analysis and Rasch modeling support the use of our tool as a single continuous construct of intubation competency. Based on our item analysis, performance on all pieces of our assessment tool correlated strongly with level of training, except for the microskill, assessment of potential anatomic difficulty. We think this may be related to the simulated environment and the absence of typical visual cues present when performing a physical examination on a live patient. Because learners were aware that this was not a difficult airway simulation, they may have not thought that completing this step was necessary, resulting in lower entrustment scores. Additionally, the sample consisted of trainees in internal medicine and pulmonary/critical care, who may have had less prior experience with preintubation airway assessment than anesthesia trainees. Further attention to how this component functions in the real-world environment will help clarify whether this skill behaves differently than other components of the tool.

There are multiple additional components not addressed in our preliminary validity study that we will seek to address in future studies. First, all fellows in our study were in a pulmonary/critical care training program. Although the D-CITE tool could conceivably be used in emergency medicine, anesthesia, and critical care programs, further validity evidence would be beneficial to support its use in these training environments. Additionally, although participants could choose from multiple intubation blades, including both standard-geometry and hyperangulated blades, all intubations were ultimately completed using video laryngoscopy. Future studies, including validation in the clinical environment, should clarify if the D-CITE tool operates similarly when direct laryngoscopy is used.

Another component we hope to assess in the clinical environment is the tension between completeness of the D-CITE tool, redundancy, and usability. We recognize that 10 items in the D-CITE tool may not represent every step of an ICU intubation. For example, although the tool asks about verbalizing a backup plan before intubation, it does not necessarily capture how the operator adapted and responded during the intubation itself. Currently, the data from our preliminary validation study, such as Cronbach α < 0.9 and a single factor identified during factor analysis, suggest that the included components adequately capture the construct of intubation competency. In other words, strong performance on one component suggests the operator will likely also perform well on other components, and adding additional items would only introduce redundancy without the benefit of further discriminating operator skill level. Evaluation of the tool in the clinical learning environment will reveal whether additional components should be added to improve the tool’s performance.

Our study adds to the competency-based medical education literature in multiple ways. We have outlined a replicable process for developing future assessment tools for other complex skills. Additionally, we have shared a new evaluation tool for a high-risk procedure that uses an entrustment-based rating scale. We chose an entrustment-based approach because it most closely aligns with ICU attendings’ decision-making, and it has been shown to be effective in other procedures in critical care training.21 In this context, entrustment scales can aid in 2 types of decision-making. First, the tool can demonstrate fellows’ progress toward readiness for independent practice, ensuring fellows’ skills are progressing appropriately and identifying areas in need of further development. Second, this tool can more definitively identify when fellows are ready for independent practice. We think entrustment scales are superior to checklist-based assessments when making decisions regarding readiness for independent practice because although a participant may complete all the steps of a checklist, and thus obtain a perfect score on the checklist, this does not necessarily describe how fluidly or independently they completed those steps.

Further study in the real-world environment with a larger cohort of participants will be necessary to broaden the validity of our tool and establish an appropriate standard for independent practice. In our simulated study, faculty found the tool easy to understand and use, which is an important finding of this study because this is crucial for uptake and application to the clinical environment. Additionally, future studies will seek to capture the learning curves and trajectory of fellows’ progress toward competency.

The simulation participants in our study all came from one institution, which may limit generalizability. However, this is balanced by the fact that faculty raters were from 4 different institutions. Additionally, all fellows in our study were pulmonary/critical care fellows, despite the fact providers of multiple backgrounds perform ICU intubations. Consequently, additional work may be needed to validate our tool with other groups of trainees who intubate. Faculty in our study rated the tool highly, however, our postsimulation survey only had a response rate of 60%, and nonresponse bias may have impacted the results. Finally, raters were not blinded to the level of training of the participant evaluated, which may have impacted their evaluations.

Interpretation

Our work created the D-CITE, an entrustment-based tool for assessment of ICU intubation skills. We have validated this tool for use in the simulated environment and designed it to facilitate use in the clinical environment, where validation and establishment of competency thresholds will be needed.
