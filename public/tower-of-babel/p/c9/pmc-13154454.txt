Phenotypic heterogeneity of type 2 diabetes and risks of complications with a tree-like representation
Authors: Jiajing Che, Xianli Li, Zixin Qiu, Ruyi Li, Hancheng Yu, Jinchi Xie, Tianyu Guo, Zijun Tang, Pengfei Xia, Kun Xu, Rui Li, Kun Yang, Tingting Geng, An Pan, Gang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes shows clinical heterogeneity which cannot be fully captured by glycemic metrics, highlighting the need to better define patient phenotypes and progression pathways. This study aims to elucidate the heterogeneity of type 2 diabetes and risks of major associated diseases, as well as underlying proteomic mechanism.MethodsWe applied discriminative dimensionality reduction with trees (DDRTree) algorithm to construct a tree from seven clinical variables (body mass index, high density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglyceride, HbA1c, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and total cholesterol). Disease risks were assessed using competing risk models.ResultsThis study included 6406 individuals with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes from the UK Biobank. All seven clinical variables formed a gradient distribution across the DDRTree-derived phenotypic structure, revealing three distinct disease risks patterns: participants with adiposity, hypertension and dyslipidemia exhibited elevated risks of macrovascular complications, diabetic kidney disease, Parkinson’s disease and non-alcohol fatty liver disease; those with hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia had higher risks of myocardial infarction, diabetic neuropathy, diabetic retinopathy, depression and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; while elevated total cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol were associated with increased risks of cancer and Alzheimer’s disease. Proteomic analyses identified pattern-specific pathways: metabolic dysregulation and extracellular matrix remodeling in the first pattern, inflammatory activation and lipid metabolism alterations in the second, and immune activation with chemical carcinogenesis in the third. Furthermore, sensitivity to lifestyle factors were phenotypic-specific. These patterns were similar in the Dongfeng-Tongji Diabetes cohort, though cardiovascular disease and retinopathy risks were strongly associated with hypertension. An online tool was provided for individual risk prediction.ConclusionsOur findings reveal distinct spatial distributions of clinical features and associated disease risks in type 2 diabetes, with subgroups exhibiting unique proteomic signatures and differential lifestyle responses, underscoring the importance for personalized management for diabetes care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03147-7.

Introduction

Diabetes represents a significant global health concern, affecting an estimated one out of every nine adults worldwide [1, 2]. The disease exhibits considerable heterogeneity in pathogenesis, clinical presentation, and risk of complications, complicating patient management and treatment efficacy [3]. Elucidating this heterogeneity is therefore essential for advancing toward personalized care and improving long-term outcomes.

Clustering approaches, such as k-means, have been widely used to delineate heterogeneity in type 2 diabetes by categorizing patients into discrete subgroups based on clinical variables. While these methods have helped reveal differential risks of progression and complications across subgroups, they possess inherent limitations [4–7]. These hard-clustering approaches do not provide individualized risk predictions, and fail to capture the continuous nature of disease progression, potentially oversimplifying the complex physiological spectrum of diabetes [8].

The discriminative dimensionality reduction with trees (DDRTree) algorithm enables the generation of a branched tree structure that provides an intuitive visualization of the diabetes phenotypic spectrum [9]. However, the application of this continuous modeling approach to systematically map a broad spectrum of diabetes-associated diseases and to link this phenotypic architecture to both lifestyle factors and proteomic signatures remains limited. Whether such a framework can provide insights into heterogeneous disease mechanisms and inform personalized management strategies is still largely unexplored.

Therefore, in this study we applied the DDRTree method to model the continuous heterogeneity of type 2 diabetes across two independent cohorts—the UK Biobank and the Dongfeng-Tongji Diabetes Cohort (DFTJDC). We systematically examined diabetes-associated disease distributions across the phenotypic spectrum and assessed phenotype-specific associations with lifestyle factors. Finally, we sought to elucidate pattern-specific pathogenic mechanisms and identify potential molecular targets through proteomic profiling.

Methods

Study design and participants

The UK Biobank is a large-scale, prospective study that involved over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years from 22 centers across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. The DFTJDC is an ongoing prospective study initiated in 2018 in Shiyan, Hubei Province, China, designed to investigate risk factors, complications, and prognosis of diabetes. Diagnostic criteria for newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes are detailed in Supplementary Methods. Participants with diabetes before age 35 (likely type 1 diabetes), those with incomplete data on clinical risk factors and those with the outlier values (exceeding 5 standard deviation [SD] from the mean of the clinical measures) were excluded, leaving 6406 and 828 participants from UKB and DFTJDC, respectively.

Assessment of diabetes-associated diseases

Data on incident diseases were identified through electronic health records including those from hospital admissions, primary care, and death registers. In UKB, electronic health records were available up to 1 March 2022, 12 November 2021, and 25 October 2021 for centers in England, Wales, and Scotland, respectively. In DFTJDC, the electronic health records were available up to 26 November 2024. We considered macrovascular and microvascular complications, cancer, neurological and mental diseases, respiratory system disorders in both cohorts. Disease outcomes were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th version (ICD-10) code (Supplementary Table 1).

Measurements of type 2 diabetes-related phenotypic variables

We included seven routinely recorded phenotypic characteristics relevant to type 2 diabetes pathophysiology and associated with variation in risks of adverse outcomes: body mass index (BMI), high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride (TG), HbA1c, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and total cholesterol (TC). These variables were selected a priori based on a previously established DDRTree-based phenotypic framework [10]. In addition, five other clinical variables were also considered: waist hip ratio (WHR), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), c-reactive protein (CRP), and creatinine.

Measurements of healthy lifestyle factors

The healthy lifestyle score incorporated five components: physical activity, sleep duration, smoking status, alcohol intake, and diet. Regular physical activity was defined as ≥ 150 min/week of moderate activity, ≥ 75 min/week of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination [11]. Healthy sleep duration was classified as 7–8 h/day. Individuals with zero pack-years of smoking were regarded as nonsmokers. Moderate drinking corresponded to 1–14 g/day for women or 1–28 g/day for men [12]. A healthy diet was defined as adequate consumption of at least half of ten food components, including fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and others [13]. Participants scored one point per met criterion; total scores (0–5) reflected overall lifestyle health.

Proteomic profiling

The UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) profiled 2923 plasma proteins using Olink’s proximity extension assay for 46,788 randomly selected participants, a representative subset of the full cohort. Details of data processing and quality control are available in Sun et al. [14] study. Briefly, plasma samples were stored at − 80 °C before being analyzed in Sweden. Protein expression was quantified as normalized protein expression (NPX) values on a log2-transformed scale. After excluding eight proteins with missing values exceeding 20%, 2915 proteins were retained and standardized for subsequent analyses. Ultimately, proteomic analyses were conducted among 557 participants who were randomly selected by the UKB-PPP.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study participants were presented as mean ± SD for continuous variables, and as number (percentage) for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). In the following sections, a branch refers to a spatial segment of the tree structure inferred by DDRTree. A phenotype denotes to the set of clinical characteristics associated with each branch, summarizing the overall clinical profile of individuals located along that branch. Building on this framework, a pattern represents a higher-level integration of multiple phenotypes that exhibit similar disease-risk distributions, thereby grouping phenotypes according to common profiles of disease susceptibility.

DDRTree

DDRTree is a dimensionality reduction algorithm that projects multi-dimensional phenotypic data into a two-dimensional tree, revealing a spectrum form mixed phenotypes in the central trunk to distinct profiles in the branches [9, 15]. For the UKB analyses, we applied DDRTree using the Monocle package (version 2.34.0) to seven quantitative variables [16]. These variables were first standardized using a rank-based inverse normal transformation and then residualized for age and sex via linear regression. The resulting matrix was used to construct the final two-dimensional tree.

Internal validation

The UKB cohort was randomly divided into training (80%) and testing (20%) sets with comparable baseline characteristics (Supplementary Table 2). In the training set, phenotypic variables were reduced to two dimensions (Dim1 and Dim2) using the DDRTree algorithm. Then a generalized additive model (GAM) incorporating age, sex and seven variables was fitted to predict these dimensions. Model performance was evaluated in the testing set by comparing predicted values with DDRTree-derived coordinates using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (ρ).

To further evaluate stability, we calculated the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) across 100 subsampling iterations, each excluding 10% of participants and remodeling the DDRTree. Consistency of branch assignments was quantified by pairwise ARIs, with ARI > 0.65 considered evidence of good stability (see Supplementary Methods).

Risk estimation of diabetes-associated diseases

To assess the 5-year and 10-year individual risk for each type 2 diabetes-associated diseases, we calculated predicted probabilities from competing risk models (Fine-Gray models) using tree dimensions 1 and 2 (Dim1 and Dim2) as independent variables. All-cause mortality was treated as a competing event. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated per one-unit increase in each dimension. To mitigate reverse causation, we excluded individuals who developed outcomes within the first year of follow-up.

Quantitative the distribution pattern across the tree

Beyond the visual gradient, we assessed the spatial distribution of phenotypic profiles and outcomes using two metrics. First, phenotypic distributions were regressed against the two tree dimensions, and outcome probabilities were estimated with competing risk models to depict risk gradient. Second, global and local Moran’s I statistics quantified spatial autocorrelation, with local Moran’s I based on 100 nearest neighbors. Detailed information is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

We develop an interactive R shiny app that positions newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes patients within the phenotypic tree structure using mapping function. The application employs Cox models incorporating age, sex, and seven clinical variables to estimate complication risks, with model performance quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Protein functional enrichment analyses

Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify differential proteins between the high-risk and low-risk disease groups, with adjustment for age, sex, and TDI. Proteins with a Bonferroni-corrected threshold (P < 0.05/2915) were considered as differential proteins. Functional enrichment of these proteins was assessed for Gene Ontology (GO) biological process (BP), molecular functions (MF), cellular component (CC), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) using clusterProfiler (version 4.14.6).

Results

Phenotypic characteristics description and model performance

Phenotypic characteristics of UKB and DFTJDC are described in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. Participants in the UKB had a mean age of 59.10 (SD = 7.17) years, 39.4% were female. Participants in the DFTJDC had a mean age of 56.3 (SD = 12.06) years, 35.0% were female. The seven variables used to construct the tree in the UKB, along with their mean (SD) values, were as follows: BMI (31.67 [5.70] kg/m2), HDL-C (1.19 [0.30] mmol/L), TG (2.35 [1.30] mmol/L), TC (5.01 [1.27] mmol/L), HbA1c (53.90 [14.65] mmol/mol), SBP (143.31 [17.49] mmHg), and DBP (84.39 [9.92] mmHg).

In the UKB, the GAM models predicting the two DDRTree dimensions demonstrated strong performance (adjusted R2 values of 0.87 for Dim1 and 0.83 for Dim2; Supplementary Table 4) and high concordance between predicted and actual dimension values (ρDim1 = 0.98, ρDim2 = 0.96). Internal validation yielded a median ARI of 0.87 (IQR 0.79–0.94), indicating robust model stability (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Phenotypic heterogeneity at type 2 diabetes diagnosis in UKB

Phenotypic heterogeneity was systematically mapped onto the tree, revealing distinct spatial distributions for seven clinical variables (Fig. 1A). Two complementary metrics quantified these patterns. First, regression analyses demonstrated significant gradients along both tree dimensions (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Table 5). BMI and TG were enriched in the upper-left branches and reversely related to HDL-C, which localized to the lower-right region. Blood pressure (SBP and DBP) increased toward the upper-right branches, whereas HbA1c and TC decreased along dimension 1 and dimension 2, respectively. Second, spatial autocorrelation confirmed significant clustering: global Moran’s I-values indicated that HDL-C and blood pressure exhibited the strongest spatial structure, followed by TG, BMI, TC, and HbA1c (Fig. 1C), with local patterns detailed in Supplementary Fig. 2. Additional variables, including WHR, ALT, AST, creatinine, and CRP, conformed to the BMI and TG gradient, while gamma-glutamyl transferase paralleled the distribution of HbA1c (Supplementary Tables 6 and Supplementary Fig. 3).

Based on these distributions, five phenotypes were identified from the distal branches of the tree, excluding the central region with mixed features (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Tables 7 and 8, Supplementary Fig. 4). Phenotype 1 was characterized by concurrent elevations in adiposity, TG, HbA1c, blood pressure, liver enzymes, creatinine, and CRP, coupled with low HDL-C and TC. Phenotype 3 exhibited the opposite profile, with high TC, HDL-C, and blood pressure, but low adiposity, TG, and inflammatory/hepatic markers. Phenotype 2 was defined primarily by elevated blood pressure. Phenotype 4 and 5 shared increased HbA1c and TC with normal blood pressure; however, phenotype 4 displayed high HDL-C, whereas phenotype 5 was distinguished by obesity and elevated TG.

Heterogeneity of diabetes-associated diseases in UKB

During a median follow-up of 12.6 years, incident cases ranged from 224 to 1613 for macrovascular diseases, 242 to 793 for microvascular diseases, 1365 for cancer, 64 to 513 for neurological and mental diseases, 416 for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and 360 for non-alcohol fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Five-year progression probabilities and corresponding HRs from competing risk models were mapped across the tree (Fig. 2A, B). Significant global and local spatial autocorrelation was observed for all outcomes (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Three distinct spatial disease patterns were identified. The first pattern, located in the upper-left branches (phenotype 1 and 2), showed the highest risks for macrovascular diseases (excluding myocardial infarction [MI]), diabetic kidney disease (DK), Parkinson’s disease and NAFLD (HRs ranging from 0.67 to 0.91 for dimension 1; 1.27–1.55 for dimension 2). This cluster was characterized by higher adiposity, blood pressure, liver enzyme levels, and dyslipidemia (elevated TG and reduced HDL-C). The second pattern, localized to the left branches (phenotype 1, 4 and 5), was associated with increased risks of MI, diabetic neuropathy (DN), diabetic retinopathy (DR), depression and COPD. The risks were only significantly associated with dimension 1 (HRs ranging from 0.63 to 0.83) and were predominantly driven by hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia. The third pattern, in the lower branches (phenotype 3–5), was linked to the highest risks of cancer and Alzheimer’s disease. This area was characterized by higher TC and HDL-C, although the associations with dimension 2 were borderline significant (Alzheimer, HR = 0.49, 95% CI = 0.23–1.02; cancer, HR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.80–1.07).

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these patterns. Adjustments for lifestyle factors (physical activity, sleep duration, smoking status, alcohol drinking status, and education levels) and baseline medication use (metformin, insulin, antihypertension, and lipid-lowering medication) did not materially alter these findings (Supplementary Table 9), and the spatial associations remained consistent when assessed for 10-year disease progression probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Clustering analyses

We performed phenotype classification using the K-means clustering method for comparative purpose. According to the Gap statistic, the Silhouette score, and the Elbow method, the optimal model was composed of two clusters (Supplementary Fig. 6). Notably, cluster 1 exhibited adverse profiles across all phenotypic variables (BMI, TG, TC, HbA1c, SBP, DBP), but was not associated with elevated complication risk (Supplementary Table 10). Mapping the k-means clusters onto the DDRTree structure revealed that both clusters aligned with five distinct phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Differential associations of lifestyle factors with disease risks across type 2 diabetes phenotypes

Given the number of incident cases available after stratification, we focused specifically on macrovascular and microvascular diseases (Table 1, Supplementary Table 11). Overall, higher healthy lifestyle scores were consistently associated with reduced disease risk across all phenotypes, with HRs ranging from 0.80 to 0.88. However, the protective effects of specific lifestyle factors varied by phenotype. In phenotype 1, significant risk reduction was associated with regular physical activity (HR = 0.48), healthy sleep duration (HRs range = 0.64–0.74) and healthy diet (HRs range = 0.70–0.73), whereas alcohol consumption increased risk (HR = 1.70). For phenotype 2 and 5, healthy sleep duration (HRs range = 0.75–0.78) and nonsmoking (HRs range = 0.60–0.78) showed stronger protective effects, though alcohol consumption remained adverse in phenotype 5 (HR = 1.82). In contrast, regular physical activity (HRs range = 0.63–0.76) and nonsmoking (HRs range = 0.71–0.74) were the most salient protective factors in phenotype 3 and 4.

Proteomic profile for type 2 diabetes phenotypes

To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms linking type 2 diabetes phenotypes to associated disease risks, we characterized their plasma proteomic profiles. Guided by the abovementioned disease distribution patterns, corresponding high- and low-risk subgroups were compared, identifying 44 and 50 differential proteins in the phenotype 1–2 vs. phenotype 3–5 and phenotype 1/4/5 vs. phenotype 2–3 comparisons, respectively (Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.05/2,915; Supplementary Table 12).

KEGG and GO analysis revealed functional patterns across phenotypes (Supplementary Tables 13 and 14, Supplementary Fig. 8). Phenotype 1–2 were enriched in pathways related to metabolic imbalance and abnormal cardiac function, consistent with elevated risks of cardiovascular disease, DK, and NAFLD, and showed cytoskeleton and energy dysregulation linked to Parkinson’s disease. Phenotype 3–5 exhibited enrichment in immunity-inflammation activation, aberrant metabolism of chemical carcinogens, and drug resistance pathways, while phenotype 1/4/5 was characterized by chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and lipid metabolic abnormalities.

DDRTree analyses in DFTJDC

Using the mapping function detailed in the Supplementary Methods, all participants from DFTJDC were mapped onto the CCDRFS tree, revealing distinct distributions for each phenotypic variable (Fig. 3). Over a median follow-up of four years, we observed 74–145 macrovascular diseases, 110–115 microvascular diseases, 30 cancers, 40 COPD, and 175 NAFLD cases. The corresponding 5-year progression probabilities showed substantial variation across five phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 9). PAD and NAFLD risks were primarily driven by the combination of adiposity, dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia and hypertension (clustered in phenotype 1–2), and cancer risk was concentrated in phenotype 4–5 with elevated TC and HDL-C; both patterns were consistent with those observed in UKB. In contrast, CVD, stroke, DK, and COPD risks clustered in phenotype 2–3 characterized by high blood pressure, adiposity, and hyperglycemia, and DR risk localized to phenotype 3–4 with higher blood pressure, TC and HDL-C, showing some divergence from UKB patterns.

Visualizing individual risk in relation to their phenotype

To support clinicians and patients in visualizing the risks of associated diseases in individuals with type 2 diabetes, we developed web applications (https://dynamic-omics.shinyapps.io/ukb_shiny/ for UKB and https://dynamic-omics.shinyapps.io/dftjdc_shiny/ for DFTJDC). Each individual can be positioned within the phenotypic tree according to their risk profiles, allowing five-year predictions for macrovascular and microvascular diseases, cancer, neurological and mental diseases, and respiratory system disorders. Risk prediction models incorporated age, sex, and seven clinical variables defining the tree structure. In the UKB, using continuous phenotypic variables, along with age and sex, achieved AUCs of 0.58 to 0.83 for predicting diabetes-associated diseases. Incorporation of DDRTree dimensions resulted in slightly improved performance, with AUCs of 0.60 to 0.86. The highest discrimination was observed for Parkinson’s disease (AUC = 0.86), Alzheimer’s disease (0.83), DR (0.75), PAD (0.72), and DK (0.71). Internal validation in the UKB yielded AUCs of 0.51 to 0.84 in UKB, indicating good calibration. In the DFTJDC, models incorporating DDRTree dimensions achieved AUCs ranging from 0.58 to 0.75, with relatively better performance for stroke (AUC = 0.75), COPD (0.72), and CVD (0.72).

Discussion

Using seven routine clinical variables, we mapped the heterogeneity of type 2 diabetes across two independent populations through DDRTree. The resulting phenotypic structure, visualized as a branching tree, revealed a continuous spectrum of clinical profiles associated with distinct patterns of diabetes-associated diseases development and differential responses to lifestyle components. Proteomic signatures were identified as a key molecular correlate of these variations, representing one component of the broader biological processes underlying clinical heterogeneity. By integrating deep phenotyping, proteomic profiling, and lifestyle interaction analyses, this framework provides a foundation for precision medicine in type 2 diabetes, enabling improved risk stratification and tailored preventive strategies.

Through the application of data-driven dimensionality reduction methodologies, clinical phenotypes within the UKB population manifested substantial gradients and joint distributions that were mapped in a “tree” structure: BMI and TG were enriched in the upper-left region, HbA1c was skewed toward the left, blood pressure (SBP/DBP) was concentrated in the upper-right, while HDL-C and TC were located in the lower-right and bottom sections, respectively.

Diabetes-associated disease risks exhibited significant spatial clustering within specific regions of the tree, reinforcing the specific associations between phenotypic distribution and clinical outcomes. In the UKB, cardiovascular events and DK converged in the upper-left branch, representing an obesity-dominated metabolic syndrome phenotype. The co-distribution of clinical features within this phenotype is consistent with prior studies [9, 17], and its associated clinical characteristics and complication risk profile resemble those reported by Asplund et al. [18]. This region also exhibited increased risks of Parkinson’s disease and NAFLD, accompanied by elevated liver enzymes and creatinine levels—biomarkers of hepatic and renal dysfunction—suggesting coexisting metabolic abnormalities and shared pathogenic mechanisms.

Proteomic profiling provides crucial molecular insights into the heterogeneity of diabetes. This molecular profile clearly explains their susceptibility to conventional cardiometabolic complications: disorders in lipid and energy metabolism directly drive atherosclerosis and hepatic steatosis [19, 20], while abnormalities in cardiac structure and extracellular matrix remodeling form the pathological basis of heart failure and diabetic nephropathy [21]. Notably, enrichment of inflammatory signaling, including cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathways, mirrors molecular features previously observed in insulin resistance–dominant diabetes subgroups, supporting the presence of shared inflammatory mechanisms underlying cardiometabolic vulnerability [22]. In addition, aberrant expression of cytoskeletal and energy metabolism–related proteins may offer a molecular explanation for the elevated Parkinson’s disease risk observed in this region, suggesting a potential link between metabolic dysregulation and neurodegeneration [23].

The risks of cancer and Alzheimer’s disease were significantly elevated in regions characterized by high TC and HDL-C, exhibiting a distribution pattern almost opposite to that of cardiovascular disease. This phenomenon may reveal an underlying pathophysiological “trade-off” between different disease categories [24]. Although high HDL-C levels confer clear cardiovascular protection via reverse cholesterol transport, the concomitant persistent low-grade immune-inflammatory activation and aberrant DNA damage repair pathways in this phenotype may collectively create a common ground for both malignancies and neurodegeneration. Inflammatory mediators may promote tumor microenvironment formation and blood-brain barrier disruption in both peripheral and central nervous systems, while impaired DNA repair mechanisms further increase the risk of malignant transformation [25, 26]. In other words, individuals with this phenotype may exist in a state of “relative metabolic health but immune dysregulation”, which protects them from conventional cardiometabolic diseases yet predisposes them to age-related conditions.

Meanwhile, a phenotypic region primarily driven by severe hyperglycemia, accompanied by dyslipidemia and obesity, was strongly associated with the risks of diabetic peripheral neuropathy, retinopathy, depression, and COPD [9, 27]. Proteomic analyses demonstrate consistent patterns of immune imbalance, dysregulated lipid metabolism, and enhanced apoptotic signaling across this subgroup. This shared pathophysiological mechanism systematically explains the co-aggregation of seemingly unrelated conditions. Specifically, the chronic inflammatory state may mediate microvascular complications through endothelial injury, contribute to depression and neuropathy by disrupting neurotransmitter balance and neuronal survival [28, 29], and promote the tissue destruction and aberrant remodeling characteristic of COPD [30].

To explore ethnicity-specific outcome associations, we projected data from a Chinese diabetic cohort into the DDRTree model previously established by Qiu et al. based on a Chinese population [10]. The distribution patterns of core metabolic phenotypes-such as obesity, glycemia, and blood pressure—were well preserved in this cohort, strongly supporting the robustness of the type 2 diabetes heterogeneity framework in the Chinese population. We also observed cross-population consistency in diabetes-associated disease risks: for instance, the association between the obesity-dominant phenotype and cardiovascular disease, diabetic ketoacidosis, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease was consistently identified in both Chinese and UK cohorts.

Notably, key ethnic differences emerged. In the Chinese population, the risk of diabetic retinopathy demonstrated a stronger association with hypertension, whereas in the UK population, retinopathy risk was more closely linked to hyperglycemia. These discrepancies may be attributed to a combination of genetic background, dietary habits-such as the influence of a traditional high-sodium diet on blood pressure in Chinese individuals-and differences in clinical practice [31]. Together with the findings from Jia et al., which also emphasized the distinctive role of blood pressure phenotypes and their complication associations in Chinese individuals with diabetes, our results underscore the necessity of accounting for population-specific risk factor profiles when formulating precision prevention strategies.

Our analysis goes beyond general “healthy living” advice, revealing distinct lifestyle-phenotype associations that may inform subtype-specific prevention strategies. Individuals in the upper-left DDRTree branch-characterized by high macrovascular and microvascular complication risk-significantly benefit from adequate physical activity, healthy sleep, and a healthy diet. Interestingly, non-smoking status did not demonstrate a significant independent protective effect within this phenotype, likely because obesity-driven cardiovascular risks outweigh the effects of smoking, and smoking-related weight loss may partially mask its hazards [32]. Furthermore, in this metabolically dysregulated population, moderate alcohol intake did not show a protective effect against retinopathy compared with heavy intake, indicating that reducing or abstaining from alcohol is a more prudent recommendation.

For the phenotype characterized mainly by hypertension (Cluster 2), the focus of clinical intervention should be on smoking cessation and maintaining healthy sleep duration. Both smoking and insufficient sleep are established strong risk factors that exacerbate blood pressure fluctuations and endothelial dysfunction [33]. Interventions for this phenotype should integrate smoking cessation counseling and sleep hygiene management as components equally important to pharmacological blood pressure control.

In the relatively metabolically healthy (Cluster 3) and high-TC (Cluster 4) phenotypes, regular physical activity is central to cardiovascular protection. Exercise enhances insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism—particularly through reverse cholesterol transport—making it a cornerstone non-pharmacological strategy [34]. Among individuals with poor glycemic control (Cluster 5), not smoking and getting sufficient sleep are essential for reducing cardiovascular risk, likely through attenuating inflammation and autonomic dysfunction [35, 36]. Additionally, alcohol abstinence is critical in this group to protect against microvascular complications such as retinopathy.

Our study has several strengths. First, the DDRTree dimensionality reduction and visualization method provides a continuous representation of diabetes heterogeneity by mapping individuals onto a disease spectrum, thereby offering more information than hard clustering algorithms. Furthermore, our work integrates phenotypic distribution, complication risks, responsiveness to lifestyle factors, and proteomic mechanisms, yielding a systematic perspective on diabetes heterogeneity. Finally, the web-based tool we developed uses seven readily available clinical variables to deliver precise individual positioning within the phenotypic structure, offering practical potential for clinical guidance.

We acknowledge that several limitations of our study should be considered. First, by excluding individuals with long-standing diabetes, our findings may not fully represent the broad spectrum of patients encountered in real-world clinical practice. Second, the observed subtype-specific lifestyle associations, while hypothesis-generating, are exploratory and require prospective validation in dedicated intervention trials. Third, the phenotypic structure was constructed using routinely available clinical variables, without inclusion of direct measures of insulin resistance or beta-cell function, which may limit the resolution of specific pathophysiological mechanisms underlying certain subtypes. Finally, due to sample size constraints, the Chinese cohort was projected onto the pre-existing DDRTree. Nevertheless, the consistent distribution patterns and outcome heterogeneity observed support the utility of this approach in populations of shared ethnicity. It is anticipated that future studies, incorporating diverse cohorts and multi-omics data, will facilitate a more refined understanding of the mechanisms underlying diabetic heterogeneity.

Conclusions

Using seven clinical variables, this study identified distinct phenotypes of type 2 diabetes, and further elucidated their differential risks for multiple complications via DDRTree. We further uncovered phenotype-specific associations with lifestyle factors and linked these clinical patterns to underlying proteomic mechanisms. Our work offers a systematic framework for understanding diabetes heterogeneity, and the intuitive visualization tool we developed shows potential to facilitate clinician-patient communication and shared decision-making in clinical practice.
