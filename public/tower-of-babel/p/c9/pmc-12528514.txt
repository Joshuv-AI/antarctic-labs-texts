Post fatigue fracture resistance of lithium disilicate and zirconia crowns: vertical versus horizontal preparations (an in vitro study)
Authors: Mustafa Younus Naji Al-Obaidi, Mohammed Kassim Gholam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo assess and compare the post-fatigue fracture resistance of two types of ceramic crowns, zirconia and lithium-disilicate, when used with two margin designs (vertical and horizontal). Forty sound human premolar teeth collected from the orthodontic department were divided randomly into two main groups according to the type of ceramic material (n = 20): Group A: Lithium-disilicate and Group B: Monolithic zirconia. Each group was further subdivided according to the margin design into two subgroups (n = 10): feather-edge (1) and shoulder (2) preparation designs. After digital scanning and designing, the crowns were bonded with self-adhesive resin cement. Each assembly underwent cyclic loading (1,000,000 cycles; 100 N; 1 Hz) in water and was then subjected to axial compressive loading till fracture. Failure modes were analyzed through fractographic analysis under SEM. The highest mean of fracture resistance was recorded for subgroup B1 (Zirconia with vertical preparation: 3129 N), followed by subgroup B2 (Zirconia with horizontal preparation: 2616 N). while lithium-disilicate crowns showed lower fracture values (Group A1: 1880 N, Group A2: 1512 N). One-way ANOVA test revealed statistically significant differences in fracture resistance between the two crown materials (p < 0.05). However, there was no statistically significant difference between the vertical and horizontal preparation designs within the same material group (p > 0.05). Both zirconia and lithium-disilicate crowns demonstrated fracture strengths exceeding the level of biting forces in the premolar area, supporting the use of conservative feather-edge preparation for lithium-disilicate and zirconia crowns in posterior regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44445-025-00064-5.Keywords: Lithium-disilicate, Zirconia, Crowns, Vertical preparation, Horizontal preparation, Post-fatigue fracture

Introduction

Preparing a tooth for a prosthetic crown nowadays is generally considered invasive today, as it results in irreversible loss of tooth hard tissues that can reach up to 60% of the tooth structure (Špehar and Jakovac 2021; Ashour et al. 2024). This loss is particularly concerning when dealing with a vital tooth, as it increases the risk of pulpal irritation, subsequent inflammation, potential necrosis, and may eventually necessitate endodontic intervention. This risk is further highlighted for periodontally involved abutments, those needing a crown replacement, or badly decayed abutments. Over the years, various efforts have been made to improve tooth preparation techniques, with a focus on preserving tooth structure (Wahsh and Taha 2020).

Optimal dental restoration is determined by three key factors: fracture resistance, marginal adaptation, and esthetic integration (Hasanzade et al. 2021). Conventionally, these objectives have been achieved in all-ceramic restorations, such as lithium disilicate, through horizontal preparation techniques incorporating chamfer or shoulder finish lines (Ali and Kassim 2021; Abdulrahman et al. 2021). Although this approach is considered the gold standard, it necessitates substantial removal of tooth structure, potentially compromising the biological integrity of the tooth (Ramos et al. 2017). However, achieving an optimal equilibrium between maximal tooth preservation through minimally invasive preparation and maintaining the mechanical strength and esthetic quality of the final restoration remains a significant clinical challenge (Abdulazeez and Majeed 2021).

Fortunately, Recent advancements in dental materials and fabrication techniques have enabled the adoption of minimally invasive restorative approaches (Seydler et al. 2014). The introduction of high-strength monolithic dental ceramics has provided a viable alternative for vertical preparation (Jasim et al. 2020). This technique, often termed knife-edge or shoulder-less preparation, is designed to maximize the preservation of vital tooth structure by minimizing the reduction of enamel, dentin, and cementum, particularly in the cervical region. By reducing unnecessary tissue removal, this approach lowers the risk of pulp exposure and contributes to improved long-term dental health (Hasanzade 2021).

Regarding the materials, the material choice becomes crucial when considering the restoration's ability to withstand fractures with either vertical or horizontal design. Zirconia exhibits superior mechanical properties and resistance to crack propagation due to its polycrystalline nature. It has higher flexural strength, ranging from 900 to 1200, and fracture toughness of 6 to 10 MPa·m1/2; in contrast, lithium-disilicate has a flexural strength of 350 to 450 MPa and fracture toughness of 2.5 to 3.0 MPa·m1/.2

Vertical preparation design relies on a finish area rather than a specific finish line. The margins are determined by the laboratory technician based on the gingival tissue information (Patroni et al. 2010; Di Febo et al. 2015; Findakly and Jasim 2019; Abdulazeez and Majeed 2021). Despite the benefits of minimal tooth removal, concerns remain regarding the longevity of all-ceramic restorations, particularly their susceptibility to stress in the marginal areas. This is due to their inherent brittleness compared to their metal counterparts. Recent researches suggested using yttria-stabilized zirconia for knife-edge margins and showed promising results due to its exceptional fracture strength. However, further investigation is needed concerning glassy matrix ceramics for such applications (Cortellini et al. 2015; Stack and Millar 2022).

This research investigated the effectiveness of minimally invasive vertical preparation with lithium disilicate crowns in contrast to the traditional horizontal preparation. The null hypothesis was that there is no significant difference in post-fatigue fracture strength between lithium-disilicate crowns and zirconia crowns, regardless of whether they were prepared with vertical or horizontal preparation techniques.

Materials and methods

The current in-vitro study was approved by the MUCOD Human Research Ethical Committee, College of Dentistry, Mustansiriyah University (Code: MUOPR29). Power G was used to calculate the sample size, ensuring statistical robustness with a power level of 0.80, a significance level of 0.05, and an expected effect size.

Forty healthy maxillary premolar teeth extracted for orthodontic reasons from individuals aged 18 to 22 years were utilized. The teeth were properly cleaned, polished using fluoride-free pumice, and washed with water (Aboushelib et al. 2012). Specific criteria were applied to ensure uniformity, including normal occlusal anatomy and similar crown dimensions (mesiodistal and buccolingual) verified with a digital caliper. Teeth with caries, restorations, or cracks were excluded after examination under a digital microscope (22x). The size variation within groups did not exceed 10%, confirmed by a one-way ANOVA test, which showed no statistically significant differences (Al-khafaji and Jasim 2020).

Teeth were immersed in 0.1% thymol solution for 24 h to inhibit fungal and bacterial growth, after which they were stored in deionized distilled water to prevent dehydration until the experiment commenced (Sandhu et al. 2012; Silva et al. 2012). Each tooth was marked 2 mm apical to the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) using a permanent marker to simulate the biological width (Taha et al. 2014). The teeth were then mounted on the vertically adjustable arm of a modified dental surveyor (Saeshin, Daegu, Korea). The teeth were then embedded in a custom-designed Teflon mold (20 mm diameter, 30 mm depth) containing autopolymerizing self-cure acrylic resin (GC Pattern Resin, GC Corporation, Japan). To replicate the periodontal ligament, light-body addition silicone impression material was applied (Abdul-Haleem and Jasim, 2023).

Teeth randomization and grouping

Following teeth selection based on the inclusion criteria, randomization was performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Each tooth was assigned a unique identification number. These numbers were then entered into a column in Excel. The RAND() function in Excel was used to generate a random number for each tooth. Based on the type of material, the teeth were divided into two main groups: Group A with lithium-disilicate glass ceramic (IPS e.max CAD, Ivoclar Vivadent) and Group B with monolithic zirconia crowns (IPS e.max ZirCAD prime; Ivoclar Vivadent). Each group was further subdivided into two subgroups based on preparation design: subgroups A1 and B1 (feather-edge preparation design) and subgroups A2 and B2 (shoulder preparation design), as shown in (Fig. 1) and demonstrated in (Table 1).

Prosthetic procedures

For standardization purposes, the same operator prepared all specimens by utilizing a modified dental surveyor (Saeshin, Daegu, Korea). The surveyor's upper arm was adapted to hold a high-speed, water-cooled electric handpiece (Guilin Woodpecker Co., China). The acrylic block was fixed on the surveyor's horizontal table, aligning the bur's long axis with the tooth's long axis. This setup ensured a uniform bur taper along the tooth's axial wall (Ibraheem and Hmedat 2013). A permanent color marker was used to mark the future finish line position by drawing a line 1 mm above CEJ following the cervical line of the buccal side as a reference. The teeth preparation was done in two phases: axial preparation and occlusal preparation. Before commencing occlusal reduction, two guide marks were placed 4 mm coronal to the marked margin line on the buccal and palatal surfaces to standardize the axial wall height.

In subgroups A1 and B1, the vertical preparation was performed using a point safe-end tapered diamond stone (851012 M-FG, NTI, Germany) to have a feather-edge margin and followed by refining with a finishing bur, resulting in a 0.8 mm axial reduction. The occlusal reduction was performed following the occlusal anatomy with a total convergence of 8 degrees. For subgroups A2 and B2, shoulder preparation, a flat-end tapered stone (847 K-016 M-FG, NTI, Germany) creates a 1.5 mm axial reduction, a 0.8–1 mm shoulder margin, and a total convergence of 12 degrees. The remaining occlusal-cervical height was adjusted to 4 mm to standardize all specimens and ensure consistency across the study.

Digital impressions were taken with an intraoral scanner (I700, MEDIT Corp., Seoul, South Korea) to produce a 3D image of each tooth that was prepared following the manufacturer's guidelines. Exocad software version 3.2 (Imes-Icore GmbH, Leibozgraben, Germany) was used to design the restorations. The thickness of the crowns was 1.5 mm occlusally (maximum), 0.8–1 mm axially and 0.3 mm at the margin for knife-edge preparation, while for shoulder preparation, the thickness was 1.5 mm occlusally(maximum), 1.3–1.5 mm axially, and the margin finish line was 0.8–1 mm.

Lithium-disilicate crowns were fabricated using press-injectable ingots (IPS emax ingot, Ivoclar Vivadent). Crystallized and glazed at 840 °C for 20 min in a Programmat P310 furnace (Ivoclar, Germany). Zirconia crowns (IPS e.max ZirCAD prime; Ivoclar Vivadent) were dry milled with a 5-axis milling unit (In-Laboratory SX 900 pro, Arum Dentistry, South Korea) and then underwent high-temperature sintering using the sintering furnace (Dekema 674i, Germany), where the temperature was progressively increased to 1541 °C for 8 h.

Before adhesive cementation, the restorations were sandblasted using 50 μm aluminium oxide (Al2O3) powder at 25 MPa pressure, maintaining a distance of 10 mm, a duration of 10 s, and an angle of 45 degrees, following the manufacturer’s guidelines (Ivoclar Vivadent 2017), for proper adhesion. After verifying the fitting, the inner surfaces of the crowns were cleaned in a bath of 96% ethanol alcohol within an ultrasonic cleaner (Digital Heated Ultrasonic Cleaner; H&B Luxuries, China) for 5 min, then rinsed thoroughly with distilled water and dried using oil-free air. For lithium-disilicate crowns, the intaglio surface was treated with 5% hydrofluoric acid (IPS Ceramic Etching Gel, Ivoclar Vivadent) for 20 s, rinsed, and dried. Subsequently, a single layer of silane coupling agent (Monobond N, Ivoclar Vivadent) was applied to the prepared surface and allowed to sit for one minute.

For zirconia groups, the crowns were immersed and cleaned in 96% ethanol using an ultrasonic cleaner for 5 min to remove the remnants from the air abrasion process. The crowns were then air-dried and cleaned with Ivoclean for 20 s. Then, the pre-treated surfaces of the restorations were primed with a thin coat of an MDP-containing monomer (Monobond Plus, Ivoclar Vivadent).

The crowns were then bonded to the teeth with dual-cure resin cement (Variolink II Esthetic DC, Ivoclar Vivadent) and held in place with gentle finger pressure to ensure complete seating. Excess cement was brushed away, and light polymerization was applied for 20 s on all surfaces. The whole assembly was seated inside a custom-made loading device capable of applying a constant load of (5 kg) parallel to the long axis of the crown for 6 min to simulate biting force clinically (Anunmana et al. 2014). All bonded specimens were then stored in 37 °C distilled water for 24 h (Shahrbaf et al. 2014).

Fatigue loading and fracture resistance

After adhesive cementation, all specimens were subjected to fatigue loading with a custom-made device designed to deliver 1,000,000 cycles at 100 N load and a frequency of 1 Hz on each specimen in water at room temperature. The load cycling machine has a 3D-printed composite indenter 3.5 mm in diameter, shaped to resemble a tooth cusp (a round cone) so that it can react with the opposing test specimen, mimicking the natural contact points between opposing teeth during mastication. An articulating paper with 40 µ thickness (Hanel Articulating paper, Coltene, Switzerland) was used for adjusting the position to achieve dual contact points at the buccal and lingual cusps. The test was paused every 50,000 cycles to check for cracks or defects using dental loupes with 7 × magnification. Two examiners independently examined each specimen.Failure was predefined by large chipping, crack, or ceramic bulk fracture.

A load-to-failure test was conducted to assess the fracture strength of all crowns across all groups using a computer-controlled universal testing machine (LARYEE Universal Testing Machine, China). A stainless-steel rod with a 4 mm diameter and a rounded end, attached to the testing machine arm, was used to apply a load to each crown until fracture at a cross-head speed of 0.5 mm/min (Mitov et al. 2016). To prevent accidental peak stresses, a 0.5 mm tin foil (Hanel Occlusion Foil, Coltene, Switzerland) was placed between the ball and the restorations during load application (Kasem et al. 2023). The machine's software (Bluehill 3, Instron Industrial, USA) was used to record the highest load at failure in Newtons (N).

The failed specimens were inspected qualitatively with a stereomicroscope at 40 × magnification. Burke's classification was used to categorize and describe failure modes, as agreed upon by three examiners (Burke 1999) (Table 2). Additionally, a scanning electron microscope (SEM) was used to analyze the fractured surfaces.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS software (Statistical Package for Social Sciences, version 25.0; IBM Corp., NY, USA). The normal distribution of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the significance of the mean fracture strength differences among the four subgroups was determined using an ANOVA test. The Tukey HSD test was applied for multiple group comparisons.

Discussion

While numerous studies have examined the vertical preparation technique with zirconia crowns (Jasim et al. 2020; Abdulazeez and Majeed 2021; Gonzalez-Bonilla et al. 2024), there is a notable lack of research on the performance of lithium-disilicate crowns with thin marginal thicknesses, as in vertical preparations (Špehar and Jakovac 2024). This study aimed to address this gap by comparing the fracture strength of zirconia and lithium-disilicate ceramics when used with both vertical and horizontal preparations. The results indicated no significant difference in fracture resistance between the two preparation techniques within the same material. However, a highly significant difference was observed between lithium-disilicate and zirconia crowns when using the same preparation type. Consequently, the null hypothesis was partially accepted.

Horizontal preparation, such as shoulder and chamfer finish lines, has been used for decades as a gold standard preparation technique for highly esthetic ceramics due to its ability to provide strong support for the restoration; however, it involves significant removal of tooth structure. The vertical preparation technique was employed as a minimally invasive approach, preserving as much of the tooth structure as possible. When combined with high-strength ceramics, this approach allows for reduced invasiveness while maintaining the structural integrity and longevity of the restorations. Thus, comparing the two approaches was essential to understand the balance between conservation and mechanical strength (Cortellini and Canale 2012).

In the past, knife-edge preparations were associated with several clinical challenges, particularly related to the emergence profile and periodontal health. To compensate for the inherent brittleness of traditional ceramics, crowns fabricated for knife-edge preparations often had increased thickness in the marginal area. This led to over-contouring of the crowns, which disrupted the natural emergence profile and caused plaque accumulation, gingival irritation, and periodontal inflammation. These challenges have constrained the broader implementation of knife-edge preparations, particularly in areas requiring high aesthetic considerations.

The development of high-strength materials, such as monolithic zirconia and lithium disilicate, has significantly improved the mechanical properties of ceramic restorations. These materials exhibit superior flexural strength, fracture toughness, and resistance to crack propagation compared to traditional ceramics. These properties allow for thinner marginal designs without compromising the structural integrity of the restoration (Jasim 2023).

The improved mechanical properties of these materials have prompted clinicians to revisit the concept of knife-edge preparations. With the ability to fabricate crowns with minimal marginal thickness and optimal emergence profiles, the risk of periodontal inflammation has been significantly reduced. Additionally, adhesive cementation techniques have further enhanced the stability and longevity of these restorations, creating a monoblock effect that distributes occlusal forces more evenly and reduces stress concentration at the margins (Elsherbini et al. 2022).

In this study, human teeth were used as abutments (all freshly extracted) because their elastic characteristics affect the fracture strength of the all-ceramic restoration (Yucel et al. 2012) In addition to their strength, using human teeth allows for adhesive cementation of the crowns and stimulated the clinical situation more than any other type of abutment (Chen et al. 2023).

All steps of preparation were done using a modified dental surveyor for standardization and to minimize the variables during preparation, such as the design of the finishing line, degree of axial wall taper, and restoration's path of insertion (Kocaağaoğlu et al. 2017).

A dual-cured universal adhesive resin cement was chosen to lute all the crowns to their matching teeth due to its physical stability and elasticity (11.8 GPa), which is nearly comparable to that of dentin (18GPa) (Ashour et al. 2024). The use of other, especially non-adhesive, cements could, therefore, result in different findings (Seydler et al. 2014).

To stimulate the fatigue that results from repeated occlusal loading during function, cyclic loading was employed using a load of 100 Newtons, as it replicates the average of occlusal forces in the premolar region, and the number of cycles was 1,000,000 cycles to approximate three years of clinical function. This methodology ensured that the findings reflect realistic intraoral conditions and offered a reliable evaluation of the restorative materials' ability to withstand the mechanical stresses encountered in clinical settings.

In this study, the mean values of fracture strength for the various tested subgroups exceeded the maximum biting force of the maxillary premolar region, which generally ranges from 300 −500 N (Sakaguchi and Powers 2011; Jayakumar et al. 2023; Peyron 2024). The above finding coincided with the results of previous studies, suggesting that monolithic lithium disilicate crowns can be successfully used with a reduced marginal thickness in the posterior region without compromising their clinical performance (Lindner et al. 2023; Špehar and Jakovac 2024).

The lack of a significant difference between subgroup A1 and subgroup A2 can be explained by the nature of lithium disilicate as a material and its adhesive features. The primary strength of lithium disilicate crowns lies in the adhesive cementation and bonding to the tooth rather than the type of margin design and margin thickness. This finding showed agreement with the results of a study by (Cortellini et al. 2015) who evaluated the longevity of lithium-disilicate crowns bonded to epoxy resin abutments prepared with vertical preparation and chamfer finish lines after cyclic loading. Results showed no significant difference in fracture strength between the different types of finish line designs.

It should be noted that the mean value of fracture strength for subgroup A1 was slightly higher than A2. This can be explained by the conservative preparation of the teeth while following the protocol of vertical preparation, which preserves much of the tooth structure, and the fact that the bulk of the tooth is more resistant to failure compared to horizontal preparation.

Similarly, the lack of a significant difference between group B1 and B2 was due to zirconia's high inherent fracture toughness and flexural strength. This robustness means that the type of tooth preparation has less impact on the overall fracture strength of the zirconia crowns compared to the material's high compressive strength and resistance to mechanical fatigue. This agrees with (Kasem et al. 2020), who evaluated the performance of zirconia and zirconia-reinforced glass–ceramic systems when used with shoulder and feather-edge preparations. Results showed no significant difference in fracture resistance between the two margin designs.

Another research by (Jasim et al. 2020) evaluated the impact of two types of margin design (knife edge and light chamfer) on the fracture resistance of monolithic zirconia crowns with two occlusal thicknesses (1 mm and 0.5 mm). The authors found that the feather-edge margin has a superior outcome than the light chamfer. This finding agrees with other studies that showed vertical preparation was superior to or equal to horizontal preparation (Mitov et al. 2016; Emam and A Aleem 2020; Haddad and Azzi 2022).

Mode of failure

This study employed fractographic analysis under scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to examine the fracture pattern. The modes of failure varied significantly across the different preparation designs, with the key differences primarily related to the stress distribution and the nature of cementation.

The knife edge group showed that the crack originated from the occlusal load point at the interface and propagated toward the crown core, and due to the minimal margin thickness, the stress concentration was along the axial wall, which is why subgroups A1 and B1 had a lower incidence of type V fracture (catastrophic fractures). This fracture pattern is consistent with findings from previous studies, where the vertical preparation results in less invasive removal of tooth structure, maintaining more axial wall height. The vertical margins provided limited stress concentration at the margin, contributing to improved load distribution along the tooth surface. In shoulder groups, and due to greater structural reduction during preparation, and more material thickness at the margin, which helps to distribute stress more evenly, acting as a barrier that absorbs part of the load, the strength of the whole assembly was reduced. This increases the incidence of catastrophic failure in the A2 and B2 subgroups.

The analysis also revealed that the lithium-disilicate crowns with knife edge margins demonstrated more prominent cohesive failure with minimal chipping at the margin; again, this is explained by the structural integrity offered by the knife edge design; in addition to that, the mono-block effect created by adhesive cementation, which bonds the crown and tooth as a single unit.

Based on these findings, we recommend using a more conservative preparation with lithium disilicate crowns in the posterior region, as it demonstrated adequate strength and adaptation for this application. However, zirconia remains preferable in high-stress areas due to its superior mechanical properties.

Limitation

It is important to acknowledge that the current investigations have inherent limitations. Initially, the load was only applied vertically, not mimicking the lateral stresses found in the mouth cavity. Furthermore, thermocycling, which would more closely resemble clinical situations, was not included in the study. The results are nonetheless comparable and may be helpful for future research advancement despite all the constraints.
