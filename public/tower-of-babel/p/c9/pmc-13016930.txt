Prenatal experience of greater neighborhood disadvantage is associated with altered fetal volumetric brain growth in utero
Authors: Kevin M Cook, Josepheen De Asis-Cruz, Kushal Kapse, Kelsey Christoffel, Caitlin McDermott, Nickie Andescavage, Catherine Limperopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeighborhood disadvantage is associated with worse health outcomes and rates of neurodevelopmental disorders. In childhood, experience of greater neighborhood disadvantage is associated with atypical structural and functional brain development, especially within the limbic system. Although these differences are observed throughout the lifespan, there is a profound gap in our understanding of how early neighborhood disadvantage impacts brain development. Using a prospective cohort of 199 healthy pregnancies from around Washington, DC, we performed serial fetal brain MRIs during pregnancy. Using nested linear mixed-effects models, we assess the impact of neighborhood disadvantage on brain volumes throughout fetal development. We found that greater neighborhood disadvantage was associated with significant differences in brain volumes, especially limbic areas. Increases in neighborhood disadvantage were linearly associated with larger volumes earlier in gestation and smaller volumes approaching term age. Importantly, these relationships vary depending on fetal sex, with different regions exhibiting sex-dependent vulnerabilities. These results suggest that even prior to birth, neighborhood disadvantage is associated with altered structural brain development. While the cause of this relationship is unclear, it may stem from the impact of maternal stress and adverse environmental exposures. Our findings provide an important lens to understand the impacts of how socioeconomic inequalities can influence development beginnings prenatally.Keywords: fetal, limbic, MRI, neighborhoods, socioeconomic

Introduction

Socioeconomic status (SES) is a longstanding construct that delineates access to wealth and social capital (Liberatos et al. 1988). Broadly, this includes economic markers like household income and home value as well as social markers such as educational attainment and professional class. Most commonly, SES is discussed within the context of the household, emphasizing the individual makeup of one’s living situation on health and development. Individuals with lower SES have consistently been shown to have greater numbers of health complaints and worse prognoses throughout the lifespan, including cardiovascular and mental health (Harper and Lynch 2007; Reiss 2013; He et al. 2021; Sabel et al. 2024). Moreover, children in lower SES households experience rates of developmental delays, including language and motor development of nearly two and a half times higher than high SES households (Potijk et al. 2013; Chow et al. 2017; Last et al. 2018; Sahni et al. 2024), as well as neurodevelopmental disorders such as intellectual disabilities and autism spectrum disorder (He et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2021).

While SES is most often contextualized within the home, the wider environment is equally impactful. This community-wide SES, known as neighborhood disadvantage (ND), similarly attempts to describe access to social and economic capital, but at a wider scale to better capture more systemic factors. Common measures such as the social vulnerability index (SVI) capture the aggregate wealth and advantage of an area, including median income and educational attainment, alongside other characteristics like unemployment rate and access to transportation (Flanagan et al. 2011; Point and Tran-Photographer 2024). Importantly, ND has been found to contribute to health and behavioral problems throughout the lifespan, even after controlling for household characteristics, suggesting that it confers its own unique risk outside of household SES (Ludwig et al. 2012; Singh and Ghandour 2012). Similarly, changing neighborhoods without significantly changing household SES has been associated with relevant health changes, including evidence that moving to a higher SES neighborhood is associated with lower prevalence of obesity and diabetes (Ludwig et al. 2011), while mothers who moved to lower SES neighborhoods between pregnancies experienced higher rates of infant morbidities and mortality (Jairam et al. 2025).

Experience of both lower household SES and ND have been associated with significant changes in the brain as well. At the household level, children and adolescents experiencing lower SES exhibit atypical cortical development and language skills (Norbom et al. 2022) alongside reduced functional connectivity in sensorimotor and default mode networks (Gao et al. 2015), as well as diminished white matter connectivity and volumetric differences within similar regions (Gianaros et al. 2013; Lu et al. 2021). While at the neighborhood level, ND has been similarly associated with alterations in functional and structural brain development, with children in more disadvantaged neighborhoods exhibiting abnormal medial prefrontal connectivity, especially to limbic regions like the ventral striatum and amygdala (Ramphal et al. 2020a; Ramphal et al. 2020b; Tian et al. 2021), even after accounting for household SES. In particular, the limbic system appears most vulnerable to ND-associated changes. Exposure to greater ND has been associated with significant alterations in brain structure and function across the lifespan. Higher levels of ND have been shown to correlate with atypical connectivity and reactivity of the amygdala compared to peers with lower levels of ND (Gard et al. 2021; Ip et al. 2022; Suarez et al. 2022), and regions including the amygdala and cerebellum are significantly smaller in volume (Hunt et al. 2020; Ku et al. 2024). Importantly, these effects are long lasting, with evidence that childhood experiences of higher ND were predictive of atypical functional brain network architecture later in life (Michael et al. 2023).

Although these differences have been observed during childhood and adolescence, a significant gap remains in our understanding of how early in life associations between ND and brain development can be observed. Given evidence that these effects are pervasive after childhood exposure, it is important to understand at what ages these developmental trajectories begin to diverge in order to characterize timepoints at which meaningful remedies and interventions can be targeted. While there are a few studies that have observed differences in household SES and neonatal brain morphology and connectivity (Spann et al. 2020; Ramphal et al. 2020b; Triplett et al. 2022), only a single study has assessed neighborhood-level associations in the fetal period, focusing on the emerging functional connectome (Cook et al. 2024). This project aims to capitalize on prior work to assess whether early functional brain changes are mirrored in structural brain changes during the fetal period, and we hypothesize that the limbic system in particular is vulnerable to the impact of varying degrees of neighborhood disadvantage.

Materials and methods

Participants

We performed secondary data analyses on prospectively recruited cohorts of healthy pregnant women with normal, singleton fetal ultrasounds and echocardiograms who were recruited into studies investigating typical fetal brain development. Based on study and age of consent, one to two fetal MRI scans were performed, and all scans that passed quality assessment were used for these analyses. Recruitment took place at Children’s National Hospital in Washington, DC, and included families both from within DC and the neighboring states of Maryland and Virginia. There were exclusions for fetuses with known genetic or chromosomal abnormalities and pregnant women with complicating conditions based on review of medical records (including current self-report of any current psychiatric diagnosis, metabolic disorders like diabetes, or genetic disorders such as sickle-cell disease; as well as those with complications such as preeclampsia or gestational diabetes) or contraindications to MRI. Only brains without significant injuries and/or abnormalities, as reviewed by an experienced fetal neuroradiologist, and whose reconstructed anatomical scans met data quality criteria were included in the analyses. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Children’s National (IRB Approval: Pro00003898) and written informed consent obtained from each mother.

A total of 373 scans from 228 fetuses were acquired across the cohort. Of these, 6 scans had significant image artifacts, and 27 families did not provide an address that would allow us to determine censustract level ND (eg using a PO Box), yielding 326 scans from 220 infants. Finally, following the geographical restrictions defined below in the Experimental Design, the final sample comprised 298 scans from 199 fetuses residing in 152 census tracts.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

Fetal brain MRI were acquired using a 1.5-T GE Discovery MR450 (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI). Sagittal, axial, and coronal anatomical T2-weighted images were collected using a single-shot fast spin echo sequence with 1100 ms TR, 160 ms TE, 0.859mm3 reconstructed voxel size, and 90° flip angle and were subsequently reconstructed into a 3D anatomical image (Kainz et al. 2015). Brains were then segmented using a previously published 3D U-Net-based approach (Makropoulos et al. 2014) to yield both anatomical and tissue-specific segmentations. Resulting segmentations were then inspected and manually corrected as needed. Following inspection, volumes in cubic millimeters were calculated for each fetus using AFNI (Cox 1996) for each tissue type and then for 17 regions comprising limbic and limbic-associated regions—the bilateral amygdalae, hippocampi, anterior and posterior parahippocampal gyri, anterior and posterior cingulate gyrus, and thalami.

Experimental design and statistical analyses

We calculated ND using the CDC/ATSDR SVI (Flanagan et al. 2011; Point and Tran-Photographer 2024) for DC, Maryland, and Virginia, after which the geographic boundaries were limited to within 20 mi of DC (using the center of DC at 38°53′42.40″N to 77°02′10.93″W) to homogenize cost-of-living experiences across participants. The SVI (Flanagan et al. 2011) is calculated using the American Community Survey (U.S. Census Bureau 2021) and includes measures socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status, and housing type and transportation including population below the 150% poverty line, single-parent households, racial and ethnic minorities, and housing crowding (see SI Table S1 for the full list).

Initially, total brain volume (TBV) was assessed using a nested linear mixed-effects model for SVI, gestational age (GA) at scan, and the interaction of the two, while also controlling for participant ID to account for repeated scans nested in census tracts to account for multiple, different participants residing in the same census tract. In cases of significant interactions between two continuous variables (SVI and GA), marginal effects were calculated between SVI and volumes at each week of gestation and assessed using 95% confidence intervals. Subsequently, the same models were utilized to assess cerebral tissue types, focusing on cortical gray matter, white matter, and subcortical gray matter volumes. Given significant differences in TBV, those volumes were then normalized to TBV in each fetus. Results from models were then false discovery rate (FDR) corrected (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Following, the same nested linear mixed-effects models were used to examine specific regions of interest (ROIs) within the larger limbic system comprising medial temporal lobe structures, thalamus, and cingulate (Pessoa and Hof 2015), with volumes normalized to TBV and results FDR corrected. Finally, the ROIs were replicated to examine the differential effects of sex on the impact of SVI on volumes across gestation.

Results

Social vulnerability distribution

The final sample comprised 298 scans from 199 fetuses and were from 152 individual census tracts (Fig. 1A). We utilized census tract based on address of initial consent for each participant. The scans were conducted at an average GA of 31.50 ± 4.78 (range 18.3 to 39.7) weeks (see Supplemental Information [SI] Fig. S1 for full distribution) and were 47.77% male. Within the geographic boundary, there are 985 census tracts with a mean SVI of 0.425 ± 0.30 [0.0001, 0.9855], although the median value is 0.369 due to significant skewness toward lower vulnerability (skewness = 0.305) with fewer tracts representing higher vulnerability neighborhoods (Fig. 1B). Importantly, our final sample of 152 tracts with a mean SVI of 0.484 ± 0.32 [0.0076, 0.9855] (Fig. 1C) is significantly different from the overall population in vulnerability t(1,135) = 2.87, P = 0.004. Of note, however, our sample does not show the skew toward lower vulnerability (median = 0.453, skewness = 0.060) that is observed in the population level, suggesting that our sample may not be as representative of greater DC as a whole but instead more evenly captures tracts across the SVI spectrum.

Global tissue volumes

Initially, we assessed the relationship between SVI and TBV across GA. Alongside significant main effects for SVI (β = 94,353; P < 0.001) and GA at scan (β = 16,900; P < 0.001), there was a significant interaction between SVI and GA for total brain volume (β = −2,950; P < 0.001). Based on the marginal effects, at an earlier GA there was a positive relationship between SVI and TBV such that greater vulnerability was associated with greater TBV. With increasing GA, however, this relationship becomes less significant with the opposite relationship observed by term age with the relationship becoming negative such that greater SVI is associated with reduced TBV by 40 weeks GA.

Subsequently, brain volumes across cortical gray matter, white matter, subcortical gray matter, cerebellum, and brainstem were analyzed (Fig. 2, SI Table S2), controlling for TBV to account for the significant relationships between TBV and SVI and GA. Following FDR correction, cortical gray matter had no relationship with SVI but increased with GA at scan after controlling for TBV (β = 0.004; P = 0.002). Alternatively, there were main effects for SVI for both white matter volume and subcortical gay matter (P < 0.007), and GA and white matter volume (P < 0.001), while there were also significant interactions between SVI and GA for both volumes (P < 0.018). Based on marginal effects, white matter volumes exhibited a negative relationship at earlier GA with an experience of greater SVI being associated with smaller volumes. However, that relationship diminishes over gestation to a negligible relationship by term age, evidenced by the inclusion of 0 within the 95% confidence intervals. Conversely, subcortical gray matter volumes exhibited a positive relationship at earlier GAs with greater vulnerability associated with larger volumes. Over gestation, that relationship reverses such that by term age there is a negative relationship between SVI and subcortical gray matter volumes, suggesting that greater vulnerability is associated with reduced brain volumes.

While cortical gray matter, cerebellum, and brainstem only exhibited a significant main effect for GA, white matter volumes exhibited a main effect for both SVI and GA as well as a significant interaction and subcortical gray matter only exhibited a main effect for SVI and an interaction between SVI and GA.

Limbic region volumes

Following broader tissue-specific relationships, regional volumes of limbic regions were then normalized to total brain volumes and assessed (Table 1). While some regions exhibited a significant main effect for GA without a main effect of, or interaction with, SVI (bilateral anterior cingulate and right amygdala, P < 0.002), no ROI exhibited a significant main effect for SVI without interacting with GA. The left amygdala, bilateral anterior parahippocampal gyri, and bilateral thalami exhibit a significant interaction between vulnerability and GA. Across all regions except the left amygdala, positive relationships between SVI and volumes were observed at early gestational ages which decrease over time and are negatively associated by term age in the left anterior parahippocampal gyrus and bilateral thalami. The right anterior parahippocampal gyrus exhibited a similar pattern, but the SVI and volume relationship is not significant nearing term age. Conversely, the left amygdala exhibits a significantly negative relationship between SVI and volume early in gestation that trends positive by term age (SI Fig. S2).

Sex differences

Finally, to assess the differential impact of sex on the observed findings, we recapitulated the above limbic models to include sex. These analyses yielded significant three-way interactions for all ROIs, except the right anterior parahippocampal gyrus and left thalamus, suggesting differential impacts of SVI on volumes across gestation (Table 2). Marginal effects analyses suggest a sex-dependent impact of SVI on regional brain volumes across GA (Fig. 3), with heterogeneous relationships across regions. While the right amygdala, left anterior parahippocampal gyrus and hippocampus exhibit sex differences early in gestation, the converge approaches term age and the right hippocampus experiences a weak diverging relationship with males showing a steeper decline across gestation. The opposite is observed in the left amygdala and right posterior parahippocampal gyrus with a steeper rise than females across gestation. Finally, all four cingulate ROIs exhibit no association in female fetuses but an increasingly positive relationship for male fetuses.

Discussion

We report that experience of greater ND is associated with significant differences in global and regional volumetric brain growth during the second half of gestation, ultimately resulting in decreased total brain and subcortical gray matter volumes and increased white matter volumes by term age. This pattern is mirrored across limbic structures in the latter half of gestation, ultimately resulting in impaired growth of bilateral anterior parahippocampal gyri and thalami and increased volumetric growth of the left amygdala by term age. Importantly, the impact of ND across gestation appears to selectively impact limbic region growth in a sex-dependent manner, with varying developmental trajectories for male and female fetuses.

Our findings suggest that prenatal exposure to greater ND is associated with altered trajectories of structural brain development in the latter half of gestation, prior to extrauterine exposure postnatally. These differences occur both at the level of the whole brain as well as within limbic regions. The association between atypical limbic and limbic association area volumes is in line with what is observed in the literature, particularly our findings in the amygdala and cerebellum (Gard et al. 2021; Lu et al. 2021; Triplett et al. 2022). Limbic and limbic association regions are particularly vulnerable to ND and SES due to their fundamental role in modulating stress (Zhang et al. 2019a; Zhang et al. 2019b). Experience of ND during the fetal period likely results in an elevated state of allostatic chronic stress, whether it is directly related to placental transfer of maternal stress through exposure to glucocorticoids (Sandman et al. 2006; Jung et al. 2011) or through wider stress experiences from developmentally adverse exposures to pollutants, chemicals, inadequate nutrition, or other exposures. Longitudinal stress exposure in adults has shown significant changes in limbic system development (Zhang et al. 2019a; Liu et al. 2020) likely due to the significant presence of glucocorticoid receptors throughout the system (McEwen et al. 2016). As a result, our findings suggest that the trajectories observed later in life have their footprints in the prenatal period.

Although this is consistent with prior literature, there are additional considerations about the mechanism of action impacting these developmental trajectories. In studies of children and adolescents, they are directly and actively exposed to their socioeconomic contexts. This is not the case for fetal development, where the extrauterine world is experienced through maternal–fetal physiological interactions. One principal vector for this communication is through maternal experience of stress that is then transferred to the fetus throughout gestation. There is a significant body of literature suggesting that ND is associated with elevated stress, with studies showing that neighborhood crime is associated with elevated stress in pregnancy and early parenting (Shannon et al. 2020; Mayne et al. 2022). Prior work directly assessing stress in pregnancy have found associations between degrees of stress and the development of limbic regions in offspring (Marr et al. 2023; Weiner et al. 2024). Importantly, it has been suggested that the stress resulting from experiences of ND is the principal driver behind the impact of ND on development (Baum et al. 1999). However, it is difficult to rule out the impact of other factors including access to adequate housing and nutrition as well as exposure to toxins and pollutants. Exposure to noxious agents such as mercury and lead has long been associated with significant health and developmental problems throughout the lifespan (Goyer 1996; Golding et al. 2016; Loftus et al. 2019). These toxins and pollutants, however, are far more common in more socioeconomically disadvantaged communities (Martenies et al. 2017) for numerous economic and political reasons. It is therefore also likely that these exposures, alongside maternal stress, may account for the significant relationships observed in studies of ND; however, these are not easily capturable with SVI which relies on census data from the American Community Survey. Thus, additional work is required to tease out how the exact factors are driving the observed effects.

Importantly, we observed that these effects were variable based on fetal sex, with male and female fetuses exhibiting different relationships between ND and regional brain volumetric growth over the second half of gestation. Prior work has found that prenatal stress exposure is associated with structural brain differences throughout life in a clear sex-dependent manner (Mareckova et al. 2020; Kumpulainen et al. 2023; Weiner et al. 2024). Importantly, a direct mechanistic relationship is currently unclear, with broad sex differences observed in placental responses throughout pregnancy (Rosenfeld 2015), glucocorticoid modulation (Wieczorek et al. 2019), interactions with hormonal programming (Goldstein et al. 2014), or even changes in blood iron levels in response to stress which can be associated with subsequent developmental alterations (Zimmermann et al. 2022). Additional work is required to fully tease out the direct causal associations for the changes we observed and whether they are associated with behavioral outcomes later in life.

Our findings suggest that the neighborhoods and communities experienced by the maternal–fetal dyad are already associated with differences in brain development. While ND has previously been linked to neurodevelopmental alterations in childhood and adolescence, this places further emphasis on the importance of characterizing the communities mothers are part of during pregnancy and to offer appropriate supports when possible. While future research is needed to understand the direct mechanisms of action for our observed associations, these could include providing mental health supports, dietary supplementation, or socioeconomic supports around housing or food insecurity.

This study offers the first large-scale attempt to characterize the association between ND and brain development during the fetal period. Our findings, importantly, align with what has been observed in later development. However, there are still remaining gaps that need to be addressed in future studies. Primarily, in the case of childhood associations, the child–environment dynamic can be easily assessed given the direct interaction between the two. In the case of fetal associations, however, much of the fetal–environment dynamic is filtered through the maternal experience and environment, making the study of clear mechanisms more difficult. As discussed, there are many possible mechanisms that could explain the observed associations including environmental pollutants that pass through maternal–fetal exchanges, the impact of nutritional differences across communities, or the impact of maternal stress derived from her lived experience in a more vulnerable community. Given the naissance of this field, many of these questions need to be addressed in subsequent follow-up studies.

Our study has several key strengths that should be highlighted. First, our data comprise a large sample for fetal MRI studies, which are still highly technically challenging and prone to significant data loss. Second, our sample closely matches the wider social vulnerability of the greater DC community, offering strong generalizability across the vulnerability spectrum. Finally, this study is the first large-scale study during the fetal period to attempt to characterize these relationships. There are some limitations of this current study that should be addressed in future work. First, our findings are limited to a specific geographic area. While our sample represents a wide range of social vulnerabilities, as a whole, the region around DC may not be representative of the wider population given its dense, urban nature. Future work is required to assess generalizability to other communities. Second, although our findings in the fetal period are in line with what has previously been observed in childhood and adolescence, there has yet to be prospective longitudinal follow-up from the fetal through childhood periods. The secondary analysis design of this study allows for aggregation of a large dataset but means that certain factors not collected across all studies cannot be fully explored. As an example, despite repeated scans in approximately half of the participants, changes in address between the two scans were not captured to assess how changing environments may impact developmental trajectories. Moreover, a shared measure of stress was not collected across all participants, and thus while stress is the proposed mechanism, there is insufficient power to directly test that hypothesis with the current data, nor is there sufficient ability to delineate various types of stress and sociocultural exposures which have been found to be associated with limbic connectivity in fetuses (Spann et al. 2024). Moreover, stress derived from ND does not end at birth, with women in more disadvantaged communities at high risk for postpartum depression (Onyewuenyi et al. 2023) which can continue to impact infants and children. Importantly, while socioeconomic factors play a role in brain and behavioral development, one cannot discount the many other factors that play a significant role. For example, parenting can exacerbate or moderate the impact of ND (Whittle et al. 2017) and provide an important factor not fully captured in most studies of SES and ND in children. Future work is required to better distinguish behavioral and socioeconomic factors on the individual and community level that can often be strongly tied together to understand the ideal supports to make available to families.

Despite these lingering questions, our findings shed light on the complex interplay of wider socioeconomic environments and brain development, suggesting that the impacts are already being felt prior to birth. Our findings emphasize the need to better assess individual factors over aggregate ND in order to derive targets for broad, public health interventions to make real change in neurodevelopmental trajectories.
