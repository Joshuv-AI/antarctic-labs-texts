Effects of different high-intensity interval training modes of wattbike on anaerobic capacity in Chinese national alpine skiers
Authors: Yiheng Zeng, Yike Sun, Junlei Lin, Zhonghe Yang, Chengyu Zhou, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study compared two anaerobic high-intensity interval training (HIIT) modes with distinct work–rest structures—repeated sprint training (RST) and speed endurance maintenance training (SEMT)—on anaerobic performance in Chinese national-level alpine skiers. Eighteen Chinese national alpine skiers (age:19.8 ± 1.9 years) were randomly allocated to RST (n = 9) or SEMT (n = 9). RST consisted of 5 sets of 6 × (10 s all-out / 5 s rest) with 180 s recovery between sets; SEMT consisted of 5 × (60 s all-out / 180 s recovery). Both groups completed 12 sessions over 4 weeks (3 sessions/week). Performance was assessed 48 h before and after the intervention using countermovement jump (CMJ), a 90s lateral box jump, and a 90s Wingate test. Wingate outcomes were relative peak power (RPP), relative mean power (RMP), and fatigue index (FI). CMJ height increased in RST (+ 2.77%, p = 0.017, d = 0.995) but not in SEMT (+ 0.73%, p = 0.424, d = 0.281). The 90s lateral box jump improved in both RST (+ 9.21%, p = 0.004, d = 1.347) and SEMT (+ 7.52%, p < 0.001). Wingate RPP increased in RST (+ 2.35%, p = 0.003) but not in SEMT (− 0.09%, p = 0.972, d = 2.089), whereas Wingate RMP increased in SEMT (+ 3.91%, p = 0.017, d = 1.002) but not in RST (+ 1.49%, p = 0.056, d = 0.746). FI decreased in SEMT (− 3.38%, p = 0.001, d = 1.622) but not in RST (+ 0.08%, p = 0.943, d = 0.025). Between-group differences in change were observed for Wingate RPP (p = 0.037, d = 1.073) and FI (p = 0.02, d = 1.214). Over 4 weeks, both RST and SEMT improved sport-relevant anaerobic performance. In this cohort, RST showed greater improvements in anaerobic power-related outcomes, whereas SEMT showed greater improvements in fatigue resistance during prolonged supramaximal work.Keywords: speed endurance maintenance training, repeated sprint training, alpine skiing, anaerobic capacity, national teamSubject terms: Health care, Physiology

Introduction

Alpine skiing competitions include two speed disciplines (super-giant slalom [SG], downhill [DH]) and two technical disciplines (slalom [SL], giant slalom [GS]), which differ markedly in turning radius, speed, course length, and race duration1. Technical disciplines are characterized by frequent and rhythmical turns (approximately 1.8 s and 2.9 s per turn in SL and GS, respectively), whereas speed disciplines involve longer turns and slower movement cycles (approximately 4.0 s and 5.0 s per turn in SG and DH, respectively)2. During each turn, elite alpine skiers are exposed to peak ground reaction forces of approximately three to four times body mass in the lower limbs3, representing an extreme mechanical and neuromuscular load. In contrast, the transition phase between gates—characterized by partial unloading and forward gliding—can be regarded as a brief period of lower-intensity recovery.

Collectively, these competition characteristics resemble intermittent high-intensity actions separated by brief recovery phases. This intermittent structure closely resembles high-intensity interval exercise and highlights the work–rest ratio as a key variable when prescribing anaerobic conditioning for alpine skiers. Importantly, the relative contribution of work and recovery phases varies across disciplines, with technical events emphasizing frequent explosive actions and speed events requiring a greater tolerance to sustained high force output. Therefore, optimizing the work–rest structure of high-intensity interval training (HIIT) represents a practically relevant but underexplored aspect of conditioning periodization in alpine skiing.

The anaerobic energy metabolism system plays an important role in the final performance of alpine skiing. It has been pointed out that in alpine skiing, the energy metabolism of anaerobic metabolism (phosphagen metabolism system and glycolysis metabolism system) accounts for about 65%, and the energy supplied by aerobic metabolism system is around 35%4,5. Anaerobic metabolic energy is mainly used to maintain muscle strength and neuromuscular coordination, however, how to maintain muscle strength and ensure synergistic neuromuscular involvement is also a pressing focus in the training of alpine skiing5–7. Valentin et al. further pointed out that the proportion of aerobic metabolic energy metabolism in speed series events such as SG is 48.5%, which is almost equal to the proportion of anaerobic energy metabolism7. Consequently, anaerobic capacity is essential not only for energy supply but also for maintaining high-force muscle contractions and neuromuscular coordination under fatigue8.

High-intensity interval training (HIIT) is typically defined as repeated bouts of near-maximal or all-out exercise (≥ 90% of maximal oxygen uptake or > 75% of maximal power output) interspersed with periods of rest or low-intensity recovery9. With the development for over 100 years10, there are various categorization models of HIIT. According to the training goal-oriented HIIT categorization proposed by Stöggl TL et al.11, anaerobic HIIT can be subdivided into distinct modalities based on work duration, recovery duration, and targeted physiological adaptations. Two modalities of particular relevance to alpine skiing are repeated sprint training (RST) and speed endurance maintenance training (SEMT).

RST is defined as repeated short all-out efforts (typically ≤ 30 s) separated by incomplete recovery periods, commonly characterized by a work-to-rest ratio < 1:411. In contrast, SEMT consists of longer supramaximal efforts (typically ≤ 90 s) interspersed with longer but still incomplete recovery, commonly described by a work-to-rest ratio of approximately 1:312.

From a mechanistic perspective, these two HIIT modalities are expected to elicit distinct adaptations. Short repeated sprints with limited recovery emphasize rapid phosphocreatine resynthesis and repeated peak power production, which may be particularly relevant for the explosive turning demands of technical disciplines. In contrast, longer supramaximal intervals impose greater glycolytic strain and metabolite accumulation, thereby challenging fatigue tolerance and buffering capacity, which are critical for speed disciplines and for maintaining performance in the latter stages of a run. Accordingly, we selected outcome measures that reflect complementary aspects of anaerobic performance: CMJ as a marker of explosive neuromuscular performance; Wingate RPP as peak anaerobic power; Wingate RMP and FI as indices of sustained supramaximal performance and fatigue development; and a 90s lateral box jump as a sport-relevant assessment integrating repeated stretch–shortening actions over a duration comparable to alpine skiing demands.

Despite the widespread use of HIIT in alpine skiing preparation, direct experimental comparisons of anaerobic HIIT protocols with clearly different work–rest structures in elite alpine skiers are currently lacking. In particular, it remains unclear whether manipulating work–rest ratios preferentially enhances anaerobic power or fatigue resistance within a short, practically relevant training period.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to compare the effects of two 4-week anaerobic HIIT interventions with distinct work–rest structures—RST (10 s work / 5 s rest; work–rest ratio 2:1 within sets) and SEMT (60 s work / 180 s rest; work–rest ratio 1:3)—on anaerobic performance in Chinese national-level alpine skiers. We hypothesized that both protocols would improve sport-specific anaerobic capacity, but that RST would preferentially enhance explosive power-related outcomes, whereas SEMT would induce greater improvements in anaerobic endurance and fatigue resistance. By directly contrasting these two HIIT modalities, the present study aims to provide evidence-based guidance for conditioning periodization and work–rest prescription in elite alpine skiing.

Methods

Experimental approach to the problem

A randomized controlled study design was used to examine the effects of two HIIT protocols on anaerobic performance in elite alpine skiers. Following baseline testing, participants were randomly allocated to either the RST group or the SEMT group using a computer-generated random number sequence.

Due to the nature of the training interventions, participant blinding was not feasible. However, all performance assessments were conducted using standardized protocols and verbal instructions in order to minimize potential motivational or tester bias. Assessors were not informed of the specific study hypotheses.

All participants completed pre-intervention testing 48 h before, and post-intervention testing 48 h after, the 4-week intervention period. Tests were conducted in a fixed order (countermovement jump, 90s lateral box jump and 90s Wingate test) to prioritize neuromuscular and plyometric performance while ensuring identical testing conditions between pre- and post-assessments. Participants

Due to the elite national-team setting, sample size was determined by athlete availability and feasibility rather than by an a priori power analysis. A total of 20 athletes from the Chinese national alpine skiing team were initially screened for eligibility during a centralized training camp. Inclusion criteria were: (1) active membership in the national team; (2) absence of musculoskeletal injury or chronic disease for at least six months prior to the study; (3) ability to participate in high-intensity cycling-based training; and (4) stable dietary habits with no use of performance-enhancing supplements.

Of the 20 athletes screened, two were excluded prior to final analysis due to insufficient training compliance (failure to complete at least 11 of the 12 prescribed intervention sessions). Consequently, 18 athletes met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Following baseline testing, participants were randomly allocated to either the RST group (n = 9) or the SEMT group (n = 9) using a computer-generated random number sequence. The final cohort included athletes specializing in both technical disciplines (slalom [SL], giant slalom [GS]) and speed discipline (super-giant slalom [SG]). This distribution ensured that the investigated training protocols were evaluated across a representative range of competitive demands in alpine skiing.

The RST group consisted of 6 male and 3 female athletes (age: 19.0 ± 1.9 years; height: 172.0 ± 4.5 cm; body mass: 66.8 ± 9.1 kg), while the SEMT group consisted of 6 male and 3 female athletes (age: 20.7 ± 1.5 years; height: 172.2 ± 10.0 cm; body mass: 67.1 ± 12.1 kg). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study was approved by the Beijing Sport University Ethics Review Board for Human Participants (No. 2025104 H) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Control of concurrent training

During the 4-week intervention period, all athletes continued their regular national team training regimen, which included an average of 6 additional indoor sessions per week. To control for this, the concurrent training schedule was planned and implemented identically for both groups under the supervision of the same national team coaching staff. External load was monitored via daily training logs. While individual variations in exertion during technical sessions could not be fully eliminated, the overall volume and intensity distribution were matched between groups.

Warm-up procedures were standardized (10 min cycling at 60%–70% HRmax followed by 2 min passive recovery) for both training and testing sessions. The HRmax was estimated based on HR = 208 − 0.7× age (years old)13.

Performance assessment

All assessments were conducted in the afternoon (starting at 2:00 PM) under consistent environmental conditions.

Countermovement jump test

To conduct the CMJ test, participants were instructed to jump as high as possible. The procedure involved starting from a standing position, performing a downward countermovement to a self-selected depth, followed immediately by a rapid jump in a continuous movement. Participants were instructed to keep their hands on their hips to eliminate any potential influence of arm swing. The jumping actions were performed on a force platform (KWYP-FPW6035, KunWei), allowing for the direct measurement of vertical ground reaction forces. Data were sampled at a frequency of 1000 Hz. Vertical jump height and peak power output were calculated using the KunWei software (version 2.0; KunWei Corporation). Each participant completed two trials of the CMJ, with a 2 min rest between each trial. The highest height of the two trials was used for the final analysis.

90s lateral box jump

The test method is strictly based on the Norwegian Ironman-Alpine Skiing Fitness Test standards14. the subject prepared at the top of the box, the moment when subject jumped down from the top of the box was regarded as the beginning to start counting. The athlete jumped down to one side and back to the top, then down to the other side and back to the top and continued the process until the finish. The height of the box is 51 cm and each jump to the top of the box is counted as one jump. It is not permitted to step onto the box with both feet in sequence, instead, both legs must jump onto the box at the same time. The athlete should be mostly sideways, but some body rotation is allowed. The ultimate result was recorded based on the reps of jumps recorded in 90s.

90s Wingate test

In 1995, Bacharach D.W and other scholars published a controlled experiment to compare the correlation between Wingate30s and Wingate 90s test scores and the final performance of alpine skiing athletes, confirming that Wingate 90s test is more reflective of the anaerobic capacity of alpine skiing than Wingate 30s test15. A Wingate test was performed to assess the subjects’ anaerobic power and capacity using a cycle ergometer (Ergomedic 894 E, Monark Exercise AB, Sweden). The subjects performed a 90s all-out test with a resistance of 7.5% body mass16. Relative peak power (RPP), relative mean power (RMP), and fatigue index (FI) were recorded. RPP was defined as: (power from the highest 15s interval of six segments)/body weight (kg), RMP was defined as: (the sum of power from six 15-s segments)/6/body weight (kg), and FI was defined as [(peak power - lowest power of six segments)/peak power] × 100%.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. Sphericity was verified using Mauchly’s test, if violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. A mixed-design ANOVA (Group × Time) was used to determine the effects of the intervention. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Effect sizes for the ANOVA were reported as partial eta squared (η²), with thresholds defined as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (0.14). [17] For significant interaction effects, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction to control for type I error. Within-group and between-group differences in change scores were further analyzed using Cohen’s d (small: 0.2, medium: 0.5, large: 0.8). [17] 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were calculated for mean differences to aid interpretation. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

All variables met assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed at baseline for any performance variable (all p > 0.05), indicating that the RST and SEMT groups were comparable prior to the intervention. Although inter-individual variability was evident within some measures, baseline differences between groups were not statistically significant; therefore, a mixed-design ANOVA was considered appropriate. The use of ANCOVA was considered but ultimately not applied, as baseline values did not differ significantly between groups and the primary interest was in time × group interactions.

The mixed ANOVA revealed no significant interactions (time×group) between the CMJ (p = 0.109; η2 = 0.288), 90s lateral box jump (p = 0.569; η2 = 0.042), 90s Wingate RPP (p = 0.089; η2 = 0.318), 90s Wingate RMP (p = 0.159; η2 = 0.231). Discussion

This study investigated the effects of two anaerobic HIIT protocols with distinct work–rest structures—Repeated Sprint Training (RST) and Speed Endurance Maintenance Training (SEMT)—on the anaerobic performance of national-level alpine skiers. The main finding was that while both protocols significantly improved sport-specific anaerobic capacity (90s lateral box jump), the specific physiological adaptations diverged according to the work–rest stimuli. Specifically, RST appeared to favor adaptations in explosive power (CMJ and Wingate RPP), whereas SEMT elicited greater improvements in anaerobic endurance (Wingate FI and RMP) during prolonged supramaximal work.

Difference in anaerobic power of RST vs. SEMT

The distinct adaptations observed between RST and SEMT can be directly attributed to their contrasting work–rest structures and the associated metabolic stress imposed during training sessions. The RST protocol (10 s work / 5 s rest) elicited larger effect sizes for CMJ height and Wingate RPP, indicating superior improvements in explosive neuromuscular performance and peak anaerobic power. These outcomes are consistent with the short, maximal efforts and incomplete recovery characteristic of RST, which place a high demand on rapid phosphocreatine (PCr) turnover and repeated peak force production.

In alpine skiing, especially in technical disciplines such as slalom and giant slalom, athletes are required to generate high ground reaction forces within short turn cycles (~ 2–3 s) repeatedly throughout a run. The magnitude of the observed effect sizes (CMJ d = 0.995; RPP d = 1.384) suggests that RST effectively targets this discipline-specific requirement by enhancing the capacity for repeated high-power output under limited recovery conditions.

Gaitanos GC et al. tested the muscle metabolism during intermittent maximal exercise: 6s maximal sprints with 30s of recovery between each sprint on a cycle ergometer with total 10 rounds of sprint, they found that the first 6s sprint was provided by an equal contribution from PCr degradation and anaerobic glycolysis. However, in the tenth sprint there was no change in muscle lactate concentration even though maximal power output was reduced only to 73% of that generated in the first sprint, which revealed power output was mainly derived from PCr degradation and an increased aerobic metabolism during the last sprint18. Further studies identified this conclusion, Girard O et al. identified changes in muscle metabolism by muscle biopsies before and after the RST intervention, they found the percentage of PCr supply was 46% at the 1st sprint, whereas at the 10th the percentage of phosphocreatine supply reached 49%, and even in the presence of a decrease in the total power output, the percentage of phosphocreatine supply increased as the RST repetitions increased, the proportion of PCr supply system involved in the sprint gradually increased19. These findings supported our hypothesis on the potential benefit on anaerobic power of RST, which not only enhances general anaerobic capacity, but improves anaerobic power.

Difference in anaerobic endurance of RST vs. SEMT

In contrast, the SEMT protocol (60 s work / 180 s rest) induced significant improvements in Wingate RMP and FI, outcomes that were not significantly altered in the RST group. The 60s all-out efforts used in SEMT impose a severe demand on the glycolytic system, leading to substantial accumulation of metabolites (e.g., lactate and H+)20. The ability to maintain power output under such acidic conditions is crucial for alpine skiing, particularly in speed disciplines (SG/DH) or the final stages of a race, where blood lactate concentrations can exceed 13 mmol/L8.

The improvement in FI suggests that SEMT enhanced the athletes’ buffering capacity and tolerance to exercise-induced acidosis21. By repeatedly stressing the glycolytic pathway with sustained supramaximal efforts, SEMT likely promoted adaptations in ion regulation22, allowing the athletes to sustain a higher percentage of their peak power for the duration of the 90s test.

Common improvements: neuromuscular economy and learning effects

A notable finding was that both groups achieved large improvements in the 90-s lateral box jump test (+ 9.21% for RST and + 7.52% for SEMT). Unlike the Wingate test, which is a mechanically constrained cycling task, the lateral box jump is a complex, weight-bearing movement requiring coordination, balance, and the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC).

While metabolic conditioning undoubtedly contributed to this result, it is plausible that neuromuscular economy and coordination played a significant role. The 4-week duration of this study is relatively short for profound structural metabolic changes (e.g., mitochondrial biogenesis). Therefore, the substantial gains in the box jump may partly reflect enhanced movement efficiency and “learning effects” related to the high-intensity nature of both interventions23. The athletes likely became more efficient at utilizing the SSC and minimizing energy waste during lateral transitions, a skill directly transferable to on-snow skiing performance.

Intervention duration and adaptation mechanisms

It is important to interpret these results within the context of the 4-week intervention period. Although statistically significant changes were observed, the short duration suggests that the primary mechanisms were likely neural (e.g., motor unit recruitment) and acute metabolic tuning (e.g., enzyme activity, buffering capacity) rather than extensive structural remodeling (e.g., hypertrophy or capillary density)24. This highlights the utility of RST and SEMT as potent “shock” microcycles or tapering strategies to acutely peak specific physical qualities (power vs. endurance) prior to competition, rather than solely as long-term developmental tools.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to applied research in elite sport settings. First, the sample size (n = 18) was small, constrained by the limited number of elite athletes available in the national team. No a priori power analysis was conducted, and the study was likely powered to detect only moderate-to-large effects. Consequently, smaller but potentially meaningful differences between the training protocols may have been missed (Type II error). Second, although concurrent training load (including on-snow technical training and strength sessions) was supervised and planned identically for both groups, the inherent variability in individual exertion during these sessions represents a potential confounding factor that could not be fully isolated. Third, due to the limited number of female participants (n = 6), we were unable to conduct sex-specific analyses. Given known physiological differences in muscle metabolism and fatigue resistance between males and females25,26, future research should aim to investigate sex-specific responses to these HIIT protocols. Besides, the performance assessments were conducted in a fixed order (CMJ followed by lateral box jump, then Wingate) to prioritize the validity of the explosive power measurements. While this standardized the procedure, cumulative fatigue from the 90s box jump test may have influenced performance in the subsequent Wingate test, although this effect would be systematic across both groups and time points.

Practical applications

Based on the distinct adaptations observed in this study, coaches can strategically implement RST and SEMT within the annual training plan for alpine skiers.

Periodization strategy

General Preparation Phase (Off-Season): SEMT is recommended during the early preparatory phase or high-volume training blocks. Its ability to improve glycolytic capacity and fatigue resistance (Wingate FI) makes it ideal for building the “metabolic engine” required to tolerate high training loads and long competition courses.

Pre-competition and competition phase

RST may be more suitable during the tapering or in-season phase. Since RST elicits improvements in explosive power (CMJ, RPP) with lower total glycolytic stress compared to SEMT, it can help maintain neuromuscular sharpness while minimizing residual fatigue before races.

Implementation example

For ski training, Olympic alpine skiers typically train and compete for at most 150 days each year27, which means it is vital for physical fitness training during the off-season. For periodization arrangement, during this period, athletes can perform longer anaerobic endurance training or aerobic training with larger training volume to enhance the fundamental physical fitness, mainly for cardiorespiratory. In the competition period, physical training is governed by the racing schedule and on-snow training. Physical training only accounted for 30% of the total training volume. Due to the high intensity of these protocols, we recommend a frequency of 1–2 sessions per week to allow sufficient recovery. For example, a “Power Block” could consist of 4 weeks of RST (2 sessions/week) leading into the race season, whereas a “Capacity Block” could utilize SEMT during the summer physical conditioning period.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that four weeks of high-intensity interval training significantly improved sport-specific anaerobic performance in national-level alpine skiers, regardless of the specific work–rest structure. However, the specific physiological adaptations appear to be protocol-dependent:

RST (10 s work / 5 s rest) elicited greater improvements in anaerobic power and explosive neuromuscular performance (CMJ, Wingate RPP), making it particularly effective for developing the repeated explosive qualities required in technical skiing disciplines.
