A statistical model to determine the optimal bone mineral density (BMD) screening schedule in liver transplant recipients
Authors: Harish Reddy Koyya, Elijah Meredith, Farid Gharehmohammadi, Mahmoud Elmahdy, Ronnie Sebro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) and the American Society of Transplantation (AST) 2012 practice guidelines based on expert opinion provide surveillance frequency guidelines for dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) bone mineral density (BMD) scans after liver transplant (LT). Since then, post-transplant immunosuppression has changed. This study aims to use statistical models to identify the optimal DXA surveillance frequency after LT.Materials and Methods:This study retrospectively evaluated 402 LT recipients followed for up to 8 years post-LT, each with at least one pre-LT and two post-LT DXA scans. Linear mixed-effects (LME) models using random slopes and intercepts were used to identify whether BMD decline was linear or non-linear (quadratic) in time. Multivariate LME models were used to model the decline in femoral neck (FN), total hip (TH), and L1-L4 BMD decline after LT adjusting for demographic and clinical variables.Results:Males had higher pre-LT BMD than females (P<0.001 at all sites). The rate of BMD loss was fastest at the FN, and faster in patients with normal pre-LT BMD than in patients with low pre-LT BMD (osteopenia/osteoporosis). The model predicted that there would be a significant FN BMD decrease in patients with normal pre-LT BMD after approximately 481 days post-LT (1 year, 3 months).Conclusion:LT recipients with normal pre-LT BMD should have DXA scans ~481 days post-LT. LT recipients with low BMD pre-LT who were more likely to be treated with bisphosphonates did not need annual DXA screening.Keywords: DXA, surveillance, bone mineral density

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) is often the last-resort therapeutic option for patients with end-stage liver disease (ESLD) [1]. The post-LT period is often associated with loss of bone mineral density (BMD) and concomitant increased fracture risk [2,3]. BMD loss is linked to factors like smoking and alcohol use, which are also common in chronic liver disease and LT patients [4,5]. Most patients with ESLD seeking LT already have osteopenia or osteoporosis, and prior history of fractures [6]. Even after transplantation, commonly administered post-LT immunosuppressants such as glucocorticoids are associated with significant BMD loss [7–9]. Therefore, prioritizing the diagnosis and treatment of low BMD is also an essential component of post-LT care.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is currently the gold standard for BMD measurement [10,11]. The World Health Organization (WHO) system for diagnosis and classification of BMD is based on DXA BMD T-scores, which are determined by comparing an individual’s BMD with that of healthy young individuals [12,13]. Patients with their lowest BMD T-scores ≤−2.5 at any bone site are classified as osteoporotic, T-scores between −1 and −2.5 at any bone site are classified as osteopenic, and BMD T-scores ≥−1 at any bone site are considered normal [12,13].

Low BMD (osteopenia/osteoporosis) is associated with increased fracture and mortality risk [14,15]. The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) and the American Society of Transplantation (AST) 2012 practice guidelines based on expert opinion recommend DXA scans every 2–3 years for the first 5 years after LT for patients with normal BMD pre-transplantation, yearly for the first 5 post-LT years for osteopenic patients pre-transplantation, and screening depending on BMD progression and associated factors after the 5-year post-LT period [16].

DXA screening in high-risk patients like LT recipients could enable prompt intervention and reduce long term healthcare costs associated with osteopenia/osteoporosis including fracture treatment [17–20]. Screening too frequently could increase patients’ healthcare costs and utilize valuable medical resources. However, screening patients infrequently could reduce early intervention opportunities, increase fracture risk, and potential healthcare costs. Therefore, the aim of this study is to estimate the rate of BMD loss after LT and to empirically identify the optimal surveillance frequency for BMD DXA scans.

Materials and Methods

This study abides by the principles outlined in the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki and 2018 Declaration of Istanbul for responsible biomedical research. The Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved this retrospective cohort study protocol (approval #23–005115). Waivers exempting signed informed consent were obtained from each study participant. The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) guidelines ensuring the protection of patient health information (PHI) were followed in all data acquisition and analyses.

Patients

This retrospective study involved patients 50 years or older who underwent LTs at the Mayo Clinic between April 2015 and January 2024. Inclusion criteria included: at least 1 scan within one year prior to LT and at least 2 post-transplantation DXA scans. Demographic information such as age and sex, and clinical data including height, weight, and body mass index (BMI) were collected from the pre-transplant DXA scan. Medications including immunosuppressant drugs cyclosporine, tacrolimus, sirolimus, everolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, prednisone, methylprednisolone, bisphosphonate usage, and loop diuretic usage used were collected. Additionally, histories of prior surgeries, organ rejection, and fractures were collected using the EPIC (Verona, Wisconsin, USA) electronic medical record (EMR) system.

DXA scans

BMD values were obtained from DXA scans of the femoral neck (FN), total hip (TH), and L1-L4 lumbar spine. Sites with prior hip arthroplasty, lumbar surgeries, or implanted hardware were excluded as per International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) guidelines [21]. The DXA scanners used were the General Electric (GE) iDXA Lunar DXA (General Electric, Waukesha, Wisconsin, USA) machines. The DXA scanners were maintained by following the manufacturer recommended quality assurance testing and ISCD guidelines. Each patient was scanned using the same DXA machine to minimize BMD changes due to between-machine variation as per ISCD guidelines.

The least significant change (LSC) is described as the smallest statistically significant BMD change and was calculated by multiplying 2.77 by the precision error [22,23]. The hip BMD precision error was 0.012 g/cm2 and the hip BMD LSC was estimated to be 0.033 g/cm2. The anterior-posterior lumbar spine (L1-L4) precision error was 0.010 g/cm2 and the LSC for L1-L4 was estimated to be 0.0277 g/cm2.

Statistics

Patient demographic and clinical statistics are presented using means with standard deviations, or numbers with percentages. Linear mixed-effects models were utilized to identify predictors of BMD change over time. Full statistical details regarding change in BMD with time at each site (FN, TH, and L1-L4) & identification of fixed and random effects are shown in the Appendices B-D.

Statistics were performed using the lme4 package in Rv4.2 (R: The R Project for Statistical Computing (www.r-project.org)). The expected rate of BMD loss and the 95% confidence intervals were created for each site. The time taken to have a clinically significant BMD change at the FN and TH was estimated using the rate of expected BMD loss over time. All statistical tests conducted were two-sided, and P-values <0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

Results

This retrospective study included 402 LT recipients with an average age (±standard deviation) of 61.8 (± 5.8) years. Most patients 271 (67.4%) were male, and 329 (81.8%) White (Table 1). These patients were followed for a median of 1093 days (Interquartile range (IQR) of 731–1605 days) in total, including a range of 116 days to 2943 days (3 months 26 days to 8 years 21 days) after transplant (Table 1). Approximately 102 (25.4%), 184 (45.8%), and 116 (28.9%) of the patients met the WHO criteria for osteoporosis, osteopenia, and normal BMD respectively. There were 193 (48.0%) patients with at least one previous fracture prior to LT. Additionally, 141 (35.1%) patients developed post-LT fractures unrelated to the surgery.

All 402 patients received an immunosuppression regimen which included tacrolimus, mycophenolate, prednisone, and methylprednisolone (Appendix A). Additionally, 16 (4.0%) patients received everolimus, 6 (1.5%) patients received azathioprine, and only 1 (0.2%) patient received sirolimus. After transplant, 109 (27.1%) patients received bisphosphonates (Table 1).

The FN, TH, and L1 BMD rates of change were non-linear (quadratic), while the L2-L4 BMDs were linear (Table 2 and Figures 1 & 2). The univariate linear-mixed effects models with random slopes and intercepts showed that sex and low BMD (osteopenia/osteoporosis) prior to LT were predictors for FN, TH, and L1-L4 (P<0.001; P<0.001 respectively for all) DXA BMD values.

Before transplantation, male LT recipients had higher FN, TH, L1-L4 (all P<0.001) DXA BMD values than females (Table 2). Patients administered everolimus had lower pre-LT FN BMD (P=0.046) and patients given cyclosporin had faster rate of TH BMD loss (P=0.018) (Table 2). As expected, patients with low pre-LT BMD (osteopenia/osteoporosis) had lower BMD at the FN (P<0.001) and TH (P<0.001) immediately after LT than patients with normal pre-LT BMD (Table 2 and Figures 1 & 2). Additionally, patients with low pre-LT BMDs had slower rates of FN (P<0.001) and TH BMD (P<0.001) decline after LT than patients with normal pre-LT BMDs (Table 2 and Figures 1 & 2). Patients given bisphosphonates started with low pre-LT BMD at FN (P<0.001) but had a slower rate of FN BMD decline (P=0.038) (Table 2).

Additionally, patients who had low pre-LT BMD (osteopenia/osteoporosis) had on average a lower L1-L4 (all P<0.001) BMD compared to patients who had normal pre-LT BMD (Table 2). In addition, patients with low pre-LT BMDs had slower rates of L1 (P=0.005), L3 (P=0.042), and L4 (P=0.012) decline than patients with normal pre-LT BMD (Table 2).

The overall rate of BMD loss at any site was fastest at the FN and fastest for patients with normal pre-LT BMDs. Patients with low pre-LT FN BMD had their post-transplant FN BMDs decrease by ~1 LSC or greater on the 891st post-transplant day (~2 years 5 months) but never had such a decrease if administered bisphosphonates. The FN BMD drop ≥1 LSC occurred much earlier in patients with normal pre-LT FN BMD on the 481st post-transplant day. Therefore, this model predicts a significant BMD loss at the FN occurring within 481 days (~1 year 3 months) after LT.

Discussion

In our study, 25.4% of patients had osteoporosis, 45.8% had osteopenia, and 28.8% had normal pre-LT BMD. Also, 71.2% of the LT recipients had low pre-LT BMD. Males had higher pre-LT BMDs than females at all sites. As expected, the recipients with pre-LT osteoporosis or osteopenia had lower FN, TH, and L1-L4 BMDs than patients who had normal pre-LT BMDs. Our study revealed that patients with normal pre-LT BMDs had significantly faster FN, TH, L1, L3, and L4 BMD loss than patients with low pre-LT BMDs. The rate of BMD decrease after LT was fastest at the FN in patients with normal pre-LT BMD with their FN BMDs decreasing by ~1 LSC or greater on the 481st post-LT day. Since the BMD loss ≥1 LSC occurred earliest at the FN, we used results from this site to determine the optimal DXA screening interval.

The rate of BMD decline at the FN was faster in patients with normal pre-LT BMD than in patients who had low pre-LT BMD, FN BMD decreased by 1 LSC or greater ~1 year 1 month earlier in patients who had normal pre-LT BMD than in patients with low pre-LT BMD. Bisphosphonates such as zoledronic acid are now commonly used to treat low BMD in osteoporotic patients [24]. Therefore, treatment with bisphosphonates in osteopenic and osteoporotic recipients could have contributed to their slower rate of BMD loss than the patients with normal pre-LT BMDs.

The liver’s involvement in the vitamin D metabolism pathway plays an indirect yet significant role in bone mineralization [25]. We suspect another possibility is that the extent of liver vitamin D metabolism impairment in LT recipients with low pre-LT BMDs may have been more profound than those with normal pre-LT BMD. Therefore, the transplanted liver could have had an immediate impact in these patients with low pre-LT BMD i.e. protecting against further BMD loss by increasing vitamin D metabolism and bone mineralization. We also speculate that bone homeostatic mechanisms make it easier for patients to lose BMD when their BMD is normal, but as their BMD decreases into the osteoporotic range, it becomes progressively more difficult to lose BMD. This may be an innate feature adjusting bone resorption and bone formation rates to avoid development of potentially life-threatening fractures.

The AASLD and the AST recommend DXA scans every 2–3 years for the first 5 post-LT years for patients with normal BMD, yearly for the first 5 post-surgery years for osteopenic patients, and screening depending on BMD progression and associated factors after the 5-year post-LT period [16]. However, our study provides new evidence contrary to the current guidelines. Our data shows that the rate of post-LT BMD loss in osteopenic/osteoporotic patients, who are often treated with bisphosphonates, is not as profound as it is in patients with normal pre-LT BMD. Yearly DXA screening is probably not required once these patients are treated with bisphosphonates. A DXA scan at 2 years post-LT could be performed to confirm no significant change in BMD in patients with low pre-LT BMD.

Interestingly, BMD loss was fastest in patients who had normal BMD prior to LT. These patients had a significant BMD change within 1 year and 3 months after LT, which is 9 months earlier than the AASLD and AST suggested DXA screening frequency for LT recipients with normal BMD. The rate of BMD loss for patients with normal pre-LT BMD, reached a nadir after ~3 years 10 months and did not significantly change afterwards up until 5 years after LT. A second DXA scan at 4–5 years could be performed to confirm no significant change in BMD in patients with normal pre-LT BMD.

There are a few potential reasons for the discrepancy between expert opinion and our empirical evidence. Immunosuppressants are associated with significant BMD loss [7–9,26,27]. The AASLD and AST guidelines appear to have been developed based on research from prior to 2012 [4, 28]. In the early 2000s, decreases in post-LT graft rejection rates and increased concerns of excessive immunosuppression-associated complications have resulted in a shift of stance on immunosuppression practices. Priorities for post-LT immunosuppression shifted from focusing on graft rejection to focusing on avoiding immunosuppressant-associated side effects [29]. Corticosteroid doses for immunosuppression have decreased over time and tacrolimus-based calcineurin inhibitors use has increased and are the standard therapy for post-LT maintenance immunosuppression [29,30]. Antimetabolite agent use for maintenance immunosuppression has also increased by 77% from 1999 to 2008 [29]. The current standard immunosuppression regimen for LT recipients involves using individualized patient-specific combinations and dosages of calcineurin inhibitors, antimetabolites such as mofetil mycophenolate, and corticosteroids [31]. The patients in our study underwent LT in April 2015 or later, so we suspect that the patients in our study may have had different LT immunosuppression protocols than those patients used to establish the 2012 AASLD and AST DXA BMD screening guidelines.

Despite the concerns of BMD loss in LT recipients, there appears to be limited recent literature exploring this topic. In 2011, Wibaux et al. assessed BMDs and bone disease in 99 patients on an LT waiting list between January 2006 and December 2007, finding that that 73.7% of patients with ESLD awaiting LTs had low BMD (38.4% had osteoporosis and 35.3% had osteopenia) [6]. Their study supports our findings where we found that 71.2% of patients with ESLD had low BMD (25.4% of patients had osteoporosis, 45.8% had osteopenia) before LT. Our study had a significantly larger number of patients (402 patients), was based on more recent BMD data, and included patients over a longer chronological period of LTs (April 2015 to January 2023). A 2021 study by Li et al. following BMD changes in 83 LT recipients found that females have a higher adjusted odds ratio than males for losing BMD after LT. While our multivariable analysis found that males had on average higher pre-LT BMDs than females, there were no differences in the rates of post-LT BMD loss between both sexes [2]. This may be because we adjusted for several demographic and clinical variables, medication use, and pre-LT BMD status (osteoporosis/osteopenia) in our multivariable analysis.

Another study by Guichelaar et al. followed BMDs before and after LT in 360 patients. They found that 82.2% of the LT recipients in their study cohort had significant BMD loss during the first 4 post-LT months, with an average (±SD) BMD loss rate of 15.9% (±18.9%) per year [4]. Their BMD decline rate was probably influenced by the prevailing immunosuppression protocol. Their study included patients from 3 different LT periods i.e. 1985–1989, 1990–1995, and 1996–2000. The immunosuppression protocols prior to 1990 involved the use of cyclosporine A, and tacrolimus was only adopted for immunosuppression use in 1994. A small cohort study of 58 cirrhotic patients in 2001 showed that tacrolimus-treated LT recipients had better FN BMD recovery than those treated with cyclosporine A after LT [9]. Accordingly, our study consisting of patients with LTs after 2015 indicated significant BMD loss ~1 year 3 months post-LT i.e. a much later point than suggested by Guichelaar et al. We believe our study cohort patients are more representative of the current LT recipient population receiving immunosuppression regimens that mostly rely on tacrolimus-based calcineurin inhibitors [31].

There are a few limitations that should be considered in our study. This retrospective study involved data collection from the electronic medical record (EMR) which might not be as reliable as a prospective study. However, we consider our study cohort to represent a typical sample of LT recipients and is the largest dataset of BMD measurements in LT recipients, therefore, we suggest that these findings may be applicable to all LT recipients. The dosage of the post-LT medications such as the immunosuppressants which can influence BMD change rate could not be reliably collected from the EMR. The study was restricted to patients 50 years or older, therefore these results may not generalize to BMD changes in younger LT recipients. Similarly, the other demographics of our LT patient cohort may not be representative of those seen in other LT recipients. Degenerative changes of the lumbar spine and TH can falsely elevate the corresponding BMD measurements and since degenerative changes increase with time, these degenerative changes may change the rate of BMD decrease over time for the lumbar spine and TH [32–34]. The data identified that the rate of BMD decrease at the FN was fastest, possibly because the FN BMDs are less susceptible to false elevation due to degenerative changes unlike the lumbar spine and TH [33]. BMD loss by at least ~1 LSC or greater was observed earliest at FN than at the TH or lumbar spine, which made the rate of FN BMD loss the most clinically relevant site for estimating BMD surveillance frequency. Each LT recipient was followed for between 116 days (3 months) to 2943 days (8 years) after LT, and 20.9% (84) of LT recipients were monitored for 5 or more years (1827 days) post-LT. Therefore, our estimates of BMD were confined to 0 to 1827 days post LT.

Conclusion

Determining the optimal BMD screening time could provide early intervention and treatment opportunities to prevent fractures in LT recipients. Our study suggests that changes in transplant immunosuppression after the 2012 AASLD and AST DXA BMD screening guidelines may have affected the rate of BMD decrease in patients after LT and their subsequent DXA BMD surveillance frequency. Patients with normal pre-LT BMD had the fastest rate of BMD loss, and BMD loss was fastest at the FN, and surveillance every 1 year 3 months may be appropriate in these patients. Patients with low pre-LT BMD who were treated with bisphosphonates did not lose BMD at a rate that justified the need for annual DXA surveillance.
