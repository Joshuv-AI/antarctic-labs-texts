Indian psychiatrist at the crossroads of transgender care: Gatekeeper, advocate, or redundant authority?
Authors: Roshan F. Sutar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ICD-11 marks a conceptual shift by recognizing gender incongruence as not a mental disorder while retaining it within the classification under sexual health to facilitate healthcare access, coding, and insurance reimbursement. Thus, its continued inclusion is primarily an administrative necessity rather than a reflection of psychopathology. Although psychiatrists have increasingly viewed their role as facilitating care rather than policing identity, mandatory psychiatric documentation has often been experienced by transgender individuals as gatekeeping. The 2026 amendments to the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act further complicate this landscape by replacing self-declared gender identity with certification through designated medical boards for legal recognition. This perspective examines the historical, clinical, ethical, and legal dimensions of psychiatric involvement in transgender care within the Indian context. It argues that while psychiatry has an essential role in assessing decision-making capacity, identifying co-occurring mental disorders, managing psychosocial distress, and promoting safe hormone use, it should not function as an authority that validates or invalidates an individual’s gender identity. Recent legislative changes risk reinforcing defensive psychiatric practices and reinstating institutional gatekeeping, potentially delaying access to care and increasing reliance on unsupervised gender-affirming interventions. Drawing parallels with the consultative role of psychiatrists in organ transplantation, we propose a collaborative, multidisciplinary model in which psychiatrists serve as facilitators, advocates, and providers of mental healthcare rather than custodians of identity. Strengthening residency training, developing clear national guidelines, and harmonizing legal and healthcare frameworks are critical to achieving this transition.

INTRODUCTION

The intersection of psychiatry, law, and gender identity in India has reached a critical juncture. Historically, the psychiatric profession has often occupied a position of gatekeeper, evaluating gender diversity through a lens of pathology.[1] However, contemporary shifts, most notably the transition to ICD-11, emphasize that gender incongruence is a condition related to sexual health rather than a mental disorder.[2] Despite this global trend toward de-pathologization, the Indian psychiatrist remains embedded in a complex web of administrative requirements and emerging legal mandates.[3] As we implement the 2026 amendments to the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act,[4] there is an urgent need to redefine the clinician’s role to support autonomy rather than reinforce institutional paternalism.

HISTORICAL ROOTS AND THE RE-DESCRIPTION OF IDENTITY

The historical existence of gender-diverse communities in the Indian subcontinent, such as the kinnar or hijra, dates back, and challenges the notion that gender incongruence is a modern psychiatric construct. Textual evidence from the Mahabharata and Ramayana, as well as institutional roles documented during the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal periods, suggests that gender variance has been a recognized feature of Indian society for over three millennia.[5] These communities functioned within a socio-religious framework that did not conceptualize their identities in medical terms, operating instead under a cis–trans coexistence model.[6] In this context, psychiatric involvement may be viewed not as the discovery of a new clinical phenomenon, but as the retrospective medicalization of a naturally occurring human variation. It is also critical to acknowledge the historical asymmetry in these records: while trans-feminine identities were often culturally visible, trans-masculine experiences were frequently rendered invisible by patriarchal structures, appearing only in specific mythological contexts, such as the figure of Shikhandi.[7] Modern psychiatric models that present gender incongruence as a symmetrical construct across assigned sexes may therefore represent a significant reinterpretation of these historically embedded socio-cultural frameworks.[8]

DIAGNOSTIC SUCCESS VS. ADMINISTRATIVE ACCESS

The transition to ICD-11 represents significant epistemic progress by removing gender identity from the chapter on mental and behavioral disorders. Yet a profound paradox persists. The diagnostic labeling serves as an essential entry point for administrative and insurance systems. In India, frameworks like Ayushman Bharat require a diagnostic code for the reimbursement of gender-affirming interventions. This introduces an ethical ambiguity where the psychiatrist is tasked with labeling normal human variation to enable access to medical services. Inclusion in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) is maintained not to reflect psychopathology, but to facilitate recognition within the health system, risking a subtle form of pathologization where an identity must be coded as a condition to be legitimized.[9]

ANALOGY AND OVERLAP OF GENDER DIVERGENCE WITH NEURODIVERGENCE

A common clinical and theoretical error is the failure to distinguish gender incongruence from neurodevelopmental/neurodivergent conditions such as autism spectrum disorder. While both represent divergent patterns of behavior from normative trajectories, autism is a condition with identifiable functional correlates that often requires intervention for associated comorbidities like attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder.[10] Mental health professionals do not work to convert an autistic person to a normal person, but work on improving their function. In contrast, gender incongruence is a matter of selfhood that does not inherently involve dysfunction or necessitate psychiatric treatment. The distress associated with gender incongruence is frequently contingent on social context and stigma rather than intrinsic pathology. Consequently, it is important to note that by the age of pre-adolescence, gender incongruence is fairly established and, like cis-genders, it may also have comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders. The psychiatrist’s role should focus on exploring identity and screening for co-occurring mental health conditions rather than treating the identity itself as misinterpreted by certain stakeholders in the past, who would indulge in conversion therapy. The Indian Psychiatric Society has publicly made its stance very clear about it on multiple occasions that Gender incongruence is not a disease, and efforts should be made to avoid conversion therapy.[11]

THE 2026 LEGAL LANDSCAPE: THE RETURN OF GATEKEEPING

The legal environment for transgender rights in India is currently in a state of flux following the 2026 amendments. While earlier jurisprudence emphasized self-determination, these recent changes appear to signal a retreat toward medically mediated recognition. The amendments require certification processes involving medical boards and administrative authorities for legal recognition and access to welfare benefits, undermining efforts to say “recommendation letter” from a psychiatrist.[9] This shift effectively reinstates a gatekeeping structure, placing psychiatrists in the position of certifying identity for bureaucratic regulation rather than recommending facilitation. Although these amendments have received Presidential assent, they currently exist in an interim space of legal authority without clear operational procedures, creating significant uncertainty for both clinicians and the transgender community.

THE RISE OF DEFENSIVE PSYCHIATRY

In response to this uncertain legal climate, there is an emerging trend toward defensive psychiatry. Institutions, clinicians, and medical boards could increasingly implement rigid safeguards to mitigate medico-legal risks, given the stringent punitive punishments under recent amendments. The practices that were previously optional must now include consent from nominated representatives, restrict interventions to those aged 18 or older, require spousal affidavits, and implement expansive liability waivers. While intended as institutional protections, these measures were often optional; given the legal requirement, they may become mandatory, reinforcing paternalism and imposing significant hurdles on individuals seeking care. This development highlights the lack of a participatory approach in drafting legislation and the absence of coherent guidelines that balance clinician safety with patient autonomy.[12]

SUPERVISED MEDICATIONS

Unsupervised hormone use, often described as self-medication with gender-affirming therapies, represents a significant yet under-discussed challenge in transgender care. While sometimes framed as “hormonal misuse,” such practices frequently arise not from disregard for medical safety but from systemic barriers, including limited access to trained providers, stigma, and procedural delays associated with mandatory psychiatric evaluation. This phenomenon thus reflects both a clinical risk, and a structural failure of healthcare systems. Psychiatry has a legitimate role in this context, particularly in identifying comorbid mental health conditions, assessing decision-making capacity in complex presentations, and distinguishing stable gender identity from conditions involving impaired reality testing, such as psychosis. However, this role must be carefully bounded; excessive gatekeeping may inadvertently drive individuals toward unsafe, unsupervised interventions. A harm-reduction approach, integrating psychiatric support without restricting access, may offer a more ethically and clinically balanced pathway. Therefore, the role of a psychiatrist is justified as a care provider, team collaborator, and facilitator in transgender care.

A REDEFINED ROLE WITH THE ORGAN TRANSPLANT ANALOGY

To remain relevant without being oppressive, psychiatry must evolve from a discipline of control to one of support and accompaniment. A useful analogy for this transition is organ transplantation. In transplant medicine, psychiatrists are integral members of a multidisciplinary team not because organ failure is a mental illness, but because psychosocial evaluation, capacity assessment, and comorbidity management are essential for optimizing patient outcomes. Similarly, in transgender care, the psychiatrist’s expertise should be used to manage complex comorbidities such as mood disorders, substance use, or psychosis and to assess decision-making capacity without having the authority to validate or invalidate the patient’s identity.[13] Moreover, a strikingly low number of psychiatrists, who train residents are unaware of these processes and are therefore unexposed to such services. It is high time to include these things in psychiatry residency, exams, and exposure to transgender services in India.

CONCLUSION

The current state of transgender care in India reveals the limitations of traditional psychiatric frameworks of teaching, practices, and advocacy. If the profession remains silent or neutral, it risks perpetuating systemic inequities. Psychiatrists are uniquely positioned to act as advocates, using their clinical standing to challenge stigma and bridge the gap between biomedical systems and cultural realities, such as the kinnar tradition. The transition from gatekeeper to collaborator requires a fundamental re-examination of the foundations upon that psychiatric authority is constructed. Ultimately, the goal is to develop a system where psychiatry supports the autonomy of transgender individuals, ensuring that their smooth journey through the medical and legal systems is one of empowerment rather than pathologization.
