Group membership and stereotype content jointly affect neural processing during resource allocation decisions
Authors: Paloma Diaz-Gutierrez, Christophe Boone, Harshil G. Vyas, Carolyn H. Declerck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Both stereotypes and in/outgroup categories affect judgment and decision making, but while the former reflect socially learned generalizations about other people’s characteristics, the latter specify relationships with whom it is safe to affiliate. With neuroimaging data we substantiate these distinct functions and explore how their underlying neural correlates interact to influence behavioral biases. Participants in the MRI scanner are asked to share money with recipients who are stereotyped along a warm/cold dimension and who belong either to a (minimally defined) in- or outgroup. Whole-brain univariate analyses reveal that colder stereotypes become increasingly salient when perceived groups, with heightened activity in amygdala, anterior insula, and ACC. Activation in the latter furthermore predicts decreased tendency to share with cold outgroup recipients, while rLPFC appears to mitigate this. ROI-based multivariate pattern analyses of the data indicate that, during early perceptual processing, only group membership is represented in the amygdala, while during decision making the orbitofrontal cortex represents both groups and stereotypes in such a way that outgroups amplify the dissimilarity between warm/cold traits. Together these results suggest that outgroups are a moderator of stereotypical reasoning with the potential to polarize, but that actual biased behavior depends on the individual’s cognitive control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26265-w.

Introduction

Not everybody is treated equally. When making decisions that affect unknown others, we are influenced by their overt characteristics, such as race, age, nationality, or other stereotypes that reflect collectively shared beliefs about certain social groups, even if they are not always accurate. Stereotypes are organized around two core dimensions, warmth and competence, that justify biased behaviors: those who are seen as warm and competent are considered equals and most “like us,” while those perceived to be cold and incompetent are more likely treated as suspicious, which may lead to reduced compassion, less generosity, and, in extreme cases, violence, exclusion, and dehumanization1–6.

Similarly, categorizing others based on relationships (i.e., whether they belong to the in- or an outgroup) may lead to biased judgments and polarization. However, the functions of stereotyping and in/outgroup categorization differ: while stereotyping simplifies the world by providing broad generalizations and mental shortcuts to facilitate evaluative judgments7, in/outgroup categorization serves to track coalitions and demarcate the boundaries of the social territory in which it is safe (and even desirable) to operate8,9. That in/outgroup categories can be disconnected from stereotypical cues has been made clear by research on the “erasing race” effect. In the first experiment illustrating this8 participants readily encoded other race-faces, but they stopped relying on them as a way to categorize teams when better visual cues (colored T-shirts) became available. The authors concluded that, when alliances change, cognitive biases (stereotypes) based on race disappear as well.

The aim of this study is to uncover underlying neural differences between stereotypical and in/outgroup categorical cues, and to test the hypothesis that outgroups are a moderator that determine how stereotypes will be perceived and acted upon. As the erasing race effect shows, stereotypes are not invariably related to negative consequences. Only when stereotypes are motivationally relevant (when they signal alliance) do they acquire emotional meaning, setting the stage for stereotypical reasoning and derogation1,10. Similarly, only when outgroups are perceived as a potential threat, intimidating, for example, the ingroups’ resources, culture, or ideology, they become polarized into “them” as opposed to “us”11. Polarization makes people more aware of their social identity, causing them to attach more value to the individual traits of those who belong to their ingroup, while relying more on generalized stereotypical labels to define the outgroup12,13. As a result, negative emotions associated with cold stereotypes are more likely to surface when the target belongs to the outgroup, while they may wane in the ingroup.

Already there is a long history of neuroimaging studies in search of the neural processes that can explain why some stereotypes are worse than others. A recent study by Kobayashi et al.14 showed that stereotype representation in the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) predicts actual decision making. The more dissimilar the stereotyped social groups were represented in the OFC (for example, nurses versus surgeons), the more disparate the subsequent money allocation tended to be5. Based on the OFC’s role, both in inference-based15 as well as value-based decision making16,17, the authors propose that the OFC could plausibly serve as a route through which stereotype content informs a decision maker about how subjectively rewarding a particular monetary allocation to a certain recipient is expected to be.

The study of Kobayashi et al.14 did not show any involvement of the brain’s saliency network in stereotype representation, which we would not expect since they used a very broad range of stereotypical cues devoid of relational cues. Research on racial stereotypes, in contrast, has long noted amygdala activation in response to viewing other race pictures18–20. But when participants are instructed to individuate the stereotypical targets (which would be kin to consider them in the same way we would consider ingroup members) amygdala activation is inhibited21 and MPFC activation is sparked22. Similarly, when assigning participants in an experiment to mixed race groups, amygdala activation is no longer observed in response to other-race faces16,23,24. This research, however, treats “race” as both a stereotypical category and as an outgroup, which precludes understanding how they interact. To be able to conclude that stereotypes with negative valence become more motivationally salient in outgroups, the stereotype and in/outgroup categories need to be orthogonal.

We contribute to this research by investigating if stereotype content is processed differently depending on whether the target belongs to the ingroup or a competitive outgroup, whereby group membership cannot be confounded with stereotypes. We focus on the warm/cold dimension because research has shown it overrides the competence dimension when forming impressions of others25. Especially a cold stigma may have the potential to serve as a marker accentuating the differences between “us” and “them”26. A person perceived to be both cold and distant would raise alarm, making the cold/outgroup category particularly prone to be devalued27. This suggests that stereotype content informs judgments and decision making differently based on group membership, setting the stage for a more hostile “us versus them” dynamic when cold traits coincide with an outgroup.

The objective of this research is threefold.

First, and most importantly, we hypothesize (H1) that processing in/outgroups moderates the brain’s response to stereotypical coldness. The amygdala and aInsula are typically increasingly activated when viewing cold and incompetent people (e.g., drug addicts), which are potentially threatening and obviously dissociated from the desirable ingroup4. If the outgroup renders stereotypes more salient, cold stereotypes are expected to be associated with a heightened level of activation in the fear- and salience network when they are perceived in the outgroup compared to the ingroup.

Second, we also hypothesize (H2) that, the more the salience network is activated, the greater the tendency to disadvantage cold outgroups. We further expect this tendency to be reduced through cognitive control exerted by the lateral prefrontal cortex (LPFC). This final hypothesis stems from the prejudice control model28, stipulating that biased behavior towards negatively perceived social groups can be mitigated through the control functions of the LPFC, with input from the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex ACC (dACC). The dACC detects conflict during early perceptual processing and subsequently upregulates the LPFC to take control of decision-making. Increased right (r) LPFC activation is furthermore well-known to play a role in restraining selfishness, facilitating generosity towards strangers29–32, a propensity which would likely be challenged by cold, outgroup recipients.

Third, we aim to replicate previous findings addressing the neural substrates of in/outgroup categorization and stereotyping. Past research has shown that differentiating between traits relies especially on cortical regions such as the MPFC33–35 and the OFC14,36, while differentiating between in/outgroups involves, in addition, the amygdala, and regions of the brain’s salience network37–39. What we contribute to these studies is that we will specifically differentiate between perception during an early processing phase, and a subsequent decision phase. Although we realize that the process from perception to action is continuous, differentiating between in- and outgroups would be particularly important during early processing28. From an evolutionary viewpoint, cues that relate to group membership would be considered very salient in order to stipulate the subsequent appropriate behavior, which is to enhance the ingroup and protect it from outgroup threat26. Thus, our final hypothesis (H3) is that, during the early processing phase, in/outgroup categorization will be associated with a distinct pattern of activation in the fear and salience network, while we expect the effect of stereotypes to become apparent in trait-inference regions later during the decision phase.

We test these three propositions focusing on four regions of interest (ROIs). We include (1) the right (r) amygdala, which has long been studied with regards to fear-processing and responding to outgroup threat40, and (2) the bilateral anterior insula (aInsula), which is a core of the salience network and identified through meta-analysis as the most consistently activated region when perceiving outgroups38,39. We include (3) the ACC because it is also considered part of the salience network, reciprocally connected to and often co-activated with the aInsula, from which it is believed to receive signals that are then selectively intensified prior to cortical processing41. Also the ACC is shown by meta-analysis to be consistently activated during in/outgroup processing38,39,42, and has been associated with reduced empathy for the outgroup27. Finally, we focus on (4) the lateral orbitofrontal cortex (LOFC) because a recent study showed that systematic differences in the pattern of activation in this region drove money-allocation decisions based on the perception of others’ stereotypical traits14. The LOFC has furthermore been shown to respond to stereotypical images of religious extremism43 and to incorporate information about race when making evaluative judgments36.

Participants in our experiment (N = 49, 30 females, 24 years old on average) played modified dictator games, i.e., unilateral money allocation tasks in which it is possible for the participant to gain a financial advantage over the recipient. The fictitious recipients belong to 20 different social categories that varied on the dimension of warmth/coldness. To limit the number of experimental variables, we do not include a competence dimension. The categories spanned different occupations, age groups, religions, and nationalities (see Fig. 1) and were identical to those of Kobayashi et al.14. Prior to playing the games in the MRI scanner, participants were assigned to competing “red” and “green” teams based on their preference for artistic paintings (i.e., following a “minimal group paradigm”44–46). The competition between the teams was incentivized by attributing a monetary prize to all members of the team who came up with the most creative solution to a problem-solving task which was announced at the onset, but completed at the end of the experiment (following the procedures of Cikara & Van Bavel23. In the scanner, participants allocated money to themselves and a recipient who was either on the same- or the competing team. Although the money was fictitious, we asked participants to decide (for the purpose of this experiment) as if the money exchange were real (following the procedures of Kobayashi et al.14). There were three different allocation options, always between an equal monetary split, and one to the advantage of the participant (see Fig. 1 for the course of an experimental trial). The behavioral dependent variable is the participant’s likelihood of choosing the unequal (but self-advantageous) option across the three pay-off schemes.

The independent variables are: (1) the recipient’s group membership (a dichotomous within-subject variable indicating whether the recipient belongs to the in- or out-group); (2) the coldness rating of a specific characteristic of the recipient that corresponds to a stereotypical social category (e.g., the recipient’s occupation, ethnicity, health, age group, or religion). The coldness ratings we use in all analyses are obtained from an independent pilot study in order to ensure that stereotypes (for example, ‘nurse’) keep the same ratings, regardless of whether participants perceive them as in- or outgroup members (see Methods section and Supplementary Information, Table S1).

Results

Behavioral

Manipulation check

To make sure that the in/outgroup manipulation effectively altered participant’s perception of recipients, we asked how close they felt to them in a post-experimental questionnaire using the Inclusion of Other in Self (IOS) scale. The score was significantly higher for recipients in the ingroup (M = 5.16, SD = 1.8) than in the outgroup (M = 2.25, SD = 1.37), Wilcoxon rank test z = 5.788, p < 0.001, two-tailed.

Descriptives

The proportion of unequal decisions was greater in the outgroup (M = 0.63, SD = 0.48) compared to the ingroup (M = 0.35, SD = 0.48). The effect of the stereotype coldness ratings on the proportion of unequal choices is shown in Fig. 2, decomposed for in- and outgroup.

Regression analyses

We estimate the probability of an unequal choice using random-effects logistic regressions on panel data (120 decision per individual, see Table 1). Model 1 shows that both outgroups (coded 1) and coldness ratings significantly increase the likelihood of choosing the unequal option. In model 2 we include the different payoffs as control variables (with option 14/6 omitted), which appear to have a significant effect: increasing the relative advantage to self (20/5) or decreasing the relative disadvantage of the other (15/9) enhances the unequal (self-advantageous) choice. Finally model 3 shows that in/outgroup and coldness have independent effects and that they do not interact. The coefficients and significance levels do not change substantially when adding additional control variables, such as age, gender, IOS, trait-conservatism, and Social Value Orientation, a measure to reflect the individual’s intrinsic preference for equality (see Supplementary Information, Table S2).

To be able to link participant’s behavioral choices to their fMRI data (in order to test H2 later on), we extract the coefficients of the logistic regressions conducted on each individual separately. The measure βoutgroup expresses the extent to which an individual’s sharing decision is influenced by the recipients’ group membership, while βcoldness expresses the influence of the coldness ratings. Interestingly, these 2 coefficients are not correlated (r = − 0.036, p > 0.8), attesting that stereotypes and in/outgroups are separate constructs (H1), each with separate influences on decision making.

Because our interest is to identify which brain regions predict behavior towards in particular cold-outgroup recipients, we conduct further subgroup analyses, estimating coldness-related decisions for each individual separately for ingroup- and for outgroup trials. This gives us two additional measures, βcoldness−IN, (expressing an individual’s tendency to be influenced by the coldness of an ingroup recipient) and βcoldness−OUT (expressing an individual’s tendency to be influenced by the coldness of an outgroup recipient) The frequency distributions of these 4 measures are shown in Supplementary Information, Fig. S1.

In the next sections we report the results of the fMRI analyses. In the first part, we present univariate whole-brain and ROI analyses during early processing and decision making to test H1 and H2. Next, to address H3, we report the results of exploratory multivariate pattern analyses (MVPA) to obtain more fine-grained representations of neural activation in specific brain regions.

Neural activity during early processing

Before testing H1, we check for simple main effects of group membership on neural activation in the whole-brain and compute the contrasts between all the trials in which the recipient is in the outgroup, with all the trials in which the recipient is in the ingroup [OUT > IN] and [IN > OUT]. Neither contrast yields significant results. Similarly, to test the main effect of the cold stereotype content, we parametrically modulate all trials with the independent coldness score, abbreviated as [all trials*Coldness], which reveals no region of significantly increasing activation.

To test for a moderating effect of group belonging as predicted in H1, i.e., regions of the salience network are more activated when processing cold stereotypes in the outgroup compared to the ingroup, we compute the contrast between the parametric coldness modulation of the outgroup- and the parametric modulation of the ingroup trials [OUT*coldness > IN*coldness]. This interaction reveals significant activation in many clusters (see Table 2), including regions related to social cognition and the salience network (ACC, amygdala, aInsula), which supports H1 No clusters survive FWE thresholding in the reverse contrast [IN*Coldness > OUT*Coldness].

When we redo these whole-brain analyses using the dichotomous warm/cold variable instead of the parametric modulation of coldness, we obtain very similar results (see Supplementary Information, Table S3). The contrast [OUT(cold > warm) > IN(cold > warm)] yields significant activation in many clusters, including bilateral aInsula, amygdala and ACC (FWE p < 0.05 corrected). The reverse contrast does not yield anything significant. From these results we derive that, during the early processing stage, perceiving cold outgroup recipients recruits the salience network (ACC, aInsula, amygdala) as well as several cortical regions related to social cognition, more so than perceiving either warm outgroup or ingroup individuals.

Neural activity during late processing: decision phase

We repeat the same whole-brain analyses that we conducted for the early processing phase, at the stage when participants are considering the decision options. We model this decision phase (see Fig. 1) with a 0 duration time to best capture the neural activity at the moment participants are indicating their money allocation choice. Only the parametric modulation of all trials with coldness, [all trials*Coldness], reveals a significant cluster of activation in the right middle frontal gyrus MFC (Supplementary Information, Table S4), corresponding to a cognitive control region implicated in a variety of attention- and executive functions. The peak coordinates [42, 24, 20] correspond to a laterally-located region of the right prefrontal cortex (rLPFC) known to regulate prosocial behaviors32 and integrate inhibitory control processes with value-based decision making29,47. The colder a recipient tends to be perceived, the more this cognitive control region becomes activated, irrespective of group membership.

Predicting behavior

To test if the neural activation in the salience network during the early processing phase predicts reduced sharing especially with cold outgroup recipients (H2, we correlate each individual’s tendency to be influenced by cold stereotype content in the in- or outgroup to the neural activation in each of the 4 ROIs. As a behavioral measure, we retrieve coefficients βcoldness−IN, and βcoldness−OUT from the individual logistic regressions conducted separately for the in- and outgroup (see Behavioral Results and Supplementary Information, Fig. S1). To obtain the neural activation in the ROIs (r amygdala, aInsula, and ACC) we retrieve the BOLD signal at the peak coordinates from the whole-brain contrast [OUT*coldness > IN*coldness] which we cross-checked with independent coordinates as described in the methods, ROI definition (i.e., we made sure that the coordinates we use are also contained within a 10 mm cluster of the independent coordinates). For the LOFC (for which our own data set did not show significant activation, neither during early processing, nor during the decision phase) we use the independent coordinates reported by Kobayashi et al. 2022. All correlations are reported in Supplementary Information, Table S5. Only for the ACC [0, 14, 30] is there a significant correlation with βcoldness−OUT (rho = 0.35, p = 0.048, two-tailed). Thus, conform H2 when a recipient is perceived to be both cold and belonging to an outgroup, activation of a region contained within the ACC is associated with less equal sharing. This is not the case for βcoldness−IN (rho = − 0.02, p = 0.91), which differs significantly from the correlation for βcoldness−OUT (z = 2.13, p = 0.017).

To test that reduced sharing with outgroup participants can be mitigated by cognitive control at the time of decision making, we correlate neural activity in the rLPFC to participants’ tendency to be influenced by coldness in the outgroup. We use the peak coordinates retrieved from the parametric modulation [all trials*Coldness]. For each participant we retrieve the BOLD signal and relate this to the behavioral measure βcoldness−out. Consistent with H2, the coefficient is negative and significant at the one-tailed level (rho = − 0.31, p = 0.042), indicating that lower rLPFC activation corresponds to an increase in the likelihood of a selfish (unequal) choice when sharing with cold outgroup recipients. A one-tailed t test is justified given that we have a directional hypothesis.

To specifically test the prejudice control model28 proposing that the rLPFC upregulates the dACC to prevent biased behavior, we conduct a moderation analysis using a linear regression with βcoldness−OUT as the dependent variable. We include both the Bold signal of the ACC at the time of perception (same coordinates as above) and the Bold signal of the rLPFC at the moment of decision making (same coordinates as above) as independent variables and we compute the coefficient of their interaction. The results are summarized in Supplementary Information, Table S6. Again, we note (in model 1) a significant positive main effect of the ACC (B = 0.30 (0.089), p = 0.002), but the effect of LPFC is not significant. Model 2, which includes the interaction, indicates that the tendency to disadvantage cold outgroup recipients is influenced by the ACC: it increases when rLPFC activation is low (B = 0.31 (0.084), p < 0.001), but reverses as the LPFC becomes increasingly activated. The coefficient of the interaction term is negative and significant (B = − 0.036 (0.016), p < 0.034), indicating that with increasing rLPFC activation, the effect of ACC on the tendency to disadvantage cold outgroup recipients wanes. This is consistent with the inhibitory function of the rLPFC.

While the moderation analysis suggests that the effect of ACC on the decision-making process differs depending on whether rLPFC is activated or not, it does not provide corroboratory evidence that one is recruiting or regulating the other in response to the target’s group membership or coldness. For that purpose, we conducted a psychophysiological interaction (PPI) analysis, using ACC activation at the time of perception as the seed. We test if its effective connectivity with the rLPFC at the time of decision making increases as a function of βcoldness−OUT (the psychological factor). We also test the reverse, using the rLPFC as a seed to assess whether its connectivity with the ACC during perception changes as a function of the psychological factor. Neither a whole-brain, nor an ROI-to-ROI PPI analysis shows any change in the connectivity strength between these two regions (p’s > 0.9). Despite a superficial correlation between the activation pattern of the ACC during the early processing phase on the one hand, and the level of rLPFC at the time of decision making on the other hand (rho = 0.37, p = 0.019, two-tailed), it remains unclear whether these regions are effectively regulating each other.

Exploratory analyses

Although the whole-brain univariate analysis clearly shows that information regarding group membership and stereotypes interact during the early processing stage, we observed no main effects on brain activation. To further investigate if, and how, group- and stereotype categorization are underscored by different neural processes (H3), we further explore the data with multivariate pattern analyses (MVPA), which allows us to detect fine-grained differences in the spatial organization of activated voxels, even if they do not elicit different levels of mass-activation in that region48. MVPA reveals where in the brain, and to what extent, different categories (warm/cold and in/out) are distinguishable. To avoid dealing with 40 social categories (20 stereotypes across two different groups), we dichotomize the coldness scores to distinguish between a “cold” stereotype content (with negative values on the standardized coldness score) and a “warm” content (positive values). We repeat all the univariate whole-brain and ROI analyses, in both the early processing and decision phase, with classification (decoding) and representational similarity analysis (RSA) and refer to the Supplementary Information: Multivariate analyses and Tables S7-S8 for a full description of the methods and results. Here we highlight only the three significant findings.

First, during the early processing stage, RSA conducted on the 4 ROIs (using the same independent coordinates as described earlier for the univariate analyses) implicates the r amygdala as a site where groups, but not stereotypes, are represented. This result, which partly corroborates H3 is derived from a comparison of the mean neural representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM) to three theoretical RDMs (see in Supplementary Information, Figure S2): (1) a group model, in which only in/outgroups are encoded as distinguishable categories, (2) a coldness model, in which only warm/cold stereotypes are encoded as distinguishable categories, and (3) an interaction model, in which both warm/cold and in/out are encoded, and where in/warm (the prototypical “us”) is somewhat distinguishable from the categories that differ on one dimension (in/cold and out/warm), but most distinct from the category that differs on both dimensions (out/cold). Only for the group model is the correlation with the neural RDM significantly greater than zero (Wilcoxon z = 2.02, p = 0.043, two-tailed), while the median correlation with the theoretical coldness model is insignificant and in the opposite direction (Wilcoxon z = − 1.04, p = 0.3, two-tailed).

Second, during the decision phase, RSA conducted on the LOFC indicates that cold stereotypes are represented in the LOFC in such a way that they are made more salient (more dissimilar from “warm”) by an outgroup, which is consistent with both H3 and H2. Accordingly, we find that only the correlation between the neural RDM and the theoretical interaction model (described above) is significantly greater than 0 (Wilcoxon sign rank, z = 2.21, p = 0.027, two-tailed).

Third, and consistent with prior literature proposing that in/outgroups and stereotypes are separate constructs, a whole-brain classification analysis of the decision phase shows that the difference between warm vs. cold stereotypes is decoded in the MPFC, while the difference between in- vs. outgroups is decoded in several large clusters located more dorsally (DMPFC), as well as in distributed clusters of temporal and occipital cortices. A conjunction analysis furthermore indicated no overlap between the DMPFC representing group membership and the single MPFC cluster representing stereotypes (see Fig. 3 and Supplementary Information, Table S7).

Discussion

Are stereotypes more salient when they concern “cold” people who do not belong to our ingroup? We set out to answer this question with an experiment in which cold stereotype content and in/outgroups are orthogonal and distinguish brain responses during a perceptual (early processing) and decision phase.

A first remarkable result is that during early processing, far more regions of the brain become activated as the perceived coldness of an outgroup target increases, compared to when these same cold targets are perceived in the ingroup. The whole-brain contrast [OUT*coldness]> [IN*coldness] yielded significant activation in the three hypothesized regions of the fear- and salience network: amygdala, aInsula, and ACC49. Together these findings corroborate the engagement of the salience network during early processing and suggest that cold stereotypes become motivationally more relevant when they are perceived in an outgroup (H1).

While amygdala involvement has previously been observed in many experimental paradigms contrasting in/outgroups, the direction of activation has been inconsistent and interpreted in various ways, ranging from novelty-processing50, unfamiliarity19–21, culturally acquired negative attitudes51, and salience16. In an attempt to consolidate these disparate interpretations, Chekroud et al.40 proposed that the amygdala signals threat, which fits well with the notion that outgroups are perceived to be more intimidating when their members are believed to have cold traits, or vice versa, that cold stereotypes pose a threat only when in the outgroup (see also28,52,53. Interestingly, the amygdala response occurs in concert with heightened activity in the aInsula and ACC. This co-activation has been proposed to indicate information transfer from limbic regions where gut feelings are registered, to prefrontal cortices that drive adaptive behavioral adjustments54,55.

We also tested if increased activity in salience-processing regions (in response to the coldness of outgroup recipients) would influence choice behavior (H2). Consistently, the data show that in a region of the ACC, the BOLD signal correlates positively and significantly with the tendency to choose the unequal (selfish) option more frequently as the perceived coldness of outgroup recipients increases. Turning to the decision phase, we note prefrontal cortex engagement in the whole-brain parametric modulation of coldness. In particular, activation in the rLPFC may be indicative of enhanced cognitive control to resist decisions that are driven by categorizing cold stereotypes, possibly explaining the observed discrepancy between the significant group*stereotype interaction at the neural level during the perceptual phase, and its absence in the behavioral results (Fig. 2).

An additional moderation analysis furthermore indicates a role of the rLPFC in attenuating the correlation between ACC activation at the time of perception and subsequent choice behavior: when rLPFC activation is elevated at the time of decision making, the earlier injurious influence of the ACC on sharing with cold outgroup recipients disappears. However, a PPI analysis could not corroborate that the observed rLPFC activation is effectively coupled to the ACC activation associated with cold outgroup recipients. Hence these data do not offer conclusive evidence with respect to the idea that it is the ACC itself that recruits the rLPFC in order to control prejudicial tendencies28. It is still possible that, irrespective of the ACC, rLPFC activity begins to increase earlier during the processing phase, exerting top-down regulatory control in a more gradual manner throughout the entire process from stimulus presentation to decision making. Our statistical analyses may not have captured this, as we modeled the regressors for the early processing phase using the full interval duration, whereas those for the decision phase were modeled as zero-duration events (see Methods section). A consequence of this modeling approach is that we may have missed transient rLPFC activation that spans or overlaps both phases.

Finally, exploratory MVPA offers additional support for the proposed hypotheses. At the level of the whole-brain, differentiating the impact of in/outgroups and cold/warm stereotypes at the time of decision making occurs in non-overlapping cortical regions, which corroborates that they are separate constructs. Turning to the ROIs, RSA reveals early amygdala involvement in capturing the difference between in/out groups, but not warm/cold stereotypes. At the time of decision making, however, groups become represented in the LOFC where they exacerbate the dissimilarity between warm and cold stereotypes. In other words, if warm/in comprises the reference group (the recipients who are most like us), then the cold/out group is represented as the most dissimilar.

This study, like much experimental research on behavior conducted in artificial laboratory settings, has a number of limitations which call for caution when generalizing from these results. First, while we belief participants took the task seriously, the hypothetical nature of the allocation task may have changed participants’ incentives to share compared to what they would do in real-life interactions. Similarly, describing the recipients with words, rather than facing them directly, may have suppressed emotions. Because we instructed participants to deliberate how they would decide in the scenarios we gave them, we may have inadvertently triggered more cognition at the expense of impulsive emotions, explaining in part the overrepresentation of cortical relative to subcortical regions in our statistical maps.

Second, designing experiments requires making choices. While we verbally spelled out the 20 stereotypes, we chose to display group membership with the actual pictures (paintings) that defined the groups. We reasoned that groups often use logos or visual cues to accentuate feelings of inclusion, and that the terms IN/OUT may not have been sufficiently strong to evoke a sense of group membership. This difference in presenting the experimental stimuli, however, may have inadvertently evoked activity in observed brain regions for reasons other than the functions we postulate. For example, the results of the whole-brain MVPA during the perceptual phase show more involvement of visual cortices for decoding groups, consistent with the pictorial representation of in/outgroups. Along these lines, the increased ACC activation during the early processing phase could possibly be attributed to a “mismatch” in the presentation of words vs. pictures. We think this is unlikely, since we only observe increased ACC activation in response to cold outgroup recipients, and not in any other condition. This suggests that it is specific to the manipulation we intended.

While earlier we attributed the lack of a brain-behavior correlation to the observed increase in cognitive control during the decision-making stage, we realize that it may also stem from low statistical power (as indicated by the low β-values of the coefficients in the individual logistic regressions) and high variability among individuals. People differ in how much recipient-related information influences their preference for equal distributions. A promising direction for future research would be to explore whether there are specific neural signatures associated with distinct typologies, defined in advance based on their distaste for outgroups (high β-outgroup) and/or tendency for stereotypical reasoning (high β-coldness). Focusing on specific typologies which we suspect a priori to be either very prone or resistant to prejudicial biases could also increase the likelihood of finding statistical differences in effective neural connectivity changes. While the current data did not show the postulated ACC-rLPFC effective connectivity predicted by the prejudice control model, a regression analysis estimating the tendency to disadvantage cold outgroups suggests that the impact of ACC during early processing is tempered when rLPFC is high. Future research should attempt to replicate this finding taking into account individual differences. A testable follow-up hypothesis is that ACC connectivity serves as a neural switch turning some stereotype-prone individuals into hard-core parochials when interacting with outgroups. Based on reports that the ACC is involved in linking salient information to goal-directed behavior56, we would further speculate that strong ACC-MPFC connectivity might drive selfish decisions that hurt cold outgroup recipients, whereas strong ACC-LPFC connectivity could suppress such tendencies. Already a meta-analysis linked the dACC to monitoring outgroups, and regulating the tensions they may raise42.

In conclusion, the current findings offer two important insights for reducing stereotypical biases. If stereotypes are cognitions shaped by learning processes, their impact can be reduced by greater group inclusion. Stereotypical coldness in particular tends to become less salient and trigger weaker emotional responses when it is perceived within an ingroup. In contrast, biases against outgroups are more likely rooted in our evolutionary history. With widespread cortical and subcortical (amygdala) representation, group-related biases may be more deeply anchored in the brain and harder to eliminate, even though groups themselves can be transient. To prevent fearful emotions from driving prejudicial responses, situations where perceptions of coldness align with an outgroup are better avoided.

Second, the results suggest that generosity decreases when individuals lack the cognitive control to regulate emotions elicited by cold, outgroup members. This underscores the importance of developing a cognitive reasoning style (targeted at the LPFC) to counter impulsive emotional reactions. Raising awareness of how stereotypical labels can intensify an in/outgroup divide may encourage thoughtful reflection, helping people distinguish between natural ingroup favoritism and extreme parochialism and prejudice driven by unchecked emotions.

Methods

Pilot studies

Prior to the fMRI study, three online pilots were conducted to test the operationality of the dependent variable and the group- and stereotype manipulations (see Supplementary Methods and Figures S3-S7 for details and results). In the first pilot (N = 42), the minimal group manipulation by itself did not impact behavioral choices. Thus a second pilot (N = 67) was conducted and incentivized with competition between groups, which is the design we then implemented in the current study. The third pilot (N = 59) was conducted to obtain independent warmth/coldness ratings for the 20 social categories after translation into Dutch. Following Kobayashi et al.14 we use these independent ratings to analyze the data in this study. The ratings of the Dutch pilot correlate highly with pilot 1, (r = 0.87), with pilot 2, (r = 0.92), with ratings obtained from the study by Jenkins et al.5 (r = 0.82) and with the ratings obtained in the current fMRI study (r = 0.78 using a 9 item scale; r = 0.95 using a one item scale).

Participants

Participants (Flemish university students and employees) were recruited via e-mail invitations, social media, and flyers. The study was explained as “imaging the brain process involved when people make decisions that affect both themselves and other people in a diverse and competitive social context.” Participation was voluntary but incentivized with a 40 euro participation fee. Interested individuals were referred to a website with a screening questionnaire to make sure they were right-handed and met all MRI inclusion criteria, after which eligible participants were asked to fill in a pre-experimental questionnaire querying their demographics and some personal traits. None of the participants belonged to any of the social categories included in the experimental task. Each participant was contacted individually to schedule an MRI session at the University Hospital. While 49 participated in the experiment (30 females, 24 years old on average) 9 participants were removed from the dataset due to excessive movement prior to fMRI data analysis, leaving N = 40. All procedures were conform the Declaration of Helsinki ethical principles and approved by the Committee of Medical Ethics of the Antwerp University Hospital (UZA).

Procedure

We follow the procedures of a similar fMRI experiment investigating the effect of stereotypes on fairness decisions14, to which we add the competing out-group factor. The latter is patterned after Cikara & Van Bavel23.

Upon arrival for the experiment, participants signed an informed consent and were assigned to a red or green team based on their preferences for paintings. They were told that the two teams were competing in a creativity task which would be explained to them after the fMRI session (see Supplementary Methods). At the conclusion of the study, each member of the winning team would receive a 5 euro bonus.

After the group assignment, participants were instructed that they would make decisions in the scanner that affected themselves and others in their own or the other team. They first conducted a practice trial of the money allocation task on a laptop, followed by 120 trials in the scanner, divided in 6 scanning runs. Each trial followed the same steps as delineated in Fig. 1. Total scanning time was 40 min.

Stimuli were presented with PsychoPy57, on a screen behind the scanner, visible to participants through a set of mirrors located on the radiofrequency coil. Participants responded with MRI-compatible button boxes (left or right index) held in each hand.

After scanning, participants completed the creativity task in which they had to list as many uses for a brick as they could imagine (see Supplementary Methods). They also completed the Inclusion of Other in Self (IOS) Scale58. Last, they were shown a list of attributes related to the dimension warm/cold and asked to indicate on a scale from 1 to 7 to what extent they believed a particular attribute described each of the 20 social categories. They were also asked to simply rate the coldness of each participant to obtain a one item score (see Supplementary Information, Table S1).

Image acquisition and processing

Data were collected on a 3T Magnetom Trio MRI scanner located at UZA in Antwerpen, Belgium. T1 weighted anatomical images were acquired with a magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MP-RAGE) sequence (TR = 2100 ms, TE = 2.94 ms, flip angle = 9°, voxel size = 1 mm3). Functional images were obtained with a T2* echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence (TR = 1780 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 66°, slice thickness = 2.5 mm, voxel size = 2.5 mm3, 50 slices with simultaneous Multi-Slice acquisition). The sequence was divided into 6 runs, consisting of 220 volumes each. MRI images were pre-processed and analyzed with SPM12 software (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12). Volumes were realigned and unwrapped (to correct for movement artefacts) and slice-time corrected. Realigned functional images were co-registered with T1, after which they were normalized to MNI space using the parameters from the segmentation of the anatomical image (2.5 mm3 voxels, 4th degree B-spline interpolation). Images were smoothed using an 8 mm Gaussian kernel. Images for decoding analyses were non-normalized and unsmoothed. Low-frequency artefacts were removed using a 128 high-pass filter.

GLM estimation

For the univariate analyses, we first ran two GLMs to estimate the trial-by-trial modulatory effect of coldness, one during the processing phase, the other at the time of decision making. In both models we included a regressor for each of the two phases (processing, decision), and a parametric modulator with the coldness ratings of each trial’s social category. Second, to test for an interaction between stereotypes and in/outgroups, we ran two additional GLMs similar to the ones above, including regressors for each group (processing-IN, processing-OUT, decision-IN, decision-OUT) plus two parametric modulators with the coldness ratings, one for ingroup trials and the second for outgroup trials.

For the classification analyses we created two new GLMs after dichotomizing the coldness variable into “cold” and “warm” trials based on a median split. The GLM for the processing phase included regressors for the four conditions: processing-IN/WARM, processing-IN/COLD, processing-OUT/WARM and processing OUT/COLD, and an additional regressor for the decision. The GLM for the decision included a regressor for the perception phase, and again one regressor for each condition: decision-IN/WARM, decision-IN/COLD, decision-OUT/WARM and decision-OUT/COLD.

In all models, all regressors were convolved with the hemodynamic response function (HRF). All models also included a regressor for trial errors (if participants missed to provide a response) and six motion regressors. The regressors for the processing phase and errors were modelled with their entire duration, while the regressors for the decision phase with zero duration. The latter was chosen because we were interested in estimating neural signals at the moment a decision was made, which is common practice in fMRI research59.

For the MVPA analyses, we employed TDT-The Decoding Toolbox v.3.999G (https://sites.google.com/site/tdtdecodingtoolbox/)60. We carried out two classification analyses at a whole-brain level, one distinguishing between IN vs. OUTgroup recipients, and one differentiating between WARM vs. COLD recipients. We used non-normalized and unsmoothed images from both the processing and decision phase and used a searchlight approach with linear support vector machine classifiers61 (C1, trained and tested using a “leave-one-out cross-validation scheme” (i.e., classifiers were trained with data from 5 runs and tested in the remaining run, in an iterative fashion).

To conduct the ROI-based RSA we estimated neural representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) using Euclidean distance between pairs of conditions. These neural RDMs were Spearman-correlated with 3 different model RDMs that captured the expected dissimilarity (represented with values 0–1) between pairs of trials of the different conditions (IN-WARM, IN-COLD, OUT-WARM, OUT-COLD). To avoid inflated positive results62, we included only the lower triangle of the matrices (excluding the diagonal). The z-transformed Spearman correlations between both RDMs were averaged across all voxels in the native-space masks. The average correlation coefficients (for each participant, one per ROI and model) were then assessed for significance (> 0) at the group level with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Because group membership was based on trivial characteristics and assigned extraneously, we do not expect it to correlate with stereotype content. Therefore we do not partial out the effect that one model would have on the other when we compute the correlations63.

Independent coordinates for these ROIs were retrieved from previous publications following a functional approach, i.e., we retrieved coordinates from studies that already demonstrated a clear functional relation between a the a priori defined ROI and biased perception due to stereotypes and/or group membership.
