Nomogram for predicting acute kidney injury after primary PCI in STEMI patients: development and validation study
Authors: Ximin Li, Juzhen Yan, Yingji Chen, Xiaming Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify the risk factors for acute kidney injury (AKI) after primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and to develop and evaluate a nomogram predictive model.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on STEMI patients who underwent primary PCI in our hospital between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024. Patients were divided into AKI and non-AKI groups. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify the independent risk factors for post-PCI AKI. Based on these factors, a nomogram model was constructed and its predictive accuracy, calibration, and clinical utility were assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsA total of 227 STEMI patients were enrolled, of whom 41 (18.1%) developed AKI after PCI. NSAIDs use, female sex, blood glucose, serum creatinine, and serum uric acid emerged as independent risk factors. The ROC analysis showed that the nomogram model (AUC: 0.793) had superior predictive performance compared to the Mehran score (AUC: 0.626). Calibration plots showed strong alignment between predicted and observed outcomes, while DCA indicated a substantial net benefit. Moreover, patients in the high-risk group had significantly longer lengths of stays than those in the low- and moderate-risk groups.ConclusionThe nomogram model exhibits robust predictive accuracy and clinical utility, providing valuable support for the early identification of high-risk STEMI patients for AKI after primary PCI and facilitating the implementation of stratified preventive strategies.

Introduction

ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is the most severe subtype of acute coronary syndrome, characterized by acute and complete occlusion of a coronary artery, resulting in sustained myocardial ischemia and necrosis [1]. Compared with non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction and unstable angina, STEMI involves a larger area of myocardial injury and carries a higher short-term mortality [2,3]. Although the incidence of STEMI has declined in many developed countries thanks to advances in standardized prevention and management strategies, it remains a significant challenge in developing nations [4–8]. In China, due to the uneven distribution of economic development and healthcare resources, the overall incidence and mortality of STEMI remain high [9].

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the gold standard for reperfusion therapy in STEMI patients, offering great reductions in mortality. However, patients may experience adverse outcomes even after successful PCI [10]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is one of the most common complications, with reported incidence rates ranging widely from 5% to 30% [11–14], and exceeding 30% in high-risk groups such as those with chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes, or advanced age [14,15]. The occurrence of AKI substantially increases hospital length of stay, mortality, the long-term risk of cardiovascular events and chronic kidney dysfunction, particularly those with cardiogenic shock [16,17].

The prevention and early management of AKI after PCI face several dilemmas. First, serum creatinine (SCr) exhibits a lagging nature in reflecting renal dysfunction, while traditional diagnostic methods rely on the measurement of SCr and urine output over a period of time, which may postpone timely intervention [18]. Second, current preventive strategies, such as hydration protocols and avoidance of nephrotoxic agents, lack specificity among patients and have limited effectiveness [19]. Therefore, early prediction of AKI risk and personalized preventive strategies are crucial for improving patient outcomes.

Nomograms provide a simplified method for visualizing complex statistical models, enhancing their applicability in clinical practice. In recent years, they have been widely utilized for the development of clinical prediction models [20,21]. In this study, we collected clinical data from STEMI patients who underwent primary PCI and constructed a nomogram model, which was evaluated for its predictive performance. The model aimed to provide clinicians with a practical tool for early identification and targeted prevention of AKI.

Materials and methods

Study population

A retrospective analysis was performed on patients who presented to the Emergency Department of the Affiliated Hospital of Hangzhou Normal University between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of STEMI; (2) underwent primary PCI within the recommended time window; (3) age greater than 18 years. Exclusion criteria included: (1) active infection at admission (temperature >38 °C or requiring antibiotic therapy) or systemic autoimmune disease on immunosuppressive treatment; (2) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <15 mL/min/1.73 m2 or requiring renal replacement therapy; (3) diagnosis or treatment of solid or hematologic malignancy within the past 5 years; (4) hospitalization for less than 48 h or death within 48 h of admission; (5) missing any key clinical data, including demographics, medical history, medication usage, pre-PCI laboratory results, intraoperative details, or post-PCI SCr.

The diagnosis of STEMI was based on the 2018 Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction by the European Society of Cardiology and the American Heart Association [22]. PCI procedures were performed by experienced cardiologists in line with standard clinical practice, with a first medical contact-to-device time within 90 min. Upon the first electrocardiogram indicating STEMI, all patients received pre-procedural loading therapy consisting of 300 mg of aspirin, 40 mg of atorvastatin calcium, and either 300 mg of clopidogrel hydrogen sulfate or 180 mg of ticagrelor. An iso-osmolar, nonionic contrast agent – iodixanol, with an osmolality of 290 mOsm/kg·H2O – was utilized.

Study endpoints

Patients were categorized into AKI and non-AKI groups based on whether they developed AKI after PCI. AKI was defined according to the 2012 KDIGO guidelines [23] as meeting any of the following criteria: (1) an increase in SCr of ≥26.5 μmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) within 48 h; (2) an increase in SCr to at least 1.5 times the baseline value within 7 days; (3) a urine output of <0.5 mL/(kg·h) lasting for more than 6 h.

Data collection

For each patient, clinical data including demographics, medical history, medication usage, pre-PCI laboratory results, and intraoperative details were collected. Demographics included sex, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, blood pressure and oxygen saturation at admission. Medical history recorded the presence of hypertension, diabetes, fatty liver, and congestive heart failure (CHF). Medication usage comprised the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ACEi/ARB), and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs). Laboratory parameters included 18 indicators, such as blood glucose (GLU), white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin, c-reactive protein (CRP), creatine kinase (CK), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum uric acid (SUA) and SCr. Intraoperative details included duration of surgery and contrast dosage. Hospital length of stay and ICU admission status were recorded as prognostic indicators.

Each patient’s Mehran risk score was calculated based on clinical characteristics: persistent hypotension (5 points), intra-aortic balloon pump use (5 points), CHF (5 points), age >75 years (4 points), anemia (3 points), diabetes (3 points), contrast volume (1 point per 100 mL), and eGFR (40–60 mL/min/1.73 m2 = 2 points; 20–40 mL/min/1.73 m2 = 4 points; <20 mL/min/1.73 m2 = 6 points) [24]. The Mehran score was compared with the nomogram model for predictive performance.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and model development were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and R software version 4.4.2. Initially, univariate analysis was performed for all variables. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent sample t-test, whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were presented as percentages, with intergroup differences assessed using the χ2 test. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for the development of AKI after PCI in STEMI patients. A nomogram model was developed based on the logistic regression results and internally validated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. The predictive performance of the nomogram was compared against the Mehran risk score using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) were generated to evaluate the model’s predictive accuracy and clinical utility. Additionally, an interactive online version of the nomogram was developed using the ‘DynNom’ package in R and published via Shinyapps.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 227 STEMI patients were included in this study, comprising 187 males and 40 females (Figure 1). Among them, 41 patients (18.1%) developed AKI. Age distribution was 11.0% (18–39 years), 49.3% (40–59 years), 32.6% (60–79 years), and 6.6% (≥80 years). Patients with hypertension and diabetes accounted for 55.5% and 32.6%, respectively.

Multivariate logistic regression

The results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that female sex, GLU, SCr, SUA, and NSAIDs use were independent risk factors for the development of AKI after PCI in STEMI patients. Detailed results are presented in Table 2.

Construction and evaluation of the nomogram

A nomogram was constructed based on five independent risk factors: female sex, GLU, SCr, SUA, and NSAIDs use (Figure 2). For each patient, project their value for all risk factors vertically onto the ‘Points’ axis to read off the scores. The component scores are then summed to obtain a ‘Total Points’ value, which is projected down to the ‘Risk of AKI’ axis to read the patient’s predicted probability. An interactive online version was also developed (Figure 3), and the access link and QR code are provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

The Hosmer–Lemeshow test yielded a χ2 value of 5.825 (p = 0.667), suggesting a good fit for the model. The ROC curve analysis revealed that the nomogram demonstrated superior predictive performance (AUC: 0.793, 95% CI: 0.705–0.881) compared to the Mehran score (AUC: 0.626, 95% CI: 0.533–0.719) (Figure 4). DeLong’s test confirmed a significant difference between the two models (Z = −2.843, p = 0.004).

ROC curves of the nomogram model and Mehran score.

The optimal cutoff value was determined to be 0.228 based on the Youden index, yielding a sensitivity of 0.707 and a specificity of 0.855. Internal validation with 1000 bootstrap resamples produced a mean AUC of 0.791 (95% CI: 0.699–0.872), a mean sensitivity of 0.703 (95% CI: 0.556–0.833), and a mean specificity of 0.854 (95% CI: 0.798–0.901). The calibration intercept and slope were 0.000 (95% CI: −0.569 to 0.598) and 1.000 (95% CI: 0.679 to 1.362), indicating a high level of agreement between the predicted and actual probabilities (Figure 5(A)). And the clinical decision curve revealed that the model consistently provided positive net benefits across a wide range of threshold values (Figure 5(B)). In conclusion, this model offers a reliable basis for clinical decision-making.

Subgroup analysis by age and sex

Patients were stratified by age and sex for subgroup analysis. The nomogram demonstrated good predictive performance across all subgroups, with AUCs of 0.777 (95% CI: 0.659–0.895) in patients aged <65 years and 0.785 (95% CI: 0.643–0.927) in those aged ≥65 years, as well as 0.783 (95% CI: 0.676–0.890) in males and 0.778 (95% CI: 0.587–0.969) in females. DeLong tests indicated no significant differences between subgroups (age: Z = −0.089, p = 0.929; sex: Z = 0.047, p = 0.963), as shown in Figure 6.

ROC curves of the nomogram model for predicting AKI in subgroups: (A) age groups; (B) sex groups.

Prognostic prediction of the nomogram

Patients were stratified into three risk groups based on their nomogram-derived risk scores: low-risk (≤50), moderate-risk (50 < score ≤90), and high-risk (>90). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed a significant difference in length of stay among the groups (F = 3.818, p = 0.023). LSD analysis demonstrated that patients in the high-risk group had significantly longer length of stay compared to those in both the low-risk (p < 0.05) and moderate-risk groups (p < 0.05). Moreover, the proportion of patients requiring ICU admission increased with higher risk levels, although this trend did not reach statistical significance (Table 3, Figure 7).

Discussion

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at a tertiary grade A hospital in an eastern Chinese city. Compared with national large-scale data, our cohort had a younger mean age (56.76 vs. 63.37 years), with a higher proportion of males (82.4% vs. 68.4–71.2%), and greater prevalence of smoking and diabetes (55.21% vs. 34.7%; 32.6% vs. 11.6%), but a lower prevalence of hypertension (55.5% vs. 62.8%) [25,26]. In this study, the incidence of AKI was 18.1%, higher than that reported in some previous studies [11,13]. This may be attributed to the unique characteristics of emergency patients, such as critical illness and limited time for preprocedural hydration. Evidence shows that the incidence of AKI after primary PCI is 2.8 times that of elective PCI, with worse outcomes [27].

Epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated a marked male predominance among STEMI patients [28]. In recent years, the male-to-female ratio in hospitalized STEMI cases has increased across various countries [29], particularly among younger individuals. For instance, a Canadian study reported that 87.3% of STEMI cases in patients aged 18–45 years were male [2]. Women tend to experience their first STEMI at an older age – on average 8–10 years later than men – and are more likely to present with comorbidities [28,30,31]. These factors may contribute to the relatively poorer prognosis observed in female patients [30,32]. Additionally, evidence from multiple countries indicates that women are less frequently treated with interventional procedures [29,33], possibly due to atypical symptom presentation leading to misdiagnosis or delayed recognition, thereby prolonging myocardial ischemia and hypoperfusion [34,35].

These factors may also explain why female sex emerged as an independent risk factor for AKI in our study. Women may be more vulnerable to AKI due to physiological and anatomical differences, including lower body weight and smaller arterial diameter compared to men [36–38]. However, some studies have found no significant association between sex and AKI after adjusting for confounding factors, suggesting that AKI risk may be more closely related to treatment timing and comorbidity management rather than sex itself [36,39].

NSAIDs use emerged as the most significant risk factor in this study. In STEMI patients, reduced cardiac output, vomiting, and diuretic-induced hypovolemia amplify the nephrotoxic effects of NSAIDs. Studies have shown that higher cumulative doses, parenteral formulations, concomitant use of multiple NSAIDs, and combination with renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors or diuretics further increase the risk of AKI [40]. Moreover, metabolic competition between NSAIDs, antiplatelet agents, and statins may impose additional stress on renal function [41]. Therefore, in STEMI patients after PCI, the analgesic benefits of NSAIDs should be carefully balanced against their risks, particularly in hypovolemic patients, those requiring high doses, or those on multiple medications.

Diabetic individuals experience significantly higher rates of AKI and long-term mortality compared to non-diabetic patients [42]. Despite this, recent studies indicate that hyperglycemia, regardless of diabetic status, increases the risk of AKI after PCI, with stress-induced hyperglycemia (SIH) exerting a particularly strong impact in non-diabetic patients [43,44]. This is consistent with our findings, where diabetes prevalence did not differ significantly between the AKI and non-AKI groups, yet random GLU at admission emerged as an independent risk factor for AKI. The underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In addition to vascular injury through microvascular obstruction, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, SIH may reflect the severity of the clinical condition. When coupled with insulin resistance, it may impair renal tubular epithelial repair and disrupt glucose and lipid metabolism, thereby compromising mitochondrial function in tubular cells [45,46].

CKD is a well-known risk factor for AKI after PCI in STEMI patients, and the occurrence of AKI further elevates the risk of cardiovascular events and mortality [14,47]. Moreover, studies indicate that a lower baseline GFR is associated with an increased likelihood of AKI and other adverse outcomes [48]. This vulnerability is due to CKD’s pathological features – impaired renal vascular regulation, glomerular hyperfiltration with heightened tubular metabolism, tubulointerstitial fibrosis with vascular sclerosis, and proteinuria – which weaken kidney reserve function and increase the risk of AKI [49]. Additionally, our study observed that some patients had impaired renal function prior to PCI that normalized by discharge. This transient dysfunction may result from an acute decline in left ventricular function during STEMI, leading to renal hypoperfusion and temporary tubular epithelial injury that improves with treatment. Although these patients encounter a higher risk of adverse events during hospitalization, research indicates that their long-term prognosis is not significantly impacted [50].

Hyperuricemia also increases the risk of AKI after PCI in STEMI patients. Uric acid can activate xanthine oxidase to generate reactive oxygen species, which damage renal tubular epithelial cells and impair mitochondrial function. It also reduces nitric oxide bioavailability and promotes shedding of the endothelial glycocalyx, leading to renal microcirculatory dysfunction. Additionally, uric acid crystals may directly obstruct renal tubules or trigger inflammatory responses that exacerbate tubulointerstitial fibrosis [51,52]. SUA levels can also indicate a patient’s volume status, suggesting that patients with hyperuricemia may benefit from prioritized hydration strategies. However, the need for initiating urate-lowering treatment remains controversial. Current studies suggest that uric acid may offer cardiovascular protection within a certain range; excessively lowering uric acid could negate its antioxidant benefits – especially during acute ischemia [53,54]. In patients with normal renal function, aggressive urate-lowering therapy may be detrimental [55], and the cardiovascular risks associated with urate-lowering medications should also be considered [56,57].

In this study, no significant association was found between contrast volume and the risk of AKI. This finding may be attributable to the use of an iso-osmolar, nonionic contrast agent (iodixanol), which may mitigate the adverse effects of high contrast doses by reducing red blood cell rigidity, renal vasoconstriction, and medullary hypoxia [58]. Current evidence indicates that iso-osmolar contrast agents significantly reduce the incidence of AKI compared with low-osmolar agents, especially in high-risk patients [59,60]. In addition, several studies have identified left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) as a key risk factor for AKI and prognosis in STEMI patients [11,61]. However, LVEF was not assessed in our study due to the lack of echocardiographic data in most primary PCI cases.

Mehran score is a classical risk assessment tool [24]. Compared to it, our nomogram replaces diabetes with random GLU, incorporates NSAID use, and simplifies other components, including renal function grading. This approach focuses on the real-time clinical status of emergency patients, allowing for easier data collection and calculation. Besides, nomograms can be used directly by clinicians at the bedside and integrated into electronic medical record (EMR) systems to auto-generate risk scores and trigger alerts. EMR data collection can also expedite model validation and further clinical research [62].

Although novel biomarkers such as cystatin C, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), and kidney injury molecule-1 have demonstrated high sensitivity for early renal injury, their clinical application is limited by high testing costs and lack of inter-laboratory standardization [63,64]. In contrast, nomograms incur virtually no additional cost and can predict AKI as well as correlate with other clinical outcomes. In our study, patients classified into higher risk score groups experienced longer hospital stays and tended to have higher ICU admission rates, indicating the model’s prognostic value. Beyond the Mehran score, several big data-based scoring systems incorporate more variables and advanced algorithms. Some models focus specifically on prognostic evaluation [65,66], thus providing avenues for future research.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis, it may be affected by selection bias. Second, only 11 patients in our cohort received NSAIDs, which may lead to overestimation of this effect and preclude further stratified analyses. Finally, the nomogram lacks external validation due to the limited sample size. Future models should include more predictive factors and utilize larger or multicenter datasets to improve external validation. Prospective validation should also be actively pursued to enhance model’s accuracy and generalizability.

Conclusion

This study developed a nomogram for predicting AKI after PCI in STEMI patients, based on five independent risk factors: NSAIDs use, female sex, GLU, SCr, and SUA. The model shows excellent predictive accuracy and clinical decision-making utility. It emphasizes the need for identifying high-risk patients and implementing personalized prevention strategies, such as enhanced monitoring for female patients, optimized GLU control, hydration tailored to SCr levels, and cautious use of NSAIDs.
