Acellular Dermal Graft Augmentation Technique for Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair
Authors: Maria Victoria Pomenta, Albert Arman, Lluis Peidro, Leticia Torres, Raquel Garcia, Sergi Sastre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe arthroscopic reconstruction of the rotator cuff is currently regarded as the gold standard when a complete tendon rupture occurs. However, many studies have shown different failure rates after repair. We present a technique for a full-thickness acellular dermal matrix augmentation for arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. Augmentation with allografts has been shown to improve the initial strength of the reconstruction and support the healing and remodeling, which in turn reduces the risk of failure. This technique could potentially benefit patients functionally.

Rotator cuff pathology is the leading cause of shoulder pain, particularly in individuals over the age of 50. When a complete tendon rupture occurs, arthroscopic reconstruction is the gold standard, but many studies indicate varying failure rates after repair.1 This failure is multifactorial, often linked to the characteristics of the rupture, including its size, area, and thickness. Other factors contributing to a poor prognosis include advanced age, low bone density, female sex, fat infiltration, diabetes mellitus, a reduced acromiohumeral interval, prior surgeries, and a history of smoking.2, 3, 4 The degenerative nature of the rotator cuff tendon may also play a role in this failure. Moreover, the tension applied to the repaired tendon after reattachment to the greater tuberosity during a “double-row” or even a basic single-row repair could decrease the chances of complete healing.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in the use of biological augmentation techniques for rotator cuff repair to promote healing at the tendon-bone interface. Augmentation with allografts has been shown to improve the initial strength of the reconstruction and support the progressive regeneration of tissue by creating a favorable environment for healing and remodeling. Additionally, augmentation helps protect the tissue during healing, which in turn reduces the risk of failure (Table 1).5,6

Commercial synthetic meshes and biological matrices from various sources are now being used in clinical practice to address surgical challenges.7,8 Among these, bioprosthetic materials such as acellular dermal matrices are biocompatible9 and less prone to infection. Dermal scaffolds for tissue regeneration are proving to be effective not only in wound-healing surgeries but also in breast reconstruction, abdominal wall reconstruction, and tendon reinforcement (Table 1).10

Depending on the characteristics of the graft and its mechanical properties, several clinical applications could benefit from dermal matrices.11,12 Reports suggest that thin grafts may tear during suture tying, and recent studies have highlighted the use of thicker grafts in the reconstruction of irreparable massive rotator cuff tears, particularly in superior capsule reconstruction, which has been shown to provide pain relief and improve postoperative function.13,14

In this work, we present the surgical technique for a full-thickness acellular dermal matrix augmentation for arthroscopic rotator cuff repair.

Surgical Technique

A video presenting this technique is provided (Video 1).

Patient Positioning

Anesthesia is administered with an interscalene block followed by sedation with a laryngeal mask. The patient is placed in a lateral decubitus position with skin traction. We use 4 portals: posterior, posterolateral, anterior, and anterolateral (Fig 1).

External surgical setup of left shoulder. Patient positioning in right lateral decubitus and skin traction. Arthroscopic portals: Post (posterior), PL (posterolateral), AL (anterolateral), and Ant (anterior).

Tear margins are debrided until viable tissue is ready for tear repair. It is important to highlight the use of a burr to partially decorticate the footprint at the greater tuberosity where the tendon is attached; this is necessary to ensure a good tendon-bone union. The native tendon is reattached to the area adjacent to the articular cartilage or a slightly more lateral area as much as the tendon tension allows. Tendon lesion is measured using a calibrated probe (Fig 2), and the preparation of the graft is based on the size of the tear.

Arthroscopic posterior view of the left shoulder with a supraspinatus tear. Tendon lesion is measured using a calibrated probe, and the size of the allograft is calculated. (A) Calibrated probe to size mediolateral rotator cuff tear. (B) Calibrated probe is used to size the anteroposterior rotator cuff tear.

Graft Preparation

The acellular dermal matrix (ADM) is intentionally oversized 4 mm on each side to allow placement of sutures. In addition, some marks are drawn on the medial corners of the graft (medial anterior and medial posterior) to orient it once placed into the subacromial space. Once prepared, the graft is presoaked in a vancomycin solution for 15 minutes (Fig 3).

Full-thickness acellular dermal matrix (ADM) preparation. (A) The graft is presoaked in a vancomycin solution for 15 minutes. (B) The ADM is cut to a size of 2.5 × 2.5 cm prior to articular insertion. (C) Marks are drawn on the medial corners of the graft to orient it once placed into the subacromial space.

To perform a transosseous-equivalent technique, 2 anchors (Healicoil Knotless Suture 5.5 mm; Smith & Nephew) are implanted at the medial aspect of the footprint, 1 anterior and 1 posterior, close to the cartilage line (Fig 4). Two high-strength threads are passed through the posterior edge of the tear and 2 more through its posterior aspect (Fig 5). One set of sutures from each anchor is knotted and placed as medially as possible (Fig 6). The remaining 4 threads (Fig 7) are passed outside the arthroscopic space of the shoulder, through the medial borders of the ADM, as shown in Figure 8.

The sheath of the lateral working portal is subsequently retrieved, and the portal is slightly enlarged. The ADM is then carefully inserted by tensioning the sliding knots and accompanying them with the knot pusher (Fig 9). At this point, subacromial visualization and placement of the dermis are crucial. Time and attention are given to ensure there is no thread tangling or graft malpositioning. The ADM must be placed with anteromedial and posteromedial landmarks on top of the medial aspect of the rotator cuff tear.

(A) External view of the left shoulder showing the acellular dermal matrix (ADM) inserted by tensioning the sliding knots and accompanying them with the knot pusher. (B) Arthroscopic posterior view of the left shoulder. The ADM must be placed on top of the medial aspect of the tear. (Ac, acromion; SAS, subacromial space.)

For the transosseous-equivalent technique, 1 anterior thread and 1 posterior thread are crossed through a PEEK (polyether ether ketone) anchor (Footprint 5.5 mm; Smith & Nephew), and the anchor is implanted at the anterolateral aspect of the footprint (Fig 10A). Subsequently, 1 anterior thread and 1 posterior thread are passed through another footprint anchor and implanted at the posterolateral aspect of the footprint (Fig 10B), ensuring rotator cuff reparation to the footprint as well as augmentation with the ADM. Finally, a lateral arthroscopic exploration ensures the correct placement of the graft and the repair of the tear (Figs 11, 12).

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Postoperatively, patients are placed in a sling for 4 to 6 weeks, allowing daily pendulum motion exercises. Supervised physical therapy is started at 6 weeks, with strengthening allowed at 12 weeks postoperatively.

Discussion

Rotator cuff tears are a common injury, especially in athletes and older individuals. While some small tears can be treated nonsurgically, larger tears may require surgery. Traditional surgical treatments for rotator cuff tears have been associated with high failure rates and long recovery times. However, in recent years, a new surgical option has emerged that may provide a better alternative for some patients: dermal patch augmentation (DPA).

DPA involves the use of a graft made of human or animal tissue that is surgically attached to the damaged rotator cuff tendon. The patch serves to reinforce the weakened tendon and improve its strength and function. This technique has been used successfully in other areas of the body, such as the Achilles tendon and the knee, and is now being explored for use in the rotator cuff.15,16

One of the key advantages of DPA is that it can be performed as an arthroscopic procedure, resulting in less pain and faster recovery time for the patient. Additionally, the use of a patch allows for more immediate strength and stability of the repaired tendon, which may reduce the risk of reinjury.

Several studies have investigated the use of DPA for rotator cuff tears, with generally positive results. Avanzi et al.17 found that patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair with porcine dermal patch augmentation had significantly better outcomes in terms of strength and range of motion compared to those who underwent traditional repair without a patch. They found a higher healing rate and close-to-normal magnetic resonance imaging findings performed by medialized single-row repair and augmented by a porcine dermal patch, in comparison with a nonaugmented group.

Lee et al.18 showed, in a prospective, single-blinded, randomized controlled trial with a 5.7-year follow-up, the results for 43 patients comparing arthroscopic repair of large rotator cuff tears with allograft patch augmentation. The allograft group showed a better American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score and a significantly lower retear rate (9.1% in the allograft group and 38.1% in the control group). They concluded that if the footprint coverage was not completely covered after rotator cuff repair, allograft patch augmentation may reduce the retear rate.

While these studies are promising, it is important to note that dermal patch augmentation is not appropriate for all patients with rotator cuff tears. This technique may be more effective for larger tears or for patients with poor tissue quality.

Chalmers et al.,19 in their meta-analysis, showed that rotator cuff repair decreases tear size as compared to nonoperative treatment and may thus interrupt the natural history of rotator cuff disease. Accordingly, the future for patch augmentation seems very promising to improve healing rates and outcomes when used to augment the repair of large and massive rotator cuff tears, and patch interposition may improve outcomes with irreparable rotator cuff tears as compared with partial repair.20,21

The purpose of this technique is to show a simple and reproducible method for rotator cuff augmentation that can be easily adopted by arthroscopic shoulder surgeons to decrease surgical time (Table 1, Table 2-3). The data and results obtained in meta-analyses and reviews regarding augmentation should be taken into consideration because of the wide diversity of surgical techniques, grafts, anchors, and groups of patients. However, due to the heterogeneity of available studies, including different surgical techniques, graft types, anchor systems, and patient populations, caution is advised when interpreting results. Most systematic reviews emphasize the need for further high-quality randomized controlled trials to validate the clinical benefits of DPA and standardize its application.
