Percutaneous cholecystolithotomy and lithotripsy for managing acute calculous cholecystitis in non-surgical candidates: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Arsalan Nadeem, Ali Husnain, Aleena Ahmed, Haider Ashfaq, Hamza Ashraf, Zain Ali Nadeem, Khawaja Abdul Rehman, Shahroze Ahmad, Muhammad Rafay Shahzad Cheema
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsThis study aimed to review and update the evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of percutaneous cholecystolithotomy/lithotripsy (PCCL) for managing acute calculous cholecystitis (ACC) in non-surgical candidates. MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Embase was conducted. We included studies focusing on the use of PCCL in patients deemed ineligible for surgery owing to ACC and reporting outcomes such as technical success, stone clearance, recurrence, and length of hospital stay. This review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. ResultsMeta-analysis of 13 studies showed a technical success rate of 97%, with stone and cholecystitis recurrence in 10% and 1% of cases, respectively. The pooled average length of hospital stay was 2.79 days. Complications included retained stones (3%), duct perforations (6%), catheter displacement (5%), bleeding (4%), and bile leakage (5%). A sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of these results. ConclusionsPCCL demonstrated high efficacy with minimal recurrence and low complication rates in managing ACC in non-surgical candidates. Further randomized controlled trials are necessary to compare its efficacy and safety with standard care approaches, such as cholecystectomy or percutaneous cholecystostomy.

INTRODUCTION

Acute calculous cholecystitis (ACC) is an obstruction of the cystic duct by gallstones that leads to inflammation of the gallbladder.1 According to the World Society of Emergency Surgery, ACC is the second most common cause of complicated intra-abdominal infections, accounting for 18.5% of cases.2 Gallstones are found in 6.5% of men and 10.5% of women worldwide, and are responsible for 90% of acute cholecystitis cases.3,4 Additionally, patients with symptomatic cholelithiasis have a 20% higher risk of developing ACC than their asymptomatic counterparts.5

Patients with ACC present with pain in the right upper quadrant, nausea, vomiting, and fever. If left untreated, it can result in perforations, abscesses, or fistulae.1 Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) is the gold standard treatment because it is associated with a lower risk of morbidity and mortality than open cholecystectomy. However, in older patients or those with significant comorbidities, emergency LC carries substantial risks of morbidity (41%) and mortality (19%).3 Several studies have shown significant increases in postoperative mortality rates in otherwise healthy patients with medical comorbidities or advanced age.6 Various scoring systems, such as the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation,7 American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score,8,9 Portsmouth Physiologic and Operative Severity Score for the Study of Mortality and Morbidity,9,10 Charlson Comorbidity Index,11 American Association for the Surgery of Trauma grade,12 frailty score,13 and assessments of one or multiple organ dysfunction,14 have been used to identify high-risk patients who are ineligible for surgery.

Consequently, such patients are temporarily managed with percutaneous cholecystostomy (PC) until they become eligible for surgery. However, some patients remain poor candidates for surgery owing to ongoing comorbidities and require long-term PC drainage. These drains significantly impact the quality of life owing to associated complications.15 Percutaneous cholecystolithotomy/lithotripsy (PCCL), performed under both fluoroscopic and endoscopic (cholecystoscopic) guidance, is an alternative treatment option for such patients.16 The use of endoscopy during PCCL increases the technical success rate by enabling direct visualization, making it easier to grasp stones and ensuring that even tiny stones, which might be missed by cholecystography, are completely removed. In addition, lithotripsy may improve the clearance rates of large stones.16

Previous studies have provided limited evidence on the success rates of PCCL performed under fluoroscopic and endoscopic guidance. Additionally, there is a lack of updated information on outcomes such as symptom recurrence and complication rates in recent studies. Therefore, this study aimed to compile all available evidence and update the existing literature on the use of PCCL for managing ACC in patients ineligible for surgery.

METHODS

This systematic review and meta-analysis conformed to the standards established by the Cochrane17 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.18 This review has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews: CRD42024571350. Ethical approval was not required as the analysis was conducted using previously published data.

Data sources and search strategy

Two reviewers conducted a comprehensive search for eligible studies from inception until June 2024 using the following electronic databases: Medline (via PubMed), the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials [CENTRAL] Library, and Embase. The detailed search strings for each database are outlined in Supplementary Table 1. No search filters were applied for study type. There were no limitations based on country or race, and only articles published in English were considered. In addition, the reference lists of the included studies were manually reviewed to identify any studies that might have been missed during the search.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

The articles were selected based on the following predetermined eligibility criteria: (1) studies involving patients with ACC who were deemed poor candidates for surgery; (2) the use of PCCL as the intervention, either under direct visualization (endoscopy) or fluoroscopic guidance; (3) studies reporting on any of the following outcomes—technical success rate, stone clearance, recurrence of cholecystitis, recurrence of stones, hospital stay, and complications; and (4) studies that were retrospective and prospective cohort studies or randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Of the 13 included studies, four were abstracts of a retrospective nature19-22 and the remaining nine were retrospective studies.16,23-30 Studies involving patients with chronic cholecystitis or symptomatic cholelithiasis were excluded. Furthermore, we excluded systematic reviews, narrative reviews, scoping reviews, non-human studies, case reports, case series, book chapters, letters, editorials, and expert opinions.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened the studies using Rayyan, focusing on titles and abstracts, according to established eligibility criteria. The full texts of the remaining articles were independently and thoroughly assessed and any disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer. The data extracted from the studies included the first author’s name, publication year, country, study design, patient count, mean age, sex ratio, reasons for performing PCCL, ASA scores, and indications for the procedure. Additionally, treatment details included the type of anesthesia used, type of access, time interval between the initial cholecystostomy and PCCL, and previously noted outcomes.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers independently evaluated the quality of the included studies using the National Institutes of Health quality assessment tool, and any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer.31 The tool assessed the study quality by evaluating the clarity of the study question, description of the study population, subject comparability, inclusion of consecutive cases, intervention and outcome measures, follow-up duration, statistical methods, and reporting of results.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted in R ver. 4.4.1 (R Foundation) using the packages “meta” and “metasens”. For dichotomous outcomes, we calculated the risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a random-effects model. Means and standard deviations (SDs) were used for continuous outcomes. When data were provided using other measures, the median was converted to the mean and the interquartile range was converted to SD using methods outlined by Luo et al.32 and Wan et al.,33 respectively, provided that the data were not skewed; skewed data were excluded. When only simple ranges were provided, they were not converted into SDs according to the Cochrane Handbook guidelines,17 resulting in data exclusion. Heterogeneity across studies was evaluated using the Higgins I2; values of 25% to 50% were considered low, 50% to 75% as moderate, and >75% as severe. We applied the Knapp-Hartung adjustment to the CIs to account for any statistical heterogeneity.34 Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test35 when more than ten studies were present. To ensure robustness, sensitivity analysis was performed by omitting one study at a time. A two-tailed p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant in all instances.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the included studies

We identified 269 articles from our electronic search, 53 of which were duplicates. After screening titles, abstracts, and then full texts, 13 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis.16,19-30 Figure 1 shows the PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process. The included studies were published from 1998 to 2024. The mean age of the participants ranged from 64.2 to 81 years. The most common reason for performing PCCL was high risk owing to comorbidities. The indications and mean follow-up durations varied across different studies. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the included studies.16,19-30

Risk of bias

Most studies showed a low risk of bias, with scores ranging from 6 to 9. It could not be determined whether the cases were consecutive in six studies, or if the subjects were comparable in four studies. Additionally, one study did not report the length of follow-up, and two studies did not report statistical methods. Lastly, one study was judged to not have an appropriate length of follow-up, and two others did not appropriately describe the statistical methods. Table 2 shows a detailed risk of bias assessment of the included studies.16,19-30

Results of the meta-analysis

Table 3 depicts the results of the meta-analysis in detail.

1) Technical success rate

Technical success was defined as clearance of the stones. Meta-analysis of the 13 included studies revealed a technical success rate of 97% (95% CI, 92%–100%; I2=46%; Fig. 2).

2) Recurrence of stones

Meta-analysis of the 13 studies revealed stone recurrence in 10% (95% CI, 2%–23%; I2=75%; Fig. 3) of the patients.

3) Recurrence of cholecystitis

Meta-analysis of four studies revealed cholecystitis recurrence in 1% (95% CI, 0%–7%; I2=0%; Fig. 4) of the patients.

4) Mean hospital stay

Meta-analysis of three studies revealed a mean hospital stay of 2.79 days (95% CI, 2.60–2.99 days; I2=0%; Fig. 5).

5) Adverse events

The total complication rate was 9% (95% CI, 3%–18%; I2=64%; Supplementary Fig. 1). Retained stones were observed in 3% (95% CI, 0%–53%; I2=0%; Supplementary Fig. 2), gallbladder or cystic duct perforation in 6% (95% CI, 0%–100%; I2=70%; Supplementary Fig. 3), catheter displacement in 5% (95% CI, 0%–47%; I2=76%; Supplementary Fig. 4), bleeding in 4% (95% CI, 0%–18%; I2=0%; Supplementary Fig. 5), and bile leak in 5% (95% CI, 0%–15%; I2=80%; Supplementary Fig. 6) of the patients.

6) Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sensitivity analysis omitting one study at a time did not significantly affect the results, demonstrating the robustness of the analysis (Supplementary Figs. 7–13). There was some evidence of publication bias in the outcomes of technical success rate (Supplementary Fig. 14), stone recurrence (Egger’s test, p=0.010; Supplementary Fig. 15), and total complications (Supplementary Fig. 16).

DISCUSSION

Our meta-analysis was designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of PCCL in the management of ACC in patients deemed ineligible for surgery. Importantly, this meta-analysis is the first to comprehensively review and synthesize data on these techniques. The analysis included 13 studies with both retrospective and prospective designs and yielded several critical findings. Notably, we observed high rates of technical success, manageable recurrence rates, and variability in hospital stays and procedural requirements.

Percutaneous endoscopic procedures have been increasingly adopted in the interventional radiology community, reflecting their potential to offer less invasive alternatives with favorable outcomes.23 Fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous gallstone removal is technically feasible and clinically effective in high-risk surgical patients with acute cholecystitis.16 Percutaneous cholecystolithotomy involves three key steps: (1) PC, (2) tract dilation and cholecystolithotomy, and (3) tract evaluation and catheter removal.36 Contraindications for PCCL include uncontrolled systemic infection or sepsis, uncorrectable coagulopathy, intractable ascites, and a life expectancy of <6 months.36

Candidacy for general anesthesia is assessed by the anesthesiology team, and not all patients deemed high-risk for surgery are automatically ineligible for anesthesia. This decision is based on a comprehensive evaluation of individual risk factors. Surgical contraindications extend beyond the ability to tolerate anesthesia and may include other medical considerations such as significant comorbidities or frailty, which increase surgical risk.37 PCCL remains a minimally invasive alternative owing to its smaller access site compared to surgical interventions and its reliance on existing anatomical channels (i.e., mature PC tracts), which avoid the creation of new large incisions.

Our meta-analysis revealed a technical success rate (stone clearance rate) of 97% (95% CI, 92%–100%; Fig. 2), with a moderate level of heterogeneity observed across studies (I2=46%). These findings are consistent with those in previous studies, which report similar success rates.25,38 The high success rates highlight the potential of this procedure to effectively eliminate stones and symptoms, thereby reducing recurrence and minimizing the need for drainage catheters.6 A major advantage of this technique is that it can be performed under local anesthesia, which is a valuable alternative for patients at high surgical risk or those with critical illness.39 However, the heterogeneity observed across studies underscores the influence of factors such as operator expertise, patient selection, and procedural protocols on the outcomes.

A reported complication of the percutaneous approach is gallstone recurrence.40,41 Recurrent stones within a short time are believed to have developed from residual fragments during the operation.42 Previous studies have reported high recurrence rates ranging from 10% to 27% at 14 to 18 months.43 A study conducted by Zou et al.44 reported that the recurrence rate in 439 patients who underwent PCCL was 41% after 10 years, with 21% of these patients ultimately requiring extirpative surgery. However, our analysis found stone recurrence in 10% of the patients, which concurs with the results of a systematic review that reported a relatively similar rate.45 A possible explanation for these variable rates may be genetic and metabolic factors, which play major roles in the development of gallstones.46 Conversely, cholecystitis recurrence has been reported in only 1% of patients. This low recurrence rate, consistent across the included studies (I2=0%), suggests that percutaneous endoscopy and lithotripsy are effective in achieving sustained cholecystitis resolution in this high-risk patient population.

Analysis of the mean hospital stay revealed a pooled average of 2.79 days. Previous research also supports a hospital stay of <3 days for similar procedures.42 In contrast, the average hospital stay for patients undergoing cholecystectomy typically ranges from 5 to 10 days, leading to considerable inpatient costs (exceeding 20,000 US dollars per episode).47 The shorter hospital stay associated with PCCL can contribute toward reduced healthcare costs and quicker recovery times.

Our analysis of adverse events revealed a total complication rate of 9%, with notable complications including retained stones, gallbladder or cystic duct perforation, catheter displacement, bleeding, and bile leaks. In previous studies, bile leakage was reported as the most common complication during the procedure or after catheter removal.23 The presence of heterogeneity in some complication rates highlights the variability in patient populations, procedural techniques, and postprocedural management across studies. To minimize the risk of complications, PCCL should be performed only in well-equipped hospitals with experienced interventional radiologists and/or endoscopists.48 Preprocedural assessments should be enhanced to identify patients at a higher risk of complications, and rigorous procedural protocols, along with vigilant postprocedural monitoring, should be implemented to effectively manage and mitigate adverse events.

Some limitations affect the interpretation of this meta-analysis. The moderate heterogeneity observed in outcomes such as technical success rates, stone clearance rates, and stone recurrence suggests some variability in the study populations and procedural techniques. The retrospective nature of all the included studies introduces potential biases, such as selection bias and confounding. The small sample sizes of some studies may have affected the robustness of the results and increased the risk of publication bias. Additionally, the limited long-term follow-up data restrict insights into the durability of outcomes and recurrence of complications. Addressing these limitations in future research with standardized protocols, larger sample sizes, and extended follow-up can provide more reliable and generalizable results.

In conclusion, PCCL has shown high success rates with low recurrence rates and minimal adverse effects in patients with ACC who were ineligible for surgery. Robust RCTs are required to compare the efficacy and safety of PCCL with the current standards of care, such as cholecystostomy or PC.
