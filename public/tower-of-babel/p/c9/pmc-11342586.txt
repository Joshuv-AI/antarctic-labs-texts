Adverse events of tissue plasminogen activators in acute myocardial infarction patients: a real-world and pharmacovigilance database analysis
Authors: Fangying Liu, Guo Yang, Jia Xie, Puguang Xie, Fating Zhou, Fan Yang, Yu Ma, Fan Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTissue plasminogen activator (tPA) is recommended as the preferred thrombolytic therapy for acute myocardial infarction (AMI). This study aimed to explore tPA-related adverse events (AEs) reported in the United States Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), assess the potential safety of three preferred tPA therapies for treating AMI, and provide guidance for selecting tPA for prehospital thrombolysis.MethodFour algorithms, including ROR, PRR, BCPNN, and MGPS, were used to quantify the signals of Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase related AEs and compare the differential degrees of the three tPA-associated AEs in the actual data.ResultWe detected 18 signals of Tenecteplase-induced AE, 29 signals of Reteplase-induced AE, and 22 signals of Alteplase-induced AE. Among the three drugs, Tenecteplase had the highest signal intensity for intracranial hemorrhage-related AE, followed by Alteplase. Besides, Reteplase had the highest signal intensity for procedure-related AE and Alteplase had the highest signal intensity for arrhythmia-related AE. The time-onset analysis indicates that we should be vigilant for AEs, especially within the first week and the first 1–2 days after medication.ConclusionThis study identified and compared the signals of AE related to Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase in AMI patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-024-04121-5.Keywords: Tenecteplase, Reteplase, Alteplase, AMI, FAERS, Pharmacovigilance

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease remains the most common cause of individual mortality worldwide and its incidence is on the rise [1, 2]. A significant burden of medical care for ischemic heart disease falls upon low- and middle-income countries, some of which are experiencing an epidemiological transition marked by increasing rates and prevalence of cardiovascular diseases [2].

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a common manifestation of ischemic heart disease, characterized by myocardial cell death due to sudden reduction in coronary artery blood flow and subsequent myocardial ischemia [3]. Typical symptoms include chest discomfort or pain that is more severe than angina pectoris [4]. The high incidence and mortality rates associated with AMI lead to substantial healthcare costs, impacting individuals, families, healthcare systems, and nations economically [5]. The rapid and effective treatment of AMI patients remains an important topic of discussion.

AMI can be effectively treated with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or thrombolytic therapy [5]. Prioritize PCI treatment for AMI patients. If PCI can not be performed within 120 min of the first medical contact to open the infarct-related artery, thrombolysis should be administered within 30 min to the AMI patient [5, 6]. Thrombolytic agents degrade fibrinogen and fibrin, thereby dissolving coronary artery thrombi and restoring coronary artery perfusion [7]. Due to the time-sensitive nature of thrombolytic therapy, guidelines recommend initiating thrombolysis within 10 min of dignosis onset. However, achieving this goal in practice is challenging, prompting studies to suggest initiating thrombolysis prior to hospital admission, with the aim of reducing mortality by shortening the time to thrombolysis [5, 6]. Currently, there is insufficient clinical evidence to support the safety of prehospital thrombolysis [8–10]. The widespread implementation of prehospital thrombolysis is limited by factors such as healthcare infrastructure, population distribution (rural versus urban), emergency care systems, and the availability of interventions and surgical alternatives [8, 9]. In middle- and low-income countries, prehospital thrombolysis is even more difficult to achieve [9, 10]. Consequently, whether prehospital thrombolysis is associated with a lower mortality rate compared to in-hospital thrombolysis remains uncertain, and further guidance is needed regarding drug selection and dosage.

Guidelines recommend three tissue plasminogen activator (tPAs), namely Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase, as the preferred thrombolytic drugs for AMI, all of which have demonstrated good thrombolytic efficacy [5, 6]. Some studies suggest that Reteplase and Tenecteplase have advantages over Alteplase, which was the earliest agent used, in terms of lower cost and better thrombolytic effects [11]. However, recent research has also identified a potential increased risk of bleeding associated with Tenecteplase compared to Alteplase [12–14]. Safety data for Reteplase and Tenecteplase primarily come from small-sample prospective clinical trials, retrospective studies, or meta-analyses, all of which have limited sample sizes and follow-up times and may not fully capture relevant adverse events (AEs) [11–14]. And the data are lacking regarding the comparative analysis of real-world safety of different tPAs used for AMI comprehensively. Additionally, the time of occurrence of drug adverse reactions (ADRs) is still unknown. Therefore, this study utilized the largest drug surveillance database, the United States Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), for the past twenty years to assess the AEs associated with Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase in the treatment of AMI. This provides important insights into the safety considerations of thrombolytic therapy for AMI patients, offering guidance for clinical monitoring and risk assessment of prehospital thrombolysis in AMI patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

We conducted a retrospective pharmacovigilance study based on the FAERS database from January 2004 to September 2023. The FAERS data files consist of seven types of datasets: Demographics and Administrative Information (DEMO), Drug Information (DRUG), Adverse Event Codes (REAC), Patient Outcomes (OUTC), Report Sources (RPSR), Treatment Start and End Dates (THER), and Drug Indications (INDI), as well as deleted cases [15].

For statistical analysis purposes, all the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) data was imported into RPSR, resulting in a total of 19,514,140 reports retrieved. As FDA data is received from various sources, potential duplicate reports needed to be reprocessed. Following FDA guidelines, we conducted a duplicate data removal process prior to statistical analysis, selecting the most recent FDA_DT with the same CASEID and the highest PRIMARYID. Duplicate reports were eliminated by following FDA guidelines and identifying instances with matching FDA data (FDA_DT) and Case identification (CASEID) [16].

AEs and drug identification

The FAERS database uses the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 24.0 (MedDRA 24.0) to classify AEs [17]. MedDRA terms are organized into five levels of hierarchy, namely System Organ Class (SOC), High-Level Group Terms (HLGT), High-Level Terms (HLT), Preferred Terms (PT), and Lowest Level Terms (LLT) [17]. In our study, the REAC file within the FAERS database was utilized to identify all AE reports related to the three drugs, Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase. The frequency and severity of these AEs were described based on MedDRA classification at the SOC and PT levels. Reporters were required to assign a role code to AEs, including Primary Suspect (PS), Secondary Suspect (SS), Concomitant(C), and Interacting (I) drugs [17]. In our study, we selected “PS” to improve the accuracy of our analysis.

Furthermore, in our study, we limited the AEs reported during thrombolytic therapy using three tPAs for treating AMI, by selecting the drug indication as “Acute myocardial infarction” from INDI. This was done to increase the accuracy of our analysis.

Data mining and statistical analysis

Disproportionality analysis is a fundamental tool in pharmacovigilance studies to identify potential AEs associated with drugs [18]. This approach compares the proportion of AEs caused by a specific drug to all other drugs, aiming to detect potential signals. The general principle is that a signal is generated during data extraction when the frequency of a specific AE for a specific drug is significantly higher than the background frequency and meets certain threshold criteria.

In our study, we utilized disproportionality analysis to identify potential signals among all AEs related to the three rPTs in AMI patients. Four key specific indicators, namely Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR), Proportional Reporting Ratio (PRR), Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN), and Multi-Item Gamma Poisson Shrinker (MGPS) algorithms, were employed to assess the signals of drug-related AEs for the three drugs [18]. Among these, ROR serves as one of the key indicators to evaluate the association between a specific AE and a particular drug. In statistics, a higher ROR typically indicates a stronger association between the drug and the AE [18]. A drug-related AE that satisfies all four algorithms is considered a positive signal. The detailed calculation formulas and criteria for these four algorithms are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

In general, a higher value of these four parameters indicates a stronger signal when the incidence rate of a specific AE related to the drug significantly exceeds the background frequency within the database and reaches a certain threshold or criterion. Chi-square tests or one way ANOVA were utilized to compare the clinical characteristics of AE reports of the three drugs.

In addition, we also calculated the time of onset and severity level for drug-related AEs. The onset time was defined as the interval between START_DATE (start of drug administration) and EVENT_DATE (occurrence of AE), with exclusion of false or inaccurate AE occurrence information. Statistical analysis and data processing were conducted using R 4.2.2, IBM SPSS Statistics 26, Microsoft EXCEL 2019, and GraphPad Prism 8.

Result

Characteristics of tPAs in AMI patients

Starting from January 2004 until September 2023, a total of 16,964,230 unique reports were submitted to FAERS. As shown in Tables 1, 1,471 reports were identified as AE reports where tPAs were reported as the primary suspected drug for treating AMI patients. Among these reports, the incidence rates of Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase were 20.9% (n = 307), 26.8% (n = 394), and 52.3% (n = 770), respectively, indicating a higher occurrence of AEs with Alteplase in treating AMI compared to Tenecteplase and Reteplase. AE reports from male AMI patients accounted for 57.6%, which was more than twice the percentage reported for females (22.1%), suggesting that male AMI patients were more prone to AEs during tPA treatment. The mean ages of AE occurrences for Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase were 62.8 years (SD = 14.213), 63.7 years (SD = 11.19410), and 56.5 years (SD = 12.06343), respectively. Among these, the proportion of AEs reported from people aged 65 and above was 33.2% and 50.7% for Tenecteplase and Reteplase, respectively, which was much higher than that of Alteplase (10%), indicating that elderly patients treated with Tenecteplase and Reteplase were more prone to AEs, while AEs related to Alteplase mainly occurred in patients below 65 years of age.

Among them, the signal intensity of intracranial hemorrhage associated AEs induced by Tenecteplase was the highest among the three drugs. AE signals such as ‘death’, ‘haemorrhage intracranial’, ‘cardiogenic shock’, ‘haemorrhage’, ‘ischaemic stroke’, and others were consistent with the labeling of Tenecteplase, further supporting the concentration of AEs induced by Tenecteplase in cerebral hemorrhage-related AEs. We also identified some potential AEs induced by Tenecteplase that were not listed in the labeling, such as ‘dysarthria’.

We found that the positive AE signals of Reteplase were primarily focused on those related to arterial cannulation and other invasive treatments, such as ‘post procedural complication’, ‘procedural complication’, ‘cardiac procedure complication’, ‘iatrogenic injury’. This is consistent with the reports of previous clinical trials with Reteplase, where bleeding induced by Reteplase treatment often occurred in association with coronary artery-related procedures [19, 20]. Another point consistent with the labeling is that the signal intensity for severe bleeding, such as ‘hemorrhage intracranial’, was relatively low for Reteplase. Our study did not find a positive AE signal related to severe bleeding induced by Reteplase. Additionally, due to the positive AE signals induced by Reteplase being concentrated in many other clinical manifestations that are also present in myocardial infarction itself, such as ‘coronary artery stenosis’, ‘Coronary artery diseases’, ‘ejection fraction decreased’, ‘myocardial ischaemia’, ‘coronary artery occlusion’, ‘coronary artery thrombosis’. It is difficult to determine whether these signals are caused by the drug itself rather than the disease. We also identified some potential AEs not listed in the Reteplase labeling, such as ‘blood glucose increased’.

As for Alteplase, being the earliest tPA drug, the clinical trial results and labeling provided a more comprehensive description of AEs. Common bleeding AEs such as intracranial hemorrhage (including ‘haemorrhagic stroke’) and gastrointestinal bleeding (including ‘gingival bleeding’) were consistent with our study. Furthermore, it listed some common AEs that can be caused by myocardial infarction and/or thrombolytic therapy which were consistent with our study, such as ‘chest pain’, ‘ventricular extrasystoles’, ‘ventricular tachycardia’, ‘arrhythmia’, ‘nausea’ and ‘anxiety’. It is worth noting that we found that various cardiac arrhythmias, particularly ‘reperfusion arrhythmia’, had the highest signal intensity in Alteplase-induced AEs, and were more common compared to aother two tPAs. We also identified some potential AE signals not listed in the Alteplase labeling, such as ‘dry skin’.

Time to onset of drug-associated AEs

To identify the onset time of tPA-related AEs, we collected information on the occurrence time of Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase after removing duplicate and erroneous reports. The number of information records for Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase were 35, 246, and 18, respectively. The median onset time for Tenecteplase was 1 day [interquartile range (IQR) 1–5 days], for Reteplase was 19 days [IQR 4–35 days], and for Alteplase was 2 days [IQR 1–5 days].

As shown in Fig. 4, a significant proportion of AEs associated with Tenecteplase and Alteplase occurred within the first 7 days of AMI treatment (85.3%; 88.9%), while AEs related to Reteplase were more likely to occur within 2 months of AMI treatment. Of note, there was a possibility of AE occurrence even after 120 days of tPA medication initiation (2.9%, 5.3%, 11.1%).

Discussion

We conducted a comprehensive analysis and safety comparison of AEs related to three first-line tPA medications in AMI patients using the FAERS database, encompassing data from 2004 to 2023, over a span of 20 years. This study provides the most extensive and systematic real-world analysis and pharmacovigilance examination of AEs associated with these three drugs in AMI patients.

In the analysis of clinical characteristics, we found that the number of AEs related to Alteplase (n = 770, 52.3%) in the treatment of AMI was higher than Tenecteplase (n = 307, 20.9%) and Reteplase (n = 394, 26.8%). However, considering the difference in the total number of drug uses worldwide, further epidemiological investigations are required to validate these results. By analyzing the gender distribution of AE patients, we discovered that AEs reported by male patients receiving tPA treatment for AMI accounted for 57.6% of the total, which was more than double the percentage reported by females (22.1%, P < 0.0001). This significantly higher AE cases reported in male may be attributed to the higher incidence of AMI in males [21]. The reasons for the gender differences in incidence and treatment outcomes among AMI patients are still unclear, with only some speculations about the vascular structural characteristics of women and the protective effects of estrogen [22]. Whether this indicates that male AMI patients are more susceptible to AEs when receiving tPA treatment requires further epidemiological research. Regarding age, the results showed that the mean age of AE occurrence for Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase was 62.8 years (SD = 14.213), 63.7 years (SD = 11.19410), and 56.5 years (SD = 12.06343), respectively, indicating a concentration among the elderly population. This may be due to the higher incidence of AMI and lower return on thrombolytic therapy in older adults [23]. Of note, among AMI patients with tPA-induced AEs, the AE rate for patients aged 65 and above was much higher for Tenecteplase (33.2%) and Reteplase (50.7%) compared to Alteplase (10%). Our study suggests that the use of Alteplase in elderly AMI patients may be safer, although further clinical trials are needed to confirm this. Notably, an analysis of the regions reporting AEs revealed that the majority of tPA-induced AE reports originated from North America (85.4%), followed by Europe (28.1%). Reports from Asian countries accounted for only 10.1%. However, for Tenecteplase, only 25.1% of the unique reports came from North America and 22.5% from Europe, with the highest number of AEs reported from Asia, reaching 32.2%. This suggests a significant increase in AEs induced by Tenecteplase in the Asian population compared to Reteplase and Alteplase (P < 0.0001). This finding is consistent with recent small-scale clinical trial results conducted in Asia [13]. Some studies have suggested a higher potential bleeding risk associated with Tenecteplase compared to Alteplase [13, 23]. The results regarding the risk of bleeding from Tenecteplase are controversial [24, 25], but the underlying reasons were not explained. And the efficacy and safety under lower doses or different administration methods may be the reasons for the differences [24]. There is a lack of relevant research on Asian populations. Therefore, further evaluation of the safety of using Tenecteplase for treating AMI in the Asian population is warranted.

Through the analysis of AE outcomes in different tPA treatments for AMI, we observed significant differences in death outcomes (P < 0.001). Tenecteplase had the highest reported death rate (39.1%), followed by Alteplase (24.7%) and Reteplase (12.1%). It is noteworthy that the highest reported cause of death came from Nervous system disorders. The signal intensity of Nervous system disorders, particularly intracranial hemorrhage-related AEs, correlated with the trend of death outcomes for Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase in AMI treatment. Hence, the difference in death outcomes related to different tPA treatments for AMI-induced AEs may be associated with the occurrence of Nervous system disorders, especially intracranial hemorrhage-related AEs. Considering that intracranial hemorrhage is a common complication in AMI thrombolytic therapy [25, 26] that can cause serious consequences such as death, this is understandable.

When comparing and analyzing positive signal occurrence and signal intensity of AEs, we also discovered some interesting results, providing guidance and recommendations for the safety consideration of thrombolytic therapy in AMI patients and clinical monitoring and risk assessment of pre-hospital thrombolysis in AMI patients. Firstly, bleeding is a common AE shared by tPA drugs and a prevalent complication in AMI patients receiving thrombolytic therapy [26]. Our study found positive signals of bleeding-related AEs for all three tPA drugs, consistent with the product labels. It is worth noting that our study further revealed through comparison of positive signals and intensity at the PT level that Tenecteplase had the highest bleeding risk, while Reteplase had the lowest (p < 0.0001). Additionally, Tenecteplase was most likely to cause intracranial hemorrhage-related AEs leading to severe AE outcomes, while Reteplase mainly caused non-severe bleeding-related AEs such as gastrointestinal bleeding, resulting in milder AE outcomes. This indicates that caution should be exercised when using Tenecteplase to evaluate the risk of intracranial hemorrhage, and if patients have a higher risk of intracranial hemorrhage, Tenecteplase should be used with caution as a thrombolytic drug. Secondly, previous studies have found that fibronolysis resulting from thrombolysis with Reteplase can dissolve thrombi at the sites of injection, necessitating careful observation for potential bleeding at sites caused by procedures like arterial puncture and catheter insertion [27]. Our study also supports this finding. Reteplase had more signals related to procedural injury, and the signal intensity of “procedural complication” was the highest among the three tPA drugs (p = 0.0012). Therefore, special attention should be given to bleeding and injury caused by procedures when using Reteplase for thrombolysis. Thirdly, reperfusion injury is a common complication after thrombolytic therapy, and reperfusion arrhythmia can lead to severe outcomes such as cardiac arrest [28]. Our study found that Alteplase treatment for AMI can induce various reperfusion arrhythmia-related AEs, consistent with previous research. It is worth noting that the signal intensity of “Reperfusion arrhythmia” induced by Alteplase was the highest among the three drugs (p = 0.012). Therefore, even though the product label suggests that Alteplase causing reperfusion arrhythmia is rare, we still recommend vigilance for reperfusion arrhythmia occurring during Alteplase thrombolysis.

Due to the higher selectivity and half-life of the new generation of plasmin inhibitors, tenecteplase and reteplase are generally considered to have thrombolytic efficiency and safety that is not inferior to Alteplase, and usuaslly lower risk of bleeding than Alteplase [29]. However, the safety of thrombolytic therapy with tenecteplase in AMI patients at risk of intracranial hemorrhage deserves further investigation. This may be due to the dosage of tenecteplase in the treatment of myocardial infarction. AIS guidelines and clinical trials have shown that 0.25 mg/kg tenecteplase and 0.9 mg/kg alteplase are equally effective in treating AIS, but for safety reasons, the use of tenecteplase should not exceed 25mg [30, 31]. According to AMI guidelines and database records, adults with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) weighing 60 kg typically use 40 mg of tenecteplase [5, 6], which far exceeds the recommended dosage of 25 mg in the new AIS guidelines [30]. However, alteplase (0.9 mg/kg, not exceeding 100 mg) and reteplase (18 mg + 18 mg) do not [6]. We speculate that the dosage may be the reason for the higher intracranial hemorrhage in AMI patients treated with tenecteplase. Additionally, no direct comparison is available on tenecteplase versus reteplase, which is worth further research.

Furthermore, during tPA treatment for AMI, patients may experience many other clinical manifestations commonly associated with AMI, such as coronary artery stenosis, coronary artery diseases, ejection fraction decreased; chest pain, arrhythmia, nausea, and anxiety [4]. Although we observed strong signals in our study, as mentioned in other researches [32], we are still unable to distinguish whether these clinical manifestations are caused by tPAs.

Through the analysis of the time to onset of drug-associated AEs, we have found that the majority of AEs occur within 7 days after medication. This suggests that we should be vigilant for AEs within one week of medication, especially within 1–2 days after medication. The AEs from Reteplase thrombolytic therapy occur over a longer period, with 66.4% of AEs occurring one week after Reteplase treatment. Therefore, the observation period for AEs during Reteplase treatment for AMI should be extended accordingly.

There are still limitations in our study. Due to the lack of complete clinical information from patients and the voluntary reporting nature of the database, we cannot accurately determine the incidence of AEs. Additionally, although our assessment of signal strength is statistically significant, we have not quantified the risk level or established a causal relationship between the drug and AEs. Further prospective clinical trials are needed to validate the results.

Conclusion

This study compared the AEs and severity associated with Tenecteplase, Reteplase, and Alteplase in AMI patients. Although all three tPA therapies are significantly effective in thrombolytic treatment for AMI and each has its advantages, their safety profiles should be fully recognized when making choices. Our study suggests that when using Tenecteplase, the risk of intracranial hemorrhage should be evaluated carefully, and if patients have a higher risk, Tenecteplase should be used with caution as a thrombolytic agent. When using Reteplase for thrombolysis, special attention should be given to bleeding and injury caused by the procedure. During Alteplase thrombolysis, the occurrence of reperfusion arrhythmias should be monitored. Vigilance for AEs should be exercised within one week of medication, particularly within 1–2 days. Due to the longer occurrence time of aes during Reteplase thrombolytic therapy, the observation period for AEs during Reteplase treatment for AMI should be extended accordingly.
