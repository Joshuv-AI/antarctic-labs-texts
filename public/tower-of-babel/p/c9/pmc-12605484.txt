Gcm1 Orchestrates Lef1 Expression in Folate Deficiency-Induced Neural Tube Defects
Authors: Zhihua Cao, Lu Li, Xiaolong Gu, Baolian Li, Qi Xie, Xuefen Wu, Xinhuan Zhang, Guohua Zhang, Ke Wu, Wenbing Ren, Zhizhen Liu, Aili Fang, Shenghui Lu, Qiu Xie, Jianting Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Neural tube defects (NTDs) are severe congenital anomalies with limited therapeutic options. Increased expression of the transcription factor glial cell missing 1 (Gcm1) has been linked to NTDs, yet the mechanisms underlying this association remain elusive. Herein, folate deficiency upregulated Gcm1 expression through H4 acetylation (H4ac) enrichment in the promoter. ChIP–qPCR and Co-IP experiments revealed that folate deficiency enhanced H4 acetylation at the Gcm1 promoter via CREB-binding protein (CBP), subsequently resulting in increased Gcm1 transcription. Furthermore, Gcm1-ChIP-seq revealed lymphoid enhancer binding factor 1 (Lef1) as a potential downstream target of Gcm1 in mESCs without folate supplementation and the likely activator of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway for aberrant neurodevelopment. Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence analyses revealed significantly higher Gcm1 and Lef1 expression in a low-folate NTD mouse model than in controls. Finally, NanoString analysis of human low-folate NTD samples confirmed a positive correlation between upregulated Gcm1 expression and Lef1 expression. Collectively, these findings indicate that the CBP-dependent Gcm1 regulation of Lef1 expression is crucial in folate-deficient NTDs, revealing the potential mechanism by which NTDs are induced by low folate concentrations and providing a promising target for therapeutic intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12035-025-05346-x.Keywords: Neural tube defects, Acetylation, CREB-binding protein, Glial cell missing 1, Lymphoid enhancer binding factor 1

Introduction

Neural tube defects (NTDs) are significant birth defects that affect approximately 1 in 1000 live births worldwide; they result from the incomplete closure of the neural tube during early embryogenesis, leading to severe structural and functional abnormalities in the brain and spinal cord [1–3]. Currently, the pathogenesis of NTDs is not fully understood, and while preventive folate supplementation strategies are effective [4], the mechanism is unclear.

GCM1 is a transcription factor that was initially identified for its role in glial cell differentiation in Drosophila [5–8]. In mammals, GCM1 is expressed primarily in the placenta, where it regulates the formation and function of syncytiotrophoblasts by transactivating genes such as syncytins, HtrA4, and hCGβ, which are essential for trophoblast fusion, migration, invasion, and hormone production [6, 9, 10]. A study reported that GCM1 interacts with the WNT signaling pathway, which is crucial for normal placental cell differentiation. Specifically, GCM1 has been identified as a target gene of the β-catenin/TCF complex during syncytiotrophoblast differentiation in mice [11]. Recent studies have expanded the role of GCM1 in neural development [12, 13]. Gcm1 overexpression in mice has been shown to result in NTDs [14]; however, the molecular mechanisms underlying this process remain unclear.

As a transcription factor, Gcm1 undergoes extensive posttranslational modifications that regulate its functional dynamics. Recent studies have revealed that dual-specificity phosphatase 23 (DUSP23) modulates Gcm1 activity through the coordinated regulation of phosphorylation, dephosphorylation, and acetylation states, thereby promoting syncytiotrophoblast formation [15]. Nuclear factor erythroid-derived 2 (p45 NF-E2) has been shown to influence Gcm1 through CREB-binding protein (CBP)-mediated acetylation, ultimately suppressing syncytiotrophoblast differentiation and apoptosis during intrauterine growth restriction [16]. Additionally, caspase-14 has been identified as a novel regulator of Gcm1 through CBP-dependent acetylation to regulate apoptosis during placental development [17]. Collectively, while these studies demonstrate the essential role of CBP-mediated acetylation in maintaining Gcm1 stability, the functional consequences of the CBP-mediated regulation of Gcm1 transcriptional activity and its potential implications in neural development remain to be fully elucidated.

Gcm1 was reported to mediate Wnt signaling in placenta development [18, 19]. In Gcm1-deficient TS cells, enrichment in transcripts related to Wnt signaling and decreased Wnt antagonist NOTUM showed the relationship between Gcm1 and Wnt signaling [6]. A positive feedback loop involving Gcm1 and Fzd5 was found to direct chorionic branching morphogenesis in the placenta [20]. Lymphoid enhancer-binding factor 1 (Lef1), a member of the LEF1/T-cell factor (TCF) family of transcription factors, serves as a critical regulatory node in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and plays pivotal roles in cellular differentiation and developmental processes [21]. Lef1 depletion affects gene expression during gastrulation and results in the abnormal differentiation of cells derived from the ectoderm and mesoderm [22]. In early embryonic development, genetic interaction between Lef1 and Alx4 is required [23]. In addition, Lef1 could regulate hippocampus development [24]. Our previous investigations demonstrated that Gcm1 upregulation inducing NTDs maybe related to Wnt/β-catenin signaling activating under folate-deficient conditions [25]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the Gcm1-mediated regulation of the downstream target genes of Wnt/β-catenin signaling remain poorly characterized. Furthermore, the potential functional interplay between Lef1 and Gcm1 in the context of abnormal neural development warrants a systematic investigation.

In this study, we demonstrated that folate deficiency induces neural developmental abnormalities through the epigenetic upregulation of Gcm1 expression via histone H4 acetylation (H4-Ac) [26, 27]. Mechanistically, this regulatory process is orchestrated primarily by CREB-binding protein (CBP). ChIP-seq analysis and ChIP–qPCR experiments revealed that Lef1 may be a direct downstream target of Gcm1 in C57BL/6 mESCs without folate supplementation. These findings were corroborated in both low-folate NTD mice and clinical samples. Our results establish a molecular cascade in which CBP-dependent H4 acetylation under folate-deficient conditions promotes Gcm1 overexpression, which in turn drives Lef1 upregulation and may activate the Wnt signaling pathway, leading to NTDs. This work not only elucidates the mechanistic basis of Gcm1-mediated NTDs but also identifies potential therapeutic targets for NTD prevention and intervention.

Materials and Methods

Animal

CD-1 mice (7–8 weeks old, weighing 16–25 g) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Both male and female mice were maintained on a low-folate diet for a minimum of 8 weeks prior to mating. Sexually mature mice were paired overnight, and the presence of a vaginal plug at 8:00 am the following morning was designated as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). To induce neural tube defect (NTD) models, pregnant mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of 1.5 mg/kg methotrexate (MTX; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) on E7.5. At E9.5, pregnant mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation, and fetal phenotypes were examined under a stereomicroscope [25, 28, 29]. The embryonic teratogenicity rate of mice treated with folate-deficient food and MTX was supplied in Supplementary Table S1. Embryos exhibiting characteristic NTD manifestations were collected for subsequent experimental analyses.

Cell Culture and Folate Treatment

C57BL/6 mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) and KM mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) were kindly provided by the Stem Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The final concentration of 4 mg/L folate was used as the normal control while the folate-deficient group was treated simply with DMEM without folate (Sigma, Cat. #D2429). C57BL/6 mESCs were seeded into culture dishes precoated with MEFs cultured in DMEM supplemented with 0.1 mM nonessential amino acids, 0.1 mM glutamate, and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Invitrogen, USA) overnight. The C57BL/6 mESCs were cultured in the DMEM with or without 4 mg/L folate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), diluted with UltraPure™ distilled water (Invitrogen, USA), 1 mM glucose, 0.1 mM nonessential amino acids, 0.1 mM glutamate, 15% FBS, 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Life Technologies, USA), and 1000 U/ml leukemia inhibitory factor (Millipore, USA). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. All cell lines were authenticated by STR profiling and tested as mycoplasma-free.

Folate power (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) 10X without folate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and with 3.7% filtered sodium hydrogen carbonate (Beijing Chemical Reagent Company, China). CBP-30 (MCE, USA) was dissolved in DMSO with a final concentration of 10 µmol for 24 h.

Microarray Expression Profiling

Microarray analysis was performed using an Agilent Mouse mRNA Array (8 × 60 K format, CapitalBio, Beijing, China), which contains probes for 27,958 Entrez Gene RNAs and 7419 lncRNAs, along with 1280 control probes. Total RNA was extracted from C57BL/6 mESCs after six generations of folate deficiency. RNA quality was assessed using spectrophotometry (NanoDrop ND-1000) and denaturing gel electrophoresis. Fluorescently labeled cDNA (Cy5 or Cy3-dCTP) was prepared using Eberwine’s linear amplification method according to the manufacturer’s instructions [30]. Three biological replicates were used per condition. Data were summarized, normalized, and quality-controlled using GeneSpring software V13 (Agilent). Differentially expressed genes were identified with thresholds of |fold change|≥ 2 and p-value < 0.05 (t-test). Data were log₂-transformed and median-centered per gene using Cluster 3.0, followed by hierarchical clustering with average linkage [31]. Results were visualized with Java TreeView.

Differentiation of Folate-Free Induced Mouse C57BL/6 Embryonic Stem Cells

mESCs stably cultured in folate-free medium for six generations were maintained on a feeder layer of mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) with leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF, Millpore, USA) supplementation for 48 h (designated as Day 0). The cells were then digested using 0.25% trypsin, and residual MEFs were removed via differential adhesion. Subsequently, cells were resuspended at a density of 105 cells/mL and seeded into culture dishes pre-coated with 1% agar in folate-free medium without LIF. The cells were subjected to suspension culture for 4 days, with half-medium replacement daily. By the end of this period, spherical cell aggregates (embryoid bodies, EBs) were visibly formed (designated as Day 4). The EBs were transferred to cell culture flasks pre-coated with 0.2% gelatin and further induced in folate-free medium supplemented with 1 µM retinoic acid (RA, Sigma, USA) for an additional 4 days to promote differentiation toward the neuroectodermal lineage (designated as Day 8). Forty milligrams per liter of folate concentration was added for folate supplementation rescue experiment from day 0. Cells harvested at Day 8 were subjected to ChIP-seq experiments.

ChIP, ChIP-seq, and ChIP-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments were performed according to the instructions of SimpleChIP® Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads) (#9005, CST). Formaldehyde cross-linked chromatin was obtained from about 5 × 107 NE4C cells. Cross-linked chromatin was immunoprecipitated with antibodies to GCM1 overnight at 4 °C. Normal rabbit IgG were used as negative control. Immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed by sequencing.

Gcm1 ChIP-seq libraries were prepared using the KAPA HTP Library Preparation Kit complemented with NEXTflex DNA Barcodes from Bioo Scientific and performed by Novogene Corporation (Beijing, China). Subsequently, pair-end sequencing of the sample was performed on the Illumina platform (Illumina, CA, USA). Library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. ChIP-seq libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq (150 bp PE). Quality control was performed using FastQC (v0.12.1) with a mean Q-score > 30 across all bases. The index of the reference genome was built using BWA (v 0.7.12) and clean reads were aligned to the mm10 (GRCm38) reference genome using BWA mem (v 0.7.12). Peaks were called using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1, q-value < 0.05) against input controls, thereby obtaining information on DNA segments that interact with Gcm1 across the entire genome.

ChIP-qPCR was performed using purified DNA and 2 × M5 HiPer Realtime PCR Super mix with Low Rox (SYBRgreen, with anti-Taq) (MF797-05, Mei5bio). Two percent of the DNA was taken out as input, and the quantification of the ChIP signal was calculated as a percentage of the input. The primer sequences used for ChIP-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

GO and KEGG Analysis

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment and Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation analyses were performed on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from microarray expression profiling and differentiate Gcm1 ChIP-seq peaks. GO enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.0.5). Significantly enriched GO terms (Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component) were identified with a threshold of adjusted p-value < 0.05 using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. The enrichment analysis was executed with the enriched GO function using the “org.Mm.eg.db” annotation database (version 3.19.1) for GRCm39 mouse genome assembly. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.0.5). Statistically significant pathways were selected based on an adjusted p-value < 0.05. The enriched KEGG function was employed with reference to the KEGG pathway database (release 106.0, October 2023). Results from both GO and KEGG analyses were visualized using bar plots to illustrate the most significantly enriched terms and pathways.

Small Interfering RNAs (siRNAs)

siRNA reagents for Gcm1 were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (USA). C57BL/6 mESCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in antibiotic-free medium. After 24 h, siRNA-Lipofectamine 3000 complexes were prepared by mixing 100 pM siRNA with 7.5 µL Lipofectamine 3000 in Opti-MEM (Gibco) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for downstream analyses.

Lentiviral Construction and Transduction

Four siRNA sequences targeting mouse CBP and a negative control sequence were purchased by Addgene with Cat. #193209. shRNAs were inserted into lentivirus vector containing a CMV promoter and a U6 promoter. All constructs were confirmed by sequence analysis. All recombinant lentiviruses were produced by co-transfecting 293 T cells with the help of Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to standard protocols. Virus titers, expressed as transducing units (TU) per milliliter, were determined by measuring GFP expression in 293 T cells that were transduced with serial dilutions of concentrated lentivirus. The titers were approximately 109 TU/ml. For transduction, C57 BL/6 mESC were infected with either NC siRNA lentivirus or CBP siRNA lentivirus at multiplicities of infection (MOI) of 100. After 48 h of transduction, the cells were harvested and prepared for subsequent studies.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) Assay

The Co-IP assay was performed with a kit from ThermoFisher (Cat. #26147, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein samples were carefully normalized prior to IP to ensure equal input across conditions. Specifically, the same number of cells from the control group and the low folate group were lysed under standardized conditions, and total protein concentrations were quantified before proceeding with IP. For immunoprecipitation, 4 µg of antibody Gcm1 (Santa Cruz, USA) was bound to Protein A/G plus agarose on a rotator at room temperature for 45 min, with IgG used as a negative control. Then crosslinking of the bound antibody with 450 µM disuccinimidyl suberate was performed on a rotator at room temperature for 50 min. Simultaneously preclearing of the nuclear protein using control agarose was performed on a rotator at rest at 4 °C for 50 min. The precleared nuclear protein was incubated with the antibody-crosslinked resin overnight at 4 °C to accomplish antigen immunoprecipitation. Antigen elution was then completed on the second day and the bound proteins were detected by western blotting.

Western Blotting

For C57BL/6 mESCs, add RIPA lysis buffer to extract total protein, or extract nuclear protein according to the instructions (C500009, Sangon). Determine the protein concentration using the BCA method. Protein samples were separated by 8–15% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked in 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight. The antibodies used are as follows: anti-H3 (1:3000, D1H2, CST), anti-GCM1 (1:500, sc-101173, Santa Cruz), anti-Lef1 (1:100, ab137872, Abcam), anti-β-Catenin (1:1000, 9562S, CST), anti-CBP(1:500, sc-7300, Santa Cruz), anti-H4-Ac (1:1000, 13534S, CST), and anti-Acetylated-Lysine Antibody (1:1000, 9441 s, CST).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRNzol (TIANGEN, China). Total RNA was then reverse transcribed using the 5 × All-In-One RT MasterMix (ABS-G490, ABM), and real-time PCR was performed using 2 × M5 HiPer Realtime PCR Super mix with Low Rox (SYBR green, with anti-Taq) (MF797-05, Mei5bio) on the QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR instrument. The relative mRNA expression level was calculated using the formula 2−ΔΔCt, with GAPDH as the reference gene. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Luciferase Assay

A total of 293 T cells were seeded at a density of 5.5 × 104 cells per well in 48-well plates (250 µL medium per well) 24 h prior to transfection. The cells were co-transfected with 0.15 µg of either luc-Int2-TBE7 or luc-Int2-TBE7-mut reporter plasmid, along with corresponding negative vectors, using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h of transfection, cells were harvested, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay System. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for each sample.

Immunostaining

Brain slices (5 µm) were baked at 65 °C for 2 h, immersed in xylene and gradient ethanol, and then washed with PBS. Acidic or alkaline antigen retrieval was performed 5 times (3 min each time) according to the primary antibody incubation.

Cell Immunofluorescence (IF)

After washing with PBS, the sections were blocked in 5% goat serum (containing 1% Triton-X-100) at room temperature for 1 h, incubated in anti-GCM1 (1:100, sc-101173, Santa), and anti-Lef1 (1:1000, ab137872, Abcam) at 4 ℃ overnight. The next day, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor® 594–labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:200, ZF-0516, ZSGB-BIO) and Alexa Fluor® 488–labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:200, ZF-0512, ZSGB-BIO) at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. Mount the sections with antifade mounting medium with DAPl (P0131-5 ml, Beyotime). Fluorescence signals were captured on a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany) and analyzed using ImageJ.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The 5-µm paraffin sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and washed with distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed using heat-mediated treatment with sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 5 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation in 3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 15 min. After being washed with PBS, the sections were blocked with 5% goat serum at room temperature for 20 min and then incubated with primary antibodies against Gcm1 (1:200, sc-101173, Santa Cruz) and Lef1 (1:200, ab137872, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. Following PBS washes, the sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (ab97023, 1:200, Abcam) and/or HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab97051, 1:200, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. After additional PBS washes, the signal was visualized using a diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (ZLI-9019, ZSGB-BIO) for 10 min at room temperature. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral balsam. Images were acquired using a standard bright-field optical microscope (Olympus). The integrated optical density (IOD) of target proteins was analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 9.0 software.

NanoString

Total RNA was isolated from human brain tissues following the manufacturer’s instructions (QIAGEN, USA). Gene transcript analysis with specific probes was conducted with the classical medulloblastoma (MB) subtyping gene CodeSet (NanoString Technologies, USA). Each reaction contained 500 ng of total RNA, together with hybridization, reporter and capture probes, in a total volume of 15 µl. The hybridization reactions were incubated at 65 °C for 18 h, and the complexes were then eluted and immobilized on the cartridge in the nCounter Digital Analyser for data collection. The raw counts were normalized to the internal transcript abundances for five reference genes, namely, ACTB, TUBB, CLTC, TBP, and POLR1B. Quality control and analysis of the raw NanoString data were conducted with nSolver Analysis Software v4.0 (NanoString Technologies, USA). Student’s t test was used to compare normalized expression values between normal control samples and NTD samples.

Human Samples

All clinical samples used in this study were obtained from the city of Lvliang in Shanxi Province in northern China with informed consent from the parents. The enrolled pregnant women were diagnosed by trained clinicians via ultrasonography. The surgical procedures were performed as previously described [32]. The epidemiological method was described in detail in our previous publication [33]. NTD samples were matched with controls by gestational age with paired samples. The Ethics Board of the Shanxi Medical University approved the study protocol. The information about the clinical samples is listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of all continuous variables. For the normality data, data were expressed as means ± SEM, and unpaired Student’s two-tailed t test was used when comparing two groups. Comparisons among multiple groups were examined by one-way ANOVA plus post hoc test. For the nonnormal distribution data, they were expressed as medians (interquartile ranges) and were analyzed via the Wilcoxon Mann–Whitney test between two groups and the Kruskal–Wallis non-parametric test with post hoc Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. For categorical variables, χ2 test or Fisher exact probability test was used for the comparisons between groups. Correlation was assessed by Pearson correlation. ImageJ software was used for the quantification of western blot bands. Statistically significant differences are indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

H4 Acetylation Enrichment at the Gcm1 Promoter in mESC Without Folate Supplementation

The underlying mechanisms of Gcm1, which has been previously reported to be associated with NTDs, remain poorly understood. Our studies confirmed that Gcm1 expression is significantly upregulated in both low-folate NTD animal models and NTD samples [25]. To explore the underlying mechanisms, we first performed transcriptomic profiling of C57BL/6 mESCs without folate supplementation. As shown in Fig. 1A, Gcm1 expression was significantly elevated. Heatmap analysis further revealed that the expression of genes associated with germ layer development, such as neuroectodermal markers (Sox1, Otx2, Pax6, and Sox9) [34–38], was markedly suppressed in mESCs without folate supplementation. In contrast, the expression of genes related to mesendodermal development, such as Foxa2 and Brachyury [39, 40], was upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes highlighted their significant involvement in developmental processes (Fig. 1B). These results suggest that lack of folate alters the gene expression profile of C57BL/6 mESCs.

To validate the transcriptomic findings, we conducted RT–qPCR and WB experiments using C57BL/6 mESCs with or without folate supplementation. Gcm1 mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated in mESCs without folate supplementation (Fig. 1C and D). Folate deficiency has been reported to influence metabolic pathways, such as reducing acetyl-CoA synthesis, thereby affecting histone acetylation [41, 42]. To determine whether folate deficiency modulates gene expression and function through histone acetylation, we performed ChIP–qPCR to assess H4 acetylation (H4-Ac) enrichment at the Gcm1 promoter region, which is associated with active gene transcription [43]. Compared to the folate-sufficient control group, a significant increase in H4-Ac enrichment at the Gcm1 promoter was detected in C57BL/6 mESCs without folate supplementation (Fig. 1E). To investigate the mechanism linking folate-dependent epigenetic regulation and germ layer specification, we differentiated C57BL/6 mESCs into a neural ectodermal lineage model with or without folate supplementation (Supplementary Fig. 1B). RT–qPCR showed that the neuroectoderm markers Sox2 and Nestin decreased in mESCs without folate supplementation; furthermore, the downregulation of these neuroectoderm genes was rescued by folate supplementation (Fig. 1F). Interestingly, Gcm1 mRNA was increased in mESCs without folate supplementation but decreased with folate supplementation rescue (Fig. 1F). We also performed H4-Ac ChIP-qPCR in Gcm1 under mESC differentiation into a neural ectodermal lineage model without folate supplementation. The results showed a significant increase in H4-Ac enrichment in the Gcm1 promoter in the mESC differentiated model without folate supplementation, while folate supplementation rescued the H4-Ac enrichment in the Gcm1 promoter (Fig. 1G). Collectively, all these findings suggest that folate deficiency upregulates Gcm1 expression by increasing H4-Ac at its promoter region, which indicates that epigenetic upregulation of Gcm1 with folate deficiency maybe contributing to germ-layer specification in development.

CBP-Mediated H4 Acetylation Drives Gcm1 Upregulation in mESC Without Folate Supplementation

To elucidate the potential function of H4-Ac enrichment at the Gcm1 promoter, we performed Co-IP experiments. Interestingly, the protein levels of H4-Ac and its binding to Gcm1 significantly increased in mESC without folate supplementation compared to the folate-sufficient control group (Fig. 2A). Considering that CBP mediates Gcm1 stability, we next investigated the interaction between Gcm1 and CBP, which is also responsible for H4 acetylation. As shown in Fig. 2B, folate deficiency led to elevated CBP protein levels and increased binding between CBP and Gcm1, implying that elevated CBP likely mediated the increase in H4-Ac in mESC without folate supplementation.

To validate the role of CBP in mESC without folate supplementation, we performed lentivirus-mediated CBP knockdown in mESC without folate supplementation. Compared to the folate-sufficient control group, Co-IP assays revealed that CBP knockdown resulted in reduced acetylation and Gcm1 levels, as well as decreased CBP, Gcm1, and acetylation binding in mESC without folate supplementation (Fig. 2C). This also further implies that there was a decreased H4-Ac after CBP was knocked down. We further utilized CBP-30, a small-molecule inhibitor of CBP[44], to pharmacologically inhibit CBP activity in mESC without folate supplementation. Treatment with CBP-30 significantly suppressed CBP expression under folate deficiency, accompanied by a marked reduction in Gcm1 and H4-Ac levels (Fig. 2D). However, compared with the folate-sufficient control group, CBP-30 treatment led to a significant decrease in CBP, Gcm1, and H4-Ac levels, as well as reduced Gcm1 and H4-Ac binding (Fig. 2E). Collectively, these findings indicate that CBP, as a histone acetyltransferase, mediates the acetylation of H4 and its interaction with Gcm1, ultimately leading to the upregulation of Gcm1 expression in mESC without folate supplementation.

Gcm1 Regulates Lef1 Expression Under Folate-Deficient Conditions

To investigate the upregulated Gcm1 role in NTDs, we first analyzed 568 reported NTDs candidate genes [45]. Interestingly, pathway analysis on these 568 genes revealed that both Wnt signaling pathway and Notch signaling pathway and folate metabolism were enriched in different databases from KEGG and PANTHER separately (Fig. 3A and B). To identify downstream targets of Gcm1 under folate-deficient conditions, we performed ChIP-seq for Gcm1 in mESC without folate supplementation. Genome-wide analysis of Gcm1 binding revealed profound redistribution under folate deficiency (Fig. 3C). GO analysis also revealed the genes regulated by Gcm1 focusing on single-multicellular or multicellular organism processes, development processes, and so on (Fig. 3D). We then conducted a Venn diagram analysis to screen target genes of Gcm1. We compared genes from Gcm1 enriched genes in mESC without folate supplementation with ChIP-seq and validated NTDs candidate genes in human NTDs fetus brain with NanoString and NTDs candidate genes from GeneCards. The results revealed 17 candidate genes that may be regulated by Gcm1 without folate supplementation (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Table S4, S5, and S6). Furthermore, pathway analysis revealed that these overlapped differentially expressed genes were associated with development-related signaling pathways, including the Hippo and Wnt pathways (Fig. 3F). Collectively, all these analyses again revealed that there is a crosstalk between Gcm1 and Wnt pathways in folate-deficient NTDs.

Integrating the above findings, among the 17 overlapping genes, we selected Lef1 and hypothesized that Lef1 acts as a downstream effector of Gcm1, which is a target gene of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and serves as the nuclear endpoint of canonical Wnt/β-catenin in embryo development and brain development processes [23, 46, 47]. Consistent with Gcm1 RNA level upregulation in mESC without folate supplementation, Lef1 mRNA expression was also markedly upregulated (Fig. 4A). In addition, IGV visualization by MACS2 showed broad and diffuse enriched Gcm1 peaks around the Lef1 promoter region without folate supplementation (Fig. 4B). Also, we performed ChIP‒qPCR experiments to confirm this broad and diffuse binding between Gcm1 and Lef1. The results showed that Gcm1 could bind to the Lef1 promoter in four genomic binding locations; both of them were significantly enhanced in mESC without folate supplementation (Fig. 4C). All these results suggest the Gcm1-Lef1 regulatory interactions with folate deficiency. To further validate the regulatory relationship between Gcm1 and Lef1, we knocked down Gcm1 in mESC without folate supplementation and observed a significant reduction in Lef1 expression (Fig. 4D). Conversely, Gcm1 overexpression in mESC with folate supplementation led to a pronounced increase in Lef1 expression (Fig. 4E and 4), confirming that folate status modulates the Gcm1-dependent regulation of Lef1 and Lef1 may act as a target gene of Gcm1. In addition, it was also reported that Gcm1 was identified as a target of the β-catenin/TCF complex through a region containing TCF-binding elements (TBEs) [11]. Compared with human GCM1 protein, TBE in mouse Gcm1 was declined (Fig. 4H). We then detected Gcm1 binding with β-catenin in four TBEs with ChIP-qPCR; the result showed that there was an increased binding in TBE7 with folate deficiency (Fig. 4G). Then, a mutated TBE7 construct showed significantly lower reporter activity by luciferase assay (F4g. 4I). These results further suggest a link between Gcm1 and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

Upregulation of Gcm1 and Lef1 Expression in a Low-Folate NTD Model

To elucidate the role of the Gcm1-mediated regulation of Lef1 in NTDs, we established a low-folate-induced NTD mouse model. Embryos were dissected at E9.5, a critical stage of neural tube development [48], and a morphology analysis revealed significant defects, such as craniorachischisis and spina bifida, in the low-folate group (Fig. 5A). WB analysis of brain and spine samples at E9.5d also revealed a significant increase in the protein levels of Gcm1 and Lef1 (Fig. 5B). Specifically, compared to the control group from E9.5d, immunohistochemical staining for Gcm1 and Lef1 revealed a marked increase in both proteins in the E9.5d NTDs group (Fig. 5C). At last, these findings were validated by immunofluorescence analyses of E9.5d embryos, which confirmed the increased expression of Gcm1 and Lef1 in the affected brain tissues (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that in low-folate NTD mouse embryos, Gcm1 is highly expressed and regulates a network of downstream genes, including Lef1, which likely contributes to the pathogenesis of NTDs.

Gcm1 Is Correlated with Lef1 in Low-Folate NTD Human Samples

We validated our findings using low-folate NTD samples. We analyzed brain tissues from 12 pairs of low-folate NTD patients and gestational age-matched normal controls. Folate content analysis confirmed significantly lower folate levels in the brain tissues of NTD patients than in those of controls (Fig. 6A). Using NanoString, we quantified the mRNA levels of Gcm1 and Lef1 in the tissues. The results revealed the marked upregulation of both Gcm1 and Lef1 expression in low-folate NTD brain tissues (Fig. 6B). Correlation analysis revealed a positive relationship between the mRNA expression levels of Lef1 and Gcm1 under low-folate conditions (Fig. 6C). Western blot analysis of the 12 paired brain tissues confirmed elevated Gcm1 protein levels in the low-folate NTD group (Fig. 6D). Additionally, Lef1 protein expression was assessed in six of the pairs, revealing a significant increase in the low-folate NTD group (Fig. 6E). These results indicate that elevated Gcm1 expression in low-folate NTDs may exert pathological effects through the regulation of downstream Lef1.

Discussion

Although elevated Gcm1 expression has been implicated in NTDs, the precise mechanisms underlying this association remain poorly understood. Folate, a critical molecule in the one-carbon metabolism pathway, primarily provides methyl groups for DNA synthesis [49]. Folate deficiency disrupts one-carbon metabolism, leading to reduced S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) synthesis, which in turn decreases DNA and histone methylation, thereby altering gene expression [49, 50]. Given the reported dynamic balance between histone methylation and acetylation [51, 52], we investigated the role of histone acetylation in the upregulation of Gcm1 expression without folate supplementation. Our findings revealed that folate deficiency enhanced H4 acetylation (H4-Ac) enrichment at the Gcm1 promoter region, a process mediated by the histone acetyltransferase CBP. Also, in the folate-deficient mESC differentiation process, an increased H4-Ac enrichment in the Gcm1 promoter was observed, and this enrichment was rescued by folate supplementation. At last, we also observed disruption of Gcm1 mRNA and opposite neuroectoderm marker genes Nestin and Sox2 mRNA in the mESC differentiation process without folate supplementation. With folate supplementation, the Nestin mRNA and Sox2 mRNA were rescued. All these results suggest folate deficiency upregulates Gcm1 expression by increasing H4 acetylation at its promoter region and then influences germ layer development.

To screen the target gene of Gcm1 in folate-deficient conditions, by using genes enriched in mESC without folate supplementation from ChIP-seq and NTDs candidate genes with or without validation, we revealed that Lef1 may be a potential downstream target of Gcm1. Interestingly, IGV visualization by MACS2 showed diffuse and broad enriched Gcm1 peaks around the Lef1 promoter region without folate supplementation. This broad binding pattern was also validated by ChIP–qPCR across four promoter regions in Lef1. This indicates that folate deficiency potentiates Gcm1 binding at all four Lef1 promoter sites which reveals a previously recognized relationship between Wnt signaling and Gcm1. Then, in both low-folate NTD mouse models and clinical brain tissue samples, we observed elevated Gcm1 and Lef1 expression, with a positive correlation between their expression levels. These results indicate that Gcm1, the expression of which is upregulated under low folate conditions, plays a critical role in NTDs by regulating downstream Lef1. Our study provides novel insights into the pathogenic mechanisms of Gcm1 overexpression in folate-deficient NTDs and highlights clinically relevant folate supplementation in regulating Gcm1 as a potential NTDs intervention strategy, and our findings suggest targeted gene editing of the Gcm1-Lef1 axis could restore neurological function.

Gcm1 is a key transcription factor involved in various developmental processes, including placental development, neurodevelopment, and immune regulation [6, 8, 53]. Notably, high maternal folic acid intake can lead to aberrant gene expression and chromatin accessibility, particularly in maturing excitatory neurons of the dentate gyrus [54]. We propose that folate deficiency impairs one-carbon metabolism, reducing S-adenosylmethionine and causing genomic hypomethylation. This epigenetic alteration likely enhances chromatin accessibility and redistributes Gcm1 binding, possibly through additional modulation by folate-sensitive cofactors. In addition, Gcm1 acts as an upstream negative regulator of WNT signaling by activating NOTUM, and this repression is essential for proper extravillous trophoblasts differentiation [55]. Our previous studies also revealed that Gcm1 forms a complex with β-catenin and Tcf4 to modulate Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity under folate-deficient conditions. Furthermore, Lef1, a downstream effector of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, plays critical roles in embryonic development, cell fate determination, and immunity homeostasis [56–58]. For example, Lef1 is known to control neuronal differentiation and maturation by modulating downstream target genes such as NeuroD1 and Sox2 [59, 60]. Our results indicate that Lef1 expression is significantly upregulated under low-folate conditions; however, whether Lef1 expression is directly regulated by Gcm1 remains unclear. Using ChIP and ChIP–qPCR experiments, we demonstrated folate deficiency potentiates Gcm1 binding at Lef1 promoter sites, which reveals a previously recognized relationship between Wnt signaling and Gcm1 [6].

It has been established that mutation of the Gcm1 gene in mice results in placental dysfunction and lethality by embryonic day 10 [61]. Similarly, homozygous mutation of the Lef1 gene, leading to reduced protein expression, causes defects in tooth, mammary gland, vibrissae, and hair follicle development, culminating in postnatal lethality [62]. These findings underscore the critical roles of Gcm1 and Lef1 expression for normal murine development. Conversely, the impact of Gcm1 or Lef1 overexpression on the development, and the mechanisms involved, demands further investigation. Herein, our study revealed that both low-folate NTD mouse models and NTD human samples, the elevated Gcm1 and Lef1 expression and a positive correlation between their expression levels, suggest that dysregulated Gcm1 overexpression induces NTDs via Lef1-mediated Wnt signaling. Our result is also coordinated with ectopic expression of Gcm1 inducing an inhibition of neuropore closure and a stimulation of secondary neurulation [14]. Collectively, all these experimental results support the hypothesis that Gcm1 overexpression, under low folate conditions, targets Lef1 to disrupt neurodevelopmental defects.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not fully explore the functional consequences of the Gcm1-mediated regulation of Lef1 in the context of abnormalities in neural tube development. In future studies, additional experiments using low-folate NTD mouse models, specifically in the neural tube developing stage in E7.5–E10.5, such as adenovirus-mediated Gcm1 overexpression, should be conducted to validate the regulatory relationship between Gcm1 and Lef1. Second, the specific structural domains through which CBP interacts with Gcm1 have not yet been conclusively identified. Further experimental studies are needed to elucidate the precise regions involved in their binding interactions. Finally, owing to the limited availability of clinical samples, we were only able to analyze a subset of tissues for Lef1 expression. Moreover, the regulatory relationship between Gcm1 and Lef1 was based primarily on correlations, and thus, more direct evidence of this regulation should be sought using clinical samples to strengthen our conclusions.

Conclusions

In summary, our study revealed that in folate-deficient NTDs, upregulated Gcm1 exerts its effects by modulating the downstream factor Lef1. These findings provide novel evidence for the mechanism underlying the occurrence of NTDs due to Gcm1 overexpression, which was previously unclear. Under conditions of low folate availability, elevated Gcm1 levels may influence the functionality of neuronal differentiation processes through the regulation of Lef1, thereby leading to abnormal neural development. Our research thus identified a new potential therapeutic target for the treatment of NTDs.
