Suicidal behaviors among sexual gender-based violence victims in one-stop centers, Northeast Ethiopia
Authors: Muluken Girma Kebede, Sisay Haile, Kaleab Mesfin Abera, Zelalem Birhan, Mulat Awoke Kassa, Mamaru Melkam, Nigusie Abebaw, Tamrat Anbesaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVictims of Sexual Gender-Based Violence reported to have a higher prevalence of suicidal behaviors. This study aims to assess the prevalence of suicidal behaviors among Sexual Gender-Based Violence servivors (SGBVs) attending One-stop in North East, Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 321 victims at three one-stop centers in Northeast Ethiopia from December 1/2022 and January 30/2023. The study participants were selected using a systematic random sampling technique during the study period. Suicidal behavior was measured by the Suicidal Behavior Revised (SBQ-R). In addition, the Oslo Social Support Scale (OSSS-3) was used to measure social support, and the patient health questionnaire(PHQ-9) to measure depression. The data were entered into Epi data version 4.6 and analyzed using SPSS version 26. Binary logistic regression was carried out to identify independent predictors of suicidal behavior at a 95% confidence level. Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with a P-value < 0.05 and 95% CI were used for interpretation of the result.ResultsThe study showed the overall prevalence of suicidal behavior among SGBVs was 26.2% (95% CI (21.4, 31.3). The prevalence of suicidal ideation, plan, and attempts were 18.7% (95% CI (14.6, 23.4), 15.3% (95% CI (11.5, 19.7), and 4.7% (95% CI (2.6, 7.6) respectively. This study showed that depression [AOR = 8.08;95% CI (4.34,15.07)], family history of suicide [AOR = 2.27; 95% CI (1.05, 5.89)], and poor social support [(AOR = 4.79, 95% CI (1.05, 10.84)] were found to be significantly associated suicidal behavior among SGBVs-female victims.ConclusionSuicidal behavior among SGBVs is 26%. This finding implies in Sexual Gender-Based violence victims there might be a deconstructed social system or social support system that is not well implemented. As well as for victims attending One-stop centers effectiveness of timely assessment and aggressive interventions for factors that can lead to suicidal behaviors, and depression should be implemented.Keywords: Suicidal behavior; Sexual-gender-based violence; One-stop centers; North East, Ethiopia

Introduction

Suicide is intentionally ending one’s own life [1]. Ideation, planning, and attempted suicide are examples of suicidal behaviors, which are thoughts and actions directed toward ending one’s life [2], “gender-based violence” describes acts of aggression directed towards people because of their gender identities as determined by society. A severe kind of gender-based violence, sexual assault has a profound impact on the victims’ mental and physical health [3, 4].

Suicide has a global burden of about 900,000 people, and it is expected to reach 1.5 million by 2020, which accounts for 1.4% of all fatalities worldwide. Currently, suicide is the second leading cause of death among young people aged 15–29 years at the global level. Overall, suicide rates in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) are lower than the rates in high-income countries (HIC) of 11.2 per 100,000 compared with 12.7 per 100,000 populations, but the majority of suicide deaths worldwide occur in LMICs [5, 6].

Globally, gender-based violence (GBV) is a serious threat to human rights and public health [7]. More than 250,000 rape cases are reported annually worldwide, even if embarrassment, the conviction that there is no use in reporting, and the perception that violence is a common part of life shield women from reporting SGBV in developing nations [8]. Since it has terrible short- and long-term health effects on victims, sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) is a public health and human rights issue for many nations, as well as for institutions within nations like the United Nations [9].

It has been discovered that both man-made and natural disasters, such as war, raise the occurrence of SGBV by increasing the victim’s susceptibility to violence. History has witnessed the use of sexual violence and rape as a weapon of war, from the Japanese occupation of China and Korea during World War II to the civil conflicts in Rwanda, Uganda, and Liberia. Between 20,000 and 50,000 women—roughly half of all prewar girls—were raped in Bosnia-Herzegovina between 1992 and 1995 [10]. It was suggested that there are both direct and indirect effects of armed conflict on the occurrence of sexual violence. It leads to sexual abuse by the opposing forces; second, it can increase their susceptibility to sexual abuse by militarizing post-conflict areas, creating resource scarcity in the area, fostering patriarchal beliefs in the community, and, lastly, by softening their important support system through death [11, 12].

Suicidal ideation and attempts among SGBVs are estimated to be between 29% and 63% of cases. Suicidal behaviors are more common among SGBVs when there is a history of mental illness and the violence’s impact on mental health, when the victim is a woman when the perpetrator is identified, when the violence is intense and repetitive, and when there is insufficient social support from the victim’s family and community [13]. In Rwanda’s genocide, sexual violence was a premeditated war strategy that resulted in the assault of between 100,000 and 250,000 women over three months, and it was classified as a “war crime.” The impact of rape on generations is shown, and it is now viewed as a weapon of war during armed conflict [14]. Suicidal behaviors are more common among SGBVs when there is a history of mental illness and the violence’s impact on mental health, when the victim is a woman when the perpetrator is identified, when the violence is intense and repetitive, and when there is insufficient social support from the victim’s family and community [13, 15, 16]. Also, the interpersonal-psychological theory of suicide, which explains suicidal behaviour through acquired competence, thwarted belongingness, and perceived burdensomeness, serves as the support for the association of SGBV and suicidality. Additionally, trauma theory emphasises how being exposed to sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) impairs psychological processes and raises the risk of suicide. Particularly in environments where there is conflict, these frameworks aid in placing suicidal behaviours among SGBV victims in context [17].

Among SGBVs suicidal ideation was found in 43%, 63.2%, and 33% in Mexico, Turkey, and Canada. Suicidal attempts were found in 24.5% and 13.2% of the same population in Turkey and Canada [16, 18, 19]. Victims of gender-based violence typically have terrible short- and long-term effects on their physical and emotional well-being. It is possible for women and girls to sustain serious physical injuries, become pregnant against their will, or come into contact with HIV or other STDs. Therefore, it is vital to comprehend the frequency and contributing causes of suicide behaviors in sexual gender-based violence in Ethiopia’s post-conflict areas in order to enhance the mental health of those who have experienced such violence. In 2021, a severe conflict in Northern Ethiopia between the National Defense Forces (ENDF) and the Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF) caused widespread death, injury, and trauma, displacing nearly 2 million people and increasing their risk of psychological disorders, especially suicide [20]. Previous suicide behaviors, depression, and cognitive variables (such as an external locus of control, hopelessness, and poor problem-solving) were found to be substantially linked factors for post-assault suicidal behaviors in various studies [13]. According to a Turkish study, living alone, being penetrated by family members, experiencing abuse together with coercion and physical harm, and ongoing exposure to abuse are all linked to a higher incidence of suicidal behaviors [16]. Despite the burden, studies on suicidality among gender-based violence victims in Ethiopia are limited; this study aims to assess its prevalence and related factors in war-affected areas.

Method and materials

Study design, study area

This study assesses suicidal behavior among SGBV victims on follow-up at one-stop centers in three war-affected zones in the Amhara region of Ethiopia. An institutional-based cross-sectional study was conducted between December 1/2022 and January 30/2023 in the South Wollo, North Wollo, and Waghimra zones. According to the 2007 Census by the Central Statistical Agency of Ethiopia (CSA), these zones have populations of 2,518,862, 1,500,303, and 418,817, respectively. The areas covered are 17,067.45 km² for South Wollo, 12,172 km² for North Wollo, and 9,039 km² for Waghimra. Dessie, Woldia, and Sekota, the capital cities of these zones, are located 400 km, 503 km, and 720 km from Addis Ababa, respectively [21]. Each zone has one government-owned one-stop center in its capital: Dessie Referral Hospital, Woldia Specialized Hospital in Weldia, and Tefera Hailu Memorial General Hospital in Sekota. All one-stop centers have multidisciplinary professionals, including social workers, counselors, general practitioners, nurses, and psychiatrists. According to the Amhara Public Health Institute (APHI), at the start of the study, 700, 200, and 300 victims of sexual and gender-based violence were attending one-stop centers in South Wollo, North Wollo, and Waghimra zones, respectively.

Study population

Source population

All SGBV victims above 15 years of age attending one-stop centers.

Study population

All 15 years and above sexual gender-based violence victims (SGBV) attending one-stop centers in selected areas during the data collection period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

SGBV victims aged 15 and above attending one-stop centers were included.

Exclusion criteria

SGBV victims, who were severely ill and who could not communicate were excluded from the study.

Sampling technique

The final sample size was proportionally allocated across the three zones in the Amhara region based on population size: South Wollo (187 participants), North Wollo (54 participants), and Waghimra (80 participants). Proportional allocation was used for each selected one-stop center, and participants were chosen using a systematic random sampling technique.

Data collection method and instruments

Data were collected through an interviewer-administered questionnaire with different sections: socio-demographic factors, clinical factors, and psychosocial factors. This questionnaire was developed following an extensive review of relevant literature and existing study tools. The four-item Suicidal Behavior Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R) was used to assess suicidal behavior in sexual assault victims. Item 1 assesses lifetime suicidal ideation and attempts (4 points), item 2 evaluates the frequency of suicidal ideation over the past year (5 points), item 3 describes the threat of suicidal behavior (3 points), and item 4 measures the self-reported future probability of suicidal behavior (6 points). The SBQ-R has a sensitivity of 80% and a specificity of 91%, with scores ranging from 3 to 18. A cutoff score of 8 or above was used to determine the presence of suicidal behavior in the clinical population [22]. Additionally, responses to question number one were categorized into suicidal ideation, plan/intent, and attempt for discussion purposes. Suicidal ideation was identified if the respondent answered “yes” to having ever thought or briefly considered committing suicide [23, 24]. A suicidal plan/intent was identified if the respondent had ever planned to kill themselves. The suicidal attempt was defined as if the respondent had ever actually attempted to end their life [24]. Cronbach’s alpha score of (SBQ-R) was found to be 0.88. Depressive symptoms among SGBV victims were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), a nine-item tool with a total score ranging from 0 to 27. Each item is rated from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). A total score of 10 or higher indicates depression [25]. The PHQ-9 has been validated in the Ethiopian population with a sensitivity of 86% and a specificity of 67% [26]. Its validity and reliability have been established for use in community studies across several countries [27]. In this study, the PHQ-9 demonstrated an internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.81. The Oslo Social Support Scale (OSSS-3) is a measure consisting of 3 items that assess social support. Scores range from 3 to 14, with higher scores indicating stronger social support and lower scores reflecting lower social support. The scores are categorized into three levels: poor social support [3–8], moderate social support [9–11], and strong social support [12–14, 28]. Socio-demographic factors, history of mental illness, family mental illness and suicide, chronic diseases such as hypertension, and trauma-related factors were assessed using “yes/no” response questionnaires, with their operationalization based on various literature sources.

Operational definitions

Sexual gender based violence victims (SGBVs)

Individuals identified by the clinician as victims of non-consensual sexual contact who were unable to consent or refuse [7].

One-stop center (OSC)

A facility that provides integrated, multi-disciplinary services for SGBVs in a single physical location [29].

Data collection procedures

The English version of the questionnaire was first translated into Amharic, the local language, and then back-translated into English by an independent translator to ensure consistency and clarity. Standard tools were used to determine the outcome variables, and data collectors and supervisors received a two-day training on the study’s purpose, data collection methods, sampling techniques, and ethical issues, including confidentiality. A pretest with 16 participants was conducted at Tefera Hailu Memorial General Hospital in Sekota to assess the reliability and validity of the questionnaire, evaluate question clarity, estimate completion time, gauge respondent comfort, and gather feedback. During the data collection period, the principal investigator and supervisors conducted regular supervision, checking the collected data daily for completeness and consistency. All completed questionnaires were collected, tallied, and summarized for data interpretation, providing the researcher with clear and detailed information on the study topic.

Data analysis

The collected data was checked, coded, and entered into Epi-data version 4.6 and exported to SPSS version 26 for cleaning and analysis. Descriptive and summary statistics were used to explain the study population concerning the relevant variable. In simple binary logistic regression analysis, variables with a p-value < 0.25 were considered candidates for multivariate logistic regressions to establish the variables that were independently associated with suicidal behavior. In multivariate logistic regression analysis, statistical significance was declared at 95% CI with a p-value < 0.05. Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with a p-value < 0.05 and 95% CI reported an association between the dependent variable and the independent variables. Overall model fitness was checked with the Hosmer and Lemeshow test, and the model was fit with a p-value of > 0.05. Finally, the results of the study were presented in tables, graphs, and narrative descriptions. Tolerance and variance inflation factors were checked to test multicollinearity (maximum VIF = 2.2) or to see the unique effect of predictors on outcome variables.

Factors associated with suicidal-behavior

Before multivariable logistic regression, binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify candidate variables for multiple logistic regression. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 such as marital status, living arrangement, educational level, occupational status, residence, family history of mental illness, family suicidal history, mental illness history, chronic medical illness, depression, and social support were considered as candidate variables for the final analysis. Depression, a family history of suicide, and poor social support were variables found to be statistically associated with suicidal behavior at a p-value < 0.05.

Those SGBV victims with depression were 8 times more likely to have suicidal behavior than their counterparts [AOR = 8.08;95% CI (4.34,15.07)]. Likewise, participants who had a family history of suicide were 2.27 times more likely to have suicidal behavior as compared with SGBV victims who had no family suicidal history[AOR = 2.27; 95% CI (1.05, 5.89)]. The odds of having suicidal behavior among SGBVs victim females with poor social support were 4.79 times more likely to have suicidal behavior as compared with those female victims with strong social support [(AOR = 4.79, 95% CI = 1.05, 10.84)] (Table 5).

Discussion

The first objective of the present study was to determine the prevalence of suicidal behavior and its associated factors among females who experienced sexual gender-based violence (SGBV) in war-affected areas of North East Ethiopia. Guided by the Interpersonal-Psychological Theory of Suicide (IPTS), which posits that suicidal behavior results from perceived burdensomeness, thwarted belongingness, and acquired capability for self-harm. The findings revealed a high burden of suicidal behavior, with over a quarter of participants affected. Suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts were also notably prevalent. The use of IPTS helps to contextualize these behaviors within the broader psychological and social impacts of war and gender-based violence, offering insight into the mechanisms through which trauma may lead to suicidality. The magnitude of suicidal ideation among sexual gender-based violence victims in this study was in line with other studies done in the USA (22.8%) [30], and Australia (29%) [31]. The prevalence of suicidal ideation among SGBV in this study is in line with a study done in Nigeria, which was 15% [32]. On the other hand, it was found to be higher than 9.09% of suicidal ideation in Nigeria and Turkey (9.5%) [33]. The possible explanation might be explained by the different study designs utilized by Esere et al. [34], and suicidal ideation was among married women experiencing intimate partner violence in Türkiye [33]. Also, the differences between studies could be explained by variations in how suicidal behavior is defined and assessed, the age distribution of the study population, and the cultural context is another possible variation. However, the total prevalence of suicidal behavior in this study is found to be lower than in a study done in Ghana (38.7%) [35], and Congo (34.2%) [36]. A possible explanation might be specific adolescent groups participated in Ghana [35], and the participants in the Democratic Republic of Congo were raising children born from sexual violence as well as the respondent-driven sampling procedure was utilized [36].

Suicidal ideation was found to be lower than in some other studies done in the USA (35%) [37], Nigeria (40.8%) [35], and Ghana (38.7%) [38]. The possible reason behind the differences in prevalence was due to survey methods and different tools used to assess suicidal behavior [39]. Also, the national sample and victims included were those having both childhood and adulthood in the United States of America [37]. Additionally, the discrepancy may be due to a study population included in Ghana and Nigeria being only adolescents. The prevalence of suicidal attempts in this study is in line with a previous cross-national study done among women in Turkiye, which reported 4.8% [33], and wth findings from high school sexual violence victims in Ethiopia, which reported 6% [40].

Regarding the associated factors, this study has shown that the odds of having suicidal behaviors were 8.08 times higher among SGBVs with depression as compared to SGBVs with undepressed victims. This finding was supported by a study conducted in the USA [17] and Turkey [16]. As studied well in different literature surviving sexual abuse can cause individuals severe depressive symptoms like loss of interest in pleasurable activities, hopelessness, worthlessness, and inability to see a bright future after their experience. In another way, depressed people have lower levels of the neurotransmitter serotonin in their brains, which can lead to suicidal behavior [41]. In another study, severe depressive symptoms and guilty feelings about the trauma incident were studied to increase suicidal behavior among suicidal than non-suicidal trauma victims. This finding aligns with the Stress-Diathesis Model of Suicide, which suggests that suicidal behavior results from an interaction between preexisting vulnerability (diathesis) and stressful life events (stressors) [42].

Similarly, those SGBV victims who had a family history of suicide were about 2.27 times more likely to have suicidal behavior than their counterparts. This finding is supported by a study conducted in different settings [43]. This may be explained by the potential contribution of genetics, the psychological support system within the family, and the dynamics of the pregnancy to the development of suicidal behavior [44]. Theoretical studies suggest that individuals with a family history of the disorder are more genetically susceptible and therefore require more, or more severe, events to precipitate depression compared to those who are less vulnerable [45].

Finally, poor social support was one of the predictors of suicidal behavior in this study. The odds of having suicidal behavior among SGBVs with poor social support were 4.79 times higher as compared to SGBVs with strong social support. This study is consistent with a study done in the USA [46], and Uganda [47]. This can be due to the debilitating social consequences the survivor passes through in a country like Ethiopia. Starting from separation from her husband sexual assault victims can experience discrimination in society, verbal abuse due to having a child of the perpetrator, and being a symbol of defeat in the community she lives. This makes the victims lose social support when they need it urgently in the post-war community, which on the other hand increases suicidal wishes and behaviors. Furthermore, associated with victims low perceived social support, dissatisfaction with one’s life, and a feeling of worthlessness may increase the risk for suicidal behaviour [48].

Limitations of the study

This study determines suicidal behaviors and associated factors among sexual Gender-Based Violence victims attending one-stop centers in war-affected areas in the North-East, Ethiopia. Although it provide important baseline data, it also has some limitations encountered. First, the retrospective nature of suicidal behaviour and early adversities, recall bias may have occurred. Second, because of data were collected through an interviewer-administered method, social desirability biases may have led participants to either over-reporting or underreporting information. Third, the use of culturally non-validated tools might have influenced the result of findings. Fourth, the cross sectional design limits the ability to establish causal association between suicidal behavior and associated factors. Finally, since the study was conducted among SGBV survivors attending one-stop centers in specific war-affected zones, the findings may not be generalizable to all SGBV servivors in Ethiopia. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insight on suicidality among SGBV survivors and highlights the urgent need for targeted mental health and social support interventions. Conclusions

The current study found that more than one in four victims of Sexual Gender-Based Violence (SGBV) attending one-stop centers war-affected in North East Ethiopia experienced suicidal behavior. Depression, a family history of suicide, and poor social support were significantly associated with suicidal behavior in this population. These findings provide the urgent need of mental health screening, integrated psychosocial support within one-stop centers, as well as early interventions to adress depression and strengthening social support systems for SGBV suvivors. Although, a great attention should be pay to particular to early, culturally appropriate interventions, and addressing complex factors contributing of suicidal behavior among SGBV survivors.

Implications

Studying suicidal behaviors among sexual gender-based violence (SGBV) victims at one-stop centers in war-affected areas has significant implications for enhancing mental health support. It highlights the urgent need for specialized interventions and training for healthcare professionals to effectively address these issues. Findings can inform policy development, ensuring that resources are appropriately allocated and that support services are tailored to the unique needs of victimsvictims in conflict zones. Additionally, raising awareness and reducing stigma can improve community support and encourage survivors to seek help. Overall, this research underscores the importance of targeted mental health strategies and resource allocation to better support and protect vulnerable populations.
