Effects of imeglimin on experimental diabetic neuropathy in streptozotocin‐induced diabetic rats
Authors: Wataru Nihei, Ayako Kato, Takuma Sato, Masahiro Yamaguchi, Tatsuhito Himeno, Nobuhisa Nakamura, Kazunori Sango, Keiko Naruse, Jiro Nakamura, Hideki Kamiya, Koichi Kato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionAs a common chronic complication, diabetic neuropathy affects a substantial number of individuals with diabetes mellitus, with limited therapeutic options addressing the underlying pathogenesis. The glucose‐lowering action of imeglimin is mediated through improved insulin sensitivity in peripheral organs, including the skeletal muscle and liver, along with augmented insulin release from β‐cells. Imeglimin exerts its effects in part by modulating mitochondrial complex I, leading to the reduced production of reactive oxygen species and protection against metabolic stress. Despite these beneficial effects, the effect of imeglimin on diabetic neuropathy remains unclear. In this study, we evaluated whether imeglimin ameliorated peripheral nerve dysfunction in streptozotocin (STZ)‐induced diabetic rats.Materials and MethodsThe rats received imeglimin (200 mg/kg twice daily) or vehicle for 4 weeks, and the sensory nerve conduction velocity (SNCV), motor nerve conduction velocity (MNCV), sciatic nerve blood flow (SNBF), and intraepidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD) were assessed. Neurite outgrowth was examined in ND7/23 cells derived from the dorsal root ganglion.ResultsImeglimin treatment significantly improved SNCV, SNBF, and IENFD without affecting blood glucose levels, indicating neuroprotective effects, independent of glycemic control. Furthermore, imeglimin enhanced neurite outgrowth in ND7/23 cells, demonstrating its direct neurotrophic effect. These findings indicate that imeglimin protects against diabetic neuropathy by enhancing nerve blood flow and promoting neurite growth independent of the systemic control of glycemia.ConclusionsThis study provides supporting evidence for the potential therapeutic application of imeglimin in diabetic neuropathy.

INTRODUCTION

Among the numerous complications associated with diabetes mellitus, diabetic neuropathy is particularly prevalent, affecting millions of individuals worldwide and significantly impairing their quality of life 1 , 2 , 3 . This condition is characterized by progressive nerve damage, sensory loss, pain, and motor deficits 4 , 5 , 6 . Its pathogenesis involves multiple factors such as oxidative stress, protein kinase C activation, polyol pathway abnormalities, impaired nerve blood flow, and metabolic disturbances, including dyslipidemia; the underlying mechanisms have yet to be fully elucidated 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 . Current therapeutic approaches are mainly directed at managing glucose levels and providing relief for symptoms, highlighting the urgent need for disease‐modifying strategies that target the underlying pathology 12 , 13 . Imeglimin is an oral agent approved for the treatment of type 2 diabetes in Japan and characterized by a unique dual mechanism of action that enhances insulin secretion and improves insulin sensitivity 14 , 15 . Imeglimin exerts multiple effects, including amelioration of mitochondrial dysfunction, suppression of hepatic gluconeogenesis via complex I inhibition, and enhancement of glucose uptake in skeletal muscles 16 , 17 . Moreover, imeglimin reduces mitochondrial oxidative stress by modulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, suggesting that it has therapeutic potential beyond glycemic control 18 . In our recent study using immortalized Schwann cells (IMS32), imeglimin markedly ameliorated cellular damage resulting from fluctuations in the glucose levels by reducing mitochondrial ROS, stabilizing mitochondrial membrane potential, preventing cytochrome c release, normalizing oxygen consumption and complex I activity, and restoring ATP synthesis, thereby protecting against cell death 19 . However, it is unclear whether imeglimin has neuroprotective effects in diabetic neuropathy. On the basis of these considerations, we explored the potential neuroprotective effects of imeglimin in a streptozotocin (STZ)‐induced diabetic rat model, assessed key functional and histological parameters, and evaluated the direct effects of this drug on neuronal cells in vitro.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and treatments

Male Wistar rats (4 weeks old) were obtained from Japan SLC Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan). The animals were kept in cages at a controlled temperature of 24 ± 1.0°C, under a 12‐h light/dark cycle, and had ad libitum access to standard chow and water. At 5 weeks of age, diabetes was induced by two intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin (STZ; Wako, Osaka, Japan; 195‐15154) at a dose of 50 mg/kg, freshly dissolved in saline. Control rats received the vehicle alone. The experimental subjects were evaluated for hyperglycemia 4 weeks post‐STZ injection, and those exhibiting blood glucose levels above 300 mg/dL were considered diabetic. After the diagnosis of diabetes, the rats were assigned to one of the following four groups: control (Ctrl), control + imeglimin (Ctrl + Imeg), diabetic (STZ), or diabetic + imeglimin (STZ + Imeg). Imeglimin hydrochloride (imeglimin; 200 mg/kg, kindly provided by Sumitomo Pharma Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) or vehicle (0.5% [w/v] methyl cellulose 400 solution, Wako; 133‐17815) was administered orally twice per day for 4 weeks. All functional and histological evaluations were performed 8 weeks after STZ injection (4 weeks after diabetes confirmation followed by 4 weeks of treatment). All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Aichi Gakuin University (approval no. AGUD508).

Sciatic nerve conduction velocity

After 4 weeks of administration of the vehicle or imeglimin, we measured sensory and motor conduction velocities in the sciatic nerve. Anesthesia was induced using a fixed combination of medetomidine, midazolam, and butorphanol, and the subjects were subsequently placed on a heated pad to maintain a body temperature (37°C). Sciatic sensory nerve conduction velocity (SNCV) and motor nerve conduction velocity (MNCV) were measured using a Neuropack S‐1 (Nihon‐Koden, Osaka, Japan), as previously described 20 .

Blood flow in the sciatic nerve

After 4 weeks of administering vehicle or imeglimin, sciatic nerve blood flow (SNBF) was evaluated under anesthesia using a laser Doppler flow meter (FLO‐N1; Omegawave, Tokyo, Japan) in accordance with a previously described protocol 21 .

Intraepidermal nerve fiber densities

Rats were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, and hind paw foot pads were excised and fixed in Zamboni solution (Wako; 263‐01991) at 4°C overnight. Following stepwise incubation in 10, 20 and 30% sucrose at 4°C, the specimens were embedded in O.C.T. compound (Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan; 4583) and cryopreserved for sectioning. Frozen sections (thickness: 30 μm) were prepared using a cryostat, blocked with 1% BSA/0.3% Triton X‐100 for 1 h, incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit antiubiquitin C‐terminal hydrolase 1 antibody (1:500 dilution; EnCor Biotechnology Inc., Gainesville, FL, USA, Cat. No. RPCA‐UCHL1), and probed with Alexa Fluor 594‐conjugated goat antirabbit secondary antibody (1:500 dilution; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; A11012). Z‐stack images (depth: 30 μm) were acquired from eight randomly selected regions per section using a confocal laser microscope (LSM800, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). The intraepidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD) was determined as the number of fibers per millimeter of the epidermal basement membrane 22 .

Neurite outgrowth

ND7/23 cells were seeded in 96‐well plates and, after cell attachment, cultured for 72 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator in low‐glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Wako; 041‐19775) supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Paisley, UK; 10437‐028), 5 μM Y‐27632 (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA; HY‐10071), and 1xMEM nonessential amino acid solution (Wako; 139‐15651). Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Wako; 163‐20145) for 10 min at 25°C. Six random fields per sample were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (BZX‐810, Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the spss software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Means were analyzed using anova, followed by the Tukey post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Effect of imeglimin on body weight and blood glucose levels in diabetic rats

After confirming diabetes, the rats were assigned to one of four groups: control (Ctrl), control + imeglimin (Ctrl + Imeg), diabetic (STZ), or diabetic + imeglimin (STZ + Imeg). Imeglimin (200 mg/kg) or vehicle (0.5% [w/v] methylcellulose 400 solution) was administered orally twice per day (b.i.d.) for 4 weeks (Figure 1a). Throughout the treatment period, body weight and blood glucose levels remained unchanged, with no significant differences detected between the Ctrl and Ctrl+Imeg groups or between the STZ and STZ + Imeg groups (Figure 1b,c).

Rats were divided into four groups: control (Ctrl), control + imeglimin (Ctrl + Imeg), diabetic (STZ), and diabetic + imeglimin (STZ + Imeg). Imeglimin (200 mg/kg) or vehicle was administered orally twice daily for 4 weeks. (b) Body weight and (c) nonfasting blood glucose level during the treatment period. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. ***P < 0.001 vs Ctrl, ### P < 0.001 vs Ctrl+Imeg.

Effect of imeglimin on nerve conduction velocity in diabetic rats

To evaluate the effects of imeglimin on peripheral nerve function, the SNCV and MNCV were measured in the four groups. For SNCV (Figure 2a), the Ctrl group showed a mean value of 43.6 ± 7.3 m/s, while the Ctrl + Imeg group exhibited a comparable value of 38.1 ± 6.6 m/s. By contrast, the STZ group showed a significant reduction (26.8 ± 6.6 m/s; P < 0.01 vs Ctrl), which was significantly ameliorated in the STZ + Imeg group (39.9 ± 11.0 m/s; P < 0.05 vs STZ). Regarding MNCV (Figure 2b), both the Ctrl and Ctrl+Imeg groups showed similar values (41.7 ± 7.4 m/s and 39.6 ± 4.6 m/s). The STZ group demonstrated a reduction to 30.1 ± 9.4 m/s, which slightly improved in the STZ + Imeg group to 33.8 ± 11.2 m/s. However, neither the reduction nor partial improvement in the STZ + I + meg group reached statistical significance.

Effect of imeglimin on nerve conduction velocity in STZ‐induced diabetic rats. (a) Sensory nerve conduction velocity (SNCV) and (b) motor nerve conduction velocity (MNCV) after 4 weeks of treatment. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. **P < 0.01 vs Ctrl, # P < 0.05 vs STZ.

Effect of imeglimin on sciatic nerve blood flow in diabetic rats

Impairment of blood flow in peripheral nerves contributes to the development of diabetic neuropathy 23 , 24 . To examine whether imeglimin enhanced peripheral nerve perfusion, the SNBF was measured in the four groups (Figure 3). The STZ group exhibited a marked reduction (6.8 ± 0.4) compared with the control group (13.2 ± 1.0; P < 0.001), indicating diabetes‐induced vascular dysfunction. Imeglimin treatment significantly improved SNBF in the STZ + Imeg group (13.9 ± 1.1; P < 0.001 vs STZ), restoring it to levels comparable with those in the Ctrl and Ctrl + Imeg groups. No statistically significant differences were found between the Ctrl and Ctrl + Imeg groups. These results suggest that imeglimin improves peripheral nerve blood flow in peripheral nerves under diabetic conditions, potentially contributing to its neuroprotective effects.

Effect of imeglimin on SNBF in STZ‐induced diabetic rats. Sciatic nerve blood flow (SNBF) was measured after 4 weeks of treatment. Data are presented as mean ± SD. ***P < 0.001 vs Ctrl, ### P < 0.001 vs STZ.

Effect of imeglimin on intraepidermal nerve fiber density in diabetic rats

IENFD, a widely used morphological indicator of peripheral nerve integrity, was evaluated by PGP9.5 immunostaining of the hind paw skin. Representative images (Figure 4a) showed PGP9.5‐positive nerve fibers extending from the dermis to the epidermis in the Ctrl and Ctrl + Imeg groups. By contrast, the STZ group exhibited a marked reduction in intraepidermal nerve fibers, indicating diabetes‐induced peripheral nerve degeneration. In the STZ + Imeg group, imeglimin restored the structural integrity of these fibers, approaching the levels observed in nondiabetic rats. Imeglimin significantly increased IENFD in STZ + Imeg rats (20.1 ± 4.9), compared with STZ rats (10.1 ± 4.1; P < 0.05, Figure 4b). Nerve fiber density was restored to levels comparable to those in the Ctrl (21.9 ± 4.0) and Ctrl + Imeg (23.4 ± 5.5) groups (Figure 4b). These findings show that imeglimin preserves intraepidermal nerve fiber integrity and protects intraepidermal nerve fibers from diabetes‐associated degeneration. To evaluate the direct neurotrophic effects of imeglimin, dorsal root ganglion‐derived ND7/23 cells were cultured with or without imeglimin at concentrations of 100 μM and 1 mM, respectively. As shown in Figure 5, treatment with 1 mM imeglimin significantly increased neurite length (118.5 ± 66.5) compared with the control (101.6 ± 48.6; P < 0.001) and tended to be higher than that observed with 100 μM imeglimin (108.6 ± 55.7; P = 0.08). These findings suggest that imeglimin promotes neurite outgrowth in ND7/23 cells in vitro (Figure 6).

(a) PGP9.5 immunostaining of hind paw skin showing intraepidermal nerve fibers (red) and nuclei (blue) after 4 weeks of imeglimin treatment. Scale bars represent 20 μm. (b) Quantification of intraepidermal nerve fiber density. Data are presented as mean ± SD. **P < 0.01 vs Ctrl, # P < 0.05 vs STZ.

Effects of imeglimin on neurite outgrowth in ND7/23 cells. Neurite outgrowth in ND7/23 cells was assessed 72 h after imeglimin treatment at the indicated concentrations. Data are presented as mean ± SE (n = 279–355). ***P < 0.001.

Schematic overview of the neuroprotective actions of imeglimin in vivo and in vitro. In a type 1 diabetic neuropathy model, imeglimin improved sensory nerve conduction velocity (SNCV), enhanced sciatic nerve blood flow (SNBF), and preserved intraepidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD), while blood glucose levels remained unchanged. In vitro, imeglimin promoted neurite outgrowth in DRG‐derived ND7/23 cells, indicating a direct neurotrophic effect.

DISCUSSION

Our findings demonstrated that imeglimin protects against diabetic neuropathy by improving SNCV, enhancing SNBF, and preserving IENFD in a type 1 diabetic neuropathy model, while also promoting neurite outgrowth in DRG‐derived ND7/23 cells. In the present study, imeglimin did not significantly alter the measured blood glucose levels in STZ‐induced diabetic rats, yet it markedly improved sensory nerve conduction velocity, nerve blood flow, and intraepidermal nerve fiber density. These findings suggest that the observed neuroprotective effects are not fully accounted for by the measured blood glucose values alone. Nevertheless, because HbA1c and oral glucose tolerance test were not assessed, we cannot exclude the possibility that modest or transient changes in glycemic control contributed, at least in part, to the observed improvements. Therefore, we interpret our data as supporting neuroprotective effects that are not solely attributable to changes in systemic blood glucose levels, while avoiding the conclusion that they are strictly independent of glycemic regulation. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that imeglimin may also exert protective effects through multiple mechanisms, including improvements in mitochondrial function, reduction of oxidative stress, and enhancement of vascular endothelial function 19 , 25 , 26 . Regarding the experimental context, STZ was administered to rats at 5 weeks of age, a developmental stage at which full neural maturation has not yet been achieved. In STZ‐induced diabetic rats, the structural development of neuropathy may be influenced by the age at which diabetes is induced, and interpretation of this model is further complicated by the persistent lower body weight associated with the diabetic state. Importantly, previous studies suggest that the characteristic nerve changes observed in this model are unlikely to be explained solely by delayed maturation because treatment with aldose reductase inhibitors or vasoactive agents has been reported to improve neuronal abnormalities without restoring body growth 27 , 28 . Under these conditions, growth retardation is a characteristic feature of the STZ‐induced diabetic state, and imeglimin treatment did not restore body weight, indicating that differences in body weight persisted throughout the experimental period. Despite this persistent growth retardation, imeglimin treatment was associated with marked improvements in multiple neuropathy‐related endpoints, including SNCV, SNBF, and IENFD. Notably, these improvements were observed in the absence of body weight recovery, suggesting that differences in body weight alone, or factors associated with growth status, are unlikely to fully account for the observed functional and structural preservation of peripheral nerves. Furthermore, our in vitro experiments demonstrated that imeglimin directly promotes neurite outgrowth in ND7/23 cells. This interpretation is also consistent with our previous findings in IMS32 Schwann cells, in which imeglimin protected against glucose‐induced cellular injury, at least in part through preservation of mitochondrial function and suppression of oxidative stress 19 . Taken together, these findings support the possibility that imeglimin exerts neuroprotective actions through mechanisms that extend beyond simple effects on growth or developmental progression. Nevertheless, in this experimental setting involving young animals, developmental status and diabetic neuropathy cannot be completely separated. Accordingly, growth retardation should be considered a potential confounding factor when interpreting the relationship between metabolic disturbance and nerve alterations. From a pathophysiological perspective, sensory nerve fibers are easily affected in diabetic neuropathy, with damage typically preceding that of motor fibers 1 . Notably, early‐stage protection of these fibers may be critical for delaying disease progression and maintaining long‐term nerve function. Although detailed mechanistic investigations were not performed in the present study, our previous study in Schwann cells provided mechanistic insights into the cytoprotective actions of imeglimin. In that study, imeglimin protected against glucose fluctuation‐induced mitochondrial injury by reducing ROS generation, preserving mitochondrial membrane potential, suppressing cytochrome c release, normalizing Complex I activity, and restoring ATP production, thereby improving cell survival 19 . These findings, together with previous reports in other tissues such as the liver and pancreatic islets 18 , 29 , suggest that modulation of mitochondrial function and oxidative stress may also contribute to the beneficial effects observed in peripheral nerves, although this was not directly examined in the present study. As these factors are known to play a critical role in diabetic neuropathy 30 , similar mechanisms may underlie the observed neurovascular protective effects. Future studies should investigate whether imeglimin influences oxidative stress or mitochondrial integrity in neural tissues to further elucidate its mechanisms of action. In this study, imeglimin promoted neurite outgrowth in cultured DRG‐derived ND7/23 cells, suggesting a direct neurotrophic effect. This finding is consistent with previous reports showing that GLP‐1 receptor agonists, such as exendin‐4, which have become a promising therapeutic option for diabetic neuropathy, promote neurite elongation and neuronal survival 22 , 31 and have been shown to improve diabetic neuropathy in a recent meta‐analysis 32 . Furthermore, the improvement in nerve perfusion aligns with previous evidence showing that vascular dysfunction is a key contributor to the development of diabetic neuropathy 23 , 33 , 34 . Imeglimin has been reported to improve vascular endothelial function, as indicated by improvements in postprandial flow‐mediated dilation in patients with type 2 diabetes 35 . In addition, imeglimin increased circulating nitrite concentrations in Zucker fa/fa rats, suggesting enhanced nitric oxide production and vasorelaxation, thereby improving blood flow in the coronary and mesenteric arteries 25 . In the present study, histopathological analyses of vascular structures within the sciatic nerve were not performed, as the experimental design was primarily focused on evaluating functional changes in local nerve perfusion, as assessed by SNBF, rather than structural vascular remodeling. Although direct histological evidence of vascular alterations was not obtained, prior evidence from both experimental models and clinical studies supports a protective role of imeglimin on vascular endothelial function 25 , 35 . Taken together, these findings suggest that the observed improvement in SNBF likely reflects functional enhancement of the vascular endothelium supplying peripheral nerves. While laser Doppler–based SNBF measurements allow assessment of dynamic and functionally relevant changes in microcirculation, they do not provide direct information regarding structural vascular remodeling. Therefore, the lack of direct morphological evaluation of the nerve vasculature represents a limitation of the present study. Further histological analyses will be required to clarify the structural correlates underlying imeglimin‐induced improvements in nerve blood flow. Regarding the translational relevance of the experimental models used in this study, plasma imeglimin concentrations were not measured in the rat experiments, and therefore, direct comparison between the exposure achieved in our in vivo model and human clinical pharmacokinetics was not possible. The in vivo dose of 200 mg/kg twice daily was selected with reference to the clinically used human dose and is broadly consistent with body surface area‐based dose translation from humans to rats and FDA guidance (Reagan‐Shaw, Nihal, and Ahmad 2008). In addition, this dose range is in line with those used in preclinical studies of imeglimin in experimental diabetes models 26 , 36 . By contrast, the 1 mM concentration used in the in vitro neurite outgrowth assay is approximately 100‐fold higher than human plasma exposure reported in recent clinical pharmacokinetic studies of imeglimin 37 , 38 . Accordingly, this concentration should be interpreted as a pharmacological condition used to assess direct biological activity in a simplified experimental system, rather than as a direct reflection of human systemic exposure. Further studies incorporating pharmacokinetic assessment in animal models and a broader range of in vitro concentrations will be important to better define the translational relevance of these findings. In addition, the improvement in MNCV was modest and did not reach statistical significance. Although MNCV is an important endpoint in experimental diabetic neuropathy, previous studies in STZ‐induced diabetic models have suggested that sensory nerve conduction abnormalities may in some cases be detected earlier or more consistently than motor abnormalities 39 , and that treatment effects may also be more readily observed in sensory nerve conduction 40 . Therefore, the clearer change observed in SNCV in this study may not be inconsistent with previous reports. Nevertheless, the relatively large inter‐animal variability in MNCV measurements may also have limited our ability to detect a statistically significant treatment effect. Further studies with a larger cohort and longer treatment period will be needed to clarify the effects of imeglimin on motor nerve function. ND7/23 cells, a hybrid cell line generated by fusion of neonatal rat dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons and mouse neuroblastoma cells, were used for the in vitro neurite outgrowth assay. This cell line is widely used as an in vitro model for neurite outgrowth studies because it retains sensory neuron‐like neurite extension in response to various stimuli 41 , 42 , 43 . Nevertheless, as a hybrid cell line, it may not fully recapitulate the biological properties of primary sensory neurons. Future studies using primary DRG neurons will be valuable for confirming the physiological relevance of the observed neurite‐promoting effect of imeglimin. In the present study, peripheral nerve dysfunction was evaluated mainly using objective neurophysiological and structural endpoints. Sensory phenotypes in STZ‐induced diabetic neuropathy are known to vary with disease progression, with hyperalgesia commonly observed at earlier stages and hypoalgesia becoming more prominent later, although the timing of this transition differs among experimental diabetic models 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 . Accordingly, future studies incorporating behavioral assessments alongside neurophysiological and morphological analyses across different stages may further clarify the dynamic relationship between sensory symptoms and structural nerve alterations. Although blood chemistry parameters were not evaluated in this study, previous clinical trials have reported no clinically meaningful changes in standard laboratory parameters, including serum lipid profiles and renal function markers, during imeglimin treatment 48 , 49 , 50 . In addition, a previous study using an STZ‐induced hyperglycemic rodent model showed no significant differences in total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL‐cholesterol, or HDL‐cholesterol between imeglimin‐treated and untreated diabetic animals, further suggesting that imeglimin is unlikely to exert major effects on standard blood chemistry parameters 26 . Moreover, considering the translational potential of these findings, clinical studies are required to fully evaluate the efficacy of imeglimin in patients with diabetic neuropathy. Taken together, these findings support the potential of imeglimin as a disease‐modifying therapy with improved sensory nerve conduction and nerve perfusion resulting from its neurovascular protective effects.
