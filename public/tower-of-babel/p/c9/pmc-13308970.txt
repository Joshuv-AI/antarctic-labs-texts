Female pelvic floor protection—Where do we have to go?
Authors: Miriam Deniz, Luisa Wirth, Kathrin Beilecke, Ralf Tunn, Markus Huebner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPelvic floor protection has a growing awareness and importance in long lasting strategies to provide female pelvic floor health. This state‐of‐the‐art review article aims to summarize important future directions in this area. A literature search has been conducted within the last 20 years with pelvic floor protection‐specific search terms. Four major work packages have been identified. (1) Epidemiology and risk stratification. (2) Mechanistic and translational research. (3) Diagnostic and preventive strategies. (4) Implementation and knowledge translation. A systematic literature search was performed and after assessing inclusion criteria and quality on expert opinion, 82 out of 1636 articles were included. The alignment for future research has been part of the analysis as well as ways of how to implement evidence based information to a day‐to‐day clinical routine.

Key messagePelvic floor protection addresses the utmost need of all professionals involved in pregnancy, birth, postpartum rehabilitation, and diagnostics and treatment of pelvic floor disorders to gather together in order to improve long‐lasting women's health.

INTRODUCTION

Vaginal birth is known to be the most important risk factor for pelvic floor disorders such as urinary and anal incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse (POP). 1 These devastating conditions negatively impact women's quality of life after childbirth. 2 The prevalence of pelvic floor disorders is high, affecting almost 25% of all women during their lifetime. 3 In the USA, 300 000 women require pelvic reconstructive surgery each year. 4 Given the fact that there are approximately 3 000 000 vaginal births annually in the United States, 1 out of 10 women have to undergo surgery for POP or stress urinary incontinence (SUI). 2 Although the majority of women deliver their babies without any negative impact on their pelvic floor, 10%–15% are at highest risk for pelvic floor injury during vaginal birth and need to be identified. 2

The idea that obstetricians can consider pelvic floor protective strategies during labor and delivery is not new. 5 Handa et al. described the importance of the pelvic floor for continence and pelvic organ support almost 30 years ago. 5 The authors recommended that “Obstetricians may be able to reduce pelvic floor injuries by minimizing forceps deliveries and episiotomies, by allowing passive descent in the second stage, and by selectively recommending elective cesarean delivery.” 5 In recent times, there is growing evidence that pelvic floor protection has many aspects during vaginal birth, 6 however, focusing on the antenatal (AN) and postpartum periods as well seems to be a much more appropriate approach to assess pelvic floor protection. 7 DeLancey et al. describe these devastating conditions such as prolapse and incontinence as not surprising, “given the remarkable pelvic floor changes during delivery”. 2

Given the fact that the evidence regarding pelvic floor trauma and vaginal birth is increasingly growing, it is remarkable that all stakeholders involved are still struggling how to increase the awareness of these highly prevalent problems, to invite all health care providers such as obstetricians, midwives, physiotherapists, and urogynecologists to find a common language, straightforward guidelines and overall, the fundamental understanding to effectively improve pelvic floor health.

This state‐of‐the‐art review is a comprehensive, critical summary of the most current and high‐quality evidence and aims to address this question of how to align the future direction of pelvic floor protection using four work packages in order to anticipate future needs: Epidemiology and risk stratification.Mechanistic and translational research.Diagnostic and preventive strategies.Implementation and knowledge translation.

RESULTS

Epidemiology and risk stratification

Epidemiology

Pelvic floor disorders are common in adult women. Nygaard et al. reported that approximately 25% of women in the United States suffer from at least one pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD). 3 The study by Kenne et al. used a retrospective, cross‐sectional analysis of electronic health records from primary care within an academic healthcare system. The ICD‐10 diagnoses of 25 425 adult women were analyzed to determine the prevalence of urinary incontinence, POP, and bowel dysfunction. The key findings revealed that overall in this cohort 32.0% of women had at least one PFD. Bowel dysfunction (primarily defecation disorders) was the most common, affecting 24.6%, followed by urinary incontinence (11.1%) and prolapse (4.4%). 8 In contrast, the International Urogynecology Consultation Committee described the prevalence of POP between 20% and 65%, assessed by both questionnaires and gynecologic examination in their narrative review. 9 Regarding the frequency of different subtypes of urinary incontinence, the NHANES 2017–2018 survey reported the following prevalence rates: stress incontinence 33.9% (95% CI [29.1 to 38.6]) for women aged 20–39 years, 51.3% (95% CI [45.2 to 57.3]) for those aged 40–59 years, and 53.1% (95% CI [46.8 to 59.3]) for those aged ≥60 years. The estimated prevalence of urgency UI was 17.6% (95% CI [13.8 to 21.5]) for women aged 20–39 years, 27.9% (95% CI [23.6 to 32.1]) for those aged 40–59 years, and 49.5% (95% CI [43.9 to 55.4]) for those aged ≥60 years and mixed UI was 8.3% (95% CI [5.4 to 11.3]) for women aged 20–39 years, 15.9% (95% CI [12.9 to 19.0]) for those aged 40–59 years, and 31.4% (95% CI [26.2% to 36.6%]) for those aged 60 years and older. 10 The estimated overall prevalence of anal incontinence (including fecal and flatus incontinence) in adults is about 8.0% (95% CI [6.8% to 9.2%]), based on an actual systematic review and meta‐analysis of 80 population‐based studies with over 500 000 participants. 11 Accurately determining the true prevalence of PFDs remains challenging. Variations in how these disorders are defined—both in clinical practice and across research studies—lead to discrepancies in prevalence and incidence estimates. Additionally, the population being studied (treatment‐seeking individuals versus the general population) and the methods used to diagnose these disorders (such as validated questionnaires, physical examinations, or medical record reviews) can all influence the reported prevalence rates. 9

Risk factors

Regarding obstetric‐associated risk factors, DeLancey highlighted in a recent review that vaginal birth is the largest modifiable risk factor for prolapse and an important contributor to stress incontinence. 2 Numerous systematic reviews and meta‐analysis have summarized the obstetric factors that contribute to this risk. In particular, a prolonged second stage of labor, birth weight >4000 g, operative vaginal delivery using forceps, occiput posterior position, obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI), and levator avulsion during delivery are intrapartum risks for the development of PFD. Forceps delivery is associated with higher risk of pelvic floor trauma than vacuum delivery due to greater mechanical forces on maternal tissues. Regional variation likely reflects differences in training, instrument use, patient selection, and episiotomy practice. 2 , 12 , 13 , 14 Notably, levator avulsion is of significant importance, as the presence of a levator avulsion increases the risk of POP later in life by 7.3 times (OR 7.3) compared to women with intact pelvic floor support. 2 Maternal factors also play a crucial role in this context. Maternal age, elevated maternal BMI, and the presence of urinary and fecal incontinence during pregnancy are independent risk factors, as are parity and the number of vaginal births. 2 , 13 In contrast, women who underwent exclusive cesarean delivery exhibited a significantly lower likelihood of developing PFD later in life. Compared to spontaneous vaginal delivery, cesarean section is associated with a notable reduction in the risk of SUI, overactive bladder and POP, as demonstrated in two prospective cohort studies with a long‐term follow up 15–26 years after the initial delivery. 15 , 16 This consideration should be balanced against evidence suggesting elevated risks for fertility, future pregnancy outcomes, and long‐term developmental outcomes in children. 17

The main nonobstetric risk factors for the development of PFD include advancing age and an elevated body mass index (BMI). 16 Individuals aged 50 years and above were found to be at a greater risk of developing PFD symptoms (aOR = 5.04; 95% CI [1.35 to 11.85]). 18 Obesity, with a BMI > 30 kg/m2, increases the likelihood of developing POP by 1.75 times (OR, 1.75; 95% CI [1.17 to 2.62]). 12 Chronic constipation, heavy physical labor, and high‐impact sports are also associated with an increased risk of PFD. 19 , 20 Women who undergo hysterectomy for benign uterine conditions have been shown, in a recent meta‐analysis, to have a significantly higher risk of developing stress incontinence within 10 years (effect size 2.40; 95% CI [2.17 to 2.63]), and a 56% elevated risk of developing POP after more than 10 years (effect size 1.56; 95% CI [1.35 to 1.78]). 21 A positive family history of PFD also increases the risk of developing the condition. Recent studies suggest that the genetic risk is likely attributable to variations in genes involved in the regulation and homeostasis of the extracellular matrix, fibroblast function, and connective tissue structure. 22

Risk stratification models

A qualitative study revealed that pregnant women are interested in knowing their risk of PFD and that this knowledge would encourage them to engage in preventive activities. Furthermore, healthcare providers recognized the importance of discussing the risk of PFD, but time constraints and concerns about an increase in cesarean section rates often limited these discussions to high‐risk women or those who expressed specific concerns. 23 To facilitate counseling based on epidemiological data, a validated risk stratification model is currently available in the obstetric context for predicting PFD: the UR‐CHOICE Score. This score integrates obstetric and maternal risk factors and provides an individualized prepartum risk assessment for the development of PFD based on the mode of delivery, thus serving as a tool for prenatal counseling. 24 The predictive model is based on large birth cohorts and estimates the likelihood of PFD (urinary incontinence, fecal incontinence, prolapse) 12 and 20 years after delivery. 25 Further validation of the UR‐CHOICE score is required, particularly through prospective studies evaluating its performance in real‐world clinical settings and its ability to meaningfully inform clinical decision‐making and improve patient‐relevant outcomes. The presence of PFD in the family also plays a role. Currently, large genome‐wide association studies suggest that polygenic risk scores (PRS) for POP have a comparable predictive ability to traditional clinical risk factors and could improve individual risk prediction when combined with these factors. 22 , 26 The integration of PRS into clinical practice is still in the research phase, and to date, no validated, routinely used risk models exist.

Currently, a major challenge in risk stratification is the overall high methodological heterogeneity across studies. The American Urogynecologic Society emphasizes that the pathogenesis and causal relationships between risk factors and PFD remain incompletely understood, which has hindered the development of evidence‐based prevention and treatment strategies to date. 27

Mechanistic and translational research

Pelvic floor biomechanics

The pelvic floor muscles and ligaments counteract the downward load of abdominal organs and transient intra‐abdominal pressure that occur during activities such as sneezing, coughing, or jumping. 2 Together they also maintain urinary and fecal continence, support sexual function, and are essential for childbirth. 28

Anatomically, the pelvic floor consists of the anal sphincter complex and the perineal complex, which together close the lower vaginal canal. 28 The perineal complex comprises the levator ani muscle with its fascia coverings, and the perineal membrane. 28 The levator ani is divided into three portions, pubovisceral (also called pubococcygeal), puborectal, and iliococcygeal. 2 All these structures arise from the pelvic sidewalls and converge medially at characteristic attachment points. The pubovisceral portion lifts the perineal structures and, together with the puborectal portion, closes the urogenital and levator hiatuses. The iliococcygeal muscle forms a thin sheet that spans from the tendinous arch of the levator ani to the midline iliococcygeal raphe. Within this framework lie the urogenital hiatus (situated between the pubis and the perineal membrane) and the levator hiatus, which extends posteriorly behind the anorectal angle. 28

Pathophysiology

Currently, hiatal enlargement is the most important birth‐related risk factor for POP. 2 Handa et al. demonstrated that 20 years after delivery, 60% of women whose levator hiatus measured ≥4 cm developed POP. 29 Even before delivery, pregnancy increases hiatus size and induces tissue changes on a molecular level. 30 During vaginal delivery the fetal head is forced through the pelvic floor openings, often causing levator ani muscle injury that reduces the ability to close the hiatal openings and predisposes to prolapse. 2

Levator ani muscle avulsion, defined as unilateral or bilateral, partial or complete detachment of the puborectalis muscle from its insertion, occurs at childbirth and is a major contributor to POP and urinary incontinence. 31 , 32 , 33 Reported risk factors include low maternal BMI, prolonged labor, advanced maternal age, high birthweight, large fetal head circumference, mediolateral episiotomy, and OASI; the strongest association is with forceps‐assisted delivery, which raises the risk of levator injury approximately sixfold compared with spontaneous birth. 34 Nonetheless, hiatal enlargement cannot be explained by muscle injury alone, suggesting additional nonmuscular contributors. 2 In late‐pregnancy mouse models, the extracellular‐matrix content of pelvic floor tissue increased markedly, indicating that remodeling may also play a role. 35 Furthermore, a substantial proportion of women with prolapse exhibit little to no detectable muscle damage, pointing to other mechanisms such as age‐related loss of muscle integrity and function, myogenic and neurogenic alterations, and chronic inflammation. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39

Imaging techniques

Several imaging modalities are available to visualize the structural changes of the pelvic floor, most notably magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and transperineal ultrasound (TPUS). Both techniques are noninvasive, but they differ in cost, accessibility, and diagnostic performance. 40 , 41 While TPUS offers high specificity for detecting levator ani muscle avulsion, MRI provides greater sensitivity for this particular lesion. 42 , 43 Currently, MRI remains the gold standard as advanced reconstruction methods can display pregnancy‐ and childbirth‐induced alterations of the perineal membrane, abnormalities that are common in women with prolapse. 44 Typical MRI measurements include the area and the anterior–posterior and transverse diameters of the levator and genital hiatuses (GHs), the levator bowl volume, and the levator plate angle and shape. 28

TPUS, on the other hand, is more cost‐effective, widely available, and has already become part of routine clinical care. 40 , 45 In pregnant women with SUI, TPUS demonstrates reduced bladder‐neck support and a wider GH during a Valsalva maneuver. 46 In addition to TPUS, other ultrasound approaches such as exoanal, endoanal, and endovaginal three‐dimensional ultrasound are used to assess maternal birth trauma, including levator injury. 47 , 48 Modern ultrasound techniques therefore give clinicians a simple, inexpensive, and noninvasive way to standardize the diagnosis of pelvic‐floor trauma and to explore related symptoms. 47 Looking ahead, artificial intelligence‐driven software is expected to streamline assessment workflows and further improve diagnostic accuracy. 49

Diagnostic and preventive strategies

Diagnosis

Diagnostic approaches for PFDs encompass clinical examination, imaging modalities and patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs).

Clinical examination remains foundational. In addition to the semiquantative assessment of the location of the compartments and description of morphologic and functional pathologies, there is therapeutic strategy and, to a limited extent, outcomes to be derived.

The Pelvic Floor Research Group around DeLancey et al. 50 showed that a large urogenital hiatus in level III results in a higher risk of developing POP after birth and failure after prolapse surgery. In this longitudinal cohort of 1492 women, 153 incident prolapse cases occurred across 6977 visits, with most GH values between 2 and 3 cm. Prolapse risk increased progressively with larger GH size, and survival modeling showed markedly shorter prolapse‐free time with increasing GH. Estimated median time to prolapse ranged from >45 years for GH ≤2.5 cm to ~5.8 years for GH ≥4.5 cm. GH significantly modified prolapse risk in both cesarean and vaginal birth groups.

Diagnosis of OASI, perineal trauma and levator ani avulsion (LAA)

All women should be offered a systematic assessment in the labor room, including rectal examination, in order to exclude genital tract trauma, including buttonhole tears. 51 If there is a suspicion of an injury in the postpartum period, that cannot be clinically detected, further imaging should be performed. 52 Physical examination however may underestimate multicompartment involvement and functional abnormalities, therefore additional imaging should be performed.

The systematic review with meta‐analysis by Lima et al. has set an example that shows that episiotomy and severe perineal tear are risk factors for these anatomical changes to the PFM, and this can be useful for identifying women who are at greater risk of developing PFDs, as well as to reinforce the need for measures to avoid routine episiotomies and to prevent severe tears. 53 It should be considered that while there might be an association between episiotomy and PFD, the underlying indication for the procedure (e.g., macrosomia, rigid perineum, or instrumental delivery) may also be a relevant contributing factor.

The systematic review and meta‐analysis of the prevalence of LAA with OASI and its effects on PFD by Amin et al. has shown LAA is even prevalent following vaginal birth and it is associated with OASI in one third of the cases. 54 Three studies evaluated pelvic floor muscle strength (PFMS) using the modified oxford scoring system (MOS) and all reported a reduction of postpartum MOS in women with LAA compared to women with OASI alone. One study also commented on reduced PFMS antenatally in women who then sustained LAA (mean AN MOS in those with LAA = 3.0 versus those without = 3.6).

Postpartum ultrasound

In evaluating OASI, Pang et al. compared transperineal (TPUS) and endovaginal (EVUS) ultrasound. 55 TPUS had better diagnostic value than EVUS in detecting the external anal sphincter (EAS). For examination of the internal anal sphincter (IAS), TPUS and EVUS are comparable in terms of diagnostic accuracy. The results of the indirect comparison of EVUS (divided into 2D and 3D) and TPUS (divided into 2D and 3D) showed that the sensitivity of 3D image diagnosis of EAS was better than the 2D image, whether it was EVUS or TPUS. These results indicated that 3D‐TPUS might have better diagnostic performance compared to other diagnostic tests. Finally, the authors concluded that due to limited data available, more studies with rigorous methods are required to confirm these findings.

A systematic review and meta‐analysis of the pathophysiology of SUI from Falah‐Hassani et al. showed that observed bladder neck dilation and lower functional urethral length, bladder neck support and maximum urethral closure pressures are strong characteristic signs of SUI. 56 Chen et al. suggested that TPUS had application value in the diagnosis of SUI and had the potential to become a routine examination method to assist clinical decision‐making. 57

Validated questionnaires

Finally PROMs are increasingly used for screening and monitoring. Validated tools such as the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire‐Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ‐UI SF), modified Pelvic Floor Questionnaire (mPFQ), and electronic Personal Assessment Questionnaire‐Pelvic Floor (ePAQ‐PF) demonstrate good reliability and clinical utility for identifying symptoms and guiding triage to specialist care. 58 , 59 , 60

Prevention

It is generally assumed that reducing modifiable risk factors can help prevent the development of PFD, although the magnitude of this effect is difficult to quantify. Key preventive measures include weight management—particularly the avoidance of overweight and obesity—the treatment of chronic constipation, and the restriction of activities associated with heavy lifting or high physical strain. 61 , 62 In the context of pregnancy and childbirth, preventive strategies for PFD can be categorized into prepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum approaches. DeLancey recommends informing women about the potential long‐term consequences of childbirth and available preventive options already before delivery, thereby facilitating informed decision‐making. 2

Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT)

The most effective prepartum intervention to reduce the risk of PFD during pregnancy is structured and regularly performed PFMT. High‐quality randomized controlled trials and meta‐analyses demonstrate that PFMT during pregnancy significantly reduces the risk of urinary incontinence and severe perineal trauma, without relevant adverse effects. 63 , 64 In addition, regular AN perineal massage initiated in the third trimester appears to reduce the risk of severe perineal trauma, including third‐ and fourth‐degree tears, particularly in primiparous women. 65 Aquino et al. concluded in their systematic review that perineal massage during labor was associated with a significantly lower risk of severe perineal trauma and a higher chance of an intact perineum. 66

Cesarean section

Planned cesarean section is associated with a reduced risk of PFD; however, based on current evidence, it remains unclear which women with a given risk profile truly benefit from a primary cesarean section after careful consideration of both maternal and fetal risks and benefits. In this context, particularly after a history of OASIS, a highly individualized counseling process and comprehensive evaluation are recommended.

In a recent publication, Thakar and Sultan propose that elective cesarean section should be recommended only for women presenting with both an EAS defect and a reduced incremental maximum squeeze pressure of <20 mmHg. For asymptomatic women with low‐grade (3a) tears and normal findings on clinical assessment, endoanal ultrasound, and anorectal manometry, vaginal birth is considered an appropriate option. The authors emphasize that clinical decision‐making should integrate symptoms, clinical tear grade, and objective findings from imaging and manometric assessment, rather than relying on grade or symptoms alone. 67 With regard to the optimal timing of a secondary cesarean section, evidence remains inconclusive, as a prolonged second stage of labor and operative vaginal delivery are both associated with an increased risk of PFD, yet high‐quality comparative data are lacking [26]. A recent population‐based study suggests that cesarean section performed after a prolonged second stage (≥3 h) does not increase the risk of PFD compared with expectant management, whereas operative vaginal delivery at 3–4 or 4–5 h of second‐stage duration is associated with a significantly increased risk of PFD. 68

Intrapartum preventive strategies

Further intrapartum strategies to prevent pelvic floor injury are manual perineal protection, controlled delivery of the fetal head, avoidance of pushing against a contracted pelvic floor, manual correction of persistent occiput posterior position, perineal massage or warm compresses, early induction in suspected macrosomia, and preferential use of vacuum over forceps which may reduce the risk of levator avulsion and OASIS. 2

Postpartum preventive strategies

Regarding the postpartum period there is data investigating the prevention of PFD in asymptomatic women after childbirth, specifically regarding the effectiveness of PFMT. A recent meta‐analysis demonstrate that structured PFMT initiated in the postpartum period reduces the odds of developing urinary incontinence by approximately 37% (seven randomized controlled trials (RCTs), n = 1930; OR 0.63, 95% CI [0.41 to 0.97], I2 72%) and POP by 56% (one RCT, n = 123; OR 0.44, 95% CI [0.21 to 0.91]) in women without baseline symptoms, with moderate certainty of evidence. 69 Postnatal PFMT has been offered to all women in France since 1985 and has been introduced more recently in England. 70 Another proposed approach to enhance postpartum recovery is the use of supportive pessaries to facilitate reconnection and stabilization of pelvic connective tissue after childbirth. To date, however, there is insufficient evidence to confirm a preventive effect on PFD. Nevertheless, postpartum use of pessaries—even in women without established PFD—appears to be associated with improved subjective pelvic floor stability and potentially enhanced pelvic floor recovery. 71 , 72

IMPLEMENTATION AND KNOWLEDGE TRANSLATION

Although evidence linking pelvic floor disorders to pregnancy and childbirth is rapidly increasing, healthcare providers still struggle to find straightforward ways to integrate this knowledge into everyday practice to promote long‐term pelvic floor health.

Key principles of knowledge translation and implementation.

The OASI care bundle is a strong example of implementation and knowledge translation in pelvic floor protection because it operationalizes evidence‐based practices into routine maternity care through a structured, multidisciplinary approach. The OASI care bundle consists of four core elements: AN information for women, manual perineal protection during vaginal birth, mediolateral episiotomy at 60° when indicated, and systematic postbirth examination for perineal trauma. Its development and rollout were led by the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the Royal College of Midwives, ensuring professional consensus and broad stakeholder engagement. 81 , 82

The latest multicenter outcome data demonstrate that implementation of the OASI care bundle leads to a statistically significant reduction in OASI rates without increasing cesarean section or episiotomy rates. In a stepped‐wedge multicenter study across 16 maternity units in England, Scotland, and Wales, the OASI rate decreased from 3.3% to 3.0% after care bundle implementation (adjusted odds ratio 0.80, 95% CI [0.65 to 0.98], p = 0.03), with a sustained effect across parity and mode of birth. 83 Long‐term national registry data from Norway, where a similar care bundle was implemented, show a halving of recurrent OASI rates in second vaginal births among women with prior OASI, from 7.7% to 3.5% over two decades and further from 5.4% to 2.1% between 2002 and 2022, and a reduction in first‐time OASI rates from 1.6% to 0.6%. 73 , 84

Implementation of pelvic floor protection in clinical practice centers on routine screening, education, and integration of PFMT into obstetric and gynecologic care should include the following: At every AN and postnatal contact, women should be asked about continence status, and those who are continent should receive a structured PFMT prescription and behavioral support to prevent urinary incontinence. 63 , 85

Effective implementation requires coordinated action among women, healthcare professionals, maternity services, organizations, and policymakers. This principle emphasizes the need for collective agency and shared responsibility to prioritize pelvic floor health and prevention strategies throughout the reproductive lifespan. 74 , 86 Delivering structured, evidence‐based education—covering pelvic floor anatomy, dysfunction, and preventive measures—improves knowledge, awareness, and self‐efficacy, which are critical for adherence to PFMT and behavior change. 75 , 76 , 77 Pelvic floor protection strategies must be individualized, accounting for patient‐specific risk factors, preferences, and capabilities. Tailored verbal instructions, cues, and feedback enhance engagement and correct exercise performance. 78 Comprehensive and explicit descriptions of intervention content, progression, and decision rules are essential for reproducibility and clinical uptake. Use of standardized templates facilitates consistent implementation and knowledge translation among clinicians. 78 Implementation must proactively identify and address barriers (e.g., lack of awareness, stigma, resource limitations) and leverage facilitators (e.g., supportive environments, prompts, multidisciplinary training) to optimize adherence and sustainability of pelvic floor protection strategies. 74 , 79 , 80

These principles are supported by the current consensus in the medical literature and are critical for translating evidence into routine practice for pelvic floor protection.

DISCUSSION

Pelvic floor disorders are highly prevalent and need to be addressed if women's health is understood seriously. High variations in both definitions as well as analysis of prevalences remain challenging. Risk factors such as vaginal birth, age, and overweight have been clearly identified. However, shifting all of this information to an overall picture shows how all professions involved in pelvic floor health remain struggling. Risk stratification models exist and may be helpful to thoroughly counsel mothers‐to‐be during their pregnancy. Nevertheless, classifying women in low, middle and high risk regarding pelvic floor disorders has neither been implemented in a day to day routine, nor proven to be superior in preventing pelvic floor disorders such as urinary or anal incontinence or POP.

Pelvic floor biomechanics are not fully understood yet, but we are moving in the right direction. Understanding the levator ani subdivision and its specific function as well as the importance of both perineum and hiatus may lead us to future research projects and a better knowledge of how to reduce pelvic floor trauma during vaginal birth. The hiatal enlargement plays a critical role in pelvic floor stability.

Imaging techniques such as MRI and ultrasound in 2D and 3D have improved diagnostics and understanding of fundamental changes the pelvic floor needs to face during delivery and will for sure remain the diagnostic tools needed.

Preventive aspects will—as in other fields of medicine—gain importance in the future. Physiotherapy prior to delivery is an important example of how beneficial items are not fully understood and not implemented in a day‐to‐day routine.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our research and deeper understanding is progressing; however, a close interaction between all professions involved is mandatory. Future research projects need to address ways of implementing the growing knowledge of the female pelvic floor and aspects of pelvic floor protection.
