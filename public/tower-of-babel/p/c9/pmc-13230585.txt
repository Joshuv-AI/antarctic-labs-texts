Solution epitaxy of single-crystal ferroelectric p-i-n structure with ultrahigh photovoltaic response
Authors: Zihang Huang, Zijun Zhang, Wan-Rong Geng, Wenbo Ma, Yuyang Li, Zhanqi Zhou, Yi Fu, Ruian Zhang, Yiran Sun, Xuchao Fang, Xuanyu Jiang, Qin Xiong, Xiayi Lv, Guanshihan Du, Shangqing Li, Yanjun Fang, Yang Michael Yang, Yuhui Huang, Xiu-Liang Ma, Yongjun Wu, Kaihui Liu, Zijian Hong, Tianqi Deng, He Tian, Gaorong Han, Zhaohui Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFerroelectric materials with a switchable polarization are appealing for optoelectronic applications because of their above-bandgap photovoltage with a potential to exceed the Shockley-Queisser limit efficiency within classic p-n junctions. However, the intrinsic photovoltaic current in ferroelectrics remains far below the junction ones by orders, hindering their practical applications. Here we report an extremely large photovoltaic current density of 130 mA cm−2 with a photovoltage of 1.31 V under 375 nm light illumination in single-domain lead titanate films. Its photoresponsivity surpasses all available ferroelectric materials by two orders of magnitude. Systematic investigations reveal that an interfacial Ta dopant layer during solution epitaxy drives a polarization converting of the film via the flexoelectric effect, where surface charged Pb vacancy layer was generated for polarization screening. These two layers produce a p-i-n structure throughout the film, accounting for the ultrahigh photovoltaic response. Furthermore, a self-powered X-ray detector made of such film was explored to achieve a sensitivity of 4.73 C Gyair−1 cm−3, three orders of magnitude higher than that of commercial amorphous Se detectors. Our findings may pave the way for exploring novel detection and imaging devices based on ferroelectric materials.Subject terms: Ferroelectrics and multiferroics; Surfaces, interfaces and thin films

Introduction

The photovoltaic (PV) effect in semiconductors is the foundation of many technologies, such as solar energy conversion1, photocatalysis2, and optoelectronic sensing3. The key step to these applications is efficiently driving charge separation of electron-hole pair carriers or excitons by a built-in electric field and subsequent carrier transport. Towards this purpose, a classic p-n junction was fabricated in the crystalline silicon solar cells. Nevertheless, the efficiency of solar-cell is curtailed by inherent energy losses, subjected to the Shockley-Queisser (S-Q) limit in single p-n junction4,5. Alternatively, perovskite oxide ferroelectric semiconductors, have triggered great interests for solar energy conversion6–8, where the above-bandgap voltage from several to several thousand volts can even be produced in single-phase ferroelectrics such as LiNbO39, holding a potential to exceed such efficiency limit5. Interestingly, an electric polarization-direction-dependent PV effect has been demonstrated in BiFeO3 (BFO)10. However, the resulting PV current density turns out to be quite low, mainly due to poor bulk conductivity. Different strategies ranging from the enhanced absorption by doping11, high-efficient carrier collection by atomic force microscope (AFM) tips12,13 and polar order engineering14 have been developed to improve the PV current. Despite great efforts over the past decades to achieve polarization-based PV effects, the intrinsic PV current in ferroelectrics remains at the μA cm−2 level. The PV current mechanisms in ferroelectrics are still complex and controversial, involving shift current, ballistic current, and incompletely screened depolarization field or domain walls15. Despite great efforts, an effective strategy to significantly improve PV current in ferroelectrics towards practical devices remains a big challenge16.

For typical crystalline silicon or halide perovskite solar cells, the p-n junction or p-i-n structure plays a decisive role in achieving efficient energy conversion17,18, where electron and hole transport layers as well as built-in electric field design are achieved simultaneously. For example, in halide perovskite solar cells, meticulous selection of transport layers and precise band structure matching are essential for optimizing p-i-n functionality. Nevertheless, analogous transport layers and their derived built-in electric fields in single-phase ferroelectrics have not yet been explored. But it is very encouraging that intriguing electrical conductivity phenomena near domain walls have been discovered19 and illustrated systematically20. N-type or p-type conducting layer near domain walls in ferroelectrics could be generated through spatial confinement of charged defects, such as Bi vacancies and oxygen vacancies, compensating bound charges due to a spontaneous polarization discontinuity21. Interestingly, p-n junction based on single domain wall have been designed experimentally22. These advances suggest a possibility to enhance the PV current through domain wall confining charged defects. However, the non-uniform direction of the built-in electric field near every domain wall is not beneficial for unidirectional photogenerated carrier separation and transport. Moreover, domain walls have been identified to contribute to the high photovoltage rather than the PV current23.

In addition to domain walls, the polarization surface of single-domain ferroelectrics have been demonstrated to energetically confine various charged point defects for screening bound charges24. In principle, these defect layers near the opposite polarization surface can serve as electron and hole transport layers, as well as construct a p-i-n structure to enhance the PV effect. Especially, it has been proven that the oxygen vacancy layer prevails as a screening layer near the negative polarization surface of ferroelectrics25–27. However, oxygen vacancies hardly contribute to n-type conduction in perovskite ferroelectric oxides because of forming polarons by trapping electrons28,29.

In this work, we developed a strategy to control the polarization direction of single-domain ferroelectric lead titanate (PbTiO3, PTO) films by an interfacial Ta dopant during solution epitaxy. This enables the construction of a p-i-n structure throughout the films, where the interfacial Ta-doping layer and the polarization-screening-induced surface Pb-vacancy layer can be employed as efficient n-type electron and p-type hole transport layers, respectively. As a result, an ultrahigh PV current density of 130 mA cm−2 with a photovoltage of 1.31 V under 375 nm light illumination was obtained, two orders of magnitude higher than that of emerging ferroelectric materials. Using these films, a self-powered and direct-type X-ray detector was fabricated to demonstrate high sensitivity and stability, which is highly desired for next-generation X-ray imaging devices.

Results and discussion

Single-crystal single-domain pure PTO and Ta-doped PTO (Ta-PTO) films have been grown with a solution epitaxy method30 on single-crystal 0.7% Nb doped SrTiO3 (NSTO) substrates at a relatively low temperature (~200 °C) (see methods for more details). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed that both films exhibit a tetragonal perovskite structure (JCPDS#70-0746) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1b a single Ta-PTO (103) diffraction peak is observed together with the NSTO (103) substrate peak, indicating epitaxial growth of the Ta-PTO film on the NSTO substrate. Additionally, ϕ scan revealed four peaks at 90° intervals, indicating the single-crystal nature of the Ta-PTO film (Supplementary Fig. 1c), which also has a smooth and continuous surface (Supplementary Fig. 1d). For tetragonal perovskite PTO, the spontaneous polarization (Ps) arises from the off-center displacements of Ti ions31, was analyzed using atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). As shown in Fig. 1a, Ti ions in the pure PTO film display upward displacements relative to the Pb ion columns, indicating a downward polarization along the film’s thickness direction. The thickness of the pure PTO film is ~1030 nm. In contrast, Fig. 1b reveals opposite displacements of Ti ions in the Ta-PTO film, with a thickness of ~1100 nm. The out-of-plane displacement of Ti ions in Ta-PTO increases to ~0.20 Å at the surface, slightly smaller than the ~0.26 Å observed for pure PTO. Piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) confirms that the Ta-PTO film exhibits typical ferroelectric characteristics with a single-domain configuration (Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, the rocking curves scanned around the (002) diffraction peak further confirms the single-crystal nature of the films (Supplementary Fig. 5), with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 0.385° for Ta-PTO and 0.237° for pure PTO, further supporting the single-domain configuration.

To uncover the mechanism behind the opposite polarization direction in pure PTO and Ta-PTO films, we analyzed their interface structures. Figure 1c, d show the lattice parameters a and c. The interfacial c-value in Ta-PTO ( ~ 3.99 Å) is smaller than in pure PTO ( ~ 4.11 Å). Notably, it increases to ~4.15 Å within the first three unit cells above the interface, indicating an inhomogeneous compressive strain (~3.86%) between the interface and the inner regions of the Ta-PTO film. The interfacial strain was further analyzed using geometric phase analysis (GPA) based on atomic-level HAADF-STEM images (Supplementary Fig. 6). As shown in Fig. 1e, f, unlike pure PTO film, Ta-PTO film exhibits a distinct interfacial compressive strain (εyy) layer, likely caused by the incorporation of Ta dopants, considering the slightly larger ionic radius of Ta5+ (0.640 Å) compared to Ti4+ (0.605 Å)32. At the nanoscale, a large stress gradient can induce a local electric field via the flexoelectric effect. Such gradients can be generated artificially, for example, by localized mechanical loading with an AFM tip, as widely reported to induce flexoelectric fields and control ferroelectric polarization33,34. This effect arises from the coupling between a strain gradient and ferroelectric polarization35. Moreover, phase-field modeling was used to calculate the flexoelectric field within the PTO layer caused by strain gradient from local concentration inhomogeneity36 (Supplementary Note 1). As shown in Fig. 1g, the calculated field strength at six unit cells can be up to 130 kV cm−1, much higher than the coercive field (Ec) of PTO37, enabling polarization reversal. This flexoelectric effect energetically favors the reversal of the polarization direction of the Ta-PTO film layer near the interface from downward to upward, creating a built-in field that could switch the polarization throughout the film (Fig. 1h).

The opposite polarization direction of the films shown in Fig. 1a, b necessitates different screening mechanisms. Below the Curie temperature (Tc), Ps in PTO generates a depolarization field due to opposite surface-bound charges, which destabilizes the polarization38. Effective screening or compensation of these charges is crucial for stabilizing a single-domain structure24,39. For pure PTO film, positively charged oxygen vacancies typically screen negatively charged surfaces, while electrons from the electrode compensate for positively charged surfaces, as revealed in PbZrxTi1-xO3 and BFO films25,27. As a comparison, the cross-section microstructure of Ta-PTO film was systemically characterized by using advanced electron microscopy techniques to understand its screening mechanism.

Figure 2a, d indicates an atomically sharp interface and a smooth surface of the Ta-PTO film. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) line scanning (Fig. 2b) shows a decrease in the Ti signal and an increase in the Ta signal within three unit cells near the interface, while the Pb signal remains nearly unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 7a–c). In addition, a significant enhancement of Ta signals near the interface (Fig. 2c) can be confirmed by time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS). These results support that Ta dopants probably occupy Ti sites in the perovskite tetragonal PTO lattice. The electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) characterizations indicate that Ta doping induces oxygen vacancies near the interface (Supplementary Fig. 8). The relatively higher peak 2 compared to peak 1 in the upper O-K spectra confirms that oxygen vacancies are largely confined within ~4 nm near the Ta-PTO film interface40. Meanwhile, the Pb intensity decreases towards the surface within ~5 nm of the Ta-PTO thin film (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 7d–e). Additionally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirms the presence of Pb vacancies in the surface layer (Supplementary Fig. 9) and reveals that the Pb/Ti ratio in Ta-PTO film is ~14.1% lower than in pure PTO film. Therefore, in single-domain Ta-PTO thin films, the polarization screening by negatively charged Pb vacancies and positively charged oxygen vacancies stabilizes the upward polarization, as shown in Fig. 2f.

In addition, the Ti ion displacement related to Pb position has been used to determine the polarization intensity. For example, the Ps intensity of PTO is ~67 μC cm−2 when the displacement is ~0.38 Å24. In contrast, Ta-PTO shows a reduced polarization intensity with a ~ 0.2 Å displacement, corresponding to a Pb vacancy concentration of ~16% that can be inferred by complete polarization screening (34 μC cm−2) near the surface.

The screening mechanism in Fig. 2f could mediate a band structure of Ta-PTO films. To understand this issue, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Supplementary Note 2). The calculations were conducted at a 12.5% concentration of both Pb vacancies and Ta substitutions (Supplementary Fig. 10). The results revealed that Ta substitution for Ti (TaTi·) is more easily formed, acting as an effective donor, while Pb vacancies behave as an acceptor. The local density of states (LDOS) across Ta-doped, pristine, and Pb-vacancy-rich regions in Fig. 2g, h indicates a type-II band alignment. In the Pb-vacancy-rich region, the Fermi level is closer to the valence band maximum (VBM), raising both the VBM and conduction band minimum (CBM) by ~0.2 eV. Conversely, in the Ta-doped region, the Fermi level is closer to the CBM, lowering both the CBM and VBM by a similar energy offset. To this end, we have illustrated the energy band structure of Ta-PTO (Fig. 2i). The synergistic effect of surface Pb vacancies, the pristine phase, and the Ta-doped interfacial layer collectively forms a band-bending structure and a corresponding built-in electric field that is analogous to a p-i-n structure. Moreover, the presence of a built-in electric field was further verified by kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12)41, where the surface potential was found and decreased gradually from the Ta-PTO film surface towards the NSTO substrate.

Since a p-i-n structure is available in Ta-PTO film, it is interesting to explore PV performance of Ta-PTO film (~2.8 eV in bandgap) using a 375 nm linearly polarized laser. Figure 3a, b shows the current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the films under light excitation and dark field, respectively. Under zero bias, Ta-PTO film achieves an ultrahigh short-circuit current (JSC) of 1018.5 μA ( ~ 130 mA cm−2), remarkably surpassing the 31.6 μA ( ~ 4.03 mA cm− 2) observed in pure PTO film under 500 mW cm−2 irradiation. The corresponding open-circuit voltage (VOC) is ~1.31 V, exceeding the ~1.22 V of pure PTO film. Notably, the pure PTO film exhibits a linear J-V curve in the -1.5 to 1.5 V voltage range, indicating typical bulk PV behavior42. In contrast, Ta-PTO film shows a nonlinear J-V response, which is probably attributed to a p-i-n structure, as shown in Fig. 2i.

To further evaluate the PV performance of the Ta-PTO film, the response under varying light intensities (Ilight) were measured. JSC increases almost linearly with Ilight due to the generation of more photogenerated carriers. In contrast, VOC gradually saturates as the quasi-Fermi level separation reaches its limit (Fig. 3c)4. As shown in Fig. 3d, the JSC response to the on-off switching of light at zero bias demonstrated excellent control and stability. To accurately assess the response speed of the Ta-PTO film, a time-resolved pulse response system was utilized with a 266 nm pulsed laser. The zero bias pulse response, shown in Fig. 3e, reveals a rise time (tr) of ~0.49 μs (10% to 90%) and a decay time (td) of ~15.66 μs (90% to 10%). Figure 3f shows the photoresponsivity (R = JSC/Ilight) under zero bias and different wavelengths, with a maximum value reaching 0.5078 A W−1 at 375 nm (Ilight = 50 mW cm−2) and 0.1477 A W−1 at 254 nm (Ilight = 4.09 mW cm−2) (Supplementary Fig. 13). Moreover, R of Ta-PTO film was compared with different UV detectors based on ferroelectric and classic semiconductors from literatures, as presented in Fig. 3g. Remarkably, its self-powered response surpasses emerging ferroelectric materials by over two orders of magnitude. In the deep ultraviolet region, the responsivity of the Ta-PTO detector surpasses that of p-i-n structured third-generation semiconductors, such as GaN and SiC (Supplementary Table 1).

Ta-PTO film device exhibits outstanding PV performance not only in the UV range but also in the hard X-ray band (10–100 keV). For long-term device applications, the structural stability of perovskite ferroelectric oxides is highly desirable compared to halide perovskite43. More attractively, its high density (~7.52 g cm−3) and the large atomic number of Pb (82) make the film attractive for a direct-type X-ray detector. To evaluate the photon absorption properties, the photon cross-section database44 was used in conjunction with the material densities to calculate the photon absorption spectra of Si, amorphous Se, CdZnTe (CZT), organic-inorganic hybrid halide perovskite methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbBr3; MA = CH3NH3+), purely inorganic halide perovskite CsPbBr3, and PTO in the X-ray energy range of 0.001 to 1 MeV (Fig. 4a). Due to its high theoretical density, and disregarding K, L, and M edge resonant absorptions, PTO demonstrates an obvious advantage in absorption coefficient across the entire photon energy range.

The J-V characteristics of the Ta-PTO film detector under X-ray irradiation and in dark conditions are shown in Fig. 4b. A key performance for X-ray detectors is sensitivity (C Gyair−1 cm−3), which is calculated by determining the slope of the linear region in the current density-dose rate relationship and multiplying it by the active layer thickness. The Ta-PTO film detector achieved an impressive sensitivity of 4.73 C Gyair−1 cm−3 (Fig. 4c). As compared in Supplementary Table 2, the sensitivity of Ta-PTO detector under zero bias is much higher than that of typical X-ray detection materials. It is approximately three orders of magnitude higher than that of commercial amorphous Se detectors (0.00125 C Gyair−1 cm−3) operating under a 2 kV bias, and about 50 times higher than that of CZT detectors (0.087 C Gyair−1 cm−3). Furthermore, it surpasses the performance of CsPbBr₃ thin films (0.26 C Gyair−1 cm−3) and two-dimensional layered perovskite (BA)2(MA)2Pb3I10 (0.276 C Gyair−1 cm−3) under zero bias, indicating its superior sensitivity for advanced X-ray detection.

An ultra-low dark current of 1.8 nA cm−2 has been obtained in the detector at zero bias, resulting in an on/off ratio exceeding 500 and stable switching behavior (Supplementary Fig. 14). The radiation tolerance of the detector was evaluated by subjecting it to X-ray radiation at a dose rate of 1.46 mGyair s−1 for a total duration of 2.5 h, where a cumulative dose is up to 14 Gyair, approximately equivalent to the dose received from 10,000 chest digital radiography (DR) scans45. These performances confirm the excellent radiation stability of the Ta-PTO detector, highlighting its reliability under extended exposure to X-ray irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 15). As shown in Fig. 4d, we further constructed an automated scanning system to evaluate the self-powered X-ray imaging capability of the detector. A seahorse specimen and coil spring were selected as the imaging target (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 16a, b), successfully visualizing both the external outline and internal structure of the samples under zero bias (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 16c). In contrast, a commercial amorphous Se detector failed to obtain similar imaging resolution by using the same seahorse specimen (Supplementary Fig. 17).

In summary, we report polarization-controlled PV response in single-domain PTO films via an interfacial Ta dopant. An ultrahigh PV current of ~130 mA cm−2 at 375 nm was obtained, and the corresponding photoresponsivity under zero bias was 100 times higher than that of the emerged ferroelectrics. Interestingly, a self-powered X-ray detector with excellent sensitivity has been fabricated using this film, enabling high-quality specimen imaging under X-ray exposure. Theoretical and experimental studies reveal that interfacial Ta doping during solution epitaxy controls the polarization direction via the flexoelectric effect, while simultaneously creating a Pb vacancy layer near the film surface for polarization screening. Both n-type interfacial doping and p-type surface defect layers form a p-i-n structure throughout the film, resulting in the observed photovoltaic properties and enhanced device performance. Our findings could pave the way for energy conversion and optoelectronic devices ranging from the UV to the X-ray band by using ferroelectric materials.

Methods

Materials

Lead nitrate (Pb(NO3)2, ≥99.999%), tetrabutyl titanate (TBOT, ≥98%) were purchased from Aladdin Chemistry Co. Tantalum pentoxide (Ta2O5, ≥99.999%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Potassium hydroxide (KOH, ≥85.0%), acetone (≥99.5%), and ethanol (≥99.7%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. All the reagents were used as received. single-crystal 0.7 wt.% Nb-doped SrTiO3 (NSTO) substrates with dimensions 10 × 10 × 0.5 mm3 and (100) orientation were obtained from Shanghai Institute of Optics and Mechanics.

Film preparation

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 18, PTO and Ta-PTO thin films were synthesized by a hydrothermal solution-epitaxy method. Pb(NO3)2, TBOT, and Ta2O5 were used as metal precursors, and KOH served as the mineralizer. NSTO single-crystal substrates were ultrasonically cleaned in acetone, ethanol, deionized water, and ethanol sequentially, and then placed in a Teflon holder for drying.

In a Teflon liner, 15 mL of deionized water was first added. KOH (9.54 g) was dissolved to prepare a solution with a total precursor concentration of 6.8 M. Subsequently, 1.708 mL of TBOT was added under vigorous stirring. For Ta-PTO growth, Ta2O5 was introduced at 0.11 g, corresponding to a Ta substitution level of 10 mol% (Ta/(Ta+Ti) = 10%). The Pb/Ti molar ratio was maintained at 1.25:1 to compensate for Pb volatility. Accordingly, Pb(NO3)2 (2.04 g) was dissolved separately in 10 mL of deionized water and then added dropwise into the precursor solution under continuous stirring.

After stirring for 2 h at room temperature, the NSTO substrate was placed horizontally in the precursor solution, positioned approximately 15 mm above the bottom of the Teflon liner. The Teflon liner was then sealed inside a stainless-steel autoclave, and the hydrothermal epitaxial growth was carried out at 200 °C for 12 h. After the reaction, the samples were thoroughly cleaned by repeated ultrasonication in deionized water and ethanol, and then dried in air at 60 °C.

Characterization

The structures of the sample were characterized by Scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi SU-70 Analytical FESEM), X-ray Diffraction (XRD, Thermo ARL X’TRA powder diffractometer, Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.54056 Å), Piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM, Asylum Research, Cypher S and MFP-3D), X-Ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi). An integrated Focused Ion Beam (FIB, Helios nanolab 600) and SEM (Hatachi S4700) was used to extract thin-film lamellae from the bulk sample. Pt and Au layers were deposited on the film surface to protect from damage and increase the conductivity, respectively. Subsequent cross-sectional observations of the film were carried out using an aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (FEI Titan G2 80-200 Chemi STEM, ThermoFisher Spectra 300). The variation in composition across the thickness of PTO films were characterized by Time-of-flight secondary-ion mass (TOF-SIMS, IONTOF GmbH TOF.SIMS 5-100).

Ti-ion displacement analysis

Ti off-center displacements were quantified from atomic-level HAADF-STEM images by measuring the offset between each Ti atomic column and the geometric center of its four nearest Pb neighbors. Atomic column positions were determined using Gaussian peak fitting, and the out-of-plane displacement component was extracted to evaluate local polarization. Displacement statistics were collected over multiple unit cells to analyze polarization variation across the film thickness.

Photovoltaic measurement

Photovoltaic properties of PTO and Ta-PTO films were measured using optical units from Thorlabs and a source-measure unit (Keithley 2450). Top Ag electrodes (diameter: ~1 mm, thickness: ~20 nm, determined using an ellipsometer) were deposited by magnetron sputtering (VCT300) on the PTO surface as the top electrode, and NSTO substrate acted as the bottom electrode. The films were illuminated with light sources including a 254 nm UV mercury lamp (XingChuangUV) and lasers at 266 nm (MPL-F-266), 375 nm (MDL-III-375), 405 nm (MDL-III-405), and 532 nm (MGL-III-532). The response time was measured using a preamplifier (Stanford Research Systems SR570) and an oscilloscope (Tektronix TBS2204B).

X-ray detection performance measurement

The X-ray detection performance evaluation was carried out using an Amptek Mini-X2 tube with an energy ≤ 50 keV and a peak intensity at 22 keV. The output cone angle of X-ray was 120°, and the tube voltage was kept at 50 kV. A Radcal Accu-Gold+10×6-180 ion chamber dosimeter was used to calibrate the dose rate, which was adjusted by varying the tube current. The X-ray detector was placed at the same position as the dosimeter. The photocurrent and dark current from the device were recorded using a source measure unit (Keithley 2635B).

For the X-ray imaging test, a single-pixel imaging system was constructed. A lead mask with a 0.5 × 0.5 mm² hole was used to collimate the incident X-rays. The object to be imaged was moved between the X-ray source and the detector using an X-Y linear stage (PI M-406.2DG) with a step size of 300 µm. The photocurrent at each point was recorded using a source measure units (Keithley 2635B). For comparison, the remote-vision mobile DR uDR 380i Pro amorphous Se imaging device from Shanghai United Imaging Healthcare was used.
