Effects of playing with active video games on anthropometric indices and nutritional behaviors among children and adolescents: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Roghayeh Molani-Gol, Fatemeh Hamedi-Kalajahi, Nafiseh Rahimi, Hossein Mashhadi Abdolahi, Zeinab Nikniaz, Leila Nikniaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of overweight and obesity among children and adolescents is increasing around the world, and childhood obesity is associated with health status in adulthood. Although active video games (AVGs) have been proposed as a promising strategy to increase physical activity in youth, evidence regarding their impact on obesity-related anthropometric outcomes and nutritional behaviors, particularly among overweight or obese children and adolescents, remains inconsistent. This systematic review aims to summarize the current knowledge regarding the effects of AVGs on anthropometric indices and nutritional behaviors among children and adolescents.MethodA comprehensive systematic search was done on Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar databases without date restrictions until December 2025 by relevant keywords. All relevant original randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in English that evaluated the effects of AVGs on anthropometric indices and nutritional behaviors among children and adolescents were eligible for the present review. The quality of included RCTs was assessed by the Cochrane Handbook. The meta-analysis was conducted using the random model effect and STATA software.ResultsIn total, 39 RCTs comprising 3232 children and adolescents were included in this review. The results of the meta-analysis indicated that playing with AVGs significantly reduced the zBMI (SMD = -0.033, 95% CI (-0.060, -0.007), p = 0.013), BMI (SMD = -0.744, 95% CI (-0.839, -0.649), p < 0.001), and body fat percentage (SMD = -0.516, 95% CI (-0.785, -0.248), p < 0.001) of children and adolescents, but not body weight (SMD = 0.269, 95% CI (-0.115, 0.654), p = 0.170) of them. Moreover, the subgroup analyses revealed that AVGs significantly decreased zBMI, BMI, and body fat percentage in the studies on adolescent and subjects aged > 11 years, with sample size ≥ 100, and intervention duration > 12 weeks. Furthermore, the RCTs reported that playing AVGs significantly increased energy expenditure among children and adolescents and could cause developing healthier consumption behaviors, but did not impact the food or energy intake of children and adolescents.ConclusionThe findings of the current review revealed that playing with AVGs could improve anthropometric indices especially among adolescents. However, more studies are required to confirm these achievements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06619-7.Keywords: Active video game, AVG, Obesity, Children, Adolescents

Introduction

It was reported that over 390 million children and adolescents aged 5–19 years were overweight, with 160 million among them living with obesity in 2022 [1]. Childhood obesity has significant impacts on children’s physical health, as well as their social and emotional well-being and self-esteem. Furthermore, it correlates with poor academic achievement and diminished quality of life for the child [2]. Today, numerous children are extensively involved in screen-based and seated media activities like watching TV, using computers, and playing video games, which are thought to decrease the time children dedicate to engaging in sports and other physical activities [3, 4]. Sedentary behaviors, a decrease in physical activity, and the increasing consumption of energy-dense foods are cited as key factors contributing to the prevalence of obesity and its associated metabolic complications in children [5]. Research findings indicate that seated video games not only reduce physical activity but also lead to increased energy intake and overconsumption of food. Interestingly, this overconsumption occurs without a corresponding increase in feelings of hunger [6].

Active video games (AVGs) are interactive digital games that require whole-body movements to control on-screen actions, contrasting with traditional sedentary video games that involve minimal physical effort [7]. The new generation of AVGs has the potential to significantly contribute to energy expenditure, help children achieve recommended levels of moderate to vigorous physical activity, and improve body composition [8, 9]. AVGs may improve adherence to physical activity programs among children, especially those who are overweight, by enhancing enjoyment, motivation, and engagement compared with traditional exercise programs [10, 11]. Studies have shown that playing AVGs can acutely increase energy expenditure up to threefold compared to seated video games [12, 13]. Some previous systematic reviews also demonstrated that AVG could be a useful strategy to promote physical activity levels in children, as complementary to traditional exercise [14, 15]. For example, a meta-analysis by Ho et al. indicated that AVGs are effective in attaining vigorous, moderate to vigorous, and moderate physical activity levels among children and adolescents [14]. Despite numerous studies highlighting the positive impact of games on children’s weight and energy expenditure [14, 16, 17], there are also contradictory findings indicating that AVGs may not affect the mentioned factors [18]. Given that AVGs combine screen exposure and physical activity, they may influence both movement and eating patterns; hence, this review included nutritional outcomes to explore possible behavioral cross-effects. Moreover, nutritional behaviors were included because several AVG interventions incorporate diet-related components or influence eating patterns through changes in energy expenditure or behavioral engagement.

To our best knowledge, there isn’t a comprehensive systematic review that summarizes the current evidence regarding the effect of AVGs on nutritional behaviors in children and adolescents. Moreover, this review extends previous work by providing updated pooled estimates with the inclusion of nutritional outcomes, which have been underrepresented in prior analyses. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to comprehensively summarize the findings of studies about the impact of AVGs on body weight and associated indices and nutritional behavior among children and adolescents and present precise estimates.

Materials and methods

Data sources and search strategy

The systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline [19] (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, the protocol of this review was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251170209). The research question focuses on the potential association between playing AVGs and obesity indices in children and adolescents. A comprehensive systematic search was done on Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar databases without date restrictions until January 2025. The search strategy employed the following pattern: “(game OR Exergaming OR exergame OR gamification OR Video Game OR VR OR Virtual Reality OR Augmented Reality OR kinect OR xbox OR play station OR nintendo) AND (child OR adolescent OR children) AND (nutrition OR eating behaviour OR feeding behaviour OR food OR diet OR body composition OR body mass index OR obesity OR obese OR fat)”. Mesh terms were also applied to the PubMed search. The search strategy for databases is presented in Supplementary Table 2. Additionally, grey literature sources, clinical trial registries, and reference lists of prior systematic reviews and the included studies were screened to find all eligible studies.

The search was limited to English-language studies published up to December 2025. Literature was imported into EndNote software (Version X9) for citation management and to optimize the review process.

Eligibility criteria

The study conducted a systematic literature search to identify original full-text randomized controlled trials (RCTs) focusing on the impacts of playing with AVGs on body weight indices, body fat percentage, and nutritional behaviors in children and adolescents. As shown in Table 1, the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, Study design) criteria for this review are Population: children and adolescents aged 5–18 years without serious movement disorders, Intervention: AVGs with different intervention duration (short and long terms), Comparison: any other games such as sedentary gaming and usual activity, or no intervention, Outcomes: change in anthropometric indices and nutritional behaviors, and Study design: RCTs. Studies with multicomponent behavioral interventions were not eligible for this meta-analysis. Moreover, the review excluded studies with non-interventional designs, studies lacking control groups, conference publications, book chapters, letters, posters, commentaries, theses, editorial content, and review articles. Additionally, articles lacking full-text availability were omitted from consideration.

Study selection

Following the elimination of duplicate articles, RMG and NR independently screened the retrieved literature according to predefined eligibility criteria. The titles and abstracts of the articles were evaluated based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full texts of eligible papers were acquired and subjected to further evaluation. Studies lacking sufficient information or failing to meet the predefined criteria were excluded. Disagreements between two authors were resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer (LN).

Data extraction

Extracted data from chosen studies included the author’s name, the location and design of studies, publication year, children’s demographics (age, gender), sample size, type of AVGs, intervention duration, covariates, and conclusions regarding the correlation between AVGs and obesity indices. The included studies used varying methods to assess body mass index (BMI), body fat composition, and dietary intake, which may contribute to heterogeneity in the pooled estimates.

Primary anthropometric outcomes included change in BMI, BMI z-score (zBMI), and body fat percentage, obtained using standardized clinical measurements. Nutritional outcomes included dietary energy and macronutrient intake, which were assessed using validated dietary questionnaires. Due to variability in outcome measurement across studies (e.g., BMI vs. zBMI, and differences in dietary assessment tools), pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, as these methodological differences may contribute to high heterogeneity among the included studies and influence comparability.

Risk of bias and GRAD assessment

The quality of the included RCTs was evaluated by the Cochrane Handbook [20]. The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool includes six domains of bias and three risk-of-bias response options: “low,” “high,” and “unclear” [21]. The included studies were considered to have a “low risk of bias” if they had a low risk of bias in all three key domains (selection, detection, and performance domains); an “unclear risk of bias” if they had an unclear risk of bias in one or more than one key domain; and a “high risk of bias” if it had a high risk of bias in one or more than one key domain [21]. As blinding of participants was not feasible in AVG interventions, we explicitly evaluated the risk-of-bias implications related to performance and detection bias. Moreover, we assessed the certainty of evidence for each primary outcome using the GRADE approach.

Statistical analyses

Because outcomes were measured using different scales across studies, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was used as the primary effect size (ES) metric. The mean difference and standard deviation (SD) of the RCTs were pooled using the random-effect model to express SMD and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The SD of the mean change was calculated using the following formula: SD = square root [(SD pretreatment^2)+(SD posttreatment^2)-(2R × SD pretreatment × SD posttreatment)], in which a correlation coefficient (R) of 0.8 was assumed. When a standard error of the mean (SEM) or standard error (SE) was reported instead of SD, the SD was calculated based on the following formula: SD¼ SEM √n (n ¼ sample size in each group). The heterogeneity of included studies was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test (with a significant P-value < 0.1) and I-square test (I2 greater than 50%, showing significant heterogeneity). A subgroup analysis was conducted for zBMI and body fat percentage based on the year of publication, sample size, intervention duration, and participants’ age in the included studies. Because of the small number of RCTs, we are unable to conduct subgroup and sensitivity analyses for BMI and weight. Meta-regression was conducted when the number of eligible studies per outcome was ≥ 10. The sensitivity analysis evaluated the effect of each study on the overall ES by removing a single trial and repeating the analysis. The funnel plot, as well as the Begg and Egger tests, was used to determine the publication bias. To adjust for the impact of publication bias, the trim and fill method was applied. The meta-analysis was conducted using STATA 17.0 software (Stata Corp., College Station, TX), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Study selection

The search and study selection process (PRISMA diagram) for this systematic review is shown in Fig. 1. In total, 5970 articles were achieved through searching in PubMed (n = 1397), Web of Science (n = 2283), Scopus (n = 1620), and Google Scholar (n = 670) databases. After removing the duplicate articles, 4430 studies remained for further screening. Of these, 4371 studies were excluded in the first stage after screening the titles and abstracts of the articles. Finally, 59 articles were critically analyzed which 20 articles were excluded because of studying the effects of other types of video games (n = 3), studying other age groups (n = 4), being study protocol (n = 3), cohort (n = 1), and cross-sectional (n = 5), and studying the effects of video games only on physical activity levels (n = 4). Finally, 39 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included in this systematic review.

Characteristics of the included studies

Table 2 presents the main characteristics of 39 eligible RCTs. The included studies were published from 2007 to 2025. The location of the studies was as follows: eleven cases in the USA, four cases in the UK, four cases in France, three cases in New Zealand, three cases in Canada, four cases in China, three cases in Turkey, two cases in the Netherlands, two cases in Spain, and one case in Australia, Egypt, and Iran. Twenty-nine RCTs involved both genders of participants, and eight cases included males, and two cases included females. Totally, 3232 children and adolescents aged 7–18 years were included in the RCTs. Of the 29 included RCTs, 26 studies were on children, and 14 studies were on adolescents. The participants of the twelve included studies were overweight and obese individuals. The intervention duration in the included RCTs varied from one day to 40 weeks. The intervention was AVGs that were compared with an inactive video game or not playing video games.

As shown in Tables 2 and 21 cases of the included studies evaluated the impacts of video games on the anthropometric indices [11, 22, 24–26, 28–30, 32, 34–37, 39, 41, 45–50, 53]. Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment details of the included RCTs were described in Supplementary Table 3. Based on the Cochrane Collaboration’s criteria, 33 RCTs had a low risk of bias, and six had a high risk of bias. Most of the included RCTs have not reported clear data about random sequence generation and allocation concealment. Moreover, due to the properties of the intervention, the studies were unable to blind the participants; thus, the blinding item was not considered in the total scoring. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer. As shown in Table 3, the obtained evidence had a moderate certainty due to the high heterogeneity among the included studies based on the GRADE checklist.

Outcomes

Effects of playing AVGs on zBMI

Combining the findings of nine trials of 1113 children and adolescents evaluated the effects of AVGs playing on zBMI among children and adolescents demonstrated a significant decrease in zBMI of participants (SMD = -0.033, 95% CI (-0.060, -0.007), p = 0.013) (Fig. 2A). There was relatively a high heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 73.2%, Pheterogeneity <0.001). Based on the sub-group analysis, AVGs playing significantly reduced zBMI in studies published before 2020 (SMD = -0.037, 95% CI (-0.065, -0.009), p = 0.010), adolescents and age ≥ 11 year (SMD = -0.049, 95% CI (-0.081, -0.018), p = 0.002), sample size ≥ 100 (SMD = -0.041, 95% CI (-0.078, -0.005), p = 0.028), and intervention duration > 12 weeks (SMD = -0.062, 95% CI (-0.059, -0.030), p < 0.001) (Table 4). Sensitivity analysis revealed that by removing Maddison et al. (2011) (SMD = -0.019, 95% CI (-0.051, 0.013)), Foley et al. (2014) (SMD = -0.025, 95% CI (-0.054, -0.002)), and Christison et al. (2016) (SMD = -0.027, 95% CI (-0.056, 0.001)) the obtained effect became non-significant (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Begg test results showed that there was no significant small study bias (p = 0.297). However, there was an asymmetry in the funnel plot (Fig. 2B); thus, the Trim and Fill method was used, which did not impute any study (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Given the high heterogeneity, these results should be interpreted with caution. Because of the small number of RCTs, the meta-regression test was not conducted.

Effects of playing AVGs on BMI

Eleven studies involving 1435 children and adolescents reported the effects of AVG playing on BMI, pooling their findings indicated that playing AVG have a significant effect on reducing BMI of participants (SMD = -0.744, 95% CI (-0.839, -0.649), p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A). There was a high heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 96.6%, Pheterogeneity <0.001). Based on the subgroup analysis, AVGs significantly decreased BMI in studies on adolescents (SMD = -0.874, 95% CI (-0.977, -0.772)), participants aged > 11 (SMD = -0.865, 95% CI (-0.966, -0.764)), and with sample size ≥ 100 (SMD = -0.823, 95%CI (-0.922, -0.723)) (Table 4). However, the publication year and intervention duration of the included RCTs did not affect the findings (Table 4). Upon the sensitivity analysis, after excluding each study, the obtained effect did not change (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Egger’s tests indicated no small study effect (p = 0.611), but an asymmetry was also observed in the funnel plot (Fig. 3B). Thus, the Trim and fill method was applied, and by imputing four studies, the magnitude of ES was increased but did not alter (SMD = -0.954, 95% CI (-1.657, -0.251)) (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Given the substantial heterogeneity, these results should be interpreted with caution.

Forest plot (A) and funnel plot (B) of the effect of AVGs on BMI. CI, Confidence interval; ES, Effect size

The results of meta-regression analysis indicated that there was an association between the age of participants and the obtained ES (Coefficient = -0.629, p = 0.011) (Supplementary Fig. 3A); however, the sample size (Coefficient = 0.0001, p = 0.981) (Supplementary Fig. 3B) and intervention duration (Coefficient = 0.077, p = 0.306) (Supplementary Fig. 3C) of the included studies had no significant association with the obtained results.

Effects of playing AVGs on body weight

The pooled ESs of five RCTs comprising 643 participants demonstrated no significant reduction in the body weight of children and adolescents after playing AVGs (SMD = 0.269, 95% CI (-0.115, 0.654), p = 0.170) (Fig. 4A). There was high heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 86.5%, Pheterogeneity < 0.001). Because of the small number of included RCTs evaluating the effects of AVG playing on weight, we could not conduct a subgroup analysis. Sensitivity analyses showed that sequential removal of the study by Coknaz et al. (2019), the effects of AVGs on reducing body weight of participants became significant (SMD = -0.567, 95% CI (-1.076, -0.057)) (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Moreover, Begg test results revealed no small study effect (p = 0.327), but an asymmetry was observed in the funnel plot (Fig. 4B). Therefore, the Trim and fill procedure was done but, imputing two studies did not change the results (ES = 0.291, 95% CI (-1.050, 1.633)) (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Due to the high heterogeneity, these results should be interpreted with caution.

Effects of playing AVGs on body fat

Thirteen included RCTs, including 1702 children and adolescents, assessed the impact of playing AVGs on the body fat percentage of the participants. Combining their findings using the random effect model indicated a significant reduction in the body fat of children and adolescents after AVGs playing (SMD = -0.516, 95% CI (-0.785, 0.248), p < 0.001) (Fig. 5A). There was a relatively high heterogeneity among the trials (I2 = 56%, Pheterogeneity = 0.007). However, the subgroup analysis showed that AVGs did not reduce body fat percent in the studies on children and participants aged < 11 years old (SMD = 0.235, 95% CI (-0.266, 0.735), p = 0.358), with sample size < 100 (SMD = -0.035, 95% CI (-0.509, 0.438), p = 0.884), and intervention duration ≤ 12 weeks (SMD = -0.170, 95% CI (-0.561, 0.221), p = 0.394) (Table 4). In addition, the publication year of the RCTs did not influence the AVGs’ effects on the body fat of children and adolescents (Table 4). Upon sensitivity analysis, omitting each study did not change overall ES (Supplementary Fig. 5A). The Egger test did not detect a small study effect (p = 0.753); however, an asymmetry was observed in the funnel plot (Fig. 5B). As a result, the Trim and fill test was conducted that imputed one study and the results did not alter (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Because of relatively high heterogeneity, these results should be interpreted by caution.

Forest plot (A) and funnel plot (B) of the effect of AVGs on body fat percentage. CI, Confidence interval; ES, Effect size

The findings of the meta-regression analyses demonstrated that there was no significant association between the sample size (Coefficient = -0.002, p = 0.156) (Supplementary Fig. 6A), participants’ age (Coefficient = -0.224, p = 0.101) (Supplementary Fig. 6B), and intervention duration (Coefficient = -0.048, p = 0.180) (Supplementary Fig. 6C) of the included RCTs and the obtained results.

Effects of AVGs on nutritional behaviors

Due to the varying outcomes reported for the nutritional behaviors, a meta-analysis was not conducted. Nutritional outcomes yielded inconsistent findings, with some studies reporting a reduction in energy intake and others showing no effect. Most of the included studies demonstrated that playing with AVGs did not have a considerable effect on the food and energy intakes of children and adolescents. However, one of them showed a reduction in food intake in children and adolescents following playing with AVGs.

Discussion

The current study comprehensively summarizes present knowledge regarding the effects of various video games, especially AVGs, on body weight, body composition, and nutritional behaviors in children and adolescents. The findings of the meta-analysis demonstrated that playing AVGs were associated with modest but potentially clinically meaningful reductions in the anthropometric indices, such as zBMI, BMI, and body fat percentage of children and adolescents, although the magnitude of these effects varied across studies and should be interpreted in light of the substantial heterogeneity observed. However, AVGs’ playing did not have a significant effect on the body weight of participants. Moreover, the subgroup analyses revealed that AVGs significantly decreased zBMI, BMI, and body fat percentage in the studies on adolescents and subjects aged > 11 years, with sample size ≥ 100, and intervention duration > 12 weeks. Trends toward improvement among adolescents and with longer interventions suggest possible age- and duration-related modifiers of effect. Moreover, it suggests that AVGs may be particularly well suited for older children and adolescents, who generally have greater cognitive maturity and motor skills, potentially enabling higher participation and more sustained engagement than younger children. In addition, it highlights the importance of sustained exposure, indicating that short-term or acute AVG programs may be insufficient to produce meaningful changes in body composition. Therefore, AVG-based interventions are more likely to be effective when integrated into structured, longer-term programs, such as school-based curricula or supervised after-school settings. However, it is important to note that subgroup findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, particularly where the number of studies was limited. Regarding high heterogeneity among the RCTs, it likely stems from variability in AVG types, intervention duration, gaming platforms, participant status, and outcome measurement methods. Differences in intervention properties and intensity, and levels of supervision, may be possible sources of heterogeneity. In addition, participant characteristics such as age range, baseline weight status, and developmental stage may influence engagement, adherence, and physiological responsiveness to AVG interventions. Moreover, lack of blinding and unclear randomization procedures in several trials may have influenced subjective outcomes and contributed to bias.

These findings align with earlier systematic reviews showing that AVGs may increase short-term energy expenditure but demonstrate variable effects on obesity-related anthropometric outcomes. Some meta-analyses reported that AVG might be a good alternative for sedentary behavior and complementary to traditional exercise [55, 56]. Several systematic reviews also stress that AVG can be a complementary tool to increase physical activity and health [56–58]. AVG may help to achieve a more active and healthier lifestyle among children who are overweight and obese [59] if the AVG interventions were structured and supervised [60]. There was a negative relationship between physical activity and self-esteem with obesity among children [61]. Obesity-related comorbidities like coronary heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, cancer, and depression might arise due to decreased physical activity [62]. The enhanced indoor use of sedentary screen time is usually the reason for decreased activity in children [63]. Children usually prefer not to perform outdoor physical activity, possibly because of parental anxiety about the dangers of playing in outdoor environments, and the enjoyment of inactivity [64]. Furthermore, increased nutritional intake accompanying screen time was reported to be the main cause of obesity and its consequences. Thus, increasing activity and decreasing energy intake can attenuate the problem by reducing the adiposity rebound in the peripubertal years of childhood and adolescence. Targeting these two main causes at games was reported to be useful for the prevention of childhood and consequent adult obesity [65].

Based on the included RCTs, playing video games significantly increased the EE among children and adolescents. Moreover, most of them reported that video games did not impact the food or energy intake of children and adolescents. Games were found to be effective as educational tools for healthy dietary activity to decrease caloric intake, especially if combined with group sessions [65, 66]. Previously, these games have been indicated to encourage children and adolescents for healthy eating and exercise to prevent excess weight [66, 67]. Among these games, AVGs have recently been introduced to decrease the sedentary time for children and thus increase their caloric expenditure [68]. AVGs were regarded as a form of exercise, and the expended energy through them was equal to the physical activity of moderate intensity, which caused 3 to 6 times the amount of resting energy consumption [69]. Therefore, because of requiring physical movement to interact with a gaming console instead of a handheld controller in AVGs, it increases energy expenditure, heart rate, and daily physical activity levels, and finally improves cardiovascular endurance among adolescents [70–72]. Specifically, AVGs could be used as a means to increase energy expenditure and meet the recommendations of physical activity levels for adolescents [73, 74]. AVGs may also help modulate food intake and feelings of hunger, as enhanced physical activity is connected with improved appetite control and reduced feelings of hunger [75, 76]. Physical activity possibly influences eating behaviors through cognitive processes that are related to food intake [77, 78]. Studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging showed that acute bouts of physical activity reduce neural responses to images of food in reward regions of the brain [77, 79].

Evidence regarding AVGs and eating behavior is limited. Most studies indicated reductions in unhealthy snacking and enhancing healthy dietary behaviors, while some reported no significant dietary change, suggesting that behavioral modifications in diet require targeted nutrition components alongside physical activity interventions. The findings of the four included RCTs revealed that video game intervention leads to an increase in fruit and vegetable intake. Providing an action plan to increase the child’s fruit and vegetable intake would be beneficial as part of goal setting. As children have no experience in setting goals to eat more fruit and vegetables, they need guidance in accomplishing this task [80]. Therefore, creating specific plans of how to achieve their fruit and vegetable goals was probably beneficial and is consistent with implementation intention theory [81]. Moreover, the fruit and vegetable options offered were tailored to the child’s fruit and vegetable preferences, and the goals gradually increased in difficulty. These may have enhanced the likelihood that the child would strive to meet the goal and likely contributed to personal mastery, which is the most effective method for achieving behavior change [82]. Finally, satisfaction with behavior change is linked with behavioral maintenance in adults [83].

The most important limitation of the current review was the use of different video games, and various health outcomes were also evaluated. High heterogeneity across studies limits the generalizability of our findings, and for some outcomes, meta-regression was not performed due to the insufficient number of studies. Although most trials were rated as low risk, the absence of blinding and incomplete reporting of randomization procedures may have introduced bias. Additionally, visual inspection of funnel plots suggested potential small-study effects, indicating that smaller trials with positive results may be overrepresented. Thus, the lack of blinding, potential publication bias suggested by funnel plot asymmetry, high heterogeneity among the trials, and the dominance of short- to medium-term interventions were of the main limitations of this review. Future trials should integrate both physical and dietary components, apply standardized outcome measures, and report detailed behavior changes to clarify mechanisms underlying AVG effects. AVGs may offer a feasible addition to school-based or family-based obesity-prevention programs, especially for children who show low adherence to structured exercise.

Conclusion

The results of the current meta-analysis revealed that playing with AVGs could improve the anthropometric indices, such as zBMI, BMI, and body fat percentage, but not the body weight of children and adolescents. Moreover, subgroup analyses revealed that AVGs were more effective in reducing the zBMI, BMI, and body fat percentage of adolescents compared to children, and in RCTs with interventions longer than 12 weeks, suggesting effect modification by age and intervention duration. Moreover, the included RCTs reported that playing with AVGs increased EE and did not impact food or energy intake among children and adolescents. Therefore, evidence regarding the effects of AVGs on nutritional behaviors remains inconsistent and largely dependent on whether interventions incorporated explicit nutrition education components. Overall, AVGs show potential for improvements in some anthropometric outcomes, although evidence remains limited. More rigorously designed RCTs are required to confirm these findings and to clarify the effects of AVGs on anthropometric indices and nutritional behaviors of children and adolescents.
