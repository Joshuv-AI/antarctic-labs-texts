Optimizing the prevention of mother-to-child transmission of hepatitis B through integrated care models: a pilot study in Jiangsu Province, China
Authors: Quan Kong, Lingyun Ding, Yanjun Chen, Jie Jiang, Jiao Qian, Chu He, Jianming Ma, Jianmei Zha, Qi Liang, Yu Huang, Xiaoxiao Chen, Gengfeng Fu, Minfang Yao, Xiangjun Zhai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreventing mother-to-child transmission of hepatitis B (PMTCT) is crucial for reducing the incidence of new hepatitis B virus (HBV) infections. However, the current PMTCT interventions including maternal screening, antiviral prophylaxis, infant immunoprophylaxis, and post-vaccination serological testing, fail to reach a broader population, especially those in rural areas. This pilot study aimed to optimize the PMTCT strategy by integrating existing medical resources, with the goal of achieving high coverage of the complex intervention services.MethodsThis real-world pilot study was conducted from March 4, 2019, to May 27, 2020, in Zhangjiagang, a county under the jurisdiction of Suzhou, Jiangsu Province. We collaborated with multiple healthcare institutions to establish a comprehensive PMTCT strategy that combines decentralized and centralized service delivery. The intervention rate at each healthcare service related to PMTCT was the primary endpoint of the evaluation.ResultsA total of 11,181 pregnant women accepted HBV screening during their first antenatal care (ANC) visit. Among the 272 hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positive individuals, 64 (23.5%) were hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) positive. All 64 HBeAg-positive pregnant women (including one case of twin pregnancy) accepted the referral, of whom 53 met the criteria for antiviral prophylaxis, and 51 (96.2%) received the treatment. All 65 infants born to HBeAg-positive mothers completed post-vaccination serological testing (PVST) in designated institutions or others selected by their parents. The PVST results indicated complete success of the PMTCT strategy.ConclusionsBy integrating centralized and decentralized healthcare services through collaboration among multiple healthcare institutions, it is feasible to establish a whole-process service that provides pregnant women with more timely and comprehensive services. This strategy can improve the rate of antiviral prophylaxis for high-risk pregnant women with HBV MTCT, which is of great significance for eliminating MTCT of HBV.

Background

In 2016, the World Health Assembly issued the Global Health Sector Strategy on Viral Hepatitis 2016–2021, which set the strategic goal of eliminating the public health hazard of hepatitis B by 2030 [1]. However, chronic hepatitis B (CHB) infection remains one of the major global public health problems. In 2019, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported a total of 296 million CHB infections worldwide, with approximately 1.5 million new infections and 820,000 deaths attributed to hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection [2]. The disease burden caused by HBV remains substantial, particularly in endemic areas where mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) during pregnancy and perinatal period is the primary route of HBV dissemination [3]. Among infants infected with HBV at birth, more than 90% develop CHB, and about 20%~60% of children aged 1 to 5 years also develop the condition [4]. Therefore, eliminating MTCT of HBV is crucial for reducing the incidence of HBV infection. An alternative indicator for the WHO assessment of this goal is to achieve a prevalence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) in children aged 5 years less than 0.1% by 2030 [5].

China has the largest number of HBV infections in the Western Pacific region [6]. Before the introduction of the vaccination, approximately 30% to 50% of HBV infections were due to MTCT [7]. To reduce MTCT, the Chinese government implemented the universal hepatitis B vaccine (HepB) immunization strategy in 1992, which includes administering the birth dose vaccine (HepB-BD) to all newborns within 24 h of birth, followed by two additional doses of Hep B at 1 month and 6 months after birth. Subsequently, in 2011, the National Health Commission required maternal healthcare institutions to screen all pregnant women for HBV; all newborns must receive the timely HepB-BD, and newborns born to HBsAg-positive mothers will receive an extra dose of 100 IU hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG) within 24 h, followed by the second and third doses of HepB [8]. All of the above-mentioned vaccination services are provided free of charge, and the associated costs are borne by the government. Since then, China has formally established a mechanism for preventing mother-to-child transmission of hepatitis B (PMTCT), combining maternal HBV screening and infant immunoprophylaxis. After years of efforts, the coverage of timely HepB-BD as well as the overall vaccination of newborns has exceeded 95% [9]. Additionally, the prevalence of HBsAg in children under 5 years old has fallen to less than 1% [10].

Despite these successes, HBV MTCT still occurs in some newborns, even after immunoprophylaxis. Studies have shown that MTCT failures are mainly associated with pregnant women with hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) positive or a high viral load of HBV-DNA [11–14]. A positive HBeAg status indicates active viral replication and strong infectivity [15], and to some extent, is reflective of the HBV-DNA replication level. In China, the prevalence of HBsAg among pregnant women is approximately6.61% [16], among HBsAg-positive pregnant women, the HBeAg positive rate is 25.80% [17], indicating that a considerable number of pregnant women are at high risk of HBV transmission. Therefore, since 2020, many studies have explored the feasibility of antiviral prophylaxis for pregnant women at high risk of HBV MTCT. The result reveals that antiviral prophylaxis for pregnant women who are HBeAg-positive and have a high HBV-DNA load in the third trimester can effectively reduce the incidence of MTCT [18–21]. As a result, peripartum antiviral prophylaxis for high-risk women with HBV MTCT has been gradually adopted by the guidelines of several associations working to prevent and control liver disease [22–24]. The WHO also issued the guidelines for antiviral prophylaxis in pregnant women for PMTCT in 2020 after re-evaluation [25]. In this context, the National Health Commission of China issued the Action Plan for the Elimination of mother-to-child Transmission of AIDS, Syphilis and Hepatitis B (2022–2025) in 2022, proposing to “provide standardized antiviral prophylaxis services for pregnant women with HBV infection who meet the criteria.” At the same time, the Work Standards for the Prevention of mother-to-child transmission of AIDS, Syphilis and Hepatitis B (2020 edition) was revised and released. This document [26] outlines the technical aspects of intervention services aimed at preventing MTCT. For pregnant women with HBV infection, it is recommended that they should start antiviral prophylaxis at 28 weeks of gestation if their maternal serum HBV-DNA viral loads ≥2 × 105IU/ml in the second and third trimesters, provided they give informed consent. However, antiviral prophylaxis for pregnant women remains a recommended and voluntary preventive intervention, lacking a corresponding service framework and the target population’s awareness to accept it. As a result, the rate of antiviral prophylaxis among high-risk pregnant women remains low, with a modeling study showing that the global uptake of peripartum antiviral prophylaxis among eligible women was estimated at about3% in 2022 [6].

Currently, there is no implementation protocol for antiviral prophylaxis covering all pregnant women at high-risk of HBV MTCT in China. Most interventions are typically conducted in large general hospitals or specialized infectious disease hospitals, and the participants are primarily recruited through outpatient clinics, which are unable to reach the entire target population [27, 28]. Therefore, in this pilot study conducted in2019, a set of interventions was jointly established by several healthcare institutions in Zhangjiagang, a county-level city located in Jiangsu Province of China. This is a comprehensive implementation plan for PMTCT that adopted both centralized and decentralized approaches to deliver the interventions to the target population based on local medical resources, including maternal screening, peripartum antiviral prophylaxis, infant immunoprophylaxis and post-vaccination serological testing (PVST). It is hoped that the more comprehensive strategy will be expanded to primary healthcare institutions to expand the coverage of the interventions and help more pregnant women with HBV receive comprehensive services, thereby providinga reference for the implementation of strategies to eliminate HBV MTCT in other areas.

Methods

Study site

This pilot study was conducted from March 4th, 2019 to May 27th, 2020 in the county of Zhangjiagang, which is under the jurisdiction of Suzhou, Jiangsu Province. Zhangjiagang is an economically developed area in China with 1.26 million permanent residents in 2018 [29]. The county’s healthcare system is relatively comprehensive, with a county-level Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), a county-level Maternal and Child Health Institute (MCHI), ten midwifery institutions, and fifteen Community Healthcare Centers (CHCs) that have vaccination clinics and antenatal clinics. Additionally, the county has Zhangjiagang First People’s Hospital, a tertiary hospital with an infectious diseases department capable of diagnosing and treating various infectious diseases, including viral hepatitis. These well-established healthcare services provide a strong foundation for the implementation of the project.

Study design

Providing centralized or decentralized care services

All fifteen CHCs and ten midwifery institutions in the county were recruited to provide decentralized care services. The CHCs were responsible for screening pregnant woman for HBV, reporting HBsAg-positive cases to the MCHI, and administering the second and third dose of the HepB vaccine. The midwifery institutions were responsible for administering the HepB-BD and HBIG injections. Centralized care services, including referring eligible pregnant woman, evaluating the infection status, and providing antiviral treatment, were conducted by the MCHI or Zhangjiagang First People’s Hospital respectively. The county CDC supervised the project’s performance.

Measures

The implementation protocol of the program included four successive steps (Fig. 1):Fig. 1CHC: Community Healthcare centers; ANC: antenatal care; HBsAg: hepatitis B surface antigen; HBeAg: hepatitis B vaccine; HBIG: hepatitis B immunoglobin; PVST: post-vaccination serological testing

CHC: Community Healthcare centers; ANC: antenatal care; HBsAg: hepatitis B surface antigen; HBeAg: hepatitis B vaccine; HBIG: hepatitis B immunoglobin; PVST: post-vaccination serological testing

Step1.Screening and referring:

All pregnant women were screened for HBV infection by complimentary testing of serological markers, including HBsAg, using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) commercial kit during their first antenatal care (ANC) visit to the CHCs. Anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe, and anti-HBc should be tested in the same way if the HBsAg test is positive. Information on HBsAg-positive individuals was reported to the MCHI for standardized management. Among these, HBeAg-positive pregnant women were referred to the PMTCT clinic (P clinic) at Zhangjiagang First People’s Hospital by MCHI during their 20–24 weeks of gestation. A personal file was created for referring individuals to record the personal and PMTCT intervention information.

Step2.Antiviral evaluation and treatment:

To provide centralized services for peripartum antiviral prophylaxis, a special clinic for HBV PMTCT (P clinic) was established at Zhangjiagang First People’s Hospital, which opened once a week on a consistent schedule. At the P clinic, the referred individuals were interviewed by a hepatologist and underwent comprehensive blood tests, including liver function biochemical indicators, HBV serological markers and HBV-DNA quantity testing. Pregnant women with HBV-DNA≥2 × 105IU/ml were advised to receive antiviral prophylaxis. The antiviral drug is TDF, administered at a dosage of 300 mg daily. Regular monitoring should be conducted on a monthly basis and continued until two months after delivery. For those who did not meet the criteria for antiviral prophylaxis or who were unwilling to accept therapy, their infants received standard immunoprophylaxis. The expenses associated with the HBV-DNA test and antiviral prophylaxis of pregnant women during this phase are borne by the project funds.

Step3.Immunoprophylaxis.

All newborns received timely HepB-BD and 100 IU HBIG within 24 h of birth in midwifery institutions. The additional two doses of Hep B were administered according to the national immunization schedule in vaccination clinics. All Hep B and HBIG were uniformly distributed by Zhangjiagang CDC to ensure that newborns received immunization promptly.

Step4.Follow-up and PVST:

The MCHI of the county arranged follow-up visits for infants aged 7–12 months and collected 3 ml of venous blood for PVST to assess whether the PMTCT was successful, including HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe and anti-HBc. MTCT of HBV was defined as being seropositive for HBsAg of infants after completing PVST at 7 to 12 months. If anti-HBs levels were < 10IU/ml, indicating that the infant has not developed an immune response to the HepB, the infants was revaccinated with three doses of Hep B.

Data collection

The data collection was divided into four parts (Fig. 1), carried out by staff from different institutions, representing pregnant women at different stages of the project. All the collected information was recorded in personal file.

Data analysis

All data were statistically analyzed by SPSS26.0. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations. Differences were examined using Student’s t-test, and categorical variables were compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a test level of α = 0.05 and P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of Jiangsu Center for Disease Prevention and Control (approval number: JSJK2019-B013-01). All participants involved in this project have signed informed consent. The data were analyzed and published without any identifiable information.

Results

HBV screening and refereeing

During the implementation of the study, a total of 11,181 pregnant women utilized the ANC service. All participants were screened HBsAg at their first ANC visit, and 272 (2.4%) were identified as HBsAg positive. Among the 272 HBsAg-positive individuals, 64 (23.5%) were found to be HBeAg-positive. Discussion

In China, the implementation of hepatitis B Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI)strategy has yielded encouraging results [9, 10]. However, due to the presence of failed immune blockade, it is difficult to achieve the goal of eliminating HBV MTCT by immunization alone. Therefore, the combination of antiviral prophylaxis for pregnant women with high viral levels and neonatal immunization has been widely recommended strategy to eliminate MTCT [22–25]. In areas where the hepatitis B vaccine immunization strategy is well implemented, increasing the antiviral treatment rate among eligible pregnant women is crucial for eliminating MTCT. To include all the eligible pregnant women and their infants in the combination interventions of maternal antiviral treatment and neonatal immunization in a specific region, this pilot implementation study selected county of Zhangjiagang, which has a population of over one million, to examine the implementation effectiveness of a combined interventions protocol by integrating centralized and decentralized care services. The project’s implementation demonstrated outstanding effectiveness, with 100% of pregnant women screened for HBV in the first trimester and a 96.2% acceptance rate of antiviral prophylaxis among eligible participants. This performance exceeds the WHO’s validation criteria for the elimination of HBV MTCT, which requires ≥ 90% coverage for maternal antenatal HBsAg testing and ≥ 90% coverage for eligible women receiving antiviral treatment [30].

In real-world scenarios, the effectiveness of the HBV PMTCT project depends on the coverage and quality of the interventions involved [31]. The implementation of a project may utilize different organizational forms including hospital-based or community-based approaches [32, 33]. In 2018, a pilot study aimed at eliminating MTCT of HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis B by offering service packages for HBV pregnant women was conducted in Bao’an District of Shenzhen, China, and obtained favorable outcomes [34]. The study in Bao’an established E clinics (elimination of mother-to-child transmission clinics) within obstetrics departments of all 18 hospitals with midwifery qualifications to provide evaluation and antiviral prophylaxis services for the eligible individuals. The E clinics in Bao’an provided decentralized services, contrasting with our protocol, with involves only one P clinic offering centralized services. The differences in implementation design depend on the distribution of medical resources. Bao’an is a developed urban area with more than 5 million people, with many healthcare institutions capable of providing antiviral prophylaxis services for eligible pregnant women, therefore, a decentralized implementation design was adopted. However, the intervention subjects in our study primarily reside in rural areas with limited access to antiviral prophylaxis service. Consequently, our implementation protocol adopts a combination of centralized and decentralized services. The antiviral prophylaxis rate for high-risk pregnant women with HBV MTCT reached 96.2% in our study, surpassing the 63.6% achieved in the first year of implementation in Bao’an. These results demonstrate the advantages of centralized services for eligible individuals. Numerous prior studies have discussed the merits and drawbacks of decentralized and centralized services for patients [35, 36]. Centralized services are conducive to providing high-quality services [36]. In our study, for example, five pregnant women received antiviral treatment due to their unique conditions, while the majority of pregnant women were given antiviral prophylaxis to prevent MTCT. Antiviral treatment may need to be assessed in the context of the pregnant woman’s health status as well as her response to treatment, for this reason, hepatologist appear to be better suited than obstetricians to administer antiviral treatment for pregnant women. Additionally, some pregnant women might refuse antiviral prophylaxis for fetal safety concern, which is often related to the lack of awareness of HBV among pregnant women [37]. Therefore, it is imperative to enhance health education for pregnant women. Hepatologists can provide professional and comprehensive explanations to improve patient compliance. This may partly explain why our intervention rate is higher in our diverse population compared to the study conducted in Bao’an.

Additionally, participants involved in our project need to receive both centralized and decentralized services. Another critical aspect of strategy implementation is the assignment of a responsible institution to connect the centralized and decentralized services. In our study, the linkage was facilitated through case referrals by the county’s MCHI. The MCHI is responsible for ensuring the quality control of HBV screening and for referring of cases. As a result, the screening rate of hepatitis B prior to 24 weeks of gestation has significantly improved, with a success rate for referring eligible cases reaching as high as 100%. This represents a significant improvement over the previous survey, which reported a screening rate of 99%, but only a timeliness rate of 61.9% among pregnant women [38].

Actually, the implementation effectiveness of a specific strategy depends on numerous factors, including local economic status and healthcare resources. Despite the predominantly rural population covered by this study, the implementation site is situated in one of China’s most developed areas. The high acceptance of HBV-DNA tests and antiviral prophylaxis among the intervention subjects may be attributed to their strong economic capacity. This could also be linked to the provision of free HBV-DNA tests during pregnancy and free PVST for infants. In China, although tenofovir covered by medical insurance and there has been a significant reduction in its price through centralized procurement [39], antiviral prophylaxis may still impose an increased financial burden on pregnant women due to varying policies regarding HBV-DNA tests and medication costs across different healthcare institutions.

While our study evaluated the implementation effect of this project in a large population and garnered several valuable lessons, it also has several limitations. First, only HBeAg-positive pregnant women were referred to the P clinic, taking into account the study’s budget and the P clinic’s healthcare ability. Although HBeAg can serve as an alternative indicator for determining eligibility for antiviral prophylaxis during pregnancy [40], there is a small proportion of HBeAg-negative pregnant women with a high viral load. Expanding the coverage of the P clinic to include all HBsAg-positive pregnant women could further reduce missed diagnoses. Second, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the study had a limited participants and a short implementation timeline. Furthermore, there was no comparison of the effect on preventing MTCT before and after the project’s implementation. However, compared with other implementation projects, the implementation model of the study combining decentralized services and centralized services can still provide a useful reference for other regions. Third, the implementation site is located in an economically developed region of China, characterized by a relatively well-established healthcare service system and a high level of health literacy among residents [41], which provides favorable conditions for the implementation of this project. We will explore the feasibility and practical effects of this model in other regions in the future.

Conclusions

In regions with a high HBV immunoprophylaxis rate, it is imperative to promptly implement measures to increase the antiviral prophylaxis rate among pregnant women at high-risk of HBV MTCT. Under the guidance of the government, a reasonable strategy to prevent MTCT tailored to the local medical capacity should be established, accompanied by necessary financial support. This approach not only aligns with the WHO standard of eliminating MTCT of HBV, but also facilitates the actual elimination of HBV MTCT.
