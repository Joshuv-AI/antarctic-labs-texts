StrabNet-CQ: an integrated deep learning framework for automated strabismus classification and quantification using ocular landmark detection
Authors: Shubh Garg, Ashish Sunkarapalli, Debabrata Ghosh, Savleen Kaur, Jaspreet Sukhija
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStrabismus is a common ocular misalignment that can impair binocular vision if untreated. Conventional diagnosis and treatment rely on clinical prism diopter (PD) readings, which quantify deviation along with base direction. However, these values are coarse, manual, and subject to inter-clinician variability.MethodsWe present the development and results of StrabNet-CQ (Strabismus Network for Classification and Quantification), a newly developed deep learning-based framework for automated strabismus classification and quantification. Six hundred eye images, with and without strabismus, were analyzed by the model. A YOLOv8 model performs classification into normal and abnormal as well as subsequent classification (normal, esotropia, exotropia, hypertropia, hypotropia), with refined classification via ResNet101 on segmented eye regions from the images. Ocular landmarks are detected using ResNet18, from which horizontal and vertical deviation indices and angular deviation are computed.ResultsThe system achieved 94% accuracy in strabismus detection and 90% in strabismus classification, with high sensitivity for normal (0.91–1.00), esotropia (0.89), and hypotropia (0.90). The derived parameters show a good correlation with manual PD values(r = 0.733) that can be utilized for quantification of strabismus.ConclusionStrabNet-CQ supports objective diagnosis and holds promise for deployment in clinical settings for strabismus detection as well as quantification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04442-9.Keywords: Strabismus, Ocular misalignment, Deep learning, Classification, Landmark detection, Ocular deviation, Artificial intelligence, Strabismus quantification

Introduction

Strabismus or squint [1] is a common visual disorder that impacts both children and adults. It may result in binocular vision disorders, deficiencies in depth perception, and, in extreme instances, amblyopia [2]. Early and precise diagnosis is vital for prompt intervention, as unaddressed strabismus can lead to prolonged visual difficulties and psychosocial issues. Despite its clinical importance, conventional strabismus diagnostic approaches still depend on manual and subjective evaluation methods. Traditional diagnostic methods like the Hirschberg test, cover tests, and prism evaluations [3], rely on clinical knowledge and physical observations, rendering them susceptible to inter-observer variability and mistakes. These methods lack automation and quantitative precision, limiting their scalability in resource-constrained settings. There is a growing need for scalable, impartial, and AI-driven automated strabismus detection systems that integrate both classification and measurement functionalities to deliver clinically significant insights.

With progress in artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning [4], diagnostic tools based on computer vision [5] have shown considerable promise in ophthalmology, which has enabled accurate and automated disease diagnosis. In ophthalmology, AI-driven models have shown remarkable proficiency in detecting diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, and age-related macular degeneration via fundus imaging and posterior segment photographs [6].

Inspired by the achievements of AI in identifying different irregularities in ophthalmology and driven by the necessity to overcome the shortcomings of conventional strabismus diagnosis, a new deep learning-based approach is being put forward for thorough diagnosis of strabismus. Initially, an object detection and classification model based on YOLOv8 [9] is employed to detect eye regions and classify strabismus. Additionally, a ResNet-101 model [10] is applied to segmented eye images for strabismus classification [7, 8]. Both models are trained using a combination of open-source and clinical datasets. Second, a landmark detection model [13, 14] using ResNet-18 [15, 16] for quantification of strabismus is designed [11, 12]. We aim to describe our initial experience in the development and utilization of this deep learning program. The first phase of our study is presented here, where we aim to detect and classify Strabismus.

Methodology

The first phase of the study involved designing a deep learning-based framework for quantitative strabismus diagnosis that integrates classification and measurement functionalities in a unified pipeline (Fig. 1). This was done on an open-source dataset with five-class straight gaze images (esotropia, exotropia, hypertropia, hypotropia, and normal) for model development. To enhance generalizability, a clinically acquired dataset with the same class distribution was collected and combined with the open-source dataset for training and evaluation.

Overview of the proposed deep learning-based framework for quantitative strabismus diagnosis. The pipeline integrates data preprocessing, augmentation, binary and multiclass classification, landmark detection, and computation of deviation metrics to assess strabismus severity

Participants

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institute Ethics Committee of Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh, India. For the clinical data set. patients aged four years and above, diagnosed with strabismus at the Pediatric Ophthalmology Clinic of the Advanced Eye Centre, PGIMER, were recruited after informed consent. Exclusion criteria included a history of strabismus surgery, craniofacial abnormalities, microcornea, corneal opacities, and buphthalmos. Age-matched department, consisting of individuals without strabismus who visited the hospital for unrelated conditions or attendants accompanying strabismus patients, provided they were not blood relatives.

Image acquisition

Straight-gaze images were captured using a 12-megapixel smartphone camera with flash at a fixed distance of 35–40 cm. Participants maintained a stable head position and fixation on a common target. Spectacles were removed, and the field of view was standardized using anatomical landmarks (brow, orbital rims). Each image had a minimum resolution of 1700 × 800 pixels. Data Handling and Annotation: All photographs were taken by a single investigator, who was trained for a few sessions to ensure standardization (AS) and systematically stored in labeled folders according to diagnosis. An independent reviewer assessed the images, excluding any with ambiguous diagnoses. All photographs were anonymized and included only the eye regions without any personally identifiable information.

Data preprocessing

Various preprocessing steps were applied to normalize the dataset, improve consistency, and enhance model performance. These included (1) Auto-orientation: Image alignment was standardized using an automated process to correct variations in subject posture and head position. This reduced spatial inconsistencies and ensured that the model learned features reliably, improving generalization. (2) Resizing: All images were resized to 640 × 640 pixels to meet the model’s input size requirements. Uniform dimensions enabled efficient processing while preserving essential features for accurate prediction. (3) Segmentation: The eye region was extracted using bounding boxes to eliminate background noise. This focused the model on relevant features, improving accuracy, computational efficiency, and consistency across samples. (4) Splitting: The preprocessed and augmented dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets in a 70:15:15 ratio. The training set was used to learn patterns in the data, the validation set for hyperparameter tuning and overfitting control, and the test set for unbiased performance evaluation. This split ensured a balanced and reliable assessment of the model. (5) Data Augmentation: To enhance generalization and reduce overfitting, the dataset was augmented using controlled transformations that introduced variability while preserving essential features. The applied augmentation strategies included: (A) flipping: Horizontal flipping simulated left and right gaze variations, increasing dataset diversity, and improving robustness to facial orientation changes. (B) Rotation: Images were rotated within a range of ± 5° to simulate slight head tilts, helping the model remain accurate despite minor angular deviations during inference. (C) Brightness Adjustment: Brightness levels varied between − 20% and + 20% to mimic different lighting conditions, ensuring the model’s consistent performance across varying illumination.

Binary classification

A YOLOv8-based object detection model was implemented to automate the identification and classification of strabismus. The eye area within an image was first identified, and the detected region was subsequently categorized into one of two groups: “Normal” or “Abnormal”. The five classes in the input dataset were initially consolidated into two categories by combining esotropia, exotropia, hypertropia, and hypotropia into the “Abnormal” group, while retaining “Normal” as a separate class. The binary classifier was trained for 50 epochs (learning paths) to ensure stable convergence and reliable performance.

Multiclass classification

Following the initial coarse classification, strabismus multiclass classification was performed using both YOLOv8 and ResNet-101. YOLOv8 was employed to detect and localize the eye regions, enabling automated classification of input images into five categories. The model was trained for 50 epochs to ensure stable learning and robust performance. While the YOLOv8-based model classified the original images directly, the ResNet-101-based model operated on eye-region segments extracted using YOLOv8-generated bounding boxes. ResNet-101, a deep convolutional neural network with 101 layers, was then fine-tuned for strabismus multiclass classification. The details of how we used the model to classify strabismus are provided in Additional File 1.

Quantification

To enable precise quantification of ocular misalignment, six key landmarks were annotated per image, three per eye: lateral canthus, pupil center, and medial canthus. These annotations served as ground truth (real values) for training the landmark detection model by another convoluted network, ResNet18. The network was trained for 200 epochs. The details of the quantification are detailed in additional file 2. To assess interocular disparity, Vertical Displacement Index (VDI), Horizontal Displacement Index (HDI), and angular deviation were computed as the quantification variables for Strabismus. A visual overview of the complete workflow, from facial input to quantification of strabismus severity, is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Statistical analysis

To assess the performance of the proposed strabismus detection framework, we evaluated both classification and regression components using standard metrics. Classification accuracy was validated using confusion matrix-derived indicators, while the reliability of predicted ocular deviation parameters was evaluated against ground truth values using regression metrics. Accuracy denotes the proportion of correctly classified samples, Precision quantifies the ratio of true positives to total predicted positives, and Recall (or sensitivity) measures the proportion of actual positives correctly identified. F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, useful for imbalanced datasets. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) was defined as the average magnitude of absolute errors between predictions and true values. To assess the agreement between image-derived deviation measurements and clinically recorded prism diopter (PD) values, a Pearson correlation analysis was conducted. Three feature pairs were analyzed: (i) Horizontal Displacement Index (HDI) vs. PD for horizontal deviations, (ii) Vertical Displacement Index (VDI) vs. PD for vertical deviations, and (iii) Dominant Deviation (mm) vs. PD in both. Dominant deviation was defined as the largest displacement observed for each patient, determined as the higher of the two eye deviations. RMSE (Root-Mean-Square Error) and MAE (Mean Absolute Error) for all displacement indices were calculated.

A progressive increase in angular deviation was recorded in different types of strabismus, with values advancing from normal alignment (class mean = 3.96°) to exotropia (5.98°), esotropia (10.02°), hypotropia (12.79°), and hypertropia (18.42°). A one-way ANOVA revealed a statistically significant difference in angular deviation across strabismus types (p < 0.001), with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests indicating significant differences between the normal class and all other classes. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used to quantify the linear relationship between image-based metrics and clinically recorded PD values, with corresponding p-values derived under the null hypothesis of no correlation (r = 0). A strong, statistically significant correlation was observed (r = 0.738, p = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵) between the HDI and PD values, a weak, non-significant correlation was observed (r = 0.411, p = 0.271) between the VDI and PD values and a strong, statistically significant correlation was also found (r = 0.733, p = 8.2 × 10⁻⁷), between the dominant deviation and PD values.

Discussion

Conventional strabismus diagnosis methods, such as the Hirschberg test, the cover test, and the prism measurement, depend on the knowledge and physical observation of healthcare professionals. These methods, although common in clinical practice, can require a significant amount of time and might not provide uniformity among various practitioners. To overcome the aforesaid drawbacks of the conventional methods, AI has been explored in strabismus screening and diagnosis in broadly two different ways: (a) AI to analyze eye movement videos (i.e., eye tracking) and (b) AI to analyze ocular gaze photos. While eye-tracking technology enables precise evaluation of eye movements for strabismus screening [1, 17–19], its reliance on specialized equipment and high computational demands makes it less accessible. On the other hand, AI-based ocular gaze photo analysis is a more accessible alternative requiring only a camera for near-instant diagnosis. Lately, deep learning models to analyze ocular gaze photos for strabismus diagnosis have become increasingly popular because of their superior performance and scalability. A word cloud generated from recent literature (Fig. 7) highlights key terms such as “strabismus”, “ocular deviation”, “landmarks”, “visual diagnosis”, and “deep learning”, reflecting the increasing convergence of computer vision and AI in automated strabismus detection and eye-tracking diagnostics.

Word cloud generated from recent literature

Our paper describes results with a deep learning model for strabismus detection and quantification. The high sensitivity denotes the model’s utility and robustness. The accuracy obtained for the detection and use of deviation indices, as well as angular deviation, reinforces the utility of these parameters as a quantitative indicator of misalignment severity.

There has been some literature on deep learning for the detection of strabismus. Jiewei Lu [20] proposed RF-CNN (random forest with convolutional neural network), an end-to-end eye region segmentation and classification model for telemedicine applications, demonstrating strong detection performance. Zheng et al. [21] created a deep learning model that uses the Inception-v3 architecture for the detection of horizontal strabismus, reaching an AUC of 0.99, surpassing resident ophthalmologists. Donghwan Kim and colleagues [22] presented a CNN-based model to classify strabismus into three distinct categories: esotropia, exotropia, and normal eye alignment, using a nine-photo front view. The model reached a conclusive test accuracy of 66.7%. Figueiredo et al. [23] developed an AI app using ResNet50 to objectively classify eye versions in the diagnosis of strabismus, enhancing diagnostic precision by analyzing images of the eye position in nine positions of gaze.

To further enhance classification accuracy, CNN architectures such as EfficientNet have been developed to target segmented eye images, reducing background noise. Arif et al. [24] showed that EfficientNet-B0 improves the accuracy of the classification of the ophthalmic image, especially for the detection of fundus-based eye diseases. Joo et al. [25] explored the use of StyleGAN2-ADA for data augmentation in strabismus classification with limited data, employing ResNet50 and ResNext101 classifiers. Yarkheir et al. [26] developed a ResNet101-based model to automatically detect and classify strabismus into Exotropia and Esotropia from facial images, achieving accuracy of 86.38% for binary classification and 92.7% for multiclass classification.

Notably, YOLO, a widely recognized object detection model, has not yet been explored for eye-region segmentation and subsequent strabismus classification. This gap has inspired our work, where we implement a binary classification using YOLOv8, followed by a multiclass classification to accurately distinguish between esotropia, exotropia, hypertropia, hypotropia, and normal eye alignment. The decision to adopt YOLOv8 for strabismus detection and classification was driven by its real-time detection capabilities, excellent detection accuracy, and robustness to variations in head orientation and lighting conditions. These attributes are crucial for ensuring consistent and reliable performance across diverse clinical environments. Moreover, YOLOv8’s compact architecture enables efficient processing without compromising detection accuracy, making it suitable for deployment in time-sensitive diagnostic workflows. Initially applied for binary classification to screen for strabismus presence, the model was subsequently repurposed for multiclass classification, streamlining the analysis pipeline through an automated, two-stage detection framework.

Beyond classification, quantifying the severity of strabismus is essential for clinical decision-making. Yehezkel et al. [27] introduced an automated system that uses eye tracking and dedicated occlusion glasses to swiftly and objectively measure strabismus types and deviations in children, showing quicker results compared to manual tests. Oltrup et al. [28] developed a digitized tangent screen test that integrates digital technology to assess ocular motility across all fields of vision, significantly streamlining the examination process. Both systems enhance diagnostic accuracy but still rely on external hardware and specific set-up conditions, which can limit their practicality in varied clinical environments. We have tried to incorporate ocular landmarks and utilize them for strabismus quantification.

Deep learning-based ocular landmark detection techniques have been increasingly explored to extract key eye features for various applications. For instance, Adnan et al. [29] presented a robust real-time framework for iris landmarks detection using a variety of convolutional neural networks, including MobileNetV2 and ResNet50, which demonstrated high accuracy and efficiency in localizing key iris landmarks. Similarly, Boonnithititikul et al. [30] introduced an innovative eye landmark detection method using the Realtime Identification Transformer (RT-DETR), which achieved an accuracy of 0.974 by precisely identifying multiple landmarks around the pupil and the eye’s reflective points. These advancements highlight the capabilities of deep learning in enhancing ocular landmark detection; however, using these techniques to directly quantify strabismus could offer a superior approach. By leveraging the precise localization capabilities of deep learning models to identify and measure ocular misalignment directly from eye-region images, our method offers clinicians more detailed and accurate assessments without the need for additional specialized equipment.

Additionally, drawing inspiration from literature, we investigate deep learning model based on ResNet101, for improving the accuracy of strabismus detection by effectively reducing extraneous facial variations. ResNet-101 was employed for classification tasks to leverage its deep residual learning architecture, which incorporates skip connections to mitigate vanishing gradient problems while retaining important spatial information. Its design supports high-level feature extraction, making the model particularly effective for detecting subtle eye misalignments, even in cases where deviations are minimal. Moreover, a balance between computational efficiency and inference speed was achieved, making it suitable for large-scale clinical deployment.

As advancements occur in AI-driven ophthalmic diagnostics, maintaining clinical reliability and generalizability continues to present a challenge [31]. The proposed framework addresses these gaps by integrating YOLOv8 for real-time classification, ResNet101 for enhanced image analysis, and ResNet18-based landmark detection for quantitative deviation assessment, thereby supporting early detection, personalized treatment planning, and providing an interpretable and scalable resource for ophthalmologists. This integration contributes to improved diagnostic precision and operational efficiency, particularly beneficial in resource-constrained or remote healthcare settings. The framework is well-positioned to support large-scale screening programs, tele-ophthalmology, and remote diagnostics, ultimately reducing clinician workload and improving early intervention rates. The model’s effectiveness in landmark detection is foundational to the subsequent task of quantifying ocular deviation.

Despite the promising results, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The dataset, though diverse, can be further expanded to include broader demographic variations, diverse gaze patterns, and additional clinical subtypes to improve model generalizability. We excluded patients with reoperations and children in whom errors might be high[32, 33]. The model shows a relatively reduced performance for exotropia and hypertropia, indicating room for refinement with more patient images. Future work will focus on augmenting the dataset and incorporating real-time capabilities into telemedicine platforms. Variation in the algorithm with different photographers and camera settings has to be taken into account before deployment. Additionally, forthcoming studies will emphasize clinical validation by comparing model-predicted deviation indices with gold-standard ophthalmic assessments, such as the prism cover test.

Conclusion

The current study showcases major progress in AI-powered strabismus diagnosis by launching an all-encompassing deep learning system that combines real-time classification with quantitative evaluation of strabismus. The ability to automate both classification and severity assessment ensures that the system serves as a robust, quantitative, and clinically relevant tool for ophthalmologists. This facilitates early identification of strabismus, enables the development of personalized treatment strategies, and ultimately contributes to improved patient outcomes.
