JAK2V617F+ single-stranded DNA modulates exosome complex activity and DNA-sensing pathways in myeloproliferative neoplasms
Authors: Esra Nur Demirtaş, Ipek Naz Kadioglu, Selçuk Sözer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhiladelphia chromosome-negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (Ph-MPNs) are characterized by excessive myeloid cell proliferation due to mutations in hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells, most notably JAK2V617F mutation. This mutation leads to persistent JAK2 activation, driving genomic instability and accumulation of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) fragments in the cytoplasm. The RNA-exosome complex, a critical ribonuclease complex, plays a key role in maintaining genomic stability by degrading damaged RNAs and processing ssDNA. In this study, we examined the effects of JAK2V617F+ ssDNA on the DNA-sensing pathways and exosome complex activity in healthy and Ph-MPN-derived cells. Using an in vitro model, we cultured cord blood mononuclear cells with ssDNA isolated from the Ph-MPN cell lines. Our findings revealed that Ph-MPN-derived cells release JAK2V617F+ ssDNA, which upregulates exosome complex components, including EXOSC1, and activates DNA-sensing pathways such as cGAS-STING and TLR9 in healthy cells without inducing apoptosis. Additionally, JAK2 wild-type ssDNA stimulates DIS3 expression, highlighting its regulatory role in inflammation and RNA metabolism. These results demonstrated that ssDNA derived from JAK2V617F+ cells can alter RNA-exosome complex gene expression and nucleic acid-sensing pathways, contributing to chronic inflammation in MPNs. This study provides novel insights into the interplay between ssDNA, exosome complex, and inflammatory signaling and offers potential therapeutic targets to modulate inflammation and genomic instability in MPNs.Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides: Therapies and Applications, exosome complex, single-stranded DNA, DNA-sensing pathways, JAK2V617F, myeloproliferative neoplasms, TLR9, cGAS-STING

Introduction

Philadelphia chromosome-negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (Ph-MPNs) are clonal hematologic malignancies that originate from mutations in the hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells. The primary subtypes, polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), and primary myelofibrosis (PMF), are driven by mutations, such as JAK2V617F, which are present in 95% of PV cases and 50% of ET and PMF cases.1 JAK2V617F drives constitutive activation of the JAK/STAT pathway, leading to enhanced myeloproliferation, cytokine production, and genomic instability.2,3 This instability is further exacerbated by replication fork stalling,4 leading to the accumulation of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) fragments in the cytoplasm.5,6 These ssDNA fragments can activate nucleic acid-sensing pathways, such as the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) and Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) pathways.7,8 The activation of these pathways can lead to inflammation and contribute to the pathogenesis of MPN.

High-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is a highly abundant protein that binds chromatin and, under various stress conditions, translocates to the cytoplasm or is released extracellularly.9 HMGB1 facilitates recognition of cytoplasmic DNA, including ssDNA10 and damaged DNA.11 In the presence of elevated extracellular ssDNA levels, HMGB1 forms a complex with ssDNA, presenting it to TLR9 and triggering an inflammatory response.12 Notably, TLR9 activation by ssDNA is amplified by HMGB1, which enhances the inflammatory cascade.13,14

The RNA-exosome complex (ExosC) is a ribonuclease complex essential for RNA processing, transcription stabilization, and genomic stability.15,16 ExosC also processes stable RNAs to balance transcription and maintain genome stability by removing unstable or improperly processed mRNAs.17,18 Unlike exosomes, which secrete extracellular vesicles and promote tumor progression via intercellular communication,19 ExosC is an intracellular machinery consisting of 11 conserved subunits. The complex includes a cap region (EXOSC1, EXOSC2, and EXOSC3) and a ring structure (EXOSC4–9) that channels RNA to the catalytic subunits, EXOSC10 and DIS3, which possess ribonuclease activity and function in both the cytoplasm and nucleus.20,21,22,23,24 Due to its broad RNA substrate range, ExosC is integral to numerous cellular processes, including translation, chromatin regulation, genome architecture, DNA damage response, telomere maintenance, cell viability, and differentiation.17,18,21 Deficiencies in ExosC have profound effects on these processes, highlighting their essential roles in cellular homeostasis.25 Notably, EXOSC1 has been implicated in DNA damage by cleaving ssDNA, which increases the sensitivity of human kidney renal clear cell carcinoma cells to DNA repair inhibitors.26 Furthermore, elevated levels of EXOSC1 have been associated with C>A transversions in coding sequences, suggesting its role as an endogenous mutagen.26 Additionally, downregulation of the EXOSC8 subunit has been shown to disrupt erythroid maturation in primary erythroid precursor cells.27 Both EXOSC10 and DIS3, the catalytic subunits of ExosC, exhibit nuclease activity, further underscoring their critical roles in maintaining genomic integrity and regulating RNA processing.22,23,24 However, the role of ExosC in Ph-MPN remains unclear.

This study investigated the effect of JAK2V617F+ ssDNA on ExosC activity and nucleic acid-sensing pathways in healthy and MPN-derived cells. We hypothesized that JAK2V617F+ ssDNA may influence ExosC gene expression and thereby contribute to MPN progression. By examining these interactions, our goal was to explore novel mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of MPN and highlight potential avenues for therapeutic intervention.

Results

The role of ssDNAs in Ph-MPN pathogenesis was investigated through the function of DNA-sensing pathways, ExosC, and inflammation-related genes. Since genomic materials released into the extracellular environment are inherently cell free (cf), we use the term cfDNA to describe the broader pool of extracellular DNA fragments and ssDNA to denote specifically the single-stranded fraction of this cfDNA analyzed in our study. We established an in vitro model using cord blood (CB) mononuclear cells (MNCs) and ssDNAs isolated from various sources, including cell culture supernatants of HEL, SET2, and K562 cell lines and CB plasma. Initially, MNCs were cultured for 48 h with ssDNA, and the results were evaluated by analyzing the changes in the expression of related genes. Additionally, variations in cellular function based on the presence or absence of either ssDNA carrying JAK2V617F (JAK2V617F+ ssDNA) or wild-type JAK2 ssDNAs (CB-ssDNA) were assessed using different approaches, as illustrated in Figure 1.

Experimental design

The cfDNA isolation was performed from HEL, SET2, and K562 cell lines culture supernatants, and cord blood was used as a healthy control. Then ssDNA was isolated. Allele-specific nested PCR, ELISA for DNA methylation, and fragment analysis were performed on the ssDNA. Allele-specific nested PCR, RT-qPCR, and apoptosis assays were performed on cord blood MNCs cultured for 48 h with HEL and SET2 ssDNA. The TLR9 inhibitor E6446 was applied to MNCs cultured with HEL, SET2, or CB-ssDNA. HEL and SET2 cells were incubated with ruxolitinib and CEP33779, whereas K562 cells were incubated with imatinib to observe the effects of tyrosine kinase inhibitors on the exosome complex. After the administration of tyrosine kinase inhibitors, intracytoplasmic staining was performed to detect EXOSC1 protein expression. In addition, the effect of the combination of a TLR9 inhibitor and ruxolitinib on the exosome complex in HEL and SET2 cells was evaluated.

MPN cells secrete hypomethylated JAK2V617F+ ssDNAs

The ssDNAs were isolated from several sources, including plasma of CB (CB-ssDNA); culture supernatant of SET2 (SET2-ssDNA), HEL (HEL-ssDNA), and K562 (K562-ssDNA) cell lines; culture supernatant of a breast cancer cell line (MCF7-ssDNA); and plasma of a systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE-ssDNA) case. Initially, each ssDNA was analyzed for its length by fragment analysis in order to estimate and compare the lengths of ssDNAs between MPN, solid cancer, autoinflammatory disease, and CB. The length of the ssDNA fragments varied across the samples, with those from the SET2 and K562 cell lines ranging between 25 and 50 bases. In contrast, ssDNA from HEL and SLE samples revealed no detectable peak between the ranges 25 and 65 bases. Meanwhile, ssDNA from CB exhibited slight peaks at 40 and 50 bases, and MCF7 cells exhibited peaks between 27 and 45 bases (Figure 2A). The variation in ssDNA fragment lengths among MPN samples may be attributed to the origins of the respective ssDNAs and states of cell differentiation, as well as disease context and inflammatory conditions. We also measured the concentration of ssDNA fragments in MPN cell lines. ssDNAs derived from SET2 cells (1,346 ± 62 ng/μL) were higher than those derived from HEL cells (204.7 ± 1.9 ng/μL).

The mutational status of ssDNAs was assessed using allele-specific nested PCR to investigate the presence of JAK2V617F in ssDNAs isolated from supernatants of HEL, SET2, and K562 cell lines. The results revealed that all ssDNAs collected from HEL culture supernatant contained JAK2V617F, whereas ssDNAs collected from SET2 culture supernatant contained both wild-type JAK2 and JAK2V617F. On the other hand, ssDNAs collected from K562 did not carry JAK2V617F, but only wild-type JAK2, as expected (Figure 2B). To understand the methylation status of the ssDNA fragments, the global methylation status of ssDNA was evaluated. We found a statistically significant difference in the global methylation of ssDNAs between CB, HEL, SET2, and K562. The global hypomethylated status of JAK2V617F+ ssDNAs for both HEL and SET2-ssDNAs was noticeable compared to that of CB-ssDNAs (p < 0.05). However, the global methylation of K562-ssDNAs was not significant compared to that of the ssDNAs of HEL, SET2, and CB (Figure 2C). In previous studies, the methylation status of CpG-rich ssDNAs was shown to have important roles in varying intracellular activations.28 These findings suggest that the mutation influences ssDNA characteristics and potentially contributes to inflammation.

To investigate the genomic effects of JAK2V617F+ ssDNAs on healthy cells, ssDNA derived from the cell culture supernatants of HEL and SET2 cells was cultured with MNCs for 48 h. Following culture, the MNCs were tested using allele-specific PCR to determine whether JAK2V617F+ ssDNAs became stable inside MNCs after their uptake. After 48 h, MNCs had only wild-type JAK2 and no detectable JAK2V617F (Figure 2D). The changes in JAK2 wild-type and JAK2V617F mRNA levels in cells cultured with ssDNAs were further evaluated by real-time quantitative RT-PCR. JAK2V617F+ ssDNAs had a dramatic impact on downregulating JAK2 wild-type transcripts of MNCs with no JAK2V617F expression (Figures 2E–2I). On the other hand, HEL and SET2 cell cultures with CB-ssDNAs upregulated both JAK2 wild-type and JAK2V617F mRNA levels in HEL and SET2 cells (Figure 2Eii and iii).

The ability of JAK2V617F+ ssDNA to downregulate wild-type JAK2 transcripts in healthy MNCs suggests that these ssDNAs may interfere with normal JAK2 signaling in healthy cells, potentially contributing to the altered hematopoietic functions in the tumor microenvironment. Conversely, the upregulation of both JAK2 wild-type and JAK2V617F mRNA levels in HEL and SET2 cells upon exposure to CB-ssDNA indicates that healthy ssDNA may enhance the transcriptional activity of JAK2, including the mutant allele.

JAK2V617F+ ssDNA induces exosome complex genes in healthy cells

ExosC genes are sensitive to external genomic stimuli, coordinate endo- and exonuclease activities, and capture nucleic acids in the cytosol. To analyze the initial expression status of ExosC genes, EXOSC1, EXOSC10, DIS3, and EXOSC8 in HEL, SET2, and CB-MNCs were evaluated by real-time quantitative RT-PCR. We observed significant expression of DIS3 and EXOSC8 in MNCs compared to that in HEL and SET2 cells. Although the difference in DIS3 was statistically significant in HEL and SET2 compared to CB-MNC (p < 0.0001), EXOSC1, EXOSC10, and EXOSC8 expressions were insignificant (Figure 3A). Given the previously identified role of DIS3 and EXOSC8 in erythroblast maturation, its involvement with CB-MNCs was not surprising; however, its expressional status in HEL cells was unexpected.

EXOSC1 plays a role in cleaving ssDNA. However, there is currently no evidence linking the drive of EXOSC1 to ssDNA mutations. To investigate whether EXOSC1 expression changes in response to ssDNA with different mutational statuses and whether JAK2V617F plays a role in this activity, further analysis was conducted by analyzing the changes in EXOSC1 expression using real-time quantitative RT-PCR. In MNCs transfected with 2 ng ssDNA, a significant increase in EXOSC1 expression was observed after 48 h of incubation with HEL-ssDNA. Notably, HEL-ssDNA, which contains JAK2V617F, induced higher EXOSC1 expression than SET-2 ssDNA, potentially due to the higher mutation burden. In contrast, SET-2, which carries a heterogeneous population of JAK2 wild-type and JAK2V617F+ ssDNAs, had a less pronounced effect on EXOSC1 expression. Surprisingly, CB-ssDNAs had the lowest effect on EXOSC1 expression compared to those of HEL and SET2 (Figures 3B–3I). We also examined the changes in other ExosC genes, including EXOSC10, DIS3, and EXOSC8, in MNCs cultured with ssDNA. Of all the ExosC genes analyzed, DIS3 expression was significantly upregulated with CB-ssDNA stimulation compared to that with HEL- and SET2-ssDNAs, whereas JAK2V617F+ ssDNAs failed to induce DIS3 expression in MNCs. In contrast, there was no significant difference in EXOSC10 and EXOSC8 expression with ssDNAs of CB, HEL, and SET2 cells (Figure 3Bii). We further applied the synthesized random ssDNA to MNCs and performed gene expression analyses of EXOSC1, EXOSC10, DIS3, and EXOSC8. No significant change was detected in EXOSC1, EXOSC10, and DIS3; slight downregulation of EXOSC8 expression was determined (Figure S1). These findings indicate that the immunomodulatory and ExosC-altering effects observed with MPN-derived ssDNAs are likely not a general feature of all ssDNAs but instead depend on specific sequences or structural properties.

Since ssDNA with JAK2 wild-type showed a dramatic increase in DIS3 expression, but not JAK2V617F+ ssDNA, the effect of ssDNA with JAK2 wild type was examined in HEL and SET-2 cells carrying the JAK2V617F mutation, and the changes in expression of EXOSC1, EXOSC10, DIS3, and EXOSC8 were evaluated. Only the expression of EXOSC8 was significantly reduced after culture with CB-ssDNA in HEL cells and slightly increased in SET2 cells (Figure 3Ci and ii).

JAK2V617F+ ssDNA induces DNA-sensing pathway genes in healthy cells

We further analyzed the expression of DNA-sensing pathway genes, including cGAS, STING, TLR9, NF-κB, IRF3, and IRF7, in healthy MNCs after culturing with ssDNAs of HEL, SET2, and CB. We also evaluated the expression of interferon gamma (IFNG), which is found at high levels in the serum of patients with PV and ET29,30 during ssDNA-mediated inflammation. To clearly understand intracytoplasmic ssDNA degradation, we evaluated the response of TREX1, an exonuclease that degrades ssDNAs31 and inhibits the activation of inflammatory pathways.32 As a result, cGAS, TLR9, NF-κB, and TREX1 fold changes were significantly increased in MNCs incubated with ssDNAs from HEL, SET2, and CB compared to untreated MNCs (Figure 4Ai, iii, iv, and viii). Interestingly, unlike MPN-ssDNAs, no increase in STING expression was observed with CB-ssDNA when cGAS was overexpressed (Figure 4Aii). MNCs treated with SET2-ssDNA showed the highest NF-κB fold change, whereas IRF3 expression was elevated with ssDNAs of HEL and CB (Figure 4Aiv and v). Surprisingly, IRF7 expression was significantly induced by HEL-ssDNA (p = 0.0005) and CB-ssDNA (p = 0.0441) (Figure 4Avi). Differences in IFNG expression were also observed after ssDNA stimulation (Figure 4Avii). The most striking result was observed with TREX1, which showed an approximately 1,500-fold overexpression in response to CB-ssDNA stimulation, whereas its expression was comparatively lower (∼500-fold) in MNCs stimulated with HEL and SET2-ssDNA. No significant difference was detected between TREX1 expression induced by HEL and SET2-ssDNA (Figure 4Aviii). We further investigated other factors that might enable ssDNA-related inflammation. HMGB1 is one target that might have a role in MPN-derived ssDNA stimulation of healthy cells. HMGB1 expression significantly increased (∼2,000-fold) in cells cultured with ssDNAs, both in JAK2V617F+ and JAK2 wild-type (Figure 4Aix).

We also analyzed whether ssDNA released from healthy cells with no mutations might have a role in inflammation in MPN cell lines. We transfected the CB-ssDNA into HEL and SET2 cells for 48 h and examined the changes in the expression of cGAS, STING, TLR9, and NF-κB. CB-ssDNA did not affect TLR9, STING, or NF-κB gene expression in HEL cells (Figure 4Bi) (p > 0.05), whereas only STING expression was slightly increased in SET2 cells (p = 0.0033) (Figure 4Bii).

ssDNA modulates ZFP36L1 expression in a context-dependent manner

In addition, ZFP36L1, a member of the zinc finger protein of the ZFP36/TTP family33 and an RNA-binding protein34 destabilizes pro-inflammatory mRNA transcripts by targeting AU-rich elements in their 3′ UTR,35 thereby reducing cytokine expression.36 As a tumor suppressor, ZFP36L1 is frequently downregulated in myeloid malignancies owing to hypermethylation of its enhancer region, which underscores its potential contribution to MPN pathogenesis.37 Therefore, we assessed ZFP36L1 expression in these settings. We found that ZFP36L1 expression was significantly lower in MPN cell lines than in MNCs (p < 0.001) (Figure 4Ci). This suggests that its suppression is associated with MPN pathology. Interestingly, culturing MPN-derived ssDNAs with MNCs resulted in a significant increase in ZFP36L1 fold change in MNCs (Figure 4Cii), whereas co-culturing MPN cell lines with CB-ssDNAs caused a decrease in ZFP36L1 fold change (p = 0.0150 and p = 0.0045, respectively) (Figure 4Ciii). These results suggest that ssDNA plays a dual role in modulating ZFP36L1 expression, depending on the cellular context. In MNCs, ssDNA-induced upregulation of ZFP36L1 may serve as a protective mechanism to counteract inflammation. Conversely, in MPN cell lines, downregulation of ZFP36L1 in response to ssDNA indicates a potential mechanism through which MPN cells evade inflammatory regulation and promote tumor progression. This underscores the importance of assessing ZFP36L1 expression in MPNs to delineate its contribution to disease pathogenesis and to assess its potential as a therapeutic target.

Impact of TLR9 inhibition on inflammatory pathways

As ssDNA can trigger inflammatory responses, one potential strategy to mitigate this effect is through the inhibition of TLR9. Therefore, we assessed the impact of TLR9 inhibition in this context. In all conditions of the MNCs transfected with ssDNAs from HEL, SET2, and CB in which that TLR9 inhibitor treatment was applied, significantly decreased TLR9 expression was revealed, which might indicate the relevance of the application (Figures 5A–5C). The effects of a TLR9 inhibitor on the expression of ExosC genes were revealed only for DIS3 expression. Following treatment of MNCs with MPN-derived ssDNA, DIS3 increased (Figures 5Aii and Bii). In contrast, DIS3 overexpression in MNCs incubated with CB-ssDNA was decreased after inhibitor treatment. However, there was a significant increase compared with untreated MNCs (Figure 5Cii). The expression levels of EXOSC1, EXOSC10, and EXOSC8 remained unchanged following treatment in MNCs stimulated with ssDNAs from HEL, SET2, and CB (Figures 5Ai and ii, 5Bi and ii, and 5Ci and ii). In contrast, TLR9 inhibition caused a decrease in cGAS, STING, NF-κB, IRF3, IRF7, and HMGB1 expression in MNCs with HEL-ssDNA, while the changes in IFNG and TREX1 expression were not statistically significant (Figure 5Aiii, iv, and v). The TLR9 inhibitor applied to SET2-ssDNA stimulation reduced the expression of cGAS, STING, NF-κB, TLR9, and IRF3 in MNCs (Figure 5Biii and iv). In addition, treatment caused TREX1 upregulation but did not cause changes in the expression of IFNG, IRF7, and HMGB1. Furthermore, the effect of CB-ssDNA with a TLR9 inhibitor was also evaluated in this study. TLR9 inhibition suppressed the expression of cGAS, TLR9, NF-κB, IRF3, and HMGB1 (Figure 5Ciii, iv, and v).

Modulation of JAK2 kinase inhibitors on ExosC genes

To understand the functional role of ExosC in Ph-MPN cells, HEL and SET2 cells were treated with the JAK2 kinase inhibitors ruxolitinib and CEP33779. The expression levels of EXOSC1 (i), EXOSC10 (ii), DIS3 (iii), and EXOSC8 (iv) were evaluated both with (Figure 6) and without CB-ssDNA (Figure S2) uptake. Significantly upregulated fold changes of EXOSC1 and EXOSC10 in ruxolitinib-treated HEL cells compared to untreated controls were detected (p= 0.0033 and 0.0065, respectively) (Figures 6AI and S1Ai). However, in SET2 cells, only DIS3 showed a decreased fold-change after ruxolitinib treatment (p = 0.0067) (Figures 6Bi and S1Bi). In contrast, CEP33779 had no statistically significant effect on the expression of ExosC genes in HEL and SET2 cells (p > 0.05) (Figures S1A and S1Bii). In HEL cells, ruxolitinib treatment led to a significant increase in EXOSC1 expression. However, EXOSC1 expression remained unchanged when the cells were stimulated with CB-ssDNA alone or treated solely with a TLR9 inhibitor. Interestingly, EXOSC1 expression was decreased in HEL cells co-treated with both the TLR9 inhibitor and ruxolitinib, in contrast to the observed increase in cells treated with CB-ssDNA (Figure 6Ai). In HEL cells, the expression of EXOSC10, an exoribonuclease, increased when the cells were co-treated with the TLR9 inhibitor and ruxolitinib (Figure 6Aii). By contrast, DIS3 expression remained unaffected under the same treatment conditions (Figure 6Aiii). In addition, the expression of EXOSC8, which plays a role in erythroid cell maturation, decreased after treatment with ruxolitinib and TLR9 inhibitor (Figure 6Aiv). In SET2 cells, treatment with a TLR9 inhibitor resulted in decreased expression of EXOSC1 and EXOSC8. Additionally, EXOSC1 expression was lower in cells treated with both CB-ssDNA and ruxolitinib than in those treated with ruxolitinib alone (Figure 6Bi). The expression levels of EXOSC10 and DIS3 were not affected by the combined application of the TLR9 inhibitor and ruxolitinib in SET2 cells (Figure 6Bii and iii).

The detection of changes in intracytoplasmic EXOSC1 protein expression by flow cytometry analysis after treatment did not reveal any significant effect after ruxolitinib application on EXOSC1 in HEL and SET2 cells. CEP33779 treatment also showed no change in EXOSC1 expression in HEL and SET2 cells (Figures S3A and S3B). However, analysis of the effects of imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, on the K562 cell line showed a significant decrease in EXOSC1 (p = 0.0410) and EXOSC8 (p = 0.0105) expression. Conversely, DIS3 expression was not affected by the treatment (Figure S2C). However, imatinib significantly increased the expression of EXOSC10 (p = 0.0007), which degrades RNA via its catalytic activity. Furthermore, intracytoplasmic EXOSC1 protein levels decreased by 3.40% (p < 0.001) after imatinib treatment in K562 cells (Figure S3C).

ssDNAs’ effect on cytokine release

Cytokine release was assessed after culturing ssDNAs with MNCs and was further evaluated in HEL, SET-2, and K562 cell lines. A heatmap of cytokine release is shown in Figure S4. Only MCP-1 and interleukin (IL)-8 levels were significantly increased in MNCs transfected with CB-ssDNA for 48 h, whereas no significant changes were observed in MNCs incubated with MPN-derived ssDNA. Further analysis was performed using K562-ssDNA, which also revealed no cytokine release when cultured with MNCs. In addition, IL-8 release decreased only when healthy ssDNA was incubated with SET2 cells for 48 h. Considering the previously detected increased expression levels of cGAS, TLR9, NF-κB, IRF3, TLR9, and HMGB1, such findings were surprising. The culture concentration of 2 ng of ssDNA may have remained low for the induction of cytokine release.

Impact of ssDNA on apoptosis

To determine whether circulating cf ssDNA induces apoptosis in MPN cells, annexin V and 7-AAD labeling was performed following 48 h incubation of HEL, SET2, and K562 cells with 2 ng of CB-ssDNA. In HEL cells, ssDNA had no effect on early or late apoptosis (Figure 6C). In SET2 cells, ssDNA uptake modestly induced early apoptosis but did not affect late apoptosis. Interestingly, co-culturing with ssDNA markedly reduced early apoptosis in K562 cells and suppressed late apoptosis (Figure 6C). These findings suggest that the apoptotic response to ssDNA may vary according to cancer type and may, in some cases, promote cancer cell survival. Recent studies have suggested that the cGAS-STING pathway activates apoptosis through endoplasmic reticulum stress and NLRP3, NF-κB, IRF3, and IFN signaling, in addition to causing type I IFN release.38 Thus, apoptosis can limit inflammation and tumor growth. Therefore, we investigated whether apoptosis is induced by increased cGAS expression in healthy MNCs cultured with JAK2V617F+ ssDNA. Neither the MPN-derived JAK2V617F+ ssDNA nor the mutation-free CB-ssDNA induced apoptosis in healthy MNC, and no apoptotic response was detected in MNC cultured with ssDNAs in the presence of the TLR9 inhibitor (Figure 6D).

Impact of intact and degraded ssDNA on ExosC gene expression and substrate processing

To assess whether degraded ssDNAs influenced ExosC gene expression and function, MNCs were cultured with DNaseI-digested HEL-, SET2-, and CB-derived ssDNAs, and the expression of EXOSC1 and DIS3 was analyzed (Figure 7A). All degraded ssDNA sources led to an upregulation of EXOSC1 (Figure 7Ai), whereas degraded HEL- and SET2-derived ssDNA significantly increased DIS3 expression (Figure 7Aii), suggesting a possible compensatory transcriptional response to maintain RNA surveillance.

To evaluate the functional activity of ExosC, we examined the expression of two canonical ExosC substrates, SNORD44 (a C/D box small nucleolar RNA [snoRNA]) and 18S rRNA, in MNCs treated with intact or degraded ssDNA. Intact HEL- and SET2-derived ssDNAs upregulated SNORD44 expression, which was reversed by co-treatment with TLR9 inhibitor. CB-derived ssDNA did not significantly alter the SNORD44 levels (Figure 7Bi). Similarly, intact HEL-, SET2-, and CB-ssDNA induced marked accumulation of 18S rRNA, indicative of impaired ExosC function. TLR9 inhibition partially restored 18S rRNA degradation, with the most pronounced effect observed in MNCs exposed to CB-ssDNA (Figure 7Bii). Strikingly, DNase I-degraded ssDNAs from all three sources caused a substantial reduction in SNORD44 and 18S rRNA levels compared with non-degraded controls, suggesting that fragmented ssDNA failed to disrupt ExosC function (Figure 7Bi and ii). TLR9 inhibition did not further reduce this degradation-induced suppression, except in the CB-ssDNA group, where SNORD44 expression increased upon TLR9 blockade, whereas 18S rRNA levels remained unchanged.

Interestingly, although MNCs treated with cfDNA derived from HEL, SET2, or CB cells showed no statistically significant changes in SNORD44 expression, they exhibited a marked reduction in 18S rRNA levels. Unexpectedly, cfDNA treatment also led to a significant upregulation of EXOSC1 and DIS3 expression (Figure 7C). These findings suggest that cfDNA, comprising both double-stranded DNA and ssDNA fragments, can transcriptionally influence RNA surveillance machinery through mechanisms distinct from pure ssDNA. Collectively, these findings suggest that intact cancer-derived ssDNA impairs ExosC function in a TLR9-dependent manner, whereas highly fragmented ssDNA loses this capacity, possibly due to reduced immunostimulatory potential or diminished cellular uptake. These data underscore the importance of nucleic acid integrity in shaping host RNA surveillance responses.

Discussion

In this study, we designed a platform where the impact of ssDNA with JAK2V617F on healthy MNCs was tested by their ability to induce ExosC and nucleic acid-sensing pathways and aimed to explore the role of ssDNA in MPN pathogenesis. We used the HEL and SET2 cell lines carrying the JAK2V617F mutation. Healthy CB-MNCs and ssDNA served as controls, and the Ph-positive MPN cell line, K562, was included. We showed that cf ssDNA fragments derived from the supernatant of HEL and SET2 cells also carried JAK2V617F, as in their origin of genomic DNA. ssDNA from JAK2V617F+ cells was shorter and hypomethylated compared to JAK2 wild-type ssDNA. Hypomethylation may increase the immunogenicity of ssDNA, enhancing its ability to activate nucleic acid-sensing pathways. This aligns with previous studies, suggesting that hypomethylated CpG regions are potent activators of TLR9-mediated inflammation. Furthermore, the ability of JAK2V617F+ ssDNA to downregulate wild-type JAK2 transcripts in healthy MNCs, and conversely, the upregulation of both JAK2 wild-type and JAK2V617F mRNA levels in HEL and SET2 cells upon exposure to CB-ssDNA indicated that healthy ssDNA may enhance the transcriptional activity of JAK2, including the mutant allele. These findings suggest a dual role for ssDNA in MPN pathogenesis: promoting transcriptional dysregulation in healthy cells and exacerbating mutant JAK2V617F expression in MPN cells could exacerbate disease progression, genomic instability, and clonal dominance.

EXOSC1 expression was significantly increased in MNCs treated with JAK2V617F+ ssDNA, highlighting its potential role in ssDNA cleavage and regulation of inflammation. However, DIS3, a catalytic subunit of ExosC, was selectively upregulated by wild-type ssDNA, but not by JAK2V617F+ ssDNA. It has been shown that in hematological cancers, DIS3 silencing causes reduced homologous recombination repair, increased mutational burden, and overactivation of inflammatory interferon responses.39 This differential response suggests that wild-type ssDNA may reinforce homeostatic regulatory circuits, whereas JAK2V617F+ ssDNA may undermine these mechanisms, thereby contributing to ExosC dysfunction and inflammation. Therefore, it might be important to understand how JAK2V617F+ ssDNA suppresses DIS3 activity in future studies. Mehta et al. demonstrated that depleting DIS3 or EXOSC10 in hematopoietic progenitor cells led to a reduction in erythroid progenitors.40 Consequently, we showed that DIS3 was overexpressed in MNCs cultured with CB-ssDNA compared to ssDNAs of HEL and SET2. The suppression of ZFP36L1 in MPN cell lines, coupled with its upregulation in healthy MNCs exposed to JAK2V617F+ ssDNA, underscores its dual role in inflammation and tumor suppression. The differential response to healthy versus mutant ssDNA highlights the complex interplay between inflammatory regulation and tumor progression in MPNs. JAK2 inhibition may support the restoration of RNA homeostasis in MPN pathogenesis by downregulating the expression of EXOSC8, which in turn impairs erythroid maturation and upregulates EXOSC10, which encodes the catalytic endoribonuclease subunit of ExosC.

JAK2V617F+ ssDNA upregulated the cGAS-STING and TLR9 pathways in MNCs without inducing apoptosis. These pathways are critical for innate immune responses and have been implicated in chronic inflammation in MPNs. The length of DNA is critical for cGAS binding to nucleic acid sequences, as DNA molecules ≥36 bp enhance the binding affinity and subsequent activation of the cGAS-STING pathway,41 which is 15 times lower for cytoplasmic ssDNA than its affinity for dsDNA.42 In contrast, TLR9 activation can be enhanced by ssDNA containing CpGs as short as 2 nucleotides long.43

Interestingly, the robust increase in cGAS and TLR9 expression suggests a potential synergy in ssDNA-mediated inflammation, which is consistent with reports of cGAS-STING and TLR9 interplay in inflammatory conditions. SNORD44 and 18S rRNA serve as functional readouts for the ExosC activity. SNORD44, a C/D box snoRNA involved in rRNA methylation, is a well-characterized exosomal substrate, with increased levels reflecting impaired RNA surveillance. Similarly, 18S rRNA, a component of the ribosome, accumulates as misprocessed or excess transcripts when RNA degradation is compromised. In our model, intact ssDNA induced the expression of both SNORD44 and 18S rRNA, supporting ExosC dysfunction, whereas degraded ssDNA and cfDNA failed to do so, indicating preserved or enhanced RNA turnover and further highlighting the structural and functional specificity of ssDNA-induced exosomal impairment. Conversely, the robust induction of these DNA-sensing pathways by intact ssDNA without triggering apoptosis further supports the hypothesis that ssDNA acts as a chronic, non-lethal trigger of inflammation in MPNs, thereby maintaining the inflammatory milieu without inducing cell death. Interestingly, the strongest response to intact ssDNA was observed in healthy MNCs, suggesting that untransformed cells may be more sensitive to extracellular nucleic acids, perhaps because of an intact nucleic acid-sensing machinery or an unaltered redox/RNA homeostasis baseline.

The significant upregulation of IRF7 by HEL-ssDNA is particularly intriguing because IRF7 is a master regulator of type I interferon responses. Additionally, our recent findings highlighted the involvement of IRF7 in both HEL and SET-2 cells.44 This suggests that ssDNA derived from HEL cells may uniquely enhance interferon signaling, contributing to a feedforward loop of inflammation and immune activation. These findings collectively suggest that ssDNA-mediated activation of DNA-sensing pathways, particularly the NF-κB and IRF family members, plays a critical role in MPN pathogenesis. Considering that ssDNAs are extracellular genomic materials capable of inducing inflammation, cytokine production, and immune dysregulation,45,46 they may contribute to the pro-inflammatory microenvironment characteristic of MPNs and promote the clonal dominance of mutant hematopoietic cells.

TLR9 inhibition effectively reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory genes, including TLR9, cGAS, NF-κB, and HMGB1 in MNCs treated with JAK2V617F+ ssDNA. This suggests that TLR9 inhibitors can mitigate inflammation in MPNs, particularly when combined with JAK2 kinase inhibitors.

The observed increase in cGAS expression without a corresponding upregulation of STING suggests that STING activation may require higher intracellular concentrations or longer fragments of ssDNA for effective signaling. This aligns with prior evidence indicating that cGAS can be transcriptionally induced even in the absence of full pathway activation. In contrast, the robust responsiveness of the TLR9-NF-κB axis to ssDNA implies that this pathway is more sensitive to shorter or lower concentrations of nucleic acid stimuli. These findings support a model in which TLR9 acts as an early detector of fragmented or low-abundance ssDNA, while cGAS-STING signaling may require a more substantial nucleic acid burden to become fully engaged. Furthermore, these findings might provide insights into several potential therapeutic strategies, including suppression of TLR9-mediated pathways, which may reduce chronic inflammation in MPNs. MPNs exhibit significant genomic instability and are often driven by the JAK2V617F. This study explored how ssDNA derived from JAK2V617F+ cells affects nucleic acid-sensing pathways and ExosC in healthy and Ph-MPN cells. Taken together, our results suggest that extracellular JAK2V617F+ ssDNA can interfere with RNA surveillance and nucleic acid-sensing pathways, highlighting ExosC components and TLR9 as potential modulators of disease processes in MPNs. While further mechanistic and translational studies are needed—including sequencing of ssDNA species and exclusion of contributions from other circulating nucleic acids—our findings identify extracellular ssDNA as a previously underrecognized factor with potential impact on RNA metabolism and innate immune signaling in MPN pathogenesis.

Further studies are required to clarify the role of the ExosC in RNA metabolism and DNA repair. In particular, evaluating the effects at the protein level and in downstream signaling assays will add important functional depth. Moreover, combining TLR9 inhibitors with JAK2 kinase inhibitors (e.g., ruxolitinib) may provide synergistic benefits by targeting both inflammation and aberrant signaling in MPNs. Finally, investigating how additional MPN-associated mutations such as CALR and MPL influence ExosC activity could uncover novel therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Samples and cell lines

CB samples (n = 10) were collected from healthy donors at the Istanbul University Medical Faculty, with informed consent approved by the Ethical Review Board (approval number: 12.01.2022–684469). CB MNCs were isolated using lymphocyte separation medium (density 1.077 g/mL; Capricorn Scientific, Germany), resuspended in PBS, and used immediately or stored for future experiments.

Three cell lines were used: the erythroleukemia cell line HEL (biallelic JAK2V617F), megakaryoblastic cell line SET-2 (monoallelic JAK2V617F), and chronic myelogenous leukemia cell line K562 (wild-type JAK2). HEL and SET-2 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium, and K562 cells were cultured in IMDM medium, supplemented with 10%–20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C with 5% CO2 and ≥95% humidity.

DNA isolation, fragment analysis, global methylation analysis, and DNaseI-mediated ssDNA degradation

cf ssDNA was isolated from the culture supernatants (5 mL) of HEL, SET-2, and K562 cells using the QIAseq cfDNA Extraction Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). ssDNA was extracted from cfDNA using the ssDNA/RNA Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research, USA) and measured using NanoDrop purity ratios (260/280 > 1.8). Fragment sizes were analyzed using the RNA 6000 Nano Kit and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

A 5-mC DNA ELISA kit (Zymo Research, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 100 ng of ssDNA was added to the PCR tubes, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., Vermont, USA).

Isolated ssDNA (2 ng/μL) was incubated with 0.5 μL of 5 U/μL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) for 10 min at 37°C followed by heat inactivation using DNase I for 10 min at 75°C prior to MNC incubation and co-cultured for 48 h.

As a synthetic control, a 35-nt ssDNA oligonucleotide with random sequence (no CpG motifs) was synthesized (Sentebiolab Biotechnology, Ankara, Türkiye) and used at a concentration of 2 ng/mL.

Allele-specific nested PCR

The JAK2V617F mutation was detected using nested allele-specific PCR as described previously.47 Primers were purchased from Sentebiolab Biotechnology (Ankara, Türkiye). Agarose gel electrophoresis (2%) separated the amplification product and demonstrated a band for a mutant allele of JAK2V617F of 279 bp and a wild-type allele of JAK2 of 229 bp with a 50-bp ladder (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Treatment of cells

The cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 50,000 cells/well for MNCs and at 2 × 105 cells/well for cell lines for 24 h. MNCs were cultured with ssDNAs (2 ng) of HEL, SET2, K562, and CB at 37°C for 48 h under 5% CO2 and less than 95% humidity. TLR9 inhibitor, E6446 dihydrochloride (Selleckchem, Germany) (300 nM), was applied 30 min before ssDNA application to MNC culture and incubated for 48 h. Ruxolitinib (300 nM) was applied during the last 4 h, and CEP33779 (300 nM) (Selleck Biotechnology GmbH, Germany) was applied at the last hour of culture. K562 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well in 2 mL serum-free medium. After incubation for 24 h, the cells were treated with imatinib (kindly provided by Dr. Yücel Erbilgin, Istanbul University, Istanbul, Turkiye) (1 mM) and cultured for 24 h.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted using a Total RNA Purification Kit (Jena Bioscience, Germany). The purity and concentration of the RNA extracts were measured using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 100 ng of total RNA using the SCRIPT cDNA Synthesis Kit (Jena Bioscience, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Differences in each gene expression were detected by 2X Magic SYBR Mix (Procomcure Biotech GmbH, Austria). Expression analysis was performed using the primers listed in Table S1 on a LightCycler II 480 (Roche, Switzerland). Real-time detection of JAK2V617F and JAK2 wild-type transcript levels was performed using hydrolysis probes, as previously described.48 Primer and probe sequences are listed in Table S2.

Apoptosis analysis

The apoptotic activity of cells was evaluated by labeling with Annexin V-PE (BD Biosciences, USA) and 7-AAD (BD Biosciences, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 15 min of incubation, the cells were immediately analyzed using flow cytometry (FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA).

Cytokine release

For cytokine release analysis, the LEGENDplex Human Inflammation Panel 1 (13-plex) with a V-bottom Plate (BioLegend, San Diego, United States) was used. The cytokine panel contained IL-1β, IFN-α2, IFN-γ, TNF-α, MCP-1 (CCL2), IL-6, IL-8 (CXCL8), IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17A, IL-18, IL-23, and IL-33. To measure cell culture supernatants, standards were prepared and added to the wells of a 96-well plate, as recommended by the manufacturer. The samples were analyzed using flow cytometry (FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA).

Intracytoplasmic staining

IntraPrep Permeabilization Reagent (Beckman Coulter, Inc., USA) was used to detect cytoplasmic antigens. Anti-CSL4 (ab250393, Abcam, United Kingdom) was applied as the primary antibody, followed by the secondary goat anti-rabbit IgG (FITC) (ab6717, Abcam, United Kingdom), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The samples were analyzed by flow cytometry (FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA).

Statistical analysis

The real-time qPCR results were calculated using the cycle of threshold (CT) value with the formula 2ˆ(−ΔCT)×100. All results were analyzed using GraphPad Prism v.8 ANOVA (GraphPad Prism Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The significance of p value is shown in the legend of each figure (∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).
