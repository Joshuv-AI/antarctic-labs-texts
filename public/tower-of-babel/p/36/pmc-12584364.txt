Development of a unified deep learning approach integrating CNN-based local and ViT-based global feature extraction for enhanced cotton disease and pest classification
Authors: L. K. Dhruw, V. K. Tewari, Peeyush Soni, Arjun Chouriya, Prakhar Patidar, Naseeb Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cotton diseases and pests pose significant threats to cotton production, necessitating accurate and efficient classification methods. Despite existing advanced methods, there is a research gap in utilizing both local feature extraction and global context capture for enhanced classification accuracy. Hence, this study developed and evaluated three advanced models for cotton disease and pest classification: a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based model, a Vision Transformer (ViT)-based model, and a hybrid CNN-ViT model. These models were trained on a dataset comprising eight classes of cotton diseases and pests, namely aphids, armyworm, bacterial blight, cotton boll rot, green cotton boll, healthy, powdery mildew, and target spot. The results demonstrated that the hybrid CNN-ViT model achieved the highest overall performance with an average test accuracy of 98.5%. The CNN model showed strong performance with an average accuracy of 97.9%. The ViT models, while having self-attention mechanisms to capture context and dependencies, exhibited improved performance with increased depth. The ViT model having four transformer layers outperformed the two-layer variant, achieving an average accuracy of 97.2% compared to 96.3%. The hybrid model effectively combined the strengths of CNN's local feature extraction and ViT's global feature capture, resulting in superior classification accuracy across most classes. Future research should focus on expanding the dataset to include more diverse diseases and pests and integrating the models with autonomous platforms for spraying the chemicals, thus facilitating real-world adoption and application in agricultural settings.

Introduction

Cotton, one of the most significant cash crops globally [1], is highly susceptible to various diseases and pests, which can impact its yield and quality [2]. Diseases and pests in cotton crops such as aphids, armyworms, bacterial blight, cotton boll rot, green cotton boll, powdery mildew, and target spot can cause substantial economic losses to the farmers [3]. Accurate and timely classification of these diseases is important for effective disease management and mitigation strategies [4]. Early detection of pests and diseases allows for timely intervention [5], which can prevent the spread of diseases and minimize crop damage, ensuring better yield and quality of cotton production.

Despite advancements in agricultural technology, the classification of cotton diseases remains a challenging task [6]. Traditional methods often rely on manual inspection and expert knowledge, which can be time-consuming, subjective, and prone to human error. Image processing-based techniques, while automating some aspects of disease detection, typically involve handcrafted features that may not capture the complex patterns and variations inherent in diseased plants [7, 8]. Therefore, there is a pressing need to develop more advanced and reliable classification models that can enhance the performance of existing methods and provide more consistent results. Deep learning-based techniques provide significant advantages over traditional and image-processing-based methods in the classification of diseases. Recent studies have highlighted the growing role of deep learning and machine vision in agriculture, demonstrating their potential for tasks such as nondestructive crop monitoring and rapid classification of agricultural products using convolutional neural networks [9, 10]. The deep learning-based models, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [11] and Vision Transformers (ViTs) [12], can automatically learn and extract hierarchical features from raw image data. This leads to superior performance in identifying subtle differences between disease symptoms [13–15]. Moreover, deep learning models can generalize better to new and unseen data [16], making them more robust in real-world applications. These advantages make deep learning-based techniques a powerful tool in enhancing the accuracy, efficiency, and scalability of cotton disease classification. However, most existing deep learning models for plant disease classification focus either on local feature extraction using CNNs or on capturing global context using transformer-based architectures. These approaches, when used in isolation, often fail to fully exploit the complementary strengths of both feature types. Specifically, CNNs may struggle with capturing long-range dependencies and context, while ViTs might overlook fine-grained local patterns critical for accurate classification. This limitation restricts the robustness and generalizability of disease detection models under complex field conditions. Therefore, in the present study, hybrid deep learning-based models were utilized to classify the cotton diseases. The primary motivation of this study is to address the limitations of existing deep learning models that rely solely on either local or global feature extraction for plant disease classification. The focus is on developing a unified hybrid model that combines CNN-based local feature learning with ViT-based global context modeling.

Vision Transformers (ViTs) have shown significant potential in various image classification tasks [17]. The models excel at capturing long-range dependencies due to their self-attention mechanism, but they lack the innate spatial inductive biases of CNNs [18], which can lead to inefficiencies in learning local patterns. On the other hand, CNNs are inherently good at capturing local spatial features and possess built-in translational invariance [19], making them effective for tasks where local patterns are important. Thus, the primary aim of this study is to improve the performance of ViTs in the classification of cotton diseases by utilizing CNN-based feature extraction. Specifically, this study seeks to develop a hybrid model that integrates CNNs with Vision Transformers, optimizing the classification accuracy and robustness across the eight considered classes: aphids, armyworms, bacterial blight, cotton boll rot, green cotton boll, healthy, powdery mildew, and target spot. By doing so, we aim to address the current challenges in cotton disease classification and provide a more effective tool for the agricultural community. These models can be deployed to edge devices to apply precise chemicals on targeted portion for monitoring the diseases and pests.

Review of literature

In the past, various image processing-based methods were developed by researchers to classify the leaf diseases for various plants. For cotton plants, Ref. [20] developed a method to quantify lesion severity in cotton plants using histogram-based thresholding. They captured images of cotton leaves, converted them to RGB and HSV color spaces, and applied thresholding to segment healthy and diseased regions. Using a Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier [21], they achieved 90% accuracy in classifying infected areas. Reference [22] focused on detecting bacterial blight, Myrothecium, and Alternaria in cotton plants. They preprocessed leaf images, extracted features, and used SVM classifiers and achieved 85.0% accuracy in classifying these diseases.

The disease recognition rates of image processing-based approaches depend on lesion segmentation and hand-designed features, which require expert knowledge and are subjective. Determining optimal and robust features is challenging among many extracted ones. Additionally, most methods struggle to segment leaves and lesions from complex backgrounds, leading to unreliable results. Therefore, automatically recognizing plant disease from images with complex backgrounds was improved using CNNs by researchers in recent times. This technique automatically extracts features related to the region of interest (ROI) using filters and convolution operations [23, 24]. Similar deep learning-based disease detection frameworks using data augmentation techniques have shown success in other crops, such as potato leaf disease detection [25], supporting the robustness of CNNs in diverse crop applications.

Reference [26] developed a deep learning model for cotton leaf disease detection using fine-tuned transfer learning algorithms. They experimented with VGG [27], Inception [28], and Xception [29] models, with Xception achieving the highest accuracy of 98.70%. This model was integrated into a web application for real-time disease detection. Reference [30] conducted a comparative analysis of ResNet50, ResNet152v2 [31], and Inception V3 models for detecting cotton diseases, with ResNet152v2 achieving the highest accuracy of 88.36%. [32] used the CNNs model to classify bacterial blight, leaf curl, powdery mildew, and healthy leaves in cotton, achieving an accuracy of 97%. [3] employed a 16-layer CNN to classify diseases in cotton plants, achieving a validation accuracy of 96.15%. Reference [33] developed a CNN model for detecting cotton leaf diseases and pests, achieving an accuracy of 96.4%. Reference [34] proposed an attention-embedded MobileNet-V2 model for crop disease identification, achieving 99.71% accuracy on the PlantVillage dataset and 99.13% under cluttered conditions. Similarly, fungal disease detection across multiple crops using modified ResNeXt architecture was demonstrated by [35, 36], addressing classification challenges in heterogeneous regional datasets.

CNNs have been effective in image classification [37]. However, to enhance classification performance, it is crucial to capture long-range dependencies in images, an area where CNNs lag. Therefore, in recent times, researchers employed ViTs for cotton disease detection. This technique can effectively capture global context and long-range dependencies using self-attention mechanisms thus making them a promising alternative to CNNs for improving accuracy and robustness in complex agricultural scenarios. Reference [38] proposed a smartphone-based solution using a ViT model for identifying healthy and diseased tomato plants and achieved a testing accuracy of 90.99%, outperforming a CNN-based model. Reference [39] developed a lightweight vision transformer network for plant disease identification, replacing the multilayer perceptron (MLP) block with a custom inception block and adding skip connections to improve robustness. Evaluated on the PlantVillage and Maize disease datasets, the developed ViTs outperformed state-of-the-art CNNs, achieving 5.38% higher testing accuracy.

To further enhance accuracy in classifying cotton diseases, researchers developed a hybrid model that combines the local feature extraction abilities of CNNs with the global context and long-range dependency-capturing capabilities of ViTs. By fusing CNNs and ViTs techniques into a unified model, complex local and global features can be extracted from the image thus leading to superior prediction performance by such a model. For instance, Ref. [40] developed a hybrid CNN-ViT model for plant disease detection, achieving 98.61% and 87.87% accuracy on PlantVillage and Embrapa datasets, respectively. Extending this research, Ref. [41] added an inception block, improving accuracy to 98.86% and 89.24% for PlantVillage and Embrapa datasets, respectively. Reference [42] compared CNNs, pure ViTs, and hybrid models for plant disease classification. They found that the hybrid model provided an optimal balance of accuracy and prediction speed, achieving 96.2% accuracy. This improvement in accuracy highlights the potential of hybrid models in disease classification.

Therefore, in the present study, a CNN-ViTs hybrid model was developed and compared with standalone CNN and ViT models for cotton disease detection. This comparison highlights the strengths and weaknesses of each approach, providing a comprehensive analysis and valuable insights for improving disease detection methodologies.

Materials and methods

Dataset and its pre-processing

The dataset for this study was downloaded from Kaggle (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/saeedazfar/customized-cotton-disease-dataset) and comprises images of various cotton diseases and pests. The classes included in the dataset are aphids (Aphis gossypii), armyworm (Spodoptera litura), bacterial blight (Xanthomonas citri subsp. malvacearum), cotton boll rot (Diplodia gossypina), green cotton boll (immature cotton bolls), healthy (healthy plants without pests or diseases), powdery mildew (Leveillula taurica), and target spot (Corynespora cassiicola). Sample images of each cotton disease and pest class, including the healthy class, considered in the present study: a aphids, b armyworm, c bacterial blight, d cotton boll rot, e green cotton boll, f healthy, g powdery mildew, h target spot

Aphids are small sap-sucking insects that can cause significant damage to cotton plants by weakening them and transmitting various plant diseases. These pests can stunt the growth of the plants and lead to lower yields [43]. Armyworms are larvae that feed voraciously on cotton leaves, causing defoliation and potentially leading to severe yield reductions if not controlled in time. Bacterial blight [44, 45] is a bacterial disease that manifests as dark, water-soaked spots on the leaves, stems, and bolls of cotton plants. This disease can lead to significant defoliation and boll rot, impacting the overall health and productivity of the cotton plants. Cotton boll rot refers to a condition where fungal or bacterial infections cause the cotton bolls to rot, affecting both the quality and quantity of the cotton produced. This condition can be particularly detrimental during the harvest season. Green cotton boll is a condition where the cotton bolls remain green and do not open properly, often resulting from environmental stress or pest damage. This condition can lead to significant losses in cotton yield and quality. Healthy cotton plant images are those that do not show any signs of disease or pest infestation, serving as the control group in the dataset. Powdery mildew is a fungal disease characterized by white, powdery spots on the leaves and stems of cotton plants. This disease can reduce photosynthesis, leading to stunted growth and lower yields. Target spot [46] is another fungal disease that causes circular, target-like spots on the leaves, leading to defoliation and potentially severe reductions in cotton yield if not managed properly [47–52].

In the present study, to enhance the diversity of the dataset and improve the robustness of the model, image augmentation techniques were employed. These techniques included random rotations and horizontal and vertical shifts, which helped simulate various real-world conditions and variations that the model might encounter. Each image in the dataset was cropped from the center to a uniform size of 512 × 512 pixels to ensure consistency and facilitate processing by the ViTs-based model.

For each class, 800 images were considered, resulting in a balanced dataset with a total of 6,400 images. This balanced approach ensures that the model has sufficient data to learn and generalize from each class effectively. Additionally, each image was normalized by dividing the pixel values by 255.0, converting them into a range between 0 and 1. The image normalization ensures consistent pixel value scales, speeds up training convergence, and improves model stability by preventing vanishing or exploding gradients.

CNN-based feature extraction

The CNN architecture (as shown in Fig. 2) designed in this study for the classification of cotton diseases and pests is composed of six convolutional blocks. These blocks used convolutional layers to detect patterns, batch normalization to stabilize and accelerate training, and pooling layers to reduce spatial dimensions.Fig. 2Architecture of CNN model developed to classify images of cotton diseases and pests into eight distinct classes

Architecture of CNN model developed to classify images of cotton diseases and pests into eight distinct classes

In the first convolutional block, a convolutional layer with 16 filters, each of size 3 × 3, was applied. The activation function used was ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) [53], and padding was used to preserve the spatial dimensions of the input. This layer helped in detecting low-level features such as edges and textures. Following the convolutional layer, a BatchNormalization layer [54] was included to normalize the activations, which helped in stabilizing and accelerating the training process. A MaxPooling layer with a pool size of 2 × 2 was then applied to reduce the spatial dimensions by a factor of two, which helped in reducing the computational load and focusing on the most salient features.

The subsequent four convolutional blocks continued this pattern, increasing the number of filters to 32, 64, 128, and 256 respectively, each time followed by batch normalization and pooling layers to deepen the network and extract more complex features. However, the final block used 512 filters with a batchnormalization layer but did not include a pooling layer, maintaining the spatial dimensions at 16 × 16 pixels to ensure comprehensive feature extraction.

After the convolutional blocks, the network included a global pooling layer and fully connected layers to perform classification. A GlobalAveragePooling layer was used to reduce each feature map to a single value, effectively summarizing the feature maps into a vector. This was followed by a Dense layer with 512 units and ReLU activation, then a Dropout layer with a dropout rate of 0.5 to prevent overfitting [55]. The final Dense layer consisted of 8 nodes (one for each class) with a softmax activation function (Eq. (1)), which outputted a probability distribution over the classes. Vision transformer architecture

In this study, a Vision Transformer (ViT) model (as shown in Fig. 3) was developed to classify images of cotton diseases and pests into eight distinct classes. This model utilized the self-attention mechanism from Transformer models, originally designed for natural language processing (NLP) tasks [56], and adapted for image classification [12].Fig. 3Architecture of Vision Transformer-based model developed to classify images of cotton diseases and pests into eight distinct classes

Architecture of Vision Transformer-based model developed to classify images of cotton diseases and pests into eight distinct classes

The architecture of ViTs in the present study was configured with two Transformer layers, a hidden dimension of 384, an MLP dimension of 512, four attention heads, and a dropout rate of 0.1 to prevent overfitting. Each image (512 × 512 pixels) was divided into non-overlapping patches of size 32 × 32 pixels, resulting in a total of 256 patches per image. These patches were then flattened into vectors of size 3072 and fed into the model. This approach allowed the ViTs to handle the images as a sequence of patches, similar to how it processed tokens in a sentence for NLP tasks.

During the working of ViTs, the input patches were embedded into a higher-dimensional space using a dense layer with a hidden dimension of 384. Position embeddings were added to these patches to retain positional information, which was crucial for the model to understand the spatial relationships within the image. A class token was appended to the sequence of embedded patches, serving as a representative of the entire image during the classification process. This class token was learnable and was concatenated with the embedded patches before being fed into the Transformer encoder layers.

Each Transformer encoder layer in the ViTs consisted of several components. Layer normalization was applied first to stabilize and accelerate the training process. This was followed by multi-head attention with four heads, allowing the model to focus on different parts of the image simultaneously and capture complex relationships and dependencies between patches. A skip connection was added, followed by another layer of normalization to maintain the integrity of the learned features. The output then passed through a feed-forward neural network, consisting of a two-layer MLP with a GELU activation function and a hidden dimension of 512, followed by a dropout layer to prevent overfitting.

The output of the Transformer layers was processed through a final normalization layer. The class token, which had now aggregated information from all the patches, was extracted and passed through a dense layer with a softmax activation function (Eq. 1) to produce the final classification probabilities for the eight classes. This approach ensured that the model could effectively differentiate between the various cotton diseases and pests.

In addition to using two transformer layers in ViTs, in the present study, a ViTs model with four transformer layers was also developed to classify images of cotton diseases and pests thus allowing for a performance comparison between the two configurations. The two-layer ViT served as a baseline, while the four-layer ViT was intended to explore whether additional layers could significantly enhance the ability of the ViT-based model to capture complex features and improve classification accuracy. Architecture of hybrid model

In this study, a hybrid model integrating CNNs with ViTs (as shown in Fig. 4) was developed to classify images of cotton diseases and pests into eight distinct classes. The hybrid approach aimed to combine the feature extraction capabilities of CNNs with the self-attention mechanisms of ViTs to enhance classification performance.Fig. 4Architecture of hybrid model (CNN + ViTs) developed to classify images of cotton diseases and pests into eight distinct classes

Architecture of hybrid model (CNN + ViTs) developed to classify images of cotton diseases and pests into eight distinct classes

The CNN model consisted of six convolutional blocks, each increasing in filter size from 16 to 512, with ReLU activation, batch normalization, and pooling layers. The output of the CNN was a feature map of size 16 × 16 × 512. This feature map was then passed through a convolutional layer to match the hidden dimension required by the ViTs (configured to 384) and reshaped into 256 patches of size 32 × 32 pixels. More specifically, the CNN output feature map of size 16 × 16 × 512 is first projected using a 1 × 1 convolution layer (or dense layer) to reduce the channel dimension to 384, aligning it with the ViT hidden size. This results in a 16 × 16 × 384 tensor, which is then reshaped into 256 sequential patches (tokens) of size 384 each. These tokens are treated as input embeddings to the Vision Transformer.

In the ViT component, the reshaped patches were embedded into a higher-dimensional space using a dense layer, and position embeddings were added to retain spatial information. A class token was appended to the sequence of embedded patches, serving as a representative of the entire image during the classification process. This sequence was then fed into two Transformer layers, each consisting of layer normalization, multi-head attention, and a feed-forward neural network with GELU activation and dropout layers to prevent overfitting.

The final output from the Transformer layers was processed through a normalization layer. The class token, which aggregated information from all patches, was extracted and passed through a dense layer with a softmax activation function (Eq. 1) to produce the final classification probabilities for the eight classes. Figure 4 illustrates the hybrid model combining Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) to classify images of cotton diseases and pests into eight distinct classes. This hybrid architecture effectively leverages CNN’s strength in extracting localized patterns (such as edges, lesions, and color textures) and ViT’s capability in modeling spatial relationships and global context across the entire leaf surface. The CNN-extracted feature map of shape 16 × 16 × 512 is projected to match the ViT’s hidden dimension (384), ensuring compatibility for transformer input. These feature patches retain both spatial continuity and local semantic richness. The addition of a learnable class token and positional embeddings enables the ViT to treat the CNN-provided patches as sequential tokens, allowing attention-based modeling of their inter-dependencies. This dual-stream design not only enhances detection of visually similar symptoms (e.g., target spot vs. bacterial blight) but also improves robustness in complex backgrounds, making it particularly suitable for field-level implementation.

Training of models

For the training of the developed models (CNN-based, ViT-based, and hybrid models), the dataset was divided into training, validation, and testing sets in a ratio of 70:20:10, respectively. These models were constructed and trained for 25 epochs with a batch size of 32 images. The Adam optimizer [57] with a learning rate of 0.001 without decay scheduling was used to update the learnable weights during the training process for all models. In this study, dropout was fixed at a rate of 0.5 based on common best practices to prevent overfitting, without additional tuning.

We trained and evaluated the models on a computer having an Intel Core i7 processor, 32 GB of RAM, and a Windows 10 operating system. Additionally, it was equipped with an Nvidia GeForce RTX 2060 GPU with 6 GB of memory. The training of all models was carried out using the Keras library [58] with TensorFlow [59] as the backend, implemented in the Python programming language. The categorical cross-entropy loss function (Eq. 2) was used as the loss function for all models. Results

The performance of the developed models, CNN-based, Vision Transformer (ViT)-based, and the hybrid CNN-ViT model was evaluated using a test dataset. Key metrics such as normalized confusion matrix, precision, recall, and F1-score values were employed to assess the efficacy of each model. To facilitate a comprehensive comparison, the overall accuracy of each model was also calculated. These evaluations provided a clear understanding of each model's capability in accurately classifying the various cotton diseases and pests.

Overall, the CNN model exhibited robust performance in classifying most cotton diseases, but the visual similarity between certain disease symptoms, such as bacterial blight and target spot, underscores the need for further refinement. Enhancing the feature extraction process or incorporating additional data may improve the model's generalization and reduce misclassifications.

Discussion

Interpretation of results

The performance of four models, CNN-based, Vision Transformers (ViT) with 2 layers (ViT2), ViT with 4 layers (ViT4), and the hybrid CNN-ViT model, was evaluated for classifying various cotton diseases and pests. The results, depicted in Fig. 17, show the accuracy of each model across eight classes.Fig. 17Accuracy comparison among developed models

Accuracy comparison among developed models

The hybrid model consistently outperformed the other models, achieving the highest average accuracy of 0.985. This superior performance can be attributed to its ability to combine the strengths of both CNNs and ViTs. The CNN component captures local spatial features effectively, while the ViT component excels at capturing global context and long-range dependencies. This synergy enhances the model's ability to accurately classify diseases, especially those with subtle or complex patterns.

The CNN model also performed well, with an average accuracy of 0.979. It showed exceptional accuracy for Cotton boll rot, Green cotton boll, and powdery mildew, all achieving 0.991. The high performance of the CNN model can be explained by its proficiency in recognizing local spatial patterns and textures, which are critical for identifying specific disease symptoms.

The ViT2 model had an average accuracy of 0.963, performing best for Cotton boll rot (0.988) but struggling with target spot (0.918). The lower performance of ViT2 compared to the hybrid model and CNN can be attributed to its limited ability to capture local spatial features, which are crucial for distinguishing diseases with similar visual characteristics.

The ViT4 model showed improved performance over ViT2, with an average accuracy of 0.972. The improved performance of the 4-layer ViT model over its 2-layer counterpart can be attributed to its enhanced ability to capture complex and long-range dependencies in the image data. As the number of transformer layers increases, the model can progressively learn deeper semantic representations and more abstract relationships between different regions of the image. This is particularly beneficial for distinguishing subtle or overlapping disease symptoms that may not be easily separable in earlier layers. In the hybrid model, the inclusion of transformer layers with greater depth enhances the integration of global contextual information derived from CNN-extracted local features, thereby contributing to improved classification accuracy and robustness. The increased depth of ViT4 resulting in better performance across most classes compared to ViT2. However, it still falls short of the hybrid model, indicating that while increasing the number of transformer layers enhances performance, it does not completely compensate for the lack of strong local feature extraction that CNNs provide.

A notable observation is the performance of the target spot class. Both ViT2 and ViT4 models underperformed in comparison to the CNN and hybrid models. The target spot class involves symptoms that are visually similar to other diseases, which likely caused the transformers to misclassify instances due to their emphasis on global context over local details. The CNN and hybrid models, which can better capture these local nuances, showed higher accuracy for this class.

An in-depth analysis of individual classes reveals specific strengths and weaknesses of each model. For aphids, the hybrid model achieved the highest accuracy (0.991), closely followed by the CNN model (0.982), ViT4 (0.965), and ViT2 (0.972). The hybrid model's superior performance can be attributed to its ability to combine CNN's local feature extraction with ViT's global context understanding, allowing for better differentiation of aphids' specific visual patterns. For armyworm, the hybrid model (0.988) and CNN model (0.982) outperformed both ViT models, as armyworms have distinct local features that CNNs can capture effectively. The hybrid model's enhancement with ViT layers further improves the recognition of armyworms by adding global context to local features.

For bacterial blight, the hybrid model showed the highest accuracy (0.979), followed by the CNN model (0.967), ViT4 (0.962), and ViT2 (0.956). bacterial blight can exhibit subtle variations in symptoms, making it challenging for models that do not excel at both local and global feature extraction. The hybrid model's combined approach helps mitigate this challenge. Cotton boll rot was well classified by all models, with the hybrid model achieving the highest accuracy (0.994). The visual distinctiveness of Cotton boll rot likely contributed to the high accuracy across all models, with the hybrid model's slight edge indicating its robustness in identifying even clear-cut classes.

Green cotton boll was also accurately classified by the hybrid model (0.988) and CNN model (0.991), demonstrating nearly perfect accuracy. The simplicity and distinctiveness of the Green cotton boll class allowed the CNN model to perform exceptionally well, with the hybrid model's additional ViT layers providing slight improvement. For the healthy class, the hybrid model (0.985) outperformed the other models, indicating its robustness in distinguishing healthy plants from diseased ones. The addition of ViT layers in the hybrid model helps in maintaining high accuracy by effectively differentiating healthy features from subtle disease symptoms.

Powdery mildew was another class where the hybrid model (0.994) and CNN model (0.991) showed the highest accuracy. powdery mildew has distinct textural patterns that CNNs can capture effectively. The hybrid model's incorporation of ViT layers further enhances its performance by adding context to these local features.

The performance for the target spot class was notably challenging, with the hybrid model (0.956) showing improved performance compared to the ViT models but slightly lower than CNN (0.947). target spot involves symptoms similar to other diseases, causing higher misclassification rates. The hybrid model's combined approach reduces this issue, though the similarity in visual symptoms still poses a challenge.

In conclusion, the hybrid model consistently outperformed the other models, achieving the highest average accuracy. This can be attributed to its ability to integrate the strengths of both CNNs and ViTs. CNNs excel at capturing local spatial features, such as edges, textures, and shapes, which are crucial for identifying specific disease symptoms. On the other hand, ViTs are adept at capturing global context and long-range dependencies through self-attention mechanisms, which help in understanding the overall structure and distribution of symptoms. The comparison of model performance underscores the importance of integrating multiple types of feature extraction to achieve the best classification accuracy. The hybrid model's ability to combine local and global feature extraction results in the highest overall accuracy, making it the most effective model for cotton disease and pest classification. This study demonstrates that while individual models like CNNs and ViTs have their strengths, a hybrid approach leverages the best of both worlds, leading to superior performance in complex agricultural tasks. Further refinements, such as incorporating additional data and advanced augmentation strategies, could enhance the model's generalization and reduce misclassifications even further.

Compared to earlier CNN-only or ViT-only approaches, our proposed hybrid model effectively overcomes the individual limitations of both architectures. CNNs are highly efficient at learning local spatial features such as edges, textures, and patterns, but struggle to capture long-range dependencies and contextual cues. By combining CNN and ViT components, our hybrid model leverages the strengths of both—extracting rich local features through CNN layers and enhancing contextual understanding via transformer layers. This integration leads to improved accuracy, robustness, and generalization across visually similar or complex disease classes, as demonstrated by the superior performance of our model over standalone CNN or ViT models.

Implications for cotton diseases classification

The advancements in cotton disease and pest classification using the developed CNN, ViT, and hybrid CNN-ViT models have significant implications for real-world applications. These models, particularly the hybrid CNN-ViT, can be integrated into autonomous platforms and IoT-based systems to provide real-time monitoring and early detection of cotton diseases and pests in the field. This capability allows farmers to take prompt and precise actions, reducing the spread of diseases and minimizing crop losses. The high accuracy of the hybrid model ensures reliable identification of various diseases and pests, facilitating targeted interventions such as the appropriate use of pesticides and other management strategies. Overall, the integration of these AI-driven models into agricultural practices holds the potential to transform cotton farming, offering substantial benefits to farmers through improved disease management, cost savings, and sustainable farming practices.

The high classification accuracy achieved by the proposed hybrid CNN-ViT model makes it well-suited for deployment on edge devices integrated with field-based autonomous systems such as drones or ground robots. These platforms can utilize the model for real-time image analysis and precise disease localization. Once a disease or pest is identified, the system can trigger localized spraying mechanisms to apply agrochemicals only on affected areas, significantly reducing chemical usage, minimizing environmental impact, and lowering input costs for farmers. Such integration of AI with precision agriculture not only enhances decision-making efficiency but also supports sustainable pest and disease management practices in cotton cultivation.

Limitations and future work

Despite the promising results, the current study has several limitations that need to be addressed in future research. One notable limitation is the restriction to only eight classes of diseases and pests. To enhance the robustness and practical applicability of the models, future studies should include a broader range of diseases and pests. Additionally, the image dataset used in this study primarily contains images of the infected areas, often with minimal background complexity. For the models to generalize effectively and perform well under real field conditions, it is crucial to train them on datasets that include more complex backgrounds and varied environmental conditions. Although the proposed model is designed for efficient deployment, its hybrid architecture involves transformer blocks that may increase training time and require moderate computational resources during inference. Additionally, robustness under adverse field conditions—such as poor lighting, occlusion, or background noise, needs to be further validated using more diverse real-field imagery.

Future research should focus on expanding the dataset to encompass more diverse disease and pest classes, as well as images captured in real-world field conditions with complex backgrounds. Future work should also focus on testing the model across different geographies, seasons, and cotton varieties, as disease symptoms may vary due to agro-climatic and genetic factors. Including such diversity will improve model robustness and real-world applicability. Additionally, while the proposed hybrid model shows high accuracy, its effectiveness may depend on careful tuning and validation across diverse conditions. Thus, future work should focus on ensuring robustness under varied environments and optimizing the model for real-time field deployment. Integrating these advanced models with user-friendly mobile applications and IoT devices will facilitate their widespread adoption and practical implementation in agricultural settings. This integration will ensure that the benefits of AI-driven cotton disease classification are accessible to all stakeholders in the agricultural sector, ultimately leading to improved disease management, increased productivity, and sustainable farming practices.

Conclusions

In the present study, we developed and evaluated three advanced models—CNN-based, Vision Transformer (ViT)-based with two and four transformer layers, and a hybrid CNN-ViT model, for the classification of cotton diseases and pests. Our primary aim was to enhance the accuracy and robustness of disease and pest identification using deep learning techniques.

Our results demonstrated that the hybrid CNN-ViT model achieved the highest overall performance with an average test accuracy of 98.5%. This model effectively combined the strengths of CNN's local feature extraction and ViT's global feature capture, leading to superior classification accuracy across most classes. The CNN model also showed strong performance with an average accuracy of 97.9%, particularly excelling in classes with distinct local features such as cotton boll rot and green cotton boll. The ViT models, leveraging self-attention mechanisms, provided improved context and long-range dependency capture. The four-layer ViT model outperformed the two-layer variant, achieving an average accuracy of 97.2% compared to 96.3%.

A key finding of our study is the superior performance of the hybrid CNN-ViT model, which underscores the benefits of integrating multiple feature extraction techniques. This model not only achieved the highest accuracies for several classes but also demonstrated robustness across diverse disease and pest categories. However, the study also revealed that certain classes, such as Target Spot, presented challenges across all models, indicating the need for further refinement. In conclusion, our study successfully developed and validated advanced deep-learning models for cotton disease and pest classification, achieving high accuracy and robustness. The hybrid CNN-ViT model, in particular, showed exceptional performance, highlighting the potential of combining local and global feature extraction techniques. Our future work will focus on expanding the dataset to include more diseases and pests and integrating these models with mobile applications for real-world use.
