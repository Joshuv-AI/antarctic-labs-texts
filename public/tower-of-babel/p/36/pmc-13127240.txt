Indirect Effect of Maltreatment on Child Cooperation and Exploration Through Maternal Sensitive Guidance
Authors: Jennie M Boulus, Katherine Edler, Kristin Valentino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChild maltreatment is a pathogenic relational experience that has deleterious effects on child functioning. Mothers who have engaged in child maltreatment have difficulties with emotion socialization (i.e., maternal sensitive guidance) while reminiscing with their children about past emotional experiences. Difficulties in maternal sensitive guidance are associated with child emotional maladjustment and decreased emotion regulation; however, the quality of children's contributions to reminiscing (i.e., child cooperation and exploration) has not been examined in the context of maltreatment. The present study aimed to assess the indirect effect of maltreatment on child cooperation and exploration during reminiscing via maternal sensitive guidance during reminiscing. We also evaluated child sex as a moderator. Participants were 159 maltreating and 81 nonmaltreating mother–child dyads with 3‐ to 6‐year‐old children. Mother–child dyads reminisced about four past, everyday times that the child felt different emotions. Maltreatment was negatively associated with maternal sensitive guidance (b = −0.26, p < 0.05), and maternal sensitive guidance was positively associated with child cooperation and exploration (b = 0.81, p < 0.01). The indirect effect was significant. Multigroup analyses indicated that this indirect effect did not differ as a function of child sex. Findings demonstrate how lower levels of maternal sensitive guidance may be a process through which maltreatment negatively impacts children's cooperative and exploration while reminiscing about their past, everyday emotional experiences.Keywords: child cooperation and exploration, child maltreatment, maternal sensitive guidance, reminiscing

Introduction

Child maltreatment is a pathogenic relational experience, often characterized by a breakdown in the quality of the parent–child relationship, that has deleterious effects on child functioning (e.g., Cicchetti and Valentino 2006). Maltreatment includes caregiving behaviors that fail to meet the child's physical and emotional needs (i.e., neglect) as well as physical, sexual, and emotional abuse (Cicchetti 2016). In the United States, approximately 600,000 children experience maltreatment each year (United States Department of Health and Human Services 2023). Moreover, 89% of victims are maltreated by one or both parents; among these, the largest categories of victims were maltreated by a mother acting alone (37.4%), by a father acting alone (24.5%), or by both parents (19.2%; USDHHS 2023).

Maltreating parents tend to have difficulties with multiple forms of parenting behavior (McLaughlin and Lambert 2017). One relational dynamic that is negatively associated with maltreatment is mother–child reminiscing about children's past emotional experiences (Valentino et al. 2015). Among mothers who have engaged in maltreatment, decreased maternal warmth, support, and sensitivity during reminiscing relate to their children's socioemotional development (Valentino et al. 2019). Yet the literature largely focuses on maternal contributions to reminiscing without examining how children's contributions to reminiscing are impacted by maltreatment. Therefore, we examined how maltreatment was associated with children's contributions during reminiscing and evaluated the indirect effect of maltreatment on children's reminiscing contributions through maternal reminiscing contributions. Further, we investigated whether child sex moderated this indirect effect.

Mother–child reminiscing is a dyadic interactional exchange that supports children's processing of their past emotional experiences (Salmon and Reese 2016, for review). Elaboration and sensitive guidance are two distinct dimensions of maternal reminiscing style that may support children's propensity to engage in the co‐construction of the dialogue (Cleveland and Morris 2014; Wu et al. 2020). Maternal elaborative reminiscing is marked by the provision of rich details, open‐ended questions, and affirmations of their child's responses (Fivush et al. 2006). Further, maternal sensitive guidance reflects mothers' supportive, responsive behaviors to their children during the memory narrative (Hsiao et al. 2015; Koren‐Karie et al. 2003). The degree to which the dyad is able to collaboratively construct a coherent narrative appears to have implications for children's developing sense of self, emotion knowledge, and self‐regulation (Hsiao et al. 2015; Overbeek et al. 2022). In the present study, we focus on how maternal sensitive guidance may be particularly important for children's reminiscing contributions during early childhood, an important goal because research suggests that children's reminiscing contributions are related to their socioemotional development (Leyva et al. 2020).

As reminiscing is a dyadic process, there are two dimensions of children's reminiscing that are parallel with maternal elaboration and sensitive guidance. Similar to maternal elaboration, many studies have assessed the quantity of children's unique memory contributions, which is operationalized as each new detail that the child provides (Fivush et al. 2006). Additionally, similar to maternal sensitive guidance, the affective quality of children's contributions to reminiscing, termed cooperation and exploration, is another distinct dimension of child reminiscing characterized by children's active engagement, attentiveness, and interest (Koren‐Karie et al. 2008). Children develop a coherent representation of their past through participating in reminiscing with their mothers, which fosters their ability to form personal narratives (e.g., Bird and Reese 2006). Thus, participating in reminiscing is a critical task through which children learn to interpret their own past emotional experiences, ultimately shaping their self‐concept and coherent autobiography.

During early childhood, parents lead and provide scaffolding during reminiscing because children's skills in language and attentional focusing are developing (Salmon and Reese 2016). However, young children are still able to play an active role in the reminiscing conversations by describing more details about their feelings and memories of the past events; mothers can take their child's contributions and help integrate them into a coherent narrative (Fivush and Salmon 2023). Broadly, parental guidance has been identified as a driving force behind children's emotional development (Katz et al. 2012). Mothers' emotional support specifically relates to children's language development; when mothers solicit their children's engagement, children are given the unique opportunity to articulate their own perspectives and express their feelings (Reese 2018). Reminiscing discussions guided by mothers high in sensitive guidance may generate expressive replies from children, which then in turn may lead to improvements in children's language skills (McDonnell et al. 2022) and autobiographical memory specificity (Valentino et al. 2014). Therefore, in early childhood, mothers ideally organize the conversation for their children to be able to engage and contribute, which in turn has positive consequences for other aspects of child development.

In order to comprehensively assess how maltreatment relates to child development, it is imperative to examine how maltreatment is associated with child cooperation and exploration during reminiscing. There is evidence that maltreated children have lower levels of receptive language compared to nonmaltreated children (e.g., Stacks et al. 2011), and given that reminiscing is a language‐based task, maltreated children may also have lower levels of cooperation and exploration during reminiscing. Further, maltreatment is associated with fewer child unique memory contributions during reminiscing (Valentino et al. 2019); children's tendencies to independently elaborate on a memory with details that were not already provided by their mother may be limited in maltreated children. However, child cooperation and exploration during reminiscing has not been studied in prior research with maltreating families. Examining child cooperation and exploration will contribute to a better understanding of the impact of maltreatment on children's socioemotional development.

Process Through Which Maltreatment Impacts Child Cooperation and Exploration

One potential pathway through which maltreatment relates to child cooperation and exploration is through maternal sensitive guidance in the context of reminiscing. Past work demonstrates that maltreating mothers engage in more emotion invalidation (Shipman and Zeman 1999), decreased elaboration, and decreased sensitive guidance compared to nonmaltreating mothers (Valentino et al. 2019). Further, mothers who have perpetrated maltreatment are less emotionally involved or engage in fewer rich discussions about negative emotions than nonmaltreating mothers (Shipman et al. 2007). Insufficient maternal structuring may have downstream consequences for child cooperation and exploration (Koren‐Karie et al. 2004). For example, one study found that at ages 4 through 12, children experiencing intimate partner violence had lower levels of child cooperation and exploration than children who were not exposed, and mothers exhibited less sensitive guidance (Visser et al. 2016). Other studies found that mothers who experienced sexual abuse in their childhood showed less sensitive guidance, and their children exhibited decreased child cooperation and exploration (Koren‐Karie et al. 2008; van Delft et al. 2021). Yet no previous studies have examined maternal sensitive guidance as a mechanism that explains the association between maltreatment and child cooperation and exploration. In the present study, we anticipated that the relation between maltreatment and child cooperation and exploration would occur through maternal sensitive guidance.

Another goal of this study was to examine whether mothers socialize their children differently through reminiscing as a function of child sex. Outside of the maltreatment context, a number of studies have examined whether parents socialize their boys versus girls differently during reminiscing (Aznar and Tenenbaum 2015; Bird and Reese 2006; Buckner and Fivush IV 2000), but these studies have yielded mixed results. One meta‐analysis revealed no child sex differences in maternal elaboration (Waters et al. 2019); however, because most of the studies reviewed in the meta‐analysis focused on maternal elaboration, less is known about maternal sensitive guidance. Evidence that maternal sensitive guidance may differ for mothers of boys versus girls includes one study that evaluated the dyadic quality of reminiscing about negative events, which found that mothers exhibited higher levels of overall intersubjectivity and collaboration in mother–son dyads than in mother–daughter dyads (Laible et al. 2013). Parenting research in contexts beyond reminiscing has also demonstrated that child sex may moderate associations between parenting behavior and child behavior, suggesting that associations between supportive parent behaviors and reduced child problem behaviors are especially strong for boys (Barnett and Scaramella 2015). Still, there is limited research that investigates these processes in the context of maltreatment (Moody et al. 2018). Because it is unclear whether there are sex differences in associations between maternal sensitive guidance and child cooperation and exploration during reminiscing among children experiencing maltreatment, the present investigation aims to explore whether child sex moderates the indirect effect of maltreatment on child cooperation and exploration through maternal sensitive guidance.

Present Study

The present study examines how maltreatment is associated with child cooperation and exploration during reminiscing. Additionally, this study assesses the indirect effect of maltreatment on child cooperation and exploration via maternal sensitive guidance. The present study also contributes to the literature by evaluating whether this indirect effect is contingent upon child sex. We evaluated the following hypotheses: first, we hypothesized that child maltreatment would be negatively associated with child cooperation and exploration. Second, we hypothesized that maltreatment would be indirectly associated with child cooperation and exploration via maternal sensitive guidance. Finally, we hypothesized that the indirect effect would be significantly stronger in mother–son dyads compared to mother‐daughter dyads.

Method

Participants

The full sample included 248 children (165 maltreated; 83 nonmaltreated) between ages 3 and 6 and their mothers from a mid‐sized city in a Midwestern state. Mothers in the maltreatment group were eligible if they had a child at the ages of 3–6 living in the home, had a substantiated maltreatment history with the Department of Child Services (DCS), and demonstrated English proficiency. DCS Family Case Workers presented an informational flier and a verbal script to eligible mothers. Interested participants provided permission for their contact information to be shared with study staff. Demographically similar nonmaltreating dyads were recruited through local community organizations including Head Start, the housing authority, and the Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children. Nonmaltreating mothers were eligible if they had no prior history of maltreatment perpetration or DCS involvement. All participants provided informed consent upon enrollment and signed release forms to access their DCS records. The maltreating status of the families was verified with the DCS records and a maternal interview. Only two families did not complete the reminiscing task, so these mother–child dyads were excluded from all analyses (leaving a sample of n = 246).

Of these 246 families with reminiscing data, there were 6 mothers (n = 5 maltreating, n = 1 nonmaltreating) who scored two or more standard deviations below the sample mean (i.e., below 60; M = 84.95, SD = 12.63) on the PPVT‐4, a measure of receptive language described below. Performance on standardized language assessments, such as the PPVT, is associated with measures of intelligence (Hodapp and Gerken 1999). Given that reminiscing is a language‐based skill that involves basic verbal and cognitive abilities, these mother–child dyads were excluded from all analyses to minimize the effects of language impairments or potential intellectual disabilities on the results. This resulted in a final analytic sample of 240 dyads (159 maltreating, 81 nonmaltreating) for the present study.

Maltreating and nonmaltreating families were not significantly different in maternal race, child race, child sex, maternal age, child age, marital status, maternal receptive language, and maternal education (see Table 1). Procedure

The project was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Notre Dame with Approval Number 12‐06‐376. All participants completed a 2‐hour assessment; mothers and children completed measures with researchers in separate rooms and were reunited for the reminiscing task. All assessors and coders were naive to the families' maltreatment status.

Maltreatment Classification

DCS records were coded using the Maltreatment Classification System (MCS; Barnett et al. 1993) to confirm that the maltreating mothers were the perpetrators of at least one instance of child maltreatment and that nonmaltreating mothers did not have a history of child maltreatment. DCS records from 20% of the sample (n = 32) were double‐coded by graduate‐level coders to classify DCS records to establish inter‐rater reliability (κs = 0.81–1.0). In the present subsample of maltreated children, 12.8% experienced physical abuse, 4.50% experienced sexual abuse, 66.0% experienced physical neglect, 70.0% experienced emotional maltreatment, and 38.5% experienced moral‐legal or educational maltreatment. Maltreatment subtype comorbidity was high, with 61.5% of children experiencing more than one subtype; 32.7% experienced two subtypes, 22.4% experienced three subtypes, and 6.40% experienced four subtypes.

Mother–Child Reminiscing

Mother–child dyads reminisced about four past shared emotional experiences in which the child felt happy, sad, scared, and angry following the Autobiographical Emotional Events Dialogue paradigm (AEED; Koren‐Karie et al. 2003). Mothers were asked to nominate four past events experienced by the mother and child together, and assessors wrote the past times on notecards. Mothers were instructed to talk about the events with their children as if they were at home. For each emotion category, mothers were instructed to choose a past experience that their child would remember. The mother was instructed to discuss the happy event prior to the negative events, and the order of the negative events was counterbalanced across families. The interviewer then left the mother and child alone in the room to discuss the events with no time constraint, and they were videotaped. The minimum time that it took for mother–child dyads to complete the reminiscing task was 50 s, and the maximum time was 12 min and 53 s; the mean was 3 min and 27 s.

Mother–child reminiscing conversations were coded from videotaped recordings using the AEED coding scheme (Koren‐Karie et al. 2003). Maternal codes and parallel child codes were coded using a Likert scale from 1 to 9. Higher scores reflected more enactment of the behavior. Inter‐rater reliability was assessed with 20% of the sample (n = 52) at the beginning of the coding process and achieved for all mother codes (ICCs = 0.75–0.96) and child codes (ICCs = 0.75–0.98).

Maternal Sensitive Guidance

The maternal sensitive guidance composite variable was the mean score of all maternal scales during reminiscing, including (a) focus (mother focuses on task completion and the conversation), (b) acceptance and tolerance (mother encourages and accepts child thoughts), (c) involvement and reciprocity (mother is engaged and interested in the conversation), (d) closure of negative feelings (mother guides discussions of negative emotions towards child positive emotions and strengths), (e) structuring of the interaction (mother provides appropriate scaffolding to allow for the co‐construction of a meaningful narrative), (f) adequacy (discussed events accurately matched the emotion cue), and (g) coherence (the narrative development was clear and fluent). Past reminiscing research has empirically supported the creation of this composite for maternal sensitive guidance (e.g., McDonnell et al. 2022). The dimensions of this composite were all significantly correlated, ranging from 0.27 to 0.73 (all ps < 0.01); internal consistency across the seven maternal reminiscing dimensions was strong (α = 0.89).

Child Cooperation and Exploration

The child cooperation and exploration composite variable was the mean score of all child scales during reminiscing: (a) focus (child maintains focus on relevant themes), (b) acceptance and tolerance (child listens attentively, accepts the mother's ideas, and builds upon the ideas), (c) cooperation and reciprocity (child is involved and works cooperatively with the mother), (d) resolution of negative feelings (child ends the discussions of emotional content with a positive resolution), and (e) elaboration (child provides unique utterances to the narrative). Prior studies have also used this composite (de Schipper et al. 2025; Hsiao et al. 2015; Koren‐Karie et al. 2004). All correlations among the five dimensions of this composite were significant, ranging from 0.20 to 0.84 (ps < 0.01); internal consistency across the five child reminiscing dimensions was strong (α = 0.90).

Mother and Child Receptive Language Skills

The Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test, Fourth Edition (PPVT‐IV; Dunn and Dunn 2007) is a measure of verbal ability and receptive vocabulary for standard English skills in people ages 2.5 to 90. The test measures comprehension of words without requiring reading or writing. Participants are asked to select which of four illustrations that best represent a word read aloud by an experimenter. The PPVT‐IV was administered to all mothers and children. In this study, maternal receptive language was included as a covariate given the language‐based task of reminiscing; no significant differences between groups emerged with respect to maternal receptive language (see Table 1).

Analytic Plan

To evaluate whether there were differences in child cooperation and exploration as a function of maltreatment, an independent samples t‐test was run in SPSS. Child cooperation and exploration were used as the dependent variable, and maltreatment status (maltreating, nonmaltreating) was used as the grouping variable.

To evaluate our second hypothesis, we tested a path analysis model whereby maltreatment exerts an indirect effect on child cooperation and exploration through maternal sensitive guidance. Path analyses were conducted in Mplus (Mplus version 8.0; Muthén and Muthén 2017). Maltreatment was dichotomized into presence versus absence (1: maltreatment; 0: nonmaltreatment). Child cooperation and exploration was the dependent variable. The indirect effect from maltreatment to child cooperation and exploration through maternal sensitive guidance was specified. The indirect effect was tested using the bias‐corrected bootstrap method with 1000 re‐samples to construct 95% confidence intervals around the indirect effect (Fritz and MacKinnon 2007). To test our third hypothesis, we conducted a multigroup model by comparing a model in which the indirect effect was constrained to be equal across groups to a model in which the same pathway was freely estimated for boys and girls. A chi square difference test was used to determine whether there was a significant difference between the models in fit. In another model, we also constructed a new parameter using the “NEW” option and the “MODEL CONSTRAINT” command in Mplus to assess if the indirect effect for boys and girls was significantly different; this new parameter was calculated as the difference in indirect effects between groups (Lau and Cheung 2012). We used child sex as the grouping variable. In all analyses, maternal receptive language was included as a covariate predicting both the mediator and the outcome. Finally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in R to examine if the estimate of the indirect effect would differ in a longitudinal model (Georgeson et al. 2023). De‐identified data are available upon request.

Results

Descriptive Analyses

In support of our first directional hypothesis, maltreated children's cooperation and exploration scores were significantly lower (M = 4.39, SD = 1.14) than their nonmaltreated peers (M = 4.70, SD = 1.12), t(238) = 2.04, p = 0.02 (one‐tailed). In addition, maltreating and nonmaltreating mothers differed significantly with respect to average maternal sensitive guidance, such that maltreating mothers scored lower on sensitive guidance (M = 4.95, SD = 0.99) than nonmaltreating mothers (M = 5.25, SD = 1.05), t(238) = 2.17, p = 0.02 (one‐tailed). We evaluated the indirect effect of maltreatment on child cooperation and exploration through maternal sensitive guidance (Figure 1). Consistent with our second hypothesis, maltreatment was significantly associated with maternal sensitive guidance (b = −0.26, p < 0.05). To test the third hypothesis, we conducted a multigroup model by comparing a model in which the indirect effect was constrained to be equal across groups to a model in which the same pathway was freely estimated for boys and girls. We included maternal receptive language as a covariate predicting both the mediator and the outcome. The chi square difference test showed that the freely estimated model did not fit significantly better than the constrained model (Δχ2(3) = 3.69, p = 0.29). In another model, we also constructed a new parameter in Mplus using the “MODEL CONSTRAINT” command to further evaluate if the difference in the indirect effect between groups is significant (Lau and Cheung 2012). We used the “NEW” parameter option to construct a new parameter that was calculated as the difference in indirect effects for boys and girls. This effect was not significant (p = 0.08), so we did not further interpret the model for differences between boys and girls.

Sensitivity Analyses

Cross sectional mediation may yield biased parameter estimates; therefore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to control for potential confounds, including autoregressive and cross‐lagged correlations (Georgeson et al. 2023; see Appendix S1). In this method, T1 variables are simulated phantom variables, and T2 are the original variables in the present study. To test the estimate of the indirect effect in a hypothetical longitudinal model the range of the autoregressive correlations (r (T1 maltreatment, T2 maltreatment)) was set to values of 0.10 to 0.70 to depict a range of positive, weak to strong correlations. The potential range of autoregressive correlations is in the simulated model to test if the negative indirect effect would remain significant across a range of hypothetical correlations between T1 maltreatment and T2 maltreatment. The range of the cross‐lagged correlation (r (T1 maltreatment, T2 child cooperation and exploration)) was set to −0.20 to 0.70 based on the correlation between r (T2 maltreatment, T2 child cooperation and exploration) and to depict a range of weak to strong correlations; we chose −0.20 to expand the range of negative to positive correlations instead of only including −0.13 to 0.70. The correlation between T1 maternal sensitive guidance and T1 child cooperation and exploration was fixed to 0.67, based on the moderate correlation, r (T2 maternal sensitive guidance, T2 child cooperation and exploration) = 0.67 in the present study. The indirect effect remained negative and significant when adjusting for the autoregressive and cross‐lagged correlations, which suggests that our cross‐sectional model is robust to assumptions about longitudinal processes.

Post Hoc Analyses

We conducted post hoc sensitivity analyses including several covariates to assess the robustness of the indirect effect. In all post hoc analyses, maternal receptive language was included as a covariate predicting the mediator and the outcome. Results demonstrated that the indirect effect remained negative and statistically significant when maternal marital status, maternal education, maternal race/ethnicity, maternal age, child age, and child sex were each controlled for on child cooperation and exploration in separate models. The indirect effect, however, was nonsignificant when child receptive language was controlled for on child cooperation and exploration [95% CI −0.101, 0.035].

A final set of post hoc analyses further probed the directionality of the indirect effects (previously tested as maltreatment on child cooperation and exploration through maternal sensitive guidance) by evaluating the indirect effect of maltreatment on maternal sensitive guidance via child cooperation and exploration. We included maternal receptive language as a covariate predicting the mediator and the outcome. Results showed that the association between maltreatment and child cooperation and exploration was not significant (b = −0.189, p = 0.11), and the association between child cooperation and exploration and sensitive guidance was significant (b = 1.03, p < 0.01). The 95% confidence interval contained zero [−0.443, 0.030]; therefore, we concluded that the indirect effect between maltreatment and maternal sensitive guidance through child cooperation and exploration was not significant.

Discussion

The present study adds to the literature by investigating child contributions to mother–child reminiscing in the context of child maltreatment. As mothers sensitively guide their children during reminiscing, they provide opportunities for the child to explore their emotional world, which is an important developmental task during early childhood (Fivush et al. 2006). As children's interactions with parents are increasingly characterized by conversation, maternal sensitive guidance may provide children the space to explore their emotions and engage in the conversation with their mother (e.g., Thompson 2006). Therefore, our results underscore how mother–child reminiscing is a rich context for observing child functioning, and how maltreatment negatively influences child cooperation and exploration during reminiscing through lower maternal sensitive guidance.

We hypothesized that maltreatment would be associated with lower child cooperation and exploration, which reflects children's active participation and detailed input to the narrative. Evidence from the present study suggests that maltreated children demonstrated less cooperation and exploration during reminiscing than demographically matched nonmaltreated children, which was expressed verbally or observed in their behavior. While our composite score did not allow for us to disentangle distinct behaviors, lower child cooperation and exploration included behaviors such as impatience and difficulty in maintaining focus (Koren‐Karie et al. 2003). Additionally, lower levels of child cooperation and exploration were also marked by children's difficulty with contributing to the narrative. Children who have experienced maltreatment appear to struggle with cooperation and exploration when reminiscing about their emotions with their mothers. As children's reminiscing skills develop during the preschool years, exposure to trauma may negatively impact how children elaborate and interpret their experiences of everyday negative emotional events (Visser et al. 2016). Therefore, this study provides important empirical evidence to show that maltreatment is an interpersonal trauma that is negatively associated with child cooperation and exploration. These findings build upon past literature that has identified differences in how maltreated children reminisce compared to nonmaltreated children, including how maltreated children provide fewer unique memory contributions (Valentino et al. 2019).

In support for our second hypothesis, the sensitivity with which mothers engage their preschool‐aged children in emotional discussion was identified as an important mechanistic process through which maltreatment relates to child cooperation and exploration. Although our first hypothesis was supported because maltreated children performed significantly worse than nonmaltreated children on child cooperation and exploration during reminiscing per a t‐test, the direct effect of maltreatment on child cooperation and exploration was not significant in the path analysis model with maternal sensitive guidance as the mediator; this highlights the salient role of maternal contributions to the reminiscing conversation during early childhood. During reminiscing, children can learn the appropriate time to respond to their mothers and elaborate upon their emotional experiences. As children begin shaping knowledge about themselves, reminiscing provides an opportunity to demonstrate attentiveness and interest through actively contributing to the narrative. However, maltreating mothers may restrict the flow of dialogue, which then may have downstream consequences for children's responses. Indeed, maltreated children on average provide fewer unique memory contributions than nonmaltreated children (Valentino et al. 2019). Understanding these pathways to children's cooperation and exploration is important because child cooperation and exploration are important for children's emotional development (Laible et al. 2013). For example, children who were more engaged during reminiscing at 42 months demonstrated increased emotional understanding at 48 months (Laible et al. 2013). The identification of indirect factors that may explain the relationship between maltreatment and child cooperation and exploration supports a nuanced understanding of the processes that undergird children's socioemotional development.

We also examined whether the indirect effect of maltreatment on children's reminiscing through sensitive guidance was contingent on child sex. While a meta‐analysis that focused on maternal elaboration during reminiscing reported no differences as a function of child sex (Waters et al. 2019), there have been mixed findings with regard to maternal elaboration with boys and girls (Aznar and Tenenbaum 2015; Bird and Reese 2006; Buckner and Fivush IV 2000; Svane et al. 2022). We did not find that a model where pathways were allowed to differ by child sex fit significantly better than a model where paths were constrained. Thus, our finding aligns with the existing meta‐analysis on maternal elaboration (Waters et al. 2019). These findings suggest that in this sample, the strength of the mechanistic process through which maltreatment influences children's reminiscing does not depend on child sex.

Limitations

This study has a strong research design, yet there are some limitations. First, the indirect effect becomes nonsignificant when controlling for child receptive language in the model. Language is a developmental mechanism that provides children with the skillset to integrate their past experiences in a cohesive manner (Nelson and Fivush 2020). In early childhood, mothers support their children's verbal contributions to reminiscing by guiding them to make sense of their experiences (Fivush 2008). However, empirical studies have revealed the negative influences of maltreatment on children's receptive language (Valentino et al. 2015). For example, maltreated children exhibit deficits in syntactical sentence structure and vocabulary skills (Eigsti and Cicchetti 2004). It may be that child receptive language is an additional mechanism linking child maltreatment to children's cooperation and exploration. Deficits in receptive language could have downstream effects on the quality of children's contributions about their past emotional experiences, which is reflected in their cooperation and exploration during reminiscing. Future research should explore how child language skills may interrelate with child cooperation and exploration during reminiscing.

Second, we collapsed maltreatment subtypes to form a single maltreatment group. We did not examine maltreatment subtype or range of maltreatment severities because there were a limited number of sexual and physical abuse experiences in the present sample. Future research should aim to examine the potentially differential or compounding influences of maltreatment subtype and severity. The present study coded maternal sensitive guidance and child cooperation and exploration using a global assessment across reminiscing. Evaluating the interaction with global codes prevented coders from examining micro‐level influences of each member of the dyad on the other utterance by utterance across the conversations and may obfuscate some of the complexities of elaborative style (Neale and Pino‐Pasternak 2017).

We also acknowledge that the AEED paradigm rates the quality of discussion of four past emotional events—happy, sad, scared, angry—altogether. As such, we did not have separate codes within each event conversation to allow for the analysis of maternal and child behavior as a function of event valence (i.e., positive versus negative). From the perspective of the AEED paradigm and coding system, it is important to assess reminiscing about positive and negative events. Reminiscing about positively‐valenced events serves as a way to strengthen the child's relationships, whereas reminiscing about negatively‐valenced events facilitates children's emotional coping when challenging themes emerge (Fivush and Salmon 2023; Sales et al. 2003). Future studies that code utterance by utterance or within each event discussion may benefit from investigating potential differences in how the valence of reminiscing influences maternal sensitive guidance and child cooperation and exploration among maltreating families.

Future Directions

In future work, longitudinal research is necessary to evaluate the direction of association between maternal sensitive guidance and child cooperation and exploration. Given that the reminiscing data in this study were collected at the same time point, we cannot infer causal or directional claims. Moreover, there could be a bidirectional process occurring where maternal sensitive guidance and child cooperation and exploration shape each other's behavior during reminiscing. As children move beyond relying on their mothers to co‐construct emotional dialogues, it would be important to examine these same hypotheses across additional developmental stages. Children may have more substantial and significant contributions at later stages in their development, such as middle childhood and adolescence. To partially address the limitations of cross‐sectional data, we included a sensitivity analysis to test the indirect effect in a hypothetical longitudinal model (Georgeson et al. 2023). We found that the estimate of the indirect effect remained negative and significant across a range of cross‐lagged and autoregressive correlations, providing support for future longitudinal examination of this pathway.

Another important direction for future research is to consider the role of contingent responsivity during mother–child reminiscing. Contingent responsivity refers to how a parent responds depending on their child's preceding behavior (Ainsworth et al. 1974). During mother–child conversations, maternal contingent responsivity is associated with increased child cooperation and compliance, suggesting that mothers and children mutually influence each other, and the interaction is developed by patterns from both members (Schueler and Prinz 2013). Further, another study that used a micro‐analytic approach to examine the reciprocal process of reminiscing found that children directly respond with memory contributions following open‐ended questions, repetitions, and confirmations (Svane et al. 2021). In focusing on contingent responsivity in future research, we could identify how maternal responses are contingent upon how the child behaves, which is a key component of this type of dialogue.

Clinical Implications

Our findings have significant implications for the evaluation of parent–child interventions. For example, evidence‐based interventions that aim to improve maternal emotion socialization behaviors should assess whether there are intervention‐related improvements on child cooperation and exploration. A recent meta‐analysis revealed that interventions targeting parental emotion socialization behaviors increased positive parenting behaviors, decreased negative parenting behaviors, and improved children's emotional competence (England‐Mason et al. 2023), which are salient components of the co‐construction of reminiscing dialogues. This meta‐analytic evidence shows that higher quality of maternal emotion socialization via intervention facilitates children's healthy emotional development. Thus, it would be valuable to examine the role of both maternal and child contributions in interventions to elucidate whether improvements in child cooperation and exploration mediate the positive effects of the intervention on subsequent child socioemotional outcomes. Indeed, the quality of maternal sensitive guidance in maltreating families can be enhanced via intervention targeting reminiscing (Valentino et al. 2019). By examining the dynamic interactions between mothers and their children, future studies could advance our understanding of child cooperation and exploration as an important developmental outcome.
