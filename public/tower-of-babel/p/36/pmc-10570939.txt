A case report of severe unstable pelvic fracture with extensive perineal destruction and Morel-Lavallee lesion, with literature review
Authors: Ying Zhou, Bo Wang, Jing Wang, Ming Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and importanceUnstable pelvic fractures in children are mainly caused by high-impact injuries, which are often combined with multiple injuries. A rare type of degloving injury named Morel-Lavallee lesion (MLL) is easily overlooked in obese children.Case presentationA case of unstable pelvic fracture combined with extensive perineal injury and MLL in an obese child was discussed. The general conditions, diagnosis, and treatment were summarized.Clinical discussionUnstable pelvic fractures are associated with extremely high disability and mortality rates and are often associated with hemorrhagic shock, urethral injury, or lower-extremity dysfunction. Introduction

Unstable pelvic fractures in children are rare, but the morbidity and mortality rates are extremely high. Because the pelvis in children has significant shock absorption and postdeformation anatomical shaping ability after impact, only 10–16 % of patients require surgical intervention [1]. The rare Morel-Lavallee lesion (MLL) is easily overlooked in the initial evaluation [2].

We reported a case of unstable pelvic fracture with extensive perineal destruction and MLL in an obese girl with severe hemorrhage and reviewed the relevant literature. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria.

Presentation of case

The patient (female, 9.33 years old) was accidentally hit by a dump truck. At the time of admission, her Glasgow Coma Scale score was 10 points, and she was hemodynamically unstable. The internal urethra, vagina, anus and blood vessels were indistinguishable (Fig. 1A). The pelvic compression and distraction tests were positive. A hollow and fluctuating sensation could be felt upon compression in the region of her right waist and thigh. A computed tomography (CT) scan suggested severe damage to the pelvis; extreme instability; bone fracture of the right iliac bone, superior and inferior pubic rami, and left suprapubic ramus and ischium; separation of the pubic symphysis; and swelling of the surrounding soft tissues, with local defects (Fig. 1B, C).

A: Huge wound in the lower abdomen and perineum area of the patient, with severe damage such that the internal structures could not be distinguished. B: Three-dimensional CT reconstruction suggested pelvic structural damage, multiple fractures, and poor stability. C: Plain CT scan suggested swelling of soft tissues around the pelvis, accompanied by gas accumulation, local defects, and scattered metal-like opacity.

We immediately performed adequate fluid resuscitation on the patient and managed a multidisciplinary diagnosis and treatment involving orthopedics, vascular intervention, urology, general surgery, and burns and plastic surgery departments.

Step 1: Angiography + selective vascular embolization. The surgeon used the Seldinger technique to puncture the left femoral artery, and the dissection of the right internal iliac artery was visualized after angiography of the abdominal aorta and bilateral common iliac arteries (Fig. 2A). The 5S125 distal access catheter was inserted with the aid of a guidewire, and the Echelon10 microcatheter was superselected to the posterior trunk of the right internal iliac artery with the assistance of the Avigo microguidewire. Embolization was conducted using detachable coil systems QC-6-20-3D, QC-6-15-3D, QC-5-15-3D, and QC-4-8-3D, and angiography showed that the embolus was dense and that there was no contrast agent extravasation (Fig. 2B).

Step 2: Pelvic reduction and external fixation. Half-pins were inserted into the bilateral iliac wing, anterior superior iliac spine, and pubic ramus. The fixation was locked with a connecting rod after restoration of the anterior and posterior pelvic rings. The CT image confirmed that the alignment of the fracture site and the quality of the pelvic ring reduction were acceptable (Fig. 2C). Additionally, the rupture of the left femoral vein was surgically explored, and a suture was used to stop the bleeding.

We sutured the rectal tissue to the skin at the original anal site to shape the anus, and an anal canal was placed (Fig. 2D). Throughout the 6-month follow-up, the external fixator and all drainage tubes were successfully removed. The MLL gradually alleviated, and the survival rate of the skin graft was fair. After 1.5 years of follow-up, the fracture had healed well, and the shape of the pelvic ring had recovered well. Unfortunately, the proximal femur has myositis ossificans. The Oswestry disability index of the patient was 15.5, and the Fugl-Meyer Assessment score was 91 (Fig. 2F, G).

Discussion

Unstable pelvic fractures in children are mostly caused by high-energy injuries. Compared with adults, children have higher bone flexibility, greater joint mobility, stronger ligament elasticity, lower vascular fragility, and therefore a lower incidence (approximately 1/100,000) of these injuries. In this case, the patient was in hemorrhagic shock at the time of admission. We quickly used the pelvic strap to achieve the partial restoration of pelvic stability. Persistent fresh bleeding in the perineum was voluminous and difficult to control. Therefore, the possibility of arterial hemorrhage in the pelvic cavity was considered. We immediately started multidisciplinary diagnosis and personalized treatment to break the conventional hemostasis. Once rupture of the posterior trunk of the right internal iliac artery was confirmed, embolization was used to successfully stopped the hemorrhage.

This patient presented a type C and Torode-Zieg IV pelvic fracture [3,4]. The pelvic strap was not effective. To restore the deformed structure of the pelvis, we used external stent fixation. This type of surgical approach cannot achieve anatomical reduction but can quickly stabilize the anterior–posterior pelvic ring and reduce the volume of the pelvic cavity to stop the bleeding as soon as possible, which could significantly reduce the mortality rate [5,6]. It can also maximize the anatomical reduction of the pelvis in children and leave room for growth, thereby reducing complications such as premature fusion of the sacroiliac joint, severe pelvic tilt, and pelvic pain [7]. The results confirmed that the method effectively induced hemostasis and that pelvic reduction was good. In the subsequent imaging examination, we found no loss of pelvic morphology, no reseparation of the sacroiliac joint and good fracture healing.

MLL is a closed, latent degloving injury of the soft tissue in the area surrounding a pelvic fracture. The dermal layer function of obese children is abnormal, subcutaneous adipose tissue accumulates, and the deep fat layer is loosely attached, so the rapid shearing force makes the skin separate from the fascia layer more easily to form a large lacuna [2]. At the same time, the thicker fat layer also hinders the resorption process and visibility, so it is easier to overlook in the early stage of injury in obese children [8]. In this case, the patient had a body mass index of 34 kg/m2. Within a few hours after the injury, she had a large area of bruising and swelling around the pelvis and the right leg. Temporary open debridement and percutaneous drainage were performed to reduce the pressure and the deformed dead space. Over the next few days, the MLL progressively worsened. Therefore, we trimmed the necrotic tissue and removed the inflammatory mediators and degenerative exudate. Good treatment results were achieved with the later staging of implants.

In conclusion, the risk of unstable pelvic fractures in children is extremely high, and they are prone to the complications of hemorrhagic shock and urethral injury. Clinicians need to pay attention to the occurrence and development of MLL in obese children. Multidisciplinary diagnosis and personalized treatment provide a comprehensive and accurate assessment and approach to achieve good outcomes.
