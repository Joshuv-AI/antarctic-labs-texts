Iron-catalyzed oxidative stress reveals an exposome-related ferroptosis-resistant karyomegalic niche in BRCA1-linked renal carcinogenesis
Authors: Yingyi Kong, Yukihiro Shiraki, Kazuhiro Furuhashi, Shoichi Maruyama, Tatsuhiko Imaoka, Atsushi Enomoto, Shinya Toyokuni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early cancer initiation under chronic oxidative stress requires the survival of epithelial cells that should otherwise be eliminated by regulated cell death. However, the spatial and morphological identity of such stress-adapted cells remains poorly defined. Here, we integrated Xenium single-cell spatial transcriptomics, quantitative nuclear morphometrics, mitochondrial analyses, and human cancer datasets to investigate iron-driven renal carcinogenesis in Brca1(L63X/+) rats. Ferric nitrilotriacetate exposure induced dynamic remodeling of proximal tubular epithelium, characterized by loss of homeostatic transport and mitochondrial programs, and activation of Myc, Met, Lcn2, Anxa2 and iron-handling genes. BRCA1 haploinsufficiency expanded a karyomegalic proximal tubular state associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, chromatin hyperchromasia, nuclear remodeling, and ferroptosis-resistant transcriptional features. Subclassification of karyomegalic cells identified distinct stress-adaptive populations, including a BRCA1-associated K2 state enriched for Myc/Met/Lcn2 signaling and preferentially embedded within a pro-oncogenic epithelial–stromal microenvironment. Of note, neighboring non-karyomegalic proximal tubular cells acquired related iron-handling and survival-biased programs, indicating that karyomegaly marks a broader pre-neoplastic field rather than an isolated abnormality. Human TCGA renal cancer analyses and morphometric assessment of BRCA1-mutant breast tissue further supported the clinical relevance of karyomegaly-associated gene modules and nuclear remodeling. These findings define a spatially organized, ferroptosis-resistant karyomegalic niche as an early lesion in iron-driven carcinogenesis and establish quantitative nuclear morphology as a functional readout of exposome-related oxidative stress adaptation in genetically susceptible tissues.

Introduction

Iron is essential for life [1], yet its redox activity also drives oxidative reactions that promote mutagenesis and malignant transformation [2,3]. Although persistent oxidative stress is increasingly recognized as a trigger of tumor initiation, how damaged epithelial cells survive this hostile redox environment and acquire tumor-initiating potential remains poorly understood. We previously established a ferric nitrilotriacetate (Fe-NTA) model of Fenton reaction–driven carcinogenesis that reproducibly induces metastatic renal adenocarcinomas and enables mechanistic analysis of oxidative stress–mediated tumorigenesis [[4], [5], [6]]. However, the spatially organized molecular programs that allow a subset of injured epithelial cells to evade elimination and persist as pre-neoplastic precursors have not been defined.

Hereditary cancers account for 5–10% of human malignancies [7,8]. Among hereditary cancer genes, BRCA1 and BRCA2 are best known for their association with breast and ovarian cancers [9], but growing evidence suggests broader roles in epithelial cancer susceptibility, including renal cell carcinoma [10,11]. We recently established Brca1/2 haploinsufficient rat models that recapitulate key aspects of human disease [[12], [13], [14]]. Notably, Fe-NTA–induced carcinogenesis is markedly accelerated in Brca1 haploinsufficient rats and accompanied by recurrent c-Myc amplification and mitochondrial abnormalities during early tumorigenesis [15,16]. These findings raise the possibility that BRCA1 deficiency promotes the emergence of stress-adaptive epithelial states that evade ferroptotic elimination during tumor initiation.

Recent advances in spatial transcriptomics enable single-cell–resolution molecular profiling while preserving tissue architecture and histological context [17]. Here, we integrated Xenium-based spatial transcriptomics with quantitative digital nuclear morphometrics to investigate early carcinogenic processes under iron-driven oxidative stress. This approach identified a previously unrecognized ferroptosis-resistant karyomegalic epithelial population associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, oncogenic signaling, and iron-handling programs. Integration with human cancer datasets further suggested that related stress-adaptive programs and nuclear abnormalities may be relevant to human epithelial tumorigenesis. Together, our findings establish a spatial and morphological framework for understanding how exposome-related [18] oxidative stress intersects with hereditary susceptibility during the earliest stages of tumor initiation.

Materials and methods

Renal carcinogenesis model by ferric nitrilotriacetate (Fe-NTA)

All the animal experiments were approved by the animal experimental committee of Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine. Sprague-Dawley strain male rats of wild-type (WT) and Brca1(L63X/+; mutant, MUT) [12] were bred as described previously [15]. Representative kidney specimens of 1 and 3 weeks under the carcinogenesis protocol were obtained and fixed either in phosphate-buffered 10% formalin for 48 h for paraffin-embedding, or 2 % glutaraldehyde in 1 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for resin-embedding.

Tissue array construction

Six formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) rat kidney samples were organized into a tissue array (TA) format to enable simultaneous Xenium processing (three samples per slide; two slides per instrument run). RNA quality was pre-verified by RNAScope in situ on the sections from all the donor blocks. After confirmation, sample area was selected and manually extracted to re-form new TA blocks with samples coring within proper size of area (∼12 mm × 24 mm). From each TA block, 5 μm sections were cut with a rotary microtome (Leica RM2135) and mounted onto Xenium adhesive slides (10x Genomics). Sections were dried overnight, then stored in a desiccator until processing. All cutting and mounting steps were performed with precautions to maintain tissue morphology and RNA integrity (sterile, RNase-free tools and surfaces).

Gene panel design

A custom 50-gene Xenium In Situ probe panel (ID: TCMFHM) was designed for Rattus norvegicus using the 10x Genomics Custom Panel Designer (Table S1). Target genes were selected to represent major renal cell types and key kidney injury pathways, prioritizing established marker genes for nephron segments (proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct principal/intercalated cells), glomerular cell types (podocytes, endothelial), interstitial cells (stromal cells), immune infiltrates (inflammatory cells), and urothelium, alongside transcripts implicated in our previous differential expression array analyses [15,19,20]. Gene selection was further guided by a published rat kidney single-cell transcriptomic atlas (E-GEAD-450 [21]), retaining only genes with robust, cell type-specific expression in the reference dataset. The final panel included renal biomarkers and ferroptosis-, oncogenic/tumor suppressor-, mitochondria-associated genes relevant to renal pathophysiology [15,22]. Probes were tiled across exonic regions with high specificity, minimizing off-target homology and maximizing RNA accessibility; negative control and decoding control probes were included for quality control [23].

Xenium in situ workflow

FFPE tissue sections (5 μm) were processed using the Xenium FFPE in situ transcriptomics protocol (10x Genomics, v1.0; Demonstrated Protocol CG000580; Fig. 1A). Briefly, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval and RNA exposure, and enzymatically digested under RNase-free conditions to facilitate probe penetration while preserving tissue morphology. Probe hybridization was performed at 50 °C for approximately 18 h. After stringent washes, adjacently hybridized probe pairs were ligated for 2 h at 37 °C and amplified by rolling circle amplification (RCA) for 2 h at 30 °C to generate detectable signals [24]. Tissue autofluorescence was reduced by chemical quenching, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for visualization and segmentation. Xenium analyzer instrument

Automated imaging and transcript decoding were performed on the 10x Genomics Xenium Analyzer using software v2.0.1.0. The instrument acquired high-resolution images through 15 iterative cycles of fluorescent probe binding, imaging, and signal removal. Four fluorescent channels were used per cycle, allowing each gene in the custom panel to be identified by a unique optical barcode. Images were acquired at 200 nm/pixel resolution with a 20× objective, and Z-stack images were collected at 0.75 μm intervals. Adjacent fields of view were tiled and computationally stitched using DAPI nuclear signals as fiducial landmarks to generate a registered whole-tissue image.

Transcripts were decoded by matching fluorescent puncta to the predefined barcode codebook. Only transcripts with a Q-score ≥20, corresponding to approximately 99% decoding accuracy, were retained for downstream analysis [23]. Detected transcripts were exported with XY coordinates, gene identities, and Q-scores. Negative-control probe signals accounted for <0.03% of total reads, indicating low background noise.

Cell segmentation and transcript assignment

Cell segmentation was performed using the Xenium onboard segmentation pipeline. DAPI images were used to identify nuclear boundaries, which were expanded isotropically by up to 15 μm or until they reached neighboring cell boundaries. This nuclear expansion method generated non-overlapping cell polygons approximating renal epithelial cell boundaries. Decoded transcripts located within each polygon were assigned to the corresponding cell, producing a cell-by-gene expression matrix for downstream single-cell spatial analysis [23].

Post-Xenium histology

After transcript profiling, the same tissue sections were re-stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to correlate molecular data with tissue morphology. H&E images were acquired using a Leica Aperio AT2 slide scanner at 40× magnification and processed in QuPath 0.5.1 to generate OME-TIFF files. The H&E images were then aligned to DAPI images in Xenium Explorer, allowing transcript-defined cell types and spatial regions to be evaluated in relation to histological structures, such as glomeruli and renal tubules.

Bioinformatic analysis of spatial transcriptomics data

Data preprocessing and normalization

Xenium outputs (six rat kidney FFPE tissue samples: WT_CT, MUT_CT, WT_Fe1w, MUT_Fe1w, WT_Fe3w, MUT_Fe3w) were loaded into R (version 4.4.1) and converted to a Seurat object using Seurat v5.0.1. All data normalization was performed by SCTransform to stabilize variance transformation and to regress out sequencing-depth effects. This yielded log-normalized count data (stored in Seurat “SCT” assay) for each sample, mitigating technical variability while preserving biological variation. Low-quality cells (e.g. with extremely low transcript counts) were minimal due to Xenium's preprocessing and only cells with nCount_Xenium >0 were retained.

Data integration and clustering

Following normalization, the unified object containing data from six samples was processed for joint analysis. We re-ran SCTransform (merged), PCA (all genes; 30 PCs), FindNeighbors (dims 1–30), FindClusters (resolution 2.0), and UMAP (PCA), which yielded multiple clusters with distinct transcriptional signatures. Mixing across samples in PCA/UMAP was inspected to verify minimal batch effects. Clusters were manually annotated to kidney lineages (proximal tubule, stromal, endothelial, loop of Henle, collecting duct principal/intercalated, podocyte, inflammatory, proximal-like) based on canonical markers [21] and spatial context provided by Xenium Explorer. For focused analyses, we subset proximal_tubules and stromal_cells contents and re-ran the same pipeline on (i) the merged dataset to confirm lineage assignments and (ii) each compartment separately to resolve fine subclusters (pt_n; st_n) for downstream analyses.

Pathological score maps

Proximal-tubule clusters were defined into four pathology groups by expressional signatures and tubular morphology: normal_tubules (PT_1,2,5,6,10,11), acute_injured (PT_8), subacute_injured (PT_0,3,4,7), and dying_tubules (PT_9). Using Seurat v5.0.1 we set group identities and performed one-vs-rest DE per group with FindMarkers (two-sided Wilcoxon; min.pct = 0.2, logfc.threshold = 0.25, only.pos = TRUE). The top 10 genes by avg_log2FC per group were used for visualization (group-wise AverageExpression heatmap, z-scored by gene). For quantitative per-cell scores, we computed module scores with AddModuleScore on the available marker genes for each group (requiring ≥5 genes), using nbin = 3 and ctrl = 10 to ensure robust background control on the limited panel. Scores were then row-matched by cell_ID and merged into the Giotto (v4.2.0) object's cell_metadata for downstream spatial mapping. All plotting of score maps used fixed global min–max limits per score and unified diverging palettes to enable cross-sample comparison.

Morphological-transcriptomics analysis of nuclei

Nuclear polygons from Xenium segmentation (“nucleus_boundaries.csv.gz”) were imported into R (sf v1.0.19; tidyverse v2.0.0) to compute per-nucleus morphometrics: nuclear area, eccentricity (elongation, 0–1), circularity (4π·area/perimeter2), compactness, convexity, solidity (area/convex hull area) and perimeter/area ratio (boundary irregularity). Polygons were linked to the same cell barcodes used in Seurat; analyses were restricted to proximal tubule cells with expression taken from the SCT assay.

For each gene–feature pair, we fit single-cell linear models per FOV (WT_Fe3w: FOV_3; MUT_Fe3w: FOV_6) with the morphology feature z-scored and technical covariates (library size, detected features) included; slopes (β) and two-sided P values were recorded and FDR-adjusted within FOV (Benjamini–Hochberg). As robustness checks, we computed Spearman correlations and ran elastic net models (glmnet) to assess whether multiple features jointly predict expression; a non-zero coefficient denoted feature selection.

To obtain genotype-robust effects, per-FOV slopes were combined by sample-size–weighted averaging (pooled β), and per-FOV P values were combined by Stouffer's method (weights ∝ √n), followed by FDR across all pairs. For each gene we derived a “consensus rank” integrating pooled β, pooled FDR, and elastic-net selection.

To identify coordinated programs, each gene was represented by its vector of pooled β across morphology features. Cosine similarity was computed between genes; pairs with similarity ≥0.6 were connected and clustered by Louvain community detection to define morphology-linked gene modules.

Quantification of nuclear phenotypes and integration with transcriptomics

For fields of view (FOVs) from WT_Fe3w (FOV3) and MUT_Fe3w (FOV6), nuclei were segmented using Cellpose (v2.3.2; pretrained nuclei model) [37]. Three per-cell nuclear morphological readouts were derived from the hematoxylin channel (skimage.color.rgb2hed) within each nuclear mask: (i) hematoxylin optical density (HOD), mean channel intensity following Gaussian smoothing (σ = 0.5 μm); (ii) nuclear substructure density (NSD), the count of intranuclear hematoxylin-dense foci, detected after within-nucleus 2nd-98th percentile normalization, Otsu thresholding, and filtration by area (0.3–6.0 μm2) and circularity (4πA/P2 ≥ 0.40), expressed per 100 μm2 of nuclear area; and (iii) peripheral intensity index (PII), (mean HODperiphery − mean HODinterior)/section-median HOD, where peripheral (distance ≤ s) and interior (distance > s) zones were defined from a Euclidean distance transform of the nuclear boundary at shell thicknesses s ∈ (0.5, 1.0, 1.5) μm (Fig. S4C); section-median HOD was estimated once per FOV from a random sample of ∼2000 nuclei.

Cellpose-segmented nuclei were assigned Xenium cell identity via two-stage spatial registration. First, an existing QuPath-to-Xenium cell correspondence (established by manual landmark selection and iterative refinement) provided position anchors for an affine transform between the FOV image and Xenium coordinate spaces. Second, true nuclear centroids (QuPath nucleus geometry) were matched to the affine-registered Cellpose nuclei by reciprocal nearest-neighbor matching within the shared coordinate space, at the tightest tolerance (0.5–5 μm ladder) retaining ≥90% of the maximum achievable match count. PT filtering was performed after registration to avoid selection bias. Registration accuracy was confirmed by comparing QuPath's measurements (HOD; Fig. S4A) against Cellpose/scikit-image values.

Gene–morphometry association models (HOD/NSD/PII)

For each gene g and within a condition (WT_Fe3w; MUT_Fe3w) independently, a single-cell ordinary least-squares model was fitted:Yi=α+βg·Xig+γAi+ϵiwhere Yi is the nuclear morphometric readout for cell i (HOD; NSD; PIIs), Xig is the log1p-normalized Xenium transcript count for gene g in cell i, and Ai is Cellpose-measured nuclear area (μm2). Two-sided Wald p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons across 50 genes within each condition (Benjamini–Hochberg, FDR <0.05). Cross-condition concordance was assessed by Pearson correlation and sign agreement of per-gene βˆ between FOVs. Estimates were pooled by fixed-effects inverse-variance weighting:βˆFE=∑kwkβˆk∑kwk,wk=SEk−2with pooled Wald z-statistics BH-FDR corrected across all 50 genes. Models were implemented in Python (statsmodels v0.14; scipy).

Karyomegaly sub-stratification and peri-karyomegaly analysis

PT cells from merged Xenium dataset were defined as karyomegalic (enlarged) if nuclear area > mean+2SD within the PT cell population, and elongated if major/minor axis ratio >2. SCTransform-normalized RNA counts from karyomegalic cells were stored as a SCTx assay. Morphological features processed from nuclear polygons were stored as a MORPH assay and reduced by PCA.

Karyomegalic PTs were then reclustered by a weighted nearest-neighbor (WNN) model that integrates RNA and morphology: SCTx PCA (dims 1–30) and a PCA of z-scored morphology (dims 1–6) were combined with FindMultiModalNeighbors (modality weights recorded as SCTx.weight/MORPH.weight), followed by UMAP on the weighted graph and Louvain communities on wsnn; we grid-searched morphology PCs (2-4, 6) × resolution (0.2, 0.3) and selected the setting with the highest mean silhouette in the WNN UMAP, followed by further resolution tuning, yielding the final result (K0–K5, resolution 0.15). Gene markers were computed using Seurat FindAllMarkers (logistic regression with covariates sample, fov_num, nCount_SCTx). Morphology tiles report per-feature means with significance from two-sided linear models (BH-FDR <0.05).

Spatial context was obtained by overlaying subcluster labels onto H&E in Xenium Explorer (Fe-NTA, 3-week). For peri-karyomegalic analyses, each karyomegalic PT served as a center; PT neighbors within 30 μm were compared with PTs >30 μm away, and hallmark expression visualized as z-scored heatmaps/dot plots (Wilcoxon, BH-FDR where indicated).

Neighborhood composition and microenvironment analysis

Cell centroids (centroid_x, centroid_y) and annotations from the Seurat metadata were filtered to proximal_tubules and stromal_cells, and used for point map plotting and neighbor-associated computations. Independently for each sample, the number of stromal neighbors within a 30 μm Euclidean radius was recorded for each PT cell, by a KD-tree constructed over stromal cell centroids (FNN v1.1.4.1, R; k ≤ 500). Group differences were assessed by Kruskal–Wallis test; pairwise post-hoc comparisons used Dunn's test with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction (rstatix v0.7.3, R). Beyond cell-intrinsic analysis, we modeled proximal-tubule/stromal spatial organization with Giotto (v4.2.0; Python backend) [25]. For each field, we constructed a radius-based neighbor graph (30 μm) to capture tubulointerstitial contacts in renal tissue [26]. Community detection on this neighborhood graph using the Leiden algorithm (findNetworkCommunities) yielded microenvironment (ME) clusters, i.e., spatially coherent neighborhoods that may contain mixed PT (pt_n) and stromal (st_n) subtypes. We quantified the cell-type composition and contact enrichment for each ME cluster (normalized by cell abundance). ME prevalences were compared across the six samples to assess genotype/treatment effects. ME labels were overlaid on tissue images in Xenium Explorer to verify anatomic plausibility.

Significance stars denote: ∗ for ∣rij∣≥1.96, ∗∗ for ∣rij∣≥2.58, and ∗∗∗ for ∣rij∣≥3.00. Differential expression, statistics, and reproducibility

All analyses were performed in R (v4.4.1) unless reported otherwise. Differential expression (DE) used Seurat FindMarkers/FindAllMarkers on SCT-normalized data with two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Genes were eligible if detected in ≥5% of cells in either group; we report avg_log2FC (positive = higher in the first group), pct_in/pct_out, and adjusted P. Multiple testing was controlled by Benjamini–Hochberg; genes with FDR <0.05 and log2FC ≥ 0.25 were considered significant. Random subsampling (set.seed 42) was applied only to downsample points for visualization and was never applied in statistical testing. Box-and-violin plots display median and inter-quartile range; error bars represent 95% confidence intervals unless specified. Because the Xenium dataset included one representative specimen per genotype/treatment condition, cell-level statistics were interpreted as discovery-level measures of spatial association or enrichment rather than animal-level biological replication; key conclusions were supported by prior Fe-NTA data, mitochondrial TEM/REF analyses, and TCGA-based human validation.

Mitochondrial cristae morphometry analysis

Transmission electron microscopic images were acquired on a JEM-1400PLUS (JEOL; Tokyo, Japan). Images were analyzed in Fiji/ImageJ (v1.54p). After pixel calibration, individual mitochondria were manually outlined as ROIs with area (μm2) recorded. Within each ROI, cristae ridges were segmented using Trainable Weka Segmentation (Fiji) to obtain a probability map for cristae vs. matrix. High-confidence pixels were thresholded and processed with Ridge Detection to obtain ridge centerlines, followed by Skeletonize (2D/3D) and Analyze Skeleton to quantify branch geometry. Derived per-mitochondrion metrics were:Total cristae length (μm) = Σ branch lengths;Mean branch length (μm) = Σ branch lengths / number of branches;Cristae length density (μm/μm2) = total cristae length / mitochondrial area.

Metrics were exported as CVS files and plotted in R.

Immunoblotting

Kidney cortex lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer, resolved by SDS–PAGE and transferred by standard methods. Primary antibodies included: anti-FTMT (ab124889, Abcam), anti-Mfrn1 (PA5-26720, Invitrogen), anti-Mfrn2 (PA5-42498, Invitrogen); anti-ACTB (AC-15, Sigma) served as loading control. Bands were quantified in ImageJ, normalized to ACTB, and analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9 (unpaired t-test; significance thresholds: ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001; n.s., not significant).

Primary rat embryonic fibroblasts (REFs) and treatments

REFs were derived from E13–15 embryos by standard protocols [28]. Genotypes and sex were determined by PCR [15,29]. Male-derived WT and Brca1-MUT REFs were maintained in DMEM (043-30085, Wako, Osaka, Japan) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biowest; Nuaillé, France) and Mito + Serum Extender (355006, Corning, USA). REFs were immortalized via lentiviral transduction of SV40 (Addgene#22298) packaged with pspAX2 (#12260) and VSV-G (#14888). For oxidative stress, cells were treated with Fe-NTA (500 μM, 6 h) prior to fixation.

Immunofluorescence (confocal)

Cells were fixed, permeabilized and stained with anti-TOM20 (11802-1-AP, Proteintech) and anti-γ-H2AX (05-636, Sigma-Aldrich); nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. A Zeiss confocal microscope (LSM880, Carl Zeiss) was applied for image acquisition.

Mitochondrial respiration

Oxygen consumption (OCR) and extracellular acidification (ECAR) were measured on a Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer (Agilent) following manufacture's instructions. Briefly, REFs were seeded (6 × 104/well) in Seahorse XF DMEM (103575-100) supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine (103577/8/9-100) on a Seahorse XFe24 plate and incubated overnight. The XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (103015-100) was run with sequential injections of oligomycin 1.5 μM, FCCP 1 μM, and rotenone/antimycin A 0.5 μM. Cell count was quantified using BioTek Cytation 5 and Keyence BZ-X800 for normalization. Data were processed per manufacturer's instructions and analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9 (unpaired t-test with significance shown in Fig. 8E ).

TCGA transcriptomic cohorts

To evaluate the clinical relevance of karyomegaly-associated transcriptional programs identified in our experimental model, we analyzed data obtained from the TCGA Pan-Cancer Clinical Data Resource, for TCGA-KIRC as the primary validation cohort, and additional tumor types for exploratory analyses, including TCGA-BRCA, TCGA-COAD, TCGA-LIHC, TCGA-LUAD, and TCGA-LUSC [30].

RNA-seq data were retrieved via TCGAbiolinks v2.32.0 [31] from the NCI GDC. Tumor-adjacent solid tissue normal (STN) samples were used for the primary analyses, whereas primary tumor samples were evaluated separately for independent validation. Raw counts were normalized by the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method using edgeR v4.2.2 [32] and converted to log2-counts per million (log2-CPM; prior count = 1). For duplicate gene entries, the isoform with the highest mean expression was retained.

Karyomegalic module definition and scoring

Four gene modules were evaluated (Fig. S7A). Three gene modules were defined based on findings from our Xenium spatial transcriptomics data. Module activity was quantified by single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) using GSVA v1.52.3 [33], computed independently within each tissue compartment (STN or tumor). Only modules with at least three genes present in the expression matrix were retained. Patient-level scores were derived as mean across available samples.

Survival analysis

Clinical annotations, including progression-free interval (PFI) and overall survival (OS), were obtained from curated TCGA clinical datasets. For each module, patients were stratified into high- and low-score groups using the cohort-specific median module score as the cutoff. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated using the survival and survminer packages.

Associations between module activity and clinical outcome were evaluated using log-rank tests and univariable Cox proportional hazards models. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated from Cox models. Analyses were performed separately for PFI and OS endpoints. Cohorts with fewer than five outcome events or insufficient matched samples were excluded from survival testing.

A tiered strategy was used for Multiple-testing correction. We designated TCGA-KIRC as the confirmatory cohort and analyzed non-renal cancer cohorts in an exploratory manner. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was therefore performed separately within confirmatory and exploratory tiers. Statistical significance was assessed primarily using the tier-specific FDR-adjusted results.

BRCA1 mutation carrier and control definition

BRCA1 germline carriers were defined as described [34]. Non-carrier controls were identified by a two-tiered exclusion strategy requiring availability of diagnostic WSIs and absence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic germline variants in BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, ATM, CHEK2, RAD51C, RAD51D, BARD1, and NBN. Controls were further selected by two-stage propensity score matching to control for clinical covariates [35]. The final cohort comprised 20 BRCA1 germline carriers and 63 matched non-carrier controls (76.2% ductal, 4.8% lobular, 19.0% other histological subtypes).

Histopathological image analysis

Diagnostic whole slide images (WSIs) for TCGA-BRCA were obtained from the NCI Genomic Data Commons (GDC). Germline variant data were accessed from the TCGA PanCanAtlas Germline dataset [34] under controlled GDC access (dbGaP: phs000178), in compliance with the TCGA data use agreement.

Regions of interest (ROIs) containing well-formed ductal tubules were manually annotated in ductal carcinoma WSIs from both groups using QuPath v0.4 [36] (15 controls, 7 carriers; ROI count was limited by preserved adjacent normal portions). Nucleus segmentation was performed using Cellpose v2.3.2 [37], and morphological features were extracted using scikit-image. A post-hoc quality filter was applied to remove spurious detections. Nucleus-level analyses are presented as exploratory findings; patient-level analyses constitute the primary statistical inference.

Results

Xenium in situ on early stages of Fe-NTA-induced renal carcinogenesis

To identify early alterations during iron-driven renal carcinogenesis, we analyzed kidneys from wild-type (WT) and Brca1 mutant (L63X/+; MUT) rats subjected to Fe-NTA administration, as previously described [15]. Because rat-compatible spatial transcriptomic panels remain limited, we designed a custom 50-gene Xenium panel covering renal structural markers from single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets [21], together with genes related to iron metabolism, mitochondrial function, and tumorigenesis based on our previous studies [5,38].

Six kidney samples representing control, 1-week, and 3-week Fe-NTA exposure in WT and MUT rats were analyzed across two Xenium slides. DAPI-based segmentation captured approximately 324,765–531,476 cells per sample, with median transcript detection of 62–104 transcripts per cell (Fig. 1A). Post-Xenium H&E staining preserved tissue morphology and enabled direct integration of spatial transcriptomics with histopathology (Fig. 1A–D). Seurat-based clustering identified 53 cellular clusters, which were annotated into 10 major renal cell categories based on marker expression and histological context (Fig. 1B and C; Fig. S1A and B). Molecular annotations closely matched H&E-defined structures, supporting robust cell-type assignment even with a focused gene panel (Fig. 1D). Cluster composition varied across genotype and treatment conditions, with prominent changes in proximal tubules and stromal cells (Fig. S1C), indicating early remodeling of epithelial and interstitial compartments during Fe-NTA-induced carcinogenesis.

Characterization of key-gene signatures in early renal carcinogenesis

We next focused on transcriptional remodeling of proximal tubular cells (PTs), the principal target of Fe-NTA-induced renal carcinogenesis. Refined clustering of PT and stromal compartments improved resolution of treatment- and genotype-associated cell states (Fig. S2A and B). Single-cell expression analysis revealed reciprocal regulation of homeostatic and injury-associated genes in PTs (Fig. 2A). In control kidneys, PTs highly expressed renal function and mitochondrial metabolism genes, hereafter referred to as “friend key-genes,” including Lrp2, Pink1, and Amacr. In contrast, Fe-NTA exposure induced “foe key-genes” associated with injury and tumorigenesis, including Myc, Met, Lcn2, and Anxa2, with concomitant reduction of homeostatic markers.

Several foe key-genes were induced as early as 1 week after Fe-NTA exposure in both genotypes (Fig. 2A). Although Met, Anxa2, Lcn2, Sfxn3, and Klf4 showed partial reduction by 3 weeks, Myc and Slc22a17 remained elevated or further increased. At 3 weeks, MUT PTs showed stronger expression of foe markers than WT, whereas Lrp2 and Amacr were more strongly reduced, indicating genotype-dependent epithelial remodeling under oxidative stress. Spatial mapping with Giotto confirmed that friend key-genes were enriched in intact tubular segments, whereas foe key-genes localized to histologically abnormal regions with tubular dilation, disruption, or loss of recognizable tubular organization (Fig. 2C).

Because karyomegalic PTs are a characteristic lesion in Fe-NTA-treated kidneys [39,40], we quantified nuclear morphology using Xenium-derived nuclear polygons (Fig. 2B). Fe-NTA exposure was associated with nuclear enlargement, reduced compactness and circularity, and increased eccentricity. WT karyomegalic cells had larger nuclei than MUT cells under comparable treatment conditions (Fig. S5A). These data indicate that nuclear remodeling accompanies early Fe-NTA-induced epithelial injury and is modified by Brca1 haploinsufficiency.

Dynamics of diverse PT subtypes in early renal carcinogenesis

To resolve PT dynamics during early carcinogenesis, we reclustered PTs and identified 11 subtypes (Fig. 3A and B). Homeostatic PT clusters 1, 2, 5, 6, 10, and 12 predominated in control kidneys and expressed renal functional and mitochondrial genes. In contrast, clusters 0, 3, 4, 7, 8, and 9 emerged after Fe-NTA exposure and showed increased injury-, repair-, or foe key-gene expression with reduced homeostatic programs. Genotype-specific differences became evident after Fe-NTA exposure: WT kidneys showed transient loss of normal tubular states at 1 week with partial recovery by 3 weeks, whereas MUT kidneys showed expansion of injured clusters, particularly clusters 4 and 7.

The PT subtypes represented distinct biological states (Fig. 3B). Oxidative Stress–Adaptive PTs (cluster 0; OA_PT) expressed antioxidant and metabolic genes such as Cubn, Slc40a1, Lrp2, Slc27a2, and Gpx4, together with moderate Myc and Met expression. DNA Damage–Response PTs (cluster 3; DR_PT) expressed repair and regenerative markers including Mki67, Rad51, Bard1, and Bap1. Maladaptive Repair PTs (clusters 4 and 7; MR_PT) expressed Lcn2, Slc22a17, and other foe key-genes, with cluster 4 retaining more friend key-gene expression than cluster 7. Transient Regenerative PTs (cluster 8; TR_PT) expressed proliferation and DNA repair markers, whereas Ferroptotic PTs (cluster 9; Fer_PT) were often isolated necrotic or pyknotic cells within otherwise preserved tubules, as confirmed by Xenium Explorer alignment with H&E images (Fig. S3A).

Spatially, DR_PT localized mainly near the cortex–medulla boundary in WT kidneys at 1 week but was less evident in MUT kidneys (Fig. 3C). MR_PT populations frequently co-localized within MUT tubules, whereas WT tubules showed greater admixture of homeostatic, oxidative stress–adaptive, and transient regenerative states. Stromal reclustering further identified 13 stromal subtypes with genotype- and treatment-dependent composition (Fig. 3D). After 3 weeks of Fe-NTA, MUT kidneys showed expansion of stromal states enriched for regenerative, injury-response, iron-handling, matrix-remodeling, and macrophage-recruiting signatures, including ST_1, ST_3, ST_5/8, ST_6, and ST_9.

To summarize PT status spatially, we generated pathology-informed module scores (Fig. S3B–D). Spatial score maps showed that normal tubular scores declined after 1 week of Fe-NTA exposure and partially recovered in WT kidneys by 3 weeks, whereas MUT kidneys showed more persistent dysfunction (Fig. 3E). Interestingly, scores representing renal injury and cell death (acute/subacute injured score; dying tubules score) displayed inverse spatial patterns to normal tubules score at 1-week Fe-NTA treatment, with reduced acute injured score at 3-week Fe-NTA treatment, marking the progression of homeostatic PT clusters toward subacute injury and the initiation of cellular adaptations linked to ferroptosis resistance and potential oncogenic transformation.

In Fe-NTA 3-week samples, morphology-associated coefficients revealed coordinated relationships between nuclear shape and PT transcriptional state (Fig. 4B). Oncogenic and iron-associated genes, including Lcn2, Anxa2, Met, Pcbp2, Myc, and Sfxn3, were positively associated with eccentricity and convexity, whereas circular and compact nuclei were associated with preserved PT markers such as Slc27a2, Lrp2, Pink1, and Tfrc. Although the overall direction of associations was similar between WT and MUT kidneys, MUT showed stronger coefficients, indicating amplified coupling between nuclear morphology and stress-associated transcription under Brca1 haploinsufficiency.

We then converted morphology–gene β profiles into gene–gene networks using cosine similarity. Under the pre-specified criteria of FDR ≤0.05, β ≥ 0.10, and cosine ≥0.70, robust modules were absent in control kidneys and WT Fe-NTA 3-week kidneys, whereas WT Fe-NTA 1-week kidneys showed compact Slc27a2–Lrp2–Pink1 and Mki67–Anxa2 modules (Fig. 4C). In contrast, MUT kidneys retained modules at both 1 and 3 weeks, including a recurrent Slc27a2–Lrp2 core and an Lcn2–Anxa2 injury-associated core, suggesting genotype-dependent persistence of morphology-linked transcriptional programs.

To identify transcriptional correlates of nuclear chromatin organization during early carcinogenic nuclear transformation, we derived three nuclear morphometric parameters: hematoxylin optical density (HOD), nuclear substructure density (NSD) [41], and peripheral intensity index (PII) [42], from H&E images of WT and MUT Fe-NTA 3w kidneys. HOD provided quantitative readout of bulk nuclear chromatin compaction. In WT, the genes most strongly associated with HOD (e.g., Gpx4, Fth1, Ncoa4, Pcbp1, Tfrc) were uniformly negative, indicating that more robust iron-homeostatic programs correspond to lower nuclear hematoxylin density, consistent with a more open chromatin state (Fig. 4D; Table S2). In MUT, PT identity genes (e.g., Amacr, Slc27a2, Lrp2) again showed strong negative associations, whereas top positive associations were observed for Sfxn3, Sting1, Mcub, S100a9, Anxa2, and Lcn2, suggesting that chromatin compaction is associated with mitochondrial alteration and stress responses in Brca1 deficient carcinogenesis. Despite that majority of significant genes were genotype-specific, effect direction was replicated across genotypes for Lrp2, Slc27a2, and Amacr (negative in both) and for Lgals1, Anxa2, Pcbp2, and Bard1 (positive in both). Fixed-effects meta-analysis was consistent with an overall pattern during oxidative stress-based carcinogenesis, where homeostatic genes downregulate with chromatin compaction, while carcinogenetic pathways across injury/iron-overload/fibrosis are activated, especially more pronounced in BRCA1 mutants.

NSD [41], which detected hematoxylin-dense intranuclear foci, showed no detectable gene-level association in either genotype (Fig. S4B), consistent with its proposed role as constitutive, transcriptionally silent chromatin compartment (e.g., chromocenters). PII captured the distribution of peripheral heterochromatin (lamina-associated domains (LADs)), providing a complementary readout of nuclear architectural organization. In WT, PT metabolic genes (e.g., Pink1, Slc27a2, Tfrc, Fth1, Gpx4, Ncoa4, Pcbp2) were positively associated with PII, indicating that they correspond to greater peripheral enrichment of condensed chromatin, consistent with intact LAD organization (Fig. 4E; Table S2). In MUT, Slc27a2, Amacr, and Lrp2 retained similar positive associations, while Mcub, Sting1, and Cdkn1/2a showed significant negative associations not observed in WT, implying a distinct transcriptional signature related to peripheral heterochromatin loss [43,44]. As with HOD, cross concordance was low overall, although Slc27a2, Gpx4, and Pink1 replicated a consistent positive direction in both genotypes. Meta-analysis identified positive pooled effects for PT/iron/mitochondrial metabolic genes (e.g., Lrp2, Slc27a2, Amacr), alongside negative effects including Cdkn1/2a, Mcub, Lcn2, Myc, and Lgals1 (Fig. 4E).

Together, these results indicate that HOD and PII both capture a shared transcriptional axis linking proximal tubule homeostasis to chromatin reorganization, with this signature more extensive in MUT. These findings also demonstrate that nuclear chromatin morphometry can be systematically linked to spatial transcriptomic profiles, identifying candidate chromatin-state markers of early renal carcinogenesis.

Compared with other PTs, enlarged nuclei showed consistent Cdkn1a upregulation and Pink1 downregulation, with heterogeneous induction of DNA damage repair and regenerative genes (Fig. 5A; Fig. S5C and D). Elongated cells showed impaired epithelial function with increased Sfxn3 and modest Rad51 and Mki67 induction (Fig. S5B). We next investigated the morphological and transcriptional features of re-clustered karyomegalic subpopulations. Among all clusters, K1 displayed the most pronounced nuclear enlargement with irregular, rough nuclear contours, and thus most closely aligned with the canonical pathological definition of karyomegaly (Fig. 5B, D). Transcriptional profiling further revealed strong induction of Cdkn1a, coordinated with elevated Ncoa4 and reduced Fth1, indicative of stress-associated cell-cycle arrest with an active ferritinophagy-mediated iron-mobilization program (Fig. 5C, S5E). K4 was enriched for markers of proximal tubule homeostasis and metabolic competence (e.g., Lrp2, Slc27a2, Amacr, Pink1), suggesting maintenance of epithelial transport and mitochondrial quality control in WT (Fig. 5B, C). And in morphological perspectives, K4 showed relatively more solid and compact nucleus. Strikingly, K2 and K5 co-expressed Met, Lcn2, and Myc, pointing to an adaptive stress program with oncogenic features, whereas significant nuclear morphology features included elongation yet smaller area than K1. We interpret these states as a potential early pro-tumorigenic niche in BRCA1-haploinsufficient kidneys that may represent an actionable window for intervention in carcinogenesis.

To determine whether karyomegaly represents not only an altered epithelial state but also a local epithelial microenvironment, we profiled PT neighbors located within 30 μm of either karyomegalic or non-karyomegalic PT cells. PTs adjacent to karyomegalic cells showed increased expression of iron-handling, oxidative stress–adaptive, and pro-survival genes, including Lcn2, Anxa2, Mki67, Sfxn3, Cdkn1a, and Myc, together with reduced Amacr, Aqp3, and Pink1 (Fig. 5E). These signatures partially mirrored those of karyomegalic cells themselves, indicating that surrounding epithelial cells acquire a stress-adapted, survival-biased state. This interpretation is supported by the induction of factors linked to ferroptotic vulnerability, including Lcn2, which can modulate labile iron availability and antioxidant defenses [45], and by reduced Pink1, suggesting impaired mitophagy [46].

Subtype-stratified analysis further showed that PTs surrounding K2 cells were enriched for Myc, Lcn2, Slc40a1, and Met, with reduced Amacr, Pink1, and Slc27a2, whereas PTs surrounding K4 cells maintained metabolic and mitochondrial homeostasis genes (Fig. 5E). Thus, karyomegalic states appear to act as focal organizers of local epithelial programs, with BRCA1-haploinsufficient karyomegaly defining a stress-adaptive, iron-handling, proto-oncogenic niche permissive for iron-driven carcinogenesis.

To characterize these changes, we performed Leiden-based microenvironmental (ME) clustering, using previously defined PT and stromal subclusters within a 30 μm radius. Twelve predominant MEs were retained for analysis (Fig. 6B; Fig. S6B). ME_2 and ME_5 showed marked differences between WT and MUT kidneys after 3 weeks of Fe-NTA exposure (Fig. 6C). Spatial projection revealed that ME_2 localized mainly to the cortex–medulla boundary, whereas ME_5 predominated in the upper cortex (Fig. 6D). ME_2 was enriched in WT_Fe3w and contained stromal programs associated with cell cycling, DNA repair, and moderate injury responses. In contrast, ME_5 predominated in MUT_Fe3w and showed stromal signatures related to iron storage, ferroptosis protection, matrix remodeling, and angiogenesis, including Fth1, Ftl1, Pink1, Ncoa4, Gpx4, Slc40a1, Vegfa, and Col3a1 (Figs. 3D and 6B; Fig. S6C).

In ME_2, mitochondrial and PT metabolic genes correlated strongly with Gpx4, whereas in ME_5 they correlated more strongly with Mki67 and Ncoa4. To assess mitochondrial function, we established immortalized primary rat embryonic fibroblasts (REFs) from male WT and Brca1-MUT embryos (Fig. 8C). After Fe-NTA-induced DNA double-strand breaks, MUT REFs showed fragmented mitochondrial networks, whereas WT cells retained more fused or branched mitochondria (Fig. 8D). Seahorse assays showed reduced basal and maximal oxygen-consumption rates, reduced proton leak, and increased coupling efficiency in MUT REFs compared with WT (Fig. 8E). These findings indicate that BRCA1 haploinsufficiency alters mitochondrial architecture, iron handling, and respiratory function during Fe-NTA-induced stress.

Clinical validation of karyomegalic transcriptional modules identifies prognostic risk states and morphology-associated programs

To assess clinical relevance, we analyzed TCGA RNA-seq cohorts, designating TCGA-KIRC as the primary validation cohort. Three modules were derived from karyomegalic clusters in the rat model: KaryoCore (CDKN1A, MET, LCN2, SLC11A2), representing shared karyomegalic identity; Karyo2 (COL3A1, MYC, SFXN3, SLC22A17, VEGFA, BRCA2, LGALS1, CGAS, MCUB), representing the BRCA1-associated oncogenic K2 state; and Karyo4 (AMACR, PINK1, UMOD, LRP2, SLC27A2), representing a homeostatic karyomegalic state. A literature-curated FerroResistance module (GPX4, SLC7A11, AIFM2, NFE2L2, HMOX1, NQO1, FTL, GSS) [[49], [50], [51]] was also evaluated (Fig. S7A).

In TCGA-KIRC tumor-adjacent solid-tissue-normal samples (STN; n = 72), higher KaryoCore and Karyo2 activity was associated with shorter progression-free interval (PFI) and overall survival (OS), whereas Karyo4 and FerroResistance were not significant in this compartment (Fig. 9A). In TCGA-KIRC primary tumors (n = 537), Karyo4 was associated with favorable OS and PFI, whereas Karyo2 was associated with adverse outcomes for both endpoints. FerroResistance showed a concordant adverse association with PFI, while KaryoCore was not prognostic in tumors. These associations were not reproduced in other carcinoma STN cohorts examined (COAD, LUAD, LUSC), although LIHC showed a non-significant adverse trend for Karyo2 (Fig. S7B). Because TCGA-BRCA STN transcriptomic signals are limited by sparse ductal epithelium, we analyzed diagnostic whole-slide images from TCGA-BRCA cases with known germline BRCA1 status [34]. ROIs containing well-formed ductal structures adjacent to ductal carcinoma were manually annotated, followed by nuclear segmentation and morphometric analysis in BRCA1 germline mutation carriers and matched non-carrier controls. At the nucleus level, BRCA1-mutant carriers showed significant differences in eccentricity, solidity, and extent compared with controls (Fig. 9B). Although patient-level significance was not reached, effect sizes suggested less compact and more irregular nuclear contours in BRCA1-mutant ductal epithelium.

Finally, we correlated nuclear features with karyomegalic module activities by genotype. In BRCA1-mutant carriers, Karyo2 activity positively correlated with nuclear eccentricity (Spearman ρ = 0.82, p = 0.034), whereas KaryoCore activity correlated with nuclear solidity (ρ = 0.86, p = 0.024) (Fig. 9C). These relationships were absent in non-carrier cases (Fig. S7C). Nuclear area analyses showed divergent but non-significant trends across modules (Fig. S7D). Although limited by sample size, these findings support the concept that BRCA1-associated karyomegalic transcriptional programs are linked to nuclear remodeling in human epithelial tissue.

Discussion

In the present study, we combined single-cell spatial transcriptomics with digital nuclear morphometrics and identified a ferroptosis-resistant karyomegalic epithelial population as the earliest detectable preneoplastic alteration during exposome-related [18], iron-driven carcinogenesis. This stress-adaptive state was markedly expanded in Brca1 haploinsufficient rats, linking hereditary cancer susceptibility to impaired ferroptotic elimination under iron-catalyzed oxidative stress during tumor initiation. (Fig. 7).

Spatial transcriptomics has transformed tissue biology by enabling molecular profiling while preserving histological architecture. Here, we further integrated spatial transcriptomic data with quantitative nuclear morphology derived from H&E sections. In diagnostic pathology, nuclear atypia remains one of the most fundamental indicators of cellular state, yet its quantitative integration with molecular programs has remained limited. By establishing digital metrics for nuclear size, contour smoothness, chromatin density, and nuclear substructure, we generated a framework that translates classical histopathological features into spatially resolved functional genomic states. This approach revealed that nuclear enlargement and hyperchromasia closely track ferroptosis-resistant and stress-adaptive transcriptional programs during early carcinogenesis.

Our analysis identified a karyomegalic proximal tubular population enriched for oncogenic, iron-handling, and ferroptosis-resistance pathways. Importantly, neighboring non-karyomegalic epithelial cells displayed highly similar transcriptional profiles, suggesting that karyomegaly marks a broader pre-neoplastic field rather than an isolated cellular phenotype. Although karyomegalic cells themselves may represent a transient stress-adaptive state, they function as a histological beacon for the earliest detectable stages of tumor initiation under oxidative stress.

A central feature of this state was mitochondrial dysfunction. Electron microscopy previously demonstrated abundant small, interconnected mitochondria in Brca1 mutant kidneys after Fe-NTA exposure [15], consistent with stress-induced mitochondrial remodeling. Concordantly, mitochondrial stress assays demonstrated impaired respiration and reduced metabolic flexibility, likely driven by a broad failure of mitochondrial quality control. Because mitochondria are major execution sites of ferroptosis [52], these alterations likely promote ferroptosis resistance and aberrant epithelial survival under iron-catalyzed oxidative stress. Reduced 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (HNE) production detected by HNEJ-1 staining may similarly reflect depletion of mitochondrial substrates for lipid peroxidation [15,53]. Further investigation into biogenesis and maintenance of the diminished functional mitochondrial pool will likely clarify how exposome-related iron stress establishes and sustains the ferroptosis-resistant niches. Moreover, Brca1 haploinsufficiency perturbs iron homeostasis even under physiological conditions [15], potentially increasing susceptibility to stress-adaptive transformation through heightened iron demand during DNA synthesis [54].

Importantly, integration with human cancer datasets suggested that this ferroptosis-resistant karyomegalic state is conserved across multiple epithelial cancers beyond the experimental rat model (Fig. 9). These findings support a broader model in which oxidative stress and hereditary susceptibility cooperate to generate stress-adaptive epithelial states that evade ferroptotic elimination during early tumorigenesis.

In conclusion, this study establishes a spatial and morphological framework for dissecting early tumor initiation by integrating single-cell spatial transcriptomics with digital pathology. We identify a ferroptosis-resistant karyomegalic epithelial state associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and expanded under BRCA1 haploinsufficiency, linking nuclear morphology to stress-adaptive pre-neoplastic programs across experimental and human tissue contexts. These findings provide a framework for understanding how exposome-driven oxidative stress intersects with hereditary cancer susceptibility during the earliest stages of tumor initiation. Limitations include the use of a preclinical male rat model, a restricted Xenium gene panel, and representative rather than biologically replicated specimens for each Xenium condition. Accordingly, single-cell statistical significance should be interpreted as supporting spatial cell-state associations rather than animal-level biological replication, with biological relevance strengthened by independent mitochondrial analyses, prior Fe-NTA observations, and human TCGA validation. Future studies should determine whether targeting mitochondrial adaptation or ferroptosis resistance can prevent tumor initiation in genetically susceptible tissues exposed to carcinogenic oxidative stress.
