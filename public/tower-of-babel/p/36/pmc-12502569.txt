Spatial distribution of informal sexual relationships among reproductive age women in Ethiopia: spatial and multilevel analysis from Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey
Authors: Dagne Deresa Dinagde, Shambel Negese marami, Gizu Tola Feyisa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, more than 1 million new sexually transmitted diseases (STIs) are acquired every day, posing a serious threat to world health as a result of informal sexual activities such as multi-partner sexual practices and premarital sexual initiation. Exposure to informal settings increases the risk of unprotected sexual practices, sexually transmitted infections such as HIV/AIDS, unwanted pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and can adversely affect the social and economic status of adults. Hence, identifying geographical areas at risk for informal sexual relationships in Ethiopia is very important.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional survey using a two-stage stratified sample technique was carried out between January 18 and June 27, 2016. A total of 15,683 reproductive age women participated in the analysis, which used information from the 2016 EDHS. The distribution of informal sexual relationships was assessed using the spatial autocorrelation statistic (Global Moran's I) to see if it was randomly distributed, clustered, or dispersed. The correlation between informal sexual relationships and explanatory variables was found using a multilevel logistic regression model, and statistical significance was established at p < 0.05 and 95% CI.ResultsOverall, 51.14% of reproductive age women had informal sexual relationships. In certain areas of Ethiopia, regions like Amhara (14.1%) and Tigray (11.4%) experienced high levels of informal sexual relationships, while Dire Dawa (6.3%), Harari (5.3%), and Addis Ababa (7.7%) were considered lower-risk areas. The spatial distribution of these interactions showed clustering patterns. The highest hotspot areas of informal sexual relationships were observed in central part of Ethiopia. The being under 25 (AOR = 4.02, 95CI: 3.90–5.26), being agrarian (AOR = 1.17, 95CI: 1.04–1.32) and pastoralist region (AOR = 1.34, 1.20–1.52), living in rural areas (AOR = 1.24, 95 CI: 1.09–1.41), not using internet (AOR = 1.14, 95 CI: 1.01–1.37), unemployment (AOR = 0.88, 0.75, 90) and poor household (AOR = 1.22, 1.11, 1.38) were statistically significant.ConclusionAccording to this study, half of reproductive age women fall into informal sexual relationships in Ethiopia. As a result, it is crucial to focus on the areas that are most affected.Keywords: Informal sexual relationship, Premarital sexual practice, Multiple sexual partners

Background

The majority of sub-Saharan Africans, 70% are under 30 years old, establishing Africa as the continent with the youngest population in the world. However, this youthful population can only contribute to the growth of the continent if the new generations are given the tools they need to reach their full potential. It is particularly crucial that they maintain their health by avoiding communicable diseases of the hot age like STIs, HIV and hepatitis is important [1]. According to this estimate, in the coming three decades, there will likely be the greatest increase in the proportion of young people [2]. This significant group of the population should not be subjected to sexually dangerous behaviors such as early sexual initiation, numerous partner relationships, early marriage, unprotected sex, and premarital practices [3, 4].

According to various scholars’ presentations, sexual behaviors are described and compared in terms of social norms, and societies’ conception of sexuality is unique to their culture [5–7]. Thus, informal sexual relationships included sexual activities like premarital sexual practices, multiple sexual activities, and early initiation of sexual activity before the age of 18 [7–9]. Informal sexual relationships (ISR), a dangerous sexual habit, are becoming more common in more traditional societies in the modern world, where social networks and international contacts are essential elements of any social framework [7].

According to the WHO, every day more than 1 million STIs are contracted; chlamydia (129 million cases), gonorrhea (82 million cases), syphilis (7.1 million cases), and trichomoniasis (156 million cases) will account for 374 million new infections in 2020. In 2016, it was estimated that more than 490 million people had genital herpes and that 300 million women had HPV infections, which are the main causes of cervical cancer and anal cancer in males who have sex with men. Globally, 296 million people are thought to have chronic hepatitis B [10]. Analysis of data from several specific African nations revealed that although sub-Saharan Africa is home to only about 12% of the world's population, it is responsible for more than 70% of the burden of HIV infections worldwide [11, 12]. Every month, the risk of engaging in risky sexual behavior increases by 3% in Ethiopia [13].

According to the latest WHO data, more than 1 million new sexually transmitted diseases (STIs) are acquired every day, posing a serious threat to world health as a result of informal sexual activities such as multi-partner sexual practices and premarital sexual initiation [14]. For both women and the feature generation, having an informal sexual relationship is bad for their health, society, and economy. It was a risk factor for unprotected sexual practices, sexually transmitted diseases including HIV/AIDS, unwanted pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and even harm adults through social and economic status [6, 15, 16].

To combat this, The WHO has established various frameworks and set global targets and standards for the prevention, testing, and treatment of STIs. They promote the assessment of the economic impact of STIs and the enhancement of surveillance systems. Additionally, the WHO monitors antibiotic resistance in gonorrhea and spearheads the global research agenda on sexually transmitted infections, focusing on the development of diagnostic tests, vaccines, and new treatments for gonorrhea and syphilis [10, 17].

Prior research conducted in Ethiopia on a small and large scale found that Pre-marital sex, multiple partner sexual practice, and early sexual beginning have all been studied individually among reproductive age in Ethiopia. Age, residence, educational status, parent- youth discussion, having smart phone, using addictive substances, being sexually active, not attending school, living in developing region, early-time first-sex, religion, closer relationship with parents and religion are determinant factors identified by scholars [7–9, 18].

To the best of our knowledge, In Ethiopia, there has been no study conducted on informal sexual relationships among reproductive ages, and knowledge of them is very important to designing policies and strategies to fight against widely spreading sexually transmitted infections. This secondary data analysis is crucial for assessing long-term outcomes and effects that may not have been thoroughly addressed in previous studies, despite relying on older data. The interpretation of this study is both original and informative, providing a fresh perspective based on new insights and will be base for new data studies. Thus, this study was designed to determine the geographical variation, and prevalence of informal sexual relationship among reproductive age in Ethiopia using the 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS).

Methods

Study setting and design

Ethiopia is the second-most populated country in Africa; it is the home of more than 120 million citizens, who live in urban and rural areas of the country [19]. The country is located in the Horn of Africa, sharing a border with Eritrea to the north, Djibouti and Somalia to the east, Sudan and South Sudan to the west, and Kenya to the south. Administratively, Ethiopia has two administrative cities and nine geographical areas [20].

Data sources and study population

The source of the dataset used for this study was the 2016 EDHS. The survey was conducted by the Ethiopian Public Health Institute at the request of the Federal Ministry of Health, and it was the fourth Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) conducted in Ethiopia. The survey, according to the Ethiopian EDHS report, was carried out between January 18 and June 27, 2016. The DHS website was used to acquire the real data for this investigation (https://www.dhsprogram.com/), and Ethiopian shape files were downloaded from the open Africa website. All reproductive age females who were either permanent residents of the selected households or visitors who stayed in the household the night before the survey were the source population.

Sampling producers

Ethiopia's 2007 Population and Housing Census (PHC) served as the basis for the sampling frame for the 2016 EDHS. A complete list of all 84,915 enumeration areas (EAs) was seen in the census frame. An EA is an area that typically has 181 households. The sampling frame includes details regarding the EA location, residence (urban or rural), and projected number of residential households. Two stages of stratification and selection were done for the 2016 EDHS sample. 21 strata of sampling were produced after dividing each region into urban and rural areas. To create 21 sampling strata, the first stage of stratification was undertaken by region, followed by stratification of each region into urban and rural areas. According to the size of the EA in each sampling stratum, 202 urban and 443 rural enumeration areas (EAs) totaling 645 were chosen. A predetermined number of households (28 per cluster) were systematically chosen from the households listed in the second step [21].

Study variables and measurements

Dependent variable

The outcome variable, informal sexual relationship (ISR), was composed of three variables (i.e., premarital sexual practices with a marital partner or non-marital partners, multiple sexual partners, and early initiation of sexual activity before the age of 18) [7, 8, 22]. The independent variables were either factors at the individual level, including age, religion, chat chewing, drinking alcohol, wealth index, educational status, having ever heard of HIV/AIDS, residence, and region, age of first sex, smartphone, and media exposure, or community-level factors like religion, residence, community-level media exposure like exposure to TV or radio, and community-level education.

Media exposure

Women's knowledge of the risks associated with informal sexual relationships is directly influenced by media exposure. Because of this, the women had media exposure if they had access to either radio or television, or both. On the other hand, if neither was available to them, they had no access to media [23].

Reproductive age

In this study, the term"reproductive age"refers to women under the age of 50, specifically those in their reproductive years [24].

Community level education

Individual respondents'educational levels were recoded to ‘yes’ if they had any education, as opposed to"No"if they had never attended school. The proportion of participants in each cluster that was < 50% was recoded as having a'low'level of community education for women, and those that were ≥ 50% were recoded as having a'high'level of community education for women [25].

Community level poverty

The wealth indices of specific respondents were recoded, such that the terms"poor"and"middle"and"rich"were changed to"poorer"and “rich"respectively. Each cluster's percentage of participants who were < 50% were recoded as having'poor'levels of community poverty for women, and those who were ≥ 50% were recoded as having'rich'levels of community poverty for women [26].

Data management and processing

After the pre-test was completed with the necessary modifications, the data was collected. The complicated sampling design was compensated for using the SVY command. After that, sample weighting was carried out to make up for the strata's different selection probabilities. Data cleaning was conducted by running frequency for consistency with the EDHS-2016 descriptive report. Recoding, variable generation, labeling, and analysis were done using STATA/MP version 17. In order to describe the study participants'socio-demographic characteristics, descriptive statistics were used.

Finally, in bivariate logistic regression, those variables with a p value less than 0.2 at model I and model II were selected to the final model and this analysis was done in four models. The first model (without an explanatory variable or null model), the second model (only individual-level variable), the third (only a community-level variable), and the last model (both community-level and individual-level variables).

The probability of occurrence of outcome (i.e. informal sexual relationship) among reproductive age women and association between explanatory factors and outcome were measured by AOR with respective 95% confidence level. Variables at model-III with a p-value less than 0.05 were deemed to be significantly related. The variations were measured by using ICC, Median Odds Ratio (MOR) and Proportional Change in Variance (PCV). ICC shows the variation in informal sexual relationship among young age women due to community characteristics. It can be calculated as: ICC = (variance of interest)/(total variance) = (variance of interest)/(variance of interest + unwanted variance) [28]. MOR is the median value of the odds ratio between the area at highest risk and the area the lowest risk when randomly picking out two areas and it was calculated as: MOR = exp. (√(2 × δ2 + 0.6745)) ≈ exp.(0.95δ). PCV measures the total variation attributed to individual-level variables and community-level variables in the final model (model-III) [8]. Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was checked by using standard error at cutoff point ± 3 and three variables showed multicollinearity effect (i.e., respondent’s level of education, smoking and drinking alcohol) and omitted from multivariate logistic regression analysis.

Spatial data analysis

In this study, spatial autocorrelation and hot spot analysis were performed using Arc Map V.10.8 software. To determine if informal sexual relationships were randomly distributed, clustered, or scattered across space, the global spatial autocorrelation statistic (Global Moran's I) was used. The distribution of informal sexual relationships is dispersed, clustered, and random, respectively, according to Moran's I value, which is near to −1, + 1, and 0. To determine whether the informal sexual relationship is a hot or cold spot, a hot spot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi*) was conducted. Z-scores and p values were used to calculate the hot and cold spot values for spatial clusters. In addition, the spatial interpolation analysis, which makes use of Kriging ordinary interpolation, was utilized to predict the prevalence of informal sexual relationships for values that were not sampled or measured from sampled data.

Socio-demographic characteristics

In total, 15,683 reproductive age women were included in the study, and 9247 (58.96%) of them were between the ages of 15 and 29. In this study, 10,335 participants (66%) lived in rural areas, and nearly half of the women (45%) had no formal education. In terms of wealth, 23.45% of the women belonged to the richest quintile, and 6413 (41%) were orthodox religious believers. More than half of participants, 56%, had no exposure to the media, 4937 respondents, 31.5%, had been living at low community educational levels; more than half, 54%, had low community poverty levels; and just 1 in 4 participants, 24%, live in metropolitan areas (Table 1).

Discussion

In this study we discovered that ISR in Ethiopia are not random and have spatial variance. The main concentrations were situated in southern Amhara area, Addis Ababa, and central Oromia. It was discovered that 51% of Ethiopian reproductive age women had at least one form of informal sexual relationship (ISR). They were evaluated to see if they had any kind of ISR. Accordingly, 3.2% of participants reported having sex before marriage (cohabitation), about 3% of respondents had a history of having an additional sexual partner within the previous year, and roughly half of respondents had a history of sexual practice before the age of 18. This evidence was supported by [7–9].

The final spatial analysis model showed that there were considerable regional differences in the spatial distribution of reproductive age women's informal sexual relationships. Age, internet use, occupation, and individual wealth index were thus significantly associated with ISR at the individual level, whereas residence and region of the women were significantly associated at the community level with ISR among reproductive age women in Ethiopia.

Those under 25 year’s old women had a 4 times and nearly 3 times higher likelihood of having an informal sexual relationship when compared to those older women. This finding was supported with multilevel [29] and primary studies [30] conducted in Ethiopia and in Ghana [31]. This was attributed to the maturational phases that adolescents experience as they approach maturity, as well as the fact that this is a time when females are known to experiment and explore. Additionally, young women attract both young and old men because they effortlessly impart a sense of youth, make them feel desired, and are unconcerned with commitment.

Women who live in agrarian or pastoral areas are more likely to have ISR than women who live in metropolitan areas. The population's rural and pastoral lifestyle puts them at risk for poor economic growth, a lack of infrastructure, network inaccessibility, a lack of reproductive education, and low media exposure, all of which contribute to the spread of poverty and ignorance. Two Chinese review studies supported this conclusion [32, 33].

The internet is crucial for facilitating communication, teamwork, and information sharing among a huge number of individuals. However, accessing the internet poses a risk for the development of bad behavior due to the abundance of sexually explicit content available online [34]. In Ethiopia, those young women who had not been using the internet were nearly two times more likely to expose themselves to ISR. This finding was supported by studies done in Ethiopia [29], sub-Saharan Africa [35], Nigeria [36] and Iran [7]. This may be due to the ease with which sensitive topics and information pertaining to sex may be shared online, facilitating the pursuit of sexual satisfaction.

We also found that household poor wealth index was significantly related with ISR. The women living in poor households had a 22% likelihood of having ISR as compared to their counterparts. This finding was inconsistent to study conducted in Ethiopia [31]. However study conducted among Haitian women supported this finding [37]. Women who are economically vulnerable may turn to sex trade as a means of income, raising their risk of contracting an STD or HIV. Due to the fact that women who trade sex frequently have a lot of partners and might not be able to negotiate the usage of condoms.

Regarding occupation, the likelihood of having ISR among unemployed women is 88% less likely when compared to that of employed women. Being jobless or a homeworker will cause low sexual desire due to overloaded homework that removes the desire of think about sexual play. This evidence was supported by different studies [9, 33, 38].

Implication of the study

The study can enhance the understanding of the safety associated with protected sexual relationships. Policymakers can leverage this information to build public confidence in the prevention of sexually transmitted diseases and address concerns related to adverse events. Overall, the findings will benefit governments, healthcare professionals, stakeholders, researchers, policymakers, healthcare implementers, and the general public.

Conclusion

This study showed that among reproductive age Ethiopian women, there was a regional clustering or variance in ISR. The hot spot clusters were found in Addis Ababa, central Oromia, and the southern Amhara areas. Young age, poor household, Internet use, living in agrarian or pastoralists (being from other region rather than, Tigray, Oromia Amhara Addis Ababa, Harar and Diredawa). As a result, paying close attention to the study's components and high-risk areas could reduce ISR.
