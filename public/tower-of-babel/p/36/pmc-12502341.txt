Hispidulin suppresses osteosarcoma by directly targeting FABP4 to disrupt lipid metabolism and inhibit the PI3K/AKT pathway
Authors: Xuhui Yuan, Shaolin Yu, Zhengxing Zeng, Liren Yi, Bo Yu, Lingxiao Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteosarcoma (OS), a prevalent primary bone malignancy, has a dismal prognosis in metastatic cases (5-year survival < 30%), highlighting the need for novel therapies. Hispidulin (HIS), a natural flavonoid, shows anticancer potential, but its precise mechanism and direct target in OS are uncharacterized.PurposeThis study aimed to delineate HIS’s anti-neoplastic mechanisms in OS, focusing on its impact on lipid metabolism, associated signaling, and its direct molecular target.MethodsIn vitro anti-tumor effects of HIS were assessed (CCK-8, colony formation, Transwell, flow cytometry). RNA sequencing and molecular docking identified regulatory pathways and targets. Western blotting, lipid metabolism assays, and rescue experiments explored mechanisms. In vivo efficacy was evaluated using xenografts.ResultsHIS potently inhibited OS cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion, with minimal toxicity to normal osteoblasts. It induced G2/M arrest and apoptosis. HIS directly targeted Fatty Acid Binding Protein 4 (FABP4), modulating lipid metabolism and subsequently inhibiting the PI3K/AKT pathway. This reduced intracellular free fatty acids and fatty acid synthase activity. FABP4 overexpression abrogated HIS’s anti-tumor effects. In vivo, HIS impeded tumor growth and reduced Ki67 and FABP4 expression.ConclusionHispidulin exerts robust anti-tumor activity in OS by directly targeting FABP4, thereby disrupting lipid metabolism and suppressing the oncogenic PI3K/AKT cascade. HIS is a highly encouraging therapeutic candidate for OS.Keywords: Osteosarcoma, Hispidulin, Lipid metabolism, PI3K/AKT pathway

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS), as the most common primary malignant bone tumor, poses a serious threat to the lives of children and adolescents due to its highly invasive nature [1, 2]. Although the introduction of multimodal therapeutic strategies has, to some extent, improved the prognosis for patients with localized disease, the high propensity of this tumor for early pulmonary metastasis remains a core challenge leading to treatment failure and high mortality [3, 4]. For patients who present with or subsequently develop metastatic disease, the clinical outlook is grim, with the five-year overall survival rate lingering below 30% without significant breakthroughs [5]. This stark clinical reality underscores the urgent need to explore the mechanisms of OS metastasis and to develop novel therapeutic strategies with innovative targets [6–9].

To sustain their uncontrolled proliferation, potent invasive and migratory capabilities, and resistance to apoptosis, tumor cells must undergo profound metabolic reprogramming, a process now widely recognized as one of the hallmarks of cancer [10, 11]. In osteosarcoma, this metabolic adaptation is particularly pronounced. Among these changes, the dysregulation of lipid metabolism has emerged as a central hub, providing critical dual support for its malignant biological behaviors [12]. On one hand, through accelerated de novo fatty acid synthesis (DNFS), it supplies an abundance of structural precursors for the synthesis of cellular membranes and signaling molecules (e.g., phospholipids) required for rapid cell division [13]. On the other hand, it fuels high-energy cellular activities by providing ample ATP through enhanced fatty acid uptake and subsequent β-oxidation (FAO) [14]. This addiction-like reliance of osteosarcoma cells on lipids necessitates the upregulation of key molecules within lipid metabolic pathways. For instance, proteins critical for fatty acid uptake and intracellular trafficking, most notably Fatty Acid Binding Protein 4 (FABP4), as well as rate-limiting enzymes in the synthesis pathway like Fatty Acid Synthase (FASN), are frequently overexpressed in osteosarcoma tissues, and their high expression often correlates with a poor prognosis [15, 16].

The role of Fatty Acid Binding Protein 4 (FABP4), a key intracellular lipid chaperone, is gaining increasing attention in oncology. Aberrant overexpression of FABP4 is often correlated with aggressive tumor behavior and poor prognosis across various cancer types. For instance, in ovarian cancer, FABP4 has been shown to facilitate the uptake of fatty acids from surrounding adipocytes, thereby fueling metastasis and chemoresistance. Similarly, in breast and prostate cancer, FABP4 has been reported to support cancer cell proliferation and invasion. These studies collectively highlight FABP4 as a critical metabolic node and a potential therapeutic target in several solid tumors. However, despite its emerging roles in other malignancies, the specific function and molecular mechanisms of FABP4 in osteosarcoma, a unique bone-derived malignancy, remain largely unexplored, representing a key knowledge gap that this study aims to address. While this reliance on specific metabolic pathways confers a survival advantage, it also exposes an inherent vulnerability, creating a unique therapeutic window for targeted intervention.

Crucially, this metabolic rewiring is not an isolated event but is intricately interwoven with the constitutive activation of intracellular oncogenic signaling pathways. In the majority of osteosarcoma cases, the phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) signaling pathway is found to be aberrantly activated [17]. Serving as a central hub that governs cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism, the activated PI3K/Akt pathway can directly promote lipid synthesis, for instance, by activating the transcription factor SREBP-1c to upregulate the expression of genes like FASN [18, 19]. The relationship between lipid metabolism molecules like FABP4 and oncogenic signaling is complex and highly context-dependent. In many cancers, FABP4 appears to sustain the activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway, creating a self-reinforcing “vicious cycle” that drives tumor progression. However, it is noteworthy that FABP4 can also play a tumor-suppressive role in other contexts, for instance by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt pathway in endometrial cancer [20]. Therefore, identifying therapeutic agents that can sever the malignant link between this oncogenic signaling and metabolic reprogramming has become a highly attractive therapeutic strategy for osteosarcoma.

Natural products continue to be an invaluable reservoir for the discovery of novel therapeutic agents with unique mechanisms of action [21–23]. Hispidulin (HIS), a naturally occurring flavonoid, has attracted significant interest due to its well-documented pleiotropic anticancer properties [24]. Its bioactivity is frequently attributed to the modulation of central oncogenic signaling nodes; for instance, HIS has been reported to suppress the PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK pathways in diverse cancer models, thereby inhibiting proliferation and inducing apoptosis [25, 26]. Furthermore, nascent but intriguing evidence suggests that HIS may also function as a metabolic modulator. A notable study in glioblastoma demonstrated that HIS could inhibit de novo lipogenesis, an effect linked to the activation of the energy-sensing kinase AMPK [27, 28]. Despite these important insights, the existing literature presents a fragmented picture. The observations of HIS-mediated effects on signaling pathways and metabolic processes have largely been made in parallel, and a causative mechanistic bridge connecting these phenomena, particularly within the context of OS, has not been established. Critically, the identity of a direct molecular target for HIS that could serve as the upstream initiator of these downstream cellular events remains a significant enigma. This critical disconnect in our understanding raises two fundamental questions: first, can the therapeutic potential of HIS be effectively harnessed against the aggressive biology of osteosarcoma? Second, what is the precise molecular cascade through which HIS exerts its effects—does it function by directly targeting the lipid metabolic machinery, and if so, what is its primary molecular interactor?

This study was designed to systematically address the aforementioned questions and to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of HIS against osteosarcoma. We comprehensively evaluated the effects of HIS on the malignant phenotypes and tumor metabolism of OS cells through a series of in vitro experiments. Building on this, we employed high-throughput RNA sequencing to screen for key regulated pathways and potential target genes in an unbiased manner. Finally, the in vivo antitumor efficacy and safety of HIS were validated using a xenograft tumor model.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and reagent handling

The research utilized three cell lines obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China): two human osteosarcoma (OS) lines, MG63 and 143B, and one normal human osteoblast line, hFOB 1.19. We maintained these cells in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), which was enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China). All cultures were kept in a standard humidified incubator at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere, and experiments were consistently performed on cells between passages 10 and 20. For treatments, we prepared 10 mM stock solutions of Hispidulin (HIS) and the PI3K activator Recilisib (both from MedChemExpress, MCE, Shanghai, China) by dissolving them in DMSO.

Quantifying cell viability

To determine how HIS affects the viability of OS cells, we employed the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Our protocol involved seeding 2 × 104 cells into each well of a 96-well plate. After an overnight incubation that allowed for cellular attachment, we exposed the cells to a gradient of HIS concentrations (from 0 to 60 µM) for periods of 24, 48, or 72 h. Upon completion of the treatment interval, we added the CCK-8 reagent to each well. The metabolic activity of viable cells converts the kit’s reagent into a colored formazan product over a 1–2 h incubation at 37 °C. A microplate reader then quantified the resulting color intensity by measuring absorbance at 450 nm, providing a direct measure of cell viability.

Assessing long-term proliferative capacity

The ability of single cells to form colonies, a measure of long-term proliferative and survival capacity, was assessed following HIS treatment. We initiated this assay by plating a low number of cells (500 per well) in 6-well plates. For two weeks, these cells grew in medium containing various HIS concentrations (0–30 µM), with the medium being refreshed every three days to maintain consistent compound exposure. At the study’s conclusion, we fixed the resulting colonies with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained them with 0.1% crystal violet to make them visible, and then manually enumerated any cluster containing more than 50 cells.

Probing cellular migration and invasion

We utilized Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size; Corning, NY, USA) to investigate the impact of HIS on OS cell motility. The experimental design began with a 24-hour serum starvation of the cells. We then placed a suspension of 5 × 104 cells in serum-free medium into the upper chamber of the Transwell insert. To induce cell movement, the lower chamber was filled with DMEM containing 10% FBS, serving as a chemoattractant. To specifically assess invasion, we modified this setup by pre-coating the insert’s membrane with Matrigel (BD Biosciences), which mimics the extracellular matrix. After a 24-hour incubation period, we removed any non-motile cells from the upper surface. The cells that had successfully traversed the membrane were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted across five random microscopic fields.

Analyzing cell cycle and apoptosis

To understand the mechanisms behind HIS-induced growth inhibition, we used flow cytometry to analyze the cell cycle and apoptosis. For cell cycle analysis, we treated cells with HIS for 48 h, harvested them, and fixed them overnight in 70% ethanol at −20 °C. Staining with a propidium iodide (PI) and RNase A solution allowed for the quantification of DNA content and thus cell cycle phase distribution. For apoptosis detection, we subjected similarly treated cells to a double-staining protocol using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, USA). A BD FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer performed the data acquisition, and we processed the raw data using FlowJo software to determine the percentage of cells in each phase or apoptotic state.

Immunoblotting to profile protein changes

To probe the molecular pathways affected by HIS, we performed Western blotting. After a 48-hour treatment with HIS, we lysed the cells on ice using RIPA buffer fortified with protease and phosphatase inhibitors to preserve protein integrity. A BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) determined the protein concentration of the resulting lysates. We then separated equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg) from each sample via SDS-PAGE and transferred them to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Following a blocking step with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting key proteinsCyclin B1 (monoclonal, 1:1000, ImmunoWay, Suzhou, China), CDK1 (monoclonal, 1:1000, ImmunoWay, Suzhou, China), p21 (monoclonal, 1:1000, ImmunoWay, Suzhou, China)BAX (monoclonal, 1:1000, ImmunoWay, Suzhou, China), BCL2 (monoclonal, 1:1000, ImmunoWay, Suzhou, China), Vimentin (monoclonal, 1:1000, ImmunoWay, Suzhou, China), E-Cadherin (monoclonal, 1:1000, ImmunoWay, Suzhou, China), N-Cadherin (monoclonal, 1:1000, ImmunoWay, Suzhou, China), PI3K (monoclonal, 1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), phospho-PI3K (monoclonal, 1:500, Cell Signaling Technology), AKT (monoclonal, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), phospho-AKT ( monoclonal, 1:500, Abcam), MEK (monoclonal, 1:1000, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), phospho-MEK (monoclonal, 1:500, Servicebio), FABP4 (monoclonal, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and β-actin (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China).

The following day, membranes were washed three times with TBST (10 min per wash) and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies ( Goat anti-Rabbit IgG HRP, 1:10000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at room temperature. After three additional washes with TBST, protein bands were visualized using an ECL reagent (Millipore) and captured with a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to β-actin.

Multi-Omics and metabolic investigations

A comprehensive transcriptomic analysis using RNA sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform provided an unbiased view of the gene expression changes induced by a 48-hour HIS treatment in 143B cells. We validated key findings from this dataset using qRT-PCR. To directly link these changes to metabolism, we measured intracellular free fatty acid (FFA) levels with an Amplex Red-based kit and fatty acid synthase (FASN) activity by monitoring NADPH consumption (kits from Beyotime). These metabolic readouts were normalized to total protein content. To identify a direct molecular target, we performed computational molecular docking simulations using the Schrödinger Maestro suite, focusing on the interaction between HIS and the crystal structure of FABP4 (PDB ID: 2NNQ). A binding energy (docking score) of less than − 5.0 kcal/mol was considered indicative of a stable and favorable interaction. The Glide Standard Precision (SP) scoring function was used to evaluate binding affinity, and a binding energy (docking score) of less than − 5.0 kcal/mol was considered indicative of a stable and favorable interaction. To validate our docking protocol, the co-crystallized native ligand was first removed and then re-docked into the active site of FABP4; the protocol’s ability to accurately reproduce the known binding pose was confirmed by calculating the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD).”

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

To assess alterations in gene expression levels following HIS exposure, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was employed. Osteosarcoma cell lines MG63 and 143B were incubated with either Hispidulin or DMSO (vehicle control) for a 48-hour period. Post-treatment, total RNA was procured from the cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), adhering strictly to the supplier’s protocol. The yield and integrity of the isolated RNA were ascertained; concentration and A260/A280 purity ratios were determined with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), while RNA integrity was confirmed by visualizing distinct ribosomal RNA bands on a 1% agarose gel.

Subsequently, 1 µg of the purified total RNA served as a template for first-strand cDNA synthesis. This reverse transcription step was performed utilizing the HiScript® II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), as per the manufacturer’s guidelines to eliminate potential genomic DNA contamination. For the quantification of specific transcripts, qPCR reactions were assembled using ChamQ™ SYBR® qPCR Master Mix (High ROX Premixed) (Vazyme) and executed on a LightCycler® 480 instrument (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Each biological sample was analyzed in technical triplicates within a 20 µL reaction volume. This volume typically comprised 10 µL of SYBR Green master mix, 1 µL of cDNA template (corresponding to a defined amount of initial RNA), optimized concentrations of forward and reverse primers (0.4 µL each of a 10 µM stock), and nuclease-free water to reach the final volume. The thermal cycling parameters commenced with an initial denaturation phase at 95 °C for 30 s, succeeded by 40 amplification cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and a combined annealing/extension step at 60 °C for 30 s. To validate the amplification of a single, specific product, a melt curve analysis was conducted at the conclusion of each qPCR run.

The relative abundance of target mRNA transcripts (FASN, FADS1, FADS2, SCD, and ACOX2) was determined using the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt approach) method. Gene expression data were normalized against the constitutively expressed housekeeping gene, GAPDH. All gene-specific oligonucleotide primers were designed and procured from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), with detailed sequences provided in Table 1 .

Plasmid transfection and overexpression

To investigate the role of Fatty Acid Binding Protein 4 (FABP4) in the anti-tumor effects induced by Hispidulin, functional rescue experiments were performed via plasmid overexpression. The human FABP4 overexpression plasmid and its corresponding empty vector (used as a negative control) were purchased from MIAOLING PLASMID. For the transfection procedure, osteosarcoma cells (MG63 and 143B) were seeded at an appropriate density in 6-well plates and cultured to 70–80% confluency. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were harvested for subsequent experiments, including CCK-8, colony formation, flow cytometry, and Western blot assays. The efficiency of FABP4 overexpression was confirmed by detecting its protein levels via Western blot.

In vivo xenograft model for efficacy and safety

To test the therapeutic potential of HIS in a living system, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model in male BALB/c nude mice. This was done by injecting 1 × 107 143B cells into the right flank. Once tumors reached a volume of approximately 100 mm3, we randomized the mice into groups (n = 5 each) to receive daily intraperitoneal injections for 35 days of either vehicle (DMSO), 10 mg/kg HIS, or 20 mg/kg HIS. Randomization was performed using a computer-based random number generator to ensure unbiased allocation. Furthermore, the investigators responsible for tumor measurement and subsequent data analysis were blinded to the treatment group assignments throughout the study. We monitored tumor growth and mouse body weight weekly. At the experiment’s conclusion, we euthanized the mice, and excised the tumors for weighing and subsequent histological and immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis.

All animal care and experiments in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Jinan University’s Animal Ethics Committee and adhered to institutional guidelines. (IACUC-20240314-12) and the study was reported according to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Histology and statistical approach

For histological examination, we fixed the excised tumors in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded them in paraffin, and stained sections with H&E. IHC staining with antibodies against Ki67 and FABP4 provided insights into proliferation and target engagement in vivo. For all statistical analyses, we used GraphPad Prism 9. Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). We used Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple-group comparisons. We defined statistical significance as a p-value < 0.05. All experiments were performed a minimum of three times.

Results

Hispidulin selectively impedes the malignant phenotypes of osteosarcoma cells in vitro

To initiate our investigation into Hispidulin’s (HIS, chemical structure depicted in Fig. 1A) potential anti-neoplastic actions, its impact on osteosarcoma (OS) cell viability was first assessed. Two distinct human OS cell lines, MG63 and 143B, were exposed to a spectrum of HIS concentrations (ranging from 0 to 60 µM) for durations of 24, 48, and 72 h. Data from the CCK-8 assay compellingly showed that HIS attenuated the viability of both MG63 and 143B cells in a manner contingent on both the applied dose and the exposure time (Fig. 1C, D). Significantly, when the identical assay was conducted on the non-cancerous human osteoblast cell line, hFOB 1.19, HIS demonstrated only negligible cytotoxicity, even at elevated concentrations (Fig. 1B). Quantitatively, the IC50 values for Hispidulin at 48 h were approximately 28.5 µM for MG63 and 22.3 µM for 143B cells, whereas the IC50 for the normal osteoblast hFOB 1.19 cells was > 60 µM, confirming its favorable tumor selectivity and safety profile.This finding points towards a promising therapeutic index, with HIS exhibiting preferential activity against malignant cells.

Expanding on these initial observations, we evaluated HIS’s influence on the capacity for long-term proliferation via a colony formation assay. Following a two-week incubation period with HIS (at concentrations of 7.5, 15, and 30 µM), a pronounced, dose-responsive decline in both the quantity and dimensions of colonies formed by MG63 and 143B cells was evident when juxtaposed with the DMSO-treated control cohort (Fig. 1E, F).

Our focus then shifted to the metastatic attributes of OS cells, a primary factor contributing to patient mortality. Employing Transwell assays, we observed that HIS treatment markedly curtailed the migratory aptitude of both MG63 and 143B cells (Fig. 1G, H). To simulate a more intricate facet of metastasis, an invasion assay was performed, requiring cells to navigate a Matrigel-coated membrane. In this more demanding experimental setup, HIS robustly diminished the invasive potential of the OS cells, again displaying a dose-dependent effect (Fig. 1G, H). Cumulatively, these in vitro results establish that Hispidulin effectively counteracts several key hallmarks of OS malignancy, encompassing proliferation, anchorage-independent survival, migration, and invasion.

HIS induces G2/M cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and reverses epithelial-mesenchymal transition

To elucidate the cellular processes underpinning the observed anti-proliferative efficacy, we first examined HIS’s effect on cell cycle dynamics. Flow cytometric analysis of propidium iodide (PI)-labeled cells indicated that a 48-hour exposure to HIS led to a notable build-up of both MG63 and 143B cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle. This G2/M accumulation was mirrored by a concomitant reduction in the G1 phase population (Fig. 2A, B).

Concurrently, we explored whether HIS could instigate programmed cell death. Through Annexin V-FITC/PI dual staining, it was demonstrated that HIS treatment, in a dose-escalating manner, amplified the proportion of apoptotic cells (including both early and late apoptotic stages) in both MG63 and 143B cell lines (Fig. 2C, D).

To further investigate the molecular underpinnings of these cellular events, Western blot analysis was conducted. In keeping with the G2/M arrest, a substantial decrease in the expression levels of pivotal G2/M checkpoint regulatory proteins, specifically CDK1 and Cyclin B1, was observed subsequent to HIS treatment (Fig. 2E–H). Congruent with its pro-apoptotic action, HIS altered the cellular equilibrium by diminishing the levels of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, while concurrently elevating the pro-apoptotic protein Bax. Furthermore, corroborating the noted suppression of cell motility, HIS treatment fostered a shift in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers. Specifically, an upregulation of the epithelial marker E-cadherin was noted, alongside a simultaneous downregulation of the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin and Vimentin (Fig. 2E–H). These findings suggest a multifaceted mechanism whereby Hispidulin mediates its anti-cancer effects through the induction of G2/M phase arrest, stimulation of apoptosis, and inhibition of the EMT program essential for metastatic dissemination.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals HIS-mediated alterations in lipid metabolism and key cancer pathways

To achieve a broad and unbiased insight into the molecular perturbations induced by HIS, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on 143B cells subjected to 15 µM HIS or DMSO for 48 h. This comprehensive analysis identified a considerable cohort of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 1475 upregulated and 796 downregulated genes (Fig. 3A). Visual representations of these significant expression shifts are provided by a heatmap and a volcano plot (Fig. 3B, C).

Subsequent pathway enrichment analyses were undertaken to decipher the biological relevance of these DEGs. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis highlighted a significant overrepresentation of several crucial signaling networks, with the “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway,” “MAPK signaling pathway,” and “MicroRNAs in cancer” being particularly prominent (Fig. 3D, E, G). Notably, “Metabolism” also surfaced as a major enriched category. To explore this further, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted, which revealed a significant negative enrichment of gene collections pertinent to lipid metabolism (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these transcriptomic findings strongly indicated that Hispidulin’s mode of action encompasses the modulation of cellular lipid metabolic pathways, likely exerted through its impact on the PI3K-Akt and/or MAPK signaling cascades.

To validate the transcriptomic findings, the mRNA expression levels of several key genes involved in lipid metabolism were measured by qRT-PCR. Consistent with the GSEA results, HIS treatment significantly downregulated the expression of FASN, SCD, FADS2, ACSL3, and ACOX2 compared to the DMSO control group (Fig. 3H).

Collectively, these transcriptomic findings strongly suggest that Hispidulin’s mode of action involves the modulation of cellular lipid metabolic pathways, likely exerted through its impact on the PI3K-Akt and/or MAPK signaling cascades.

HIS suppresses fatty acid synthesis by specifically inhibiting the PI3K/AKT pathway

Steered by the revelations from our transcriptome-wide analysis, we proceeded to experimentally validate the premise that Hispidulin influences lipid metabolism via either the PI3K/AKT or MAPK signaling routes. We initially confirmed HIS’s effect on lipid metabolic processes by directly quantifying key metabolic parameters. As hypothesized, HIS administration led to a dose-related reduction in both the intracellular abundance of free fatty acids (FFA) and the enzymatic activity of fatty acid synthase (FASN) within both MG63 and 143B cells (Fig. 4A, B).

Subsequently, to ascertain which signaling pathway was predominantly involved, we assessed the activation state of these two pivotal cascades using Western blot. The data revealed that HIS treatment significantly attenuated the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT, the central constituents of the PI3K/AKT pathway, without affecting their total protein expression. In stark contrast, no discernible alterations were observed in the phosphorylation status of MEK, a key kinase in the MAPK pathway, following HIS exposure (Fig. 4C, D). This observation strongly suggests that the PI3K/AKT pathway, rather than the MAPK pathway, functions as the principal signaling conduit mediating the metabolic consequences of Hispidulin. To further substantiate this deduction, a rescue experiment employing a PI3K/AKT pathway activator was performed, which effectively countermanded the HIS-induced suppression of fatty acid synthesis(Fig. 4E–H). These convergent data lines indicate that Hispidulin’s anti-neoplastic activity in OS cells is primarily mediated by its capacity to inhibit the PI3K/AKT signaling axis, thereby dampening lipid metabolism.

FABP4 is a direct molecular target of hispidulin, and its overexpression abrogates his-induced anti-tumor effects

Our correlative analysis of transcriptomic and proteomic datasets pinpointed Fatty Acid Binding Protein 4 (FABP4), a vital protein in lipid transport and metabolic regulation, as being substantially downregulated following HIS treatment. To discern whether FABP4 was merely a downstream effector or a direct molecular interactor of Hispidulin, computational molecular docking simulations were performed. These simulations predicted a energetically favorable and stable binding engagement between Hispidulin and the active binding cleft of the FABP4 protein, implying a potential direct inhibitory interaction (Fig. 5A).

To functionally ascertain the pivotal contribution of FABP4 in mediating HIS’s anti-neoplastic activity, a series of rescue experiments involving the ectopic overexpression of FABP4 in OS cells were undertaken. As demonstrated by CCK-8 and colony formation assays, the enforced expression of FABP4 markedly attenuated the suppressive effects of HIS on both cellular proliferation and colony-forming capacity (Fig. 5B–D). Analogously, flow cytometric analysis disclosed that FABP4 overexpression partially counteracted the apoptosis induced by HIS treatment (Fig. 5E, F).

Furthermore, Western blot analysis corroborated these functional rescue observations at the molecular level. Overexpression of FABP4 effectively nullified the HIS-induced alterations in key regulatory proteins. Specifically, it reversed the downregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and the cell cycle-associated proteins CDK1 and Cyclin B1. It also negated the changes in EMT-associated markers, counteracting the HIS-induced decrease in E-cadherin and the increase in Vimentin and N-cadherin (Fig. 5G, H). Taken together, these results furnish compelling evidence identifying FABP4 as a crucial and direct molecular target through which Hispidulin executes its anti-tumor functions in osteosarcoma.

Hispidulin effectively suppresses osteosarcoma tumor growth in vivo

Finally, to affirm the therapeutic potential of Hispidulin within a living system, a subcutaneous xenograft model was generated in nude mice using 143B cells. Subsequent to tumor establishment, mice were administered either DMSO (vehicle control), 10 mg/kg HIS, or 20 mg/kg HIS. Upon conclusion of the experimental period, tumors in the HIS-treated cohorts were discernibly smaller and possessed significantly lower mass compared to those in the control group (Fig. 6A, B). Analysis of the tumor growth kinetics demonstrated that HIS administration, especially at the 20 mg/kg dosage, resulted in a substantial and temporally dependent reduction in tumor volume throughout the treatment regimen (Fig. 6C). Critically, no significant variations in the body weight of the mice were noted across the different treatment groups, suggesting that the effective doses of Hispidulin were well-endured and lacked major systemic toxicity (Fig. 6D).

Histopathological examination of the excised tumor tissues provided additional confirmation of HIS’s efficacy. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining indicated diminished cell density and augmented necrotic regions in tumors derived from HIS-treated mice relative to the control cohort. Moreover, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining corroborated our in vitro observations at the tissue level. The expression of the proliferative marker Ki67 was markedly reduced in the HIS-treated groups. Consistent with our mechanistic elucidations, the expression of the direct target, FABP4, was also significantly downregulated in the tumors of mice receiving Hispidulin (Fig. 6E, F). These in vivo findings lend strong support to our in vitro data, confirming that Hispidulin effectively curtails osteosarcoma growth by targeting the FABP4-orchestrated lipid metabolism pathway.

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the inhibitory activity of the natural flavonoid Hispidulin (HIS) in osteosarcoma (OS) and its underlying molecular mechanisms, providing new evidence for its anti-cancer properties. Our findings reveal for the first time that HIS influences lipid metabolism by directly targeting Fatty Acid Binding Protein 4 (FABP4) and inhibiting its key downstream PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. These effects ultimately converge to inhibit the migration, proliferation, and invasive capabilities of OS cells, while simultaneously enhancing the trend towards apoptosis and effectively arresting the cell cycle at the G2/M phase (Fig. 6G).

Our in vitro experiments clearly demonstrated the potent anti-tumor effects of HIS on OS cells. First, we found that HIS inhibited the viability and long-term colony formation of OS cells (MG63 and 143B) in a dose- and time-dependent manner, while exhibiting minimal cytotoxicity toward normal human osteoblasts (hFOB 1.19). This indicates a favorable tumor-selective activity, which is a crucial characteristic for developing anti-cancer drugs with low side effects. This broad anti-proliferative activity is consistent with findings reported for HIS in various other malignancies, such as gallbladder cancer and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [29–31].

Second, apoptosis plays a critical role in tumorigenesis and progression [32]. Our study found that HIS effectively drives OS cells toward apoptosis in a concentration-dependent manner by modulating the expression balance of the Bax/Bcl-2 protein family. This apoptotic mechanism aligns with observations in other malignant tumors, such as melanoma and gastric cancer [33, 34]. Third, cell cycle dysregulation is a hallmark of cancer [35]. Interestingly, our investigation revealed that HIS induced G2/M phase arrest in OS cells, which differs from the G0/G1 arrest reported in other cancer types, suggesting a tumor-specific effect [34]. This observation is consistent with our mechanistic finding of PI3K/Akt pathway inhibition. The PI3K/Akt pathway is a known key positive regulator of the G2/M transition; therefore, its potent inhibition in osteosarcoma cells, which are highly dependent on this pathway, likely leads to a specific G2/M arrest.Fourth, given that metastasis is the primary cause of poor prognosis in OS patients, we found that HIS significantly impaired the migratory and invasive capabilities of OS cells. Mechanistically, HIS suppressed the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by upregulating the epithelial marker E-cadherin while downregulating mesenchymal markers N-cadherin and Vimentin, a process crucial for tumor cells to acquire metastatic potential [36].

To more comprehensively uncover the molecular mechanisms behind HIS, we conducted an unbiased exploration using transcriptome sequencing. KEGG and GSEA analyses pointed to the significant enrichment of several key signaling networks, including the PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways, as well as the “lipid metabolism” pathway. These pathways are known to be central regulators of cell survival, proliferation, and metabolic reprogramming [37]. Subsequent functional validation experiments clearly demonstrated that HIS significantly inhibited the phosphorylation and activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. Notably, however, it did not produce a significant effect on the MAPK pathway, which contrasts with its action in other cancers like AML [38]. and further highlights the context-dependent nature of its activity. This series of evidence strongly suggests that in OS, the PI3K/AKT pathway is the primary upstream signal mediating the biological effects of HIS, likely through which it regulates the reprogramming of lipid metabolism in tumor cells.

A novel finding of this study is the identification of FABP4 as a direct molecular target of HIS in OS, a conclusion supported by multi-omics analysis, molecular docking, and functional validation. FABP4 is a key protein in lipid transport and metabolism, and its high expression in various tumors is closely associated with poor prognosis [39]. Its role is particularly critical in the bone metastatic niche, where FABP4-mediated lipid uptake from bone marrow adipocytes is essential for tumor growth, and its expression has been detected in metastatic OS lesions [40]. Our functional rescue experiments provided strong evidence for the central role of FABP4: overexpression of FABP4 significantly reversed the inhibitory effects of HIS on OS cell proliferation, apoptosis, and EMT. This discovery adds a new dimension to the anti-tumor mechanism of HIS, suggesting that by directly targeting FABP4, HIS may simultaneously block both the energy supply (lipid uptake) and the growth signals (PI3K/AKT pathway) of OS cells, thereby exerting a synergistic inhibitory effect.

The in vivo xenograft model experiments further confirmed the anti-tumor potential of HIS. The results showed that HIS administration (especially at the 20 mg/kg dose) effectively inhibited the growth of 143B cell xenografts without significantly affecting mouse body weight, suggesting good safety and tolerability at effective doses. These in vivo effects are consistent with those previously observed in other tumor models, such as melanoma [41]. Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis of the excised tumor tissues provided strong in vivo evidence for our in vitro findings, showing that the expression of the proliferation marker Ki67 and the direct target FABP4 were both significantly downregulated in tumors treated with HIS.

Although this study provides strong evidence for the therapeutic potential of Hispidulin in OS, we must acknowledge that several limitations remain before its potential clinical application. First, our findings are primarily based on in vitro cell lines and subcutaneous xenograft models, which do not fully recapitulate the complex tumor microenvironment, heterogeneity, or immune system interactions found in human osteosarcoma. Second, a key limitation is that this study does not address the pharmacokinetic properties of Hispidulin; key parameters such as its absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME), and its bioavailability remain unknown, which are critical for designing optimal dosing regimens. Furthermore, as a natural flavonoid, the physicochemical properties of Hispidulin itself, such as drug stability, solubility, and formulation challenges, must be addressed in future development. Although no significant side effects were observed in our animal model, potential off-target effects and systemic toxicities must be carefully monitored in longer-term studies and more complex models. Therefore, future research must not only employ more clinically relevant models, such as patient-derived xenografts (PDX), but also urgently requires comprehensive pharmacokinetic and toxicological studies to provide the necessary scientific basis for the clinical translation of Hispidulin. Although no human clinical trials for Hispidulin exist, preclinical data reveal both its translational potential and its challenges. The primary hurdles are its low oral bioavailability and short half-life observed in animal models. On a positive note, Hispidulin has demonstrated clear in vivo anti-tumor efficacy and a unique ability to efficiently cross the blood-brain barrier [30]. Therefore, Hispidulin stands out as a valuable lead compound whose future clinical application hinges on improving its pharmacokinetic properties through formulation or medicinal chemistry.

In summary, this study elucidates the multi-target anti-tumor effects of Hispidulin in osteosarcoma and its associated molecular mechanisms. We confirmed that HIS reshapes lipid metabolism by directly targeting FABP4 and inhibiting the key PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby significantly suppressing the proliferation, migration, and invasion of OS cells, while inducing apoptosis and G2/M phase cell cycle arrest. These findings suggest that HIS is a promising therapeutic candidate for OS. Given that previous studies have shown HIS enhances the antitumor activity of various chemotherapeutic drugs, future research should focus on exploring the synergistic effects of HIS with existing standard OS chemotherapies, such as doxorubicin and methotrexate, to evaluate its potential to improve efficacy, reduce toxicity, and overcome drug resistance.
