MTHFR polymorphism is associated with increased adverse events and poor clinical outcomes in gastric cancer patients with adjuvant S-1 chemotherapy
Authors: Minsu Kang, Jin Won Kim, Ju Hyun Lee, Lyoung Hyo Kim, Woochan Park, So Hyun Kang, Young Suk Park, Ji-Won Kim, Hyeon Jeong Oh, Sang-Hoon Ahn, Yun-Suhk Suh, Do Joong Park, Hye Seung Lee, Hyung-Ho Kim, Keun-Wook Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
S-1, an oral fluoropyrimidine composed of tegafur (a prodrug of 5-fluorouracil), gimeracil and oteracil, is widely used for gastric cancer. However, data on genetic variants influencing S-1 metabolism and their clinical impact remain limited. We enrolled 334 patients who underwent gastrectomy between July 2007 and May 2013, followed by adjuvant S-1 therapy. Genotyping of 46 SNPs in six genes (MTHFR, DPD, OPRT, TS, CYP2A6, TP) was performed to investigate their association with AEs and outcomes. MTHFR rs1801133 C > T was associated with higher hematologic AEs in recessive model (OR = 3.00, p = 0.006), and wild-type haplotype (ht2) was associated with fewer hematologic AEs in dominant model (OR = 0.34, p = 0.003). TP rs470119 G > A was associated with increased non-hematologic AEs in recessive model (OR = 3.42, p = 0.045). Overall AEs were reduced with ht2 of MTHFR in recessive model (OR = 0.43, p = 0.042). With a median follow-up of 93.2 months, MTHFR rs1801133 C > T and CYP2A6 rs28399468 G > T correlated with shorter disease-free survival in dominant model (HR = 2.02, p = 0.014; HR = 1.93, p = 0.046; respectively). MTHFR rs1801133 C > T was associated with worse overall survival in dominant model (HR = 1.95, p = 0.023). Overall, MTHFR rs1801133 C > T was associated with increased hematologic AEs and poorer survival, suggesting its potential relevance as a candidate biomarker in the context of adjuvant S-1 therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-38429-3.

Introduction

S-1 is an oral chemotherapeutic agent consisting of tegafur (a prodrug of 5-fluorouracil [5-FU]) along with two 5-FU-modulating substances—gimeracil, an inhibitor of dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD), and oteracil, an inhibitor of 5-FU in the gastrointestinal tract—and has been widely used for gastric cancer (GC) in East Asian countries1. The Adjuvant Chemotherapy Trial of S-1 for Gastric Cancer (ACTS-GC) demonstrated that 1 year of adjuvant S-1 treatment significantly reduces tumor recurrence and improves overall survival (OS) in curatively resected GC patients2. At 5 years, the disease-free survival (DFS) rate was 65.4% in the S-1 group versus 53.1% in the surgery-only group (hazard ratio [HR], 0.653; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.537–0.793) and the OS rate was 71.7% versus 61.1% (HR, 0.669; 95% CI, 0.540–0.828), respectively. Retrospective studies have suggested no significant differences in DFS and OS between 1-year S-1 treatment and 6-month capecitabine plus oxaliplatin, particularly in early-stage disease3,4. These findings may support considering S-1 as a convenient oral option for selected patients.

Given that patients should take S-1 orally for a year, managing treatment-related adverse events (AEs) is important for ensuring good drug compliance, leading to positive clinical outcomes. Previous studies have suggested that single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in genes involved in 5-FU metabolism—such as methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR), dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD), orotate phosphoribosyltransferase (OPRT), thymidylate synthase (TS), cytochrome P450 2A6 (CYP2A6), and thymidine phosphorylase (TP)—may affect both the toxicity and efficacy of 5-FU-based chemotherapy5–8. Nonetheless, S-1 differs from infusional 5-FU in that it is a prodrug combined with gimeracil and oteracil, which can modify 5-FU metabolism and toxicity profiles9. Accordingly, pharmacogenomic associations may also differ between S-1 and intravenous 5-FU. However, pharmacogenomic studies for S-1 are sparse. Furthermore, most of the previous studies were conducted in heterogeneous populations across studies and focused on SNPs of a single gene. The results of these are inconsistent. Therefore, comprehensive studies investigating the relationship between SNPs in genes associated with 5-FU metabolism and their influence on both AEs and clinical outcomes in patients undergoing adjuvant S-1 chemotherapy after GC surgery are needed.

This study aimed to assess the correlation between the SNPs identified from blood samples and their impact on both the AE profiles and clinical outcomes in patients receiving adjuvant S-1 chemotherapy following GC surgery. Findings from this study are expected to aid in identifying genetic biomarkers that can help guide the appropriate management of patients undergoing adjuvant S-1 therapy after surgery.

Methods

Patients and sample collection

This study was based on prospective cohorts of GC patients maintained at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital10–13. In these cohorts, all patients underwent curative gastrectomy followed by adjuvant S-1 chemotherapy. Eligibility criteria included histologically confirmed gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma; pathologic stage II–III according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 6th or 7th edition at the time of diagnosis—with all cases subsequently reclassified using the 8th edition to ensure consistent staging for this study’s analysis; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2; and adequate bone marrow, renal, and hepatic function. Blood samples were prospectively collected for pharmacogenomic analyses, and comprehensive clinical data—including demographics, treatment details, outcomes, and toxicity profiles—were systematically recorded.

Using this prospectively maintained cohort database, we conducted a retrospective analysis. Among patients who underwent GC surgery between July 2007 and May 2013 and received adjuvant S-1 monotherapy, those with both SNP analysis results (derived from blood samples collected prior to the initiation of adjuvant therapy) and toxicity evaluations were included. The primary aim of our study was to identify key germline SNPs involved in S-1 metabolism that influence toxicity and survival in gastric cancer. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for the use of archived blood samples and clinical data. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (B-2504-966-110). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Treatment and toxicity assessment

S-1 was administered orally for 4 weeks, followed by a 2-week rest period. S-1 treatment was repeated every 6 weeks, and this interval was considered one cycle. The dosage of S-1, administered twice daily, was determined based on the patient’s body surface area (BSA): 40 mg for BSA < 1.25 m2, 50 mg for BSA between 1.25 and 1.49 m2, and 60 mg for BSA ≥ 1.50 m2. Dosage adjustment or schedule change (i.e., changing from a 6-week treatment interval to a 3-week interval) was made according to observed AEs, as previously documented11,12. The planned duration of adjuvant S-1 treatment was one year, provided there was no tumor recurrence, unacceptable AEs, or patient refusal.

AEs were graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Toxicity Criteria (version 4.0). All AEs were prospectively recorded at each treatment visit, and the highest grade observed during S-1 therapy was used for analysis. Dose intensity (DI) was calculated as the total S-1 dose per BSA of the patient divided by the entire treatment duration. The relative dose intensity (RDI) was computed by dividing the received DI by the planned DI.

SNP genotyping

Germline DNA extracted from peripheral blood collected prior to initiation of adjuvant therapy was used for all genotyping analyses. We conducted the SNP array analysis to identify the genetic factors influencing the response to S-1 treatment. A total of 46 SNPs across six different genes (MTHFR, DPD, OPRT, TS, CYP2A6, and TP) were selected based on strong prior evidence from pharmacogenomic studies14–19. Genotyping for all loci was performed using the Fluidigm high-throughput platform and Fluidigm EP1 SNP Genotyping 96.96 Dynamic Array (Fluidigm Corp., South San Francisco, CA). The discrete genotype data were analyzed using the BioMark SNP Genotyping analysis software (version 4.3.2). After genotyping, linkage disequilibrium (LD) among SNP pairs was evaluated using Haploview v4.2 software downloaded from the Broad Institute (http://www.broadinstitute.org/mpg/haploview) by examining Lewontin’s D’ (|D’|) and the LD coefficient r2 between all pairs of bi-allelic loci20. Highly correlated SNPs (r2 > 0.8) within the same gene were grouped into haplotypes, whereas SNPs showing low LD were analyzed individually. Haplotypes were estimated using PHASE v2.1 software21, and those with a frequency ≥ 0.05 were included in subsequent analyses.

Statistical analysis

Each SNP was evaluated under co-dominant, dominant, and recessive genetic models. Logistic regression models were used to compare genotype distributions and to calculate odds ratios (ORs), 95% CIs, and corresponding P-values using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). In the co-dominant model, outcomes were evaluated by separately comparing the wild-type genotype (AA) with the heterozygous genotype (Aa) and the homozygous variant genotype (aa). Statistical significance in the co-dominant model was concluded if the overall P-value was significant and the ORs or HRs for both AA vs. Aa and AA vs. aa were in the same direction. In the dominant model, the heterozygous and homozygous variant genotypes (Aa + aa) were combined and compared against the wild-type genotype (AA). In the recessive model, the homozygous variant genotype (aa) was compared with the combined group of wild-type and heterozygous genotypes (AA + Aa). The Kaplan–Meier method was used to calculate the DFS and OS. DFS was defined as the time from gastrectomy to the recurrence of the tumor, any cause of death before relapse of disease, or censoring. OS was defined as the period between the time of gastrectomy and the date of death due to any cause or censoring. Cox proportional hazard models were used to analyze the influence of specified risk factors on survival outcomes in the multivariable analysis. P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and was not violated in the reported Cox models. This study was reported in accordance with the REMARK22 recommendations, and a completed checklist is provided in the Supplementary File 1.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between July 2007 and May 2013, a total of 446 patients underwent gastrectomy for GC, and blood samples were collected for pharmacogenomic analysis. Among these patients, 379 received adjuvant S-1 chemotherapy, and a subset of 374 patients underwent prospective evaluation for S-1 toxicity. Finally, 334 patients who underwent toxicity evaluation and were able to undergo SNP genotyping with the collected blood samples were included in this study (Fig. 1. CONSORT diagram). The median age was 59 years (range, 20–82 years), and 209 patients (62.6%) were male. Tumor stages, reclassified according to the AJCC 8th edition, were distributed as follows: stage IB, 6 (1.8%); stage IIA, 99 (29.6%); stage IIB, 75 (22.5%); stage IIIA, 68 (20.4%); stage IIIB, 75 (22.5%); and stage IIIC, 11 (3.3%). Other detailed characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. Planned one-year treatment with S-1 was completed by 260 patients (77.8%), and the mean number of delivered S-1 chemotherapy cycles was 7.3 (95% CI, 7.0–7.6). Mean and median RDIs during all S-1 therapy cycles were 81.2% (95% CI, 78.3–84.2) and 93.8% (IQR, 77.8–100.0), respectively. Overall, S-1 treatment was well tolerated, with anemia, anorexia, nausea, and diarrhea being the most common AEs (Table 2). Neutropenia was the most common grade 3–4 AE (10.2%), followed by abdominal pain (8.1%) and diarrhea (7.2%). Among the patients with grade 4 neutropenia, one (0.3%) developed febrile neutropenia. Additionally, one patient (0.3%) died of hepatic failure. The median follow-up duration was 93.2 months.

Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors contributing to AEs associated with S-1 treatment, adjusting for age and sex based on previous literature23,24. In the analysis of hematologic adverse events (Table 4), rs1801133 C > T and haplotype containing only this variant (ht1) of the MTHFR gene showed the association with higher rate of AEs in the recessive model (OR = 3.00, P-value = 0.006; OR = 3.13, P-value = 0.004; respectively). In contrast, wild-type haplotype (ht2) of the MTHFR gene showed association with a lower rate of AEs in the dominant model (OR = 0.34, P-value = 0.003). In the analysis of non-hematologic AEs (Supplementary Table 2), rs470119 G > A of the TP gene linked to a higher rate of AEs in the recessive model (OR = 3.42, P-value = 0.045). In the analysis of total AEs (Supplementary Table 3), ht2 of the MTHFR gene had an association with a lower rate of AEs in the recessive model (OR = 0.43, P-value = 0.042).

Association between candidate SNPs and clinical outcome

Significant associations were found between selected SNPs and clinical outcomes of S-1 treatment. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to identify factors contributing to clinical outcomes, adjusting for stage and age. In DFS analysis (Supplementary Table 4), rs1801133 C > T of the MTHFR gene was associated with shorter DFS in the dominant model (HR = 2.02, P-value = 0.014) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, ht1 of the MTHFR gene (HR = 1.86, P-value = 0.026) (Fig. 2B) and rs28399468 G > T of the CYP2A6 gene (HR = 1.93, P-value = 0.046) (Supplementary Fig. 1) also showed significant associations with shorter DFS in the dominant model. In OS analysis (Supplementary Table 5), rs1801133 C > T of the MTHFR gene showed association with shorter OS in the dominant model (HR = 1.95, P-value = 0.023) (Fig. 2C), as was ht1 of the MTHFR gene (HR 2.05, P-value 0.015) (Fig. 2D).

Association between candidate gene polymorphisms and relative dose intensity

The relationship between candidate SNPs and RDI was investigated (Supplementary Table 6). The MTHFR rs1801133 C > T polymorphism, a key SNP in our study, was associated with increased hematologic AEs and worse DFS and OS. However, no significant differences in RDI were observed among the different genotypes. Likewise, most of the other SNPs and genes analyzed showed no significant association with RDI. While MTHFR rs1801131 A > C showed a statistically significant association with higher mean RDI in the recessive model (P-value = 0.002), the small number of patients with the CC genotype (n = 9) limits the clinical relevance of this result.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed 46 SNPs across six genes related to 5-FU metabolism (MTHFR, DPD, OPRT, TS, CYP2A6, and TP) to investigate factors influencing toxicity and prognosis in patients undergoing adjuvant S-1 chemotherapy after GC surgery. Our findings indicate that the rs1801133 C > T SNP (C677T, Ala222Val) of MTHFR gene was associated with a higher incidence of hematologic AEs, as well as worse DFS and OS.

The MTHFR enzyme regulates folate homeostasis by catalyzing the conversion of 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate (5,10-methylene-THF)—a folate form critical for thymidylate synthesis—into 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5-methyl-THF), which is required for methionine biosynthesis and DNA methylation. The MTHFR gene polymorphism rs1801133 C > T results in an alanine-to-valine substitution at position 222 in the enzyme’s catalytic domain, which is vital for its function in folate metabolism. Previous studies have shown that the T allele in the rs1801133 C > T polymorphism reduces MTHFR enzyme activity. Individuals with the homozygous variant TT have approximately 70% lower enzyme activity, and heterozygous variant CT carriers have about 35% lower enzyme activity compared to those with the wild-type CC genotype25–27. This enzymatic reduction can lead to increased levels of 5,10-methylene-THF, potentially enhancing 5-FU-mediated TS inhibition. Such metabolic alterations may contribute to both heightened toxicity and altered clinical outcomes in patients receiving adjuvant S-1 chemotherapy. Importantly, in the context of S-1 therapy, gimeracil-mediated inhibition of dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase results in sustained systemic exposure to 5-FU, which may further amplify the downstream effects of folate-pathway perturbations caused by reduced MTHFR activity, particularly in rapidly proliferating hematopoietic cells. In our study, the MTHFR rs1801133 TT genotype was associated with increased hematologic side effects, as well as worse DFS and OS. Similar associations between MTHFR rs1801133 C > T and fluoropyrimidine-related hematologic toxicity have been reported in Asian colorectal cancer cohorts28. Furthermore, previous research has shown that patients harboring this variant experience poorer prognosis when treated with adjuvant fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy after colorectal cancer surgery29. Taken together, these convergent data highlight the clinical relevance of MTHFR rs1801133 C > T as a key folate-pathway variant that may influence both toxicity and outcomes under fluoropyrimidine-based therapy.

One patient died from hepatic failure, and notably, this patient carried the MTHFR rs1801133 C > T SNP, which is the key polymorphism highlighted in this study due to its significant association with increased hematologic AEs and reduced DFS and OS. This SNP has a relatively high MAF of 0.410 (Table 3), indicating that even common variants can contribute to severe toxicity and should not be overlooked. In addition, one patient experienced grade 4 diarrhea, which resolved after discontinuation of S-1, and two patients developed grade 4 neutropenia, including one case of febrile neutropenia. These patients did not carry the MTHFR rs1801133 variant but harbored other SNPs, suggesting that multiple genetic factors may contribute to the risk of severe toxicities. Together, these findings underscore the potential utility of integrating pharmacogenetic data into clinical decision-making to better anticipate and manage adverse events during adjuvant S-1 chemotherapy.

While previous studies have explored the association between SNPs in genes involved in 5-FU metabolism and chemotherapy-related toxicity or outcomes, relatively few have focused specifically on S-1. Although S-1, capecitabine, and intravenous 5-FU share a common mechanism of action as fluoropyrimidines, differences in formulation and metabolic pathways may lead to variations in clinical efficacy and toxicity profiles. In particular, orally administered agents such as S-1 and capecitabine are subject to interindividual variability in bioavailability, largely due to differences in drug absorption. In addition, although both S-1 and capecitabine are prodrugs that require enzymatic activation, their metabolic pathways differ30. S-1 is primarily converted to 5-FU in the liver, where gimeracil enhances systemic 5-FU exposure by strongly inhibiting DPD enzyme, and oteracil reduces gastrointestinal toxicity by suppressing 5-FU phosphorylation in the gastrointestinal tract. In contrast, capecitabine undergoes a three-step enzymatic activation process. It is first metabolized in the liver by carboxylesterase, then further processed by cytidine deaminase, which is highly expressed in the liver and tumor tissues, and finally converted to 5-FU by thymidine phosphorylase, which is more active in tumor tissue. This tumor-selective activation contributes to reduced gastrointestinal and bone marrow toxicity. These pharmacokinetic and pharmacogenomic distinctions between S-1 and capecitabine may influence how genetic variants affect drug metabolism and clinical outcomes. Therefore, specific pharmacogenomic study addressing S-1 are needed. In this study, we identified MTHFR rs1801133 C > T as a clinically relevant SNP associated with increased toxicity and poorer outcomes after S-1 treatment, which has not been emphasized in prior studies. While previous research has suggested that variants in TS or CYP2A6 may affect S-1–related toxicity and efficacy, the results have been inconsistent31–33. In our analysis, the CYP2A6 rs28399468 G > T variant was associated with shorter DFS in the dominant model, but this finding could not be meaningfully connected to other toxicity parameters or OS, and therefore should be viewed as exploratory and subject to confirmation in larger, pharmacokinetically annotated cohorts.

In our cohort, carriers of the MTHFR rs1801133 C > T variant tended to have a shorter duration of adjuvant S-1 treatment (7.2 vs. 7.6 cycles, CT/TT vs. CC) and a higher proportion of grade 3/4 hematologic adverse events (12.5% vs. 8.9%, CT/TT vs. CC), although these differences were not statistically significant. At the cohort level, this did not result in a significant reduction in RDI. This likely reflects the fact that most adverse events during adjuvant S-1 monotherapy were low grade, allowing dose intensity to be largely maintained despite increased toxicity. Importantly, the lack of a measurable difference in RDI does not diminish the clinical relevance of this variant. Our cohort included a fatal case of hepatic failure in a patient carrying rs1801133, and the variant was consistently associated with both increased toxicity and poorer survival outcomes. These findings suggest that MTHFR rs1801133 C > T may help identify patients at higher risk of toxicity during adjuvant S-1 therapy. However, its clinical utility remains to be established and should be validated in prospective studies designed to assess genotype-guided management strategies. If confirmed, early identification of this variant may support risk stratification and more personalized monitoring or toxicity management.

A recent study demonstrated that personalized dose adjustments of fluoropyrimidines based on DPD gene variants did not compromise prognosis, suggesting that dose modification may enhance safety in patients genetically predisposed to severe AEs34. In line with this, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recently emphasized the necessity of screening for DPD deficiency before initiating fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy to minimize severe toxicity risks (January 24, 2025, FDA Safety Announcement), reinforcing the importance of pharmacogenetic evaluation in clinical practice. While DPD polymorphisms are a well-recognized determinant of fluoropyrimidine-related toxicity, their prevalence varies across ethnic groups35. Previously reported high-risk variants, such as rs3918290 G > A in the DPD gene36, were not detected in our cohort (Table 3). Instead, in our study population, MTHFR rs1801133 C > T was significantly associated with increased toxicity and poorer prognosis following S-1 chemotherapy, highlighting the potential relevance of ethnicity-specific pharmacogenetic markers. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring genetic screening strategies based on population-specific genetic risk factors to optimize the safety and efficacy of adjuvant S-1 therapy in gastric cancer patients.

When analyzing multiple SNPs, there is an inherent risk of false-positive findings due to multiple comparisons. While more stringent statistical thresholds are typically warranted in such cases, we consider that our study design justifies the use of a conventional significance threshold (P < 0.05) for several reasons. First, we calculated LD between all SNP pairs and grouped highly correlated SNPs into haplotypes. This approach reduced redundancy and minimized the number of effectively independent comparisons, thereby addressing the issue of non-independence. Second, haplotype analysis complemented individual SNP analysis by capturing combined effects of linked variants, which may better reflect biological relevance. Third, all genes and SNPs analyzed in this study were selected based on prior evidence implicating their relevance to 5-FU metabolism, efficacy, or toxicity. Taken together, the use of LD-based haplotype grouping and a hypothesis-driven candidate gene approach supports the application of a P-value threshold (P < 0.05) without additional correction for multiple testing. Similar analytical strategies have been adopted in previous studies that evaluated predefined SNPs using a conventional significance threshold without multiple-testing correction37,38.

This study has several limitations. First, the lack of pharmacokinetic data for S-1 prevented us from directly assessing the relationship between polymorphisms and S-1 metabolism. Second, data on concomitant medications were not available, which could have influenced CYP activity and required dose adjustments. However, in clinical practice, physicians typically manage significant drug-drug interactions, making it unlikely that this limitation had a major impact on our findings. Third, although germline variants are unlikely to be systematically confounded by baseline clinical factors, our simplified adjustment strategy may not fully capture the influence of all treatment-related variables. Despite these constraints, this study offers a relatively comprehensive pharmacogenetic analysis by concurrently evaluating multiple SNPs across key 5-FU metabolism-related genes, considering their potential interactions. Although the analysis was retrospective, it was based on a prospectively collected cohort with systematically recorded toxicity data and clinical outcomes. The inclusion of 334 patients with a median follow-up of 93.2 months adds to the robustness and potential clinical relevance of our findings. To fully establish the clinical utility of MTHFR rs1801133 C > T, future prospective studies incorporating genotype-based treatment algorithms and predictive modeling will be essential.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study indicates that the rs1801133 C > T polymorphism in MTHFR gene is associated with an elevated risk of hematologic toxicities and shorter DFS and OS in patients undergoing S-1 adjuvant chemotherapy. The findings of this study suggest that the MTHFR rs1801133 C > T polymorphism may represent a potential genetic biomarker to inform treatment decisions and optimize adjuvant S-1 therapy in patients with GC following curative surgery.
