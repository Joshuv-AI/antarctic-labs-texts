SILICONE FINE-NEEDLE ASPIRATION RETINAL BIOPSY
Authors: Mojca Globočnik Petrovič, Nika Vrabič, Veronika Prevodnik Kloboves, Simona Miceska, Ana Pajtler Rošar, Nataša Vidovič Valentinčič
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 41-gauge silicone fine-needle aspirational biopsy is valuable for obtaining high-quality retinal and subretinal samples when primary intraocular lymphoma is suspected. The technique provides high-quality samples for cytologic analysis.

Intraocular lymphoma is a diagnostically challenging condition, especially when ocular tissue is the site of primary involvement. This article focuses on tissue sampling in primary vitreoretinal lymphoma (PVRL), a diffuse large B-cell lymphoma of immune-privileged sites, a specific subgroup of primary central nervous system lymphoma, where the primary manifestation occurs in the eyes.1

The diagnostic complexity of PVRL underscores the importance of timely diagnosis. Early diagnosis and treatment may improve the prognosis for a better visual outcome2 and longer survival rate.3

Cytopathological or histopathological identification of lymphoma cells is the gold standard for PVRL diagnosis. Pars plana vitrectomy with vitreous biopsy (VB) is a valuable technique for providing the material for cytopathological examination. However, retinal/subretinal tissue sampling may be necessary when vitreous infiltration is scarce. Various surgical strategies exist4–6 of which fine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) is considered the least traumatic.7–9

To enhance diagnostic capabilities, we tested a 41-gauge flexible silicone fine-needle aspirational biopsy (S-FNAB) for suspected PVRL. The small needle diameter and flexibility allow for sampling even smaller retinal/subretinal lesions. This study assessed the technique's ability to provide adequate retinal specimens for PVRL diagnosis and report its complication rate.

Methods

A 41-gauge S-FNAB for retinal/subretinal biopsy is described. In this retrospective single-center study, data were reviewed on 34 patients suspected of PVRL who underwent vitrectomy with or without retinal/subretinal tissue biopsy for intraocular sampling in the Eye Clinic University Medical Centre Ljubljana between January 2010 and March 2023. Of 34 patients, 7 underwent S-FNAB; we report their preoperative clinical findings, data on intraocular tissue sampling, and cytopathological diagnoses.

The same vitreoretinal surgeon performed all S-FNABs. The research adhered to the tenets of Helsinki. Informed consent was signed by the patients followed at the Eye Clinic of the University Medical Centre Ljubljana. Ethical approval was obtained from the National Commission of Medical Ethics (N. 0120-379/2022/3). We report preoperative clinical findings: optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging using spectral-domain OCT (Heidelberg Spectralis; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) or swept-source OCT (Triton, Topcon, Tokyo, Japan), blue-autofluorescence imaging using Heidelberg Spectralis (Spectralis; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), and fundus photography.

In two patients (patients V and VI), biopsy sites of the retinal lesions by S-FNAB were further studied with an overlay of fundus photography and preoperative OCT imaging. Biopsy sites were marked on fundus images extracted from intraoperative video recordings, followed by biopsy site marking in Spectralis on preoperatively taken infrared, blue-autofluorescence, and OCT images. Retinal vessels were used for image alignment (Figure 1). Total retinal thickness at the marked biopsy sites was measured on OCT images with Spectralis software at a 1:1 µm setting.

Vitreous Biopsy

A VB was performed using a standard procedure. A three-port 23 gauge pars plana vitrectomy was performed. The first step—manual vitreous aspiration with an activated vitrectomy cutter (low cutting rate)—was carried out under air infusion; 1 to 3 mL of undiluted vitreous was collected in the syringe through a gentle aspiration by the assistant. The undiluted vitreous specimen was placed in 1 mL of in-house collection cell medium (4.5% bovine serum albumin, 0.45% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) in phosphate buffer solution with 90,000 IE/mL of penicillin, and 4 g/mL of garamycin), which was followed by the collection of 5 to 7 mL of diluted vitreous sample obtained through pars plana vitrectomy with induced posterior vitreous detachment if not previously present.11

Retinal/Subretinal Biopsy; Silicone Fine-Needle Aspiration Biopsy

The biopsy entry site was planned preoperatively. When PVRL was suspected in both eyes, visual potential and accessibility to the planned surgical site were considered when choosing the surgical eye. Larger and more easily accessible retinal/subretinal infiltrates were targeted, avoiding vessels and the macular area when possible. In very small lesions, accessibility of biopsy sites and significant changes in retinal architecture determined the site of S-FNAB.

The extendible 41-gauge subretinal injection needle (DORC, Netherlands) consists of a 23-gauge external needle and a 41-gauge extendable silicone subretinal cannula. The injection needle was connected to a 5-mL syringe through a 20 to 30-cm long silicone tubing (Figure 2, A and B). The role of tubing was to assure the stability of the surgeon's hand movements and prevent the possible transmission of small movements generated by the assistant while performing aspiration (Figure 2C). The needle was introduced through the pars plana vitrectomy port 3.5 to 4.0 mm posterior to the limbus. The extendable silicon cannula was guided tangentially into the retinal lesion, avoiding major vessels. Once the needle was inserted into the retina and reached subretinal or sub-retinal pigment epithelium (sub-RPE)) space (as confirmed on the intraoperative OCT imaging), the assistant performed a gentle aspiration. In patient V, the cannula was inserted in the area of serous retinal detachment (Figure 1). The biopsy material hollowed out the lesion and was aspirated to the silicone cannula. The number of passes and entry sites was dependent on the lesion size and the presence of biopsy material in the cannula. If biopsy material in the tip of the cannula was not evident, up to four passes with back-and-forth (2 mm) movement with minimal redirection were performed in a single entry site, and up to four entry sites were performed. The volume of the aspirated sample depended on the size of the lesion. The silicone tip was filled with the biopsy material up to 5 mm in length.

To prevent the loss of cells from the cannula, the assistant applied gentle aspiration during the passage of the cannula through the vitreous cavity until the material was ejected into the medium. Endolaser was not performed around the self-sealing retinotomies,12 and the vitreous cavity was filled with air. The aspirated cells in the silicone needle tip were rinsed several times with the in-house cell collection medium to prepare cell suspension (Figure 2D). The obtained undiluted vitreous, diluted vitreous, and S-FNAB specimens were immediately transported to the Department of Cytopathology at the Institute of Oncology Ljubljana for further processing.

Results

Seven patients underwent S-FNAB. Postoperative follow-up (range: 3–29 months, median 11 months) was available for all seven patients.

In patients I, II, and VII, who presented with a larger retinal lesion, the S-FNAB proved valuable as a single-site and single-pass biopsy. In other patients, the passage was repeated up to four times in the same or, when coexistent, in nearby lessons.

Silicone Fine-Needle Aspiration Biopsy Results

Indications for S-FNAB were as follows: 1) pathological changes predominately involved the retinal tissue (in three patients: II, III, IV), 2) inconclusive VB (in three patients: I, VI, VII), and 3) absent vitreous exudation (in one patient: V). Primary vitreoretinal lymphoma was confirmed by cytopathological examination supported by flow cytometry from S-FNAB-acquired samples in six out of seven (86%) patients (Figure 3). In the remaining patient, S-FNAB yielded a paucicellular sample, which confirmed the presence of lymphoma cells but did not allow for the classification of lymphoma and a definitive diagnosis. Nevertheless, the lymphoma cell lineage and the diagnosis of PVRL were given from the concomitant VB sample.

Discussion

This is the first study demonstrating the value of retinal/subretinal aspiration biopsy using a flexible silicone 41-gauge needle in diagnosing PVRL. In this case series, the diagnosis of PVRL was established in six out of seven patients whose biopsy material had been obtained through the S-FNAB technique. The S-FNAB sample was paucicelluar in the remaining patient and did not provide data on the lymphoma type. Notably, no severe intraoperative or postoperative complications were associated with the procedure.

While similar methodologies, using a transvitreal biopsy approach by a 41-gauge nano vitreoretinal subretinal gateway device designed for subretinal injections, have been utilized to diagnose choroidal malignant melanoma,13 our study underscores the unique contribution of S-FNAB to the diagnostic process for PVRL.

The 41-gauge extendable, flexible silicone needle was first introduced for the pneumatic displacement of subretinal hemorrhage14 and has been previously used to apply various drugs under the retina and remove subretinal perfluorocarbon liquid.15–17 A very thin (0.0711 mm) and flexible silicone needle allows penetration into the retinal and subretinal space, enabling the aspiration of lymphoma cells from retinal and subretinal lesions. Repeated gliding of the silicone needle at the biopsy site may increase the probability of obtaining a suitable material. Given that the margins of the lesions are more likely to harbor the pathologic process than the central lesion, the tissue sample should extend to the normal retina.18 For this reason, the macular region is unsuitable for the full-thickness chorio-retinal biopsy. However, high-quality samples in the macular region were safely obtained through the S-FNAB in this series (patient V).

Another challenge lies in sampling shallow retinal/subretinal infiltrates. Previously reported 25-gauge FNAB was used for sampling lesions with thickness ranging from 1.3 to 3.7 mm.7 However, in this study, the S-FNAB provided high-quality specimens from smaller, micro-retinal infiltrates with retinal thickness at the biopsy sites measuring 544 to 642 µm and 231 to 392 µm in patients V and VI, respectively.

Silicone fine-needle aspiration biopsy may thus be particularly advantageous for sampling retinal infiltrates even when they do not significantly increase retinal thickness or remain limited to the macular region. We propose S-FNAB should be considered for each patient individually in the following clinical settings: alongside VB as a first-choice approach in patients with a high clinical suspicion of PVRL and no vitreous infiltration, and as a second choice, following inconclusive VB.

Despite the small sample size, no complications were associated with the 41-gauge S-FNAB in our study. The absence of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment and vitreous hemorrhage may be attributed to the small biopsy site and the experienced surgeon performing the technique. Nevertheless, the complication rate may still be lower than in other retinal tissue biopsy techniques. In chorioretinal full-thickness tissue biopsy, rhegmatogenous retinal detachment occurred in 6.8% to 10% of patients, and vitreous hemorrhage was reported in 6.8% to 22% of patients.4,19,20 Srivastava et al6 described retinal/subretinal biopsy performed by cutting/aspirating with a vitrector in patients with potential vitreoretinal lymphoma and reported retinal detachment in 2 of 10 eyes following the procedure. Sarafzadeh et al7 reported no rhegmatogenous retinal detachment following a 25-gauge FNAB in seven patients with potential vitreoretinal lymphoma but did observe one case of vitreous hemorrhage (14.3%) and a case of postoperative endophthalmitis.

The major weaknesses of this study are the small number of patients who underwent S-FNAB and the fact that all surgeries were performed by a single experienced surgeon. Further exploration of the safety and diagnostic outcomes of S-FNAB on a larger patient population is warranted to provide detailed diagnostic guidelines.

The accuracy of VB and S-FNAB also strongly depends on effective interdisciplinary collaboration between ophthalmologists and pathologists. This collaboration is crucial for detecting the suspicion of PVRL, performing high-quality biopsy procedures, and handling the sample according to protocol. The quality of laboratories that process the specimens for the microscopical, immunophenotypic, and molecular analyses is also critical for successfully diagnosing intraocular lymphomas from purely cellular cytological samples.

In conclusion, 41-gauge S-FNAB is a valuable method for obtaining a sufficient sample of viable cells to diagnose PVRL, even in very small micro-retinal lesions. Although this study was conducted on a small number of patients, we observed good outcomes in establishing the diagnosis of PVRL and reported no severe complications.
