Geographic disparities and determinants of DTP3 vaccination coverage among children aged 0–59 months in Somalia: a multilevel analysis
Authors: Mohamed Abdirahim Omar, Mohamud Ali Mohamud, Abdifatah Ibrahim Mouse, Abdisalam Hassan Muse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Somalia, a fragile state characterized by protracted conflict and fragmented health governance, childhood immunization remains a critical challenge. The third dose of the Diphtheria-Tetanus-Pertussis (DTP3) vaccine serves as a key indicator of health system performance and retention. This study aimed to identify structural and individual-level determinants of DTP3 coverage to understand the inequities in this humanitarian setting.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data from the 2020 Somali Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS). A total sample of 1,268 children was analyzed. Multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression models were fitted to account for the hierarchical structure of the data (children nested within communities). Fixed effects were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsThe overall prevalence of DTP3 vaccination coverage was 39.51% [95% CI: 36.85–42.23]. At the individual level, higher maternal education (Secondary vs. no education: aOR = 1.83; 95% CI: 1.07–3.12), wealth status (Rich vs. poor: aOR = 2.41; 95% CI: 1.53–3.79), and antenatal care (ANC) attendance (3–4 visits vs. zero visits: aOR = 2.19; 95% CI: 1.43–3.36) were significantly associated with higher odds of vaccination. Older children had higher odds of vaccination compared to infants, suggesting delayed rather than timely uptake. At the community level, children in rural (aOR = 0.40; 95% CI: 0.28–0.57) and urban (aOR = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.45–0.91) areas had lower odds of vaccination compared to those in nomadic communities. Significant regional disparities were also observed.ConclusionsDTP3 coverage in Somalia is critically below the WHO 90% target. Vaccination status is shaped by a complex interplay of socioeconomic status, maternal healthcare utilization, and geographic location. Policy interventions must urgently address the ‘urban penalty’ identified in this study, where settled urban-poor populations now face lower odds of vaccination than nomadic groups targeted by mobile outreach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13031-026-00777-2.Keywords: DTP3, Vaccination, Somalia, Multilevel analysis, SHDS, Immunization coverage, Public Health, Fragile State

Introduction

Childhood immunization is globally recognized as one of the most cost-effective public health interventions, significantly reducing morbidity and mortality from infectious diseases [1]. Within the framework of the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), the third dose of the Diphtheria-Tetanus-Pertussis vaccine (DTP3) serves a dual purpose: it provides essential protection against three fatal bacterial diseases and functions as a critical proxy indicator for the strength, accessibility, and continuity of health systems [2]. Completing the three-dose DTP series requires sustained engagement with healthcare services, making DTP3 coverage a standard metric for monitoring the ability of immunization programs to retain children within the health system beyond the initial point of contact [3]. This metric is central to the current global “zero-dose” agenda championed by Gavi and the WHO, which identifies DTP3 non-recipients as the primary indicator of under-immunized clusters and health system inequity.

Improving vaccination coverage is integral to achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), specifically SDG 3 (“Ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages”). DTP3 coverage is a designated indicator for Target 3.b (Indicator 3.b.1), which aims to provide access to safe, effective, quality, and affordable vaccines for all. Furthermore, increasing immunization rates is a direct mechanism for achieving Target 3.2, which calls for ending preventable deaths of newborns and children under 5 years of age [1].

Despite significant global progress, Somalia remains one of the most challenging environments for healthcare delivery. Decades of protracted conflict, political instability, and climate shocks—ranging from devastating droughts to severe floods—have severely fragmented the health infrastructure [4]. Consequently, Somalia bears one of the highest under-five mortality rates globally, estimated at 106.1 per 1,000 live births, with vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) being a leading contributor [5]. Recent data from the Somali Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS) indicates that full immunization coverage remains critically low, with only approximately 13% of children receiving all basic vaccinations, falling drastically short of the World Health Organization’s (WHO) target of 90% coverage required to achieve herd immunity [1, 6]. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests an ‘urban penalty’ in fragile settings, where rapid urbanization and fragmented services leave the urban poor more vulnerable than some mobile populations.

The barriers to vaccination in Somalia are multifaceted, involving a complex interplay of individual, household, and contextual factors. Previous research has highlighted significant disparities driven by maternal education, household wealth, and maternal age, where children of younger, less educated, and poorer mothers are at the highest risk of non-vaccination [7, 8]. Furthermore, structural determinants such as residence type—particularly among nomadic and rural populations—and regional instability play a pivotal role in limiting access to services [9].

National averages often mask profound sub-national variations. The prevalence of “zero-dose” children (those who have received no routine vaccines) is estimated to be disproportionately high in conflict-affected and inaccessible districts, creating clusters of high disease susceptibility [10, 11]. Recent geospatial modeling in sub-Saharan Africa suggests that DTP3 coverage is a more sensitive indicator of health system continuity than the first dose (DTP1) or BCG [12, 13]. While recent studies have examined general vaccination status in Somalia [14, 15], there is a need for a focused multilevel analysis that specifically isolate the determinants of DTP3 coverage to understand the factors hindering vaccine series completion and health system retention.

Therefore, this study aims to estimate the prevalence of DTP3 vaccination coverage and identify its associated determinants among children aged 0 to 59 months in Somalia. By utilizing a multilevel analysis of national survey data, this research seeks to distinguish between individual-level risks and community-level variances, providing actionable evidence to guide policymakers in designing targeted interventions for the “Reaching Every Child” strategy in fragile settings [16].

Materials and methods

Study design and objective

This cross-sectional analytical study investigated individual- and community-level determinants of the third dose of the Diphtheria-Tetanus-Pertussis (DTP3) vaccine among children aged 0 to 59 months in Somalia. The primary objective was to identify the sociodemographic, maternal, and contextual factors associated with the likelihood of receiving DTP3 vaccination in this population, utilizing a multilevel modeling approach to account for the hierarchical nature of the data (children nested within communities).

Data source

Data were extracted from the nationally representative 2020 Somali Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS). The SHDS employed a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design, where enumeration areas (EAs) were selected as primary sampling units, followed by household selection within each EA [6] The SHDS was implemented by the Somali National Bureau of Statistics (SNBS) in collaboration with the Ministry of Health and Human Services. Data collection took place between February 1, 2018, and January 31, 2019. Ethical approval for the original survey was obtained from the Somalia National Health Research Ethics Committee and the ICF Institutional Review Board. Approval to access the dataset was obtained from the Somali National Data Archive (SoNADA). The data for this study were accessed on December 22, 2024. The dataset contains no individual identifiers that could compromise participant confidentiality, and the data was used for analysis purposes only.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The study focused on living children aged 0 to 59 months. From the original dataset of 2083 children, a sub-sample of children with complete information on DTP3 vaccination status and all key explanatory variables was extracted for analysis. Children with missing data on essential covariates (such as maternal education, birth order, or vaccination cards) were excluded to ensure model stability. The final analytical sample consisted of 1,268 children. To assess potential bias from the 815 excluded observations, a sensitivity analysis was conducted comparing the sociodemographic characteristics (wealth, residence, maternal education) of the included and excluded subsets. No significant differences were found (p > .05), suggesting that the missingness was likely at random and did not substantially bias the results. (See Supplementary Table S1)

Measurement of variables

The selection of individual-level covariates was guided by existing literature on immunization determinants [8, 17, 18]. The binary outcome variable was DTP3 vaccination status. A child was coded as 1 (“Vaccinated”) if they had received the vaccine, as confirmed via “Vaccination date on card,” “Reported by mother,” or “Vaccination marked on card.” Conversely, a child was coded as 0 (“Not Vaccinated”) if they had not received the dose (response recorded as “No”). The explanatory variables were categorized into individual-level and community-level factors: Individual-level factors included maternal characteristics (education level, tetanus toxoid vaccination status, antenatal care [ANC] visits, media exposure, and respondent’s age), child characteristics (sex, age in months, birth order, and birth size), and household characteristics (wealth status). While the SHDS originally calculates wealth in quintiles, for this analysis, they were collapsed into three categories (Poor, Middle, and Rich) to ensure sufficient sample sizes across sub-groups. Community-level factors included the place of residence (urban, rural, or nomadic) and the administrative region. These community factors were assigned to all survey observations within the same cluster (enumeration area) to capture shared environmental and structural influences.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 16. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were calculated to summarize the sample characteristics. To ensure national representativeness, survey weights provided by the SHDS were applied during the analysis. Bivariate associations between each explanatory variable and vaccination status were assessed using simple logistic regression. A significance threshold of P < .05 was used. Multicollinearity was checked using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with all variables showing a VIF < 2.5, indicating no significant multicollinearity. Given that the data consisted of children nested within clusters (communities), a multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression (random intercept model) was performed to account for the between-cluster variability. Fixed effects were reported as Adjusted Odds Ratios (aOR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs). Random effects were evaluated using the Cluster Variance, ICC, Median Odds Ratio (MOR), and Proportional Change in Variance (PCV). The ICC represented the proportion of total variance in vaccination explained by between-cluster differences. The MOR quantified the variation in vaccination probability between clusters. Model fit was assessed using the Log-Likelihood (LL), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better model fit.

Results

DTP3 vaccination prevalence and geographic distribution of coverage

The study analyzed data from a sample of 1,268 children aged 0 to 59 months. The overall prevalence of DTP3 vaccination coverage was 39.51% [95% CI: 36.85–42.23] (n = 501). The remaining 60.49% [95% CI: 57.76–63.14] (n = 767) of children were not vaccinated.

Figure 1 illustrates profound regional inequalities in vaccination uptake. DTP3 coverage varied by over 35% points across the country, ranging from a low of 21.1% [95% CI: 10.9–36.8%] in Togdheer to a high of 56.4% [95% CI: 46.2–66.0%] in Bari. High-coverage clusters were observed in Bari (56.4%), Mudug (51.5% [95% CI: 41.7–61.2%]), and Awdal (51.1% [95% CI: 37.1–64.9%]). Conversely, critically low coverage was concentrated in Gedo (24.2% [95% CI: 12.6–41.5%]), Sool (25.5% [95% CI: 15.7–38.5%]), Woqooyi Galbeed (27.6% [95% CI: 17.6–40.4%]), and Bay (29.1% [95% CI: 18.6–42.4%]). The capital region, Banaadir, exhibited moderate coverage at 36.6% [95% CI: 29.1–44.7%].

Sociodemographic characteristics

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic distribution. Regarding maternal characteristics, the majority of mothers had no formal education (73%). Household wealth status was categorized into three groups (Poor, Middle, and Rich) rather than quintiles to ensure robust sample sizes for analysis; the largest proportion fell into the middle category (50%), followed by the poor (29%) and the rich (21%). A small proportion had attained secondary (6%) or higher (2%) education. Exposure to mass media was low, with 81% of respondents reporting no exposure at all to TV or radio. Antenatal care (ANC) utilization was limited; 47% of mothers had zero visits, while only 14% attended the recommended 3 to 4 visits. Regarding child characteristics, the sample was nearly evenly split by sex (51% male, 49% female). The largest age group represented was 48 to 59 months (37%), followed by 36 to 47 months (25%). Most children were of birth order 2 to 3 (66%). Most deliveries occurred at home (65%) rather than at health facilities. In terms of residence, 43% lived in urban areas, 26% in rural areas, and 31% were nomadic. The sample was distributed across all administrative regions, with Banadir (11%) and Lower Juba (9%) contributing large proportions.

Bivariate analysis

Bivariate logistic regression analyses indicated significant associations (p < .05) between DTP3 vaccination status and several factors. Maternal education was significantly associated with DTP3 uptake (p < .001). Wealth status also showed a strong association (p < .001). ANC visits demonstrated a highly significant relationship (p < .001), as did maternal tetanus vaccination (p < .001). Place of delivery was significant (p = .033). Child age was also significantly related to vaccination status (p = .034). Place of residence showed highly significant differences in vaccination coverage (p < .001). Several variables showed no significant association with DTP3 vaccination status, including sex of the child (p = .393), media exposure (p = .092), respondent’s age (p = .239), birth order (p = .371), birth size (p = .152), and region (p = .474). Maternal health service utilization demonstrated a substantial effect. Antenatal care (ANC) attendance was strongly associated with vaccination status; mothers with 1–2 visits (aOR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.26–2.33) and 3–4 visits (aOR = 2.19, 95% CI: 1.43–3.36) showed significantly higher odds of vaccinating their children compared to those with no visits. Child age was another significant predictor; older children (48–59 months) had markedly greater odds of being vaccinated compared to infants aged 0–11 months (aOR = 4.51, 95% CI: 2.23–9.11). Birth order showed an inverse relationship, where children of birth order four or higher were significantly less likely to be vaccinated compared to first-born children (aOR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.18–0.79).

At the community level, place of residence was significantly associated with vaccination coverage after adjustment. Compared to nomadic communities (reference category), children in rural (aOR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.28–0.57) and urban (aOR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.45–0.91) areas had substantially lower odds of vaccination. Regional disparities were also evident; children in Togdheer (aOR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.11–0.91) and Sool (aOR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.14–0.96) were significantly less likely to be vaccinated than those in Awdal.

Discussion

This study provides a nationally representative, multilevel analysis of the determinants of DTP3 vaccination coverage among children aged 0–59 months in Somalia. The prevalence of DTP3 coverage was found to be 39.51% [95% CI: 36.85–42.23], confirming that Somalia remains significantly below the global average of 84% and the WHO target of 90% required to interrupt disease transmission [18, 19]. This finding is consistent with studies from South Africa (40.8%) [20], in Indonesia (40%) [21], and aligns with other studies using the Somali Demographic and Health Survey (SDHS) data, which report full immunization coverage between 11% and 13% [22, 23]. While our finding is higher than those reported for East Africa (30.79%) [24], Africa as a whole (35.5%) [25], and Ethiopia (30%) [26], it remains substantially lower than rates observed in West African nations like Nigeria (69.6%) [27] and Senegal (92.0%) [28].

Socioeconomic and maternal empowerment

Maternal education and household wealth emerged as the primary predisposing and enabling factors for vaccination, consistent with findings from Ethiopia [17, 29], Kenya [30] and Pakistan [31]. Children of mothers with secondary education were significantly more likely to be vaccinated than those with no education (aOR = 1.83). This association likely reflects higher health literacy and increased autonomy among educated mothers, empowering them to navigate health systems and prioritize preventive care despite structural barriers. Furthermore, the wealth gradient observed—where children from rich households had over twice the odds of vaccination compared to the poor (aOR = 2.41)—highlights that financial barriers remain a primary deterrent. In Somalia, these barriers are multifaceted, encompassing direct costs of transport, indirect opportunity costs from lost wages, and the frequent unaffordability of vaccines at health centers despite official policies [32, 33]. This wealth gradient is a persistent theme in Somali and regional immunization literature [23, 34].

The continuum of care

The study found a strong dose-response relationship between Antenatal Care (ANC) visits and DTP3 uptake. Mothers who attended 3–4 ANC visits were significantly more likely to vaccinate their children compared to those with zero visits (aOR = 2.19). This finding is highly consistent with the concept of the “continuum of care,” where contact with the health system during pregnancy fosters trust and establishes a pathway for postnatal services [35, 36]. ANC visits serve as a critical signal of a mother’s ready access to a health facility and provide essential opportunities for healthcare staff to inform and encourage parents regarding the full immunization schedule [37, 38]. Conversely, the lower odds of vaccination for children of higher birth orders (four and above) compared to first-born children (aOR = 0.38) suggests that as family size increases, maternal time and resources may become more constrained. This finding contradicts some international studies from 48 countries [39]and the United States [40], but aligns with observations in Southeastern Ethiopia [41], where firstborn children often receive more timely care.

The urban penalty and geographic disparities

A pivotal finding of this study is the identification of an “urban penalty,” where children in settled urban (aOR = 0.64) and rural (aOR = 0.40) areas had significantly lower odds of vaccination compared to those in nomadic households. While unexpected compared to other African contexts [8, 9, 42], this likely reflects the successful implementation of intensive mobile health clinics and “Reach Every Child” (REC) outreach strategies that prioritized nomadic clusters during the 2020 survey period. This suggests that while nomadic-targeted interventions are succeeding, the fixed-site health system for settled populations is failing to bridge the gap for the “urban poor.” In cities like Mogadishu, the reliance on fee-for-service private clinics and under-resourced public facilities creates a barrier for the settled poor, who may be currently underserved compared to mobile populations receiving emergency aid [9]. Regional disparities further emphasize this, with regions like Togdheer and Sool showing significantly lower odds of coverage compared to Awdal, likely reflecting sub-national variations in governance, security accessibility, and the presence of humanitarian aid organizations [43].

The association between older child age (48–59 months) and higher vaccination odds compared to infants (0–11 months) points to a reliance on periodic catch-up campaigns rather than the routine EPI schedule [44]. This “delayed vaccination” phenomenon signifies a failure of the routine health system, leaving younger infants unprotected during their most vulnerable window of susceptibility [45]. Furthermore, cultural misconceptions regarding vaccine adverse effects remain a significant barrier to completion [23]. Our analysis confirms that low immunization coverage is a critical public health crisis, contributing to Somalia’s persistently high under-five mortality rate, estimated at 106.1 deaths per 1,000 live births [34].

Policy implications

To address these inequities, the Ministry of Health must move beyond blanket approaches. Policy interventions should prioritize the integration of DTP3 catch-up doses into routine ANC visits to capitalize on existing maternal health contacts. Furthermore, the successful nomadic mobile unit model should be adapted to reach underserved urban-poor settlements and IDP camps that are currently bypassed by static infrastructure. Strengthening fixed-site public facilities in urban centers is essential to ensure they are as accessible and proactive as the mobile units serving nomadic groups. These strategies are vital for achieving the 2030 Immunization Agenda and reducing the burden of vaccine-preventable diseases in Somalia.

Limitations

Some limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of temporal precedence or definitive causal links between the identified determinants and vaccination status. Second, reliance on maternal recall for vaccination status creates a risk of recall bias, potentially overestimating coverage, although this is a known challenge in all DHS surveys. Third, the temporal gap between the 2020 data collection and the current context means that recent acute shocks are not reflected. Finally, while approximately 800 observations were excluded due to missing data, our sensitivity analysis confirmed no significant sociodemographic differences between the included and excluded groups, suggesting that selection bias is minimal.

Conclusion

This multivariable multilevel logistic regression analysis confirms that DTP3 vaccination coverage in Somalia is critically low and deeply inequitable. The 39.51% coverage rate represents a profound health system failure that leaves millions of children vulnerable to preventable death and disease. The identification of an “urban penalty” and the success of nomadic outreach provide a critical roadmap for future interventions. Vaccination status is shaped by a complex hierarchy of maternal education, wealth, healthcare engagement, and geographic residence.

Recommendations

To address this crisis, the Somali Ministry of Health, in collaboration with the WHO and humanitarian partners, must move beyond blanket approaches and implement equity-focused, multi-pronged strategies:

Integrate Immunization into the Continuum of Care: The Ministry of Health should mandate the integration of DTP3 catch-up screening and doses into all routine ANC and postnatal visits to capitalize on existing maternal health contacts.
