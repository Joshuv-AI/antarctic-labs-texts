Applying liver transplant techniques in complex liver resection for patients with hilar cholangiocarcinoma: in situ hypothermic portal perfusion without VV bypass
Authors: Hongming Xing, Xinyu You, Jianhua Huang, Yu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBismuth–Corlette type IV hilar cholangiocarcinoma (HCCA) with invasion beyond the bifurcation of the anterior and posterior branches of the right portal vein, defined as the P-point, poses a significant challenge for radical resection due to its extensive involvement of the biliary ducts and vascular structures. However, radiotherapy and chemotherapy provide limited response rates for such HCCA patients. Based on the Mayo Clinic protocol, neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with living donor liver transplantation has shown better prognosis in Bismuth-Corlette type IV HCCA, the shortage of donor livers and high costs are major obstacles. Radical resection remains crucial for Bismuth-Corlette type IV HCCA. Ex vivo liver resection and autotransplantation complicates vascular reconstruction for patients with portal vein invasion, while in situ hypothermic perfusion enables tumor resection with total blood flow occlusion, minimizing bleeding and aiding recovery. For patients needing liver vascular exclusion over 60 min, especially with chronic liver disease, hypothermic perfusion is recommended. Therefore, our team attempted to explore the feasibility of in situ hypothermic perfusion techniques in treating Bismuth-Corlette type IV HCCA with invasion beyond the P point of the portal vein.Case presentationWe report a case of radical resection of Bismuth-Corlette type IV HCCA with invasion beyond the P point of the portal vein achieved through in situ hypothermic perfusion technique. Preoperative neoadjuvant therapy was administered with gemcitabine plus cisplatin for four cycles. Radical resection of the lesion and vessels took 10.5 h, with 90 min of liver ischemia. Postoperative pathology showed well-differentiated HCCA, 4.5 × 4.3 cm, negative microvascular invasion, margins > 5 mm, and no lymph node metastasis. The patient recovered well, had no major complications, was discharged after 14 days, and follow-up showed CA199 reduced to 136 U/mL. Chemotherapy started two months later with gemcitabine and oxaliplatin for four cycles. The patient has been monitored for two years without recurrence or complications.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that in situ hypothermic perfusion techniques can achieve radical resection of Bismuth-Corlette type IV HCCA with invasion beyond the P point of the portal vein. This surgical approach broadens the surgical treatment options for Bismuth-Corlette type IV HCCA and similar malignancies.Keywords: Hilar cholangiocarcinoma, Hypothermic perfusion technique, Vascular reconstruction

Background

HCCA is the most common extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, accounting for more than 50% of intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [1]. It is complex in terms of its anatomical location, difficult to resect and insensitive to radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Therefore, only approximately 1/5 of HCCA patients meet the surgical indications at the initial diagnosis [2], especially for advanced HCCA, such as Bismuth‒Corlette IV HCCA, for which surgical resection is very difficult, and when the degree of tumor invasion exceeds the bifurcation of the anterior and posterior branches of the right portal vein, defined as the P-point [3], portal vein resection reaches the limit point, and radical resection is difficult to achieve [4–7]. However, the in situ hypothermic perfusion technique was used to increase liver tolerance to the anemic phase and complete radical resection of tumors with complex vascular invasion [8–11]. As a tertiary academic center with organ transplantation and oncology units, we leveraged our vast experience in complex surgeries to investigate the viability of in situ hypothermic perfusion technique for treating Bismuth-Corlette type IV HCCA with portal vein P-point invasion. This case report provides real-world evidence to support broader clinical application.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old male patient was admitted for “epigastric pain for approximately 1 month.” The laboratory test results were as follows: CA199 > 12,000 U/mL (≤ 43 U/mL), CEA 9.04 ng/mL (≤ 5.0 ng/mL), and TBIL 148 µmol/L (< 26 µmol/L). The ICG-R15 was 6.3%. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a mass shadow at the junction of the right lobe and the caudate lobe, measuring approximately 41 mm×32 mm (Fig. 1a). Three-dimensional reconstruction revealed that the lesion invaded the right portal vein P point (Fig. 1b), the hilar bile duct, and the bile duct dilated (Fig. 1c), Notably, the right hepatic artery originated aberrantly from the superior mesenteric artery.

a Enhanced CT image showing tumor invasion of the portal vein; b Three-dimensional reconstruction showing that the tumor invaded more than the P point; c the tumor was located at the confluence of the bile ducts and invaded the bilateral hepatic ducts

Because TBIL was 2 times higher than the upper limit of the normal value, PTCD was subsequently performed under ultrasound guidance, and the puncture site was the S6 bile duct. After 2 weeks, the TBIL concentration decreased to 34.6 µmol/L, and then, the following neoadjuvant therapy was given: gemcitabine (1000 mg/m2), d1, and cisplatin (70 mg/m2), d8, every 3 weeks, and chemotherapy for 4 cycles. After an MDT discussion, the patient had Bismuth‒Corlette type IV HCCA, the degree of tumor invasion exceeded the P point of the residual hepatic portal vein, traditional surgery was difficult to perform for radical resection, and neoadjuvant therapy was not effective; thus, hepatectomy under hypothermic perfusion of the portal vein in situ was planned.

The first hepatic hilum was clamped following the in situ priority principle. Hepatic transection along the ischemic demarcation plane revealed tumor invasion extending beyond the P-point of the right portal vein branch, corroborating the findings of preoperative imaging. The LHV was isolated (2–3 cm) for outflow preservation prior to division. Subsequent caudate lobe dissection and parenchymal transection achieved complete left hemihepatectomy, leaving the tumor-infiltrated portal vein as the sole remnant connection(Fig. 2b). After the right hepatic artery, portal vein, superior hepatic vena cava and inferior hepatic vena cava were clamped, the portal vein affected by the lesion was resected. During this process, 4 °C UW solution was administered via the anterior and posterior right portal vein branches (Fig. 2c). The left hepatic vein stump served as the outflow tract for the UW solution (Fig. 2a). The surface of the right liver was cooled using iced gauze. Intraoperative frozen-section pathological examination of the right hepatic duct stump revealed no tumor. Subsequently, the lesion was completely resected, and lymph nodes surrounding the common hepatic artery and adjacent common bile duct were cleared. The main portal vein trunk and its anterior and posterior right branches exhibited a defect > 180° at point P with an irregular surface (Fig. 2d). Therefore, a bovine pericardial biological patch was used to repair the defect (Fig. 2e). The infusion tube was removed before initiating portal vein reconstruction. After completing the reconstruction, warm Lactated Ringer’s solution was administered intravenously. The first hepatic hilum was opened to flush out the intrahepatic UW solution. Then, the divided left hepatic vein, which served as the hepatic outflow for perfusion, was clamped. The clamped inferior vena cava was opened, and the abdominal cavity was irrigated with warm saline according to protocol until the liver core temperature reached ≥ 36 °C, after which circulatory stability was achieved. The surgery was completed after the hepaticojejunal basin anastomoses were finished (Fig. 2i). The operation time was 10.5 h. The total liver ischemia time was 90 min, which consisted of a 25-minute warm-ischemic interval achieved by the Pringle maneuver and a 65-minute period of TVE. The TVE duration included 24 min of in situ hypothermic perfusion and 28 min dedicated to portal vein reconstruction. There were no surgeries or anesthesia-related complications. The postoperative pathological report revealed highly differentiated HCCA, the size of the lesion was 4.5 × 4.3 cm, microvascular invasion was negative, the negative margin was > 5 mm, and there was no lymph node metastasis (0/2). Two months post-surgery, the patient achieved uneventful recovery and initiated adjuvant GemOx (gemcitabine 1000 mg/m², oxaliplatin 80 mg/m², day 1, q14d × 4 cycles). During the two-year follow-up period, the patient demonstrated favorable prognosis, with no recurrence or severe complications observed.

Discussion

Bismuth‒Corlette type IV HCCA with tumor invasion extending to the P-point of the portal vein is widely regarded as a condition that poses significant challenges for achieving curative resection [4–7]., neoadjuvant therapy, even when attempting to facilitate subsequent surgical resection, has demonstrated limited clinical efficacy in achieving tumor downstaging or improving resectability rates [1–6]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with allogeneic liver transplantation via the Mayo Clinic protocol has achieved a good prognosis in patients with Bismuth‒Corlette IV HCCA [12], but the shortage of donor livers and high cost remain as obstacles. Radical resection by surgery remains an important option for Bismuth‒Corlette IV HCCA. In situ hypothermic perfusion technology enables complex tumor resection to be performed under total hepatic blood flow occlusion, reducing intraoperative bleeding, clearing the surgical field, and facilitating intraoperative liver protection and postoperative functional recovery [13]– [14]. Moreover, in situ perfusion greatly prolonged the tolerance of the liver to ischemia, for which total liver occlusion was reported to be expected. When TVE >60 min is anticipated, especially in patients with chronic liver disease, hypothermic perfusion technology is recommended [8–11]. Compared with in situ cold perfusion, VVB optimizes hepatic perfusion via shunted blood flow, effectively mitigating ischemia risk. In complex hepatectomy procedures, the VVB maintains sustained perfusion, reducing the incidence of intraoperative liver injury. However, its implementation requires specialized equipment and technical expertise, increasing procedural complexity and associated costs [15].

Leveraging the proven oncological safety of TVE for lesions encroaching on the second porta hepatis, we have translated this paradigm to tumors with predominant first-portal invasion [16]. The present case corroborates the technical feasibility and oncologic validity of this extension, underscoring its merit for prospective, protocol-driven evaluation.

In-situ hypothermic perfusion mitigates ischemic injury, extends safe clamp time, and enables radical resection with reconstruction of hitherto irreparable vessels; long-term outcomes show negligible hepatic failure or portal-vein thrombosis [8].

Conclusions

Despite extensive invasion of the portal vein and biliary confluence, enabled by in situ hypothermic portal perfusion, this patient underwent en-bloc tumour clearance with synchronous vascular resection and reconstruction during a brief warm-ischaemic interval. This approach is likely to mitigate postoperative morbidity and the risk of post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF). The successful translation of liver-transplant techniques to this setting mandates sophisticated institutional expertise and seamless multidisciplinary collaboration; its broader applicability requires prospective validation and consensual refinement.
