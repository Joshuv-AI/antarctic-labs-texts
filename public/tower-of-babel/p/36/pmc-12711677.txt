Aqueous humor biomarkers in ocular oncology: Insights and advances
Authors: Lara Cheslow, Signe Caksa, Rachel E Cain, Scott A Waldman, Andrew E Aplin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryOcular malignancies, though relatively rare, pose significant risks to patients’ vision, quality of life, and potential for developing metastatic disease. Due to the eye’s complicated and delicate anatomy, diagnosing ocular malignancy and monitoring its progression can be technically challenging. Current approaches include direct tumor biopsy, vitreous humor sampling, and imaging. However, these techniques are associated with rare, yet potentially severe, side effects and can be invasive or limited in detecting microscopic disease. Recent studies have introduced aqueous humor sampling as a promising, minimally invasive alternative to diagnose ocular malignancy, guide therapeutic management, monitor response to treatment, and track early recurrence. As oncologic biomarker research continues to progress, incorporating aqueous humor sampling into clinical practice may refine diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapeutic approaches to enhance patient outcomes. This article reviews the latest advances in aqueous humor biomarkers in common ocular malignancies, including uveal melanoma, intraocular lymphoma, and retinoblastoma.

Introduction

Ocular malignancies are currently diagnosed through clinical examinations and imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography, ultrasound, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and fluorescein angiography.1,2 In ambiguous cases, direct tumor biopsies are performed, but this procedure carries risks of vision loss and retinal detachment and is contraindicated in certain cancers such as retinoblastoma (Rb) due to risk of tumor seeding and dissemination.3,4 Furthermore, direct tumor biopsies are invasive, limiting their utility in monitoring response to therapy, and are typically unable to detect microscopic tumor residuals or recurrences before they become apparent on imaging. Over the past few decades, sampling vitreous humor, the gel-like substance that comprises most of the eye, has been explored as an alternative approach for diagnosing borderline malignancies and monitoring therapeutic responses.5 However, due to its substantial volume, vitreous humor dilutes cancer markers, reducing biopsy sensitivity. Moreover, while relatively safe, sampling vitreous humor risks serious adverse consequences, including retinal detachment and vitreoretinal traction or hemorrhage.6

Aqueous humor (AH), the clear 0.2 to 0.3 mL of fluid occupying the anterior chamber (AC) of the eye between the cornea and the lens, offers an accessible target for liquid biopsy via AC paracentesis (Figure 1).7 Due to its small volume, AH is a relatively concentrated reservoir of biomarkers that reflects broader intraocular pathology, and unlike vitreous humor sampling, obtaining AH is minimally invasive with a lower risk of adverse events. A recent analysis of over 1,200 AH biopsies in pediatric patients found one procedure that induced a self-resolving subconjunctival hemorrhage, resulting in an overall complication rate of 0.08%. No moderate or severe complications, such as vision loss or tumor seeding, were identified over a 16-month follow-up.8

Uveal melanoma

Uveal melanoma (UM) is the most common intraocular malignancy in adults and the most fatal eye cancer.11 UM arises from the transformation of melanocytes, neural crest-derived and pigment-producing cells found in the uveal tract of the eye (Figure 3A). A majority of UM form in the choroid (90%), with fewer tumors arising from the ciliary body (6%) or iris (4%).11,12 The malignant transformation of uveal melanocytes is associated with chromosomal alterations, including chromosome (chr) 3 monosomy and gain of the long arm of chr8, and gene mutations in G protein-coupled receptor signaling components, including G protein subunit alpha-q (GNAQ), G protein subunit alpha-11 (GNA11), phospholipase C beta-4, and cysteinyl leukotriene receptor 213; mutually exclusive GNAQ/GNA11 mutations occur in the vast majority of UM and lead to constitutive activation of the Gαq/11 GTPases.11 Additional genetic events—including inactivation or loss of BRCA1-associated deubiquitinase 1 (BAP1), a histone deubiquitinase; mutations in splicing factor 3b subunit-1 (SF3B1), an RNA splicing factor; and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 1A X-linked, a translation initiation protein—are associated with UM progression and metastatic potential.14 Gene expression profiling (GEP) using the DecisionDx-UM test, which measures the expression levels of 15 UM-related genes in tumor tissue, is used to stratify primary UM tumors according to metastatic risk.15 GEP class 1 tumors have low risk of metastasis, whereas GEP class 2 tumors have elevated metastatic risk. Although <3% of patients initially present with metastatic disease, ∼50% of all patients develop distant metastasis, most commonly to the liver (70%–90% of metastatic UM), often years to decades after their initial diagnosis.16 Whereas 5-year relative survival rates for primary UM are approximately 85%, this falls to 67% and 16% for patients with regional and distant metastasis, respectively.17

Most cases of UM are diagnosed in older adults 50–70 years of age, with up to one-third of patients asymptomatic at presentation.18 More commonly, patients with primary tumors experience monocular blurry vision, visual field defects, photopsia, or complete vision loss.19 The diagnosis of primary UM relies on physical examination findings via slit-lamp biomicroscopy, indirect ophthalmoscopy, gonioscopy, and transillumination to examine the anterior and posterior eye.11,19 Ultrasonography and ultrasound biomicroscopy are used to assess the size, characteristics, and local invasion of UM, whereas fluorescein angiography can determine tumor vascularity.11 OCT may be employed in the diagnosis of small or early-stage UM,20 whereas MRI is rarely used to assess for local invasion.21 In cases of diagnostic uncertainty, a more invasive approach with fine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) is often required,22 and this is not always successful.23,24 Despite the plethora of diagnostic modalities available for primary UM, missed or incorrect diagnoses occur and can delay access to eye-conserving care.18,25

Current treatment

Treatment of primary UM is effective at preventing primary tumor recurrence in ∼85% of cases and has trended away from local surgical resection or enucleation toward eye-conserving radiation therapy, particularly plaque brachytherapy and particle beam radiation.26,27 Long-term patient outcomes with enucleation are similar to those with radiation therapy,28 such that the former approach is typically reserved for cases of eye-conserving treatment failure, large tumor size, or optic nerve involvement.26 Patients who are asymptomatic or have small, slow-growing UM tumors may instead undergo observation with frequent imaging.29 Additionally, given the risk of distant metastasis, patients with primary UM undergo staging work-up and long-term post-treatment surveillance to evaluate for distant metastasis.30 Tebentafusp/Kimmtrak, a T cell receptor-bispecific fusion protein, recently became the first US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved therapy for unresectable or metastatic UM.31,32 This biologic recognizes glycoprotein 100 on UM cells and CD3 on T cells, bringing the two in proximity and promoting T cell-mediated killing of cancer cells. Of note, this therapy is only available to human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A∗02:01-positive patients. In 2023, the FDA also approved the melphalan/hepatic delivery system for liver-directed chemotherapy based on results from the phase 3 FOCUS study.33 Targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors that are widely used for cutaneous melanoma have shown little efficacy in treating UM.28

Diagnostic biomarkers

The accurate and prompt diagnosis of UM is critical for patients to receive eye-conserving therapy and appropriate metastatic work-up. AH sampling is emerging as a potential minimally invasive diagnostic approach for UM, with elevated levels of angiogenic proteins and inflammatory markers associated with UM (Figure 3A); both processes are associated with UM progression.11 An early study showed the feasibility of using differential protein expression in AH to identify UM from non-UM eyes with 89% sensitivity.34 Several subsequent studies have found elevated levels of vascular endothelial growth factor type A (VEGF-A) in AH from enucleated UM eyes, as compared to eyes from patients undergoing cataract surgery,35,36,37,38 findings that are replicated when obtaining AH intraoperatively.39 VEGF-A levels in AH correlated with tumor thickness.35,38,40 Angiogenin, placental growth factor (PLGF), and stromal-derived growth factor-1 are additional angiogenic proteins found in AH that differentiate UM from benign pigmented intraocular tumors or cataract eyes37,41,42; thus, a panel of angiogenic proteins, rather than a single marker, may be helpful in UM diagnosis. Several studies have also identified growth factors, including epidermal growth factor, basic fibroblast growth factor, and nerve growth factor-beta (NGF-β), that are elevated in AH from patients with UM.37,43 More recently, proteins specific to UM were evaluated in a prospective case-control study of AH retrieved intraoperatively from patients with UM and healthy controls undergoing cataract surgery. GNAQ and SF3B1 were found to be increased in AH from UM eyes; unexpectedly, BAP1 expression was also increased, despite being frequently lost or inactivated in UM, particularly in advanced disease including in 90% of metastatic cases.44,45 The authors hypothesized that elevated BAP1 expression in the AH of patients with UM could reflect aberrant cytoplasmic localization of this nuclear protein, which can occur in association with BAP1 mutations; alternatively, BAP1 levels may be increased due to local tissue destruction by the primary tumor, causing release of protein from nearby non-malignant cells.44 UM protein expression in the AH was not associated with patient or tumor characteristics; however, samples clustered according to elevated GNAQ and BAP1 versus elevated SF3B1 expression, with the GNAQ plus BAP1 cluster associated with chr3 monosomy as determined by cytogenetic analysis of tumor sampled via FNAB.44

Beyond these tumorigenic proteins, inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-8 are increased in AH from UM eyes as compared to control eyes37,38,39,41,43,46; the concentration of each cytokine positively correlated with retinal detachment and tumor thickness, respectively, parameters that are associated with disease burden in primary UM.43,46 The chemokines monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), interferon gamma-induced protein 10, and chemokine C-C motif ligand 5 (CCL5, also known as RANTES) are also elevated in UM,37,38,39,41,46 with one study finding an almost 10-fold increase in MCP-1 in AH from UM versus cataract eyes.43 For diagnostic utility, these inflammatory markers would need to be contextualized according to a patient’s clinical presentation, given that they can also be elevated in AH with uveitis.47 Of note, the studies listed earlier have small samples sizes, predominantly use cataract control groups, and employ narrow proteomic analysis techniques, including ELISAs or bead arrays, to assess for differential protein expression in AH.48 Future studies examining AH could consider differentiating UM from other ocular malignancies and utilizing larger proteomic arrays or alternative screening techniques such as digital PCR, to provide further diagnostic insights.49 This could bolster efforts to incorporate machine learning approaches in AH analysis for the diagnosis of ophthalmic conditions, including UM.50

Prognostic biomarkers

UM prognostication requires an intraocular biopsy or resection so that chromosomal, genetic, molecular, and histological analysis can be performed on tumor tissue. FNAB is associated with a risk of hemorrhage and retinal detachment23,51; thus, less invasive AH sampling may be beneficial for evaluating patient prognosis and metastatic risk. One study examined 90 AH samples from enucleated UM eyes, finding that cytokine expression in AH clusters according to low, medium, and high expression of inflammatory markers.52 Of note, the cluster with high cytokine expression was associated with worse prognostic markers, including increased tumor thickness, higher tumor stage, ciliary body involvement, and the presence of chr3 monosomy or gain of chr8q, parameters determined by histologic and cytogenetic analysis of tumor tissue.52 Patients with high cytokine expression had significantly worse survival than those with low cytokine expression.52 Similarly, the presence of soluble HLA in the AH of enucleated UM eyes, tissue expression of which is an unfavorable prognostic indicator for UM, was associated with increased tumor size, chr3 monosomy, gain of chr8q, and inactivation of BAP1.45,53 Soluble HLA in the AH of patients with UM also was associated with worse disease-related survival.53 These studies suggest that AH sampling could be used to predict survival in UM. Proteomic analysis of intraoperative AH samples obtained from treatment-naive patients was able to distinguish GEP class 1 and GEP class 2 tumors based on 45 differentially expressed proteins.54 Pathway analysis of these proteins revealed that AH from elevated metastatic risk, GEP class 2 tumors had increased expression of inflammatory response, cell growth, and cell motility proteins similar to the proteomic analyses described in the diagnostic biomarkers section.54 AH liquid biopsy could be particularly impactful for GEP classification of primary UM, given the possibility of discordance between GEP classes on repeat FNABs.55 Further studies that relate AH profiling to actual metastatic progression, rather than risk, could position AH as an additional method for metastatic surveillance.

Therapeutic biomarkers

Beyond diagnosis and prognosis, AH liquid biopsy has the potential to track therapy response in UM. Two studies examined the expression of cytokines in AH from patients with UM before and after radiation therapy. A study of 20 patients undergoing combined Ru-106 brachytherapy plus transpupillary thermotherapy revealed increased expression of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-8, and IL-1β following treatment, and the levels of IL-6 and IL-8 positively correlated with radiation dose.46 AH from 18 patients with UM undergoing I-125 plaque therapy showed elevations of angiogenic proteins VEGF-A and PLGF following treatment but not IL-8.40 Thus, AH markers of interest may differ according to therapeutic modality.

In addition to examining protein expression, AH sampling also can be used to detect genomic biomarkers, such as cell-free circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) or microRNA (miRNA) in UM eyes. The feasibility of this was first demonstrated in animal models of UM, where mutant GNAQ ctDNA was detected following in vivo tumor inoculation and correlated with tumor size.56,57 In humans, unprocessed analytes including double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and miRNA were present in a majority of AH samples from 66 UM eyes (94.1% and 88.0%, respectively) and were elevated compared to control AH from glaucoma or cataract patients.58 Genomic analytes could be detected in the AH of even small (<2 mm) UM tumors, which can be challenging to cytologically analyze and molecularly profile, due to difficulty collecting sufficient sample via FNAB.59 The concentration of dsDNA, miRNA, and protein in AH was positively correlated with tumor stage and trended toward a positive association with GEP class,58 suggesting prognostic value of these markers in UM. When examining the levels of genomic analytes in AH from UM before and after brachytherapy, two studies found that ctDNA, dsDNA, and miRNA concentrations were increased in post-treatment samples.58,60 Of note, analysis of somatic copy number alterations (SCNAs) and targeted sequencing of post-radiation AH samples revealed chr3 monosomy, chr8q gain, and UM-associated GNAQ and BAP1 mutations,59 suggesting that genetic material sampled in the AH is tumor derived and may increase with treatment due to tumor cell death.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) also can be isolated from the AH of patients with UM.61,62 Although there was no difference in the concentration of EVs between patients with UM and controls,61,62 EV subpopulations were more diverse in UM samples.61 Paired analysis of EVs from the AH of 19 patients with UM before and after brachytherapy showed a trend toward increased EV concentration, as well as increased CD63/81+ and CD9/CD63/81+ EV subpopulations and a decreased CD63+ EV subpopulation.61 Thus, the authors hypothesized that CD63/81+ and CD9/CD63/81+ EVs may be tumor derived and represent UM biomarkers.

An increase in angiogenic or inflammatory markers, genetic materials, and EVs in AH is associated with disease burden and radiation therapy in primary UM and could be utilized to stratify patients according to therapeutic response or risk of therapy-associated side effects in the future. Additional studies would be required to compare the clinical utility of AH for evaluating UM relative to other liquid biopsy techniques, including vitreous humor and blood sampling.63 Although AH has been examined in the context of radiation therapy, there have been no studies to date that evaluate changes in AH with tebentafusp treatment. A planned phase 2 clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT06414590) will examine the ability of neoadjuvant tebentafusp to shrink treatment-naive unresectable primary UM. The study design includes collecting AH and detecting ctDNA at time of study screening and post treatment, with the goal of correlating AH ctDNA with tumor response to tebentafusp. Overall, AH biomarkers could prove impactful for the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment planning of UM and provide new insights into UM biology.

Intraocular lymphoma

IOL is a rare ocular malignancy affecting 0.047 out of 100,000 people, although its incidence is rising as immune-compromised individuals gain greater access to life-prolonging care.64,65 Primary IOL (PIOL) typically originates from vitreoretinal tissue and less commonly from uveal and optic nerve tissue.66 Secondary IOL is derived from metastatic lymphoma from non-ocular sources and occurs in fewer than 5% of systemic lymphomas.67 The majority of IOL is B cell type, is most prevalent in elderly females, and progresses from monocular to binocular in half of patients.64,66,68 Common findings on initial presentation include blurry vision (72% of patients with IOL), vitreous inflammatory opacities (92% of IOL patients), and hyper-reflective foci in posterior vitreous (53% of patients with IOL).66 Due to similarities among presenting symptoms, early imaging findings, and initial responsiveness to steroids, IOL is often mistaken for uveitis and may progress insidiously.64,66 The time from symptom onset to diagnosis varies widely but is often delayed by months to years, with an average diagnostic delay of 1–2 years.66,69 In part due to the prolonged time to diagnosis, central nervous system (CNS) involvement is common in PIOL; while only 15%–25% of primary CNS lymphomas have ocular manifestations, 56%–90% of patients with PIOL have CNS involvement.64,70 Unfortunately, progression of lymphoma to the CNS decreases the 5-year survival rate of patients with IOL from 97% (when lymphoma is confined to eye) to 54% and increases the recurrence rate from 20% to 70%.66 Therefore, early accurate identification is critical to patient outcomes.

Current treatment

IOL treatment strategies are tailored based on the extent of the disease and CNS involvement. For cases confined to the eye without detectable CNS disease, local therapies such as intravitreal injections of methotrexate and rituximab are the cornerstone of treatment.71 When CNS involvement is present, patients are treated with systemic high-dose methotrexate-based chemotherapy, which aims to address both ocular and CNS disease.71,72 Radiotherapy serves as an adjunct or alternative in specific scenarios, particularly when chemotherapy is contraindicated or in cases of localized orbital lymphoma.71,72

Emerging aqueous humor biomarkers in intraocular lymphoma

The gold standard for diagnosing IOL is vitreous cytology, which involves an invasive liquid biopsy performed under anesthesia in an operating room.70,73,74,75 When cells are obtained during biopsy, flow cytometry identifying B cell markers in vitreous humor has 82.4% sensitivity and 100% specificity for diagnosing IOL.76 However, the true sensitivity of vitreous cytology ranges from 30% to 70% due to sparse tumor cells in the vitreous, rapid degeneration of malignant cells in vitreous fluid, and steroid treatment prior to biopsy leading to lymphoma cell lysis and reduced cellular yield.77,78

AH sampling has emerged as a more accessible potential approach to diagnose and monitor IOL. Like in vitreous cytology, flow cytometry and histopathology on cells isolated from AH can directly identify lymphoma through B cell markers like CD19, CD20, and CD22 and can detect mutations in the myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MYD88) gene, a widely recognized marker of lymphoma.79,80

Recently, studies exploring the clinical utility of AH biopsy have expanded to include extracellular markers available in the samples, with most of the focus on cytokines associated with B cell activity (Figure 3B). Elevated IL-10 levels in the AH distinguish IOL from uveitis, where IL-10 is typically absent, and increasing levels of IL-10 strongly correlate with diagnostic specificity.66,81,82,83 Conversely, high IL-6 levels favor an inflammatory or infectious uveitis process over lymphoma. The IL-10/IL-6 ratio further enhances diagnostic accuracy, with a ratio greater than 1 strongly correlating with IOL, while a ratio below 1 suggests uveitis.75 A recent retrospective study analyzing IL-10 and IL-6 levels within over 1,000 samples of AH from controls and patients with IOL found a diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of 90.6% and 99.3%, respectively.84 ctDNA isolated from AH has also demonstrated diagnostic potential; recent studies have found that both cell-free and cell-based MYD88 L265P mutations are identifiable in 83% of patients with IOL.85 A separate smaller study determined that the ability to detect MYD88 L265P mutations was comparable between AH versus vitreous humor samples; out of 11 patients with IOL with MYD88 L265P mutations found in the vitreous humor, 10 were identified on AH analysis.86 Beyond accurately identifying IOL, changes in AH biomarkers may reflect response to therapy, offering AH monitoring as a potential method to guide long-term treatment. In one study, 87.5% of patients with residual IL-10 levels in AH following treatment experienced ocular relapse within 5 years,82 and several case reports found declining levels of MYD88 L265P mutations in AH-derived DNA over the course of IOL treatment.85,87 Further investigation is required to define pathologic thresholds of IOL markers within AH biopsies and determine which asymptomatic patients could benefit from extended therapy.

Retinoblastoma

Rb is the most common pediatric ocular malignancy, accounting for 3% of all childhood cancers.7 Rb is most frequently caused by inactivating mutations in the tumor suppressor gene RB1, which may be familial (45%) or sporadic (55%), and the resulting tumor presents unilaterally (84%) or bilaterally (16%).88,89 At diagnosis, Rb most often presents with leukocoria, or loss of the red reflex, and/or strabismus.89,90,91 More advanced tumors may also present with proptosis and iris neovascularization, which are generally associated with poor prognosis. Rb is typically diagnosed clinically and confirmed with indirect ophthalmoscopy, a full fundoscopic exam including photography, and MRI to identify potential invasion into neighboring structures such as the optic nerve and brain.87,88 Because the overwhelming majority of patients with Rb are younger than 5 years of age and cannot comply with comprehensive ophthalmic examinations, thorough diagnostic and prognostic determinations are typically performed under general anesthesia. A recent study determined that the median total exposure time to anesthesia for pediatric patients with Rb is 713 min, and the impact of this exposure on neurodevelopment is an active area of investigation.92

Current treatment

In developed countries, the survival rate of Rb patients approaches 99%. However, this favorable outcome frequently comes with significant sacrifice. Although eye-salvaging treatments including intravitreal chemotherapy, local tumor destruction, and rarely, targeted radiation have become more popular in recent years, primary enucleation is recognized as the most curative approach and is performed in 40%–66% of patients with Rb.93 Even after enucleation, 24% of patients are at risk for systemic metastasis, necessitating the subsequent use of adjuvant therapy in a select population with high-risk tumors.94

The decision of whether a patient needs eye-salvaging therapy, enucleation alone, or enucleation with adjuvant therapy is based on clinical judgment. There are no established biomarkers for identifying chemotherapy-sensitive vs. -resistant tumors, and there is little agreement in the field on which tumor characteristics warrant the use of adjuvant chemotherapy post enucleation.94 Given the lack of consensus on treatment guidelines, current approaches run the risk of both over- and under-treating Rb. A major challenge in selecting the appropriate primary approach in advanced Rb presentations is the absolute contraindication to directly sampling the tumor due to the risk of seeding malignancy into extraocular structures, precluding the ability to predict resistance to chemotherapy prior to prolonged treatment.3 Furthermore, trialing chemotherapy in patients with Rb with advanced, aggressive tumors does not yield a benefit in mortality or eye salvage rates, and the prolonged time to enucleation increases the likelihood of systemic metastasis.95 Therefore, accessible biomarkers that identify chemotherapy-resistant tumors at diagnosis would be immensely valuable in weighing the risk of prolonging time to enucleation against the realistic likelihood of eye salvage in patients who do not need more invasive treatments.

Emerging aqueous humor biomarkers in retinoblastoma

Over the past decade, AH sampling in Rb has gained momentum as a potential surrogate tumor biopsy in intact eyes prior to enucleation, and clinical trials are underway to assess its diagnostic and prognostic value (Figure 3C) (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04959097). Beyond the possible utility in risk stratification, validating a reliable, longitudinal AH biomarker could potentially reduce exposure time of pediatric patients to general anesthesia. ctDNA is among the most promising Rb AH biomarkers identified to indicate tumor burden and susceptibility to treatment, and a longitudinal study recently revealed that an increase of ≥15% of AH-derived ctDNA from the time of Rb diagnosis correlates with a 90-fold increased likelihood of enucleation.96,97

Further analysis of ctDNA has identified two major emerging markers of aggressive, resistant tumors that are likely to warrant enucleation without a trial of chemotherapy. The first of these markers is ctDNA SCNAs, which reflect genomic instability. Pioneering research by Berry et al. examining molecular signatures in Rb eyes found significantly elevated SCNAs in the AH of 92% of enucleated eyes compared to 38% of salvaged eyes.98 The same group recently concluded a 5-year prospective cohort study that analyzed ctDNA within the AH from 26 eyes of 21 patients at the time of Rb diagnosis. In line with their previous findings, eyes with greater genomic instability, particularly with gain of chr6p within ctDNA, were associated with a 10-fold increased likelihood of enucleation.7 A separate case study analyzing ctDNA in a patient with bilateral Rb revealed a similar result; while the patient’s left eye had a chr6p gain 1.8× the limit of normal and ultimately required enucleation, the right eye had no such elevation and was salvaged with globe-sparing therapies.88 This finding aligns with established research correlating increased chr6p with more aggressive histopathological findings in enucleated Rb eyes. Gains in ctDNA chr6p parallel higher degrees of anaplasia, necrosis, and focal invasion of ocular structures in histological Rb tumor analysis, indicating high-risk malignancies that are more likely to necessitate enucleation and possible adjuvant therapy.7,99,100,101

The second major emerging AH marker that may identify Rb resistant to chemotherapy is ctDNA proto-oncogene MYCN. Increased copy numbers of MYCN are associated with larger, more aggressive tumors with a propensity for invading the optic nerve, and across multiple small-scale retrospective and prospective studies, MYCN amplifications in AH have proved highly predictive of enucleation.101,102,103 Although further studies are necessary before SCNAs and MYCN amplifications can be officially recommended, the Children’s Hospital of Los Angeles has already adopted these two promising biomarkers as prognostic indicators for patients at the time of Rb diagnosis. Beyond aiding risk stratification and therapeutic response monitoring, the data collected from this ongoing effort will be tremendously helpful in validating these markers in the clinical setting.104,105 Further analysis of AH-isolated ctDNA has suggested the potential of AH sampling to track response to therapy. In Rb, ctDNA levels in AH biopsies decline over the course of eye-salvaging treatment, reflecting a direct correlation with tumor burden.88 EVs in AH have also emerged as possible markers to stratify Rb severity and track response to therapy. One study found that at diagnosis, patients with more advanced disease exhibited higher percentages of CD63/81+ EVs. Additionally, compared to samples taken at diagnosis, AH from treated eyes had an overall decrease in CD63/81+ EVs. Interestingly, samples retaining high levels of dual CD63/81 positivity after treatment were linked to therapy resistance and an increased likelihood of enucleation.102 Additional research has identified CD133 as a tumor-specific marker on Rb-derived EVs in AH, providing a method to confirm that the EVs analyzed in future analysis indeed originate from the tumor.104 However, no study has yet leveraged this marker to track longitudinal changes in EV expression profiles during treatment, a gap that presents a promising direction for future research.

Many other potential Rb biomarker candidates are currently under investigation. An analysis of enucleated eyes found that methylation of transcription factor AP2-alpha distinguishes Rb from control or other retinal disorders with 93% accuracy. In the study, the degree of methylation in enucleated Rb retinas was comparable to that in AH-derived ctDNA, suggesting the ability to definitively diagnose Rb via AH sampling early in disease progression prior to further tumor advancement.101,103 Trefoil family peptide 1 (TFF1), a small, ectopic peptide expressed by Rb but not by healthy retina, has also gained traction as a potential biomarker in recent years. Several groups have demonstrated that high tumor expression of TFF1 is correlated with poor prognosis, and its presence in AH is associated with more aggressive tumors with greater likelihood of recurrence.106,107,108 Several cytokines (IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, and IFN-γ), growth factors (PIGF-1, HGF, β-NGF, FGF-2, and VEGF-A), and general neoplastic markers (lactate dehydrogenase, survivin, uric acid, and nucleic acids) have also been reported to be elevated in Rb compared with controls, although the generality of these elevated markers underscores the need for more specific markers and clear-cut threshold values.37,109

Conclusion

The current gold standard for diagnosing most ocular malignancies is tumor biopsy and histopathology, with a lesser role for vitreous cytology. However, these approaches are invasive, risk vision loss, may disseminate tumor cells, and have limited ability to track microscopic recurrence.7 Due to this inherent risk profile, direct biopsy is contraindicated in certain ocular cancers that form in the back of the eye, such as Rb. In other ocular cancers, the potential benefits and harms of direct biopsy must be carefully considered. Blood-based biomarkers have been explored as a less invasive alternative for detecting ocular cancers, offering an accessible approach to monitor pathologic progression.96 Unfortunately, these markers are significantly less specific to ocular malignancies, and in bilaterally presenting cancers, blood-based biomarkers are unable to differentiate between disease activity in each eye, reducing their prognostic value.110 However, as methods to enhance ocular malignancy diagnosis, prognostication, and recurrence continue to evolve, there may be a valuable future role for blood-based biomarkers used in conjunction with ocular biopsies to monitor therapeutic response and identify systemic metastasis. Tear profiling is also under investigation for its role in identifying ocular pathology, and researchers are beginning to examine its potential to diagnose superficial ocular malignancies such as conjunctival melanoma.111,112

AH sampling has emerged as a promising approach to aid diagnosis and management of common ocular cancers, balancing accessibility with accuracy in early detection and prognostication. Due to its minimally invasive approach, AH sampling allows for repeated, real-time disease monitoring, which may track treatment response and detect recurrence with greater granularity than current methods allow. AH sampling also allows for eye-specific molecular analysis, making it especially valuable for determining prognosis and guiding therapeutic approaches in bilateral disease. AH can be collected in outpatient settings, suggesting the potential for AH biopsy to monitor ocular malignancies in clinical practice.113 AH sampling followed by whole-genome sequencing of tumor or cell-free DNA is already utilized clinically at Children’s Hospital Los Angeles (LBSeq4Kids assay) to detect RB1 mutations or Rb-associated copy number alterations, requiring ∼100 μL of AH to aid in the diagnosis of Rb.104

Novel proteomic assays are particularly promising for the future discovery of liquid biopsy biomarkers in small quantities of sample. The Olink platform has been used for a targeted approach to evaluate a panel of cytokines in AH and tear samples to identify prognostic markers in UM cohorts and biomarkers associated with dry eye disease, respectively.52,114 Furthermore, the platform’s proximity extension assay is a high-throughput method that allows for assessment of normalized expression of thousands of proteins.115 This technique has detected almost 1,500 targets in the AH of intraoperative UM samples, which have been analyzed for differential gene expression according to GEP class.54 More recently, this assay was used to identify normalized protein expression of over 2,800 proteins in 80 μL of AH from UM and non-UM controls, finding 421 differentially expressed proteins between groups.49 Somalogic’s SomaScan proteomics platform, which employs aptamer-based technology for the detection of up to 7,000 proteins, has been utilized to distinguish thousands of differentially regulated proteins in the AH from patients with infectious versus noninfectious uveitis.116 This technology has also been integrated with single-cell transcriptomics and artificial intelligence approaches to study eye disease mechanisms.113,115 Finally, mass spectrometry represents the most untargeted approach to liquid biopsy proteomics and gives researchers the opportunity to perform pathway and functional analysis of resulting proteomes or analyze post-translational modifications.113 AH from patients with Rb analyzed by mass spectrometry was used to distinguish between disease stages, but this methodology has also been applied to evaluate changes in the AH following therapeutic intervention in patients with glaucoma or age-related macular degeneration, as well as when comparing AH from glaucoma eyes to healthy controls.2,117,118,119 With the increasing availability of novel proteomic and sequencing platforms, the small quantity of AH obtained from liquid biopsy will be increasingly informative for intraocular malignancy detection and monitoring.

While studies on AH biomarkers have yielded promising results in UM, IOL, and Rb, more research is needed to clinically validate these findings and establish standardized thresholds for future widespread adoption. To date, research on AH biomarkers for monitoring ocular cancers has been concentrated on the malignancies described in this review. Studies on accurate AH biomarkers in other types of ocular malignancies are in the early stages, and further research is necessary to determine the utility of AC paracentesis in identifying diagnostic, prognostic, and metastatic markers for ocular cancers such as conjunctival melanoma, medulloepithelioma, and intraocular rhabdomyosarcoma.117 Furthermore, isolated reports of corneal abscesses, small hyphemas, endophthalmitis, and allergic conjunctivitis in response to preparatory iodine have been reported following AH biopsy.120 Although these complications are extremely rare, risks, including possible trauma to the iris and lens, should be weighed against the clinical benefit to patients before proceeding with AC paracentesis.

Beyond oncology, AH biomarkers have shown promise in the diagnosis and management of non-malignant pathology, such as glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, uveitis, degenerative retinal disorders, and even Parkinson’s disease, highlighting their broad clinical utility.75,115,121,122 As liquid biopsy techniques and molecular profiling continue to advance, integrating AH analysis into routine oncologic and broader ophthalmic practice has the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and refine therapeutic decision-making across a wide spectrum of ocular pathology.
