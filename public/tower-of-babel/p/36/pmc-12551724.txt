Lateral decubitus positioning with arthroscopic shoulder distractor for humeral intramedullary nail
Authors: Justin E. Kung, Cyrus A. Pumilia, Miraj Desai, Robert J. Ferdon, Chase Gauthier, Sam Wood, Heidi Ventresca, Daniel J. Cunningham, Kevin Murr, Thomas M. Jones
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:The objectives of this study are to (1) present a technique trick for intramedullary nailing of the humerus with the patient in lateral decubitus position and arm in an arthroscopic shoulder distractor to aid in utilizing the Neviaser portal to obtain access to a medialized start point and (2) present outcomes with this technique at a single institution.Design:Retrospective chart review.Setting:Academic Level 1 trauma center in South Carolina.Patients/Participants:Patients who underwent humeral nail fixation with this technique from March 2021 to March 2024 at a single academic level one trauma center were included.Intervention:Humeral intramedullary nail fixation.Main Outcome Measurements:Surgical duration, radiographic union, malunion rate, and rate of radial nerve palsy.Results:A total of 35 patients were included in the study. The median surgical duration was 60 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 75 mL. Final intraoperative fluoroscopy images demonstrated no patients had greater than 10 degrees of varus/valgus or anterior/posterior angulation. All patients achieved radiographic union and none demonstrated malreduction at final follow-up. Two cases of postoperative radial nerve palsies occurred, yet neither was directly attributed to the nailing procedure.Conclusions:Intramedullary nailing of the humerus in lateral decubitus, combined with an arthroscopic distractor for a Neviaser portal start, is a reliable option for treating proximal and diaphyseal fractures. This technique achieved consistent union without radiographic malreduction. By minimizing the risk of rotator cuff violation, it can serve as a valuable addition to the orthopaedic traumatologist's operative armamentarium.

1. Introduction

Plate osteosynthesis (plating) and intramedullary nailing (nailing) are common operative strategies for proximal humerus and humeral shaft fractures.1 Currently, there is no consensus as to whether plating or nailing is a more effective treatment option for proximal humerus or humeral shaft fractures.2 Multiple studies have shown similar results with both techniques in union and radial nerve palsy; however, nailing has historically been demonstrated to result in higher reoperation rates and inferior shoulder function outcomes, particularly when performed using traditional lateral entry points.1,3,4 Humeral nailing is often performed in the supine or beach chair positions with utilization of the lateral deltoid splitting incision.5 With this approach, the supraspinatus tendon is at risk of damage, which may account for the overall worse shoulder function after nailing. In addition, the acromion may block access to the appropriate medial start point, thereby necessitating the use of a lateralized start point, which may generate a varus deformity that has been previously associated with inferior functional outcomes.6 Furthermore, even if a medial start point is achieved with the traditional lateral entry, the acromion may push the nail more laterally during insertion in osteopenic bone.

The Neviaser portal is a superiorly based shoulder portal posterior to the distal clavicle and medial to the acromion. It has been asserted as a valid alternative option for nailing when acromiohumeral relationships do not allow for a traditional lateral deltoid-splitting approach.7–9 Furthermore, nail insertion is through the supraspinatus muscle belly, which may affect shoulder function less so than damaging the supraspinatus tendon.10 The objectives of this study are to (1) present a technique trick for intramedullary nailing of the humerus with the patient in lateral decubitus position and arm in an arthroscopic shoulder distractor to aid in utilizing the Neviaser portal to obtain access to a medialized start point and (2) present outcomes with this technique at a single institution.

2. Methods

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained. All patients who underwent humeral intramedullary nail fixation (Current Procedural Terminology code 24516) by a single orthopaedic traumatologist from March 2021 to March 2024 at a level one academic trauma center were queried. This search yielded 38 patients. A chart review was performed to collect demographic data, details of their surgical procedures, and clinical details regarding postoperative course. Preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative radiographs were reviewed to classify fracture patterns according to OTA/AO Fracture and Dislocation Classification, obtain radiographic parameters of fracture reduction, and determine union. All patients who were younger than 18 years of age, pregnant, or incarcerated were excluded from the study.

3. Surgical Technique

Indications for surgical fixation using this technique included proximal and middle third humeral shaft fractures (OTA/AO types 11 and 12) not amenable to nonoperative treatment, open fractures requiring debridement, and fractures occurring in the context of polytrauma where early stabilization is preferred. Exclusion criteria include patients who are unable to be positioned laterally, open growth plates, small humeral shaft canal diameter precluding intramedullary fixation, and fractures within 5 cm of the distal metaphysis or those extending distally enough to compromise distal fixation. The patient is positioned on a radiolucent table in the lateral decubitus position with a deflatable bean bag positioner with the operative extremity toward the ceiling. The nonoperative extremity is placed anteriorly onto an arm board. All bony prominences are padded in standard fashion and an axillary roll is placed. The bean bag and patient are then secured to the table with a seatbelt to allow for tilting of the operating table during the procedure to assist with obtaining imaging. The hand of the operative extremity is padded with 2 abdominal gauze pads and cotton roll and placed into a traction sleeve. The arthroscopic distractor is placed on the anterior side at the foot of the bed and rope is used to attach the traction sleeve to weights placed over the arthroscopic distractor. Our technique typically utilizes 10–15 pounds of traction to suspend the arm without causing significant gapping of the fracture site; however, the amount of traction can be tailored accordingly. The operative extremity is then prepped and draped in a sterile fashion with care taken to allow access medially at the shoulder for the starting point.

Once positioned, the C-arm is brought in from the anterior side of the patient. Generally speaking, Grashey views are taken with C-arm parallel to the floor and scapular Y views taken with the C-arm perpendicular to the floor (Fig. 1). Maneuvering of the C-arm and tilting of the operating table may be utilized to ensure adequate Grashey and scapular Y and to reduce bony or soft tissue overlap.

At this point, gross reduction can be evaluated. Traction alone reliably provided gross reduction; however, the surgeon can adjust the trajectory of shoulder traction. The traction tower can be raised or lowered to affect coronal plane alignment, brought anteriorly or posteriorly to affect sagittal plane alignment, and the traction sleeve or device rotated to affect axial plane alignment. Commonly, the most advantageous position is 20–40 degrees horizontal abduction and forward flexion in the range of 15–30 degrees. Adjustments can be made to the traction amount and height of the arthroscopic distractor to help achieve this arm position; however, it is recommended to use the lowest effective traction amount to avoid theoretical risk of a second hit on the radial nerve. These adjustments are performed with the aid of a nonsterile assistant. Often times, adjusting these parameters is sufficient to obtain final reduction, especially in diaphyseal fractures in which the nail itself will aid reduction. If insufficient, further closed means are attempted such as strategic placement of towel bumps or applying manual pressure with a blunt instrument, similar to the closed reduction of other long bones. Percutaneous reduction techniques may also be employed if local anatomy allows. Finally, a limited open approach and reduction may be performed and maintained via clamps, provisional plates, or cables. Traction is then left in place throughout the remainder of the case, assisting in maintenance of reduction.

Once reduction is attained, the case proceeds to placement of the intramedullary nail. At our institution, the Neviaser portal is utilized for placement of the nail as described in the literature.7,8,10 Importantly, the desired starting point (in line with the humeral shaft in the coronal and sagittal planes for a straight humeral nail) is brought medially with our technique due to horizontal abduction of the arm in the traction device (Figs. 2 and 3). This favors the utilization of the more medial Neviaser portal as opposed to other laterally based approaches such as the transdeltoid with placement through the rotator interval. It also favors the use of a straight humeral nail as opposed to a system with a lateral bend. However, the feasibility of approach and nail design use should be individualized to each patient depending on native anatomy. In our technique, if difficulty with the Neviaser portal is suspected, feasibility is tested intraoperatively via placement of a K-wire in what would be the appropriate trajectory of the starting guidewire through the Neviaser portal. Generally, a 2–3 cm longitudinal incision is made over the Neviaser portal (the soft triangle posterior to the clavicle and medial to the acromion), in line with the anatomic axis of the humeral shaft in the coronal and sagittal planes. The starting guidewire is introduced into the proximal humerus, again in line with the proximal humeral shaft in both planes (if using a straight nail). The proximal humerus is then opened with a reamer, ensuring that appropriate trajectory is maintained. A protective soft tissue sleeve was used in all cases to minimize injury to the supraspinatus muscle belly. A standard ball-tipped guidewire is placed into the intramedullary canal and advanced across the fracture site. Length is measured. The canal is then reamed to the appropriate size, and the intramedullary nail is impacted into place (Fig. 4). Proximal interlocking screws are placed utilizing a targeting arm, taking care to avoid violation of the articular surface. Distal interlocking screws are placed in a posterior-to-anterior (P–A) trajectory using the perfect circle technique. This orientation is facilitated by the lateral position and arthroscopic shoulder distractor, which allows direct visualization of the posterior arm, traction which acts as a stabilizing force, and easy modification of arm flexion–extension using the arthroscopic shoulder distractor to aid in obtaining perfect circle imaging and reducing the need for substantial C-arm manipulations. Once complete, the shoulder joint and supraspinatus are irrigated thoroughly. Wounds are closed in multilayered fashion. Weight-bearing status and range of motion is prescribed as indicated based upon fracture pattern. Early range of motion is preferred if construct stability allows.

4. Results

Overall, 38 adult patients underwent intramedullary humeral nailing during the study period. Of these, 35 patients underwent nailing in the lateral decubitus position as described in the surgical technique, and 3 were polytrauma patients nailed in the supine position to accommodate concomitant procedures. The Neviaser portal approach was utilized for all 35 patients nailed in the lateral decubitus position, whereas the traditional lateral approach was utilized for the 3 patients nailed supine. Of the 35 patients, the median age was 60 years old, median body mass index was 27.1, and 23 were female (Table 1). Twenty-one patients were injured from a fall, 6 as the result of gunshot wounds, 4 from motor vehicle collision, 2 from assault, 1 from motorcycle collision, and 1 from other. This technique was able to be utilized for a variety of OTA/AO proximal and diaphyseal humeral fracture patterns, most commonly 12C3.

Intraoperatively, 14 patients had limited open procedure to perform excisional debridement of open fractures, aid in fracture reduction, or provide supplementation fixation (Table 2). Cerclages were used in 5 patients, and plates were used in 6 patients for supplemental fixation. The median surgical time from incisional start to closure finish was 60 minutes, with the shortest procedure time of 38 minutes. The median estimated blood loss (EBL) was 75 mL. Final intraoperative fluoroscopy images showed that no patients had >10 deg of varus/valgus or anterior/posterior angulation. Of the 35 patients, 2 were noted to have a new radial nerve palsy postoperatively. One patient was taken back to the operating room 2 days after the index procedure for exploration due to postoperative radial nerve palsy. The cable was noted to have cause tension on the nerve although it did not capture it. It was subsequently removed. Postoperatively, this patient's radial nerve palsy was noted to be much improved with 4+/5 wrist extension strength at the 8-month follow-up. As for the second patient with a radial nerve palsy noted postoperatively, it was unclear whether this patient had a preoperative radial nerve palsy because the patient was intubated and sedated after sustaining multiple gunshot wounds including one to the humerus, and thus radial nerve function could not be examined preoperatively. Postoperatively, this was noted to have a radial nerve palsy, and an electrodiagnostic study performed 4 months postoperatively showed severe, subacute radial neuropathy at the level of the humeral fracture. Of the 35 patients, 7 were lost to follow-up. Postoperatively, all patients with follow-up (N = 28) reached radiographic union. The average follow-up was 22.8 weeks.

5. Discussion

Intramedullary humeral nailing is increasingly being utilized as a viable fixation method for proximal and diaphyseal humeral shaft fractures.11 Typically, patients are placed in beach chair or supine position, and a lateral deltoid splitting incision is used to access a start point lateral to the articular margin.5 In this study, we described the technical trick of humeral nailing in the lateral decubitus position with the operative arm in an arthroscopic shoulder distractor to abduct the shoulder and aid in aligning a medialized start point with the Neviaser portal. In this single surgeon cohort, we report no malreductions with angulation >10 degrees, a median operative time of 60 minutes, and no radial nerve palsies that can be directly attributable to nailing technique.

Perhaps the most important, and also most controversial, aspect of humeral nailing is determining the nail entry location. Proximal humerus fractures in particular are known to have a tendency to develop a varus deformity and several studies have shown that excessive varus results in poor functional outcomes.6 The ideal start point to correct varus involves medializing the nail entry point as much as possible and aiming down the shaft or slightly laterally while inserting the guidewire, reamer, and nail. Traditional humeral nailing utilizing a lateral deltoid-splitting approach with nail entry through the greater tuberosity inherently makes this process difficult, as the starting point is lateralized and leaves little room to aim laterally (Figs. 2 and 3). Therefore, this technique requires the surgeon to obtain and maintain a near-perfect anatomic reduction of the fracture throughout the procedure and also requires bone quality that can continue to maintain the reduction after nail placement. Another commonly used entry point is the anterolateral (rotator interval) approach, which avoids direct violation of the rotator cuff insertion; however, this technique may still limit a colinear start point in patients with limited shoulder mobility, larger body habitus, or significant acromial overhang.

Much of the criticism toward humeral nailing stems from a concern for iatrogenic injury to the rotator cuff and articular cartilage of the humeral head during nail entry, with many studies finding that patients do often suffer from postoperative pain, stiffness, and loss of function due to these iatrogenic injuries.1,3 This has motivated surgeons to find alternate nail entry points and techniques including the use of the rotator interval, greater tuberosity osteotomies, and the use of the Neviaser portal.8,10,12,13 The Neviaser portal has been routinely utilized in shoulder arthroscopy and splits the supraspinatus muscle belly as opposed to injuring the tendinous insertion of the muscle, which may further assist in preserving rotator cuff integrity. Gerich et al8 utilized the Neviaser portal for beach chair humeral nailing in a cohort of 27 patients and concluded that it was a safe alternative with reduced operating room time and radiation time compared with the deltoid splitting approach. The drawback to using this start point is that it leads to articular cartilage damage.10 Knierim et al10 performed a cadaveric study involving humeral nailing through the Neviaser portal and noted that all nails passed entirely through supraspinatus muscle belly and 13 of 15 nails were entirely on articular surface. Ideally, the only cartilage affected by this start point is on the superior aspect of the humeral head, and this would only engage the glenoid once the arm is beyond 90 degrees of abduction; however, excessive medialization of the start point can place more central portions of humeral head articular cartilage at risk. Notably, Barret et al14 recently demonstrated the reliability of nailing through the Neviaser portal in the lateral decubitus position, reporting favorable alignment and clinical outcomes in a multicenter cohort. Our technique builds on this approach by incorporating an arthroscopic shoulder distractor to provide controlled traction, abduction, and fine-tuning of shoulder position to improve fracture alignment and intraoperative reproducibility.

Nailing with the patient in lateral decubitus positioning has been described for intramedullary femur fixation, but humeral nailing in the lateral position is not commonly described in the literature.15–17 During nailing of a femur, positioning the patient laterally improves access to medial proximal femur start points, increases ease of obtaining fluoroscopic views, and helps fracture alignment start out in valgus. For similar reasons, intramedullary humeral nailing with the patient in the lateral decubitus position and the use of an arthroscopic shoulder distractor provide these benefits. First, abducting the arm to 20–40 degrees in the distractor helps align the Neviaser portal with the medialized starting point and the proximal shaft (Figs. 2 and 3). In a subtrochanteric cadaveric femoral nailing study, Ostrum et al18 reported that lateralized starting points induce varus deformity and should be avoided and even encouraged slightly medial start points. We extrapolate this biomechanical principle to proximal humerus fractures and aim for a medial start point accessed through the Neviaser portal, which can be facilitated with the arthroscopic shoulder distractor. Second, this distraction and abduction aids in reduction by providing continuous traction and manipulation of the initial fracture alignment into mild valgus, and thus final fracture alignment can be fine-tuned with the passage of the nail (Fig. 4). In our experience, the use of axial traction has not consistently resulted in fracture distraction; however, attention is paid to the potential for this intraoperatively. If distraction is visualized fluoroscopically, traction can be adjusted accordingly, and traction can be removed before interlocking. Third, because the distractor is placed on the side of the bed anterior to the patient's feet, the operative limb is flexed approximately 20 degrees. This allows the proximal extension of the long axis of the humerus to pass posterior to the head, which aids in the passing of the ball tip guidewire. Thus, this technical trick was successfully applied even to patients with cervical collars. Fourth, lateral positioning facilitates a posterior-to-anterior trajectory for distal interlocking screws, which offers a safer alternative by avoiding the anterior neurovascular structures and utilizing the relatively unencumbered distal posterior humeral surface.

The outcomes of this surgical cohort are comparable with those found in the literature. Beeres et al19 performed a meta-analysis comparing plate osteosynthesis and humeral nailing. They reported a nonunion rate of 3.0% with plate osteosynthesis vs. 4.3% with humeral nailing. In the current study, there were no nonunions noted in patients with adequate follow-up. Furthermore, no patients had malunions >10 degree of deformity in the coronal or sagittal planes. Operative duration in this current study was 60 minutes, which is comparable with other humeral nailing studies that vary from 50.8 minutes to 121 minutes.20,21 Of note, Beeres et al found that humeral nailing had a statistically significant shorter operation duration of 26 minutes on average compared with plate osteosynthesis. For secondary radial nerve palsies, Beeres et al report a 6.9% rate with plate osteosynthesis and a 2.5% rate with humeral nailing. In the current study, we observed a low secondary radial nerve palsy rate; none of which could be directly attributed to the humeral nailing technique itself.

Although this technique has numerous advantages, there are still certain disadvantages to be aware of. Positioning in the lateral decubitus position can introduce difficulty when managing a patient with multiple traumatic injuries, whereas supine positioning would allow multiple procedures to be performed without having to reposition or redrape the patient, and in certain cases allow more than one procedure to occur simultaneously. There may also be cases of spine instability after trauma that limit the ability to safely position the patient in the lateral decubitus position. In addition, supine positioning may be prudent for the hemodynamically unstable patient in the case that chest compressions are required. Also, the long-term clinical outcomes of articular cartilage damage incurred when utilizing the Neviaser portal have yet to be determined. As for study limitations, this study design did not include a control group, which makes it difficult to compare these outcomes directly to conventional nailing, plating, or various start points for nailing. As well, this study was also limited to a single institution and single orthopaedic trauma surgeon, which introduces bias based on surgeon skill level, technique, and comfort, and thus these findings may not be generalizable to other clinical settings. Last, although one proposed advantage of the Neviaser portal is that it avoids violating the supraspinatus tendon, potentially preserving shoulder function, we did not assess postoperative rotator cuff integrity or functional outcomes. Future studies incorporating dedicated shoulder outcome measures and imaging are needed to determine whether this technique offers a true functional benefit over traditional approaches.

6. Conclusions

Intramedullary nailing of the humerus in lateral decubitus positioning with the operative arm in an arthroscopic shoulder distractor to facilitate a medialized start point using the Neviaser portal is a viable treatment option for various proximal humerus and diaphyseal humerus fractures. No patients in this cohort had radiographic malreduction or nonunion, and the median surgical time was 60 minutes. Further studies are warranted to determine if this method achieves improved patient-reported outcomes compared with alternative fixation strategies.
