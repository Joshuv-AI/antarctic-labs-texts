Aging in rural areas: balancing land, care, and connection of family caregivers of persons with dementia: a qualitative analysis
Authors: Jasmine Santoyo-Olsson, Mara Rosenberg, Catherine Chesla, Veronica Yank
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesCaregiving for people with dementia is especially challenging in rural areas of the United States, where services and support are often limited. This study explores how contextual factors—environmental conditions, resource availability, and family and social dynamics—intersect to shape experiences of rural family caregivers.Research Design and MethodsThis secondary analysis uses qualitative data from a nationwide randomized controlled trial of an online workshop for rural family caregivers. Semi-structured interviews with 55 participants explored how living in a rural, farming, or small town area influenced their caregiving experiences. Narrative analyses were conducted to identify themes.ResultsThree themes emerged: navigating rural tranquility and environmental challenges, caregiving in resource-limited settings, and variable support from family, friends, and neighbors. Caregivers described the dual impact of their environment—while rural tranquility and perceived safety were valued, they were offset by the need for constant vigilance and the physical demands of land maintenance when caring for a person with dementia. Caregivers also reported challenges accessing specialized care, citing limited availability and inadequate dementia-specific communication from providers that impeded access to information and resources. As a result, many managed care independently to fill critical gaps. While family support was essential for some, it was not always available. Despite familiar surroundings, many caregivers felt isolated, as friends or neighbors remained distant observers rather than hands-on helpers.Discussion and ImplicationsThese findings highlight the complex realities of rural dementia caregiving, where environmental conditions, resource limitations, and social factors intersect to shape caregiver experiences. Aging in rural areas offers comfort but burdens caregivers significantly. Addressing gaps in service accessibility, improving provider communication, and strengthening informal support networks are critical steps toward reducing caregiver burden and enhancing well-being for caregivers and people with dementia.

Caring for people living with dementia in rural U.S. areas is challenging due to limited services and support. This article presents qualitative data illustrating the difficulties of balancing rural life, managing care with limited resources, and varying levels of family support. It highlights the complex realities of rural caregiving, emphasizing the need to address home safety, physician communication, rural healthcare infrastructure and workforce, community support, and local and virtual support networks. The findings call for innovative solutions to improve home safety, physician–caregiver communication, incentives for rural healthcare providers and services, and comprehensive support systems to better support rural caregivers.

Background and objectives

Aging in place—defined as living safely and comfortably in one’s own home and community—is a mainstream strategy for caring for older people, including people with dementia.1,2 According to U.S. Census data, 20% of the population lives in rural areas,3 and 18% of rural residents are 65 years and older compared to less than 14% of urban residents.4 The ability of people with dementia to age in place plays a role in their quality of life. Remaining at home allows people with dementia to maintain a sense of familiarity and engage in everyday activities.5,6 For people with dementia living in rural areas, aging in a familiar environment can enhance their sense of identity, facilitate better access to community services, and help them maintain social connections.7 In rural areas, maintaining social connections might be easier than in urban areas due to the presence of strong networks of friends and family who can offer support services.8,9

Aging in place has increasingly placed the responsibility of care on caregivers. Caregivers are family members or friends who provide support and home care for people with dementia. Caregivers frequently report positive feelings about caregiving, such as family togetherness and the satisfaction of helping others, yet they also report higher levels of stress and care burden, negatively impacting their own health.10 Furthermore, the environment, particularly in rural areas, can significantly impact the ability of both people with dementia and their caregivers to age in place successfully.

Caregivers living in rural areas face unique challenges that significantly impact their caregiving roles and health. One in five rural adults are caregivers, and nearly 20% provide care to someone with dementia.11 Rural caregivers report higher rates of stress, depression, and financial distress from caregiving compared to their urban counterparts,12,13 in part influenced by the rural environment. Key environmental differences include infrastructure, sociodemographic characteristics, and limited access to essential resources like healthcare, transportation, and formal caregiving services (e.g., respite care, support groups, paid in-home services).10 Hospital closures and the scarcity of specialty care and nursing homes further exacerbate these issues.14,15

Despite growing interest in rural caregiving, existing literature remains predominantly quantitative. Systematic reviews from 1990 to 2022 identified only 14 qualitative studies on U.S.-based caregivers of people with dementia.12,13,16–18 Qualitative studies have primarily focused on limited aspects such as caregiver knowledge of dementia,19–22 the use of formal and informal supports,23–27 caregiver needs,28,29 managing behaviors of dementia,30,31 and the experiences of husbands caring for their spouse with dementia.32 However, research has yet to adequately explore the significant gap in understanding how unique characteristics of rural environments and cultural attributes intersect and cumulatively impact caregivers in these regions. Specifically, there is limited attention to how multiple contextual factors—such as environmental conditions, limited service availability, and shifting family dynamics—intersect to shape the lived experiences of caregivers in rural communities.13,18 For an environment to be considered dementia-friendly, both the social and built environments must have supportive features to allow people with dementia to live well.33 To help rural residents age in place for as long as possible, it is essential to understand the unique challenges that people with dementia and caregivers experience in relation to the rural environment. The objective of the current study is to explore how intersecting contextual factors—including environmental conditions, resource availability, and family and social dynamics—shape caregivers’ ability to support aging in place for persons with dementia.

Method

Study design

This secondary exploratory analysis uses qualitative data collected from caregivers after their participation in the parent study of a pragmatic hybrid randomized controlled trial (RCT) and implementation evaluation of the Building Better Caregivers workshop, an intervention designed to decrease depression and stress among caregivers residing in rural areas. The parent RCT and intervention are described elsewhere.34 Briefly, caregivers were recruited in collaboration with rural-serving community organizations. Caregivers were randomly assigned to the workshop (intervention) or attention control group (two 15–30-min phone calls from study staff). Building Better Caregivers is an online 6-week workshop that provides caregivers training in how to provide better care and helps caregivers learn how to manage their own emotions, stress, and physical health. Online informed consent was obtained from all participants for the parent RCT, which includes the use of de-identified data from semi-structured interviews for future studies by other researchers. Participants were assured that their participation was confidential and voluntary, and they were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. The University of California, San Francisco institutional review board approved the protocol (approval #18-25814).

For this secondary exploratory analysis, we apply a narrative approach35,36 to analyze data from semi-structured interviews to explore the lived experiences of rural caregivers. Narrative analysis was chosen because this methodology was well-aligned with our goal of generating new knowledge that can be applied in real-world settings and was appropriate to the richness of responses to the caregiving and rural context questions.35,36 Narrative research allows for understanding participants’ experience in a comprehensive way, including accounts of their situations within their structured and social world. We did not use a theoretical framework to guide the analysis. Interviews were designed to understand the rural context of participants, including those randomized to the intervention who did not participate. The aim was to better understand the circumstances under which they provided caregiving, which might also highlight potential needs for adapting the intervention to better serve rural caregivers and accommodate the rural context.

Sample

Upon completing the 12-month RCT, a subset of caregivers from the parent RCT randomized to the intervention group were invited to participate in a semi-structured debriefing interview as part of the workshop’s implementation evaluation. Inclusion criteria of the parent RCT consisted of adult self-identifying as living in a rural, farming, or small-town area of the United States; providing at least 10 hr of care a week to a family member or friend with dementia; stress 4+ on 10-point scale; having internet access; and having English proficiency. The goal for the semi-structured interviews was to recruit 12–15 caregivers from each of four U.S. Census regions (West, Midwest, South, and Northeast) to achieve geographic diversity. A total of 55 caregivers completed interviews.

Data collection

The 30-min semi-structured debriefing interview was conducted via telephone or video conferencing, according to the participant’s preference. Interviews were conducted by one of two trained research coordinators, the principal investigator (V.Y.), or a co-investigator (C.C.). Interviews were audio-recorded, de-identified, and transcribed. An open-ended interview guide was used to query participants. All participants were asked the same questions. To set the stage for the interview, we began with the statement, “We are very interested in learning about your experiences as a caregiver and with the Rural Dementia Caregiver Project.” The opening questions of the interview focused on their caregiving context (“Tell me about your caregiving routine and responsibilities”) and their rural context (“How has living in a rural, farming, or small-town area influenced your experiences as a caregiver”). This analysis centered on these first two questions, before the interview proceeded to intervention-specific queries (e.g., what got in the way or helped participants participate, how and why they did/did not do workshop activities, suggestions for improvements). Sociodemographic characteristics of caregivers and people with dementia consist of age, sex, race or ethnicity, educational attainment, the caregiver’s U.S. Census region based on state of residence, and the care recipient’s relationship to the caregiver and living arrangement.

Data analysis

Narrative analysis was guided by a goal to understand participants’ stories based on their situational context and concerns; a comprehensive analysis that attended to participants’ language, emotion, and situational detail was therefore used.35,36 The analysis included four steps.35,36 The first step involved repeated in-depth reading of the transcripts to summarize each narrative while considering the content, structure, and emotional tone of how things were said in the narrative. The second step involved identifying themes and patterns that were identified inductively within the data. Themes and patterns were iteratively revisited and updated, as needed, with every 20 transcripts until all 55 transcripts were analyzed. The third step involved comparing and contrasting narratives between participants to determine similarities and differences. The last step was to identify brief narrative excerpts that best illustrated the identified themes. Names and personal details within narratives were modified to ensure anonymity. We use pseudonyms, represented by anonymous letters, to describe the caregiver’s relationship to the person with dementia. Although two researchers (J.S.-O. and M.R.) primarily conducted the direct analysis of the narratives, emerging themes, observations, and discrepancies were discussed with the other authors (V.Y. and C.C.) in weekly meetings. When varied interpretations arose, the team examined the narrative text to come to an agreement on its interpretation. The data were annotated in Microsoft Excel to assist in organizing and coding the data and to record researchers’ summary notes and observations.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the 55 caregiver participants and their care recipients. Among caregivers, the mean age was 62.9 (standard deviation [SD] = 11.3). The majority were female (82%) and self-identified as White (87%). Sixty-seven percent had completed a college degree or higher. Participants represented all Census regions: 36% lived in the West, 29% in the Midwest, 20% in the South, and 15% in the Northeast. Most caregivers provided support to a spouse (56%) or parent (33%). Among people with dementia, the mean age was 75.5 (SD = 10.7), 53% were male, 89% identified as White, 35% completed a college degree or higher, and over half (56%) lived with their caregiver. Dementia diagnoses varied: 31% were diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease, 9% with Lewy body dementia, 7% with vascular dementia, 7% with frontotemporal dementia, and 31% with other or mixed types of dementia.

Navigating rural tranquility and environmental challenges

Rural beauty that demands watchfulness

Most caregivers highlighted the beauty of rural living, which offers ample space in the natural environment for growing vegetables and having access to animals. The rural setting gave caregivers an opportunity to keep their care recipients active and engaged in the early stages of the disease by going on walks together, visiting nearby farm animals, or sitting on the porch to watch the garden. These simple activities often became sources of joy and connection.“We go for a walk. Our blocks here are about a mile. So we usually go about a mile and a half, because she likes to see the animals, horses, sheep, goats, and chickens. We walk around and try to visit everybody on a daily basis. They’ve become like her best friends. I think it’s been wonderful, we’ve really enjoyed the slower pace.”—Mrs. A is caring for her sister, Ms. Z

Some caregivers talked about how rural areas gave them a sense of security and peace of mind, such that doors are left unlocked and keys are left in cars. A few caregivers discussed how the rural space kept their care recipient contained and protected, which eased their level of worry as they cared for their loved ones. One caregiver also talked about how the rural space probably allowed their care recipient to keep their driver’s license for longer because traffic around their town was slower than traffic in an urban area.“We live on a dead-end street and about a quarter mile to the next street, I don’t know if [my husband] could walk that far or not, but he wears an ID bracelet just in case. If we lived in the city, I’d have to worry about him stepping off a curb and getting hit by a car or talking to the wrong person. So, for lots of reasons I like [being rural] better, it’s more containing for him.”—Mrs. M caring for her husband, Mr M

Additionally, caregivers faced practical challenges related to the rural setting, such as the difficulties their loved ones experienced when taking walks or using walkers on gravel or dirt driveways and roads. I was doing everything and making all the major decisions…I knew that I did not wanna stay on the farm with him. I could not do this. When home becomes a barrier

Caregivers discussed that providing care in an older home presented significant challenges as the dementia progressed, affecting the mobility of their care recipient. Caregivers who lived on family farms tended to live in older homes. Narrow hallways and having bathrooms and bedrooms on different floors created barriers to bathing, toileting, and even providing access to emergency services, which was distressing and overwhelming for caregivers.“My mom starts hollering from her room. We end up calling the ambulance. They can’t get the gurney down the hall. That’s another thing, narrow halls in houses. Anyway, so thankfully, my buddy, his friend is a mortician, he moves bodies a lot, so they did a blanket carry out to the ambulance gurney in the living room and transferred her to the ambulance…I felt overwhelmed that I couldn’t help.”—Ms. B caring for her mother, Mrs. V

As the dementia progressed, caregivers in older homes expressed heightened concerns about how to manage and maintain essential home utilities such as heating and water. People with dementia sometimes forget how to use or tend to these systems, creating safety risks and adding stress to the caregivers’ already demanding responsibilities.“Our house, which we built ourselves, needs a lot of repairs and trying to get my husband to accept that and let me bring people in has, it’s always an argument, it’s always something to be put off…There’s a real concern for me over this winter using the wood stove. My husband could not get his head around getting the wood on time even with my nudging, and that’s a real problem. So we don’t have firewood, which is a real concern for me, getting dry firewood, and keeping it safe.”—Mrs. G caring for her husband, Mr G

Burden of land maintenance

Many caregivers owned a family farm or large acreage of land and noted the significant challenges of managing care for their care recipient while handling land maintenance and other home responsibilities. When their care recipient was bedridden or in a wheelchair and needed constant supervision, caregivers often felt trapped or unable to leave them alone to drive the trash to the landfill or to maintain the property. Some caregivers tried to get respite so that they could handle essential tasks like land maintenance and house and repairs themselves, but these moments of relief were often difficult to arrange.“We had a tremendous amount of legal and functional things that we had to do because we owned and operated a farm. It was really hard for me to figure out what to take care of first—which do I do? [My husband] tried to farm and he fell off a combine… He realized he was done farming, which was horrific for him to decide. So we hired someone to do the stuff that he couldn’t do.”—Mrs. E caring for her husband, Mr E

Caregivers discussed feeling overwhelmed by the paperwork and other tasks that their care recipient used to handle, which added to their emotional strain. Several caregivers were forced to sell their house, land, or animals because the upkeep while caring for another human became overwhelming and left them with no other option, a decision that often came with feelings of loss and heartache.

Caregiving in resource-limited settings

Alone in search for dementia care

Caregivers had difficulty finding reliable care, particularly specialty care, for their care recipient in the local area, which made it challenging to obtain a dementia diagnosis and receive subsequent care. Some caregivers had seen the same primary care physician for many years, but were concerned that they lacked specialized dementia knowledge. Caregivers who were able to access a local neurologist from small-town hospitals expressed reservations about older neurologists not being up-to-date with the latest developments or concerns that the high turnover rate of younger neurologists reduced the consistency of longitudinal care. Caregivers often had to travel long distances to access specialized care (neurologists) from academic or larger health systems. These challenges made it frustrating and difficult for people with dementia and caregivers to build a trusting and consistent relationship with a physician who understood their medical history and needs.

An overwhelming number of caregivers discussed how the initial dementia diagnosis was delivered insensitively by physicians. Physicians sometimes dismissed caregivers’ concerns, offered no further appointments, and only advised caregivers to join a support group. These interactions were frustrating and stressful for caregivers. Some caregivers had to do their own research to advocate for their care recipient and themselves.“When I questioned the doctor about [dementia types] he just said ‘it doesn’t really matter, we treat them the same.’ When I read about them I thought they were very different. I realized that I had to be a very assertive advocate for my husband and for myself. I emailed a question to the doctor through the portal. I explained, ‘I live on a farm. I am an hour away from a doctor or a hospital. What is the trajectory? Receiving home visits was especially important for caregivers when the distances required for travel were substantial. Burden of filling the resource gap

While several caregivers were able to secure caregiving resources such as respite, home health care, and Meals on Wheels, many caregivers found it challenging to secure appropriate formal caregiving resources. Challenges included affordability, strict service eligibility criteria, confusing application processes, and an inability to identify staff to help despite multiple attempts. A few caregivers living near state borders identified caregiving resources in neighboring states but learned they required an entirely new packet of Medicaid paperwork or were ineligible, which left them feeling defeated. Caregivers generally found the process of accessing formal resources to be overwhelming and inefficient. When caregivers were able to find resources, it meant long travel times to day programs or driving themselves to pick up food from Meals on Wheels because there were no drivers for their area. These challenges led to frustration and burnout.

A few caregivers had to rely on a community network of non-professionals to help meet their caregiving needs.“The Parkinsonism started to get harder, and I needed time away. Isolation in once familiar or insular communities

As dementia progressed, caregivers’ social relationships became strained, and their community involvement dwindled as caregiving responsibilities increased. Small-town dynamics and barriers to accessing social opportunities for caregivers and people with dementia made it difficult to maintain connections. Discussion and implications

This study explored the experiences of caregivers of people with dementia in rural settings, highlighting both the unique benefits and significant challenges of aging in place in these areas. The findings show how intersecting contextual factors—navigating rural tranquility and environmental challenges, caregiving in resource-limited settings, and variable support from family, friends, and neighbors—shaped the emotional and social impacts on caregivers and influenced their ability to help people with dementia age in place.

One of the most striking findings was the evolving and intersecting impact of the environmental challenges on rural caregivers. This aligns with prior literature highlighting that the physical environment can provide both personal comfort and safety while simultaneously presenting challenges for older adults with evolving disabilities.37 As dementia progresses, caregivers face increasing challenges such as preventing wandering, falls, and other safety concerns, requiring heightened vigilance and interventions that strain caregivers’ physical and emotional resources.37 Implementing practical home modification programs (e.g., installing gravel stabilizers to firm up loose gravel paths) may improve safety and accessibility in older rural homes. Moreover, as our findings demonstrate, living in older homes, common in rural settings, presents practical challenges such as narrow hallways, stairs, and wood-burning stoves, which are not well-suited to the safety and accessibility needs of older adults with disabilities.37 Environmental obstacles combined with caregiving responsibilities often force caregivers to consider selling property or animals to allow them to maintain their care duties. While respite care could alleviate some of this burden, it is often scarce in rural areas. Alternatively, hiring help to manage the land could alleviate the burden on caregivers, although it is resource-intensive. In anticipation of this, future planning programs could be developed to assist caregivers in preparing for long-term care needs. These findings underscore the importance of understanding how caregivers balance the perceived benefits of rural living with the intersecting responsibilities of maintaining safety, property, and emotional well-being for themselves and people with dementia.

Caregivers in resource-limited settings struggle to access healthcare due to geographic isolation, a shortage of medical specialists to diagnose and treat dementia, frequent physician turnover, and suboptimal communication practices. These obstacles delay dementia diagnoses and prevent timely access to treatments that may slow disease progression and support people with dementia functioning. These findings are similar to other studies that highlight the difficulty of accessing specialized dementia care in rural areas.38 Although loan repayment programs aim to address physician shortages, caregivers remain concerned about physicians’ short-term positions and frequent replacements.39 Enacting funded policies of offering permanent incentives, such as competitive salaries, housing assistance, and tailored professional development, could encourage physicians to commit to long-term rural practice. Frequent physician turnover hampers physician–patient communication,40 leading caregivers to feel dismissed and alone, increasing the emotional toll of navigating the healthcare system. Improving communication and increasing physician awareness and sensitivity to caregivers’ dementia-related challenges can help address these issues.40 Caregivers value home visits and telemedicine, as these options reduce the physical and emotional strain of transporting people with dementia to healthcare facilities.38 Implementing policies that expand home visits and telemedicine services, as well as investing in community-based healthcare training programs, can foster local talent and create a stable workforce dedicated to serving rural communities. These findings highlight the need to expand flexible care options and improve healthcare delivery models for people with dementia and caregivers in rural areas.

Caregivers in rural areas experience variable support from family, friends, and neighbors, presenting unique challenges and dynamics that highlight the critical role of family and community support. Family members often play a central role in caregiving, with some caregivers benefiting from the proximity of nearby relatives who share caregiving responsibilities. This shared responsibility can alleviate caregiver stress but can also create family strain, especially when caregivers feel unable to ask for help or their needs for assistance are unmet. Additionally, there is an expectation to provide care, which intersects with cultural norms in rural communities where caregiving is often seen as a moral or familial responsibility.41,42

Caregivers report mixed experiences regarding support from friends and neighbors. While some community members offer vigilance from afar, others avoid interaction due to unpredictable behaviors exhibited by people with dementia. These findings challenge the common assumption that rural communities are inherently close-knit and highlight the social and emotional complexities of caregiving support systems in these areas. Research indicates that rural communities offer inconsistent levels of support, exacerbating caregiver stress.43 To address this, fostering awareness and education about dementia through community workshops could encourage understanding and interaction, and help create dementia-friendly communities.44,45 Creating structured volunteer programs or training uncertified non-professionals can provide reliable respite and support, reducing caregiver stress and enhancing the overall support system in rural areas.

Caregivers in rural areas experience significant social isolation, often losing touch with their former social networks due to caregiving duties. Geographic distance, caregiving demands, and sparse populations limit opportunities for caregivers and people with dementia to maintain or establish meaningful connections with neighbors. Caregivers who relocate to rural areas in older age find it difficult to form new relationships, intensifying their isolation.43 Because social support plays a crucial role in buffering the negative effects of caregiving,46 the loss of a caregiver’s close-knit community exacerbates their isolation, making it essential to develop strategies to mitigate this emotional burden. Potential strategies include creating local and virtual support groups to offer regular opportunities for connection and mutual support and using online platforms to assist caregivers who cannot attend in-person meetings due to distance or caregiving duties.

Limitations

The sample may not be representative of all rural caregivers, as participants self-identified as rural, and geographic variations were not accounted for. The study predominantly includes White, college-educated caregivers with internet access, which may limit the generalizability of findings. The exploration of caregiving experiences in rural areas through open-ended narrative questions was constrained by time limitations during debriefing interviews. Additionally, the two rural-specific questions were part of a larger study, limiting the depth of inquiry into the rural context. Future research should conduct in-depth interviews focused solely on rural caregiving experiences to expand upon the present findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the unique challenges faced by rural caregivers.

Conclusions

Caregivers in rural areas face a unique set of challenges due to the intersection of physical constraints, such as older homes and the demands of land maintenance, with the emotional and logistical burdens of caregiving in geographically and socially isolated environments. While the natural environment offers benefits like tranquility and safety, the progression of dementia necessitates additional measures to ensure the well-being of both caregivers and people with dementia. These measures include more accessible healthcare services, increased community support, and policy interventions that account for the complex factors shaping rural caregiving. Addressing these needs will improve the quality of life for caregivers and enhance the care and quality of life for individuals with dementia.
