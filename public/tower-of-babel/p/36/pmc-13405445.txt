Examining the Clinical Efficacy of Novel Beta-Lactam and Beta-Lactamase Inhibitor Antibiotics: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Mohamed Hamed Elkholi, Mohamed Ibrahim Shoaib, Zeyad Alrais, Khalid Omar Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:This study evaluated the efficacy of novel β-lactam/β-lactamase (NβL/βLIs) combination against Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections.Methods:We searched CENTRAL, MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Google Scholar, and clinical trial registries through April 20, 2025, and included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing NβL/βLIs with best available therapy (BAT). Pediatric, noncomparative, and in vitro studies were excluded. We performed meta-analyses using random effects models to calculate odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), summarized using GRADE methodology. We included 22 RCTs with 11,238 participants. All RCTs except one were conducted worldwide between 2012 and 2025. Approximately 52% of participants used novel β-lactamase inhibitors. Risk of bias (RoB) was assessed using the RoB 2 tool.Results:NβL/βLIs were as effective as BAT (OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.95–1.31; I2 = 29%; 21 studies, 7630 participants). In multidrug resistance (MDR) infections, NβL/βLIs showed a trend toward benefit (OR 1.23, 95% CI 0.95–1.58; I² =12%; 17 RCTs, 1713 participants). For microbiological cure, NβL/βLIs were superior (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.06–1.66; I2 = 68%; 22 studies), mainly due to improved efficacy in complicated tract infections. Safety outcomes were comparable between groups.Conclusions:Novel βL/-βLIs are as effective as BAT in treating Enterobacteriaceae and P. aeruginosa infections, including MDR strains.

INTRODUCTION

Gram-negative pathogens, including Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas, are responsible for 60%–70% of severe infections in critically ill patients.[1] These bacteria exhibit high mortality rates and are often antibiotic-resistant.[23] Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) occurs when pathogens develop mechanisms, such as enzyme production, efflux pumps, and target-site mutations.[4] In 2019, global bacterial infection-related mortality was approximately 9 million resistant bacteria. By 2050, the cumulative global cost of AMR is projected to reach USD 100 trillion.[5] Recognizing this urgency, the World Health Organization has prioritized infections caused by Enterobacteriaceae, including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, for 2024.[5] β-lactam antibiotics are naturally occurring compounds characterized by a four-membered β-lactam ring, which binds penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). PBPs are enzymes that facilitate cross-linking of peptidoglycan chains during bacterial cell wall synthesis. β-lactams mimic the natural substrate, disrupting the final stage of cell wall maturation, leading to bacterial lysis.[6] They also trigger autolysin-mediated degradation, abnormal morphology, and membrane destabilization, resulting in leakage, metabolic stress, impaired nutrient uptake, and death. Weakened cell walls increase susceptibility to immune clearance and complement-mediated lysis. Furthermore, cell wall inhibition results in the accumulation of toxic precursors and alters morphology, thereby affecting normal function. In addition, cell wall damage enhances the activity of other antimicrobials by increasing permeability and disrupting biofilms, making bacterial communities more vulnerable.[4678]

Bacterial resistance to β-lactam antibiotics complicates infection treatment. Mechanisms include β-lactamase production, which hydrolyzes the β-lactam ring, PBP alterations that reduce antibiotic affinity, and changes in cell membrane permeability through porin modifications or efflux pumps, collectively driving multidrug resistance (MDR).[4] β-lactamases are central to resistance and are classified by the Ambler system into four classes (A, B, C, and D) based on structure.[4] Combining β-lactams with β-lactamase inhibitors prevents enzyme-mediated degradation and improves outcomes against resistant bacteria.[9]

Avibactam and relebactam are diazabicyclooctane (DBO) β-lactamase inhibitors with a characteristic bicyclic structure that binds the enzyme’s active site, inhibiting degradation and restoring antibiotic activity. Avibactam is used with ceftazidime or aztreonam, while relebactam is combined with imipenem and cilastatin.[91011]

Vaborbactam and taniborbactam are novel cyclic boronic acid derivatives. The combination of Vaborbactam and meropenem inhibits serine β-lactamases, conferring resistance.[121314]

Ceftolozane/tazobactam combines a novel (fifth generation) cephalosporin with an established inhibitor. The modified side chain of ceftolozane stabilizes it against AmpC β-lactamases, prevents hydrolysis, and provides strong activity against Pseudomonas.[15]

Enmetazobactam, a recently approved penicillanic acid sulfone-extended β-lactamase (ESBL) inhibitor, acts as a cefepime-sparing therapy by preventing ESBL breakdown.[16]

These new β-L/β-LI are used to treat complicated intra-abdominal infections (c-IAIs), complicated urinary tract infections (c-UTIs), and hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated pneumonias (HAPs/VAPs).[17]

Selecting effective antibiotics against Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas is increasingly challenging,[8] due to rising antibiotic resistance. While traditional antibiotics are cheaper and widely available, their efficacy continues to decline. In contrast, novel β-L/β-LI agents provide promising alternatives, especially in resistant cases.[1718]

In this study, we assessed the effectiveness of novel β-L/β-LIs antibiotic combinations against Enterobacteriaceae and P. aeruginosa infections.

METHODS

We followed Methodological Expectations of Cochrane Intervention Reviews.[192021]

Eligibility criteria

Study types

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with participants randomly assigned to at least two antibiotic regimens. Quasi-RCTs and crossover trials were excluded. Cluster-RCTs were eligible but not identified. Studies were limited to adults aged >18 years with Enterobacteriaceae or P. aeruginosa infections. Studies lacking quantitative data, in vitro studies, or those involving patients with other infections were excluded. In addition, we excluded patients with chronic infections such as cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis, as these could influence antibiotic response.

Participants

This study included adults (≥18 years) with Enterobacteriaceae or P. aeruginosa infections diagnosed using predefined criteria. For heterogeneous populations, data were extracted from eligible participants. The authors were contacted when studies lacked separate data. Studies were excluded if quantitative data were unavailable or if they used in vitro conditions.

Types of interventions

The intervention group received novel intravenous (IV) β-L/β-LI combinations: aztreonam–avibactam, ceftazidime–avibactam, ceftolozane–tazobactam, cefepime–enmetazobactam, cefepime–taniborbactam, imipenem–cilastatin–relebactam, and meropenem–vaborbactam.

The comparator was the best available therapy (BAT), as specified in each study.

Information sources

Search methods for study identification

We followed Cochrane guidelines[19] and applied the PICO framework to define eligibility criteria and improve search. All search methods and strategies are presented in Supplementary Material 2.

Electronic search

We searched five databases: Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), MEDLINE, Embase (via OVID), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL via EBSCO), and Google Scholar. We also searched Clinicaltrials.gov and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. The final search was completed on April 20, 2025.

Other sources

We screened reference lists of included studies. No language restrictions were applied.

Data collection and analysis

Study selection

We followed Cochrane Conduct Standards.[19] References were managed using EndNote software,[22] including duplicate detection and manual verification. After removing duplicates, at least two authors independently screened titles and abstracts against the inclusion criteria. Full texts of eligible or uncertain studies were then retrieved and assessed independently by two reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction and management

Two authors independently extracted data using an agreed form based on PICO elements. The interquartile range, confidence interval (CI), and standard error were converted to standard deviation (SD). We calculated the group mean and SD when required.[23] Discrepancies were resolved through discussions. All authors assessed the protocol, analysis, and manuscript. We contacted the original authors when necessary.

Risk of bias assessment

We used the RoB 2 tool to assess risk of bias across all domains, rating studies as high, some concerns, or low.[242526] We provided evidence for our judgments and noted any disagreements with the algorithm recommendations. Bias assessments were conducted using RoB 2 Excel (accessed May 2025). For critical outcomes, we focused on per-protocol effects. For important outcomes, we analyzed intention-to-treat effects. If there was a disagreement among the reviewers, a third reviewer assisted in reaching a consensus.

Treatment evaluation

We utilized odds ratios (OR) to analyze the outcomes in a binary format.[20] Meta-analyses were conducted when treatments and participants were sufficiently similar. We extracted infection data and standardized visits to compare clinical success rates. We performed a random-effects meta-analysis and reported assumptions regarding treatment success.[23]

Unit of analysis issues

We compared β-L/β-LI with the BAT in 20 studies. Two RCTs included three arms: two with β-L/β-LI at different doses and one with BAT. We combined the intervention groups for comparison with BAT, focusing on the drug efficacy at varying concentrations. Outcomes were reported at different time points and standardized to end-of-therapy (EOT), TOC, and LFU visits. Assumptions are detailed in Supplementary Materials 1 and 3; Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Handling missing data

Investigators were contacted to verify study characteristics and obtain missing data when possible. When this was not possible and missing data could cause serious bias, we assessed the impact using sensitivity analysis.[26]

Assessment of reporting bias

We evaluated potential reporting bias using both graphical and statistical methods. We used funnel plots to identify asymmetry and quantify prospective bias. Furthermore, two authors independently used the RoB2 tool to assess potential bias from missing data and predict bias direction.[27]

Synthesis methods

We tabulated data to facilitate comparison and analysis. Meta-analyses were conducted only when participants, treatments, and clinical questions were similar.[2829] All assumptions are explicitly stated. Analyses were performed in Review Manager (RevMan) using inverse variance weighting with a random-effects model to account for clinical diversity.[30] Between-trial variance was estimated using Restricted Maximum Likelihood, with heterogeneity calculated using CI. For effect estimates, the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman method was used when ≥3 studies had nonzero heterogeneity, and the Wald-type method was applied for two studies or zero heterogeneity. Prediction intervals provided the expected range of true treatment effects.[172331]

Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

We used both visual analysis and the I2 test to determine the degree of heterogeneity.

Predefined subgroup analyses of critical outcomes were planned to identify potential effect modifiers and guide tailored interventions for improved efficacy and safety.[19]

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of our findings and explore the effects of different modeling approaches. This approach enhanced comparability and strengthened the reliability and validity of the conclusions.[32]

Certainty of evidence assessment

We created a summary of the findings table for all outcomes listed in the Outcome Measures section for all three follow-up visits. We used the five GRADEpro GDT considerations (overall risk of bias, consistency of effect, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias) to assess the quality of the body of evidence, as it relates to studies that contributed data to the meta-analyses of pre-specified outcomes.[1833343536]

Consumer involvement

Consumers were not included in this review due to limited resources, although we used core outcome sets developed with consumer involvement.

RESULTS

We searched five databases and two clinical trial registers, identifying 6989 potentially relevant articles. The most recent search was completed on April 20, 2025.

After removing duplicates, 5612 studies were screened by title, abstracts, and keywords. Of these, 5514 studies were excluded: 5387 irrelevant, 51 editorials, and 76 reviews. Of the 98 full-text articles assessed, 73 were excluded for the following reasons: case studies (n = 9), insufficient details (n = 8), healthy volunteers (n = 9), unavailable PDF (n = 6), reviews (n = 17), renal studies (n = 12), and pediatric participants aged <18 years (n = 12). Of the 25 reports considered for qualitative analysis, three trials were excluded, leaving 22 RCTs for quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) [Figure 1].

Included studies

We included 22 studies: Carmeli 2016;[37] Carmeli 2025;[38] Kaye 2018;[39] Kaye 2022;[40] Chaftari 2024;[41] Kollef 2019;[42] Li 2025;[43] Lucasti 2013;[44] Lucasti 2014;[45] Lucasti 2016;[46] Mazuski 2016;[47] Motsch 2020;[48] Qin 2017;[49] SIM 2017;[50] Solomkin 2015;[51] Titov 2021;[52] Torres 2019;[53] Vazquez 2012;[54] Wagenlehner 2015;[55] Wagenlehner 2016;[56] Wagenlehner 2024;[57] Wunderink 2018.[58] In total, 11,241 participants were enrolled in these studies, of whom 52.8% received novel β-L/β-LI therapy. Except for Chaftari 2024, all studies were multicenter; conducted in Europe, North America, Australia, and Asia, and published between December 2012 and October 2024.

Characteristics of studies

All studies were published as full-text papers. Most (17 of 22) focused on single-site infections, whereas five included patients with mixed infections. For three of these, the outcomes were clearly distinguished by the infection site: Carmeli 2016, Carmeli 2025, and Motsch 2020, which included most participants from the main infection site.[373848] Subdivisions: Carmeli-b 2016; Carmeli-b 2025; Motsch-b 2020. These included the remaining participants with infections. Two studies, Chaftari 2024 and Wunderink 2018, did not provide a clear distinction.[4158] Therefore, these patients were included in the mixed-infection subgroup.

Participants characteristics

The study participants had a mean age of 54.1 years (SD = 23.4), with males comprising 51% of the sample. The most prevalent infections were cUTI (45.7%), c-IAIs (31.8%), and HAP/VAP (18.5%) infections. Due to variations in treatment success across studies, we made assumptions during data compilation and performed a sensitivity analysis.

Risk of bias in included studies

We assessed the risk of bias across all RoB 2 domains, including funding bias.[33] In total, 16 studies exhibited a low risk of bias, while five studies presented some concerns regarding bias. In addition, three studies raised issues with only one RoB domain. Full details are provided in Supplementary Material 8; Supplementary Table 3.

Bias due to missing outcomes

The efficacy analyses included 7630 of the 11,241 randomized patients (67.8%). To assess whether this discrepancy influenced results, we used the RoB2 algorithm to address missing data.[29] Our findings suggested minimal risk of bias. Nonetheless, five studies raised concerns about missing outcomes, although this did not significantly affect the overall results. Full details are provided in Supplementary Material 8; Supplementary Table 3.

Funding bias assessment and conflict of interests

We evaluated potential funding bias, as many trials were sponsored by pharmaceutical companies.[282966] This assessment included sponsors’ influence on study design, analysis, and reporting to ensure robust conclusions. Several authors received consulting fees from pharmaceutical companies, which may have created potential conflicts of interest. All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by an international review board. Independent reviews validated the methodology and findings to ensure research credibility, scientific soundness, and minimal funding bias.

Evidence showed no significant differences in adverse effects or all-cause mortality between NβL/βLIs and BAT [OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.71–1.09; I2 = 0%; 22 studies, 11,241 participants; moderate-certainty evidence; Supplementary Figure 4]. Clinical cure at the end-of-therapy visits

This analysis included 4473 participants from 16 studies. Evidence suggests NβL/βLIs show little to no difference in clinical cure at EOT visits (OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.61–1.79; I2 = 70%; 16 studies, 4473 participants; very low degree of certainty). Of the 1000 eligible patients, 908 achieved clinical cure in the control group compared to 912 (95% CI, 855–945) with NβL/βLI treatment. The results showed no significant statistical or clinical differences between the subgroups.

Clinical cure at the late follow-up visits

Clinical cure rate at LFU visits in eligible participants treated with NβL/βLIs did not increase clinical success (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.01–1.39; I² = 0%; 14 studies, 5069 participants; low degree of certainty).

All adverse events

Across 22 studies (11,238 participants), NβL/βLIs showed no difference in overall adverse events compared with controls (OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.43–1.22; I² = 96%; moderate degree of certainty).

Serious adverse events: intention-to-treat analysis

NβL/βLIs did not increase serious adverse events (OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.86–1.27; I² = 29%; 22 studies, 11,238 participants; moderate degree of certainty).

Discontinuation due to adverse events

NβL/βLIs did not increase the risk of therapy discontinuation due to adverse events (OR 1.06, 95% CI 0.77–1.47; 22 studies, 11,238 participants; moderate degree of certainty).

Reporting biases

We evaluated potential reporting bias using both graphical and statistical methods. Funnel plots were used to identify asymmetry [Supplementary Figures 1-4 and 6B]. Two authors independently assessed bias for missing data using the RoB2 tool to predict the bias direction.

DISCUSSION

This study examined data from 7630 participants across 22 RCTs comparing NβL/βLIs and the BAT. Evidence indicates that NβL/βLIs are as effective as the BAT for treating Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas spp. infections, including MDR strains, and for achieving microbiological cure. In addition, NβL/βLIs increase the clinical and microbiological success of complicated tract infections. These drugs are superior to BATs for patients with cUTIs. Furthermore, the findings show that these novel drugs do not increase the risk of adverse events, and that their all-cause mortality rates were comparable with existing antibiotics.

Agreement and disagreement with other studies and reviews

An earlier meta-analysis by Wilson revealed that four NβL/βLIs produced outcomes comparable to those of BAT for the treatment of Gram-negative bacteria. The study conducted by Wilson et al. had limitations owing to its small sample size and reliance on observational studies.[59] Another meta-analysis conducted by Huang, which examined the efficacy of NβL/βLIs in comparison with BAT for the treatment of P. aeruginosa, also yielded similar results.[60]

This meta-analysis suggests that, in the context of MDR strains, NβL/βLIs demonstrate a marginally higher clinical cure rate than the BAT. This was primarily attributed to their enhanced efficacy in treating cUTIs caused by MDR strains. This finding aligns with previous meta-analyses and current guidelines from other organizations that have highlighted the potential of NβL/βLIs as a valuable treatment option for MDR cUTI.[61626364] In line with our findings, the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) recommends CAZ/AVI, MER/VAB, and IMI/CIL/REL as primary treatments for pyelonephritis and cUTIs caused by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) that are resistant to both ertapenem and meropenem. This recommendation also applies to KPC-producing infections outside of the urinary tract.[65]

In cases of HAP/VAP, no clinically or statistically significant differences between NβL/βLIs and BAT were found, a conclusion that was also reached by Wilson et al.[63] Furthermore, the 2016 ATS and 2017 ERS guidelines for HAP/VAP do not specifically recommend NβL/βLI.[6667] Meanwhile, the NHS guidelines list CAZ-AVI as a treatment option for patients with severe HAP and MDR HAP/VAP.[68]

This meta-analysis revealed that in cases of cIAIs, the efficacy of NβL/βLIs is comparable to that of the BAT, which aligns with previous findings.[697071] The 2024 Surgical Infection Society Guidelines recommend metronidazole plus CAZ/AVI, MER/VAB, and IMI/CIL/REL for patients at risk of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales infections. This promotes carbapenem-sparing therapy based on meta-analyses showing that metronidazole with cephalosporin or penicillin provides comparable effectiveness.[72]

The varying effectiveness across infection types may be due to differences in the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics at different infection sites. Factors such as infection severity, immune response, and bacterial resistance influence outcomes, with the benefits being more evident in cUTIs. The high prevalence of cUTIs in the study population may have influenced the overall findings in favor of NβL/βLIs.[737475] Moreover, resistance to NβL/βLI combinations can lead to variations in their effectiveness.[76] The mechanisms that confer antibiotic resistance include genetic mutations, enzyme overexpression, and structural changes.[7778]

Enterobacterales develop resistance to βL/βLI combinations through mutations in β-lactamase genes, including blaKPC, blaCTX-M, and blaAmpC.[79] In K. pneumoniae, mutations in the Ω-loop of KPC carbapenemases (D179Y, D179N) lead to resistance by altering enzyme structure to enhance β-lactam hydrolysis or reduce inhibitor binding.[8081] In addition, β-lactamase gene overexpression with the loss of outer membrane porins (OmpK35 and OmpK36) reduces drug permeability and contributes to resistance.[82] Efflux pump upregulation also reduces intracellular antibiotic concentrations.[83] In Enterobacter cloacae and Citrobacter freundii, resistance occurs through mutations in chromosomal AmpC β-lactamases, including G156D substitutions and deletions near the R2 binding pocket.[84] The loss of porins by ESBL or carbapenemase production increases resistance. Enterobacterales may acquire plasmid-mediated β-lactamases such as GES, VEB, and PER, expanding their resistance spectrum.[8586]

In P. aeruginosa, resistance primarily arises through mechanisms such as mutations in β-lactamases, changes in porin channels, and the regulation of efflux pumps. For instance, a key factor is mutations in the chromosomal AmpC enzyme, known as Pseudomonas-derived cephalosporinase (PDC).[878889] These mutations in the Ω-loop region enhance the enzyme’s ability to hydrolyse β-lactams.[90] Amino acid substitutions in PDC contribute to resistance, with variants such as E219K and V211A increasing catalytic efficiency against drugs such as ceftolozane-tazobactam.[91] P. aeruginosa can also overexpress PDC, lose the OprD porin, and upregulate efflux pumps, such as MexAB-OprM and MexXY-OprM, thereby reducing intracellular antibiotic concentrations.[9294] Resistance can be further exacerbated by mutations in regulatory genes that lead to the depression of PDC expression.[9394]

We demonstrated that the safety profile of NβL/βLIs was consistent with previous findings, encompassing serious adverse events and overall mortality. This observation aligns with those of previous meta-analyses and recommendations.[6595]

Limitations

This study focused on infections caused by Enterobacteriaceae or P. aeruginosa in RCTs, limiting its relevance to other bacterial infections and pathogens whose treatment responses may differ due to resistance mechanisms and susceptibility profiles. Second, nearly half of the cases involved cUTI, whereas cIAI accounted for approximately one-third of the cases, and HAP/VAP accounted for one-fifth. The results may have been skewed in favor of NβL/βLIs because of the significant number of participants with cUTI. Third, Individuals with skin and soft tissue infections were not included in the RCTs. Consequently, the efficacy of NβL/βLIs in treating these conditions remains uncertain and cannot be inferred from available evidence.

Furthermore, research has highlighted variations in the definitions of resistance and microbiological eradication, which affect outcome measurements and the comparability of results. These differences can affect the perceived efficacy of treatments. Despite these inconsistencies, NβL/βLIs remain viable alternatives to carbapenems for treating infections caused by Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas spp.

CONCLUSIONS

Data from RCTs involving adults with infections caused by Pseudomonas and Enterobacteriaceae, including resistant strains, suggest that NβL/βLIs are as effective and safe as the current best practices. However, a significant increase in the overall clinical success is unlikely, except in cases of cUTIs, where a slight but potentially meaningful improvement was observed.

A systematic review protocol is essential for assessing the efficacy of NβL/βLIs by allowing full access to detailed findings to ensure analytical transparency, enabling a thorough representation of effectiveness from trials to practice for stakeholder decision-making. Furthermore, the efficacy of NβL/βLI combinations in HAP/VAP, bacteremia, and skin infections requires further research. Combinations of meropenem and vaborbactam, cefepime, enmetazobactam, and taniborbactam should be evaluated in RCTs to fill data gaps and improve patient outcomes.
