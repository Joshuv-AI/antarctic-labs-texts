Application of the Modified Universal Soil Loss Equation (MUSLE) for the prediction of sediment yield in Agewmariam experimental watershed, Tekeze River basin, Northern Ethiopia
Authors: Yonas Reda, Awdenegest Moges, Hailu Kendie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study utilized the Modified Universal Soil Loss Equation (MUSLE) to predict sediment loss and evaluate the model's performance in the Agewmariam experimental watershed in order to support the planning, management, and appropriate use of the soil and water resources in the watershed. The natural resources conservation service (NRCS) curve number method was used to model runoff energy factor. By overlaying maps of runoff energy, soil erodibility, slope length and steepness, cover management, and support practice factors with assigned values, the cumulative effect of these parameters for the suspended sediment yield was calculated using the ArcGIS raster calculator. The runoff energy factor was the most sensitive parameter, followed by slope length and steepness factor. To improve the model's fit to the local conditions, the initial abstraction to storage ratio (λ) of the runoff energy factor was reduced to 0.023, and the MUSLE model coefficient and exponent were adjusted to 1 and 0.59, respectively. During calibration, the mean observed and estimated suspended sediment yields were 0.2 and 0.23 ton/ha, respectively, while during validation, they were 0.7 and 0.53 ton/ha, respectively. The model evaluation showed that the MUSLE model, without calibration, was not appropriate for estimating runoff and sediment yield. However, with appropriate calibration, the model showed good performance with a coefficient of determination (R2), coefficient of efficiency (E), and index of agreement (d) of 0.85, 0.85, and 0.96 respectively, during calibration and 0.84, 0.65, and 0.83 respectively, during validation. Based on these findings, this study suggests that the calibrated MUSLE model can be used to prioritize soil and water conservation interventions within the watershed or can be extrapolated to neighboring similar watersheds. Further refinement of model input parameters using more data from the watershed is recommended to increase the prediction accuracy of the model.Keywords: MUSLE, Curve number, Runoff modeling, Sediment yield, Conservation, Agewmariam watershed

Introduction

Soil erosion by water is a serious problem worldwide [1], and it is particularly evident in Ethiopia, where poverty and environmental degradation have had a profound impact [2,3]. During the rainy season, water erosion is the primary form of soil erosion, leading to the formation of rills, gullies, and sediment-laden brown rivers as clear evidence of substantial soil loss [3]. There are an estimated 60 million hectares of agriculturally productive land in Ethiopia, with approximately 27 million hectares experiencing erosion, 14 million hectares eroded and in need of rehabilitation, and 2 million hectares lost annually, resulting in a total loss of 2 million cubic meters of topsoil per year and an average soil loss of 100 tons per hectare from cultivated lands [2]. To mitigate these problems ensuring the efficient management of soil and water resources is essential for both food production and human well-being, as emphasized by Ref. [4].

The absence of readily available information on suitable conservation technologies for farmers and field officers poses a significant challenge to watershed management [5]. While detailed observations and measurements of erosion are essential, they can be both costly and time-consuming. Balancing the need for immediate action to prevent environmental damage with the resources required for detailed measurements presents a significant challenge [6]. To address this issue, improved soil erosion prediction technology is vital for assessing the current state of soil and water resources and planning for optimal land use as it provides accurate predictions of sediment yield and evaluates management strategies [1]. In addition, modeling water erosion is essential for understanding soil erosion processes, predicting runoff and erosion rates, and selecting appropriate control measures with well-developed models that offer accurate estimates of erosion risks [4]. Furthermore, sediment yield models that require runoff input can be integrated with hydrologic models to forecast daily sediment yield [7], contributing to a comprehensive understanding of erosion processes and effective resource management. This integrated approach equips policymakers, conservationists, farmers, and other land users with the necessary tools to make informed decisions about soil and water resource management.

The universal soil loss equation (USLE), revised universal soil loss equation (RUSLE), modified universal soil loss equation (MUSLE), and soil loss estimator for southern Africa (SLEMSA) are widely used empirical soil erosion models [8,9]. These models have been successful in supporting conservation management decisions and reducing soil erosion from agricultural lands [10]. The MUSLE is a modified version of the USLE that incorporates the runoff factor to consider the antecedent soil moisture to predict sediment yield more accurately [11]. This model increases sediment yield prediction accuracy, eliminates the need for sediment delivery ratio, and applies to individual storms [7,10,12]. The MUSLE model has been extensively applied worldwide, with varying reported successes relative to measured data [13], and has demonstrated a significantly higher efficiency of 71.5 % compared to the 53.5 % efficiency of the USLE model, highlighting the importance of considering runoff in soil loss prediction and emphasizing the MUSLE model's effectiveness in estimating soil loss and informing conservation programs [9].The MUSLE model integrates the runoff energy factor, which replaces the rainfall erosivity factor in the USLE. This is typically done using the NRCS runoff curve number technique to estimate daily runoff volume [7]. The MUSLE model is consistently applied in conjunction with the NRCS curve number method, as it provides benefits in improving the model's capacity and applicability across different research contexts [7,[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. The NRCS curve number method has the potential to be used for basins of almost any size and complexity, and can offer solutions for large areas by subdividing the watershed and employing appropriate routing [18]. Additionally, calibrating the model on a monitored watershed can allow for the extrapolation of short-term data into long-term periods or prediction of water yield for adjacent ungagged watersheds [7].

Developments are in progress to improve and simplify the use of the MUSLE model, such as the development of a soil erosion modeling tool called ArcMUSLE, which applies the MUSLE equation, curve number, and graphical peak discharge methods within an advanced GIS analysis environment [15]. The MUSLE model has also been utilized in various Ethiopian regions for soil erosion and sedimentation modeling, demonstrating its versatility and potential applicability [9,19]. The current research progress in this field highlights the widespread use and versatility of the MUSLE model, particularly when combined with the NRCS curve number method. This study sought to evaluate the applicability of the MUSLE model and enhance the accuracy of sediment yield prediction under local conditions in the Agewmariam experimental Watershed. By leveraging the MUSLE model's capacity to predict storm event-based sediment loss using hydrological data from the study watershed, the aim is to improve soil and water conservation planning and implementation, addressing the high spatial and temporal variability of environmental conditions while supporting effective watershed management.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The Agewmariam watershed, located in Sayda kebele and approximately 23 km southwest of Sekota town, in the Waghimra administrative zone of the Amhara regional state in Ethiopia. The watershed forms part of the larger Tekeze River basin (refer to Fig. 1), and covers an area of 155.39 ha, spanning the geographical coordinates 12°31′40″ N to 12°32′33″ N latitudinal and 38°55′14″ E to 38°56′15″ E longitude. The topography of the Agewmariam watershed is characterized by an undulating terrain, with a diverse range of land slopes varying from nearly flat (<3 %) to extremely steep (>50 %). The land-use composition of the watershed comprises primarily cultivated land (63.3 %), followed by scattered bushes (24.7 %), dense bushes (7.2 %), bare land (2.0 %), and settlement areas (2.8 %).

Map of the Agewmariam watershed.

According to the traditional agroecological classification, the study area falls under the Dry-Woina-Dega zone [3], characterized by a unimodal rainfall pattern extending from late June to early September, with the dry season spanning from October to May. The region experiences an erratic rainfall distribution, with a mean annual rainfall of 679 mm. The average minimum and maximum annual temperatures are 12.5 °C and 26.5 °C, respectively [20].

The watershed is home to 259 households, with an average family size of four individuals per household. Primary income sources include crop production and livestock rearing, supplemented by casual labor and food aid support. The dominant crops cultivated in the area include sorghum, barley, teff, and wheat, with an average arable land holding size of 0.5 ha. Animals are primarily fed crop residue and some hay collected from farm boundaries [21].

Data collection

The data collection process for estimating sediment yield and model evaluation encompassed various methods, including field surveys, measurements, and utilization of Google Earth, along with data adopted from literature or generated through geographic information systems (GISs) and other analyses. A comprehensive transect walk was conducted across the watershed, yielding valuable observations.

Biophysical data, such as slope, soil depth, stoniness, past erosion severity, land use type, and soil texture, were gathered from 155 soil sampling points within a 100 m*100 m grid. Soil samples, extracted using an auger from the top 30 cm soil depth, underwent analysis for physical and chemical properties. Additionally, image and elevation data from Google Earth Pro © 2019 CNES/Airbus [22] were digitized to map land use/land cover and prepare a high-resolution (2 m*2 m) digital elevation model (DEM).

The precipitation data recorded daily from 2018 to 2020 were collected using a manual rain gauge installed within the watershed area. To quantify sediment loss, an artificially constructed channel control section was used to evaluate runoff depth and velocity, alongside a flow meter positioned at the watershed outlet. Additionally, runoff samples were collected to determine the suspended sediment concentration.

Results and discussion

The MUSLE model simulation involved the estimation of various component parameters, including the runoff energy factor, soil erodibility, slope length and steepness factor, cover management factor, and support practice factor.

Hydrologic modeling for runoff energy factor estimation

The hydrologic modeling process in this study focused on the estimation of the runoff energy factor, a critical imput for the MUSLE model. The estimation of the runoff energy factor considered the soil texture classes in the watershed, specifically related to soil hydrologic groups A, B, and C only. The watershed's average curve number value was initially determined to be 56.4 for AMC II, which was adjusted to 37.1and 74.4 for AMC I and AMC III, respectively, to account for variations in soil moisture conditions. Further adjustments were made to the curve number value based on the watershed's slope characteristics [24], and calibrated to align with observed runoff values. Following adjustments for the land slope, the average curve number was calculated to be 59.9, which was close to the calibrated value of 61.77 (refer to Table 2). The calibration process revealed an initial abstraction to storage ratio (λ) of 0.023 for the Agewmariam watershed, consistent with recommendations from various researchers [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50]]. For instance Ref. [46], suggested reducing it to 0.05 for the Soil-Water-Balance (SWB) version 2 model [47], proposed a value of 0.03 for Indian conditions [48,49], recommended values of 0.014 for Greece and Czech Republic respectively, and [50] proposed a value of 0.01 for the Ethiopian highlands.

The analysis of the hydrologic modeling results indicated that the discharge is influenced by both rainfall intensity and amount, introducing hidden variability and errors in the estimation of runoff depth and peak discharge. These observations are in line with the findings of [14], which highlighted the limitations of the NRCS curve number method in accommodating the variability in rainfall intensity, particularly in semiarid regions such as the study area. The modeling of runoff peak discharge also exhibited notable deviations (refer to Fig. 2), prompting the need for alternative approaches, other than the graphical peak discharge estimation method, to better capture the influence of rainfall distribution variability.

Estimated and observed runoff discharge and peak discharge graph for calibration and validation periods.

The geographic location and topography of the Agewmariam watershed within the Ethiopian highlands play a significant role in influencing rainfall intensities and distributions, as does the applicability of empirical hydrologic relations and methods [28]. Therefore, it is essential for designers and watershed managers to employ hydrologic modeling approaches and parameters that are tailored to the specific conditions of the study region, rather than relying solely on generalized methods. Consistent with [51,52], the insights gained from this study suggest that the direct use of the NRCS-developed type II design storm curve may not be suitable for the watershed conditions in the Agewmariam area. Even after calibration, the estimated unit peak discharge required further refinement to better match the observed data, emphasizing the need for location-specific adjustments and the incorporation of regional characteristics in the hydrologic modeling process.

Building on the findings of this study, future research should focus on the development of more comprehensive hydrologic modeling approaches that can better accommodate the variability in rainfall intensity and distribution within the Agewmariam watershed in the Ethiopian Highlands. This may involve the development of a representative design storm curve, the integration of additional parameters, or the adaptation of existing methods to capture the unique characteristics of the study region. Collaboration with local experts and the incorporation of long-term monitoring data may also help to refine the modeling parameters and enhance the applicability of the results to other watersheds within the region.

Soil erodibility factor (K)

In the study of the watershed, five distinct textural classes were identified, including Sandy Loam, Sandy Clay Loam, Loam, Loamy Sand, and Sand. The organic carbon content across these classes varied from 0.02 % to 3.18 % (refer to Fig. 3). Moreover, the soil erodibility factor (K) values ranged from 0.12 to 0.237, falling within the range established by previous research [32] of 0.1–0.5 Mg ha−1 MJ−1 mm−1.

Soil texture, organic carbon, and soil erodibility factor (K) maps.

When comparing these findings with those of prior studies conducted in the same watershed [36], a higher erodibility factor value was observed than the previously reported range of 0.079–0.173. This disparity could be attributed to the utilization of different formulas for determining the soil erodibility factor (K). The selected formula enabled calculations even in the absence of soil permeability and structure data; however, it imposed a limitation by restricting the results within the range of 0.1–0.5. In contrast, other sources suggest a broader range extending from 0 to 0.7 [53].

The investigation highlighted that the region with the highest erodibility factor value is situated in the upper northwestern part of the watershed (refer to Fig. 3), signifying a heightened vulnerability to erosion in this area. This particular zone is characterized by a low organic matter content and the presence of fine soil particles that are easily transportable by water. Conversely, towards the southeast side of the watershed, the erodibility value (K) diminishes and reaches its minimum on the eastern side where a higher proportion of sand particles exist, rendering them less prone to transport and runoff generation.

The slope length & steepness factor (LS)

The analysis of the slope length and steepness (LS) factor was a critical component of this study, as it plays a crucial role in influencing soil erosion processes within the Agewmariam watershed. Previous studies have confirmed that increasing the slope gradient and slope length cause higher overland flow velocities, resulting in increased soil erosion and a higher LS factor [30,36,[54], [55], [56]]. Additionally, the specific methods used for calculating the LS factor can influence the values obtained [57].

The results of this study show that the watershed's LS factor value ranges from 0 to 791 (see Fig. 4), with an area-weighted average of 19, indicating the overall slope characteristics of the area. This differs from the findings of [36], who reported LS factor values ranging from 0 to 311 for the same Agewmariam watershed, with an average LS factor of 16.33. The difference in the LS factor range and average can be attributed to the use of different DEM resolutions in the two studies. This study utilized a higher-resolution 2-m DEM, which was able to capture the local topographic features more accurately, leading to a wider range and higher average LS factor values compared to the coarser 30-m DEM used by Ref. [36]. This is in line with the findings of [58], who noted that as the DEM resolution and accuracy increase, the landscape will be more accurately described, and erosion estimates will approach actual values.

Elevation, slope class, and slope length & steepness factor (LS) maps.

Interestingly, a large portion of the watershed, approximately 99.5 % of the total area, exhibited LS factor values below 50, while only a small fraction (0.5 %) had values exceeding 50, highlighting the presence of localized areas with heightened erosion risk. The areas with higher LS values were characterized by natural streamlines and hilly terrain with significant slope changes (see Fig. 4), demonstrating the influence of local topographic features on soil erosion. These findings align with the results of [36], who also noted that the northern and southern parts of the study area, along with hillsides and gullies, had higher LS values and represented higher susceptibility to erosion.

By utilizing a high-resolution DEM (2-m scale) in this study, the researchers were able to capture the high variability in slope conditions more accurately, mitigating edge effects during watershed delineation and enabling better identification of areas at risk of soil erosion, as noted in earlier studies [35,58,59]. The improved representation of terrain features at this fine resolution provided valuable insights into the spatial distribution of slope characteristics within the Agewmariam watershed. These findings highlight the importance of considering local topographic features in assessing soil erosion risks and emphasize the significance of high-quality DEM data in enhancing the accuracy of erosion modeling and risk assessment processes using the ArcGIS environment [60]. High-resolution DEM, such as those generated using Google Earth elevation data, should be used, because they can provide better relief information, especially for areas with flat topography where adequate data may not be available or are too costly to generate [35].

In conclusion, the results of this study demonstrate the importance of using high-resolution DEM data to accurately capture local topographic features and their influence on soil erosion processes within the Agewmariam watershed. The findings highlight the presence of localized areas with higher LS factor values, indicating heightened erosion risk, and underscore the significance of considering these spatial variations in informing erosion mitigation strategies and land management practices.

Cover factor (C)

The study area consists of five distinct land use/land cover types, each of which is allocated a unique cover factor (C) value (refer to Table 4) as outlined in previous research [30,36,61]. Notably, the bare land located in the upper south and middle regions of the watershed stands out with a cover factor value of 1, signifying a heightened vulnerability to erosion processes. In contrast, the cultivated land, which encompasses the largest portion of the area, displays varied cover factor values depending on the specific crop types cultivated (refer to Fig. 5). Additionally, the scattered bush and dense bushlands are attributed Cover Factor values of 0.14 and 0.012, respectively, indicating a comparatively lower susceptibility to erosion dynamics.

Land use and cover factor (c) maps.

The distribution of cover factor values across the different land use/land cover types within the watershed provides valuable insights into the potential erosion risks associated with each category. The pronounced susceptibility to erosion observed in the bare land areas underscores the need for targeted erosion control measures in these regions to mitigate soil loss and environmental degradation [30]. Moreover, the varying cover factor values assigned to the cultivation land highlight the nuanced impact of different crop types on erosion vulnerability, suggesting the importance of tailored land management strategies based on specific agricultural practices [36].

Furthermore, the contrasting cover factor values allocated to the scattered bush and dense bushlands emphasize the role of vegetation density in influencing erosion susceptibility within the watershed [61]. Areas characterized by denser vegetation cover exhibit lower cover factor values, indicating a higher degree of protection against erosive forces compared to more open or sparse vegetation landscapes. This distinction underscores the significance of vegetation conservation and restoration efforts in enhancing soil stability and erosion resistance across different land use categories in the study area.

Support practice factor (P)

The support practice factors in the study were assigned based on specific land use type and land slope. Bushland, scattered bush, and settlement areas were given a support practice factor of 0.8, as referenced in Refs. [36,62]. Additionally, cultivation land was divided into five classes depending on slope (see Table 1), with distinct support practice factor values allocated to each class according to Ref. [35]. Notably, the highest support practice factor values were identified in the middle sections of the watershed, particularly in bushlands and steep slope cultivation lands. Conversely, the lowest values were observed in the upper and lower parts, which correspond to flat slope cultivation lands implementing terracing practices (refer to Fig. 6).

Sediment yield estimation and model evaluation

Sediment yield estimation

Estimating sediment yield is a critical focus of this study, as it provides insights into the erosion dynamics within the Agewmariam watershed. To improve the accuracy of sediment yield prediction using the MUSLE model, the model parameters were calibrated for the local conditions based on measured data from the watershed, as suggested by Refs. [[63], [64], [65], [66]]. Furthermore, in the regionalization of the MUSLE model, parameters directly affecting erosion, such as cover, conservation practices, soil erodibility, and topography were estimated using literature and comparative methods; in contrast, the parameters without direct impact on the erosion process, namely the coefficient 'a' and exponent 'b', were determined through calibration, as outlined in Ref. [63]. By calibrating the coefficients related to the runoff energy factor and sediment loss rate, modifications were made to the original MUSLE formula to enhance its representation of sediment yield within the watershed and the modified formula (Eq (16)) is now presented as follows:

where: Y represents the sediment yield from an individual storm in metric tons, Q is the storm runoff volume in m3, qp denotes the peak runoff rate in m3/sec, K signifies the soil erodibility factor, LS stands for the slope length and gradient factor, C represents the crop management factor, and P is the erosion-control-practice factor.

The original MUSLE formula typically uses a coefficient of 11.8 and an exponent of 0.56 for the runoff energy factor [12]. However, in this study, the exponent was recalibrated to 0.59 based on the specific characteristics of the Agewmariam watershed. This modification is in line with the findings of previous studies, where researchers have adjusted the exponent to better represent the local conditions. For example [66], increased the exponent from 0.56 to 0.75 [65], substituted it with 0.65, and [64] used 0.613 [63]. also concluded that the best exponent (b) of the MUSLE for the Ethiopian condition was 0.59. These modifications were made to better represent the relationship between the runoff energy factor and sediment yield for the local conditions of the Agewmariam watershed.

Similarly, the coefficient in front of the formula was changed from the original 11.8 to 1. This calibration was based on a comprehensive analysis of watershed characteristics and previous research findings [66]. recommended that the coefficients of the runoff term could be estimated from the climate and hydrologic characteristics of the basins. Accordingly [65], decreased the coefficient to 0.79, and [64] used 5.43. The recalibration of the coefficient to 1 for the Agewmariam watershed provided a better fit to the observed data without compromising the overall model performance.

The modifications made to the MUSLE equation were based on a comprehensive analysis of the Agewmariam watershed's characteristics and previous research [[63], [64], [65], [66]], which was necessary to enhance the model's representation of sediment yield dynamics (see Table 5 and Fig. 7), given the watershed's unique environmental and hydrological conditions. During the calibration period, the mean observed and estimated suspended sediment yields were 0.2 and 0.23 ton/ha, respectively. It was observed that the model slightly overestimated the overall sediment yield compared to the observed values. Conversely, during the validation period, the mean observed and estimated suspended sediment yields were 0.7 and 0.53 ton/ha, respectively, indicating some underestimation in the predicted values (see Table 5). The consistently low p-values (<0.05) for the slope coefficients during calibration and validation indicate the model effectively captured the relationships between observed and estimated parameters, suggesting a robust model fit and high predictive capability that instils confidence in the model's ability to reliably estimate sediment yield and other hydrological variables within the watershed.

The variability in sediment yield can be attributed to the temporal distribution of rainfall intensity within the area as highlighted in Ref. [66]. Notably, the simulation of two runoff events with identical rainfall amounts resulted in different sediment measurements (see Table 5), yet the prediction values remained consistent, underscoring the influence of rainfall intensity on the accuracy of the predictions. Rainfall intensity is a crucial factor in determining the erosive power of precipitation and its impact on sediment detachment and transport. Higher rainfall intensities can generate higher peak runoff rates and increased surface flow velocities, leading to enhanced sediment mobilization and transport, while lower rainfall intensities may result in reduced erosive forces and lower sediment yield. The model's sensitivity to rainfall intensity highlights the importance of accurately representing the temporal distribution of rainfall events in the sediment yield estimation process. Factors such as the duration, frequency, and intensity of rainfall can significantly impact the model's ability to accurately predict sediment yield.

Model evaluation

The sensitivity analysis conducted in this study, in accordance with previous findings [14], highlighted the runoff energy factor as the most influential parameter, closely followed by the slope length and steepness factor [36,54]. Notably, factors such as runoff, slope length and gradient, crop management, and erosion control practices significantly influenced the model outputs [67], whereas the initial abstraction-to-storage ratio exhibited the least sensitivity, primarily due to its narrow range of input values (refer to Table 6). It is essential to recognize that the sensitivity of a particular parameter is often interconnected with the values of other related parameters, underscoring the limitations of employing a one-at-a-time analysis approach [36]. Therefore, adopting alternative sensitivity analysis techniques could offer a more holistic assessment of the model's behavior.

Conclusion and recommendation

This comprehensive study on the application of the MUSLE model in the Agewmariam watershed provided valuable insights into the determinants of sediment yield, emphasizing the significance of considering local hydrologic conditions, soil characteristics, topographic features, land cover types, and erosion control practices to accurately predict sediment loss.

The calibration and validation processes of the MUSLE model revealed the need to adjust specific parameters, such as the initial abstraction-to-storage ratio and curve number values, to align the modeling of runoff depth and peak discharge with the local conditions. This study also highlights the importance of calibrating the MUSLE model's coefficient values and exploring alternative peak discharge estimation methods, particularly incorporating rainfall distribution type curves suitable for Ethiopian conditions, as crucial steps for enhancing the model's performance. Furthermore, the use of fine-resolution digital elevation model (DEM) data for terrain representation was found to be essential for improving the accuracy of the model in estimating sediment yield.

Despite some limitations in the accuracy of the model, the model remains a valuable tool for prioritizing soil and water conservation interventions within the Agewmariam watershed and can be extrapolated to neighboring watersheds with similar characteristics. Refining the model input parameters, such as runoff energy, soil erodibility, slope length and steepness, cover management, and support practice factors, through the collection of more detailed watershed data, and ensuring appropriate calibration can contribute to increasing the prediction accuracy of the MUSLE model. Additionally, further research is recommended to assess the long-term performance and reliability of the calibrated MUSLE model in predicting sediment yield under various land use and management scenarios within the Agewmariam watershed and similar regions. Finally, the exploration of alternative peak discharge estimation methods and the incorporation of rainfall distribution type curves suitable for Ethiopian conditions can enhance the applicability and accuracy of the MUSLE model in the region, contributing to improved sediment yield estimations, enhanced erosion risk assessment processes, and the development of sustainable land management practices.
