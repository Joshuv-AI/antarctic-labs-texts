Comparison of compaction techniques for filling isthmuses using Biodentine in mandibular molars
Authors: Evren Sarıyılmaz, Öznur Sarıyılmaz, Rüya Sessiz, Gülşah Uslu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to compare the filling volume and surface area of Biodentine placed in mandibular molar isthmuses using three different compaction techniques (manual, ultrasonic, and sonic endomotor system).Materials and methodsThirty resin mandibular molars with simulated apical-third isthmuses were randomly assigned to three compaction groups: manual, ultrasonic, and sonic endomotor system. Biodentine was compacted manually, with indirect ultrasonic activation, or using a sonic endomotor system. All specimens were scanned with high-resolution CBCT, and filling volumes and surface areas were quantified in 3D Slicer. Data were analyzed with one-way ANOVA.ResultsWhile the sonic endomotor group showed the highest mean filling volume (8.11 ± 0.79 mm³), followed by the ultrasonic (7.93 ± 1.52 mm³) and manual (7.33 ± 0.80 mm³) groups, no statistically significant differences were found among the groups for either filling volume or surface area.ConclusionsUnder the conditions tested, no statistically significant differences were detected among the three compaction techniques in terms of filling volume or surface area. No superiority of ultrasonic or sonic activation over manual compaction was demonstrated within the resolution limits of this CBCT-based methodology.Keywords: Calcium silicate cement, Cone-beam computed tomography, Ultrasonic activation, Sonic activation, 3D Slicer

Introduction

One of the main causes of endodontic treatment failure is the inability to adequately clean and hermetically seal the complex anatomy of the root canal system [1]. However, anatomical variations such as ramifications and isthmuses may compromise these objectives. An isthmus is a narrow, ribbon-shaped structure connecting two or more root canals, and due to the presence of pulp tissue, debris, and microorganisms, it may predispose the tooth to persistent infections and treatment failure [2, 3].

The prevalence and morphological characteristics of isthmuses vary depending on the diagnostic method used and the type of tooth examined. Isthmuses are most frequently observed in the mesial roots of mandibular molars and the mesiobuccal roots of maxillary molars [4]. The prevalence of isthmuses in the mesial roots of mandibular molars has been reported to range from 40% to 100% [3–7], being particularly concentrated in the apical third [3, 8, 9].

Filling of isthmuses during root canal treatment poses a challenge beyond canal shaping and irrigation protocols. Therefore, selecting appropriate filling material and placement technique is crucial [10]. Because the lateral compaction technique is often insufficient to effectively fill isthmuses, the continuous wave of condensation technique and calcium silicate–based biomaterials have gained prominence [10–12]. Calcium silicate–based biomaterials are widely used in endodontics for their excellent biocompatibility and superior sealing potential [13]. Among these materials, Biodentine exhibits biological properties comparable to those of MTA and has emerged as a reliable alternative because of its ease of handling and favorable clinical performance [14].

Although orthograde filling of root canal isthmuses with calcium silicate–based materials is not a routine endodontic procedure, isthmuses may serve as a standardized model for evaluating material placement in narrow, irregular, and difficult-to-access spaces [15]. Calcium silicate–based cements such as MTA and Biodentine are used clinically in situations that require adaptation within complex anatomical defects, including apexification/apical plug procedures, perforation repair, internal root resorption defects, and root-end filling [16–17]. These materials are favored in such indications because of their biocompatibility, sealing ability, and capacity to set in moist environments, making them suitable for challenging endodontic conditions [17]. Therefore, evaluating how different compaction techniques affect Biodentine placement within a standardized isthmus model may provide preliminary information relevant to the handling of calcium silicate–based materials in irregular anatomical spaces.

Previous studies investigating ultrasonic activation during placement of calcium silicate–based materials have reported conflicting results. Some studies suggested improved condensation quality and reduced porosity, whereas others found no clear advantage over manual placement [11, 18–25]. Because adaptation of these materials within narrow anatomical spaces depends not only on the amount of material placed but also on its distribution along the cavity walls, filling volume and surface area were considered relevant quantitative parameters for assessment.

A new endomotor, 812 MT (Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Guilin, China), has recently been developed to perform sonic activation. According to the manufacturer, the device combines sonic vibration with rotary motion to facilitate the delivery, placement, and compaction of MTA in anatomically narrow and complex areas such as lateral canals [26]. Recent evidence indicates that sonic-assisted compaction may affect the placement of calcium silicate–based materials in challenging endodontic models; however, the reported findings remain inconsistent [27, 28]. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has specifically evaluated Biodentine compaction in standardized mandibular molar isthmus cavities while also assessing the performance of the 812 MT system in this anatomical context.

This study aimed to compare the filling volume and surface area of Biodentine placed in mandibular molar isthmuses using three different compaction techniques (manual, ultrasonic, and sonic endomotor system).

Evaluation was conducted using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) in mandibular molar isthmuses. The null hypothesis stated that there would be no difference in filling volume among the compaction techniques.

Materials and methods

The sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1 software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) based on an effect size (f) of 0.920 derived from the study [29]. An F-test family with a significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.95 was applied, yielding a minimum sample size of eight specimens per group. To increase the planned statistical power, ten samples were included in each group.

Standardized resin tooth models were preferred to minimize anatomical variability and to allow reproducible comparison of the compaction techniques under controlled experimental conditions. A total of 30 resin mandibular molar teeth containing isthmuses were used in this study. The mesial root canals were designed with a size of 40.04 and terminating separately at the apical region. An isthmus measuring 3 mm in height, located 2 mm above the root apex, was created in the apical third of the mesial root during the fabrication of the resin teeth (Fig. 1a and b). The specimens were then divided into three groups according to the Biodentine compaction technique employed. Before the placement of Biodentine, the transparent resin teeth were positioned in the sockets of an endodontic training model to ensure stable positioning and prevent operator bias during the filling procedures (Fig. 1c).

a Resin mandibular molar model with a simulated isthmus located in the apical third. b Buccal view of the resin tooth showing the mesial root configuration. c The teeth were positioned in the sockets of an endodontic training model to ensure stable placement during the filling procedures

Manual compaction

Biodentine (Septodont, Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, France) was prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions by mixing the powder with five drops of liquid in a capsule using an amalgamator for 30 s. Biodentin was delivered incrementally into the mesiobuccal and mesiolingual canals using a size 1 (red) MAP System carrier (Produits Dentaires SA, Vevey, Switzerland). After placement of each increment, compaction was performed with a size 50 hand plugger (Fanta Dental, Shanghai, China). The procedure was continued until the isthmus was filled and the material reached 3 mm coronally beyond the filled isthmus region.

Ultrasonic compaction

Biodentine (Septodont, Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, France) was prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions by mixing the powder with five drops of liquid in a capsule using an amalgamator for 30 s. Biodentin was delivered incrementally into the mesiobuccal and mesiolingual canals using a size 1 (red) MAP System carrier and condensed with a size 50 hand plugger. During condensation, indirect ultrasonic activation was performed by contacting the ultrasonic tip (ETBD, Acteon Group, France) with the plugger for 10 s, repeated three times during the filling process. Ultrasonic activation was applied using the VDW Ultra device (VDW, Munich, Germany) at 30% power. The filling was then extended 3 mm coronally beyond the filled isthmus region.

Sonic endomotor system (812 MT) compaction

Biodentine (Septodont, Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, France) was prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions by mixing the powder with five drops of liquid in a capsule using an amalgamator for 30 s. Biodentin was delivered incrementally into the mesiobuccal and mesiolingual canals using a size 1 (red) MAP System carrier. A ProTaper X3 file (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was attached to the 812 MT sonic endomotor system (Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Guilin, China) and operated according to the manufacturer’s instructions in T-mode+, a preset sequence mode designed to assist placement of calcium silicate–based materials. In this mode, the device cycles through preset movement patterns, including SGP, counterclockwise (CCW), and clockwise (CW) motion, as the operator advances through the filling procedure [26]. The file was inserted into the canals up to the apical third at the manufacturer-recommended 90° SGP and 150 rpm. Biodentine was compacted using a counterclockwise (CCW) up-and-down motion at 100 rpm with sonic activation for 10 s. To reduce voids trapped in the apical third, a clockwise (CW) motion with sonic activation was applied between cycles. After each activation cycle, the material was further condensed using a size 50 hand plugger to improve adaptation. This procedure was repeated three times during the filling process until the isthmus was filled and the material extended 3 mm coronally beyond the isthmus region.

After the completion of the filling procedures, the coronal access cavities were sealed with a temporary restorative material, and the specimens were stored at 37 °C in 100% humidity for one week to ensure complete setting of the material.

Subsequently, each tooth was positioned in a silicone mold for stabilization during imaging, and CBCT scans were obtained. All CBCT image acquisitions were performed by an experienced dentomaxillofacial radiologist using a NewTom 5G XL unit (Verona, Italy) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, under the following parameters: 110 kV, 0.1-mm slice thickness, 8 × 5 cm field of view, and 9-s exposure time. A high-resolution scanning protocol was used, and the images were exported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format.

To quantify the amount of Biodentine within the isthmus and subsequent apical root canal segment, the DICOM files were imported into 3D Slicer software (version 5.4.0; Surgical Planning Laboratory, Harvard University, Boston, MA, USA). Due to the inherent variability of grayscale values in CBCT imaging and the lack of stable Hounsfield Unit (HU) calibration [30], a fixed threshold could not be applied across all datasets. Therefore, for each CBCT scan, an experienced dentomaxillofacial radiologist with eight years of experience determined the threshold range that best represented the dental material. For image analysis, the segmented region was standardized using the canal terminus as the apical boundary and the upper border of the isthmus as the coronal boundary. The filled isthmus and canal segments were semi-automatically segmented in three dimensions, with manual refinement performed to remove incorrectly included structures or artifacts. The filling volume and surface area were calculated and recorded, with surface area serving as a complementary parameter to reflect the extent of material distribution along the isthmus and canal walls (Fig. 2). To assess intra-examiner repeatability of the segmentation measurements, volumetric and surface area measurements were repeated in 10 randomly selected specimens. Reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement. The ICC values were 0.878 (95% CI: 0.577–0.968; p < 0.001) for filling volume and 0.815 (95% CI: 0.426–0.950; p = 0.001) for filling surface area.

a Three-dimensional reconstruction of the isthmus and apical canal segments in the CBCT image, b the three-dimensional reconstruction of the same tooth obtained after segmentation in 3D Slicer software

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was verified with Levene’s test. Since the assumptions for parametric analysis were met, one-way ANOVA was used to compare the three groups separately for filling volume and filling surface area. When a significant overall difference was found, Tukey’s HSD test was planned for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The mean filling volumes and surface areas of the fillings for each group are presented in Table 1. One-way ANOVA showed no statistically significant difference among the manual, ultrasonic, and sonic endomotor system compaction groups in terms of filling volume (p = 0.260) or filling surface area (p = 0.428). The sonic endomotor compaction group showed the highest mean filling volume (8.11 ± 0.79 mm³), followed by the ultrasonic (7.93 ± 1.52 mm³) and manual (7.33 ± 0.80 mm³) compaction groups. The manual, ultrasonic, and sonic endomotor compaction groups showed mean surface areas of 28.61 ± 2.36 mm², 29.33 ± 4.01 mm², and 30.42 ± 2.58 mm², respectively.

Discussion

This study aimed to compare the filling volume of Biodentine placed in mandibular molar isthmuses using three different compaction techniques: manual, ultrasonic, and sonic endomotor. The null hypothesis, which stated that there would be no significant differences in filling volume or surface area among the compaction methods, was accepted. Within the limitations of this CBCT-based in vitro model, no significant differences were detected among the compaction techniques in terms of isthmus filling. These findings suggest that, under standardized laboratory conditions, ultrasonic and sonic-assisted techniques did not demonstrate superiority over manual compaction. However, the results should be interpreted cautiously, as the spatial resolution of CBCT may not allow reliable detection of microvoids or subtle interfacial defects.

The mesial roots of mandibular molars, particularly in the apical third, exhibit the highest prevalence and morphological complexity of isthmuses, which are clinically significant structures that may complicate cleaning and shaping procedures [3, 4]. Accordingly, based on the classification proposed by Fan et al. (2010), a 3-mm-high sheet-like (Type I) isthmus located in the apical third was designed in the resin mandibular molar models to standardize the experimental setup [3]. In most previous studies investigating isthmus filling, extracted natural molars were used to simulate clinical conditions. In the present study, the use of resin teeth allowed precise control of canal dimensions and isthmus geometry, eliminating the natural anatomical variability observed in extracted teeth and ensuring homogeneity among the experimental groups [12].

Oh et al. (2016) evaluated three different obturation techniques in the mesial roots of mandibular molars using micro-CT and reported that filling the isthmus regions was more challenging than filling the main canals [10]. They found that both the continuous wave technique and MTA obturation produced more homogeneous fillings with fewer voids in the apical third compared with the cold lateral compaction technique. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2022) demonstrated that, in band-shaped isthmuses, the continuous wave technique achieved superior filling quality compared with the single-cone and lateral condensation techniques [12], whereas Keleş et al. (2018) found no significant difference between warm vertical compaction and the single-cone technique in terms of filling quality or void ratio [11].

To date, no study has directly investigated the influence of the placement technique of calcium silicate–based cements on isthmus filling. From a clinical perspective, the present study should not be interpreted as advocating routine orthograde sealing of root canal isthmuses with Biodentine. Rather, the standardized isthmus model was used as a reproducible representation of a narrow and irregular anatomical space in which placement of a calcium silicate–based material may be technically challenging [15]. Calcium silicate–based cements such as MTA and Biodentine are clinically used in situations involving complex anatomical defects, including apical plug procedures, perforation repair, internal root resorption defects, and root-end filling [16–31]. In this respect, the present model may provide preliminary information relevant to the handling and placement of such materials in difficult-to-access spaces rather than simulating a routine clinical procedure. However, Keleş et al. (2018) evaluated gutta-percha obturation performed with the continuous wave of condensation technique and MTA placement using manual and indirect ultrasonic methods in the mesial roots of mandibular molars with Vertucci Type II configurations through micro-CT analysis [11]. That study found no significant differences among techniques in the apical region and, consistent with the present findings, reported that indirect ultrasonic energy had no notable effect on the quality of calcium silicate–based material placement in the isthmus areas.

In perforated internal resorption cavities, indirect ultrasonic activation used for the placement of MTA and Biodentine was also reported to produce no superior results compared with manual compaction [22]. Similarly, when calcium silicate–based cements were placed as apical plugs, no significant difference in marginal adaptation was observed between manual compaction and indirect ultrasonic activation [24]. Consistent with the present findings, these studies demonstrated that, in both perforation repair and apical plug applications, ultrasonic activation and manual condensation exhibited comparable effectiveness.

One of the first studies to investigate the effect of ultrasonic energy on MTA compaction was conducted by Aminoshariae et al. (2003), who compared direct ultrasonic activation with manual compaction and reported that the manual technique was superior to direct ultrasonic activation applied to the material [23]. Subsequent studies, however, have reported conflicting findings. For example, Yeung et al. (2006) filled acrylic blocks with MTA along the entire canal length and demonstrated that indirect ultrasonic activation produced a denser filling than the manual technique [19]. Dinçer et al. (2020) compared MTA and Biodentine as coronal barrier materials using micro-CT analysis and found that ultrasonic activation significantly reduced microporosity in the Biodentine group, although it did not result in a marked improvement in marginal adaptation [18]. These discrepancies among studies may be attributed to differences in evaluation methods (e.g., weight measurement, radiographic analysis, or micro-CT imaging) and experimental variables such as the location of the applied material (apical vs. coronal), the filled volume, canal geometry, and the intensity of the ultrasonic energy applied.

More recent studies have also suggested that activation-assisted placement may influence the behavior of calcium silicate–based materials, although the results remain inconsistent. For example, ultrasonic activation has been associated with improved condensation quality, reduced void formation under certain application times, and changes in interfacial performance, whereas other studies have reported no clear superiority over conventional placement depending on the material, evaluation method, and clinical simulation model [32–34].

The handling characteristics of Biodentine should be considered when interpreting the present findings, because this material is sensitive to manipulation variables. Previous evidence has shown that ultrasonic activation can affect the physicochemical properties of Biodentine, including setting time, flow, dimensional change, ultrastructural morphology, and calcium release [35]. In addition, placement technique may influence interfacial behavior, as ultrasonic placement has been associated with higher push-out bond strength than manual placement, and indirect ultrasonic activation has been reported to improve bond strength and tubular penetration of Biodentine [20, 36]. Moreover, changes in manipulation protocol itself have been shown to alter the physical, chemical, and microstructural characteristics of Biodentine [36]. Therefore, the differences observed among the compaction techniques in the present study may be partly related not only to the mode of condensation but also to the sensitivity of Biodentine to manipulation conditions and time-dependent changes in consistency during placement [35, 37].

The 812 MT (Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Guilin, China) is a recently introduced endomotor designed to provide sonic activation during the placement of calcium silicate–based materials. In the present study, neither the 812 MT system nor ultrasonic activation demonstrated superiority over manual compaction in terms of isthmus filling. Recent evidence suggests that sonic-assisted rotary compaction may improve certain placement-related outcomes in other models; for example, in simulated open-apex teeth, sonic-activated rotary compaction showed the lowest open porosity and the greatest time efficiency among three MTA placement techniques, although it also produced the highest closed porosity [27]. In contrast, in a 3D-printed perforating internal root resorption model, manual, indirect ultrasonic, and 812 MT compaction techniques produced no statistically significant differences in obturation quality [28]. Therefore, the findings related to the 812 MT system should be considered exploratory and interpreted within the limitations of this CBCT-based in vitro model. Although no statistical superiority was demonstrated in the present study, sonic or ultrasonic systems may still offer practical advantages in selected clinical situations, particularly when calcium silicate–based materials must be delivered into narrow, irregular, or difficult-to-access spaces. Potential benefits may include facilitated apical delivery, improved handling in confined areas, and greater procedural convenience; however, these possible advantages remain speculative within the limits of the present in vitro model. Further studies are needed to determine whether different sonic parameters, application times, or experimental conditions may influence the placement performance of calcium silicate–based materials in complex root canal anatomies.

From a clinical perspective, the findings suggest that activation-assisted compaction should not be assumed to provide superior filling of narrow isthmus-like spaces when compared with careful manual compaction. However, because the present model does not reproduce all biological and clinical variables of natural teeth, the results should be regarded as preliminary information relevant to material handling in irregular endodontic defects rather than direct evidence for routine clinical decision-making. Future studies should also evaluate whether activation-assisted systems provide procedural advantages, such as reduced placement time, improved ease of handling, or greater operator convenience, because these outcomes were not assessed in the present study.

In this study, CBCT was used as the evaluation method because it enabled non-destructive three-dimensional assessment of the filled isthmus regions in a standardized experimental model and provided a practical and accessible imaging approach. However, compared with micro-CT, which is widely considered the gold standard for detecting voids and microstructural defects in obturation studies, CBCT has lower spatial resolution. Therefore, although CBCT was suitable for overall volumetric assessment, it may not have been sufficiently sensitive to detect small cavities, microvoids or subtle interfacial defects. In addition, the hydration behavior of calcium silicate–based materials may differ in resin models from that in natural dentin. Another methodological limitation is that, although the manual compaction procedure was performed using the same carrier, hand plugger size, and filling extent, the exact magnitude and direction of the compaction force could not be strictly standardized. Operator-dependent variation in manual pressure may therefore have influenced material adaptation and the comparability of the manual compaction group with the activation-assisted groups. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to clinical conditions.

Another limitation is that the effect size used for sample size calculation was relatively large and was derived from a previous study conducted under different experimental conditions. Although this approach provided an a priori basis for sample size estimation and the number of specimens was increased beyond the calculated minimum, the study may still have been insufficiently sensitive to detect smaller but potentially relevant intergroup differences. Therefore, the possibility of a Type II error should be considered when interpreting the nonsignificant findings, and these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence among the techniques.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this CBCT-based in vitro study, no statistically significant differences were detected among manual, ultrasonic, and sonic endomotor compaction techniques in terms of filling volume or surface area in mandibular molar isthmuses. Under the conditions tested, no superiority of ultrasonic or sonic-assisted compaction over manual compaction was demonstrated. These findings should be interpreted within the resolution limits of the methodology.
