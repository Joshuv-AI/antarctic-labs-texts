Prostatic morphometric parameters and ureteral access sheath placement failure during retrograde intrarenal surgery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ahmet Yıldırım Balık, Necati Ekici, Burak Ayvacık, Arda Taşkın Taşkıran, Mehmet Ali Özel, Ekrem Başaran, Dursun Baba, Alpaslan Yüksel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to evaluate the potential effect of benign prostatic hyperplasia-related prostatic morphometric parameters on ureteral access sheath (UAS) placement failure in male patients scheduled for retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS).MethodsBetween September 2019 and March 2025, 151 male patients scheduled for RIRS due to renal stones at a tertiary referral center were evaluated retrospectively after applying strict exclusion criteria to obtain a more homogeneous cohort. Patients with a preoperative ureteral stent, prior prostate surgery, alpha-blocker use, urethral or ureteral stricture, or unsuccessful passage of a 7.5 Fr semirigid ureteroscope were excluded. Patients were divided into UAS Successful (n = 93) and UAS Failure (n = 58) groups. Demographic characteristics, including body mass index (BMI), were obtained from the medical records. Hydronephrosis status and prostatic morphometric parameters, including intravesical prostatic protrusion (IPP), prostate volume (PV), prostatic urethral angle (PUA), and prostatic urethral length (PUL), were assessed on preoperative computed tomography by an experienced radiologist.ResultsIn comparative analyses, no statistically significant differences were found between the groups in terms of age, BMI, stone characteristics, or any of the assessed prostatic morphometric parameters (p > 0.05). Preoperative hydronephrosis was more frequent in the UAS Failure Group than in the UAS Successful Group (70.7% vs. 51.6%; p = 0.020). In univariable analysis, preoperative hydronephrosis was associated with higher odds of UAS placement failure (OR: 2.261; 95% CI: 1.127–4.537; p = 0.022). In the adjusted multivariable model, preoperative hydronephrosis remained independently associated with UAS placement failure (adjusted OR: 2.247; 95% CI: 1.103–4.581; p = 0.026), whereas none of the prostatic morphometric parameters showed an independent association. Length of hospital stay was significantly longer in the UAS Failure Group (p = 0.005).ConclusionProstatic morphometric parameters were not significantly associated with UAS placement failure, whereas preoperative hydronephrosis may be a potential factor associated with UAS placement failure. Given the selected nature of this cohort, larger multicenter studies including patients with a wider range of prostatic anatomy are needed to validate these findings.Keywords: Benign prostatic hyperplasia, Hydronephrosis, Intravesical prostatic protrusion, Prostatic urethral angle, Retrograde intrarenal surgery, Ureteral access sheath

Introduction

In recent years, with advances in endoscopic instrumentation and increasing surgical experience, retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) has become a widely used minimally invasive method, particularly for the treatment of renal stones ≤ 20 mm [1]. In RIRS, the ureteral access sheath (UAS) is frequently preferred because it reduces intrarenal pressure and provides effective drainage, while also improving visualization, potentially shortening operative time, and facilitating multiple re-entries into the collecting system [2]. However, the use of a UAS is not entirely risk-free. It has been reported that ureteral wall injuries ranging from simple mucosal erosion to smooth muscle perforation may occur, particularly during placement, and although rare, can lead to long-term complications such as ureteral stricture [3–6].

However, routine use of a UAS may not be feasible in every patient. This situation, defined in the literature as “UAS placement failure,” has been reported to vary widely between 4% and 23%, depending on the center and patient selection [7, 8]. The reasons for failure are generally associated with ureteral strictures, impacted stones, or the presence of a previously unstented ureter (virgin ureter). In addition, preoperative predictors of UAS placement success have been investigated in the literature; in the series by Yavuzsan et al., preoperative ipsilateral hydronephrosis, lower platelet count, and male gender were found to be associated with UAS placement failure [9]. Subsequently, a recent retrospective cohort study reported that factors such as body mass index (BMI) and ureteral orifice configuration may also influence the success of UAS placement [10]. Based on these findings, interest has grown in identifying objective and measurable anatomo-radiological markers that could better predict failure. In a recent study, pelvic anteroposterior diameter and pelvic lateral diameter measured on kidney-ureter-bladder (KUB) radiographs were reported to be associated with UAS placement failure [11].

These failure rates observed in male patients suggest that UAS placement difficulty may be related not only to bony anatomy and ureteral narrowing, but also to prostate-related changes in lower urinary tract anatomy. BPH is not confined solely to an increase in prostate volume (PV); it leads to complex morphological changes in parameters such as prostatic urethral length (PUL), prostatic urethral angle (PUA), and intravesical prostatic protrusion (IPP) [12–14]. It has been reported that particularly prominent IPP and increased PUA can elevate the bladder neck and trigone anatomy, causing a “J-hook” deformity in the distal ureter, and this anatomical distortion may create a mechanical barrier by restricting the maneuverability of endoscopic instruments [15]. Even in cases where a semirigid ureteroscope can be advanced smoothly and no obvious ureteral narrowing is identified, UAS advancement may still be difficult. In such cases, because failure cannot be explained solely by low ureteral caliber or fixed ureteral obstruction, more subtle anatomical factors, such as prostatic morphometry, bladder neck–trigonal relationships, and ureterovesical junction alignment, may become more relevant. Unlike a semirigid ureteroscope, which can be directed under direct visual guidance and has a certain degree of maneuverability, the UAS is advanced over a guidewire without active steerability and may therefore be more susceptible to prostate-related changes in bladder neck and trigonal anatomy. Accordingly, these anatomical changes may theoretically alter the alignment between the sheath, guidewire, ureteral orifice, and distal ureter, particularly in the presence of a fish-hook configuration.

Nevertheless, distal ureteral and ureteral-orifice characteristics may be more directly involved in UAS advancement than bladder outlet anatomy alone. In line with this concept, recent evidence suggests that pre-stenting and pharmacological ureteral relaxation may reduce access failure during ureteroscopy, supporting the potential importance of ureteral compliance and functional ureteral readiness in this process [16]. Therefore, the extent to which this theoretical mechanical interference translates into a “difficult ureter” scenario or UAS placement failure in clinical practice remains unclear.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to directly evaluate the relationship between the morphometric parameters of the prostate and prostatic urethra and UAS placement outcomes during RIRS, and to assess these measurements in relation to UAS placement failure. In this study, we aimed to investigate whether UAS placement failure during RIRS is associated with objective radiological measurements of prostatic anatomy and to determine whether any of these parameters are independently associated with UAS placement failure.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This single-center retrospective cohort study was carried out in the Urology Department of Düzce University Faculty of Medicine, a tertiary referral center. We reviewed the records of patients diagnosed with renal stones who were scheduled for retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) between September 2019 and March 2025. The study was approved by the local ethics committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

All consecutive patients scheduled for RIRS within the specified timeframe were screened using the hospital information system, anesthesia charts, and operative reports. The study included male patients aged 18 years or older who were scheduled for RIRS for renal stones, in whom an intraoperative attempt at UAS placement was made, and who had high-resolution noncontrast computed tomography (NCCT) images suitable for prostatic measurements. Patients were excluded if they had a history of ipsilateral RIRS or ureteroscopic intervention, a preoperative double J stent (secondary cases), prior prostate surgery, current alpha blocker use, or a diagnosis of urethral or ureteral stricture. Patients were also excluded when cystoscopy revealed ureteral orifice abnormalities, including orifice narrowing or stenosis, or orifice insufficiency. To reduce the probability of UAS failure being primarily attributable to an intrinsically narrow ureter or fixed ureteral narrowing, and to obtain a more uniform cohort, we additionally excluded cases in which a 7.5 Fr diagnostic semirigid ureteroscope could not be advanced smoothly along the ureter before the UAS attempt because of marked resistance suggestive of low ureteral caliber or ureteral narrowing. Surgical procedure and UAS placement protocol

All surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia in the lithotomy position following a standardized protocol. The operation started with diagnostic cystoscopy to visualize the ureteral orifice. A 0.035-inch, 150-cm hydrophilic-tipped guidewire (Sensor™, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) was then safely advanced into the target ureter under fluoroscopic guidance. Next, the ureter was entered over the guidewire using a 7.5 Fr semirigid ureteroscope (Karl Storz®, Tuttlingen, Germany). At this stage, the ureter was assessed along its course using the 7.5 Fr semirigid ureteroscope to evaluate ureteral caliber and to identify stone impaction, strictures, or any anatomical pathology that could create a mechanical barrier to UAS advancement. Patients with an organic ureteral stricture or a marked anatomical pathology that could prevent sheath advancement were excluded, thereby reducing the possibility that UAS failure was caused by a fixed ureteral obstruction. After confirming a normal ureteral caliber, the ureteroscope was withdrawn and a 9.5/11.5 Fr ureteral access sheath (Flexor®, Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA) was advanced over the guidewire. The 9.5/11.5 Fr UAS was used uniformly in all cases because this sheath size is among the smaller commonly used access sheaths in clinical practice; this approach was preferred to minimize the risk of ureteral trauma or ischemia and to reduce the potential confounding effect of sheath diameter when evaluating UAS placement failure. An attempt at UAS placement was performed in a uniform manner in all cases. To minimize the risk of ureteral injury, forceful advancement was avoided in cases in which marked resistance was encountered during sheath advancement, and no additional active dilation methods, such as balloon dilation or sequential dilators, were used. Depending on the surgeon’s intraoperative assessment, cases with unsuccessful UAS insertion were managed either by proceeding with flexible ureteroscopy without a UAS or by placing a ureteral double J stent and postponing RIRS to a second session.

Outcome measures and data collection

The primary endpoint of the study was defined as the success of ureteral access sheath (UAS) placement. UAS placement failure was defined as the inability to safely advance the 9.5/11.5 Fr UAS over the guidewire because of marked tightness or resistance at the level of the ureteral orifice and/or along any ureteral segment, and the attempt was stopped because of concern for potential ureteral injury. In contrast, UAS placement was considered successful when the sheath could be advanced through the ureteral orifice and along the ureter without marked resistance or the need for forceful manipulation. Standard maneuvers included gentle advancement of the sheath over the guidewire under fluoroscopic guidance without forceful rotation, active dilation, or repeated traumatic attempts. Accordingly, patients were divided into two groups: the UAS Successful Group (n = 93) and the UAS Failure Group (n = 58).

All clinical data were obtained from the hospital information system, anesthesia records, and operative notes. Demographic and clinical variables included age, BMI, stone laterality, and comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure, severe liver disease, and chronic kidney disease. Perioperative parameters such as ASA score, use of anticoagulant therapy, length of hospital stay, and the presence of intraoperative complications were also recorded. Variables related to stone burden and anatomical characteristics were assessed on preoperative high-resolution noncontrast computed tomography (NCCT) images. Accordingly, stone density (mean HU), number of stones, maximal stone diameter (mm), the second transverse diameter (mm), and stone surface area (mm²) were calculated.

Ipsilateral hydronephrosis was initially evaluated as absent or present on preoperative non-contrast CT images. In patients with ipsilateral hydronephrosis, severity was graded from Grade 1 to Grade 4 using a semiquantitative radiological grading system adapted from the basic morphological principles of the Society for Fetal Urology (SFU) classification [17] and similar to CT-based grading approaches previously used in adult stone disease studies [18, 19]. Grade 1 was defined as isolated renal pelvic dilatation without calyceal dilatation. Grade 2 was defined as renal pelvic dilatation accompanied by limited or mild calyceal dilatation. Grade 3 was defined as more diffuse dilatation of the renal pelvis and calyces, with fornicial blunting and/or papillary flattening. Grade 4 was defined as severe pelvicalyceal dilatation accompanied by renal parenchymal thinning. Representative CT examples of hydronephrosis grading are shown in Fig. 2.

Prostatic morphometric measurements which, were the main variables of interest in the study, were also obtained from the same NCCT images and were performed by a radiologist with at least 10 years of experience in urogenital radiology. The evaluated parameters included intravesical prostatic protrusion (IPP, mm); prostate dimensions in the right–left (R-L) (transverse), anteroposterior (A–P), and superoinferior (S-I) (craniocaudal) axes (mm); prostate volume (PV); prostatic urethral angle (PUA, degrees); and prostatic urethral length (PUL, mm), all of which were included in the analysis. The measurement techniques and representative examples are presented in Fig. 3. Prostate volume (PV) was calculated using the ellipsoid formula (R-L × A-P × S-I × 0.52).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented according to their distribution as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), and categorical variables as number and percentage. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables presented as mean ± SD were compared using the independent-samples t test, whereas variables presented as median (IQR) were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. As a sensitivity analysis, PV and IPP were dichotomized at 40 mL and 5 mm, respectively, and compared between the groups using the chi-square test. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied because the between-group comparisons were exploratory in nature; therefore, these findings were interpreted cautiously. To evaluate factors associated with UAS placement failure, univariable logistic regression analyses were first performed. Subsequently, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed by including BMI, preoperative hydronephrosis, and the main prostatic morphometric parameters, namely IPP, PV, PUA, and PUL. Preoperative hydronephrosis was entered into the primary regression model as a binary variable (absent vs. present) because only seven patients had Grade 3 hydronephrosis and no patients had Grade 4, limiting the suitability of an ordinal analysis. Hydronephrosis grade was therefore evaluated as a secondary exploratory variable. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted odds ratios (adjusted ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analyses of the study data were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA), and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For the sample size calculation, the UAS placement failure rate of 22% reported by Mogilevkin et al. [20] was used as the reference value. Assuming that the UAS failure rate in the present study could be 15% points higher than this reference value, the “Proportion: One Group: Difference from Constant” test was used. With an effect size of g = 0.15, a statistical power of 95%, and α = 0.05, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 123 patients. The calculation was performed using G*Power version 3.1.9.2. The final cohort of 151 patients exceeded the calculated minimum sample size.

Results

A total of 151 male patients who met the inclusion criteria were evaluated. The mean age was 46.57 ± 14.71 years (18–80). Overall, UAS placement was successful in 93 patients (61.6%), who comprised the UAS Successful Group, whereas failure was observed in 58 patients (38.4%), who comprised the UAS Failure Group. A comparison of demographic and clinical characteristics is presented in Table 1. No statistically significant differences were found between the groups in terms of age, BMI, stone laterality, ASA score, or comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and chronic kidney disease (p > 0.05). Mean BMI was 27.60 ± 6.00 kg/m² in the UAS Successful Group and 26.48 ± 5.19 kg/m² in the UAS Failure Group (p = 0.244). However, length of hospital stay was significantly longer in the UAS Failure Group (p = 0.005). Among the 58 patients with UAS placement failure, flexible ureteroscopy was continued without a UAS in 50 patients (86.2%), whereas ureteral double-J stenting followed by delayed RIRS was performed in 8 patients (13.8%).

In univariable logistic regression analysis, preoperative hydronephrosis was associated with an increased likelihood of UAS placement failure (OR: 2.261; 95% CI: 1.127–4.537; p = 0.022). Other evaluated demographic, stone-related, and prostatic morphometric parameters did not show a statistically significant association with UAS placement failure in univariable analyses. Subsequently, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed including BMI, preoperative hydronephrosis, and the main prostatic morphometric parameters, namely IPP, PV, PUA, and PUL. S-I, R-L, and A-P prostate diameters were not entered separately into the multivariable model because these measurements were used as components of prostate volume calculation, and their simultaneous inclusion with PV could increase multicollinearity. In this adjusted model, BMI was not independently associated with UAS placement failure (adjusted OR: 1.044; 95% CI: 0.981–1.112; p = 0.171). Preoperative hydronephrosis remained independently associated with UAS placement failure (adjusted OR: 2.247; 95% CI: 1.103–4.581; p = 0.026), corresponding to approximately 2.2-fold higher odds of failure. In contrast, none of the evaluated prostatic morphometric parameters were independently associated with UAS placement failure, including IPP (adjusted OR: 1.008; 95% CI: 0.879–1.156; p = 0.908), PV (adjusted OR: 1.016; 95% CI: 0.977–1.055; p = 0.420), PUA (adjusted OR: 1.004; 95% CI: 0.951–1.059; p = 0.876), and PUL (adjusted OR: 1.086; 95% CI: 0.986–1.196; p = 0.096) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, we found that demographic characteristics, including BMI, stone features, and detailed prostatic morphometric measurements, including IPP, PV, PUA, PUL, and prostate dimensions, were not significantly associated with UAS placement failure. In contrast, preoperative hydronephrosis remained independently associated with UAS placement failure in the adjusted multivariable model, corresponding to approximately 2.2-fold higher odds of failure. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly evaluate the relationship between BPH-related prostatic morphometric measurements and UAS placement failure during RIRS.

Our findings regarding prostatic morphometry are particularly relevant in the context of the ongoing debate on whether BPH-related anatomical changes influence surgical access. Although Scarpa et al. [21] suggested that a large prostate volume does not represent a definitive obstacle to semirigid ureteroscopy even for experienced surgeons, Livadas et al. [22] reported that the presence of a prominent median lobe may lead to failure even during basic endoscopic maneuvers. Similarly, Mamoulakis et al. [15] demonstrated that BPH can cause anatomical distortion along the distal ureter, mechanically hindering advancement of rigid instruments and potentially altering the angle of entry. These mechanical challenges reported with semirigid instruments raise the question of whether prostatic factors may also influence the placement of a similarly sized UAS.

Building on this rationale, a key aspect of our study is the direct assessment of the relationship between prostatic morphometric measurements and UAS placement success. In our analyses, none of these parameters were significantly associated with UAS placement outcomes. From a clinical perspective, this finding raises two main considerations. First, in patients with prostatic features such as a prominent median lobe, a high bladder neck, increased prostate volume, or enlarged prostate dimensions, UAS failure should not be anticipated solely on the basis of prostatic anatomy before RIRS. Second, when UAS placement cannot be achieved in a patient with marked prostatic measurements, the failure should not be automatically attributed to prostatic factors; other possible mechanisms, particularly distal ureteral and ureteral-orifice characteristics, should also be considered. This interpretation is supported by recent studies focusing on ureteral factors rather than bladder outlet anatomy. In a recent study, CT-based ureteral diameter measurements were significantly associated with difficult UAS placement, and patients with difficult access had smaller ureteral diameters [23]. Similarly, Mao et al. prospectively measured UAS insertion resistance and showed that bladder emptying significantly reduced distal ureteral insertion resistance and distal ureteral injury [24]. They also discussed that bladder filling may alter the ureteral lumen and the angle between the ureter, ureteral orifice, and posterior urethra through changes involving Waldeyer’s sheath. Taken together, these findings suggest that ureteral caliber, distal ureteral resistance, ureteral wall compliance, and ureteral-orifice dynamics may be more directly involved in UAS advancement than prostatic anatomy alone. However, because the cohort was not predominantly composed of older patients and strict exclusion criteria were applied, the possible contribution of severe BPH-related anatomical changes, particularly in patients with extreme prostatic anatomy, to UAS placement difficulty cannot be fully excluded.

One of the key factors supporting the reliability of our results is the rigorous methodology used for patient selection. In all included patients, diagnostic semirigid ureteroscopy was performed before attempting UAS placement to rule out organic strictures, thereby reducing the possibility that failure was primarily attributable to fixed ureteral obstruction. In the literature, Mogilevkin et al. [20] demonstrated that a preoperative stent or a history of ureteroscopy, and Koo et al. [25] and Higazy et al. [26] showed that preoperative alpha-blocker use can increase ureteral compliance and facilitate UAS passage. In line with these data, patients with these factors were excluded to better evaluate the potential effect of prostatic morphometric parameters and hydronephrosis. The UAS placement failure rate in our cohort was 38.4%, which was substantially higher than the rates reported in some previous studies. This may be partly explained by the selected nature of our cohort and by our safety-oriented protocol, in which UAS failure included not only the inability to pass the ureteral orifice but also the inability to safely advance the sheath because of marked resistance along any ureteral segment, without forceful advancement or active dilation. Therefore, the higher failure rate should be interpreted in the context of virgin ureters and a conservative UAS placement protocol.

Yavuzsan et al., in one of the notable studies on this topic, investigated the dynamics of UAS placement failure and demonstrated that preoperative hydronephrosis is an independent risk factor for failure [9]. They explained this significant association by suggesting that urinary stasis secondary to stone obstruction may create a favorable milieu for infection and inflammation. Consistent with their findings, preoperative hydronephrosis remained independently associated with UAS placement failure in our cohort. However, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct causal relationship. Rather, hydronephrosis may be a marker of underlying ureteral inflammation, edema, or altered ureteral compliance, which may increase resistance during UAS advancement. In addition, hydronephrosis-related alterations in renal pelvic and ureteral peristaltic dynamics may affect ureteral tone and wave patterns, which could theoretically contribute to increased functional resistance in the distal segment. These hypotheses highlight the multifactorial relationship between hydronephrosis and ureteral physiology and suggest that future studies should explore these mechanisms in greater depth using real-time functional assessments.

Another important issue that should be considered is the clinical impact of UAS failure and how this impact may be reduced by alternative management strategies. In contemporary clinical practice, several different approaches may be considered after failed UAS placement. For example, RIRS without UAS has been reported to show no clear disadvantage in terms of stone-free rate, ureteral injury, operative time, or length of hospital stay; however, UAS use may reduce postoperative fever and infection rates [27]. Therefore, sheathless RIRS may be feasible in selected cases, provided that appropriate patient selection is maintained. In this context, direct in-scope suction (DISS) systems have attracted attention because of their potential to improve visibility and dust or small-fragment evacuation without a conventional UAS, and promising outcomes have been reported in selected patients [28]. In our study, a notable clinical finding was the longer length of hospital stay in the group in which UAS placement failed. This may be explained by procedural modifications after failed UAS placement, such as continuing sheathless or placing a double-J stent for delayed RIRS, which may delay discharge and lead to more cautious postoperative monitoring. This result, however, should not be interpreted as prolonged hospitalization being due to sheathless RIRS itself. Rather, it reflects the clinical consequences of unplanned UAS failure and the subsequent individualized management decisions in our series.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center and retrospective design may have introduced limitations related to local practice patterns and documentation processes. Second, the relatively limited sample size and the limited representation of extreme values, such as very large prostate volumes, marked intravesical protrusion, a very high bladder neck, or pronounced prostatic angulation, may have limited the power of the analysis to detect subtle but clinically meaningful morphometric effects on UAS placement outcomes; therefore, the possibility of a type II error cannot be completely excluded. This may be partly related to our attempt to create a more homogeneous cohort by reducing potential confounding factors that could directly affect UAS passage. However, this selected cohort structure, the restrictive exclusion criteria, and the relatively high UAS placement failure rate may limit the direct comparability of our findings with routine clinical practice. In addition, we did not perform a formal cost analysis to quantify the impact of UAS failure on healthcare expenditures through prolonged hospitalization and the potential need for a second session. Finally, although the radiological measurements were performed by an experienced urogenital radiologist, no interobserver variability or reproducibility analysis was conducted.

Conclusion

Within the morphometric range represented in this cohort, the evaluated prostatic morphometric parameters were not significantly and independently associated with UAS placement failure in male patients undergoing RIRS. In contrast, preoperative hydronephrosis remained independently associated with UAS placement failure. In addition, patients with unsuccessful UAS placement had a longer length of hospital stay. Nevertheless, the absence of statistically significant associations between the evaluated prostatic morphometric parameters and UAS placement failure should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no effect, particularly considering the study design and selected cohort characteristics. Further multicenter studies including older patients and patients with larger prostate volumes, more prominent median lobes, or more pronounced intravesical prostatic protrusion are needed to better clarify the potential role of prostatic anatomy in UAS placement outcomes.
