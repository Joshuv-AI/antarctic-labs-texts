Adults with congenital heart disease experience worse short- and mid-term graft survival following heart transplantation from DCD donors: The early US experience
Authors: Alexander R. Berg, Danielle M. Mullis, Aravind Krishnan, Elbert E. Heng, Nataly Montano Vargas, Daniel I. Alnasir, Alyssa C. Garrison, Y. Joseph Woo, John W. MacArthur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDonation-after-circulatory-death (DCD) heart procurement is enlarging the donor pool, yet its safety in adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD) is uncertain. We compared early (90-day) and mid-term (3-year) graft outcomes after DCD versus donation-after-brain-death (DBD) heart transplantation in ACHD recipients.MethodsUsing the United Network for Organ Sharing registry (1 January 2018 – 1 April 2025), we identified adults (≥18 y) with ACHD undergoing isolated heart transplantation. Retransplants and multiorgan procedures were excluded. The primary endpoint was graft failure (death or retransplant). Survival was analysed with Kaplan-Meier curves, multivariable Cox models, and 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity-score matching (caliper = 0.25 SD) adjusting for donor and recipient age, sex, body-mass index, renal and hepatic function, support devices, listing status, prior sternotomy, and regional ACHD center volume.ResultsAmong 726 ACHD transplants, 61 (8.4%) used DCD grafts and 665 (91.6%) used DBD grafts. Baseline clinical characteristics were similar, although DCD grafts had longer ischemic times (median 5.3 h vs 3.8 h, p < 0.001) and more frequent exvivo perfusion (65% vs 5.8%). Unadjusted 90-day and 3-year graft survival were lower after DCD (log-rank p = 0.009 and 0.040, respectively). On multivariable analysis, DCD procurement remained an independent risk factor for graft failure at 90 days (HR 2.56, 95% CI 1.23–5.17) and 3 years (HR 2.11, 95% CI 1.03–3.50).Propensity-matched analysis (n = 148) confirmed inferior 90-day survival for DCD recipients (log-rank p = 0.020). Post-operative morbidity and length of stay did not differ between groups.ConclusionsIn the early US experience, ACHD recipients of DCD hearts experienced significantly worse short- and mid-term graft survival than those receiving DBD hearts, despite comparable peri-operative morbidity. Until preservation strategies further mitigate warm-ischemic injury, careful candidate selection is warranted when allocating DCD grafts to complex ACHD patients.

Introduction

Orthotopic heart transplantation (OHT) remains the definitive treatment for end-stage heart failure.1 Yet, there is a disparity between availability and the demand for organs. There is not only a growing number of patients being bridged with mechanical circulatory support,2 but there is also a growing number of adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD) requiring heart transplantation.3, 4 This patient cohort is rapidly growing due to prior medical and surgical breakthroughs as well as improved lifelong management.5 ACHD patients present unique challenges when it comes time for transplantation, as they often have complex structural abnormalities, have had prior heart surgery thus making chest re-entry much riskier, have multiorgan dysfunction, and are often highly sensitized after receiving blood products.6, 7 Adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD) often spend significantly longer and have worse outcomes on the waitlist.8 Registry data demonstrate that ACHD recipients experience higher early post-transplant mortality but superior long-term conditional survival compared with non-ACHD patients.9

In recent years, donation after circulatory death (DCD) has emerged as a promising method to expand the donor pool. Early DCD heart transplants were performed in very limited numbers during the pioneering era of heart transplantation, with donors and recipients located in adjacent operating rooms.10 Over the ensuing decades, clinical adoption of DCD heart transplantation was minimal. However, substantial preclinical and early clinical research in the 2010s in Australia and the United Kingdom provided the foundation for renewed interest, highlighted by a Sydney group’s report of distant-site DCD heart procurement.11 This approach has since been adopted internationally.12, 13, 14 The first DCD heart transplant in the United States was performed at Duke University Hospital in late 2019,15 and the practice has expanded nationwide. Over the past few years, there have been many studies analyzing the preliminary outcomes of DCD heart transplantation.16, 17, 18, 19, 20 The current consensus seems to be that there are comparable short-term outcomes for DCD versus donation after brain death (DBD) heart transplants,21, 22 with DCD heart transplant recipients possibly experiencing higher rates of primary graft dysfunction.23, 24 Early national experiences in ACHD demonstrated poor outcomes, with worse 90-day survival.25 While outcomes of heart transplantation after DCD have been studied in the broader population,21, 22, 23 data specific to adults with congenital heart disease remains limited. Are DCD hearts a promising avenue to expand the donor pool for adults with CHD, or are DCD hearts a detriment to this vulnerable patient cohort? In this study, we aim to evaluate short- and mid-term outcomes of DCD versus DBD heart transplantation in adults with congenital heart disease, assessing differences in overall survival and graft survival in the early US experience.

Methods

Institutional Review Board (protocol #80039) approval was obtained for this project. A retrospective cohort analysis was performed using the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) database, which included data from the Organ Procurement and Transplant Network as of April 1st, 2025. Adult patients undergoing heart transplantation were identified in the UNOS registry between January 1, 2018, and April 1st, 2025Patients aged < 18, retransplants, and multiorgan transplants were excluded from the cohort.

Our primary outcome was graft failure, defined as the time from transplantation to the first occurrence of either recipient death or retransplantation, as captured in the UNOS database. All data are presented as frequencies (percentages) or median (interquartile range). Between-group differences were compared using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. Univariable linear regression was used to evaluate trends in patient age and waitlist duration over time. Survival was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox regression was used to compare the hazard of death between DCD and DBD heart transplants after adjusting for potential confounders. Covariates were selected a priori based on literature review and clinical plausibility to impact post-transplantation outcomes.

For the analysis of secondary outcomes, univariable and multivariable logistic regressions were performed for categorical outcomes, and linear regression was performed for continuous outcomes. Multivariable factors were chosen based on significance in univariable regression and clinical associations. Multivariate Cox regression was performed to identify risk factors for 90-day and three-year mortality between subgroups. We characterized regional ACHD transplant experience by identifying, within each UNOS region, the single center with the highest cumulative ACHD transplant volume—designating it as the “highest-volume ACHD center” (1 = highest-volume center, 0 = all others)—and included this binary covariate in all multivariable Cox and logistic regression models as done previously.3 To mitigate residual confounding, we conducted propensity score matching using a nearest-neighbor algorithm with a 0.25-SD caliper, pairing each case with up to two controls (1:2 ratio). Matching variables included donor and recipient age, sex, and BMI; ECMO support at transplant; prior cardiac surgery; urgency status; renal function (eGFR and serum creatinine); and total bilirubin. The matched cohorts were evaluated with Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and their baseline characteristics are detailed in Supplemental Table S2. For all analyses, a p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA/SE v18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Program participation rose from 3 centers in 2020 to 25 in 2024 and 26 by April 2025 (Figure 1A). Baseline characteristics

Median age (36 yr DBD vs 38 yr DCD, p = 0.19), male sex (61% vs 66%, p = 0.50), BMI (25.8 vs 25.8 kg m⁻², p = 0.43), race/ethnicity (p = 0.20), and renal, hepatic or hemodynamic indices were comparable. ECMO (3.0% vs 1.8%, p = 0.59), ventilator (1.1% vs 0%, p = 0.44) and IABP support at listing (4.8% vs 4.9%, p = 0.87) did not differ. DCD recipients spent less time in hospital before transplant (median 8 vs 19.5 days, p = 0.02) and were far more likely to receive ex-vivo-perfused grafts (65.0% vs 5.8%, p < 0.001). Donor age and BMI were similar (p = 0.36 and 0.09). DCD grafts were predominantly male (81.8% vs 60.0%, p < 0.001), experienced longer ischemic times (median 5.3 vs 3.8 h, p < 0.001) and traveled farther to recipients (313 vs 226 nautical miles, p = 0.009) (Table 2). More DCD candidates were more often Status 4 at listing (63.6% vs 49.3%) and at transplant (40.0% vs 23.1%), while more DBD recipients were transplanted at Status 2 (46.0% vs 36.4%); neither distribution was statistically significant (p = 0.20 and 0.29). Graft survival

Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated inferior graft survival for DCD recipients at both 90 days (log-rank p = 0.009; Figure 2A) and 3 years (log-rank p = 0.04; Figure 2B). These survival differences were supported by unadjusted Cox regression analyses, which showed significantly higher hazard of graft failure among DCD recipients at 90 days (HR 2.50; 95% CI 1.23–5.16; p = 0.012) and borderline significance at 3 years (HR 1.83; 95% CI 1.00–3.36; p = 0.05). At three years, risk remained higher for DCD recipients (HR 2.11, 95% CI 1.03–3.50, p = 0.042; Supplemental Table 1). eGFR kept its protective effect (HR 0.99, p = 0.003). ECMO (p = 0.55) and volume (p = 0.74) did not influence long-term outcomes.

Propensity-matched cohort

A 1:1 propensity score match produced excellent covariate balance: recipient age, sex, BMI, race/ethnicity, renal and hepatic indices, ECMO or IABP at listing, listing status, and donor age and sex all met the prespecified threshold of |standardized difference| < 0.10 (Supplemental Table 2). Residual imbalance persisted only for donor BMI (Std Diff = 0.34) and ischemic time (Std Diff = 0.99). Despite this adjustment, DCD recipients still exhibited significantly poorer 90-day graft survival (log-rank p = 0.02) (Supplemental Figure 1).

Cause of death

Among adjudicated deaths (Supplemental Table 3), graft failure accounted for a larger share in the DCD cohort (33.3% vs 9.6%), though the difference was not significant (p = 0.20). Infection, organ failure, cerebrovascular, and cardiopulmonary deaths were similar between groups.

Discussion

Although registry and single-center studies of unselected adults report comparable short-term outcomes for DCD and DBD grafts,21, 22 our analysis demonstrates significantly poorer 90-day and three-year graft survival in ACHD recipients of DCD hearts. Nuanced graft selection for ACHD candidates is thus essential as DCD practice evolves.

ACHD recipients differ fundamentally from non-ACHD adults. They often present with multiorgan dysfunction, elevated systemic and pulmonary venous pressures, heightened HLA sensitization, and multiple prior sternotomies.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 26, 27, 28, 29 In our cohort, reduced pre-transplant eGFR, a marker of systemic disease, was the strongest predictor of early graft failure. DCD grafts inevitably endure warm ischemia followed by reperfusion; a sequence linked to primary graft dysfunction.23, 24 ACHD patients have limited physiological reserve for this insult: pulmonary vascular disease raises right-ventricular afterload, venous congestion hampers cytokine clearance, repeat sternotomy prolongs implantation and cold ischemia, and sensitization heightens antibody-mediated injury. This convergence of a frail recipient with the warm-ischemic burden of DCD grafts constitutes a “two-hit” mechanism.30, 31

Refined preservation strategies may mitigate this risk. Early U.S. experience with normothermic regional perfusion31 and beating-heart procurement32 suggests these techniques blunt ischemia–reperfusion injury and may prove especially advantageous for complex ACHD recipients. Prospective evaluation of congenital populations is warranted.

High procedural volume is generally protective in heart transplantation,33 and registry analyses in ACHD confirm better early and one-year outcomes at high-volume centers.3, 34 In our DCD cohort that advantage disappeared: although nearly half of DCD grafts were placed at designated high-ACHD regional centers, compared with roughly one-third of DBD grafts, graft survival remained inferior. The warm-ischemic burden intrinsic to DCD procurement, superimposed on prior operations, abnormal anatomy, and chronic hemodynamic stress, appears to negate the usual benefit of institutional experience. Continued accrual of DCD cases and refinement of preservation and implantation protocols may narrow this gap, but current evidence counsels caution when allocating DCD grafts to highly complex ACHD candidates.

Our study has several limitations. First, the relatively small number of DCD heart transplants in adults with CHD introduces potential selection bias. Additionally, confounding may persist due to the unique surgical histories and hemodynamic complexities of CHD patients, despite our efforts to adjust through propensity score matching, multivariable analysis, and subgroup comparisons. While we were able to account for the presence of prior cardiac surgeries, the UNOS database lacks the granularity to identify specific congenital anatomies, such as single ventricle physiology, Fontan circulation, or associated liver disease. Variables related to primary graft dysfunction were also excluded due to their recent inclusion and incomplete capture. More broadly, the UNOS dataset does not provide detailed perioperative, anatomical, or hemodynamic data, which limits the depth of congenital-specific analyses.
