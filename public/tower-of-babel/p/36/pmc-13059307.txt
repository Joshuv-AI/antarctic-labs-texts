LKCAFormer: a lightweight transformer with large-kernel cooperative attention for the segmentation of field maize leaf diseases
Authors: Jian Hu, Xinhua Jiang, Julin Gao, Xiaofang Yu, XueLiang Fu, Chengjun Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn smart agriculture, accurate segmentation of maize-leaf diseases in real field imagery supports timely intervention, but remains challenging under cluttered backgrounds, uneven illumination, occlusion, and diverse lesion morphology. We present LKCAFormer, a lightweight encoder–decoder segmentation network that integrates two key components: (i) a three-stage Large-Kernel Cooperative Attention encoder (LK-COAT) that progressively enlarges the effective receptive field via large-kernel depthwise convolutions while preserving fine boundaries using cooperative channel–spatial gating; and (ii) a cross-scale decoder (CSDecoder) that fuses shallow edge/detail cues with deep semantics to refine lesion boundaries at low computational cost. We evaluate LKCAFormer on CD&S and a controlled single-leaf variant derived from it (Single-CD&S), using disease IoU as the primary endpoint. Robustness is further assessed on a 266-image complex-case subset curated from held-out test data, together with paired two-sided tests. On Single-CD&S, LKCAFormer achieves 76.23 ± 2.25 disease IoU and 86.70 ± 1.96 Dice, yielding a modest + 0.58 IoU gain over the strongest lightweight baseline (SwiftFormer). On the more challenging CD&S benchmark, LKCAFormer reaches 69.09 ± 1.65 disease IoU and 78.87 ± 2.13 Dice, outperforming the strongest baseline (SegFormer) by + 4.05 IoU; gains on the complex-case subset are statistically significant. LKCAFormer remains compact (3.68 M parameters; 1.13G FLOPs), corresponding to approximately 12.7% of U-Net’s parameters and 1.47% of its FLOPs, while retaining practical end-to-end throughput under a unified profiling protocol. Limitations include fixed dataset splits, the lack of cross-device latency/energy benchmarking, and the absence of multi-seed variability analysis. Future work will extend validation across crops and sensors and provide deployment-oriented, hardware-aware latency and energy evaluations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08409-w.Keywords: Maize leaf disease, Lightweight, Large-kernel, Cooperative attention, Semantic Segmentation

Introduction

Maize is a major staple crop and an important raw material for feed and industry. In recent years, driven by climate variability and environmental factors, the incidence of maize leaf diseases has shown an increasing trend. Leaf diseases not only impair photosynthesis and hinder plant growth but also threaten grain quality and yield, leading to substantial economic losses. Timely and accurate detection and diagnosis are therefore essential for effective disease management. Traditional diagnosis largely relies on manual symptom observation and expert experience. However, in large-scale farming scenarios, manual inspection is labour-intensive, time-consuming, and prone to subjectivity, making it difficult to meet practical requirements. Consequently, computer-vision-based automatic analysis has attracted increasing attention as a promising alternative. Nevertheless, disease segmentation in real-field images remains challenging: complex and cluttered backgrounds, uneven illumination, and diverse lesion morphology—often including small, scattered, and texture-rich spots that can be easily confounded with venation—still lead to unsatisfactory accuracy in existing approaches. Addressing these challenges is important for enabling more precise disease management and improving maize yield and quality.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), as a core architecture in deep learning, have been widely adopted in agricultural vision tasks. A number of CNN-based segmentation networks have been proposed, such as U-Net [1], PSPNet [2], SegNet [3], and various versions of DeepLab [4–6]. More recently, shybrid designs that combine the strengths of U-Net-style decoding and DeepLab-style multi-scale context modelling have demonstrated improved boundary recovery and efficiency. For example, Divyanth, Ahmad [7] collected 1,050 maize leaf images and compared SegNet, U-Net, and DeepLabV3+, selecting U-Net for leaf segmentation and DeepLabV3 + for lesion segmentation. Yang, Shan [8] further proposed an improved DeepLabV3 + that incorporates U-Net-like spatial detail recovery, achieving higher segmentation accuracy. Despite these advances, CNNs typically rely on stacking local operators to enlarge receptive fields, which can be suboptimal when modelling long-range dependencies and complex spatial variations in cluttered field scenes.

To better capture global context while retaining local detail, Transformer-based segmentation models have been developed by introducing self-attention for global information modelling. ViT [9] established an attention-only paradigm for vision, and subsequent models such as SegFormer [10] and PoolFormer [11] extended this idea to semantic segmentation, often outperforming CNN-based counterparts on several benchmarks. However, for maize leaf disease segmentation, accurately delineating fine-grained lesion boundaries remains difficult, especially for small lesions under uneven illumination or when lesion textures resemble veins. In addition, both deep CNNs and attention-based Transformers can be computationally demanding, which limits their practicality for deployment on resource-constrained devices and for real-time field applications.

Recent large-kernel CNNs and attention-augmented backbones can enlarge receptive fields and improve boundary quality, yet many are designed for generic segmentation settings and moderate compute budgets. In this paper, we refer to the in-the-wild imaging conditions in maize fields—such as cluttered backgrounds, partial occlusions, and uneven illumination, together with small, scattered lesions that are easily confounded with venation textures—as realistic field complexity. We use this term consistently in the remainder of the paper to avoid repetitive enumerations. Our goal is an applied and lightweight solution for in-field maize disease segmentation under realistic field complexity. To this end, we develop LKCAFormer, which combines a three-stage large-kernel encoder augmented with cooperative attention and a cross-scale decoder, aiming to expand the effective receptive field towards leaf-level context while preserving boundary details. Overall, our contribution lies in task-driven architectural integration and systematic empirical validation, rather than proposing a new learning paradigm.

Related work

Plant disease segmentation

In recent years, CNN and Transformer-based models have been widely applied to plant disease segmentation. Zhang and Zhang [12] and colleagues introduced residual blocks and residual paths to develop an enhanced U-Net for plant leaf disease image segmentation. On a single maize leaf disease augmentation dataset, a segmentation accuracy of 94.07% was achieved. Zhu, Ma [13] proposed a two-stage DeepLabV3 + algorithm with adaptive loss for segmenting apple leaf diseases, which effectively addressed the challenges of leaf and disease extraction in complex environments. This model achieved segmentation accuracy of 98.70% for leaves and 86.56% for diseases. Zhang, Li [14] focused on apple leaf spot disease and brown spot disease, proposing a disease segmentation model based on DFL-UNet+CBAM. This model used a hybrid loss function combining Dice Loss and Focal Loss to refine the weight relationships between features, enhancing the channel features of diseases while suppressing those of healthy leaf regions. The overall disease segmentation accuracy reached 95.16%. Chang, Wang [15] applied the Vision Transformer for plant disease recognition and introduced an EFG module to enhance local feature extraction. Experimental results demonstrated that this method outperformed ViT, PVT, and Swin. Thai, Le [16] proposed an improved Transformer-based model, FormerLeaf, for cassava leaf disease datasets, employing an attention pruning algorithm to select the most important attention heads at each layer, reducing complexity and improving segmentation accuracy. While these models have demonstrated superior performance in agriculture, they involve extensive matrix computations, leading to high computational overhead.

CNNs with large kernels

Traditional CNN architectures have limitations in kernel design, mainly manifested in a rapid increase in computational complexity as the network depth grows. To bridge the performance gap between Transformers and CNNs, ConvNeXt [17] incorporates Transformer design ideas into the ResNet architecture. By adjusting the training process of Swin Transformers, altering the computation ratios at different stages, reducing the number of activation and normalization layers, and using larger kernel sizes, ConvNeXt is able to improve segmentation performance. Recently, Ding et al. [18] reexamined the importance of large kernel designs in CNNs. Their proposed RepLKNet employs 31 × 31 large kernels, achieving a 0.3% accuracy improvement over Swin Transformers on the ImageNet classification task and outperforming ResNet-101 by 4.4% on the MS-COCO object detection task. However, because large kernels significantly increase the number of parameters and computational load, their application in segmentation tasks is limited. For instance, Peng, Zhang [19] pointed out that as the size of convolution kernels increases, the number of model parameters rises, which can lead to overfitting and ultimately harm segmentation performance pointed out that as the kernel size increases, the number of parameters also increases, leading to potential overfitting and indicating that large kernels may degrade segmentation performance. To address this issue, they proposed a Global Convolutional Network (GCN) that uses large 1×k and k×1 convolution kernels to enhance semantic segmentation results. Furthermore, experiments with the latest model, SLaK [20], have shown that the performance of RepLKNet tends to stabilize when kernel sizes exceed 31, 51, or 61. To tackle the optimization challenges associated with extra-large kernels during training, SLaK decomposes the large kernel into two rectangular convolution kernels (for example, 51 × 5 and 5 × 51) and employs dynamic sparsity techniques to reduce the number of trainable parameters, thereby achieving more efficient feature extraction.

Lightweight segmentation models

In recent years, an increasing number of researchers have begun focusing on lightweight segmentation models. For example, El-Assiouti, El-Saadawy [21] proposed Lite-UNet, which outperforms the standard U-Net, achieving slight increases of 0.06% in Dice coefficient and 0.12% in IoU. At the same time, Lite-UNet reduced model parameters, FLOPs (floating point operations per second), and inference time by 15.9 times, 25 times, and 6.6 times, respectively. Zhang, Li [22] customized a lightweight U-shaped sharpening perception Transformer architecture (UPFormer) specifically for segmenting grape leaf diseases, achieving a better balance between performance and efficiency. Additionally, Zhang and Lv [23] designed the TinySegformer model for agricultural pest detection tasks, significantly enhancing semantic segmentation capabilities while reducing parameters. Furthermore, Shi, Lin [24] developed a lightweight context-aware network (LCNet) that accelerates semantic segmentation processing while ensuring inference speed and segmentation accuracy, thus achieving a good balance between computational efficiency and prediction performance in mobile application scenarios. Although the aforementioned lightweight models can maintain high accuracy while reducing parameters and improving efficiency, their segmentation performance remains insufficient on maize leaf disease datasets with complex backgrounds and high disease similarity.

Attention mechanisms

The attention mechanism is a key technology to extract local key information. Accordingly, researchers have proposed several segmentation models that combine attention mechanisms to capture key information and suppress irrelevant features. For example, Sheng, Kang [25] designed new attention modules—GLM and BAM—that integrate and refine high-level semantic and spatial information. They developed an edge-guided segmentation method for complex environments, called EdgeSegNet, which achieved average MIoU scores of 0.909 and 0.942 on apple and peach datasets, respectively. Lu, Lu [26] proposed a precise segmentation method for fruit leaf disease images based on EAIS-Former, which uses a custom extra-large kernel attention module to capture more tiny spots, resulting in more refined segmentation of leaves and disease spots. Chen, Zhou [27] introduced an enhanced selective large-kernel attention module that adaptively recalibrates the weights of feature map regions along the channel and spatial dimensions, allowing the network to focus more on high-contribution areas and reduce interference from less informative regions. Meanwhile, Yan, Shao [28] developed a broadcast self-attention block to capture key fine-grained features globally while avoiding the heavy computational cost of complex matrix multiplications and multi-dimensional exponentiation. Although these attention-based segmentation models have achieved good results in their respective applications, they still fall short when dealing with maize leaf diseases. This is because the diseased regions and the complex background pixels are highly similar, which hinders accurate segmentation. Therefore, efficiently distinguishing between similar disease areas and healthy leaf regions in complex backgrounds remains a challenging problem that needs to be addressed.

Materials and methods

Dataset

We evaluate LKCAFormer primarily on the publicly available CD&S dataset [29] and a controlled single-leaf variant derived from it (Single-CD&S). CD&S contains 1,540 field images of maize leaves with three common diseases—Northern Leaf Blight (NLB), Northern Leaf Spot (NLS), and Gray Leaf Spot (GLS)—captured under natural conditions. Many images include multiple diseased leaves in the same frame, exhibiting realistic field complexity that better reflects practical deployment.

To construct Single-CD&S, we re-annotate a subset of CD&S images under the following criteria: (i) each image contains at least one clearly visible diseased leaf; (ii) one target leaf is selected for annotation according to a consistent priority (the most clearly visible leaf with the most complete disease patterns and minimal occlusion); (iii) the selected leaf and its diseased regions are fully visible and not severely occluded; and (iv) images with severe motion blur or strong over-/under-exposure that substantially degrade disease visibility are excluded. For each selected image, only the target leaf and its disease regions are annotated, while other leaves (if present) are treated as background to match the single-leaf setting. Annotations are created using Labelme [30] with three classes—leaf, disease, and background—with representative examples shown in Fig. 1. This process yields 1,274 re-annotated images, referred to as Single-CD&S. Data augmentation

For reliable evaluation, each dataset is first split into training and test sets with an 8:2 ratio. The training split is further divided into training and validation subsets with a 9:1 ratio. To mitigate overfitting and improve generalisation, we apply a range of data augmentation techniques to the training and validation sets, including geometric transformations (random horizontal/vertical flips, random cropping, and random scaling) and photometric adjustments (colour and brightness variations) implemented via the Augmentor library [31]. We additionally adopt advanced semantic-segmentation augmentations from MMsegmentation [32].

The test sets are kept strictly independent and are used only once for final evaluation. They are not used for model selection, early stopping, or hyper-parameter tuning, and no test-time augmentation is applied.

Complex-case subset

To assess robustness under realistic field difficulty, we curate a complex-case subset of 266 images drawn exclusively from the held-out test splits. Images are pre-specified to satisfy at least one challenging condition, including (i) multiple overlapping leaves, (ii) cluttered soil/weed backgrounds, (iii) strong illumination variation, or (iv) partial occlusion. Importantly, this subset is defined based only on scene attributes (independent of model outputs), fixed prior to analysis, and never used for training, validation, or model selection. All robustness verification and statistical significance analyses reported in the Results section are conducted exclusively on these independent test data, including the complex-case subset.

Model design

Overview

The overall architecture of LKCAFormer is shown in Fig. 2(a). It follows an encoder–decoder design with two key components: (i) an LK-COAT encoder for efficient multi-scale feature extraction, and (ii) a CSDecoder that progressively restores spatial details for accurate boundary delineation. The encoder targets both leaf-level context and lesion-level textures, while the decoder compensates for boundary and small-lesion information that can be weakened by downsampling.

CSDecoder

After obtaining the features , we employ three CSDecoder blocks to progressively merge high-level semantics with low-level spatial details (Fig. 2(c)). At the i-th decoder block, we take the encoder feature (same scale) and the upsampled decoder feature from the deeper stage as inputs. After the final stage, a segmentation head aggregates the last decoder feature with shallow details and upsamples the logits to the original image resolution to produce the prediction map.

Cross-scale attention

In natural field scenes, uneven illumination and cast shadows often weaken boundary contrast, while disease edges can be visually similar to healthy tissue and background clutter. Together with the typically low proportion of diseased pixels, these factors make it difficult to recover small lesions and accurate contours. Although the LK-COAT encoder provides multi-scale representations, fine boundary cues may still be attenuated by downsampling. To refine leaf and lesion boundaries during decoding, we introduce a lightweight Cross-Scale Attention(CSA) module (Fig. 2(c)). At decoder stage i, CSA uses the same-scale encoder feature as guidance to re-weight the upsampled decoder feature within a local neighbourhood, thereby emphasising disease-relevant edges/textures while suppressing background responses. Experimental settings

Training protocol

All experiments are implemented in PyTorch [34] and based on the public MMsegmentation codebase [32]. Unless otherwise stated, all models are trained on two NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. During training, images are randomly cropped to 512 × 512. We used the AdamW [35] optimiser with a cosine-annealing learning-rate schedule. Models are trained for 500 epochs with batch size 16, initial learning rate , minimum learning rate , and weight decay .

Reproducibility and resource reporting

To support reproducibility, all experiments are run with a fixed, pre-specified random seed(s = 0) under a fully specified MMSegmentation configuration. We additionally report peak GPU memory usage and wall-clock training time under this setting. For inference efficiency, we profile end-to-end performance at 512 × 512 with batch size 1 using a fixed warm-up protocol, reporting both latency (ms/image) and throughput (FPS) under identical runtime settings for all methods. Results measured on an RTX 4090 should be interpreted as a hardware upper bound under our profiling protocol rather than a direct estimate of edge-device performance.

Evaluation metrics

The quantitative metrics used to evaluate the model’s performance include Precision [22], IoU [10], Dice coefficient [36], and Recall [22]. Higher IoU and Dice values typically indicate greater overlap between the predicted and ground truth results, which corresponds to more accurate segmentation.

Here, TP represents the number of positive pixels correctly classified as true positives. TN denotes the number of negative pixels correctly classified as true negatives. FP refers to pixels classified as leaves but are actually part of the background. FN represents pixels classified as background but are actually part of the leaves.

For multi-class segmentation, we compute IoU/Dice/Precision/Recall per class and report macro-averaged results where specified.

On Single-CD&S, LKCAFormer achieves 76.23 ± 2.25 IoU and 86.70 ± 1.96 Dice, improving over SwiftFormer by + 0.58 IoU. Overall, the larger gains on CD&S suggest that LKCAFormer’s advantages are amplified under realistic field complexity.

In terms of efficiency, LKCAFormer remains compact (3.68 M parameters; 1.13G FLOPs) while achieving 14.97 FPS under the above end-to-end profiling protocol. We note that throughput does not necessarily scale with FLOPs: operator implementations (e.g., attention-style kernels), tensor reshaping, kernel-launch overhead, and memory access can dominate runtime. Because we report deployment-style end-to-end throughput (including resizing and argmax-based post-processing), the FPS values reflect practical inference latency rather than pure backbone compute.

Robustness and statistical significance verification

Given the high variability of field maize imagery, we complement the overall comparison with a dedicated robustness and statistical verification suite. We evaluate robustness on an independent complex-case test subset, and further stress-test models using controlled inference-time perturbations (label uncertainty, illumination shifts, and sensor/codec degradation) as well as reduced input resolution to reflect deployment constraints.

Failure-case analysis and actionable directions

Figure 10 highlights that the remaining errors are predominantly boundary-localised mismatches and small-lesion misses, while the dominant failure patterns vary by disease. For NLS, false negatives often occur on micro-lesions under low contrast, whereas occasional false positives align with specular vein highlights. For NLB, elongated, venation-aligned plaques are prone to over-extension or fragmentation, producing alternating FP/FN bands near lesion tips and along flanks. For GLS, denser lesions and richer background texture lead to scattered background false positives (striations mimicking lesions) and mild dilation/merging that overestimates lesion extent. Across all diseases, the Boundary±3px view frequently shows thin discrepancy bands along the leaf rim, suggesting near-miss localisation rather than global class confusion; occlusion and uneven illumination further amplify these errors by increasing false positives near contact edges and false negatives in shadowed regions. Overall, the residual limitations can be summarised as rim-adjacent ambiguity, micro-lesion sensitivity, and context confusion under dense lesions or occlusion.

Error maps by disease. Two examples are shown for each disease (NLS: top two rows; NLB: middle two; GLS: bottom two). Columns (left to right) show the input image, ground truth (GT), prediction (Pred), FP&FN overlay (red: false positives; blue: false negatives), and Boundary±3px, which visualises boundary discrepancies within a ± 3-pixel tolerance band

These signatures motivate three targeted extensions. (i) Boundary-aware supervision to improve rim and lesion-edge localisation (e.g., contour-band reweighting, distance-transform/contour losses, or an auxiliary boundary head). (ii) Small-lesion recall enhancement via hard-pixel reweighting (focal-style weighting or online hard-example mining), lesion-centred sampling/oversampling, and intermediate deep supervision. (iii) Vein/texture deconfounding to suppress venation-aligned and texture-induced false positives, e.g., an auxiliary vein/edge branch with negative supervision or lightweight texture/frequency regularisation. As a complementary safeguard, uncertainty estimation (e.g., MC dropout or ensembles) could flag ambiguous rim/occlusion regions for human-in-the-loop review and active learning. We will evaluate these extensions using boundary-focused metrics (e.g., boundary F-score) together with controlled stress tests under illumination shifts and occlusion.

Discussion

As an applied segmentation study, our primary contribution is a task-driven integration and empirical optimisation of established components to achieve a favourable accuracy–efficiency trade-off in field maize disease segmentation. Specifically, LKCAFormer combines large-kernel convolutions, cooperative attention, and cross-scale decoding to enlarge the effective receptive field toward leaf-level context (including venation patterns) while preserving fine boundary details. Experiments on Single-CD&S and CD&S improve disease IoU, with statistically significant gains on the held-out complex-case subset (p < 0.001).

From a practical standpoint, more reliable segmentation translates to more stable downstream indicators in field workflows. Predicted masks can be converted into management-relevant proxies such as the lesion coverage ratio, , where denotes the predicted diseased-pixel set and the predicted leaf-pixel set. Additional signals include spatial distribution cues (e.g., rim-adjacent vs. central lesions), and uncertainty-aware filtering can flag ambiguous cases for human review. Hence, robustness improvements under realistic field complexity and deployment constraints strengthen the stability of severity-related signals used by decision rules, beyond pixel-level metric gains.

Quantitatively, LKCAFormer achieves higher disease IoU than strong CNN and Transformer baselines while remaining lightweight. On Single-CD&S, the margin over the strongest baseline is modest, consistent with a near-saturation controlled setting. On CD&S, which exhibits pronounced realistic field complexity, the gains widen substantially. On the 266-image complex-case subset, LKCAFormer maintains significant advantages over representative baselines (DeepLabV3+, SegFormer), indicating improved robustness under failure-prone conditions. Notably, the strongest competing baseline varies by disease, likely reflecting differences in lesion morphology and scene interference (e.g., fine scattered patterns vs. larger contiguous regions). LKCAFormer remains consistently competitive across these regimes, suggesting that its context aggregation and boundary-preserving fusion mitigate disease-specific failure modes.

Mechanistically, the LK-COA module enlarges the effective receptive field via large kernels, while cooperative attention helps retain disease-relevant fine details; the CSDecoder further improves boundary refinement by fusing shallow edge cues with deep semantics. Ablations support this interpretation: performance increases up to a moderate LK-COA depth, after which gains saturate and efficiency degrades, motivating the default configuration.

Although LKCAFormer uses only a small fraction of U-Net’s parameters and FLOPs, throughput does not necessarily scale with FLOPs. Large-kernel and attention-style operations can be memory-traffic intensive, and end-to-end latency depends on implementation details and the runtime stack. We therefore report Params/FLOPs together with end-to-end FPS/latency under a fixed profiling protocol. Importantly, RTX 4090 measurements should be interpreted as a hardware upper bound rather than direct edge-device performance. To partially bridge this gap without a unified edge testbed for all baselines, we include a resolution robustness evaluation (512/384/256) on the complex-case subset to emulate constrained compute/bandwidth and quantify the accuracy–efficiency trade-off; device-specific benchmarking and optimisation remain complementary future directions.

Several limitations remain. First, our evaluation follows fixed dataset splits and a unified training protocol; further validation is needed to assess generalisation across different crops, geographic regions, imaging sensors, and acquisition resolutions. Second, the proposed kernel schedules were tuned for 512 × 512 inputs, and their optimal settings may differ at other resolutions, requiring additional exploration. Third, although we quantify robustness under realistic scene complexity, controlled perturbations, and downsampling, we have not yet performed systematic cross-device latency/energy benchmarking under a unified software stack, nor have we characterised performance variability across multiple random seeds.

Future work will extend validation under stronger domain shifts; pursue deployment-oriented optimisation (quantisation, structured pruning, and hardware-aware search) with device-level latency/energy benchmarking; explore multi-task pipelines that couple segmentation with severity estimation; and incorporate uncertainty estimation and data-efficient learning to reduce annotation cost and improve reliability in ambiguous field conditions.

Conclusion

We present LKCAFormer, a lightweight segmentation model that balances accuracy and efficiency for field maize-disease segmentation. On Single-CD&S, LKCAFormer achieves 76.23 ± 2.25 disease IoU and 86.70 ± 1.96 Dice, improving over the strongest lightweight baseline (SwiftFormer) by + 0.58 IoU. On the more challenging CD&S benchmark characterised by realistic field complexity, LKCAFormer reaches 69.09 ± 1.65 disease IoU and 78.87 ± 2.13 Dice, widening the margin to + 4.05 IoU over the strongest baseline (SegFormer). On the held-out 266-image complex-case subset, LKCAFormer further improves per-disease IoU over the per-disease best baseline, and paired two-sided tests on per-image IoU satisfy the pre-specified significance threshold (p < 0.001), indicating that the gains are unlikely to arise from sampling noise under the fixed protocol. In terms of efficiency, LKCAFormer remains compact (3.68 M parameters; 1.13G FLOPs), corresponding to ~ 12.7% of U-Net’s parameters and ~ 1.47% of its FLOPs, while retaining practical end-to-end throughput under a unified profiling setup. Collectively, these results suggest that combining a three-stage large-kernel cooperative-attention encoder with a cross-scale decoder improves boundary fidelity and the recovery of small/scattered lesions under realistic field complexity. Limitations include the absence of cross-device latency/energy benchmarking and validation beyond the current data regimes. Future work will extend evaluation under stronger domain shifts, conduct on-device latency/energy profiling with deployment-oriented optimisation (e.g., quantisation, structured pruning, and hardware-aware search), and explore uncertainty-aware, data-efficient training and multi-task pipelines to reduce annotation cost and further strengthen robustness.
