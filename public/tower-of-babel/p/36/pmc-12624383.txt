Survival Under Prolonged Cold: Duration of Stress, an Overlooked Factor in Freezing Tolerance Tests
Authors: Rajeev Arora
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFreezing tolerance (FT) is widely assessed in laboratories by measuring electrolyte‐leakage (EL) from tissues exposed to sub‐zero temperatures. This method employs cooling programs which typically result in extracellular freezing in herbaceous tissues causing cellular desiccation, a major determinant of injury manifested in EL, among other dysfunctions. Conventionally, samples are exposed to given sub‐zero temperatures (certain freeze‐desiccation) and thawed out after holding them for 0 to ~30 min at the target temperature before measuring EL. The higher the EL, the greater the injury. This protocol mostly ignores the effect of prolonged freezing at a given sub‐zero temperature on the severity of injuries as well as the potential for post‐thaw recovery. Research from the present author's laboratory and a few others shows that prolonged freezing causes greater injury than relatively shorter freezing times at the same temperature, that is, despite ‘fixed’ freeze‐desiccation, resulting in higher EL and water‐soaking, lower quantum efficiency of photosystem II and higher oxidative stress. The readers of this minireview are briefly introduced to the basic understanding of the mechanics of the EL assay, its application to assess reversible versus irreversible injury, the process of freezing (ice‐nucleation, cooling rates), equilibrium freezing, and freeze‐desiccation, all predominantly in the context of herbaceous and thermally homogenous tissues without barriers to ice progression. Within this framework, a data‐driven discussion is shared to highlight the significance of including freezing duration (FD) in FT tests. Ideas presented herein may refine methodologies for evaluating FT that validly predict plants' responses to natural freezes. This may also incentivize the exploration of cellular/molecular mechanisms for differential responses to varying FDs at a fixed temperature.

Introduction

Laboratory‐based freeze–thaw assays are routinely used to determine freezing tolerance (FT) of excised tissues in diverse plant species. Typically, in these tests, tissues are cooled to a series of sub‐freezing temperatures and then removed for thawing right away or held at each temperature typically for up to ~30–60 min before removal for thawing. Samples are then thawed out at 0 to 4°C for ~12 to 20 h. From one of the earliest reports by Dexter et al. (1932) until today, the structural and functional impairment of cell membranes is accepted as the early lesion of freeze‐injury, leading to the leakage of symplastic contents (see below, Section 2). Therefore, measuring electrolyte‐leakage (EL) from tissues has emerged as by far, the most common method for quantifying damage inflicted by freeze–thaw procedures (Palta et al. 1977a; Lim et al. 1998; Kovaleski and Grossman 2021). Other methods have also been used, though much less frequently, such as chlorophyll fluorescence, triphenyl‐tetrazolium chloride staining, vital staining, visual symptoms (water‐spoking, tissue browning), regrowth assays etc. As per the EL‐based method, higher electrolyte‐leakage compared with unfrozen controls indicates an injury, and the higher the leakage, the greater the injury of the plant. Usually, the extent of the injury, determined as a value between 0% and 100% at various sub‐freezing temperatures, is determined from the raw EL data. This is achieved, typically, by relativization of real EL from the samples by normalization against the minimal EL occurring from the unfrozen control, that is, 0% injury, as well as the maximum leakage from the coldest treatment temperatures, or the −80°C/boiling‐destroyed samples taken as 100% injury. The percent injury values obtained for unfrozen controls and progressively colder freezing treatments are subjected to curve fitting, typically resulting in a sigmoid response. The LT50, a temperature causing 50% injury, is then extracted from this curve and defined as FT of the plant tissue. Sub‐freezing temperatures warmer than LT50 are conventionally regarded as ‘sub‐lethal’ stress. Readers are referred to the following articles for a more comprehensive explanation of various methods used to derive the percent injury values from raw EL data: (Flint et al. 1967; Zhang and Willison 1990; Sutinen et al. 1992; Lim et al. 1998; Kovaleski and Grossman 2021).

The severity of stress has also been assessed by comparing EL from the injured tissues at ‘right‐after‐thawing’ with that after a certain ‘post‐thawing period’, whereby a lower leakage after the post‐thawing period, relative to right‐after‐thawing, indicates a recoverable/moderate injury. In contrast, a higher ion‐leakage from tissues after the post‐thawing period, relative to right‐after‐thawing samples, suggests a severe/irreversible injury (Palta et al. 1977b; Arora and Palta 1991; Chen et al. 2013; Vyse et al. 2020). The concept of LT50 has been routinely applied also to methods where FT is determined using regrowth assays, such as for winter cereals (Pomeroy et al. 1975) and turfgrasses (Gusta et al. 1980; Anderson and Wu 2010). For such studies, young plants of winter cereals, free of the soil of the roots, are exposed to a series of increasing freezing temperatures for a few minutes in a temperature‐controlled freezer. Plants are then thawed out for 12–24 h and transplanted into flats and placed in a controlled environment for regrowth. Survival is determined after a few weeks, and FT is expressed as the temperature at which 50% of plants are killed.

Equilibrium Freezing and Freeze Desiccation

The fact that ice typically forms in the extracellular spaces during a natural freeze was first recognized in the late 1800s to early 1900s (Arora 2018 and references therein). This occurs due to random ice nucleation because extracellular fluid contains heterogeneous ice nucleators and has a higher freezing point than the intracellular aqueous solution (Ashworth 1992). It is also well established that ice nucleation in plants (across the spectrum of species) during a natural freeze occurs at relatively mild sub‐freezing temperatures, that is,−0.5°C to −3.0°C (Ashworth and Davis 1986; Pearce and Fuller 2001; Arora 2018) and that cooling rates in the sub‐freezing range are relatively slow, that is, 1°C to 3°C/h (Levitt 1980; Steffen et al. 1989; Pearce and Fuller 2001). Since the water potential of ice declines at ~1.2 MPa °C−1 and ice has a lower vapor pressure at a given subfreezing temperature than that of supercooled water (Salt 1963; Hansen and Beck 1988), osmotically active water is drawn from the protoplasm and the cell walls and freezes as extracellular ice, resulting in freeze desiccation and a ‘caving‐in’ of cell walls (cytorrhysis), together with an increase in ice mass in the intercellular spaces (Pearce and Willison 1985; Pearce and Ashworth 1992; Stegner et al. 2020). For a thermally homogeneous system, for example, a leaf without spatial variation of temperature and with a fixed water content across the lamina, as the temperature further drops, the slow cooling continues to permit diffusion at a similar speed of all freezable cellular water to extracellular ice as fast as the temperature drops (Ball et al. 2004). This leads to a sufficient increase in the symplastic solute concentration of a steadily lowered freezing point, resulting in an equilibrium between the chemical potential of the protoplasm and the extracellular ice, that is, −1.86°C per osmolal (Osm) for aqueous solutions (Haynes 2016). Such freezing is termed ‘equilibrium freezing’, which is a typical occurrence during natural frosts for actively growing herbaceous plants and vegetative tissues, the experimental systems of primary focus in this review. On the other hand, xylem parenchyma and buds of several woody perennials and even herbaceous reproductive structures of certain alpine shrubs undergo non‐equilibrium freezing and survive the cold by deep supercooling and thus avoid freeze desiccation (George et al. 1982; Kuprian et al. 2014).

It is important to note that the time required for the attainment of ‘equilibrium’ between chemical potentials of the desiccated symplast and extracellular ice at a given temperature is also a function of the size of the plant sample, the concentration of the cell sap or the tissue hydration status, and membrane permeability. For a relatively smaller, actively growing unacclimated herbaceous plant or excised tissues, this time is relatively short (few min). For example, in our temperature‐controlled freezing of unacclimated spinach leaves, all exothermic heat at sub‐zero temperatures is dissipated in < 10 min, as indicated by the correspondence between temperature of the cooling‐bath and that recorded by a thermocouple attached to the leaf, that is, return of sample temperature to nucleation temperature following the onset of freezing. In support of this observation, unacclimated canola leaves became isothermal with ambient temperatures within 15–20 min, following an exothermic event due to extracellular ice formation (Gusta et al. 2004). In contrast, cold acclimated tissues, although with relatively lower water content than unacclimated, can take longer to reach equilibrium between vapor pressures of the symplast and the extracellular ice (Gusta et al. 2004). Slower efflux of water from acclimated cells could, in part, be due to the ‘bound state’ of water or greater dry weight in cold acclimated tissues. Attainment of ‘equilibrium’ can be ascertained by tracking the correspondence between the freezing temperature to which the sample tissue is being exposed and that of a reference tissue detected by an attached thermocouple during the freezing test; visually this can be confirmed by using infrared thermography to study in situ freezing in plants/tissues (Ball et al. 2004; Arora et al. 2023). Equilibrium freezing‐induced freeze‐desiccation of the symplast, when exceeding a certain threshold, causes deleterious structural and functional perturbations in membranes and the cellular biochemistry, one manifestation of which is EL. Cryomicroscopy has confirmed cytorrhysis to occur during equilibrium freezing of plant tissues (Pearce 1988). Assuming no major injury to cell membrane permeability during freeze–thaw or its full recovery, the melted water would re‐enter cells during thawing thereby undoing the cytorrhysis, restoring original cell volume, and removing the water‐soaked appearance of tissues which was apparent right‐after‐thaw. Therefore, the extent of water‐soaking has also been used as one of the visual symptoms and a quantitative measure of freeze–thaw injury (Palta et al. 1977b; Arora and Palta 1986, 1988, 1991). It bears mentioning that while not a typical occurrence for actively growing herbaceous/vegetative tissues under natural freezes, intracellular freezing can occur under conditions of unrealistic, rapid cooling whereby the rate of temperature decline exceeds the kinetic limitations of water efflux through membranes to extracellular ice (Arora 2018 and references therein). This does not allow osmotically active water in the cell to remain in chemical equilibrium with extracellular ice. With temperature sufficiently lowered, the supercooled metastable liquid freezes spontaneously resulting in intracellular freezing destroying the membrane systems and finally causing death (Mazur 1966; Levitt 1980).

Freezing Duration (FD) Vis‐à‐Vis Freeze‐Desiccation

From the discussion under Section 2 it follows that ‘equilibrium freezing’ to a given sub‐zero temperature should result in a ‘fixed’ amount of freeze‐desiccation of the symplast, regardless of the length of freezing. Or, as a corollary, the amount/size of extracellular ice crystals should be constant at a fixed freeze‐desiccation, irrespective of FD. Research with wheat leaves, cereal crowns, onion epidermal cells, potato leaves and Afroalpine ‘giant rosette’ leaves (Gusta et al. 1975; Chen et al. 1976; Palta et al. 1977; Marcellos and Burke 1979; Beck et al. 1984) shows that such scenario particularly holds true for herbaceous, vegetative tissues that do not contain a deeply supercooled aqueous compartment and when the cooling proceeds slowly together with ice‐nucleation near 0°C (Beck et al. 1984). It is important to note that ice‐nucleation and gradual cooling are typical of a natural autumn or spring freeze (Steffen et al. 1989). On the other hand, non‐equilibrium freezing has been demonstrated in woody tissues which undergo deep supercooling (Gusta et al. 2009) or for the supercooling leaves of certain hardy palms, such as Trachycarpus fortunei (Stegner et al. 2024). Furthermore, it is noteworthy that even under a scenario of ‘equilibrium freezing’, there could be spatial and temporal variation in freeze‐desiccation across the same leaf, potentially brought about by different hydration status of cells across lamina (tip, base, margins, proximal or distal to mid vein) and/or varying cell wall rigidity of palisade vs. spongy parenchyma, the latter, potentially, influencing the vapor pressure gradient between cellular water and extracellular ice (Ball et al. 2004; Stegner et al. 2022).

Freezing curves of herbaceous tissues, as determined by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy or psychrometry, have the formula: Lt/L0 = ∆Tm/T + b, where Lt and L0 are the amounts of liquid water at T and 0°C, respectively, ∆Tm is the melting point depression, and b is the liquid water that does not freeze, so‐called, bound water. Such equilibrium freezing response is characteristic of ideal solutions wherein relative content of liquid or frozen water is governed only by the osmotic potential; for herbaceous plant cells, it would be the osmotic activity of the symplast. Results from Nagao et al. (2008) are particularly noteworthy in the context of the amount of freeze‐desiccation vs. FD at a fixed temperature. These authors cryofixed Arabidopsis thaliana leaves, having undergone an equilibrium freezing to −2°C or −4°C, for a short‐term (immediately after reaching a target freezing temperature) or long‐term freezing (held up to 1–4 days at the same target temperature). Their results from an average of 70–80 individual measurements indicated that the size of intracellular ice crystals produced by cryofixation was the same in the leaf samples at right after freezing versus 3‐d exposure to freezing at −2°C, that is, 0.298 ± 0.098 μm and 0.306 ± 0.087 μm, respectively (Figure 2). This visual, quantitative evidence supports the notion that freeze‐desiccation at a given freezing temperature was similar in Arabidopsis leaf cells, regardless of the FD; intracellular ice crystals herein are from the water left inside the cell after equilibrium freezing at a given temperature.

Key Components of Freezing Tolerance (FT) Tests

While much progress has been made in the last few decades towards understanding the biology of plant FT, for example, mechanisms of injury, recovery, cold acclimation, deacclimation, and freezing tolerance versus avoidance, one question that continues to be debated among researchers is: how to refine laboratory methodologies to test FT that validly predict the plants' response to a natural freeze? Field survival is the ultimate measure of freeze‐/winter‐hardiness of a given plant. But such tests are impractical due to variable temperature and winter severity, microgeographic environmental variation, and the illusiveness of a ‘diagnostic’ winter and freeze conditions which, ideally, provide information about the coldest temperature with no damage and the warmest temperature with damage, and a spectrum of freeze‐injuries in these brackets. Therefore, controlled freeze tests in the laboratory provide useful tools for predicting field performance of plants since they allow replicated trials over time, provide precise temperature control, and the tests can be conducted in a relatively short time (see Section 1). But it is challenging to simulate natural freezing in the laboratory which requires controlling multiple environmental factors that are difficult, if not impossible, to manage, such as moisture, wind, rhizosphere temperature, cyclic freeze–thaw, flooding and ice encasement, radiational cooling etc. A few other factors that are relatively easier to control in the laboratory tests can also significantly impact the severity of freeze‐injury to plant tissues, such as the developmental stage of a tissue/plant, the severity of the freezing temperature, the rate of cooling, ice‐nucleation and its temperature, rate of thawing, and so forth (Steffen et al. 1989; Weiss and Palta 1991; Gusta et al. 2009; Gusta and Wisniewski 2013; Lim et al. 2014; Pardos et al. 2014; Arora 2018).

Reliable and robustly conducted freezing tests are critical in FT screening programs designed for selecting superior cold‐hardy genotypes. It is, therefore, imperative that at a minimum, the relevant test conditions that are manageable, such as ice‐nucleation close to 0°C, slow cooling (1°C/h–3°C/h), gradual thawing, be incorporated in designing the freezing tests to mimic the stresses encountered during a natural freeze. Barring occasional non‐compliances, these factors are generally given due attention by the plant cold hardiness research community.

Freezing Duration, a Significant Factor in Freezing Tolerance Tests

Staying alive under prolonged frozen states in colder climates is crucial for the survival of not only perennials, but also the young transplants of horticultural annuals that may be prone to exposure to extended and erratic cold spells, especially during the spring, the frequency of which is on the rise with changing climate. Kranner et al. (2010) also highlighted that the plant's response to stress varies from somewhat ‘elastic’/moderate injury after a short‐term stress to ‘plastic’/severe injury after long‐term, persisting stress. Therefore, FD deserves inclusion in the laboratory freezing protocols for assessing true FT. And yet, the effect of FD (minutes, hours, days) at a given temperature is largely ignored in laboratory tests, and, conventionally, plant tissues are exposed to treatment temperatures for a short and fixed duration of a few minutes (Min et al. 2014).

Pomeroy et al. (1975) was among the first to demonstrate that prolonged versus short duration of equilibrium freezing (to a fixed temperature) can result in significantly different injuries to winter wheat. For example, cold acclimated seedlings of Kharkov winter wheat had an LT50 of ~−20°C as determined by an FT test where plants were exposed for a few minutes to a series of incrementally colder temperatures before being thawed out and evaluated for survival in a regrowth assay. These plants exhibited 100% survival when exposed to −6°C for up to 5 d. In contrast, 1, 3, 6, 12, or 24 h exposure to −16°C (4°C warmer than LT50) resulted in the survival of 80%, 63%, 53%, 33%, and 17%, respectively; no survival was noted after 5 d exposure. In another study using a similar FT test ensuring equilibrium freezing, Gusta et al. (1997) noted the LT50 for cold‐acclimated Siouxland and Norstar winter wheat to be identical at −23°C; the water potential of the desiccated symplast at −23°C was ~−27 MPa. However, exposure to −15°C (substantially milder than −23°C) was sufficient to kill 50% of the seedlings of these cultivars when held at this temperature for 6.5 d (Siouxland) or 13 d (Norstar); the water potential of the desiccated symplast at −15°C is ~−17 MPa. Two conclusions can be drawn from these observations: (1) these winter cereals can tolerate extreme freeze‐desiccation (symplast water potential of −27 MPa) for a brief period, but not moderate desiccation (−15 MPa) for long periods, and (2) prolonged freezing tests revealed Norstar to be far more winter‐hardy than Siouxland despite their identical mid‐winter LT50 based on conventional FT tests. Waalen et al. (2011) tested the effect of short‐term versus long‐term freezing in a laboratory FT test where young seedlings of winter canola cultivars were subjected to an ‘equilibrium freezing’ protocol. For short‐term freezing, the plants were removed immediately for thawing after reaching freezing treatment temperatures or were exposed to −8°C for 4, 7, 9, 11, 14, 17 or 24 d for prolonged freezing treatments. The data indicated the LT50 of ‘Viking’ and ‘Tosca’ winter canola to be ~−19°C. However, 50% of ‘Viking’ seedlings were killed by exposure to −8°C for ~8 days. In contrast, it took ~21 d exposure at −8°C to inflict the same extent of damage to ‘Tosca’ seedlings. Skinner and Garland‐Campbell (2008) reported that winter wheat cultivars with an LT50 of ~11°C to −13°C (when plants were held briefly at target freezing temperatures) were actually 100% killed by only −5°C when exposed for several weeks. A few others have also substantiated the argument that true FT of certain winter annuals is best revealed by a prolonged freeze test rather than a short‐term freezing (Roberts 1985).

Depending upon the extent of freezing stress, a tissue could either be moderately injured from which it is able to sufficiently recover once the stress has passed or be severely/irreparably damaged (Palta et al. 1977b; Arora and Palta 1991; Chen et al. 2013; Chen and Arora 2014). Given the significance of post‐thaw recovery for the realistic measure of ultimate FT, Min et al. (2014) conducted a study to investigate the effect of FD on not only the severity of injury but also post‐thawing recovery potential, using precisely controlled equilibrium freezing trials on ‘Bloomsdale’ spinach leaves; leaf cells of this cultivar had a cell sap concentration of ~0.3 Osm (water potential of ~−0.67 MPa). This group used four physiological parameters (EL, chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv/fm), water‐soaking, and oxidative stress in form of abundance of reactive oxygen species) to more comprehensively define the extent of injury at varying FDs. LT50 for this cultivar based on the EL method and equilibrium freezing where leaf samples were exposed for 30 min at each freezing treatment was ~−5.5°C (Figure 3A inset). Accordingly, two freezing temperatures −4°C and −4.5°C (1°C to 1.5°C milder than the LT50), which would conventionally be regarded as sub‐lethal, were used for this study. Under equilibrium freezing, the water potential of the leaf cell sap at these temperatures would be ~−4.8 and −5.4 MPa, respectively, and the symplast concentration, due to freeze‐desiccation, would be ~2.2 Osm (efflux of ~86% freezable water) and ~2.4 Osm (efflux of ~88% freezable water), respectively. Leaf tissues were exposed at each of these temperatures for four FDs, that is, 0.5, 3.0, 5.5, and 10.5 h. Subsequently thawed out tissues were evaluated for injury. Results from all the methods of injury assessment showed incremental injury at longer FDs (Figure 3A–C; chlorophyll fluorescence data not shown here but was congruent; see Min et al. 2014). When stressed at −4°C for 0.5 h, leaves were essentially uninjured, just as the unfrozen controls. In contrast, when frozen for 10.5 h, tissues sustained ~50% injury (Figure 3A). This observation highlights that a temperature otherwise interpreted as ‘non‐injurious’ by a freezing test employing relatively shorter freezing duration could translate to LT50 at prolonged freezing despite the same freeze‐desiccation. Results further revealed that leaves frozen at −4°C and −4.5°C, respectively, for 10.5 h sustained roughly the same amount of injury as those frozen for only 30 min at −5.5°C (~50% injury) and −6°C (~70% injury; compare Figure 3A inset with the main figure). In another study with ‘Jersey Giant’ asparagus, Arora et al. (1992) noted a substantially higher spear‐kill (78%) in the unprotected field plots compared with 17% spear‐kill under plastic; notably, temperature close to spears in both treatments dipped below the LT50 (−2.8°C) as determined by equilibrium freezing‐based EL test in the laboratory. The temperature monitoring revealed that spears under plastic were exposed to −2.8°C for < 15 min whereas those uncovered experienced a temperature of 1°C to 1.5°C colder than the LT50 for up to 4–5 h. Results from spinach and asparagus studies, coupled with those from winter annuals discussed before, emphasize the importance of a combined effect of the ‘degree’ and the ‘duration’ of stress on the overall magnitude of injury/damage.

Impact on Freezing Duration on Post‐Stress Recovery Potential

The significant impact of FD in freezing tolerance tests is further accentuated by studying the post‐stress recovery potential of plants. Min et al. (2014) noted that equilibrium freezing of spinach leaves to −4.5°C (~2.4 Osm symplast) for 3 h versus 5.5 h resulted in either a recoverable or irreversible injury, respectively (Figure 4), as assessed by the ion‐leakage from tissues right after thaw versus 6‐d recovery period. Data from Figure 3A and Figure 4 taken together, further emphasize that exposure to only 0.5°C colder temperature can translate an otherwise sub‐lethal (recoverable) freeze injury into a lethal (irreversible) lesion, solely due to prolonged exposure to a given temperature. Evidently, leaves were identically injured to a recoverable point (~30% injury) after 3 h at either −4°C or −4.5°C (Figure 3A). However, while injury at −4°C after 5.5 h remained statistically unchanged, leaves were irreversibly damaged at −4.5°C after 5.5 h. Similar conclusions were drawn by Min et al. (2020) in another study with ‘Bloomsdale’ spinach leaves using a similar experimental approach and FDs. Based on the results, these authors introduced a parameter LD50, the freezing duration causing 50% injury, which was estimated to be ~3.1 h for ‘Bloomsdale’ spinach when frozen to −4.5°C; it was defined as the ‘threshold duration’ beyond which tissues were irreparably injured. Such definition of plant FT is almost non‐existent in the literature and deserves due consideration in laboratory protocols for the reasons discussed earlier in this review. ‘Stress duration’, as a factor, can also be a valuable inclusion in testing and for defining tolerance to other environmental stresses, such as chilling, drought, heat, salt and so forth (Kranner et al. 2010). As a cautionary note, LD50 has been routinely used for a long time in animal science and medicine literature as the ‘lethal dose’, that is, the amount of an administered chemical substance causing death in 50% of tested animals, a measure of toxicity of a substance or a drug.

Concluding Remarks

Research‐based knowledge that the severity of freezing damage to crops can substantially vary as a function of freezing duration should be of great practical value to growers. Additionally, in context with conducting research on low temperature stress biology, the discrepancies among laboratories for reported LT50 for the same species/tissue could be attributable, in part, to the differences in FDs used in freezing tests. It may be argued that the LT50 of plant tissues based on a short‐term freezing assay, routinely used and widely reported in the literature, could overestimate FT, compared with the LT50 or plant survival after an extended exposure to identical temperatures. It is therefore important to also include the ‘duration of stress’ as a variable while designing methodologies to evaluate plant FT.

Lastly, a prolonged freezing at a given temperature (freeze‐desiccation) being more injurious than shorter freezing at the same temperature opens an interesting area of research to explore cellular mechanisms for this differential response and brings up new questions. Could the increased duration of physical strain from ice crystals pressing on the cell and cell shrinkage due to desiccation amplify damage over time? Research shows that different cellular functions (membrane transport, photosynthesis, respiration etc.) can be differentially tolerant to the same freezing temperature (Steffen et al. 1989; Vyse et al. 2020). But do different cellular loci also differ in tolerance to varying durations of freezing at a fixed temperature? If so, how, and what is the nature of these lesions? In the context of the ice crystals' interaction with the cell wall, extended adhesion of ice to cell wall polymers (Takahashi et al. 2021) may exacerbate physical strain/injury at prolonged freezing, a hypothesis worth testing. Since EL is one of the earliest physiological indicators of freeze–thaw injury and prolonged freezing results in higher EL, the question also arises: are lesions to the plasma membrane after a relatively long exposure to concentrated phytotoxic symplast (caused by freeze‐desiccation) the same as those observed after prolonged freezing? This question was explored by Nagao et al. (2008) who compared ultrastructural perturbations in the plasma membrane of Arabidopsis thaliana leaf cells after an equilibrium freezing to a given temperature with those from unfrozen samples exposed for a similar duration to a salt solution corresponding to the symplast osmolality produced by freezing at that temperature. Figure 5 illustrates this comparison through freeze‐fracture replica photographs of leaf samples frozen to −2°C for 4d (Figure 5A) and the unfrozen leaves exposed to a 1.08 Osm balanced salt solution (Figure 5B). The results indicated similar ultrastructural changes to the plasma membrane in the form of the presence of intramembrane particles (IM) and intramembrane particle‐free areas (S) by the two treatments and suggest that extended exposure to concentrated phytotoxic ions resulting from freeze‐desiccation may be one of the causes of accentuated injury after prolonged freezing. How these structural aberrations lead to higher EL is unknown at present. In the context of the ‘duration of freezing’ topic, a rather old but elegant study by Siminovitch and Levitt (1941) on the physical state of protoplasm vis‐à‐vis freezing injury in plant cells could be pertinent. These authors noted that the longer the leaf sections of Catalpa and Cornus were left in a plasmolyte, the greater the deplasmolysis injury was. They ascribed the injury during deplasmolysis to the stiffening of the membranes due to prolonged plasmolysis; cells plasmolyzed for shorter durations by an equimolar and same plasmolyte were either uninjured or less injured. Although plasmolysis cannot be directly comparable to what plant cells undergo during freezing stress, both stresses lead to the caving in of the protoplast and cellular dehydration; moreover, cells regained their normal shape following deplasmolysis or thawing (if uninjured) resulting from rehydration. These observations coupled with those of Nagao et al. (2008) and our work with spinach (Min et al. 2014, 2020) bolster the need for in‐depth investigations of structural and functional aberrations in cell membranes following prolonged freezing. Future research exploring these questions will be a welcome addition to the field of freeze‐stress biology.
