Prevalence and factors associated with unplanned pregnancy among women of childbearing age in Ceará, Brazil: a cross-sectional study with population representativeness
Authors: Anamerinda de O Diaz, Sabrina G M O Rocha, Luciano L Correia, Valbert O Costa Filho, Igor P F Romeiro, Anamaria C e Silva, Jocileide S Campos, Dirlene M I Silveira, Sâmia G M O Giacomini, Márcia M T Machado, Hermano A L Rocha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveUnplanned pregnancy is a major public health issue associated with negative maternal and child health outcomes. Intimate partner violence (IPV) and socioeconomic inequalities are key predictors. This study aimed to analyze the prevalence and factors associated with unplanned pregnancy among women of childbearing age in Ceará, Brazil.MethodsWe conducted an analytical cross-sectional study using data from the 2023 Ceará Maternal and Child Health Survey (Pesmic). A total of 3,200 women aged 10–49 years were selected through multi-stage systematic sampling. The outcome was unplanned pregnancy. Independent variables were grouped into IPV-related factors, socioeconomic factors, and sexual/reproductive factors. We performed multivariate logistic regression; adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported.ResultsThe prevalence of unplanned pregnancy was 31.8% (95%CI 30.0–33.6). In multivariate analysis, IPV emerged as a strong predictor (aOR = 2.2; 95%CI 1.6–3.1). Other independent risk factors included low education, not being in a stable union, food insecurity, and urban residence. Among reproductive factors, multiple sexual partners, history of STIs, never using hormonal contraceptives, ever use of IUD, and ever use of emergency contraception were significantly associated.ConclusionWe found a high prevalence of unplanned pregnancy among women in Ceará. Intimate partner violence was a key risk factor, and multiple dimensions of socioeconomic vulnerability (low education, being single, food insecurity, urban marginalization) and certain reproductive behaviors (multiple partners, STI history, suboptimal contraception use, IUD or emergency contraception ever use) were independently associated. These findings highlight the need for integrated interventions that address violence prevention within reproductive health, reduce social inequalities, and improve education and access to consistent, effective contraceptive methods. Comprehensive policies strengthening women’s autonomy and ensuring safe reproductive choices – including access to safe, legal abortion when needed – are urgently required to reduce unplanned pregnancies and support women’s health in Ceará.Keywords: Unplanned Pregnancy, Intimate Partner Violence, Contraceptive Use, Risk Factors, Women's Health

Introduction

Unplanned pregnancies are a major public health problem worldwide, with significant implications for maternal and child health [1, 2]. These pregnancies are often associated with negative outcomes, such as inadequate prenatal care, higher rates of unsafe abortions and a greater risk of obstetric complications, as well as having an impact on women’s mental health (increased stress, anxiety and depression) [3]. It is crucial to distinguish that while unsafe abortion procedures pose severe risks to women’s physical and mental health, safe and legal abortion, when accessible, is a fundamental component of comprehensive reproductive healthcare and does not carry these adverse impacts [3]. Women who experience unplanned or unwanted pregnancies, especially those resulting from violence, have the right to access safe and legal services to terminate such pregnancies, consistent with their reproductive autonomy.

The frequency of unplanned pregnancies is higher in settings with low and medium socioeconomic development, where resources for reproductive planning and access to sexual education are limited [2]. For example, in Ethiopia and other regions, high unmet family planning needs correlate with elevated unplanned pregnancy rates [2]. In Brazil, national studies report a high prevalence of unintended pregnancies [1, 5]. This phenomenon is associated with socioeconomic inequality, low schooling and irregular access to reproductive planning services [6]. In addition, discontinued use of contraceptive methods and misinformation about contraception contribute to high unplanned pregnancy rates [6]. Among other factors identified in the literature are gender inequalities, low education, young maternal age, barriers to modern contraceptive use, and poor sexual/reproductive education [7]. Cultural and religious influences also affect contraceptive acceptance. These data reflect the importance of prevention actions addressing both individual and structural determinants of reproductive outcomes [8].

The association between intimate partner violence (IPV) and unintended pregnancies is increasingly documented [3, 4]. IPV is a significant risk factor for unintended pregnancy and reflects reproductive coercion within relationships. Women experiencing IPV are significantly more likely to have unintended pregnancies; for instance, many studies show that sexual violence by a partner contributes directly to pregnancy risk [3, 4]. Although national-level data exist, there is a gap in region-specific studies, especially in contexts like Ceará – a state in Northeast Brazil with high poverty and unique social conditions. Given Brazil’s size and deep regional inequalities, national averages may mask important local variations. Therefore, with a focus on IPV, this study aimed to analyze the prevalence and factors associated with unplanned pregnancy among women of childbearing age in the state of Ceará, Brazil, to contribute to understanding its dynamics in this specific context.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was an analytical secondary cross-sectional study of complex survey data with population representativeness for the state of Ceará in 2023. We used data from the seventh edition of the Ceará Maternal and Child Health Survey (Pesmic 2023), a series of statewide household surveys carried out periodically using consistent sampling methods [9, 10]. Ceará, in Northeast Brazil, has about 9 million inhabitants (51% women) and over 80% of its territory is semi-arid, with high poverty prevalence [11].

Sampling and population

The sample consisted of 3,200 women aged 10–49 years, randomly selected from 3,200 households drawn through a systematic multi-stage sampling. The sampling frame for the 2023 Pesmic survey was specifically designed to evaluate mother-child dyads. Consequently, for each of the 3,200 randomly selected households, only one eligible woman was invited to participate. This approach ensured a direct link between maternal reproductive history and household environmental factors, resulting in an analytical sample of 3,200 women from 3,200 unique households. Municipalities were selected proportional to population, then census tracts and households were randomly chosen following IBGE protocols [10].

Data collection

Trained professionals conducted face-to-face household interviews using a structured questionnaire, posing direct questions to the participating women in their homes. Anthropometric measurements of the women were also taken. The questionnaire covered demographics, socioeconomic status, reproductive history, contraceptive use, and behaviors including IPV.

Variables

The outcome, unplanned pregnancy, referred to ‘ever’ having experienced such an event. For independent variables, IPV and sexual behaviors (such as number of partners) referred to the ‘last 12 months’, while history of STIs and contraceptive use referred to ‘lifetime’ (ever use) or ‘current use’ as specified. Independent variables were grouped into blocks:

Intimate partner violence (IPV) factors: A composite binary variable (yes/no) indicating the presence of IPV. This was assessed using the modified HITS (Hurt, Insult, Threaten, Scream) scale. The instrument comprises four items asking how often a partner: (1) physically Hurts you; (2) Insults or talks down to you; (3) Threatens you with physical harm; and (4) Screams or curses at you. Responses are recorded on a frequency scale (1 = Never to 5 = Frequently), yielding a total score of 4–20. A score > 10 was used as the cutoff for IPV presence. Additionally, feeling unsafe in the home was included as an indicator [12].

Sociodemographic factors: Age (years), education (years of schooling), marital status (stable union/married vs. no stable union/not married), employment (employed for salary vs. not), family income (in minimum wages), participation in government assistance programs (yes/no), food insecurity (measured by the EBIA scale), area of residence (urban vs. rural), and access to public health (Family Health Strategy home visits, health insurance) [13].

For analysis, this was dichotomized into Food Security (0) vs. Food Insecurity (≥ 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical processing followed a dual-software workflow: Stata version 18.0 was employed for the core analysis to utilize the svy command suite, ensuring robust variance estimation for the multi-stage cluster design. SPSS v 23, IBM Inc., was utilized for initial data cleaning. Survey design and clustering were accounted for using svy commands. Descriptive statistics summarized sample characteristics. Bivariate logistic regression was used to identify variables associated with unplanned pregnancy (p < 0.20), comparing each category to its reference group. Variables significant at p < 0.20 in bivariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. We utilized multivariate logistic regression models to estimate Adjusted Odds Ratios (aOR). To account for the complex survey design and the correlation of observations within census tracts, we employed survey data analysis commands (svy in Stata) with robust variance estimation. This approach adjusts standard errors for clustering effects without specifying a hierarchical random-effects model, aligning with our objective to estimate population-averaged associations. The analysis was carried out with unweighted data because the selection probabilities were homogeneous. The sampling design employed a multi-stage selection strategy where municipalities were selected with probability proportional to size (PPS), balanced by the selection of a fixed number of households per cluster, rendering the sample self-weighting. However, the clustering effect of the design was strictly accounted for using svy commands to adjust standard errors. Beyond that, the sampling design was inherently self-weighting (EPSEM) because municipalities were selected with probability proportional to size (PPS) and a fixed number of households (20) was maintained per cluster. To ensure representativeness, we compared sample demographics with 2022 IBGE census data for Ceará, finding high consistency in urban/rural distribution and maternal schooling. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Ethical aspects

The study followed Brazilian ethical regulations. Participation was voluntary with signed informed consent, and data confidentiality was maintained. The project was approved by the appropriate Research Ethics Committee (protocol 5.614.102).

Discussion

In this study we found a high prevalence of unplanned pregnancy among women in Ceará, with almost one-third reporting at least one such event. Our findings highlight the complex interplay of multiple factors, particularly intimate partner violence (IPV), socioeconomic vulnerabilities, and certain sexual and reproductive behaviors. Specifically, women who experienced IPV were more than twice as likely to report an unplanned pregnancy, underscoring the profound impact of domestic violence on reproductive outcomes. Beyond IPV, we observed significant associations with structural factors indicative of marginalization and socioeconomic stress: low education, food insecurity, urban residency, and not being in a stable union. Additionally, behavioral factors – such as having multiple sexual partners, a history of STIs, never using hormonal contraceptives, and ever using IUDs or emergency contraception – emerged as predictors of unplanned pregnancy. These combined findings highlight how broader societal conditions and limited reproductive autonomy shape women’s vulnerability to unintended pregnancies.

Our study makes several important contributions. By using a population-representative sample from Ceará, a state in Northeast Brazil with high socioeconomic inequality, we provide regional data that complement national studies [1, 5]. National averages in Brazil may obscure local variations, especially in socioeconomically challenged areas. Notably, the strong association between IPV and unplanned pregnancy (aOR = 2.2) in our analysis is consistent with global evidence [3, 4] but highlights its prominence in this context. This finding emphasizes the need to integrate violence prevention into reproductive health services. Equally unique is our finding that urban residency was associated with higher unplanned pregnancy risk – a counterintuitive result suggesting that in Ceará, urban living may coincide with extreme marginalization. Informal settlements and concentrated poverty in urban peripheries can severely limit access to quality family planning resources and social support. This underscores the importance of addressing urban vulnerability in addition to rural health efforts. These insights—especially the IPV link and urban marginalization—are critical for tailoring public health policies to local needs beyond what national averages suggest.

The association between IPV and unplanned pregnancy may be partly mediated by reproductive coercion, a form of sexual violence where women’s reproductive choices are controlled. Our data used the modified HITS scale, capturing physical and verbal abuse, but did not include direct measures of reproductive coercion (such as birth control sabotage or forced pregnancy). However, the literature indicates that reproductive coercion often co-occurs with IPV and greatly increases unintended pregnancy risk. For example, women experiencing reproductive coercion are known to have much higher rates of unintended pregnancies [16]. Although we did not measure this explicitly, the strong IPV effect suggests that behaviors such as condom sabotage or contraceptive interference by partners may contribute to the outcomes we observed. Future research should directly assess reproductive coercion to elucidate these mechanisms more clearly.

Beyond violence, socioeconomic vulnerability played a central role. The association between low education and unplanned pregnancy reinforces findings that limited schooling restricts reproductive autonomy and access to family planning information. Similarly, the higher risk among women not in a stable union may reflect sporadic sexual activity where consistent contraception is less prioritized, or gaps in access to services often targeted at married couples. Interestingly, urban residency was associated with higher risk, likely reflecting the dense pockets of poverty and social exclusion found in the peripheries of Ceará’s urban centers, where barriers to health access persist despite geographical proximity.

Our findings regarding contraceptive behavior also warrant careful interpretation. The fact that ever use of emergency contraception (EC) was associated with unplanned pregnancy should not be viewed as misuse of EC [17, 18]. Instead, it likely reflects situations where women required post-coital contraception due to unprotected sex, contraceptive failure (e.g. condom breakage, missed pills), or sexual violence/reproductive coercion. In contexts of IPV, a partner may force unprotected intercourse or prevent contraceptive use, making EC necessary. Thus, “ever using EC” in our data may serve as a marker of past sexual vulnerability or inconsistent contraceptive access rather than being inherently problematic. This underscores the need for comprehensive education on consistent contraceptive use and accessible support for women experiencing violence, rather than labeling EC use as inappropriate [18].

The counter-intuitive association with IUD use (ever vs. never) also merits discussion. IUDs are highly effective, so one would not expect higher pregnancy odds among past users. In cross-sectional data, this likely reflects selection factors: health providers may preferentially offer or recommend IUDs to women who have already experienced unplanned pregnancies or are deemed at high risk, especially in public health settings serving vulnerable groups. It is plausible that many women in our sample chose an IUD after having an unplanned pregnancy to prevent future occurrences. There is also a possibility (though rare) of actual IUD failure leading to pregnancy. Furthermore, women who have had an unplanned pregnancy may recall their contraceptive history (including IUD use) more accurately. All these factors can produce the observed association in a cross-sectional analysis. Thus, we do not interpret IUD use as causing unplanned pregnancy, but as reflecting women’s subsequent contraceptive choices or provider practices.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. Second, although the sample is representative of Ceará, the findings may not generalize to other regions of Brazil or countries with different contexts. Third, data were self-reported, so sensitive topics (IPV, sexual behavior, contraception) might be underreported due to social desirability or recall bias. Fourth, we lacked certain variables that could influence outcomes (e.g., detailed mental health status, relationship quality). Finally, the Pesmic survey did not include detailed questions on condom use (ever use, consistency, etc.) in a form usable for this analysis. Since condoms are a key contraceptive and STI-prevention method, their omission is a limitation. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable regional insights for public health planning in reproductive health.

In conclusion, unplanned pregnancies among women in Ceará are common and driven by a multifactorial constellation of factors. Intimate partner violence and broader socioeconomic vulnerabilities were major determinants. To reduce unplanned pregnancy rates, interventions must be multifaceted: integrating violence prevention into reproductive health programs, improving education and access to effective contraception, and addressing the socioeconomic conditions that constrain women’s reproductive choices. Ensuring access to safe, legal abortion services is also critical to protect women’s health and autonomy. Policies that strengthen women’s autonomy, reduce social inequalities, and provide comprehensive sexual and reproductive health services will be crucial to improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. Firstly, its cross-sectional design means we can identify associations but not establish causality. Secondly, despite rigorous sampling yielding a representative state-wide sample, the results may not generalize beyond Ceará to other regions of Brazil or countries with different cultural and healthcare contexts. Thirdly, our data are self-reported on sensitive issues such as IPV, sexual behavior, and contraception, which may be subject to underreporting or recall bias. Fourthly, we did not have measures of women’s mental health or relationship quality, which may also influence pregnancy planning. Additionally, the survey did not include detailed questions on condom use (e.g., ever use, consistency) that could be analyzed; since condoms are an important contraceptive and STI-prevention method, this omission is a limitation. Finally, IPV was measured for the 12 months preceding the interview, whereas unplanned pregnancy was recorded as a lifetime event. This desynchronization prevents a strict causal interpretation, as the reported violence may have occurred after the pregnancy in some cases. Thus, the observed association reflects a clustering of social and relational vulnerabilities rather than a established temporal sequence. Despite these constraints, the study provides robust regional insights into unplanned pregnancy and can inform targeted public health strategies.

Conclusions

Our findings highlight the high prevalence and multifactorial nature of unplanned pregnancies among women of childbearing age in Ceará. The strongest predictor was intimate partner violence, and significant associations were also found with socioeconomic disadvantages (low education, poverty, single status, urban marginalization) and certain sexual/reproductive behaviors (multiple partners, STI history, inconsistent contraceptive use, ever IUD/EC use). These results emphasize the need for integrated public health interventions that address violence prevention, socioeconomic support, and comprehensive contraceptive education. Policies and programs that strengthen women’s autonomy, reduce inequalities, and ensure access to effective family planning (including safe abortion services) are essential to mitigate unplanned pregnancy rates and improve women’s reproductive health in the region.
