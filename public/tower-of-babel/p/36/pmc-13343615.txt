What we count, and who gets counted: Population Health Metrics on World Population Day
Authors: Shereen Hussein
Published: 2026

World Population Day was established by the United Nations in 1989 to mark the moment the world’s population reached five billion two years earlier and to invite reflection on what sheer numbers mean for human well-being [1]. Nearly four decades on, the defining feature of global demography is no longer growth but divergence. Some populations are ageing and beginning to shrink; others remain young and fast-growing, with ever-increasing mobility and migration. Life is lengthening in many places while it is violently truncated in others. Against this backdrop, the question that should occupy a journal devoted to population health metrics is deceptively simple: what do we count, and who gets counted? On this World Population Day, we argue that the second question has become the more urgent of the two and that answering it well is the central methodological challenge of our era.

A decade of converging shocks

The trends reshaping populations fall into two groups. The first comprises slow-moving structural shifts. Populations are ageing on an unprecedented scale: by 2050, more than two billion people will be aged 60 or older, with roughly three-quarters of that increase occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [2]. A widening gap between life expectancy and healthy life expectancy means more years lived with dependency, disability, and complex care needs, and a corresponding shift in what our metrics must capture, away from mortality alone and towards function, morbidity, and the demand for long-term care. Alongside ageing sit migration and forced displacement, and, in an increasing number of countries, below-replacement fertility and depopulation. Each rewrites the denominators and dependency ratios that population health assessment traditionally depends on.

The second group are acute, compounding shocks. Armed conflict has reached levels not seen in generations: the Uppsala Conflict Data Program recorded 59 active state-based conflicts in 2023, the highest number since systematic records began in 1946, while the Global Burden of Disease study attributes some 96,500 deaths to conflict and terrorism in 2021. However, evidence shows that such figures are contested and almost certainly undercounted [3]. Pandemics and health insecurity, brought into sharp attention by COVID-19, continue to expose the fragility of mortality surveillance [4]. Climate change is now an inescapable driver of population health, acting through heat, food and water insecurity, and displacement [5]. And cutting across all of these is a fourth shock that strikes at measurement itself: the abrupt contraction of global health financing. Development assistance for health fell by an estimated 21% between 2024 and 2025, driven largely by a 67% reduction, more than US$9 billion, in United States contributions [6]. These cuts fall hardest on LMIC research and on data infrastructure, such as surveys, civil registration and surveillance and demographic surveillance sites, which make populations visible in the first place.

This is the heart of the matter. Conflict, displacement, climate disruption and defunding do not merely harm health; they degrade our ability to see that harm. The populations made most vulnerable by these shocks are precisely those our instruments were already least able to count.

The inequality beneath the data

Population health metrics carry an implicit democratic promise: that everyone counts equally. In practice, data availability is profoundly unequal, and that inequality maps onto disadvantage. Older people are under-represented in many population-based surveys, which are frequently designed around working-age adults and households, and their care needs are poorly captured by mortality-centred indicators. People in LMICs remain comparatively data-poor, largely dependent on modelled estimates with wide uncertainty intervals, where high-income settings enjoy rich administrative records. Furthermore, some populations are excluded almost by construction, including those who do not live in conventional households and so fall outside standard sampling frames altogether.

Among the most consequential excluded groups are homeless and displaced people and those living in institutions: prisons, immigration detention, long-term care and nursing homes, psychiatric facilities and other congregate settings. These are not small or marginal populations. They are often older, sicker and more socially disadvantaged than the general public, and they face health risks, including infectious disease transmission, mental ill-health and premature mortality, risks that are amplified by the institutional environment itself. Yet they are routinely absent from household surveys, inconsistently captured in routine statistics, and rarely disaggregated in summary measures of population health [7]. The result is a systematic blind spot: the very settings that concentrate need are the ones our headline metrics overlook.

This is not a counsel of despair but a research agenda. The exclusion of the old, the institutionalised and the data-poor is a methodological problem, and methodological problems are this journal’s reason to exist. Accurately estimating the experience of populations that conventional surveys miss, through innovative sampling, record linkage, small-area estimation, capture–recapture, verbal autopsy, and the integration of administrative and routine data, is exactly the kind of work Population Health Metrics exists to advance. The journal’s recent output shows the approach in practice: Ismail and colleagues develop a diagnostic index to estimate long-term care need across the Middle East and North Africa precisely where individual-level data on ageing and disability are absent and older people are largely missing from national surveys [8], while Kowal and colleagues quantify unmet health-care need among older adults across 83 countries to track progress on universal health coverage in an ageing world [9]. Counting the uncounted is not a niche concern at the edge of the discipline. It is the discipline’s unfinished core.

Why this matters for us

The remit of Population Health Metrics is to ensure accurate summary measures of population health and inequality in population health, descriptive epidemiology at the population level, burden-of-disease and injury analysis, and comparative risk assessment. This is precisely the toolkit these converging trends demand. Each shock is a stress-test of measurement: mobile denominators in displacement, contested numerators in conflict, degraded surveillance in pandemics and under funding cuts, and absent sampling frames for institutionalised and ageing populations. In a crisis, methodological rigour is not a luxury. It is how scarce resources are aimed correctly, how interventions are held to account, and how the invisible are brought into view.

The journal has consistently met this demand. On conflict, Samet’s commentary “War and Population Health: there is no metric for the horror” confronted the moral and methodological limits of counting wartime suffering [10], while a 2025 analysis of conflict mortality in the Gaza War advanced formal methods for quantifying uncertainty in exactly the settings where data are most contested [11]. The journal’s COVID-19 and mortality collection assembled methods for estimating pandemic excess deaths. On ageing in LMICs, work such as the assessment of disease burden among older Brazilian adults using Global Burden of Disease estimates illustrates the kind of population-level measurement we seek to publish [12]. Running through all of it is a single commitment, restated in our recent reflection on the journal’s scope: to refine, not merely describe, how the health of populations is assessed.

A vision for the next five years

We want the next five years of Population Health Metrics to be defined by one ambition: to be where the field develops the methods to keep counting when counting gets hard. Our open collections already point in this direction, including Measuring Population Health in the Era of Climate Change (open through February 2027), a collection on Advancing the Measurement of Social Connection for Population Health, and a collection on the Methodological and Epidemiological Implications of Continuous Changes in Population Dynamics. To these we are adding new priorities.

First, having closed our earlier collection on the health impacts of war and armed conflict, we are launching a successor collection on conflict and population health, a deliberate response to a trajectory that, regrettably, has not reversed. We invite methodological advances in mortality estimation, displacement measurement, and the assessment of indirect health burdens.

Second, we invite a dedicated focus on ageing and long-term care: the measurement of dependency, functioning, healthy and disability-free life expectancy, care needs, and the care workforce. Given that the coming surge in older populations is weighted towards LMICs, this is where measurement innovation will matter most, and where a journal that looks beyond mortality can make a distinctive contribution.

Third, and cutting across every collection, we commit to equity in measurement itself, paying attention to who gets counted. We actively encourage methods that work in data-poor and disrupted settings, reach institutionalised and other hidden populations, and close the data gap for older people and LMICs. We continue to support authors for whom resources are a barrier, so that the geography of authorship does not reproduce the geography of data poverty.

The number we mark on World Population Day is only as meaningful as our ability to see the people behind it. For the next five years, Population Health Metrics’ commitment is to keep all of them visible.
