Development and validation of the student readiness and anxiety scale for AI-assisted biology teaching (SRAS-AIBT)
Authors: Meryem Konu Kadirhanoğulları, Esra Özay Köse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As the use of artificial intelligence in education increases, determining student readiness and anxiety has become a necessity; however, the lack of a measurement tool specific to biology education in the literature formed the basis of this study. This study aims to address this gap by developing a scale that can measure student readiness and anxiety about the use of artificial intelligence in biology teaching. This research employed an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design. First, new items were developed and an item pool created by gathering student opinions based on relevant concepts and literature reviews. Then, the prepared items were submitted to expert opinions, and after expert evaluations, validity and reliability tests of the scale were conducted using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). This study involved 322 students in the EFA phase and 300 students in the CFA phase, aged 18–22, studying biology in a province in eastern Turkey. The developed scale consisted of two factors and 15 items. The scale was developed as a 5-point Likert-type instrument ranging from ‘Strongly Disagree’ (1) to ‘Strongly Agree’ (5). According to the EFA results, the cumulative variance ratio was determined to be 73.084%. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient was calculated as 0.95. The CFA results revealed that the two-factor model had a sufficient fit, and the values χ²/df = 3.7, RMSEA = 0.08, CFI = 0.94, and NNFI (TLI) = 0.93 support the structural validity of the model. As a reliable and valid instrument, the SRAS-AIBT serves as a practical tool for educators to design AI-assisted biology lessons tailored to student needs, thereby contributing directly to the improvement of instructional methods in this rapidly evolving field.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence is a broad field of computer science that aims to enable computers, software, or computer-controlled machines to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence. This field is not only technical but also multidisciplinary, drawing from various scientific disciplines such as biology, psychology, linguistics, education, mathematics, and engineering1–5.

The introduction of artificial intelligence into the field of education contributes to the adoption of innovative approaches in learning processes and enhances the quality of instruction. For a technological transformation in education to occur, students’ readiness for a new learning style is crucial. This is only possible through the development of students’ cognitive, affective, and psychomotor skills6,7. In the education system, educational goals can be better achieved and managed with artificial intelligence8. Indeed, studies in the literature indicate that artificial intelligence is effective in influencing mental processes and achievement, offers an effective approach in learning-teaching processes, and can successfully predict learning outcomes9. However, its rapid development continues to evoke uncertain and mixed feelings among users, often stemming from concerns about its societal impact and misunderstandings regarding its nature10,11,]12. Despite these concerns, AI’s innovative solutions for complex problems continue to drive its growth in science and education13–15[,16. The success of AI-assisted educational applications is directly related to understanding how students use this technology and the challenges they face. Such assessments can improve the use of artificial intelligence in the education system and increase the effectiveness of learning processes17.

In the field of biology, which holds an important place in the education system, the use of artificial intelligence is transforming learning and teaching processes into a more effective and advanced structure. Biology is a challenging subject to teach and learn, involving complex relationships between unfamiliar and abstract concepts18. As an important discipline supporting the development of both individuals and societies, biology plays a significant role in education and research processes19. The use of artificial intelligence in learning processes within this field can shape the challenges faced by both students and educators. Therefore, examining individual approaches to artificial intelligence is crucial for understanding the effects of this technology on students’ psychological and emotional states20.

Numerous studies exist in the literature on AI-assisted educational applications21,13,22–24. These studies generally examine aspects such as student and teacher opinions regarding the use of AI25–29, student achievement30–32; and measurement and evaluation33–35. However, various psychological scales related to artificial intelligence have also been developed36–41. For example, in their study, Karaca et al.,7 aimed to develop a valid and reliable psychometric measurement tool to evaluate the perceived readiness levels of medical students regarding artificial intelligence technologies and their applications in medicine. Similarly, Rezazadeh et al.,42 conducted a study aimed at translating and psychometrically evaluating the Persian version of the Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale developed for medical students. Liu and Liu,43, in their study, developed a new scale to measure artificial intelligence (AI) anxiety in university EFL teachers in China. As a result of exploratory factor analysis conducted with 251 EFL teachers and confirmatory factor analysis conducted with 415 teachers, the scale showed high reliability and validity. Alshaibani et al.,44 developed and validated the Artificial Intelligence Anxiety Scale (AIAS) to measure researchers’ concerns about the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in academic writing.

There are still limited studies in the literature regarding AI-assisted biology education applications. Most of these studies focus on artificial intelligence applications, and no studies related to scale development in this specific topic have been found. Bakirova et al.,45, investigated the development of students’ research skills in biology courses through the use of artificial intelligence technologies. Their findings showed that the use of AI technologies improved the quality of biology education and provided an innovative way to enhance students’ research competencies. Yolanda et al.,46 aimed to analyze the use of AI, particularly ChatGPT, in the learning process of Biology Education students. The results of the study show that most students use ChatGPT in their academic activities and that it provides significant benefits in terms of accelerating the understanding of materials, completing assignments, and reducing workload. Akhmedovna,47 studied the application of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies in biology education in schools. The study shows that AI integration facilitates the individualization of the educational process, improves the visualization of content, enhances critical and analytical thinking, and supports students’ research skills.

As Boateng et al.,48 state, as science progresses and new research questions arise, new scales become necessary. While existing studies have enabled significant steps towards understanding the psychological dimensions of AI-assisted educational processes, a study specifically focused on biology teaching has not been found. In this context, developing a scale that can measure student readiness and anxiety regarding the use of artificial intelligence in biology teaching would be an important step in filling the gap in the existing literature. It is believed that scale development work in this field will make a significant contribution to improving educational processes. In this context, this research aims to develop the “Student Readiness and Anxiety Scale for Artificial Intelligence-Assisted Biology Teaching " (SRAS-AIBT).

Materials and methods

This research employed an exploratory sequential mixed methods design. In this design, the qualitative phase occurs first, followed by the quantitative phase49. The qualitative phase may utilize methods such as interviews, grounded theory, case studies, or thematic content analysis. The results of the qualitative phase were used to develop or guide the subsequent quantitative phase. The quantitative phase typically involves a survey or other quantitative data collection tool. This design allows researchers to first gain an in-depth understanding with qualitative data, and then make broader generalizations with quantitative data50. Accordingly, the qualitative phase of the research involved creating a pool of items through interviews; the quantitative phase encompassed the process of testing these items through statistical analysis. In this research, a scale development study was planned based on the eight-step process proposed by DeVellis51 to determine the development process of the SRAS-AIBT. The procedures performed in each step are detailed below. The steps of the scale development process are shown in Fig. 1. The first five of these stages constitute the qualitative part of the study, while the last three constitute the quantitative part.

Step 1

In the scale development process, the concepts of “Artificial Intelligence,” “Readiness,” and “Anxiety” were first defined. Subsequently, past studies were examined to determine the characteristics, scope, and limitations of these concepts. In particular, students’ readiness levels for AI-assisted biology teaching and their anxiety levels associated with this new teaching method formed the key points of this measurement. Readiness, in its simplest sense, is the state of being cognitively, affectively, socially, and psychomotorically prepared to perform an activity52, while anxiety refers to a psychological, physiological, and behavioral state arising from a real or potential threat to well-being or survival in animals and humans53.

Step 2: The most important stage of the scale development process is creating a comprehensive pool of items that can accurately represent the construct to be measured48. At this stage, the existing literature was thoroughly reviewed; measurement tools used in similar research on artificial intelligence, readiness, and anxiety were examined (e.g., 44,54,55,7,56,43,37,42,40,57). Based on this literature review, the initial items were prepared. Streiner et al.,58 emphasized that after clearly defining the scale’s purpose, existing items should be reviewed or new items should be developed based on qualitative data obtained from the target audience. Accordingly, interview questions were prepared by considering a literature review and these questions were used in interviews with 20 students. Students were asked questions about their perspectives on AI-assisted biology teaching, their level of knowledge about AI technologies, and their willingness to use this technology. They were also asked about their anxiety levels regarding the use of AI, and their concerns, difficulties, and expectations when using this technology. New items were developed based on the students’ responses, and this data was combined with existing statements in the literature to create a comprehensive pool of items. Interviews continued until a saturation point was reached where no new or different dimensions emerged in the students’ opinions.

Step 3: In conclusion, a comprehensive pool of 45 items was prepared by combining the qualitative studies (focus groups and interviews) conducted in the first stage and the statements from measurement tools taken from similar studies in the literature. Subsequently, items with erroneous wording and similar expressions were eliminated, reducing the number of items to 34.

Step 4: At this point, the prepared 34-item draft scale was presented to 8 faculty members who had previously conducted studies on the subject in order to obtain their expert opinions. The Lawshe technique was used to evaluate the expert opinions. The Lawshe technique involves a group of experts, consisting of at least 5 and at most 40 people, evaluating the suitability of the items in the scale to the scope of the research and expressing their opinions through a specific form. In this technique, experts rate each item by dividing it into three categories: 1- should be removed, 2- should be revised, 3- should remain. The content validity ratio (CVR) for each item was calculated using the formula = (Ne − N/2)/(N/2)59. According to Ayre and Scally’s60 content validity ratio table, the critical CVR value is 0.75 in a case where the number of experts is 8. When Table 1 is examined, the CVRs (Content Validity Ratios) for the 34 items were calculated based on the evaluations provided by 8 experts. 13 items with a CVR value lower than 0.750 were removed. The average CVR of the remaining 21 items yielded a CVI (Content Validity Index) of 0.809. As a result, since CVI ≥ CVR, it can be stated that the content validity of the 21-item structure is statistically significant.

Step 5: At this stage, some “confirmation items” were added to the scale to check whether participants were answering carefully, consistently, or randomly marking answers. For example: “If you have read this statement carefully, please mark ‘Agree’.” Checking consistency by inserting contradictory statements, etc.

Qualitative stages have contributed to the item writing process in scale development in several ways: They have clarified the dimensions of the construct to be measured, strengthened the content validity of the item pool, and increased the suitability of the items in terms of meaning and expression for the target audience.

Sample

Two different sample groups were used in the study. To determine the factor structure of the SRAS-AIBT developed in the study, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was performed on the first study group. The first sample group consisted of 322 students (171 girls and 151 boys) aged 18–22, taking biology classes in a province in eastern Turkey. To test whether the resulting factor structure was validated as a model, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed on the second study group. The second study group consisted of 300 students (165 girls and 135 boys) aged 18–22, taking biology classes at the same level (Step 6). Convenience sampling, a type of purposeful sampling method, was preferred in the study. This method is frequently used when systematic or random sample selection is not possible61.

Sample size refers to the number of people who must be included in a study to effectively answer a research question62. In the literature, different studies use different criteria for sample size. Comrey and Lee63 stated that for factor analysis, 50 people is a very weak sample size, 100 people is weak, 300 people is good, 500 people is very good, and 1000 people is excellent. Kline64 stated that a sample of 200 people is sufficient, while Field65 emphasized that a sample size at least five times the number of items should be used. Maccallum66 suggests that the sample size should be between 100 and 200. Therefore, it can be said that the sample size in this research was sufficient.

Data collection

During the data collection process, the survey was prepared using Google Forms and sent to participants via email, and also shared via WhatsApp through teachers at the school. At the beginning of the survey, participants were informed about the purpose of the research and ethical guidelines, and it was stated that participation was voluntary and that responses would be completely anonymous. Participants were assured of their anonymity and participated in the survey. After the data was collected, invalid or incomplete responses were filtered out and the data was prepared for analysis. With the approval of the ethics committee, the entire process was carried out with respect for the confidentiality and rights of the participants.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee at Kafkas University Social and Human Sciences Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committees and documented under approval number 2025(80). Before commencing the formal investigations and testing, the researchers obtained informed consent from all the participants involved in the study. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Data analysis

In the analysis of the research data, the SPSS software package was used for exploratory factor analysis, and the AMOS software package was used for confirmatory factor analysis. The reliability of the scale was determined using the Cronbach Alpha test. To evaluate the suitability of the obtained data for exploratory factor analysis (EFA), the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and the Bartlett sphericity test were applied (Step 7).

In performing exploratory factor analysis (EFA), a common technique such as principal component analysis (PCA) was generally preferred67. The primary purpose of using PCA is to identify items that best represent the total variance in the item pool, thereby determining the strongest indicators for Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) presented in the next stage of the study. While there are differing opinions on the use of PCA and Common Factor Analysis in psychometric research, PCA’s effectiveness, particularly in summarizing the dataset and its functionality as a preliminary preparation step before CFA, are supported in the literature68,69. The modeling of latent constructs was subsequently validated through Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), which is specifically designed to test the hypothesized latent structure. As a result of the first exploratory factor analysis, items with loadings on more than one component and common variance (communality) values below 0.30 (Q13, Q15, Q17, Q19, Q20, Q21) were removed from the scale, and EFA was repeated on the remaining 15 items. The oblimin rotation method was applied as the rotation technique. The main reason for choosing this rotation technique is the existence of correlations between the sub-dimensions of the scale70. The oblimin rotation method allows for relationships between factors and thus takes into account the shared variance between factors71. Furthermore, to determine how many significant factors the measurement variables represent, it is recommended to look at the eigenvalues and explained variance ratios72. Confirmatory factor analysis

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is a statistical strategy specifically designed to identify and explore hypothetical structures that are clearly evident in erroneous indicators74. To verify the scale sub-dimensions and the overall structure, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed using IBM AMOS software to test the fit of the data to the assumed model. The values obtained at the end of the analysis showed that the model did not exhibit a good fit. When the modification suggestions resulting from the analysis were examined, modifications were made to items Q8-Q9; Q7-Q16; Q16-Q18; Q1-Q4; Q5-Q6; Q11-Q12; Q10-Q14; Q1-Q5. These modifications considered not only statistical indices but also the theoretical content and semantic overlap of the items. In the literature, correlating the error terms of items within the same sub-dimension that measure similar conceptual structures is an accepted method for controlling shared error variance and improving model fit75,76. To determine the reliability level of the SRAS-AIBT, the Cronbach’s Alpha internal consistency coefficient (α) was calculated, and the overall α value of the scale was determined to be 0.95. The α values for the factors of the SRAS-AIBT were also calculated as 0.95 for the Readiness factor and 0.92 for the Anxiety factor, respectively. These findings indicate a high level of internal consistency and reliability for the scale. Although very high alpha coefficients may sometimes suggest potential item redundancy, the factor loadings and item–total correlation values indicated that each item contributed meaningfully to its related construct. Therefore, all items were retained in the final form of the scale.

Discussion

The integration of artificial intelligence into education has begun to significantly impact the field79. AI enables more efficient results in education through functions such as personalizing teaching processes, monitoring student achievement, evaluating assignments, and automating administrative tasks80. Furthermore, the widespread adoption of AI applications in education is expected in the future; the integration of innovations such as voice and facial recognition systems, virtual reality (VR) technologies, and three-dimensional hologram-based learning environments will create a more dynamic and interactive learning environment81.

However, alongside the potential benefits of AI technologies in education, there are also many concerns regarding the ethical dimensions and societal impacts of these technologies. The use of artificial intelligence systems in education raises serious concerns about ethical questions regarding the technology and how the AI ecosystem will transform the social structure82,83. The application of AI technologies in education can increase emotional responses to technology, especially anxiety levels. The success of integrating such technology into education depends on how much students embrace the technology and their confidence in its use84. From this perspective, a person’s readiness level forms the basis of learning in a given situation. Thus, if one is not prepared for a changing situation, learning in that area will not be possible85.

In this study, a comprehensive scale development process was carried out based on the eight-stage process proposed by DeVellis51. The scale development process requires a meticulous approach from researchers due to its complex and multi-stage structure86. The statistical analyses that form the basis of the process strongly support the structural validity of the proposed model. Indeed, the high KMO value obtained (0.939) and the significance of the Bartlett Sphericity Test confirmed that the data set has an excellent level of suitability for factor analysis69. Item purification and factor extraction were performed in the Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) stage. The analyses revealed that the scale consists of two main factors: Readiness and Anxiety. The factor structure was validated in the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) stage. CFA results confirmed the adequacy of the two-factor model, indicating a satisfactory fit between the proposed structure and the observed data. This strengthens the validity of the developed instrument and supports its use in assessing students’ psychological dispositions toward AI in biology learning contexts.

The emergence of these two distinct factors can be interpreted through the dual nature of students’ responses to technological innovations. While readiness reflects students’ cognitive preparedness, perceived competence, and willingness to engage with AI-supported biology learning environments, anxiety represents the affective barriers characterized by uncertainty, perceived difficulty, and hesitation toward the use of artificial intelligence in educational contexts. Readiness for artificial intelligence refers to the levels of competence, awareness, and motivation necessary to effectively interact with AI technologies in a professional context7; while anxiety is defined as a state of alertness that disrupts attention in response to perceived threats or unresolved fears87. Readiness for artificial intelligence should be interpreted not only as cognitive awareness but also as professional competence and digital literacy88.

The distinction between these two constructs is theoretically meaningful, as it suggests that students’ engagement with AI in biology education is not unidimensional but rather shaped by both facilitating and inhibiting psychological factors. The factor structure obtained in this study is consistent with the notion that technology adoption in education involves both positive readiness-related beliefs and negative emotional responses. AI anxiety refers to fears and concerns about the technology. Inability to adapt to technology can trigger emotional problems such as anxiety, tension, and stress in individuals89. These anxieties encompass concerns about AI’s potential for privacy violations, manipulation of human behavior, and loss of control. Furthermore, the impact of AI technologies on the workforce and perceptual anxieties fueled by science fiction representations increase distrust of this technology. The lack of transparency in AI and the potential sense of loss of control it creates over humans are also significant factors that deepen anxiety90. Findings from existing studies highlighting readiness and anxiety among students in the field of artificial intelligence in education have shown that students’ interaction with AI technologies is shaped by both cognitive readiness and emotional barriers91–93. These research highlights that AI-related anxiety in educational settings is often associated with concerns about job replacement, learning difficulty, and technological uncertainty, whereas readiness is linked to perceived usefulness, self-efficacy, and prior experience with digital tools. In line with these findings, the current study suggests that biology students’ readiness toward AI reflects both cognitive and motivational preparedness, while anxiety represents an affective response that may hinder engagement with AI-supported learning environments. Unlike existing scales in the literature, this study focuses directly on the context of ‘Artificial Intelligence in Biology Education’ and aims to measure students’ unique responses to artificial intelligence. This is considered to provide a more in-depth and qualitative analysis compared to existing tools, as it offers a field-specific readiness measurement rather than a general level of technological anxiety.

SRAS-AIBT can serve as a ‘diagnostic tool’ for educators in identifying students exhibiting high AI anxiety or low readiness. Based on the scale results, targeted educational strategies or supportive workshops can be designed to reduce students’ anxiety levels and increase their readiness.

Our research aimed to improve the SRAS-AIBT, and its validity and reliability have been established. However, despite being rigorously conducted, this study has some limitations. First, the fact that the study was applied only to students in a specific region of Türkiye may make it difficult to generalize the findings to different geographical and cultural contexts. However, it is thought that the impact of this limitation is low and similar results can be obtained in other regions. In addition, the fact that the sample groups used in the research consisted only of students studying biology may limit the validity of the obtained findings only to students in this field. It is thought that using a similar scale in other academic fields or in students at different educational levels would be an important step in confirming the generalizability of the results. Due to the limited sample size, additional statistical criteria such as parallel analysis were not used to determine the number of factors. Furthermore, Principal Components Analysis (PCA) and scree plot were preferred for data reduction during the exploratory analysis phase. Although PCA is primarily considered a data reduction technique rather than a latent variable modeling approach, it was used in the present study to explore the preliminary structure of the scale and to identify the clustering tendency of the items during the initial stage of scale development. Furthermore, the factor structure obtained through PCA was subsequently tested and supported by Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Nevertheless, this methodological preference is acknowledged as a limitation, and future studies using common factor analysis methods and diversifying factor retention criteria with techniques such as parallel analysis could contribute to a more comprehensive evaluation of the psychometric properties of the scale.

Conclusions

To the best of the authors’ knowledge and based on the reviewed literature, no psychometric tool specifically measuring artificial intelligence readiness and anxiety in biology education was identified. In this respect, the SRAS-AIBT is expected to contribute to the literature as a measurement tool developed for this context. Although this new scale was developed for biology teaching, it can be used in different fields with the necessary adaptations. In particular, it is thought that SRAS-AIBT may also be applicable to students and educators in other scientific disciplines as AI-assisted teaching processes begin to be applied in wider fields.

The findings of this scale may be useful for educators and curriculum developers in measuring student readiness and anxiety levels and adapting teaching methods accordingly. When examining potential directions for future research, it is primarily necessary to test the scale in different cultural and linguistic contexts to ensure its global applicability. Furthermore, as AI tools become a permanent part of classrooms, longitudinal studies are crucial to monitor changes in student readiness and anxiety levels over time. Also adapting this framework to other scientific disciplines, such as chemistry or physics, is thought to contribute significantly to understanding discipline-specific differences in AI integration. Finally, it is thought that this study will provide a solid foundation for similar research in the future and help us to better understand the impact of AI-assisted educational tools.
