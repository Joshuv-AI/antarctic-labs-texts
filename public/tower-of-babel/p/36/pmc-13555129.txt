How I Investigate Borderline Prolonged aPTT: An Integrated Laboratory Approach to Mixing Studies and Factor Analysis
Authors: Nikolaos Androulakis, Eleni Nioti, Antonios Dilintas, Christina Kalpadakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBorderline prolongation of routine clotting assays—particularly the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)—is a common interpretative challenge in laboratory hematology. These reproducible but mildly prolonged results often lie just beyond the upper reference limit and can trigger unnecessary follow‐up, delays, or misinterpretation. Conventional tools such as 1:1 mixing studies may appear corrective due to dilutional masking of weak inhibitors, residual anticoagulants, or low‐grade lupus anticoagulant (LA) activity, especially in assays with limited sensitivity. In our laboratory, borderline prolongations prompt a structured, hypothesis‐driven workflow that escalates interpretative resolution in sequential steps. We begin with verification of analytical validity and exclusion of confounders (e.g., DOACs, pre‐analytical errors), then apply conventional mixing studies interpreted via quantitative correction indices (e.g., Rosner, percent correction). When results remain indeterminate or discordant, we escalate to enhanced mixing ratios (e.g., 4:1, 1:4), followed by factor assays and dilutional analysis including parallelism assessment. Here, we outline an integrated decision‐support framework for investigating borderline aPTT prolongation. The framework aligns traditional and enhanced mixing strategies with factor‐level interpretation into a coherent, stepwise pathway featuring explicit stop–go points. It is intended for use in advanced or referral coagulation laboratories and emphasizes interpretative rigor over binary thresholds. By optimizing existing tools within a layered interpretative structure, this approach seeks to reduce false reassurance and enhance clarity in borderline aPTT interpretation.

Introduction

Borderline prolongation of routine clotting assays—particularly the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) and, less commonly, the prothrombin time (PT)—represents a frequent and diagnostically ambiguous finding in clinical laboratory hematology [1, 2, 3]. These mild, reproducible deviations from the upper reference limit are often encountered in preoperative screening, emergency workups, and routine evaluations, where they may prompt follow‐up investigations, delay procedures, or trigger unnecessary specialist referrals [4, 5].

The challenge lies not in the severity of the abnormality but in its interpretative uncertainty. Conventional 1:1 mixing with normal pooled plasma often yields apparent correction, traditionally interpreted as suggestive of factor deficiency. However, this may reflect dilutional masking of weak inhibitors, low‐titer lupus anticoagulant (LA), or residual anticoagulant effects, especially in assays with low sensitivity to such disturbances [6, 7, 8]. As a result, borderline prolongations are particularly vulnerable to falsely reassuring or indeterminate interpretations when binary correction criteria are applied without context [9, 10].

Guidelines from the ISTH offer detailed algorithms for LA detection and factor inhibitor workups, but do not explicitly address borderline clotting times as a distinct diagnostic category [11, 12, 13]. In practice, this often leads to fragmented application of correction indices, inconsistent use of mixing ratios, and lack of structured escalation when results fall into gray zones [14].

In our laboratory, reproducible borderline prolongation is treated as a problem‐solving scenario requiring layered interpretation. This article presents an integrated decision‐support framework that aligns conventional mixing, correction indices, enhanced mixing ratios, and targeted factor‐level analysis—including dilutional behavior and assay parallelism‐ into a structured, stepwise interpretative pathway. It is intended to support laboratory interpretation in advanced coagulation settings, not to serve as a prescriptive clinical algorithm.

While the present framework is primarily focused on the investigation of borderline prolonged aPTT, it may also be extended to cases of combined PT and aPTT prolongation within a similar interpretative context. However, evaluation of such cases requires tailored factor‐level assessment reflecting involvement of the common pathway (factors II, V, X and fibrinogen).

Defining Borderline Prolongation: Analytical and Pre‐Analytical Considerations

Borderline prolongation of aPTT, and occasionally the PT, does not constitute a discrete diagnostic entity, but rather a continuum between analytical variation and low‐grade biological abnormality [1, 14]. These abnormalities are typically reproducible, persistently just beyond the upper limit of the local reference range, yet lack overt clinical correlation or bleeding phenotype [2]. They are diagnostically ambiguous: not fulfilling criteria for classical deficiencies or inhibitors, yet not clearly artefactual.

This ambiguity is compounded by mixing studies: borderline prolongations often appear to “correct” numerically on 1:1 mixing, which may falsely suggest a benign result [5, 6].

Analytical and Pre‐Analytical Contributors

There is no universal numerical threshold for defining borderline clotting times. Platform‐specific differences in activator type, phospholipid concentration, and clot detection methodology result in inter‐assay variability in sensitivity to deficiencies, inhibitors, and anticoagulants [6, 15, 16]. Consequently, a result considered “borderline” on one system may fall within the reference range on another.

Technical variables can disproportionately affect results in the borderline range. Minor pre‐analytical issues such as underfilling tubes, delayed centrifugation, prolonged storage, or citrate dilution due to high hematocrit can induce subtle, persistent prolongation [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Similarly, acute‐phase responses may transiently elevate factor VIII or fibrinogen, skewing phospholipid‐dependent assays like aPTT [22].

Residual anticoagulants ‐particularly direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)‐ are a major confounder in contemporary settings [7, 8]. Their presence, even at subtherapeutic levels, may subtly prolong clotting times and mimic borderline inhibitor patterns. Early exclusion using thrombin time (TT), anti‐Xa activity, or neutralization tools (e.g., DOAC‐Stop) is recommended [23].

Therefore, verification of pre‐analytical integrity and anticoagulant exclusion should precede interpretation. Borderline prolongation should not prompt escalation unless confirmed on repeat testing under optimal conditions.

Defining “Borderline” PT or aPTT

In practice, borderline prolongation is best defined operationally as: A reproducible elevation of PT or aPTT, slightly exceeding the laboratory‐specific reference interval, which remains unexplained after pre‐analytical review and does not clearly fulfill correction‐based interpretative criteria.

Recent ICSH guidance (2024) suggests that mixing studies should not be automatically triggered by minimal deviations, and that cutoffs ≥ 3 SD above the mean may reduce unnecessary testing due to biological or analytical noise [23]. Fixed numerical ratios are thus of limited value.

Furthermore, population‐specific and analytical factors may influence the interpretation of borderline clotting times. Differences in assay performance and sensitivity, as well as variability between reagents, may affect what constitutes clinically meaningful prolongation [15, 18, 23]. Therefore, borderline thresholds should be interpreted within the context of the local population and assay characteristics rather than as fixed universal cut‐offs.

Borderline status becomes particularly relevant when post‐mixing values remain near the upper limit of normal, or when correction indices yield discordant or borderline results.

Borderline Prolongation as a Diagnostic Gray Zone

Classical interpretative algorithms are designed for overt abnormalities and often fail in borderline settings. Weak inhibitors, residual anticoagulants, or low‐titer LA may be diluted during 1:1 mixing, producing false correction [7, 8]. Conversely, mild factor deficiencies near the lower limit of normal may yield partial correction, resisting binary classification [13].

These patterns define borderline prolongation as a distinct interpretative gray zone, where dilutional masking, assay variability, and conflicting correction indices undermine clarity [6, 23].

Recognition of this zone—neither fully normal nor overtly pathological‐ justifies the use of structured, layered investigation, integrating pre‐analytical verification, quantitative correction indices, enhanced mixing ratios, and factor‐level analysis as detailed in the following sections.

Conventional Mixing Studies: Principles and Limitations

Mixing studies are the cornerstone of initial laboratory evaluation for prolonged clotting times. Their primary function is to help differentiate between factor deficiencies, which correct upon addition of normal plasma, and inhibitors, which typically do not [5, 6]. While conceptually straightforward, their interpretative reliability decreases significantly in borderline cases, where minor numerical fluctuations, weak inhibitors, or assay limitations can mask true pathology.

The 1:1 Mixing Study: Concept and Execution

In the standard approach, equal volumes of patient plasma and normal pooled plasma (NPP) are mixed (1:1), and the clotting time is remeasured either immediately or after incubation at 37°C for 1–2 h [5, 6]. Immediate correction suggests factor deficiency, while delayed prolongation (after incubation) may indicate time‐dependent inhibitors, such as anti‐FVIII autoantibodies [12, 24].

However, this binary interpretation—“correction” vs. “non‐correction”—is poorly suited for borderline prolongations. In such settings, small analytical shifts or low‐level biological effects can shift results across fixed cut‐offs, leading to misclassification [5, 16]. For example, an apparent “correction” might reflect dilutional masking rather than true normalization.

Thus, reliance on visual or fixed‐ratio criteria alone is insufficient, particularly when baseline prolongation is mild or borderline.

Importantly, non‐neutralizing antibodies may produce apparent correction in mixing studies while remaining clinically relevant, as they may not directly inhibit factor activity in clot‐based assays but can alter factor clearance or function. This phenomenon may mimic factor deficiency patterns and should be considered in cases with discordant laboratory and clinical findings.

Factor lability during incubation may influence clotting times and potentially contribute to apparent prolongation. The use of control mixtures—where patient plasma and normal pooled plasma are incubated separately prior to mixing—may help distinguish true inhibitor effects from factor instability. However, this approach was not systematically applied in the present framework and is considered a potential refinement in specialized settings.

For enhanced mixing strategies (e.g., 4:1 and 1:4 ratios), interpretation remains contextual and is based on directional changes relative to the 1:1 mixture rather than fixed numerical cut‐offs, with lack of correction or re‐prolongation supporting the presence of inhibitor activity.

Enhanced Mixing Strategies Beyond 1:1

In cases of borderline clotting time prolongation—where conventional 1:1 mixing is inconclusive or discordant with clinical context—escalation to enhanced mixing strategies may improve interpretative clarity [6, 23]. These approaches manipulate the patient‐to‐normal plasma ratio to amplify subtle biological effects and refine diagnostic hypotheses. They should not be used for routine screening, but rather applied selectively in a hypothesis‐driven, stepwise framework.

Important Caveat

As noted in recent ICSH guidance (2024), the 4:1 strategy may yield false‐positive inhibitor impressions if the NPP has < 100% activity, particularly in borderline factor deficiencies [23]. Factor‐level assays should accompany this strategy when used for interpretative decisions.

The 1:4 Mixing Strategy: Supporting Mild Deficiency Hypotheses

The 1:4 ratio (20% patient, 80% NPP) increases factor replenishment and may confirm or exclude mild factor deficiencies when 1:1 results are borderline [2, 13]. Factor‐Level Investigation: From Screening to Mechanistic Insight

When conventional and enhanced mixing strategies fail to provide conclusive interpretation in borderline clotting times, targeted factor‐level analysis adds a mechanistic layer of insight [2, 13]. This level of investigation is particularly relevant when correction indices are discordant, or when persistent ambiguity remains after 1:1 and alternative mixing ratios.

Targeted Factor Assays in Borderline Settings

Test selection should follow the pattern of prolongation and preceding mixing behavior. Within this framework, isolated borderline aPTT prolongation represents the primary focus; however, in cases of combined PT and aPTT abnormalities, investigation is extended to include common pathway factors (II, V, X) [2, 13] alongside intrinsic pathway components. Accordingly, factor selection is guided by the pattern of prolongation, with intrinsic pathway factors (VIII, IX, XI, XII) prioritized in isolated aPTT abnormalities, while common pathway components are considered when combined PT and aPTT prolongation is present. In isolated borderline aPTT, factors VIII, IX, and XI are typically evaluated; factor XII and other contact factors may be assessed in aPTT prolongation without bleeding history, but their clinical significance is limited, and results should be interpreted with caution [27].

It should also be recognized that aPTT reagent sensitivity to factor deficiencies varies considerably, and mild reductions in factor activity (e.g., 50%–60%) may already result in clotting times exceeding the upper reference interval, depending on reagent characteristics. Therefore, borderline prolongation may reflect assay sensitivity rather than true clinically significant deficiency.

Results near the lower limit of normal—particularly in one‐stage clot‐based assays—are vulnerable to variability. Mild reductions should be contextualized within mixing behavior, reproducibility, and broader clinical/laboratory data [16, 28].

The Integrated Decision‐Support Framework

The diagnostic complexity of borderline clotting time prolongation does not stem from insufficient tools, but from their fragmented, uncoordinated use [6, 9, 23]. Conventional mixing studies, correction indices, alternative ratios, and factor assays each provide partial insight, but when used in isolation, they risk misclassification or overtesting.

The framework proposed here integrates these tools into a structured, layered algorithm designed for borderline or discordant cases. It is intended for expert interpretation in referral or advanced coagulation laboratories and aims to support—not replace—clinical decision‐making.

Laboratories should also ensure internal consistency of mixing ratios, incubation conditions, and index calculations prior to clinical application.

Importantly, this framework is not intended as a reflexive or screening‐based testing pathway. Rather, it is applied selectively in cases of persistent, unexplained, or clinically discordant clotting time abnormalities, where a structured interpretative approach is required.

Strengths and Considerations

The proposed decision‐support framework addresses a longstanding interpretative gap in laboratory hematology: the systematic approach to borderline clotting time abnormalities. By aligning mixing studies, correction indices, enhanced ratios, and factor‐level analysis into a unified algorithm, it offers a structured, mechanism‐based alternative to binary or fragmented interpretation.

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite its structured approach, the proposed decision‐support framework has limitations that merit explicit acknowledgment.

First, it is reagent and platform‐dependent. The behavior of PT and aPTT assays in response to factor deficiencies, inhibitors, or anticoagulants varies significantly by manufacturer, due to differences in activators, phospholipid content, and clot detection [11, 23, 28]. Therefore, correction indices, cut‐offs, and parallelism thresholds discussed herein require local validation and should not be applied generically.

Second, the framework is interpretative, not prognostic. It has not been validated against bleeding risk, perioperative outcomes, or clinical decision impact [10, 12, 16]. Its goal is to enhance laboratory clarity, not to supplant disease‐specific guidelines or clinical scoring systems.

Third, its application assumes access to specific assays and interpretative expertise. It is best suited to advanced coagulation settings with personnel trained in assay limitations and mixing dynamics. In high‐throughput environments, indiscriminate use may increase workload without diagnostic benefit [2, 13].

Finally, borderline abnormalities remain susceptible to biological and temporal variability (e.g., acute‐phase response, recent treatment, intercurrent illness). Even with rigorous methodology, residual uncertainty may persist, reinforcing the need for qualified, non‐binary reporting [1, 18].

In addition, the full implementation of this multi‐step framework may not be feasible in resource‐limited settings, where access to enhanced mixing strategies or advanced coagulation assays may be restricted. In such contexts, a simplified, stepwise adaptation based on available laboratory resources may be more appropriate.

Importantly, no outcome data are currently available to demonstrate that this framework improves patient care, reduces bleeding events, or alters clinical decision‐making. Therefore, it should be viewed as an interpretative decision‐support approach rather than a validated outcome‐improving diagnostic strategy.

Conclusions

Borderline prolongation of PT and aPTT represents a common, often underappreciated diagnostic challenge in laboratory hematology. Traditional tools—especially 1:1 mixing—are vulnerable to dilutional artifacts, correction misinterpretation, and residual anticoagulant masking when used in isolation [5, 6, 7].

This article proposes a coherent, laboratory‐focused framework that integrates mixing studies, correction indices, enhanced ratios, and factor‐level analysis into a stepwise, hypothesis‐driven workflow tailored to borderline and ambiguous cases.

Rather than offering new assays or rigid cut‐offs, the framework emphasizes contextual reasoning, escalation logic, and interpretative concordance, offering laboratories a structured response where conventional algorithms fall short [6, 23].

Adoption of this approach may improve clarity, consistency, and diagnostic confidence in navigating the gray zone of mild clotting abnormalities, not by eliminating uncertainty, but by structuring how we manage it.
