Protocol for gene knockdown in Trichoplax adhaerens using a silica nanoparticle-mediated RNA interference technique
Authors: Qiuyao Guo, Wanqing Li, Weiqiang Zheng, Meng Qiu, Chengtian Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for gene knockdown in Trichoplax adhaerens using a silica nanoparticle-mediated RNA interference (RNAi) technique. We describe steps for preparing silica nanoparticles, assembling RNAi complexes, and delivering them to live specimens. This protocol enables reproducible suppression of target gene expression in T. adhaerens and achieves transcript knockdown efficiencies of approximately 50%–70%, as assessed by quantitative PCR.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Li et al.1Subject areas: Molecular Biology, Gene Expression, Evolutionary biology

Before you begin

Innovation

RNA interference (RNAi) is a widely used approach for investigating gene function through sequence-specific suppression of gene expression. However, experimental tools for functional genetic analysis in Trichoplax adhaerens remain limited. Here, we describe a silica nanoparticle–mediated RNAi method that enables reproducible gene knockdown in Trichoplax.

The protocol provides a straightforward and easy-to-follow workflow that includes the preparation of silica nanoparticles, the assembly of RNAi complexes, and their delivery to Trichoplax. By enabling the delivery of RNAi molecules to live specimens, the method supports functional studies of target genes in T. adhaerens. Using this approach, we achieve typical transcript suppression efficiencies of approximately 50–70%, as assessed by quantitative PCR. By organizing these steps into a single workflow, the protocol provides a practical method for RNAi experiments in Trichoplax.

Synthesis of spiky silica nanoparticles

Timing: ∼1–2 h (sequence design and ordering only)

Design of shRNA

Timing: ∼1–3 h (sequence design and ordering only)

14.Design two to three target sequences (19–21 bp) for each gene following a protocol described in He et al,3 with minor modifications to the design procedure.

Design two to three target sequences (19–21 bp) for each gene following a protocol described in He et al,3 with minor modifications to the design procedure.

Expected outcomes

Virus-mimetic SSNs have been demonstrated to be a highly efficient and biocompatible carrier for siRNA transfection.5 When prepared following the synthesis method described in this protocol, SSNs are expected to exhibit uniform nanoscale morphology and good dispersibility, as observed by scanning electron microscope (SEM). SEM imaging typically shows well-dispersed particles with uniform morphology and nanoscale dimensions (Figure 7A). Consistently, dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis is expected to show a narrow size distribution, while zeta potential measurements generally indicate a stable surface charge including a clear negative-to-positive shift after amine functionalization (Figures 7B and 7C).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, p-values were determined using a two-tailed Student’s t test, with significance indicated as ∗∗∗ (p < 0.001) and ∗∗∗∗ (p < 0.0001). Data inclusion criteria were based on successful amplification or measurement, and any technical failures were excluded from analysis.

Limitations

In this study, we established a gene knockdown method in Trichoplax adhaerens, a representative species of the phylum Placozoa. It is noteworthy that the knockdown efficiency typically reaches approximately 50–70% for most target genes, and achieving a stronger reduction in gene expression remains challenging. This limitation is likely attributable to the small cell size of Trichoplax (approximately 3–5 μm) and the generally low expression levels of many genes. Consequently, knockdown efficiency is variable and not uniform across different cell types. Alternatively, repeated dosing regimens or the use of alternative nanoparticle formulations with improved delivery properties may further enhance knockdown efficiency. Finally, because this approach results in only transient suppression of gene expression, the development of strategies that enable long-term or stable gene silencing will be necessary in future studies. Nevertheless, in most cases, this partial knockdown is sufficient to elicit detectable phenotypic changes.

Incorrect water changing procedures during 24-hour seawater replacement may cause physical damage of animals and SSNs loss (related to Step 11).

Potential solution

Gently dislodge animals adhering to walls to the bottom using a pipette. Wait 5 min, then perform partial seawater replacement: aspirate 50 μL of the supernatant only. Gently pipette to avoid removal of SSNs.

Problem 5

Presence of redundant homologous genes (related to Step 6).

Potential solution

Perform simultaneous knockdown of multiple homologous genes by mixing multiple shRNAs or siRNAs with SSNs. Verify the knockdown efficiency of each individual gene before proceeding to downstream analysis.
