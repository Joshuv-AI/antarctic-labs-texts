Multilevel factors associated with self-reported sexually transmitted infections among sexually active women in Africa: a socio-ecological analysis
Authors: Ebenezer Kwesi Armah-Ansah, Eugene Budu, Cyprian Issahaku Dorgbetor, Rabbi Tweneboah, Lucy Faustina Ladzro, Emmy Kageha Igonya, Elom Hillary Otchi, Charity Oga-Omenka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSexually transmitted infections (STIs) continue to pose a significant public health challenge globally, negatively impacting reproductive health and increasing the likelihood of infertility, complications during pregnancy, and mother-to-child transmission. Despite the increasing burden of self-reported (SR) STIs, there is limited evidence on their prevalence and associated factors across Africa. Applying the socio-ecological model (SEM), this study sought to estimate the prevalence and identify the factors associated with SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa.MethodsThe study used the most recent Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) conducted in 29 African countries. A weighted sample of 340,179 sexually active women who had ever had sexual intercourse in the past 12 months and had complete information on all variables of interest were included. Guided by SEM, explanatory variables were conceptually mapped onto its four levels (individual, interpersonal, community, and societal/structural). The analysis was conducted using Stata version 14.2. Descriptive, chi-square, and multilevel regression analyses were carried out. Frequencies, percentages, and odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values were used to present the results.ResultsThe pooled prevalence of SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa was 18.43% (95% CI: 18.00%–18.70%), ranging from 47.9% in Liberia to 3.9% in Ethiopia. Guided by the SEM levels, significant associations were identified at the individual, interpersonal, and societal/structural levels. At the individual level, women aged 45–49 [aOR = 0.71; 95% CI = 0.66–0.76] and women whose sexual debut occurred at age 20 or older [aOR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.73–0.79] had lower odds of SR-STIs than women aged 15–19 years and those whose sexual debut occurred before age 20 years, respectively. At the interpersonal level, women living in female-headed households had lower odds of SR-STIs [aOR = 0.96, 95% CI = 0.93–0.99]. At the societal/structural level, women in Southern Africa had lower odds of SR-STIs [aOR = 0.35; 95% CI = 0.32–0.39] than those in West Africa.ConclusionThis research revealed a high prevalence (18.43%) of SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa, with substantial cross-country and sub-regional variation. The findings demonstrate the utility of the SEM in understanding SR-STIs, as significant factors were identified across individual, interpersonal, community, and societal/structural levels, highlighting that SR-STI risk is shaped by a complex interplay of multilevel determinants. These findings call for targeted, context-specific interventions that address factors at multiple levels, including enhancing STI education and screening as part of routine reproductive health and HIV services. Additionally, governments should implement region-specific strategies to improve reproductive health outcomes.Keywords: DHS, Prevalence, Public health, Sexually active women, Africa, STIs

Introduction

HIV, viral hepatitis, and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) remain major global public health challenges [1]. Despite substantial advances in policy, scientific research, diagnostics, prevention, and treatment, STIs continue to affect millions of people globally [2–5]. Among people aged 15–49 years, more than one million new STIs occur each day, with an estimated 374 million new cases of chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, and trichomoniasis occurring in 2020 alone [6]. This burden is disproportionately higher in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in Africa, where the estimated annual number of STI cases is approximately 96 million [1].

In response to this sustained global burden, the World Health Organization (WHO) has set targets to reduce the incidence of STIs by 2030, calling for the scale-up of national prevention and control strategies [7]. These measures, which are in line with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), focus on improving surveillance, prevention, early diagnosis, and targeted actions for at-risk groups, all within a framework that promotes human rights and gender equality [8].

Sexually transmitted infections not only cause immediate health issues but are also linked to adverse social, economic, and health consequences, including pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), infertility, and illnesses, as well as increased susceptibility to HIV infections [9–11]. Women face a higher risk of both acquiring STIs and experiencing their complications due to biological factors, a greater chance of having asymptomatic infections, and social and sexual dynamics that increase their exposure risk [3, 12–15]. These risks are worsened by ongoing diagnostic challenges in many African health systems, such as insufficient and costly diagnostics for asymptomatic infections and drug resistance, misdiagnosis, and low-test sensitivity [6, 16]. Practitioners in Africa and other LMICs rely on syndromic management using clinical algorithms (based on observed symptoms such as vaginal/urethral discharge) for rapid and simple detection and treatment. However, as highlighted in several studies [17–20], syndromic management can sometimes be ineffective in diagnosing STIs, particularly asymptomatic ones. It can often lead to either overtreatment or missed detection of asymptomatic STIs [1, 6]. Untreated STIs among women can lead to serious long-term outcomes, including ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain, congenital infections, and tubal infertility, which are also strongly associated with poor mental health outcomes, stigmatization, and intimate partner violence [21–26].

In this context, self-reported (SR) STIs represent a pragmatic approach for understanding the STI burden in the Africa region [3, 9]. Self-reporting STIs can support targeted prevention and control strategies and help identify populations at higher risk of STIs where syndromic management alone may be insufficient [26]. Therefore, research on the prevalence and factors associated with SR-STIs does not only add to literature in Africa but can help facilitate targeted interventions and efficient allocation of resources. Therefore, this study assesses the prevalence of SR-STIs and utilizes socio-ecological model to investigate the factors related to SR-STIs among sexually active women.

Theoretical framework

This research was guided by the socio-ecological model (SEM) to investigate the prevalence and associated factors of SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa. The SEM provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how multiple interacting factors across different levels influence health outcomes, particularly SR-STIs. The SEM recognizes that individual behaviors are shaped by complex interactions between personal, interpersonal, community, and societal factors [27, 28].

The SEM has been used in various health settings to analyze healthcare usage and health-related behaviors [29–31]. By applying the SEM, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of how multilevel factors collectively influence self-reported STIs among sexually active women across 29 African countries.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

This study used secondary data obtained from the most recent Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) conducted between 2011 and 2024 in 29 African countries. The datasets were accessed from the DHS Program database (https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm). The surveys spanned more than a decade, with the earliest dataset being from Congo (2011–2012) and the most recent datasets from Angola, Lesotho, Mali, Nigeria, and Zambia (2023–2024) (Table 1). The countries were grouped into four sub-regions: Southern Africa (Lesotho, Namibia, and Zimbabwe); East Africa (Burundi, Comoros, Ethiopia, Madagascar, Malawi, Mozambique, Rwanda, Uganda, and Zambia); West Africa (Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d'Ivoire, The Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Liberia, Mali, Nigeria, Sierra Leone, and Togo); and Central Africa (Angola, Cameroon, Chad, Congo, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Gabon).

Detailed description of the study sample

Countries were included if they met the following criteria: (1) the most recent DHS was conducted between 2011 and 2024; (2) data were available on self-reported STI symptoms (genital discharge, genital sore/ulcer, or reported STI diagnosis); (3) the survey contained complete information on the outcome and explanatory variables; and (4) respondents were sexually active women aged 15–49 years who reported sexual intercourse within the 12 months preceding the survey. Countries whose most recent DHS was conducted before 2011 were excluded to ensure comparability and contemporariness of pooled estimates across countries. Based on these criteria, 29 countries were included in the final analytic sample.

We used data from the women's files (ages 15–49 years). The DHS is a nationally representative cross-sectional survey program that collects data approximately every five years [32, 33]. The final analytic sample comprised 340,179 women (Table 1). We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines in drafting this manuscript [34].

Description of variables

Outcome variable

The outcome variable in this study examined whether sexually active women in Africa self-report sexually transmitted infections in the past 12 months prior to the various survey years. This variable was derived from three questions in the DHSs (had any STI in the last 12 months; had genital discharge in the last 12 months; and had a genital sore or ulcer in the last 12 months). The response options were “no, yes, and don’t know”. In this study, the responses were categorized into “No” (no, and don’t know) to be “0” and “Yes” (yes) to be “1”.

Independent variables

Seventeen independent variables were selected for this study based on empirical evidence from previous studies and the guiding principles of the socio-ecological model (SEM) [35–38]. The SEM posits that health outcomes are shaped by interactions between factors operating at multiple levels, including individual, interpersonal, community, and societal/structural contexts. Accordingly, the explanatory variables were categorized into these four levels.

Individual-level factors

Individual-level factors comprised personal demographic characteristics, knowledge, reproductive experiences, and health-related behaviors that may be associated with the risk of self-reported sexually transmitted infections (STIs). These variables included age (15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49 years), age at first sex (≤ 19 years, ≥ 20 years), educational attainment (no education, primary, secondary/higher), religion (Christian, Islam, Traditional, no/other religion), comprehensive HIV and AIDS knowledge (no, yes), condom use during last sexual intercourse (no, yes), employment status (not working, working), ever tested for HIV (no, yes), history of terminated pregnancy (no, yes), and number of sexual partners excluding a spouse in the preceding 12 months (zero, one, two or more).

Interpersonal-level factors

Interpersonal-level factors reflected relationship and household dynamics that may influence sexual behaviors, decision-making, autonomy, and access to resources. These factors included marital status (never married, married, cohabiting, widowed, divorced) and sex of household head (male, female).

Community-level factors

Community-level factors represented contextual characteristics of the immediate social and residential environment that may influence health behaviors and access to information. These variables included mass media exposure (no, yes) and place of residence (urban, rural).

Societal/structural-level factors

Societal/structural-level factors captured broader socioeconomic, health-system, and geographic contexts that may shape access to healthcare services, resources, and health opportunities. These variables included wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest), health insurance coverage (no, yes), and sub-region (West Africa, Central Africa, East Africa, Southern Africa).

Operational definitions

Mass media exposure

Mass media exposure was defined by activities such as listening to the radio, watching television, and reading newspapers and magazines. The response options for these three variables were the same: “not at all,” “less than once a week,” and “at least once a week.” Based on the literature [39, 40], we categorized the responses into two groups: “no,” indicating no exposure to mass media (not at all), and “yes,” indicating exposure to mass media (less than once a week and at least once a week).

Comprehensive HIV and AIDS knowledge

Comprehensive HIV and AIDS knowledge was measured by asking sexually active women in Africa about their agreement or disagreement with five statements: (a) always use condoms during sex can prevent HIV transmission; (b) a healthy looking person can have HIV; (c) a person can get HIV from mosquito bites; (d) a person can get HIV by sharing food with person who has AIDS; and (e) having one sex partner only, who has no other partners. A composite score was generated based on correct answers to all five statements, with comprehensive HIV and AIDS knowledge classified dichotomously as “Yes” (1) for those who answered all items correctly and “No” (0) for those who provided at least one incorrect response [41, 42].

Pregnancy termination

The DHS categorizes pregnancy termination to encompass pregnancy that resulted in miscarriage, induced abortions, or stillbirth. The response options were "Yes" or "No". For this research, the variable, "terminated pregnancy,” was defined as a binary indicator, marked as "Yes" for women who reported having a pregnancy terminated at any point and "No" for those who did not. This methodology aligns with earlier studies utilizing DHS data [43, 44]

Statistical analysis

Stata version 14.2 was used to analyse the data for this study. Three levels of analysis (univariate, bivariate and multivariate) were carried out. At the univariate level, a graphical representation was used to illustrate the prevalence of SR-STIs across Africa (Fig. 1). In the bivariate analysis, descriptive statistics related to the characteristics of the study and SR-STIs were generated. The prevalence of SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa was displayed as a bar chart. Subsequently, Table 2 displayed a bivariate analysis of the percentage of SR-STIs across the independent variables. Specifically, the explanatory variables’ weighted frequencies and percentages were reported with p-values from Pearson's chi-square (X2). A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was used as the criterion for statistical significance (Table 3). Statistically significant variables in the bivariate analysis were moved to the multilevel regression model. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to present the results of the multilevel regression.

Multilevel regression analysis was performed to identify the factors associated with SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa. Multicollinearity was checked among the independent variables using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and the results showed no evidence of high collinearity (mean VIF = 1.25, maximum = 1.76, minimum = 1.04). The highest VIF was observed for wealth quintile, and the lowest for pregnancy termination.

In the multilevel regression, six models (Models 0-V) were constructed (Table 4). The first model (Model 0) was the empty model, which had no explanatory variable. Models I, II, III and IV were based on the components of the socio-ecological model: individual-level factors, interpersonal-level factors, community-level factors, and societal-level factors, respectively. The final model (Model V) was the complete model that contained all the factors. All variables were entered into the final multilevel model simultaneously to allow for mutual adjustment, controlling for potential confounding across all included factors. Model comparison was done using the log-likelihood ratio (LLR) and Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) tests. The study sample was weighted (v005/1,000,000) and analyzed using Stata’s survey set command (svy) to account for the survey's complexity and the results' generalizability. We restricted our analysis to complete cases; therefore, all missing values were dropped. The extent of missing data was assessed prior to exclusion. Nevertheless, the potential impact of excluding missing cases on the precision of estimates is acknowledged as a limitation.

Results

Prevalence (%) of self-reported sexually transmitted infections among women in Africa

The pooled prevalence of SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa was 18.43% [95% CI: 18.00%–18.70%]. Although the pooled estimate shows high statistical precision due to the large sample size, substantial heterogeneity existed across countries, with prevalence ranging from 3.9% in Ethiopia to 47.9% in Liberia (Fig. 1).

Socio-demographic characteristics of sexually active women in Africa

Table 2 presents the socio-demographic characteristics of sexually active women in Africa, with the majority of participants aged 25–29 years (19.4%), had secondary and higher education (37.9%), were married (57.4%), were employed (71.6%), were not covered by health insurance (88.4%), had a history of terminated pregnancy (83.2%), had no sex partners excluding spouse in the last 12 months (82.4%), had ever been tested for HIV (58.2%), had a comprehensive HIV and AIDS knowledge (67.4%), had first sex at age ≤ 19 years (81.0%), and did not use a condom during their last sexual encounter (92.2%). Over half of the respondents (63.5%) had been exposed to mass media, while 64.3% identified as Christians, 73.8% had male heads of household, 22.1% belonged to the wealthiest quintile, 60.2% lived in rural areas, and 42.2% resided in West Africa, respectively.

The analysis indicated that factors such as age, education, marital status, employment status, health insurance coverage, terminated pregnancy, number of sexual partners excluding spouse in the 12 months, ever tested for HIV, condom use during last sex, age at first sex, mass media exposure, religion, sex of household head, wealth index, place of residence, and sub-region were statistically significantly associated with SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa (Table 2).

Prevalence of SR-STIs among sexually active women in African

In Table 3, the highest prevalence of SR-STIs was reported among those aged 20–24 years (20.6%), with secondary or higher education (20.5%), those never married (21.7%), those working (18.7%), and those covered by health insurance (19.5%). Regarding terminated pregnancy, women with a history of pregnancy termination (24.1%), had two or more sex partners excluding spouse in the last 12 months (37.8%), had ever been tested for HIV (19.1%), used a condom during last sex (21.8%), and had sex at age 19 or less (19.4%) had the highest proportion of SR-STIs. The highest proportion of SR-STIs was observed among those exposed to mass media (20.5%), Muslims (21.8%), male household heads (18.5%), individuals in the wealthiest quintile (20.2%), residents of urban areas (21.1%), and women in West Africa (23.7%).

Multilevel regression analysis on the factors associated with STIs among sexually active women in Africa

Table 4 presents the multilevel regression results. The final model (Model V), which contains all the independent variables. However, at the individual level, higher odds of SR-STIs were observed among women with primary education [aOR = 1.17; 95% CI = 1.13–1.21], Muslim women [aOR = 1.19; 95% CI = 1.14–1.25], women who used condoms during their last sex [aOR = 1.07; 95% CI = 1.02–1.12], working women [aOR = 1.07; 95% CI = 1.04–1.11], ever terminated a pregnancy [aOR = 1.46; 95% CI = 1.41–1.50], women who had ever tested for HIV [aOR = 1.25; 95% CI = 1.21–1.29] and women with two or more sexual partners excluding spouses were more likely to self-report STIs [aOR = 3.06; 95% CI = 2.81–3.34] compared to those who had no formal education, Christians, never used condoms during their last sex, not working, to those who had never terminated a pregnancy and never tested for HIV, and those who had no other sexual partner., respectively.

At the interpersonal-level, the likelihood of SR-STIs was higher among sexually active women who were cohabiting [aOR = 1.46; 95% CI = 1.38–1.56] compared to those who were never married while at the community level the likelihood of SR-STIs was higher among sexually active women who were exposed to mass media [aOR = 1.28; 95% CI = 1.23–1.32] compared to those who were exposed to mass media.

At the societal-level, higher odds of SR-STIs were observed among sexually active women who were in the richest wealth index [aOR = 1.12; 95% CI = 1.05–1.20] and covered by health insurance [aOR = 1.03; 95% CI = 0.96–1.05] compared to those who in the poorest wealth index and not covered by health insurance, respectively.

At the individual level, the likelihood of SR-STIs was lower among women aged 45–49 [aOR = 0.71; 95% CI = 0.66–0.76] and women whose first sexual encounter occurred at age 20 or older [aOR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.73–0.79] compared with those aged 15–19, and who had their first sex below age 20. At the interpersonal level, female household heads [aOR = 0.96, 95% CI = 0.93–0.99], and at the societal-level, women in Southern Africa had a lower likelihood of self-reporting STIs [aOR = 0.35; 95% CI = 0.32–0.39] compared to those in male household heads compared to those in West Africa, respectively.

Random effects (variance measures) results

As shown in Table 4, the AIC values decreased progressively from the null model to Model V, indicating that Model V had the best fit. This substantial decrease in the models supports the goodness of fit of model V, which had all the explanatory variables. Model V is the complete model that was selected for predicting the factors associated with SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa. In the empty model, substantial variations were observed in the likelihood of factors associated with SR-STIs among sexually active women across the clustering of the primary sampling units (PSUs) [σ2 = 0.08, 95% CI 0.07–0.10]. The ICC value for the null model shows that 24.07% of the variation in the SR-STIs among sexually active women was attributed to the between-cluster variations of the characteristics. The variation between clusters then decreased to 21.59% in Model I, which was the individual-level factors. The ICC then increased to 23.01% in Model II, 24.29% in Model III, 28.34% in Model IV which had only the interpersonal-level, community-level and societal-level factors, respectively. In the final model (Model V), the between-cluster variation decreased to 22.68%. This can be attributed to the variations in how the PSUs are clustered, which influence the differences in SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa.

Discussion

Sexual and reproductive health are significantly impacted by STIs [45, 46]. The WHO has supported countries to develop national strategic plans and guidelines that will not only create an enabling environment allowing individuals to discuss STIs, adopt safer sexual practices, and seek treatment, but also strengthen capacity to monitor STI trends, enhance and scale up health intervention and primary prevention, and facilitate the adoption of point-of-care tests [47]. This study investigated the prevalence of, and factors associated with STIs among sexually active women in Africa, based on the most recent DHS dataset.

This study estimated the prevalence of SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa to be 18.43%. This finding highlights a critical public health concern with important implications for women's sexual and reproductive health and broader societal impacts. Compared with other LMICs, Africa exhibits the highest prevalence of STIs, largely attributable to limited sexual health resources and inadequate healthcare infrastructure [47, 48]. The observed prevalence of SR-STIs highlights the need to strengthen programs and policies across Africa to ensure sustained, affordable access to STI prevention and treatment services [9, 45]. Compared with previous studies, the prevalence reported in our study is higher than the 11.6% found among reproductive-age women in Southeast Asia [49], 6.92% among adolescent girls in SSA [9], and 5.8% among either married or cohabiting women in SSA [3]. However, it is lower than the prevalence of 28.0% in Uganda [50], 19.4% in Eswatini [2], and 14.1% in Mali [51]. The variations in prevalence could be attributable to the differences in sexual behaviour, sociocultural norms, access to STI screening and treatment services, and the reliance on the self-reported data [9, 52].

The study found a regional variation in the prevalence of STIs across Africa, with Liberia, a country in West Africa, having the highest prevalence of 47.9%, while the least was reported in Ethiopia, a country in East Africa, with a prevalence of 3.9%. To reduce the high prevalence of STIs, coordinated programmes and policies have been implemented in Liberia through a partnership between the government, its agencies, and non-governmental organizations. Among these are the national dual HIV/syphilis rapid tests and strategic allocation of resources directly to county and district health teams, which is crucial for ensuring service quality and comprehensiveness [53].

However, at the sub-regional level, our results revealed that sexually active women from Southern Africa had lower odds of SR-STIs. This finding is noteworthy given that Southern Africa bears the highest global burden of HIV [54]. Several factors may explain this paradox. Possible explanations include the intensive HIV prevention investments (e.g., PEPFAR, Global Fund) that integrated STI services, higher health-seeking behavior and treatment coverage, reducing infectious periods; regional differences in sexual networks and condom use; and stronger health system infrastructure.

The regional variation in the prevalence of STIs across Africa calls for the implementation of improved measures by the respective subregions towards mitigating the high prevalence of STIs on the continent. We therefore advocate for comprehensive STI education and promotion as an effective means of reducing the burden of STIs in Africa. For instance, China's comprehensive approach to STI management, bolstered by a loan from the World Bank and executed through community-based collaborations, led to notable advancements in knowledge, behavioral change, and prevalence rates [55]. Specifically, in areas where the project was implemented from 2002 to 2007, the percentage of the general population providing correct answers to HIV/AIDS/STI knowledge questions increased from 3.1% to 17.4%, and condom use during the most recent sexual activity increased from 19.8% to 30.1% [55].

Using the SEM levels, several variables are either risk or protective factors associated with SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa. These risk factors are education, condom use during last sex, mass media exposure, employment status, marital status, religion, wealth quintile, health insurance coverage, terminated pregnancy, ever tested for HIV, and the number of sex partners excluding spouses in the last 12 months. However, age, sex of household head, age at first sex, and subregion were protective factors.

This study found that the prevalence of SR-STIs decreased with increasing age, consistent with findings from Eswatini [2] and Malawi [56]. Several biological and behavioral mechanisms help explain why women aged 20–29 years carry a higher burden of STIs. The biological susceptibility of women aged 20–29 years to STIs cannot be overemphasized and therefore requires stringent measures to curb sexual exploitation [57, 58]. Women aged 20–29 years are often more likely to participate in sexual behaviors associated with increased STI acquisition risk, including early sexual initiation, relationships with significantly older partners, and inconsistent condom use, typically in situations where power imbalances hinder their ability to negotiate safer sex [9, 59–61]. Structurally, younger women may have limited access to comprehensive sexuality education and youth-friendly health services, which can reduce awareness of STI symptoms and preventive practices, contributing to delayed care-seeking and continued transmission [62, 63].

Education is widely recognized as a factor that enhances women’s autonomy, understanding of health issues, and access to healthcare services [64]. Consistent with previous studies, we found a significant association between education level and SR-STIs [64–66]. We found that sexually active women who had at least a formal education had higher odds of SR-STIs compared to those without formal education. This outcome does not necessarily suggest that educated women face a greater biological risk; rather, it may indicate a heightened awareness of STI symptoms, better health-seeking behaviors, and an increased tendency to identify and report infections. Women with formal education are more inclined to access information regarding sexual and reproductive health, get tested, and utilize health services, thereby enhancing the likelihood of SR-STIs. Systematic reviews have documented similar trends, revealing that education enhances knowledge and reporting of sexual health issues without necessarily leading to an increase in risky sexual practices [67, 68]. Conversely, women with limited or no education may experience underdiagnosis and underreporting due to a lack of awareness, stigma, and decreased access to healthcare, which conceals the actual incidence of STIs in this demographic [69].

In line with research conducted in Africa [2, 69, 70] and Indonesia [71], sexually active women who were employed reported higher incidences of SR-STIs compared to those who were unemployed. A plausible reason could be that employed women tend to have better access to health information, screenings for STIs, and healthcare services due to financial resources or benefits related to their jobs, which enhances their ability to recognize symptoms and increases the chances of testing and receiving a diagnosis [69].

Consistent with a previous study in Africa [72], sexually active women who were cohabiting had higher odds of SR-STIs. A possible explanation is that cohabiting women may engage in unprotected sex with partners who have multiple sexual partners and may also lack the autonomy to negotiate safer sex, thereby increasing their risk of acquiring STIs and, consequently, their likelihood of self-reporting STIs [73, 74].

Similar to previous studies conducted in Africa [2, 9, 35, 50], this current study found that sexually active women with two or more sexual partners excluding their spouses were more likely to have higher odds of SR-STIs compared with those who had no sexual partners aside from their spouse in the last 12 months. Possible reasons include a lack of communication and romantic intimacy, dissatisfaction with a partner's sexuality, or inadequate sexual skills [56]. To mitigate SR-STI risk in this population, focused interventions should emphasize comprehensive sexual health education, the promotion of regular condom use, and routine STI screenings, while also addressing gender power dynamics and enhancing access to women-centered sexual and reproductive health services.

Consistent with studies conducted in China [75], South Africa [76], and the United States of America (USA) [77], this study found that women who had ever terminated a pregnancy had higher odds of SR-STIs. Closely related is religion, our study found that sexually active Muslim women had higher odds of SR-STIs. This finding is consistent with a country-level study in Uganda [50]. A possible explanation for this could be attributed to the widespread practice of polygamy [50].

In this study, sexually active women in Africa who have subscribed to health insurance had higher odds of SR-STIs. This finding is similar to another study in SSA [69, 78]. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it is likely influenced by reverse causality and differential detection rather than increased biological risk. One potential reason for this is that these sexually active women might have better financial means to obtain STI screening and testing [69]. Furthermore, a higher socioeconomic status is frequently linked to increased opportunities for travel for work or leisure and enhanced involvement in social networks, which could lead to more chances for multiple sexual relationships and, as a result, a higher likelihood of reporting STI symptoms [77]. Consequently, uninsured women may experience underdiagnosis and underreporting due to financial and access barriers, potentially masking the true burden of SR-STIs in this group. These findings underscore the importance of strengthening equitable access to STI screening and prevention services, particularly among women without health insurance coverage. Additionally, ever being tested for HIV was associated with SR-STIs, which is consistent with a study conducted in Sierra Leone [78].

Sexually active women in Africa who used condoms during their last sex had higher odds of SR-STIs compared to those who had not used condoms during their last sex. Similar associations have been reported in studies conducted in SSA [35], Europe [79], and Ghana [80]. However, this should not be interpreted as condom ineffectiveness. This association likely reflects reverse causality, incorrect condom use, and STI testing among condom users. Substantial evidence confirms that consistent and correct condom use remains highly effective in preventing STIs [81, 82].

Research consistently shows that starting sexual activity later is linked to a lower risk of STIs among women [83, 84]. Several studies conducted in Africa [83, 85] and Korea [85] have supported this finding that the odds of SR-STIs were lower for sexually active women who had their first sex after age 19. The plausible reason could be that women aged 20 years and older may not engage in sexual practices that increase transmission risk in Africa [86]. Therefore, initiatives that successfully delay sexual initiation among young people are logical components of a broader approach to decreasing sexually transmitted infections (STIs) among adolescents, who are significantly affected by the STI epidemic [85].

As confirmed by studies in SSA [9, 35] and Nigeria [87], this study found that sexually active women in Africa exposed to mass media had higher odds of SR-STIs. Mass media exposure has proven to be one of the main tools for ensuring comprehensive education and the dissemination of vital information when it comes to sexuality education [35]. Another possible reason could be that some mass media outlets, including newspapers and television, may promote content that includes sexually risky behaviours which could influence sexual decision-making in certain contexts [9]. Overall, these findings highlight mass media as a powerful platform for STI prevention while underscoring the need for accurate and evidence-based sexuality education messaging.

In studies conducted in Africa [9, 78], women in the richer and richest wealth quintiles were found to have increased odds of SR-STIs compared with those in the poorest wealth quintile. One potential reason for this is that these sexually active women might have better financial means to obtain STI screening and testing [78]. Furthermore, a higher socioeconomic status is frequently linked to increased opportunities for travel for work or leisure and enhanced involvement in social networks, which could lead to more chances for multiple sexual relationships and, as a result, a higher likelihood of reporting STI symptoms [88]. This finding is the direct opposite of studies conducted in Bangladesh [89] and the USA [90], where poorer participants were found to have higher odds of STIs.

In conclusion, the regionally diverse prevalence of SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa highlights the critical necessity for tailored, gender-sensitive, and equity-focused strategies that tackle predisposing, enabling, and needs factors while enhancing health systems, broadening fair access to screening and treatment, and utilizing education and mass media for effective sexual health advocacy.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This research used the most recent datasets from DHS in Africa, which incorporate strong and well-established methods for data collection and have been extensively employed in prior studies. Another strength is the robust analytical and statistical methodology applied to enhance the reliability of the results. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional and secondary nature of the DHS data restricts the ability to establish causality and raises the possibility of reverse causation, as the analysis was confined to women's data files. This is particularly pertinent for variables such as condom use during last sex, HIV testing history, health insurance coverage, and mass media exposure. For instance, women who experienced STI symptoms may have subsequently sought HIV testing, used condoms, or accessed health information through media, rather than these factors increasing their risk of STIs. Thus, these associations may reflect reverse causality or detection bias rather than causal effects and should be interpreted with caution. Second, although a large sample size was employed, the results may not be generalizable to all sexually active women in Africa. Consequently, the outcomes of this research should be viewed with caution. Additionally, care should be taken when interpreting the odds, as we recognize that the higher odds ratios related to some variables might be due to the substantial sample size used in this study.

Third, the DHS does not provide laboratory-confirmed STI diagnoses or details regarding treatment history of STIs, which may result in misclassification and hinder differentiation between current, past, and recurring infections. In particular, the reliance on self-reported symptoms and diagnoses may lead to an overestimation of symptomatic infections while simultaneously underestimating the true burden of asymptomatic STIs, which are common among women.

Fourth, factors related to health systems, including STI screening availability, surveillance systems, and national sexual health policies, were also not directly considered and could contribute to differences across sub-regions. Finally, although multilevel modelling addressed clustering at the PSU level, residual community-level confounding related to cultural norms and gender dynamics may still exist. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable, multi-country evidence on the prevalence and multilevel factors associated with SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa, offering important insights for targeted interventions and policy development.

Conclusion and recommendation

This research offers extensive, cross-country insights into the prevalence and various factors influencing SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa. The research revealed that the prevalence of SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa was high at 18.43%. There were significant differences between countries, with rates ranging from 47.9% in Liberia to 3.9% in Ethiopia, underscoring the uneven distribution of SR-STIs across Africa. The results align with socio-ecological model, suggesting that SR-STIs are shaped by a complex interplay of individual, interpersonal, community and societal factors.

Major risk factors for SR-STIs include higher education levels, cohabitation, exposure to mass media, working, condom use during the last sex, history of pregnancy termination, ever being tested for HIV, having multiple sexual partners excluding spouse in the last 12 months, being Muslim, being in the richer or richest wealth quintile, and being covered by health insurance. Conversely, older women, late initiation of sexual activity, living in female-led households, and Southern Africa were found to be protective factors.

Addressing the significant and uneven prevalence of SR-STIs among sexually active women in Africa necessitates focused, contextually appropriate strategies that are guided by the multilevel factors identified in this research. Programs implemented by various governments in Africa should incorporate STI education and routine screening into standard reproductive health and HIV services. Also, there is the need for coordinated programs and policies that will increase health insurance coverage and improve access to quality care. Considering the significant disparities between countries, region-specific approaches that reflect contextual and health system variations are crucial. Tackling these multilevel factors is crucial for promoting reproductive health and accelerating progress towards broader sexual and reproductive health objectives in the region.
