High adiposity linked to presarcopenia and decreased cardiorespiratory fitness in sedentary young Indians independent of their high-fat–salt–sugar (HFSS) food frequency
Authors: Deepa Sanglekar, Richa Soni, Rakesh Rawal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPresarcopenia potentially affects about 10% of youth globally, which may seem an underestimate given the growing incidence of sedentarism and overnutrition. Age-independent muscle loss (“presarcopenia”) has recently been linked to increased cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in middle-aged adults. The early onset of cardiovascular complications, as observed in young Indians, prompts an analysis of non-traditional risk factors like presarcopenia. Therefore, we examine the correlation between musculoskeletal health (MH), cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), and High-saturated Fat–Salt–Sugar (HFSS) consumption in 147 healthy young Indians (mean age: 21 ± 2.16 years, 57% females). Data on body composition, CRF, physical activity, and HFSS food frequency were collected, with one-third of participants exhibiting presarcopenia and high Percent Body Fat (PBF). These traits were more common in females and individuals with normal-weight obesity (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in MH and CRF status across different HFSS frequency groups. CRF strongly correlated with obesity parameters—PBF (r = − 0.81) and visceral fat level (r = − 0.75)—while MH strongly associated with fat-free mass (r = 0.97) at p < 0.001. Since fat mass and fat-free mass are inversely linked, indirect associations between MH and CRF are inferred, suggesting obesity as a common risk factor for presarcopenia and cardiometabolic decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19152-x.Keywords: Cardiorespiratory fitness, Presarcopenia, Muscle loss, HFSS foods, Lean obesity, Indian youthSubject terms: Physiology, Systems biology, Cardiology, Diseases, Risk factors, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

Aging has been implicated as the primary catalyst for muscle decline, with elevated muscle loss most common in older adults1. Despite its strong association with old age, involuntary muscle loss (or presarcopenia) begins in early adulthood, with a decline of 3–5% every decade of adult life2,3. Common risk factors include poor nutrition, a sedentary lifestyle, inadequate sleep, and Vitamin D deficiency4. Although the etiology is not fully understood, persistent sedentary behavior is assumed to promote structural and functional changes in skeletal muscle output by altering the signaling pathways involved in muscle synthesis5. Chronic inactivity, poor nutrition, and a High-saturated Fat–Salt–Sugar (HFSS) diet can lead to muscle breakdown driven by heightened inflammation and disrupted energy metabolism6.

The relative impact of sedentarism versus natural aging on muscle loss remains unclear; however, chronic physical inactivity is considered the leading cause of age-independent muscle loss (or secondary sarcopenia), eventually leading to locomotive syndrome and a threefold increase in elderly mortality rates7–10. Sarcopenia often coexists with a high body fat percentage, leading to “Sarcopenic obesity” or obesity with low lean muscle mass (OLLMM)11,12. Studies on South Asians have highlighted a higher prevalence of normal-weight obesity or lean obesity compared with other ethnic groups13–15.

In addition, sarcopenia and cardiovascular disease (CVD) appear to have an important multifactorial correlation16. Although subcutaneous adipose tissue is the primary site of excess fat storage, higher tissue adiposity often induces ectopic fat accumulation around non-adipose tissues, such as skeletal muscles, the liver, and other vital organs17. These fatty deposits may infiltrate muscle fibers (inter- and intramuscular “myosteatosis”) and reduce their metabolic efficiency via their inflammatory secretome (inducing low-grade systemic inflammation). Muscle cells are also hypothesized to have endocrine properties with a potential regulatory effect on cardiovascular health. Individuals with a high rate of myosteatosis have been reported to suffer from reduced cardiopulmonary efficiency regardless of their skeletal mass18–20.

Muscle loss thus triggers cardiorespiratory inefficiency (as observed in middle-aged adults), leading to a significantly higher CVD risk and CVD-related mortality compared to their non-sarcopenic counterparts21–23.

The latest diagnostic criteria (2019) for presarcopenia, as defined by the Asian Working Group on Sarcopenia (AWGOS), is the loss of muscle mass estimated by a height-adjusted skeletal muscle mass (SMM) or “skeletal muscle index” (SMI) of less than 7.0 kg/m2 and 5.7 kg/m2 (bioelectrical impedance analysis), for adult males and females, respectively, without loss of muscle function or performance24.

In community health assessments, since body weight or body mass index (BMI) is the popular barometer of fitness, early signs of declining physical health, such as presarcopenia, lean obesity, and poor cardiorespiratory efficiency, remain undiagnosed25–27. As young adults appear less at-risk, they are often excluded from comprehensive screening efforts, which further impedes early intervention.

Within the discussion of cardiometabolic fitness, it is worth underscoring that India is the CVD capital of the world, accounting for one-fifth of all CVD-related deaths globally28. Additionally, Indians have a stronger predisposition to premature CVD, which is only partly explained by traditional risk factors29. The incidence of the first acute Myocardial Infarction (MI) is seen a decade earlier in Indians than in their Western counterparts30. And one in five coronary artery disease (CAD) patients in India is under the age of 4031,32.

Based on the above findings, scientific speculation that premature muscle loss could be associated with early-onset cardiovascular complications seems probable. Our cross-sectional study aims to explore the relationship between CRF and MH status in young Indian participants, while accounting for participants’ HFSS food frequency.

Methodology

Study design and protocol

A single-center fitness assessment camp was organized on September 30th, 2024, at the Department of Life Science, Food Science, and Nutrition, Gujarat University, India, as part of the National Nutrition Month program.

For the study purpose, data on body composition (bioelectrical impedance)33, cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) (YMCA three-minute step-up test)34, Physical activity (International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ))35, and High-Fat–Salt–Sugar (HFSS) foods (using a designed food frequency questionnaire) were extracted from the Health Camp (2024) database for healthy young volunteers (18–30-year-olds) who reported 6–8 hours of sitting (daily) and engaging in less than 150 min of moderate to vigorous physical activity per week36 (For sample demographics and standard/reference values for test parameters, refer to Tables 1 and 2).

All data were collected with written informed consent from participants and in line with the Declaration of Helsinki37. The study is ethically approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, Gujarat University, India (Ref: GU-IEC (NIV)/03/PhD/036) .

Fitness assessment tests

Despite the high accuracy of DEXA scans (the gold standard in musculoskeletal screening), constraints such as time, expense, and device expertise made the bioelectrical impedance system (InBody 270™) a preferred choice34. Likewise, the three-minute step test (previously validated in the Asian population)38 was preferred due to its reliability and ease of execution.

(ii)Cardiorespiratory fitness was assessed using the Young Men’s Christian Association (YMCA) three-minute step-up test.34 The brief protocol is as follows: Subjects were seated in a temperature and humidity-controlled area with a five-minute resting phase, after which their resting heart rate (RHR) was recorded using a pulse oximeter. Based on SMI & PBF cutoffs for healthy persons (Table 2), participants were grouped into “characteristics of Sarcopenic Obesity (SO)” and “Not-Sarcopenic Obese (Non-SO)”. To find if there exists a significant difference in various sets of categorical variables (e.g., gender, HFSS frequency) among the two groups, a two-tailed Chi-square analysis/Fisher’s exact t-test was performed. A Kruskal–Wallis test was performed to determine whether a substantial variance exists in VO2 max, PBF, and SMI among individuals with high versus low HFSS consumption frequency. Statistically significant covariates in the inter-group comparisons were further used to develop a regression model for VO2 max. and SMI. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to investigate the effect of covariates, such as sex, on VO2 max. Unless specified, Pearson’s Correlation values for p < 0.05 (CI = 95%) were considered significant. Using DATAtab, regression equations were derived for dependent variables: cardiometabolic fitness (VO2 max.) and musculoskeletal status (SMI), using independent variables like ‘gender/ sex’, ‘body weight’, and ‘PBF’, in the sample cohort (n = 147)*.

*There are no studies on presarcopenia prevalence in the Indian youth; hence, based on recent Korean data for youth presarcopenia40, the prevalence was assumed at 5.4%. Using DATAtab, the minimum sample size was calculated as n = 114, with a study power of 80% and a 6% margin of error at a 0.05 significance level.

Study limits

This cross-sectional study suffers from the following critical limitations:

The study does not categorize participants based on the number of sedentary hours they spend. Since most participants were college students, we did not observe substantial differences in their physical activity levels that would allow for appropriate segmentation. The HFSS FFQ is a basic version and is strongly susceptible to participant recall bias. The FFQ’s effectiveness is also limited because it cannot measure caloric intake, macronutrients, or identify dietary deficiencies. Therefore, any lack of correlation between HFSS frequency, CRF, and musculoskeletal health must be re-examined before drawing definitive conclusions. Since muscle function tests were not performed, the prevalence of sarcopenia in this cohort remains unknown. Sex as a biological variable remains deeply intertwined with the reported cardiometabolic outcomes. While females were observed to have a relatively high risk for SO, the small sample size and absence of serological analyses (for micronutrient deficiencies, etc.) indicate the need for larger datasets to confirm these findings.

Results

Participant demographics

Based on our inclusion and exclusion criteria, we extracted data for 147 healthy young college-going participants (57% females). For more details, refer to Table 1 and Fig. 1.

Body composition assessment

Using multifrequency bioelectrical impedance, values for height, weight, body mass index (BMI), total body water (TBW), total body protein and minerals, fat mass, percent body fat (PBF), fat-free mass (FFM), waist-hip ratio (WHR), visceral fat level (VFL), skeletal muscle mass (SMM), and skeletal muscle index (SMI) were estimated. Mean values (± standard deviation) for these are detailed in Table 1.

The mean height, weight, and BMI characteristics for the cohort were 163.5 ± 10 cm, 58.3 ± 13.4 kg, and 21.7 ± 4.1 kg/m2. According to the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) 2010 BMI criteria41,42, almost half the participants (n = 72, 49%) had an optimal BMI score. A higher BMI was observed in only 29.9% of them, while 21.1% were classified as underweight. However, when body fat percentage was analyzed (based on global cutoffs43 for young adults), 75% of those within a healthy BMI range had excess body fat.

Even if the PBF is assessed at a higher cutoff (i.e., an upper limit of 33% instead of 28%, as some studies suggest for Asian women)44,45, 67.1% (n = 57) of females and 61.9% (n = 91) of the total participants qualify as overweight or obese. An independent t-test analysis revealed a significantly higher PBF in females compared to males (85.9% vs. 54.8%, p < 0.0001). This may help explain the higher prevalence of sarcopenic obesity (SO) characteristics among female participants in our cohort (see Table 1).

To further evaluate adiposity levels, we analyzed their waist-hip ratio (WHR)46. Disturbingly, 70.7% of participants exhibited unhealthy WHR scores, indicating central obesity. The majority had low total body water (66.7%), low body protein (63.9%), and low mineral content (57.1%), with a higher body fat mass (51.7%). Analyzing surrogate indices indicative of muscle loss, such as the fat-mass index (FMI)47, Fat-free mass index (FFMI)48, and fat-mass to fat-free mass ratio49 revealed a higher fat-mass-to-fat-free mass ratio in 75.5% of individuals. Their FMI and FFMI measures further confirmed the higher prevalence of body fat (48.3%) and lower muscle mass (84.4%).

Regarding visceral fat, one in four participants (n = 40, 27.2%) had a concerning (> 10) visceral fat level (based on the InBODY 270™ system threshold), given its association with metabolic disorders50. Guidelines recommend a healthy VFL at a 10–12% limit of total body fat51. High visceral fat tissue is a major cardiometabolic risk factor and has been consistently linked with the development of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular mortality, regardless of PBF and subcutaneous fat52,53. We found traditional parameters (like body weight and BMI) less reliable; instead, we categorized participants based on their PBF, as “normal-weight obese (NO)”, “overweight and obese (OO)”, and “low-weight obese (LO)” to assess relative differences in muscle loss.

Low skeletal muscle mass (SMM) was observed in 67.4% of the participants, and based on AWGOS (2019) criteria, loss of muscle mass or presarcopenia was diagnosed in 41.5% (n = 61) of participants. A chi-square analysis revealed a significantly higher incidence of presarcopenia in females (67% females vs. 43.5% males, p < 0.0001) and participants with normal-weight obesity (74% NO vs. 13.6% OO, Fisher’s test, p < 0.0001). Spearman correlation showed a negative and weak (but statistically significant) relationship between PBF (%) and SMI (r (145) = − 0.27, p = 0.001). SMI was strongly correlated with lean body mass attributes, such as total body water (r = 0.97, p < 0.001), fat-free mass index (r = 0.94, p < 0.001), and body weight (r = 0.82, p < 0.001). Since muscle performance was not assessed, “characteristics of sarcopenic obesity (SO)” were defined as having both presarcopenia and high PBF, as they are more likely to develop sarcopenia and face a higher risk of metabolic disease. Based on this, approximately one in three participants (n = 55, 37.4%) exhibited SO. Those without SO traits were categorized as “Not-Sarcopenic obese” (Non-SO), and the two groups were compared regarding CRF levels and HFSS frequency in the following analysis.

Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) assessment

Using the YMCA three-minute step-up protocol, we collected participants’ data for resting heart rate (RHR) (before activity) and post-activity Heart Recovery Rate (HRRpost). Peak oxygen consumption (VO2 max.) (ml/kg/min) during the activity was indirectly estimated using the YMCA regression equation by Chung et al.39 (Refer to Eq. (1)). Based on Indian cutoff values for peak O2 consumption54, most of the participants (n = 137, 93.2%) qualified with a “Good” to “Excellent” CRF status. However, their post-activity heart recovery rate (HRRpost) was rated as either “very poor” or “poor to fair” (n = 115, 78.2%) based on the YMCA age-adjusted cutoff34.

When correlated (using Spearman correlation) with various body composition variables, VO2 max showed the strongest (negative) correlation with PBF (r = − 0.81, p < 0.001) and the fat mass to fat-free mass ratio (r = − 0.81, p < 0.001), followed by fat mass index (r = − 0.78, p < 0.001) and visceral fat level (r = − 0.75, p < 0.001). In contrast, VO2 max showed a weak correlation with other components such as SMI (r = 0.29, p < 0.001), body protein (r = 0.38, p < 0.001), and WHR (r = − 0.21, p = 0.009).

Furthermore, the Mann–Whitney U test analysis revealed significantly higher VO2 max values and, consequently, greater CRF in the Non-SO group compared to the SO participants (p = 0.015) (Fig. 2a–d).

HFSS food frequency assessment

Based on participants’ responses to the FFQ, we classified them into “Low-frequency consumers” (frequency ranging from bimonthly to rarely), “Moderate frequency consumers” (weekly), and “High-frequency consumers” (daily or biweekly). HFSS food frequency (non-normal distribution) was correlated with CRF status using the Spearman rank correlation, which showed its negligible effect on HRRpost (r (145) = − 0.03, p = 0.754) and participants’ VO2 max levels (r (145) = 0.08, p = 0.308) for different HFSS frequencies. Likewise, the correlation effects were negligible between PBF (%) and reported HFSS frequency (r (145) = − 0.02, p = 0.777). Therefore, no impact was observed on the CRF status of participants based on their reported frequency of consumption of HFSS foods.

Variance in body composition variables (statistically significant in SO vs. Non-SO comparisons), such as TBW, PBF, body weight, minerals, WHR, HRRpost, SMI, VO2 max, and others across different HFSS consumption categories, was analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis Test. A p-value of 0.639 and 0.608 (Kruskal–Wallis Test), respectively, for SMM and SMI (related to HFSS frequency), indicate no statistically significant differences in HFSS consumption (low vs. high) patterns concerning muscle mass.

Discussion

This study highlights several negative changes in young participants leading sedentary lifestyles. Specifically, muscle loss (presarcopenia) and subsequent declines in cardiorespiratory efficiency despite having “good” VO2 max values are observed in individuals with higher obesity levels. Our findings contrast with global estimates on youth presarcopenia prevalence (41.5% vs. 10% as per global estimates)27. Despite “good” CRF status (n = 137, 93.2%), poor post-activity heart rate, possible sarcopenia, and central obesity were majorly reported. A statistically significant difference in peak O2 levels between Non-SO and SO groups (Non-SO > SO, p = 0.015) and the co-occurrence of muscle loss with higher adiposity confirms a bidirectional relationship between body composition and CRF or aerobic fitness.

Our study found no correlation between HFSS food frequency and CRF/MH status; however, a growing body of research has causally linked HFSS food habits to high PBF, low-grade inflammation, and obesity risk58. “High-consumers” of HFSS foods would, therefore, typically show a higher body fat percentage compared to “low-consumers” with similar activity levels. The lack of a statistically significant association could be due to the frequency questionnaire’s inefficiency in accurately estimating consumption levels. Participants may underestimate their intake of HFSS foods, and issues such as recall bias are common with methods like diet recall and FFQ59–61. Perhaps a combination of monitoring techniques, like image capture of meals consumed, and other objective analytical tools, such as serum metabolite profiling, could have provided increased precision62.

Although muscle strength or performance was not assessed to meet the diagnostic criteria for sarcopenic obesity, the higher prevalence of its clinical markers (e.g., Low SMI, low body protein, and high PBF) suggests an impending sarcopenia prognosis. Furthermore, females have shown a higher SO risk, consistent with studies by Yang et al.63 and Hwang et al.64 but contradicts Altaf et al. (2024), who found that males have a higher risk for sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity61,62. This contradiction appears to be related to the sample size of the study. In larger samples, males are often more likely to be diagnosed with sarcopenia, as seen in geriatric populations65. We don’t know if this applies to young adults. In our analysis of covariance, “sex” had a significant interference on the observed health outcomes, thereby limiting our ability to understand the true interaction between SMI and CRF. We also found higher SO prevalence in participants with normal-weight obesity. A statistically higher (p < 0.0001) fat-mass to fat-free mass ratio was observed in participants (60.87% Non-SO vs. 100% SO) with possible sarcopenia. A “high-fat mass with high/normal BMI” was subsequently identified as the most common at-risk phenotype in young adults. Although causality cannot be established using the present dataset, it is possible that the “high-fat mass with high/normal BMI” phenotype may be responsible for poor skeletal muscle metabolism and decline in CRF via mechanisms such as ectopic fat accumulation and myosteatosis.

Our current findings reveal a key limitation: it does not categorize SO changes in participants based on their level of sedentarism. The number of sedentary hours per day has a significant impact on fitness metrics, such as the rate of muscle mass change66,67. The study also fails to account for hidden nutrient deficiencies that may speed up muscle decline (Refer to “Study Limits”). Vitamin D deficiency is surprisingly common in tropical countries, such as India68,69. Vitamin D, protein, and calcium deficiencies have been associated with low muscle mass70. Ironically, only a few studies have demonstrated positive Vitamin D supplementation effects on muscle mass and muscle remodeling71, with no clear consensus on its role in treating sarcopenia in the elderly72,73.

It is also crucial to recognize that the cutoff points for BMI, PBF, and other indices used in this study are flexible and evolve as our understanding of the overall NCD burden improves. Additionally, inconsistencies exist in the scientific literature regarding the most appropriate cutoff values for Indians concerning SMI, FMI, FFMI, and other measures, which can significantly influence data interpretation.

Despite these shortcomings, our observations importantly highlight potential sarcopenia risks among youth and their effects on cardiorespiratory fitness. Additionally, the unusual susceptibility to possible sarcopenia in normal-weight obese participants calls for further investigation. The high exposure to major risk factors like HFSS foods during childhood and adolescence increases the growing NCD burden in India. The recently updated CVD guidelines in India renew the focus on premature CVD cases, especially in urban areas, by redefining dyslipidemia and establishing new cutoffs for “non-fasting” lipid measurements to improve screening and early detection74,75.

Muscle loss and its cardiometabolic effects in young people are rarely addressed in major public health discussions. Heart health has become an increasing concern among Indian youth due to the early onset of issues and alarming reports of sudden, tragic cardiac deaths in young, seemingly healthy individuals29,30,76. While the higher CVD risk among Indians is linked to their unique biological and social factors, early life exposures to risks like obesity and muscle decline could significantly hinder optimal CRF.

The limited sample size and exploratory nature of this study restrict our ability to comment on the causal relationship between presarcopenia and decline in CRF. However, the co-occurrence of low muscle mass and high adiposity among poor CRF individuals prompts further analysis. Our study presents a strong case for careful examination in larger epidemiological studies to establish the true prevalence of youth presarcopenia. Longitudinal studies and intervention trials could show whether diet and physical activity therapies can improve muscle mass and, consequently, CRF.

Conclusion

Presarcopenia is more prevalent in young adults than previously assumed. Our study infers an indirect association between skeletal muscle mass and cardiorespiratory fitness (both linked through adiposity). Body composition measures, such as total percent body fat and fat-free mass, predict cardiometabolic health and the decline of muscle mass. A high PBF is strongly linked to reduced cardiorespiratory efficiency. Since it has an indirect connection with skeletal muscle mass or overall lean fat-free mass, obesity may increase the risk of presarcopenia in sedentary young Indians. Our study also demonstrates that obesity assessment and the YMCA three-minute step test can be practical tools for predicting muscle loss and cardiometabolic efficiency during health camps. However, large-scale studies are needed to confirm these findings, especially the causal link between SMM loss and CRF decline.
