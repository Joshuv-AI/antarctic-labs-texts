Food away from home and the risk of non-communicable diseases among young working adults in Pune, India: a smartphone-based dietary assessment
Authors: Swapnil Godbharle, Angeline Jeyakumar, Hema Kesa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe shift towards increased consumption of food away from home (FAFH) has been recognized as a significant contributor to the global rise in non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Despite this, dietary assessment in such contexts often relies on traditional methods prone to recall bias. This study, therefore, employed a novel smartphone-based dietary tracking application called “FoodLog” to investigate the factors associated with FAFH consumption and its relationship with NCD risk among young working adults in Pune, India.MethodsA case-control study was conducted with 1,000 participants (330 cases, 670 controls), aged 25–45 years. Dietary intake was recorded over five consecutive days using the ‘FoodLog’ app, designed to minimize recall bias. Sociodemographic data were collected via a semi-structured Google Forms questionnaire. Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios were calculated to assess associations between FAFH consumption, participant characteristics, and NCD risk.ResultsApproximately 35% of participants reported consuming FAFH during the previous week. FAFH consumption was significantly associated with male gender, being married, employment as a consultant, and living alone. Correlations were also found between self-reported chronic conditions and variables such as age (36–45 years), certain professions, marital status, physical inactivity, alcohol consumption, smoking, non-vegetarian diets, and FAFH consumption. FAFH consumers had a 1.48 times higher likelihood of having chronic conditions (AOR = 1.48, CI: 1.25–1.87).ConclusionsThe use of a mobile dietary app offers promising avenues for scalable nutrition monitoring. Findings support targeted public health interventions tailored to urban subgroups and encourage multidisciplinary research to address contextual dietary determinants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01180-5.

Background

Food consumption patterns have changed significantly worldwide, with an increase in consumption of food away from home (FAFH). FAFH refers to all foods and beverages that are prepared outside the home, regardless of where they are consumed. This includes meals and snacks purchased and eaten at restaurants, cafes, or street vendors, as well as food obtained through takeout, delivery services (such as Uber Eats, Zomato, or Swiggy), and ready-to-eat items from food shops or canteens [1, 2]. Factors driving this shift include globalization, urbanization, economic growth, a rising middle class, longer work hours, and more women joining the workforce. FAFH consumption is notably higher in developed nations. The USDA reported in 2020 that over 50% of food spending in the U.S. was on eating out [3], with similar trends seen in other developed countries like Canada, Australia, Europe, Japan, and South Korea [4–7].

However, the most notable increase in FAFH consumption is occurring in developing countries due to rapid urbanization, economic growth, and rising incomes. In India, the percentage of households eating out rose from 23% to 39% between 1994 and 2010 [8]. The traditional Indian diet, once rich in grains, pulses, vegetables, and home-cooked meals, is increasingly being supplanted by processed, calorie-dense, and ready-to-eat foods. Urban residents, faced with time constraints and longer commutes, increasingly opt for fast food, takeaway, and packaged snacks readily available in metropolitan markets and through food delivery platforms [9, 10].

Cities like Pune, Mumbai, Chennai, Hyderabad, and Bangalore are experiencing significant changes in food habits due to rising incomes and globalization. Pune, with its thriving information technology (IT) sector and educational institutions, has attracted many young professionals, leading to busier lifestyles and a greater reliance on FAFH, especially among young adults. Notably, males in this demographic tend to eat out more and spend more on FAFH than females, likely due to socio-cultural norms and differences in work roles. Insights from Pune are relevant not just locally but also to other urban cities in India, witnessing similar growth and lifestyle changes among young adults [10–13].

This rise in FAFH consumption has significantly contributed to the global increase in non-communicable diseases (NCDs), due to their high levels of unhealthy fats, sugars, and calories. In India, NCDs accounted for 53% of total deaths in 2005, projected to rise to 67% by 2030. FAFH consumption is also linked to metabolic disorders such as high blood pressure and cholesterol, leading to a higher risk of heart disease, stroke, and diabetes. Additionally, larger portion sizes of FAFH increase total energy intake, worsening the risk of metabolic syndrome and related NCDs [14–18].

Despite the importance of this issue, research on FAFH patterns and related health risks among young urban Indian professionals remains scarce. Existing studies frequently rely on 24-hour recalls or food frequency questionnaires (FFQ), which are subject to recall and reporting biases [10, 19]. To address these limitations, this study utilized “FoodLog,” a validated smartphone app enabling real-time dietary assessment, thereby improving accuracy and participant compliance. Using this innovative tool, we investigated the determinants of FAFH consumption and its association with NCD risk in young working adults in Pune, India.

Methods

Study design

A case-control study design was employed for its efficiency in examining associations between exposures like FAFH and NCDs within a practical timeframe. Cases consisted of young adults who self-reported having been diagnosed by a physician with chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cancer, or cardiovascular diseases (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke). Controls were selected in a 1:2 ratio from the same population and consisted of individuals who self-reported having no history of these conditions.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study applied specific inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure a well-defined sample of young working adults aged 25 to 45 years. Eligible participants were employed across diverse sectors such as IT, finance, insurance, professional services, customer service, and healthcare, fluent in English, possessed an Android smartphone necessary for using the FoodLog app, and had spent at least one year in their current organization. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, eating disorders, temporary residence, cognitive impairments, and concurrent participation in other dietary or health interventions.

Study setting/locale The study was conducted in Pune, India, a city located in the western Indian state of Maharashtra.

Study sample The study employed a purposive sampling approach to recruit young professionals in Pune. While purposive sampling may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability, efforts were made to enhance sample diversity by including participants from multiple sectors, ensuring representation across genders, educational backgrounds, and varying work experiences. This approach aimed to capture a broad spectrum of the target population’s characteristics, thereby improving the relevance and applicability of the findings within the urban professional community.

Sampling frame and sample selection

The research employed a comprehensive approach to actively recruit participants for the study. This involved leveraging several communication channels, such as WhatsApp and email, to ensure maximum outreach and engagement. The researcher contacted the Human Resources (HR) departments of different companies and organizations across various industries, including technology, finance, customer service, and healthcare, to reach potential participants. The team requested these HR departments to distribute the study invitation to their employees via WhatsApp and email, as direct outreach to the employees was not permitted due to company policies. By partnering with HR departments, the research team was able to access a broad pool of potential participants across specific professional sectors.

Sample size

The sample size for this study was calculated using the two-population proportion formula in Epi-Info software version 7.2.2.2. The assumptions made to estimate the required sample size were as follows: the expected proportion of exposure among cases was 50%, and at the same time, among controls, it was 40% since there were no prior studies on the determinants of NCDs in this population. With a 95% confidence level, 85% power, a case-to-control ratio of 1:2, and an odds ratio of 1.5, the sample size was further adjusted to account for a 10% non-response rate and a design effect of 2. This resulted in a required sample size of 347 cases and 693 controls for a total of 1040 participants from Pune, India. A final sample of 1,000 young adults (330 cases, 670 controls) was included in the analysis.

Data collection

Data collection for both cases and controls occurred from January to August 2024 using a semi-structured questionnaire of 52 questions, administered via Google Forms and the FoodLog app. The questionnaire was developed through a rigorous process, starting with a review of literature on FAFH consumption and NCD risk factors among urban adults in India. Relevant items were adapted from validated instruments like the WHO STEPS survey and the National Family Health Survey (NFHS). Context-specific questions were added to meet the study’s unique focus. The draft was pilot-tested for clarity and relevance, with feedback used to finalize the instrument, ensuring cultural appropriateness and comprehensiveness.

The questionnaire included questions related to the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, gender, education level, and anthropometric measurements, including height and weight. Additionally, the questionnaire captured information about the participants’ lifestyle factors, including their job description, personal habits (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption), hours spent in sedentary work, physical activity levels and dietary patterns, as well as the health status of the participants, including the presence of chronic conditions or NCDs.

A customized smartphone application called ‘FoodLog’ was utilized to gather comprehensive dietary intake data. Participants were asked to use the app diligently for five consecutive days, including weekends, to record all food and beverages consumed during this period. This duration balances reducing recall bias, accounting for day-to-day variability, and maintaining participant compliance. Prior usability studies demonstrated that sustained use over multiple days is feasible and yields accurate dietary data [20].

Participants were asked to provide detailed information on the types, quantities (in grams/millilitres), costs, and sources of consumed items, ensuring thorough documentation of dietary patterns. To reduce recall bias, participants were also instructed to enter information as soon as possible after consumption. Additionally, they were encouraged to specify whether the food was prepared at home or away from home to accurately capture FAFH consumption. The FoodLog app was pilot tested before the study using the Mobile Application Usability Questionnaire (MAUQ) to assess user-friendliness, acceptability, and compliance. Pilot results demonstrated high usability and participant adherence, supporting its suitability for real-time dietary data collection. Further validation results of FoodLog are under preparation for publication.

Data analysis

The statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics 21.0 for Windows (IBM SPSS, Inc.). Initially, univariate descriptive statistics, including measures of central tendency (means), variability (standard deviations), and frequencies, were computed for each variable in the dataset. Subsequently, chi-square tests of independence were employed to examine the associations between sociodemographic and behavioural variables and both FAFH consumption and the risk of NCDs. Additionally, both crude and adjusted odds ratios were calculated to investigate the strength of the relationships between sociodemographic and behavioural variables, FAFH consumption, and the risk of NCDs. A significance level of P ≤ 0.05 was utilized to determine statistically significant associations. For the regression models, adjustments were made for key confounding variables, including age, sex, marital status, educational attainment, occupation sector, living arrangement, physical activity, smoking status, and alcohol consumption.

Ethical considerations

Before the commencement of the study, all participants were required to provide informed consent by selecting a checkbox option in Google Forms. The consent process encompassed furnishing users with comprehensive details regarding the study, including information about the purpose of the research, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality measures, and contact information for the researchers. The study was part of the author’s doctoral research and received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) of Savitribai Phule Pune University (SPPU) after thoroughly reviewing the research protocol, informed consent documents, and measures to ensure participant confidentiality. The study was assigned the reference number SPPU/IEC/2021/109, indicating its compliance with the ethical guidelines and standards set by the university’s Institutional Ethics Committee. No incentives or compensation were provided to participants for using the FoodLog app or for study involvement.

Results

Table 1 shows the sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants. Of the 347 cases and 693 controls invited, 330 cases and 670 controls participated, resulting in 95.1% and 96.6% response rates, respectively. Therefore, the analysis involved a total sample size of 1000 participants. The mean age of the participants was 35.70 years, with a standard deviation of 5.44 years. Over half of the participants were female (50.4%), and the majority were married or in a domestic partnership (66.2%), resided with their immediate family (54.4%), and identified with the Hindu religion (66.9%). Approximately 40% of the participants were employed in the field of information technology (IT). The mean total number of family members living in a household was 4.26, with a standard deviation of 1.21. The mean number of earning members living in a household was 1.82, with a standard deviation of 0.60.

Table 2 displays the behavioural characteristics of the study participants. More than half (53.5%) of the participants participated in some form of physical activity. The mean duration of the physical activity was 46.32 min (SD 10.67 min). The mean time participants spent on leisure activities daily was 1.17 h (SD 0.40 h), while the mean time spent on sedentary work was 8.50 h (SD 0.59 h). About 23.9% of the participants consumed alcohol and 36% smoked cigarettes. About 22.7% of the participants were overweight, while approximately 0.7% were obese. The participants had a mean height of 165.9 cm (SD 6.1 cm) and a mean weight of 65.1 kg (SD 7.5 kg). More than half of the participants (56.5%) ate meals at least thrice daily. Only 36.4% of the participants ate fruits at least three times a week, and 51.6% ate vegetables at least five times each week. About one-third (71.2%) of the participants consumed non-vegetarian food. Over half of the participants (61.8%) added salt to their meals during or before consumption. The average amount of money participants spent on food and groceries in the past seven days was 2644.20 rupees (SD 1215.17 rupees) ($30.22 ± 13.89).

Table 3 below shows the patterns of FAFH consumption and associated dietary behaviours in the study population. When asked if they had consumed FAFH in the past seven days, 35.2% of the participants responded affirmatively. Among these participants, 36.1% consumed FAFH in restaurants, 33.5% in food shops or takeaways, and 15.1% from street vendors. More than three-fourths (78.6%) of the participants paid for the FAFH they consumed in the seven-day recall period. About 71% of participants consumed FAFH three or more times during the same period. The average expenditure on FAFH during this period was 1084.78 rupees, with a standard deviation of 605.05 rupees ($12.40 ± 6.91).

Table 4 presents the description of FAFH data obtained through the utilization of the ‘FoodLog’ application. The table contains details about all meals recorded by participants in the app over five consecutive days. Among the 1000 participants, 729 (72.9%) used the FoodLog app to document their dietary intake data. These 729 participants collectively recorded a total of 13,724 meals within the application. About 27.9% of these meals were reportedly consumed outside the home environment (FAFH). The primary venues for FAFH consumption were food shops or takeaways (29.8%), followed by restaurants (27.4%) and street vendors (17.5%). Notably, FAFH consumption was most prevalent during dinner (32.1%), followed by breakfast (26.5%). This prevalence likely reflects the flexibility that evenings offer for socializing and leisure activities, making dinner the preferred time for FAFH consumption in this demographic. The average expenditure per FAFH meal over the five days was recorded at 84.78 ± 25.07 Rupees ($0.97 ± 0.29).

Discussion

To the author’s knowledge, this study represents the first examination of the factors influencing FAFH consumption and its relationship with NCD risk among young working adults in Pune, India. The sociodemographic characteristics of the study populations were duly considered, and appropriate representation from all categories, including age, gender, education, marital status, and religion, was represented. In addition to the sociodemographic determinants, the study encompassed specific behavioural determinants, including physical activity, alcohol consumption, tobacco use, Body Mass Index (BMI), fruit and vegetable intake, non-vegetarian consumption, and expenditure on food and groceries. Additionally, the study investigated specific elements related to FAFH consumption, including the location of FAFH consumption, payment for FAFH, frequency of consumption, and expenditure on FAFH. The study also explored the association between FAFH consumption and other variables and the risk of NCDs.

Furthermore, unlike many previous studies that relied on traditional dietary assessment methods such as 24-hour recalls or FFQs, which are prone to recall and reporting biases, this study utilized the FoodLog smartphone application for real-time dietary intake recording. Digital tools, like FoodLog, prompt users to record foods immediately after consumption, thus reducing recall bias and enhancing accuracy [21, 22]. These technology-assisted approaches offer advantages such as improved portion size estimation, standardized food entry, and participant preference for convenience compared to paper-based tools. Recent research supports that digital dietary assessment methods not only improve compliance and user engagement but also address some of the systematic errors present in traditional methods, particularly for populations comfortable with digital technology [23, 24]. Therefore, the use of a validated mHealth app in this study strengthens the reliability of dietary data and represents a novel strategy to better capture dynamic eating behaviours, such as food away from home, in urban populations.

According to the multivariable logistic regression analysis results, a noteworthy correlation was observed between the consumption of FAFH and several demographic and behavioural factors, including gender, occupation, marital status, living conditions, physical activity, alcohol consumption, and cigarette smoking. The analysis revealed a statistically significant association between FAFH consumption and male gender. Notably, findings from other international studies have presented heterogeneous outcomes, with some indicating higher FAFH consumption among males [25–27] and others demonstrating greater FAFH consumption among females [28, 29]. For instance, research from China similarly shows that men tend to eat out more frequently than women, reflecting gendered social norms and occupational roles that influence eating behaviours [30]. In contrast, studies from Brazil have documented more nuanced patterns, sometimes reporting higher FAFH consumption among females, potentially due to differing cultural roles around meal preparation and social dining [31]. This variability in results underscores the complex and multifaceted nature of FAFH consumption patterns across different populations and regions.

The analysis also revealed a significant association between greater FAFH consumption and certain professions. This finding suggests, in line with prior research [32], that time constraints and competing professional commitments may serve as a potential obstacle to the preparation and consumption of home-cooked meals. The contemporary demands of professional life, compounded by rigorous schedules and travel obligations, pose a challenge for individuals in effectively prioritizing and dedicating ample time to grocery shopping, meal preparation, and home cooking, potentially leading to a higher reliance on readily available FAFH options [33].

The results showed a greater likelihood of FAFH consumption among married participants or those in a domestic partnership. However, multiple studies have indicated that being married is generally correlated with increased consumption of healthier foods [34, 35]. This is likely due to factors such as increased availability of time for meal preparation, shared responsibility for household chores, and greater social support and accountability for maintaining a balanced diet within a household setting [36].

Moreover, higher odds of FAFH consumption were also associated with those living alone. This finding aligns with a previous study, which also found a link between living alone and increased FAFH consumption [37]. Living alone in city environments is increasingly associated with social isolation, which diminishes motivation to prepare balanced, home-cooked meals. As a result, individuals in single-person households often rely more on ready-to-eat and convenience foods, many of which are less nutritious and higher in calories, saturated fat, and sodium. Urban living further reinforces this dependency by providing easy access to fast food and delivery services, making it convenient to opt for FAFH. The cultural emphasis on communal meals in Indian society means that those living alone miss out on the social support and meal structure afforded by family or cohabitants, which often contributes to better dietary quality [38–40]. The cultural significance of communal eating in India may enhance dietary quality among married individuals, while its absence for those living alone could contribute to poorer nutrition, illustrating how social dynamics influence eating behaviours.

The findings also indicated that higher odds of FAFH consumption were associated with physical inactivity, alcohol consumption, and cigarette smoking. This finding aligns with a previous study, which also found FAFH consumption and an overall lifestyle that is inclusive of other NCD risk factors such as physical inactivity and smoking, suggesting a potential clustering of unhealthy behaviours [41].

The multivariable logistic regression analysis findings also revealed a statistically significant association between specific determinants, including FAFH consumption, and the prevalence of chronic conditions among the study participants. The analysis considered a wide range of potential determinants, including dietary habits, demographic factors, and lifestyle choices, providing a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing the presence of chronic conditions in the studied population. The regression analysis showed an association between chronic conditions and specific factors, including age, occupation, physical activity, alcohol consumption, tobacco use, and FAFH consumption.

The odds of having any chronic condition escalated with the increased age. This observed trend aligns with the results of comprehensive studies conducted in multiple countries, indicating a consistent pattern of age-related susceptibility to chronic health issues [42–44]. The progressive decline in muscular strength with age can lead to muscular atrophy, potentially explaining the increased risk of NCDs [45]. The analysis also revealed a statistically significant correlation between chronic conditions, specific sedentary occupations, and physical inactivity. Numerous studies have yielded comparable results, demonstrating a substantial association between sedentary behaviour and an elevated risk of developing chronic conditions [46–48]. These studies have emphasized the importance of incorporating regular physical activity and taking frequent breaks from sitting to mitigate the potential health risks associated with sedentary occupations [49].

In this study, a positive association between alcohol consumption and chronic conditions was observed. The findings corroborate with several other studies, reinforcing the link between alcohol consumption and the risk of chronic health conditions [44, 50]. This relationship is attributed to the detrimental impact of alcohol on diverse physiological pathways, including organ impairment, compromised immune function, and disruption of metabolic processes [51]. A similar association was observed between smoking cigarettes and the development of several chronic health conditions. Multiple epidemiological studies have consistently indicated a strong relationship between smoking cigarettes and the onset of preventable NCDs, such as lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [52–54]. Smoking cigarettes has been widely recognized as one of the leading modifiable risk factors for a wide range of preventable diseases, alongside other unhealthy lifestyle choices like poor dietary habits and physical inactivity [55].

The results of multivariable logistic regression also found a positive, significant association between FAFH consumption and the development of chronic conditions. Numerous studies have consistently demonstrated the link between FAFH and increased risks of obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease, even after controlling for potential confounders [1, 2, 56]. This could be due to the typically higher calorie, fat, sugar, and sodium content of most FAFH items compared to home-cooked meals [57]. These findings underscore the role of FAFH as a potential risk factor to the growing global burden of NCDs.

Conclusion

This study underscores the critical importance of developing nuanced, culturally sensitive interventions that account for the complex interplay of individual, social, and environmental factors influencing FAFH consumption and associated NCD risk among young urban adults. Adopting a multidisciplinary and holistic research approach will enable a deeper understanding of these dynamics and facilitate the design of tailored solutions that cater to the unique needs of different communities. Future research should prioritize evaluating the effectiveness and acceptability of diverse intervention strategies across varied population groups. Ultimately, evidence generated through such efforts will inform the implementation of comprehensive programs that effectively mitigate the rising burden of diet-related NCDs in rapidly urbanizing contexts.

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several notable strengths. First, it comprehensively examines the intricate relationship between socioeconomic factors, lifestyle choices, and dietary habits in a rapidly urbanizing region of India. Second, the case-control study design provides a robust framework for investigating the association between FAFH consumption and NCD risk markers, while the rigorous statistical analysis enhances the reliability of the findings. However, it is also essential to recognize some limitations of the study. The case-control design, while efficient for investigating associations, inherently restricts causal inference, preventing definitive conclusions about the directionality or temporality of relationships between FAFH consumption and NCD risk. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported dietary intake and NCD status introduces potential recall and social desirability biases. Participants may underreport socially undesirable behaviours such as alcohol consumption and smoking, while overreporting healthy behaviours, potentially attenuating or exaggerating true associations. Despite adjusting for numerous known confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as personal food preferences, specific aspects of the food environment, and psychosocial variables may also affect the observed relationships. To address these limitations, future longitudinal studies with objective dietary and health assessments are warranted to better elucidate causal pathways and permit more robust, generalizable conclusions.

Policy implications

The findings from this study highlight an urgent need for targeted public health interventions tailored to the diverse subgroups identified, particularly young urban adults with varied sociodemographic and behavioural profiles. Workplace-based interventions could play a pivotal role, given the significant influence of occupation and work-related lifestyles on FAFH consumption. Strategies might include promoting access to healthier meal options in office cafeterias, implementing nutrition education programs, and creating supportive environments that encourage regular physical activity during work hours. Additionally, regulatory measures such as mandatory food labelling at restaurants, takeaways, and street vendors could empower consumers to make informed dietary choices, particularly by providing clear information about calorie content, portion sizes, and nutritional value. Collaboration with the food industry to reformulate recipes for reduced fat, sugar, and sodium content in FAFH offerings would further complement these initiatives. Public policies must also consider social and environmental determinants, addressing urban food environments that shape availability and affordability of healthy options, and targeting at-risk populations vulnerable to unhealthy eating behaviours.
