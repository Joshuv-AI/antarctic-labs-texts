Pulmonary manifestations associated with positive anti neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies: The tip of the iceberg
Authors: Rania Kaddoussi, Mourad Elghali, Jamel Saad, Imene Chaabane, Saoussen Cheikhm'hamed, Souhir Ksissa, Lobna Loued, Ahmed Ben Saad, Wajih ghribi, Asma Ghourabi, Sameh Joober, Nabil Sakly, Naceur Rouetbi, Melek Kechida
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEven though anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) are frequently linked to ANCA associated vasculitis (AAV), it's important to understand that other illnesses, including lung diseases, can also manifest as ANCA positivity. Finding the incriminated pathology might be difficult.AimTo report four ANCA-associated cases with a diagnosis problem.MethodsFour patients were recruited from the allergo-pneumology department of the Fattouma Bourguiba University Hospital of Monastir, Tunisia, over two years (2020–2022). Indirect immunoﬂuorescence technique on neutrophil cells (Euroimmun, Germany) was used for ANCA screening with a positivity limit of 1/20. ANCA typing was carried out by a line-blot technique (Euroimmun, Germany).ResultsThis case series reports four cases (age range: 41–67 years, sex ratio: 0.3) that presented with pulmonary manifestations associated with ANCA positivity. Two patients had perinuclear ANCA with anti-myeloperoxidase on typing, and two cases had cytoplasmic ANCA with one case of anti-leukocyte proteinase 3 on typing. Final diagnoses were pulmonary tuberculosis (case 1), systemic lupus erythematosus (case 2), bronchiolitis obliterans organizing pneumonia (case 3), and pulmonary aspergillosis with AAV (case 4).ConclusionA panel of diagnoses may be evoked in front of positive ANCA, making the diagnosis difficult to determine and requiring multidisciplinary interactions, with imaging and histological investigations having a crucial role in guiding the final decision.Keywords: Antibodies, Antineutrophil cytoplasmic, Lung diseases, Tomography, X-Ray Computed, Histological techniques

Introduction

Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) area group of autoantibodies targeting cytoplasmic granular antigens of neutrophils [1]. ANCA screening, typically conducted through indirect immunofluorescence (IIF), reveals two primary patterns when positive: cytoplasmic ANCA (cANCA: Fig. 1) and perinuclear ANCA (pANCA: Fig. 2) [2]. While multiple ANCA may manifest these patterns, anti-leukocyte proteinase 3 (PR3) and anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO) are recognized as the most specific antibodies when characterized via alternate enzyme-linked immunoassay techniques such as ELISA or line-blot [3].

ANCA are associated with necrotic inflammation of small vessels, known as “ANCA associated vasculitis” (AVV). This heterogeneous group of diseases includes mostly granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), microscopic polyangiitis (MPA), and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) [4]. Renal and respiratory symptoms are the most prevalent manifestations of AVV [5].

Although ANCA is commonly associated with AAV, it's important to recognize that other differential conditions may present with ANCA positivity such as pulmonary infections [6]. Understanding this coexistence can be challenging, as AAV treatment may be fatal in case of misdiagnosed infection [7]. In this context, we present four cases where ANCA positivity coupled with respiratory features, was challenging leading to uncertainty regarding treatment.

Patients and methods

Patients

In this retrospective study, our patients were recruited from the allergo-pneumology department of the Fattouma Bourguiba University Hospital of Monastir, Tunisia, over two years (2020–2022).

Methods

A comprehensive retrospective medical chart review was conducted. A detailed past medical history included a thorough evaluation of past illnesses, current medications (daily, intermittent, and most recent use), and the events leading to the patient's presentation. A meticulous physical examination was performed to rule out critical conditions affecting vital signs (consciousness, respiration, and circulation) and to identify complaint-specific physical findings. Depending on the clinical picture, additional investigations were employed at our institution, including laboratory evaluations, imaging studies, physiological tests, or histological analyses.

Indirect immunoﬂuorescence technique on neutrophil cells (Euroimmun, Germany) was used for ANCA screening with a positivity limit of 1/20. ANCA typing was carried out by a line-blot technique (Euroimmun, Germany). The assessment of specific autoantibodies was performed according to the clinical context.

Results

The mean age was 54.2 ± 10.7 years (range: 41–67 years). There were one man and three women (sex ratio = 0.3)

•Case 1

Case 1

A 67-year-old woman, with a history of diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and coronavirus disease 19 (COVID-19) in 2019, was admitted in 2022. She has been complaining of intermittent hemoptysis since 2019, associated with asthenia, cough, dyspnea, chest pain, and itchy skin since 2021. The clinical examination revealed weight loss, cutaneous nodules of 1 or 2 cm of diameter in the upper and lower limbs, and bilateral crackles on auscultation. Chest computerized tomography (CT) scan detected lesions of encysted bronchi dilatation, accompanied by pulmonary nodules and excavation (Fig. 3). Biological investigations [blood hypereosinophilia, total serum IgE, phosphocalcic analysis, conversion enzyme, blood protein electrophoresis, tuberculin intra dermo reaction, Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MBT) screening in alveolar liquid with culture, hydatid, hepatitis B, C, and human immunodeficiency virus serologies] were negative, expect a positive severe acute respiratory syndrome of coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) PCR. Immunological analysis found positive antinuclear antibodies [ANA: AC-4 and AC-8, titer = 1/400 (positivity limit = 1/180), typing: negative], positive ANCA (p-ANCA, typing: MPO), and positive rhumatoϊd factor (RF) isotypes [RF-IgM and RF IgA titers: >200 RU/mL (positivity limit ≥20 RU/mL)]. A skin biopsy showed a granuloma with a giant cell reaction. To distinguish between tuberculosis, interstitial pneumonia with autoimmune features (IPAF), autoimmune or neoplastic diseases, a pulmonary biopsy was performed, showing the presence of caseating granulomas and concluding to the diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis, based on the 2013 World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. Anti-tuberculosis treatment was started for six months but the patient was lost at follow-up.

Case 2

Axial chest CT images (lung window) through lung bases, showing in (a) multifocal bilateral bronchiectasis, and cavitary lesions with thick irregular walls in both lower lobes (black and white arrows) surrounded by retractile subpleural consolidations. After treatment, a follow up CT scan (b) done Three months later demonstrates resolution of right lower lobe cavity (black arrow) and nearly unchanged left lower lobe cavity (white arrow).

A 46-year-old woman, with no medical history, presented with dyspnea, cervicalgia, and photosensitivity. On examination, she had bilateral lower limbs edema. The patient had anemia and lymphopenia on the blood count with a positive direct Coombs test. A chest CT-scan revealed a pericarditis with bilateral pleural effusion (Fig. 4). Biological investigations (kidney analysis, thyroid hormones, pro-BNP, and phosphocalcic analysis) were normal. Immunological assessments showed positive ANA in the serum and the pleural liquid (Serum: AC-4, titer = 1/200, typing: doubtful anti-PCNA; pleural liquid: AC-4, titer = 1/200, negative typing), positive ANCA (c-ANCA, negative typing), and normal complement fractions (C3, C4), RF isotypes, anti-phospholipid and anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies (ACCP). Pleurisy was exudative and the biopsy revealed a non-specific chronic inflammation. Based on the 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) criteria, the patient was treated as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) with hydroxychloroquine, colchicine and corticosteroids. At follow-up, the patient was complaining of a persistent dyspnea and edema, with the occurrence of asthenia, anorexia, and purpuric lesions in lower limbs. Azathioprine was added and after three months of therapy, the pericardial effusion and pleurisy disappeared, and the respiratory symptoms improved.

•Case 3

Case 3

Axial chest CT scan through lung bases (a) mediastinal window and (b) lung window showing bilateral pleural effusion (white arrows) and mild pericardial effusion (white stars).

A 63-year-old woman, with a history of diabetes, hypertension, chronic myeloid leukemia, and a cirrhosis, was followed up for accidental discovery of excavated pulmonary nodules after a decompensation of her cirrhosis. Patient lost to follow-up and came back a year later (2022), with hematuria along with the appearance of many newly angiocentric excavated lung nodules ground glass densities respecting subpleural regions and condensations (Fig. 5). Immunological analysis showed positive ANCA (p-ANCA, typing: MPO), positive RF-IgM (27 RU/mL), and positive ACCP (12 RU/mL, positivity limit ≥5 RU/mL). Nasal biopsy concluded to a slight chronic inflammation. Based on the international multidisciplinary classification of the idiopathic interstitial pneumonias, the patient was treated with glucocorticoids as bronchiolitis obliterans organizing pneumonia (BOOP) secondary to vasculitis with good outcome at follow-up.

•Case 4

Case 4

Axial and coronal chest CT scan (lung window) showing pulmonary excavated nodules (white arrows) with ground glass densities (black arrows) respecting subpleural areas.

A 41-year-old-man, with the history of pulmonary tuberculosis in 2018, presented in December 2020 with mild hemoptysis. A positive aspergillus serology was found, along with thin irregular walls cavitary lesion located in right lower lobe apical segment cavitary lesion (Fig. 6 a) in the CT scan. The diagnosis of pulmonary aspergillosis was made and the patient was treated with voriconazole. Clinical and CT scan follow-up done in January 2021 (Fig. 6 b) and November 2021 (Fig. 6 c) revealed a favorable progression with resolution of the cavitary lesion and persistent fibrosing subpleural consolidation. In 2022, the patient was readmitted for asthenia, dyspnea, and expectoration. Chest CT scan (Fig. 6 d) revealed newly developed lung cavities in the right upper apical lobe. Clinical examination was normal. Biological investigations (MBT screening and PCR, mycological test, scolex screening, sputum cytobacteriological examination) and histological findings concluded to aspergillus flavus in the sputum. Immunological analysis found positive ANA (AC-4, titer = 1/400, negative typing) and positive ANCA (c-ANCA, typing: PR3). Based on the 2022 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria for granulomatosis with polyangiitis, AAV triggered by aspergillus infection was retained with good outcome at follow-up after specific treatment.

Discussion

The presence of ANCA is not necessarily associated with AAV [6].

In our study, the presence of ANCA was screened by IIF and then typed by line-blotting. In fact, IIF and enzyme immunoassays (EIA) are commonly used for the detection of ANCA [8]. Initially, IIF was recommended as the primary screening technique for these antibodies [9]. Subsequently, high-quality immunoassays became the primary method for ANCA assessment [10]. Indeed, the most recent meta-analysis and systematic review regarding the sensitivity and specificity of ANCA testing techniques indicated that IIF exhibited lower specificity compared to EIA (IIF: c-ANCA = 98.4 %, p-ANCA = 91.4 %; EIA: PR3 = 96.8%–98.3 %, MPO = 95.6 %) [11].

In our cases, we reported a wide range of clinical symptoms and immunological and radiological findings, leading to a diagnosis problem. In all cases, the final diagnosis was retained in accordance with the internationally recognized criteria (ACR/EULAR for AAV, SLICC for SLE, etc.).

Through these cases, we have emphasized that ANCA positivity does not always mean AAV. In fact, in the study of Bornstein et al. [12], authors found that in 45 patients with AAV and 68 patients without evidence of AAV, a lung disease was present in 40 % and 22 %, respectively. In addition, they reported that 20 % of patients with a positive c-ANCA/PR3 without evidence of AAV had an infectious disease, and 14.3 % of patients with a positive p-ANCA/MPO without evidence of AAV had a non-identifiable disease. Moreover, the p-ANCA/MPO profile is recognized as non-specific for autoimmune diseases, as this type of ANCA can also be identified in infectious diseases [13].

Distinguishing between these various differential diagnoses can be challenging indeed especially for treatment decisions. Tuberculosis is a common condition in our region. Clinical manifestations can sometimes be misleading due to the lack of pathognomonic symptoms. Case 1 illustrates that differentiating between tuberculosis and AAV such as GPA, EGPA, and MPA can be challenging. In fact, clinical, radiological, and biological features of tuberculosis may closely mimic AAV, leading to complexities in management, particularly when there is also a positive immunological assessment [13].

A study conducted in Mexico revealed that ANCA were detected in 20 (44.4 %) out of 45 tuberculosis patients using IIF (16 c-ANCA, four p-ANCA) and in 18 (40 %) patients using ELISA (15 PR3-ANCA, three MPO-ANCA) [14]. This underscores the similarities observed between AAV and tuberculosis. Despite ANCA traditionally being considered highly specific for AAV, it's important to recognize that ANCA associated with TB infection does occur [14,15].

Moreover, research has indicated that ANCA is more commonly detected after TB therapy than before, implying that TB treatment itself might stimulate ANCA synthesis [16]. This diagnostic ambiguity complicates therapeutic decision-making. Initiating corticosteroid therapy or immunosuppressants needs first ruling out any underlying infection. Indeed, before starting high-dose immunosuppression in patients with a positive ANCA result, tuberculosis should be actively excluded as a potential diagnosis.

Positive ANCA can also occur in other rare scenarios, as seen in case 4. Pulmonary aspergillosis, for instance, can induce ANCA antigen expression, leading to vasculitis [17]. This unusual association can impede diagnosis and jeopardize patient prognosis significantly.

The spectrum of diagnoses to be considered in cases of positive ANCA is broad, predominantly encompassing vasculitis and tuberculosis due to their therapeutic urgency. However, other diseases should also be taken into account based on clinical guidance [17]. Therefore, therapeutic decisions for patients with positive ANCA should incorporate clinical history, radiological findings, and anatomical examinations. At times, a multidisciplinary approach may be necessary to reach a consensus.

In conclusion, achieving absolute specificity in diagnostic tests, particularly for biological markers such as ANCA, is essentially unattainable. This is because ANCA can exhibit variability among patients.
