Synthesis of a C‐2 Functionalized l‐Iduronic Acid Derivative as a Candidate Pharmacological Chaperone for MPS II (Hunter Syndrome)
Authors: Vaibhavi Nagendra, Shifaza Mohamed, Nicholas W. See, Norbert Wimmer, Daniel Neumann, Paul J. Trim, Marten Snel, Aidan J. Brock, Jack K. Clegg, Neha S. Gandhi, Kim M. Hemsley, Adeline A. Lau, Vito Ferro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA C‐2 functionalized l‐IdoA derivative was designed as a potential pharmacological chaperone for MPS II, and several routes to its synthesis were investigated. Successful synthesis proceeded via an oxidation/Horner‐Wadsworth‐Emmons sequence, overcoming significant challenges with competing elimination reactions to give alkene derivatives in good overall yields. Catalytic hydrogenation of the double bonds proceeded stereoselectively in high yield to give the desired L‐ido‐configured products, confirmed by x‐ray crystallographic analysis. These were subsequently elaborated to the target dicarboxylate prodrug 1 in good overall yield. Molecular docking studies suggest the parent compound engages with the active site and makes several H‐bonding interactions with key active site residues. Target prodrug 1 and known chaperone D2S0 were evaluated against two MPS II cell lines, but neither compound was able to reduce HS substrate levels at the doses tested.

Introduction

Mucopolysaccharidosis type II (MPS II, Hunter syndrome, OMIM 309900) is an inherited, X‐linked recessive lysosomal storage disease caused by mutations in the enzyme iduronate‐2‐sulfatase (IDS) [1, 2, 3]. The biological function of IDS is to hydrolytically cleave O‐linked sulfate groups at the C‐2 position of terminal, non‐reducing end l‐iduronic acid (IdoA) residues from heparan sulfate (HS) and dermatan sulfate (DS) in the lysosomes (Figure 1A) [4]. In MPS II, a deficiency of IDS within the lysosomes results in the progressive build‐up of HS and DS, leading to cell, tissue, and organ damage. MPS II is therefore a progressive and multisystemic disease with a broad range of clinical phenotypes, including skeletal deformities, developmental delay, progressive neurologic deterioration, and shortened lifespan. Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) has been a successful treatment for some LSDs, including the non‐neuronopathic form of MPS II [5], although there are several limitations. The recombinant enzymes cannot penetrate the blood‐brain barrier (BBB), and hence intravenous ERT is unable to ameliorate neurological symptoms. Recent investigations of IDS‐fusion proteins as molecular Trojan horses to carry the recombinant enzyme across the BBB show promise for delivering ERT to the brain [6, 7, 8]. However, other bodily parts, such as the heart valves [9] and bones/joints [10], remain poorly accessible. ERT is also very expensive, with an average annual cost of approximately US $491,000 for a 30 kg patient [11]. Therefore, alternative, safe, and cost‐effective methods of treatment for MPS II patients, which can ameliorate progressive neurological decline and target organs/systems that are inaccessible to ERT, need to be developed [9].

(A) Structures of HS and DS substrates for IDS showing terminal 2‐O‐sulfated l‐IdoA (IdoA2S). (B) Target compound 1, the methyl ester prodrug of 2, a candidate PC for MPS II.

An attractive approach for the treatment of lysosomal storage diseases is pharmacological chaperone therapy (PCT) [12, 13]. Pharmacological chaperones (PCs) are typically active‐site directed small‐molecule inhibitors that can enhance correct folding of the mutant enzyme and trafficking to the lysosomes, thus increasing the amount of correctly localised, catalytically active enzyme and improving lysosomal catabolism of the undegraded substrates. There have been few reports of either IDS chaperones or inhibitors. The commercially available unsaturated heparin disaccharide [14] D2S0, derived from enzymatic cleavage of heparin, has been shown to enhance the thermal stability of recombinant IDS and to increase residual activity of IDS in MPS II patient fibroblasts and in HEK293T cells expressing mutant IDS [15]. Several trihydroxypiperidine dendrimers, originally designed as glycosidase inhibitors, have somewhat surprisingly been shown to inhibit IDS with IC50 values up to 31 µM [16, 17]. However, they were not evaluated for their chaperone activity. There thus remains a clear need to develop PCs for IDS for the treatment of MPS II.

Various sulfamate derivatives have been shown to inactivate sulfatases [18, 19], including derivatives of D‐glucosamine [20], although on re‐synthesis the inhibitory activity of this series could not be replicated [21]. However, due to irreversible inhibition of sulfatases [22], sulfamates cannot be used as PCs because they must be able to dissociate from the enzyme once in the lysosome, leaving the enzyme to perform its catalytic function. We therefore hypothesized that simple analogues of 2‐O‐sulfated IdoA (IdoA2S) in which C‐2 is functionalized with a non‐hydrolysable sulfate group mimic may have strong affinity for IDS and thus are potential pharmacological chaperones for the treatment of MPS II. Herein, we describe the synthesis of C‐2 functionalized IdoA derivative 1, the methyl ester prodrug of putative chaperone 2 (Figure 1B), and concurrently reveal new strategies to overcome the significant challenges associated with manipulating L‐Ido and l‐IdoA scaffolds. Molecular docking studies of 2 reveal that the compound engages the active site of IDS and interacts with key residues in the active site. Furthermore, we report the evaluation as 2 as a PC for IDS in two MPS II patient‐derived cell lines.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis

Compounds mimicking IdoA2S that are negatively charged at C‐2 and C‐6 will likely suffer from poor membrane permeability. To ensure improved cell uptake and therefore more efficient chaperone activity, the target compound was designed as a prodrug with neutral charge and enhanced lipophilic character. Specifically, the PC candidate features a methyl ester at C‐6 of IdoA and a sulfate mimic at C‐2 that is neutral and also functions as a prodrug (─CO2Me, Figure 1B). Both esters are susceptible to the action of non‐specific esterases, which operate in cellulo to liberate candidate PC 2. Our synthetic strategy involved the functionalization at C‐2 of an L‐idose or l‐IdoA derivative, which we envisaged to proceed via a Horner‐Wadsworth‐Emmons (HWE) reaction of a suitably protected 2‐ulose (ketone). The HWE olefination has previously been reported for the synthesis of various sulfonate and phosphonate sugar derivatives [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Many methods exist for the synthesis of L‐idose derivatives [30, 31, 32], although most require multiple synthetic steps, while some have problems with scalability. A key intermediate common to many syntheses is the L‐ido epoxide 3 (Scheme 1). We envisioned that 3 could be readily converted to orthogonally protected L‐idose derivative 8, which could then serve as a general building block for the preparation of PC candidates. In our hands, 3 was best prepared in five steps from diacetone glucose in 65% overall yield following the route of van Boeckel [33]. Epoxide 3 was then converted into the 1,6‐anhydro‐L‐idopyranose 4 via acid‐catalyzed hydrolysis by the method of Hung [34, 35], in 31%–71% yield, depending on the scale. Acetolysis of 4 followed by reaction with thiophenol at 0°C in the presence of BF3·Et2O gave thioglycoside 5 in excellent overall yield (α/β = 10:1). Thioglycoside 5 was then converted into the corresponding methyl L‐idopyranoside 6 (α/β = 30:1) by reaction with methanol promoted by TMSOTf as previously reported [36].

Removal of the acetate protecting groups for 6 under Zemplén conditions proceeded smoothly to give triol 7, used without further purification. Treatment with benzaldehyde dimethyl acetal in the presence of camphorsulfonic acid (CSA) in DMF afforded the 4,6‐O‐benzylidene acetal 8α in good overall yield, with some β‐L‐anomer 8β was also isolated (12%). The α‐ and β‐anomers in L‐ido configured monosaccharides are difficult to distinguish by NMR spectroscopy (similar chemical shift and J1 ,2 values) [36]. To confirm the anomeric configuration of 8α, a sample was converted into its crystalline benzoate derivative 9 and subjected to single‐crystal X‐ray diffraction analysis (see Supporting Information).

In order to functionalize C‐2 of derivative 8 via the HWE reaction, the oxidation of the alcohol at C‐2 to the corresponding ketone was required. The oxidation proved more challenging than anticipated and was plagued by competing side reactions, summarized in Table 1. When alcohol 8 was subjected to Dess‐Martin periodinane (DMP) oxidation, elimination product 10 was isolated as the major product (61%) (Scheme 2). The 1H NMR spectrum of 10 showed H‐4 as a 2.0 Hz doublet at δ 5.80, which is much further downfield compared with that of 8. H‐3 was seen as a doublet of doublets at δ 4.73 with homoallylic coupling of 4.0 Hz (J 3,6a) and 5.8 Hz (J 3,6b) to the H‐6 protons, and H‐5 was absent. In addition, the lack of one set of aromatic signals and the benzylidene acetal proton confirmed the loss of the benzylidene group. The 13C NMR spectrum for compound 10 showed a carbonyl peak at δ 184.3, upfield compared with a typical ketone (≈ δ 200). The high‐resolution mass spectrum for compound 10 was also consistent with the proposed structure. The alcohol 8 is conformationally locked, and H‐5 is antiperiplanar to the substituent at C‐4, which presumably favors an E‐2 elimination of benzaldehyde, promoted by the release of acetic acid during DMP oxidation.

Borbás and Herceg have recently reported problematic side reactions, including elimination, during attempted oxidations on an L‐idose scaffold [37]. The use of PCC led to low yields and extensive decomposition, while the use of the milder Albright–Goldman oxidation (DMSO/Ac2O) gave either elimination or successful reaction, depending on the protecting group pattern [37]. Alternative protocols were then explored for oxidation of 8. Subjecting alcohols 8 and 8β to the Swern oxidation gave the desired ketones 11–12 but only in modest yields (32%), due to decomposition of either the starting material or resulting ketone (TLC). The milder, Albright‐Goldman oxidation of 8 at room temperature gave the desired ketone 11 in an improved 62% yield [38]; however, prolonged reaction time (24 h) was necessary. Ketones 11–12 were found to be unstable in our hands; the subsequent HWE reactions were carried out immediately on the crude products without further purification.

Ketones 11–12 were subjected to the HWE reaction using the in situ generated anion of trimethyl phosphonoacetate, obtained by treatment with sodium hydride, to give the desired α,β‐unsaturated esters 13–14 as mixtures of E/Z isomers in good yield (63%–82%). The formation of E/Z mixtures for 13 and 14 was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy; however, the isomers were neither separated nor characterized on an individual basis, as they converged to a common derivative in the subsequent step.

To obtain the desired C‐2 functionalized L‐idose derivatives from the α,β‐unsaturated esters 13–14, the double bond at C‐2 must be reduced stereoselectively. Pleasingly, catalytic hydrogenation of 13 in the presence of triethylamine (to prevent hydrogenolysis of the benzyl ether and benzylidene groups) selectively reduced the double bond at C‐2, furnishing exclusively the desired α‐L‐idopyranoside 15 in an excellent 82% yield (Scheme 3). The 1H NMR spectral data for compound 15 were consistent with the desired L‐ido configuration (J 2,3 = 5.0 Hz, J 3,4 = 2.9 Hz). Furthermore, the 2D NOESY spectrum showed NOE contacts between the C‐2 methylene group and the signals for both H‐1 and H‐6a/6b (see Supporting Information). Finally, the structure was firmly established by X‐ray crystallography (Figure 2). Presumably, the benzylidene group blocks hydrogenation of the bottom face of the double bond (as drawn), resulting in the L‐ido configured product. The glycoside 15 was then globally deprotected under standard hydrogenolysis conditions to obtain the fully deprotected, C‐2 functionalized α‐L‐idoside 16 in good yield (74%).

Despite the successful synthesis of C‐2 functionalized L‐idoside 16, we wished to improve the oxidation step with its low yield and associated elimination problems prior to elaboration to the final target 1. To more rapidly access an L‐idose derivative with a free OH group at C‐2, we opted to develop an alternative but similar route starting from the 4,6‐isopropylidene protected thio‐L‐idoside 17 [36], which is used for the commercial manufacture of fondaparinux and became available in our lab as a gift from Alchemia Ltd (Scheme 4). Building block 17 was converted into the known [36] methyl glycoside 18 in good yield (84%, α/β = 83:17) [39] by reaction with MeOH promoted by NIS‐TfOH. Crystallization from EtOH enabled isolation of the pure α‐anomer. Next, compound 18 was readily debenzoylated under Zemplén conditions with NaOMe in methanol to give the C‐2 alcohol 19 in quantitative yield.

Given the elimination problems that plagued the corresponding C‐2 oxidation of related substrates above, several different oxidation protocols were trialed on alcohol 19, summarized in Table 1. Alcohol 19 is a challenging substrate for oxidation, given the C‐2 OH group is sterically hindered, and possibly stereoelectronically deactivated (being both axial and antiperiplanar to the anomeric ‐OMe). Furthermore, the 4,6‐O‐isopropylidene group is both acid sensitive and, similar to 8, has H‐5 antiperiplanar to the C‐4 substituent and therefore is likely to be susceptible to elimination. Attempted Swern oxidation of 19 with DMSO activated by either oxalyl chloride or trifluoroacetic anhydride (entries 5–6) gave a complex mixture, as did the use of DMP buffered with pyridine (entry 7) and PCC buffered with NaOAc (entry 9). PDC gave no reaction (entry 8), as did TPAP with NMO·TPB [40], a variant of the Ley‐Griffith oxidation (entry 10). Albright–Goldman oxidation of 19 was successful (∼60% by NMR, entry 11) but required prolonged reaction, and several byproducts were evident. Finally, the Parikh–Doering oxidation, i.e., DMSO activated by sulfur trioxide‐pyridine complex in the presence of N,N‐diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA), was found to rapidly oxidize 19 to the desired ketone 20 in an excellent 94% yield with no evidence of elimination (entry 12). Notably, the purity of crude ketone 20 varied significantly with the reaction time, with decomposition evident by TLC and 1H NMR analysis at reaction times greater than 10 min.

In view of the instability of crude ketone 20, the compound was subjected to the HWE reaction immediately after workup and without further purification to obtain C‐2 alkene 21 in an excellent 81% yield (over 2 steps) as a mixture of E/Z isomers in a ratio of 60:40. In this case, LiOtBu (pKa ∼ 18) was utilized as a milder alternative to sodium hydride. In the next step, the mixture of alkenes 21 was selectively hydrogenated with 10% Pd/C as a catalyst to give the L‐ido configured 22 exclusively in 72% yield. While the C‐3 benzyl ether could be removed under these conditions, its presence was found to be beneficial for the chromatographic purifications in the subsequent steps. Therefore, triethylamine was added once more to the reaction mixture to prevent its hydrogenolysis. The isopropylidene group was then removed by hydrolysis with 70% acetic acid (aq.), pH = 2–3 in DCM to give diol 23 in acceptable yield (69%). The primary alcohol of 23 was then selectively oxidized to the carboxylic acid with catalytic TEMPO and (diacetoxyiodo)benzene (BAIB) as the stoichiometric oxidant in aqueous acetonitrile. Without purification, the crude product was esterified with methyl iodide and KHCO3 in DMF to give methyl ester 24 in moderate yield (40% over two steps). Finally, hydrogenolysis of the benzyl ether gave the target diester prodrug 1 in 80% yield.

Molecular Docking Studies

We next turned our attention to a molecular docking study of the fully deprotected parent compound 2 with IDS (PDB ID: 5FQL) [4], with a view to investigating its potential interactions with the active site of the enzyme. Docking was performed using HADDOCK 2.4 [41] following the procedure described by Schwarz et al. [42]. Both 1 C 4 and 4 C 1 conformations of the ligand were studied. It was found that the ligand binds in the 1 C 4 conformation (Figure 3) in the active site, making hydrogen bonds to key residues K347, K479, and C84 (the catalytic formylglycine), while docking of the 4 C 1 conformation results in a twisted conformation to accommodate binding.

Biological Results

Hunter Patient Cell Line Characteristics

IDS gene mutations in MPS II (Hunter syndrome) patients are heterogeneous at a molecular level with at least 779 unique point variants, the most common type being missense variants (42.4%), followed by small frameshift deletions/insertions and nonsense mutations (32.7% and 11.3%, respectively) [43]. Although the mutations are distributed throughout the length of the IDS sequence, there are several reported hotspots, such as codon 468, which is the most prevalent codon variant with at least six different mutations identified [43]. Analysis of the crystal structure of IDS suggests that the arginine residue encoded at this site confers structural stabilization through key interactions of the basic guanidinium group with “several nearby loops, helical turns and beta‐strands” [4]. We therefore procured an MPS II lymphoblastic cell line harboring the p.R468P for evaluation of the putative pharmacological chaperones. MPS II patients with this mutation are described as having a rapidly progressing phenotype and exhibit clinical signs including global developmental delay, osteoarticular defects, macrocephaly, and hepatosplenomegaly [44, 45]. We also acquired a second MPS II cell line expressing a p.P358R IDS mutation. This mutation is found in the N‐terminal subdomain 1 [4] and is also associated with a rapidly progressing disease course [46]. The variant and sampling details are outlined in Table 2.

Effects of in Vitro Compound Treatment on Intracellular HS Storage

To investigate the effect of the putative chaperone 1, MPS II LCL cells were co‐cultured with 125 µM of compound 1, with the compound replenished daily in cell culture media for five consecutive days. This dose was selected based on the compound availability at the time of the assays. Compared with vehicle‐treated healthy cells, HS substrate load was elevated in vehicle‐treated MPS II lymphoblasts expressing p.R468P and p.P358R IDS mutations by tandem mass spectrometry (p ≤ 0.0001 and p = 0.0040, respectively; Figure 5a,b). Co‐culture with compound 1 did not impact the HS levels in either MPS II cell line at this dose (Figure 5a,b). A higher concentration of 1 mM of compound 1 (supplied daily for 5 days) was subsequently assessed, but also did not alter HS levels in compound 1‐treated MPS II cells (Figure 5c,d).

In addition, we evaluated the known chaperone D2S0 against p.R468P and p.P358R IDS mutations at a final concentration of 10 µM, a dose reported to approximately double IDS activity following administration to MPS II fibroblasts expressing the p.P231L IDS mutation [15]. D2S0 did not alter HS storage levels in either of the MPS II cell lines evaluated here (Figure 5e,f). This suggests that the p.R248P and p.P358R IDS mutations expressed in our patient cell lines were unresponsive to D2S0 treatment. This contrasts with the improved IDS chaperone activity measured in HEK‐293 cells expressing p.N63D, p.L67P, p.A85T, p.R88H, p.Y108S, or p.P231L IDS over‐expressed mutations treated with D2S0 at this same concentration, with up to 40‐fold increases in enzyme activity reported [15]. D2S0 treatment also reduced lysosomal storage (alcian blue‐positive staining) of MPS II fibroblasts expressing the p.P231L IDS mutation [15].

Pharmacological chaperones typically act by stabilizing the misfolded enzyme. If p.R468P and p.P358R IDS mutations result in significant alterations to the enzyme structure, compounds 1 and D2S0 may not be able to rescue and stabilize the mutant protein to restore proper function. IDS activity is also dependent on the sample matrix analyzed. For example, Liebaers and Neufeld [48] have demonstrated that normal lymphocytes and serum have 4‐fold and 18‐fold less IDS activity, respectively, than fibroblast extracts. It is possible that the baseline IDS levels in MPS II LCL cells are too low to enable ‘rescue’ of the misfolded protein in this cell type. Future studies screening compound 1 against other predicted‐to‐be responsive IDS mutations, such as those reported in Hoshina et al. [15], or in other cell types (like MPS II patient fibroblasts or HEK 293 cells that over‐express mutant IDS forms), would be informative.

Conclusions

Several synthetic strategies for obtaining C‐2 functionalized L‐idose or IdoA derivatives were investigated. The preparation of various protected derivatives of L‐idose or IdoA with a free hydroxy group at C‐2 proceeded smoothly; however, these compounds were prone to competing elimination reactions during the subsequent oxidation and HWE steps. Elimination during oxidation could be minimized via the use of DMSO/SO3·Py as the oxidant (the Parikh–Doering oxidation), while the HWE reaction with trimethyl phosphonoacetate and base gave the unsaturated carboxylate derivatives 13–14 in reasonable overall yields. Pleasingly, catalytic hydrogenation of the double bonds proceeded in high yield and with exquisite selectivity to give the desired L‐ido‐configured products, confirmed by x‐ray crystallographic analysis. These were subsequently elaborated to the target dicarboxylate prodrug 1 in good overall yield. Molecular docking studies of the deprotected parent compound 2 suggest that it engages with the active site and makes several H‐bonding interactions with key residues, including the catalytic formylglycine residue C84. Target prodrug 1 and known chaperone D2S0 were evaluated for their chaperone potential against IDS p.R248P and p.P358R mutations, but neither compound at the doses supplied was able to reduce HS substrate levels in these patient cell lines.

Experimental Section

General

Reagents were purchased from Merck and Co. and were used without further purification. All reactions were performed in oven‐dried glassware and were monitored by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) using silica gel F254 aluminum‐backed sheets. Compounds were visualized using anisaldehyde dip. NMR spectra were measured on Bruker Avance 300 and 500 MHz spectrometers and referenced to the residual solvent peaks (δH = 7.26 ppm, δC = 77.0 ppm). 2D NMR experiments, including 1H‐1H COSY and HSQC, aided in structure elucidations and signal assignments. Optical rotations were measured on a Jasco P‐2000 polarimeter. Melting points were measured on a Digimelt MPA161 apparatus. Low‐ and high‐resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (LRMS/HRMS) data were obtained with Bruker HCT and Bruker micrOTOFQ spectrometers, respectively, in positive ionization mode. Flash chromatography was performed on silica gel (230–400 mesh, Grace) under pressure with specified eluants.

General Procedure for Zemplén Deacetylation/Debenzoylation

Molecular Docking

Protein Preparation

The protein was prepared according to the procedure described by Schwarz et al. [42] using UCSF Chimera (version 1.19) and Biovia Discovery Studio Visualizer (v25.1.0.24284). The x‐ray crystal structure of human IDS was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 5FQL) [4]. To prepare the protein for docking, all post‐translational modifications were removed, including the ions and solvent molecules, except for the calcium ion and the one water molecule (552) adjacent to the binding site. This water molecule mediates hydrogen bonding between the ligand and the residues Y348 and K479. In the 5FQL structure, the catalytic formylglycine residue C84 is sulfated. Therefore, to return C84 into the catalytically active form, all the side chain atoms past the β‐carbon were truncated, and the residue identifier was changed to DDZ so that HADDOCK could reconstruct the missing atoms with parameters that would enable the formation of the covalent bond.

Ligand Preparation

The low‐energy conformers of the ligand were generated using the RDKit Python Library within Windows subsystem for Linux (Ubuntu) environment. An ensemble of 100 ligand conformers was generated. Only the conformers that adopted the 1 C 4 (n = 11) and 4 C 1 (n = 12) chair conformations were selected for docking. For each conformer, an ensemble pdb file was generated and formatted using the PDB‐tools web server [57, 58]. This ensured the PDB file was in alignment with the HADDOCK 2.4 mandatory formatting requirements, such as segment IDs, atom nomenclature, and coordinate consistency.

Docking Calculations

Molecular docking was performed following the procedure described by Schwarz et al. [42] using the HADDOCK 2.4 webserver via the guru interface [41]. Under the input parameters section, for IDS (Molecule 1), residues H138 and H229 were automatically identified as active from a pre‐defined set of residues (D45, D46, C84, R88, K135, H138, H229, H335, H347, and K479) based on their surface location and solvent accessibility, while the passive residues were defined automatically within a 6.5 Å radius of active residues. The ligand (Molecule 2) was defined as active by assigning residue 1 to the active site parameters. No distance restraint file was provided to guide the docking protocol. Under the docking parameters section, the sampling was increased to 2000/400/400 for it0/it1/itw, respectively. The final water refinement was turned off. The clustering method was set to RMSD with a cut‐off of 2.5 Å. All other parameters were left to their default settings. Detailed results from the HADDOCK simulations are provided in the (Table S3).

Biological Studies

Cell Lines and Culture

The use of the human cell lines in this study was approved by the Flinders University Human Research Ethics Committee (protocol 5799). The following patient‐derived cell lines were obtained from the NIGMS Human Genetic Cell Repository at the Coriell Institute for Medical Research: GM03798 and GM10866. An additional MPS II cell line (cat. no. 23204) was prepared as described by Filocamo et al. [59], and purchased from the Telethon Network of Genetic Biobanks. Patient or participant consent was not applicable for this study. All cell lines were lymphoblasts (LCL) transformed with Epstein‐Barr virus, and the cell lines' details are described in Table 2. LCL cell lines were routinely cultured at 3 × 105 cells/mL media in 15% FCS (Bovogen; FBSFR‐S00FU or Thermofisher Scientific, 10099141), 1× GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 35050061), and penicillin‐streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific 15070063), made to volume with RPMI 1640 (Sigma, R8758), with the flasks standing upright in a humidified incubator set at 37°C with 5% carbon dioxide. The cells were fed every 3–4 days with complete media replacement after centrifugation at 100 g.

Drug Treatment and Cell Harvest

Compounds were reconstituted in sterile water for injection (Fresenius Kabi Australia). Pharmacological chaperone D2S0 was purchased from Carbosynth (heparin disaccharide III‐S trisodium salt; cat. no. OH28908). Apparently healthy or MPS II LCL cells were seeded at 1.5 × 104 cells/well in 48‐well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat. no. 150687) and allowed to settle overnight. Compounds, or an equivalent volume of water, were then administered on each of five consecutive days. The day following the final treatment, the cells were centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in 200 µL of 20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, pH 7.2. The intracellular contents were liberated by sonication or by six alternate cycles of rapid freezing/thawing in a dry ice slurry and 37°C water bath. The total protein content was measured using Thermo Fisher Scientific MicroBCA kits (cat. no. 23235) at an absorbance of 562 nm on a Spectramax iD5 plate reader.

Heparan Sulfate and IDS Activity Assays

HS was quantified utilizing the protocol established by He et al. [60], using 10 µg of lyophilized total protein equivalent. In brief, following butanolysis digestion, the relative peak area of HS disaccharides in intracellular extracts was quantified by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) using an API4000 QTrap or API5500 QqQ instrument (ABSciex, Concord, Ontario, Canada), interfaced with a Waters Acquity UPLC system and Analyst 1.6.2 software (ABSciex). Peak areas were normalized to total protein content.

IDS activity methods were adapted from the conditions described by Voznyi et al. [47]. Assays were conducted for 4 h at 37°C in the presence of 1.25 mM 4‐methylumbelliferyl α‐l‐idopyranosiduronic acid 2‐sulfate disodium salt substrate (cat. no. EM03201; Carbosynth; 1.25 mM), followed by a 24 h‐incubation with recombinant α‐iduronidase enzyme sourced from R&D Systems (cat. no. 4119‐GH) at a final concentration of 1 µg/mL. Fluorescent counts were measured using a Molecular Devices SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader at an excitation of 355 nm and emission of 460 nm and were compared to a standard curve prepared from 4‐methylumbelliferone (cat. no. M1381; Sigma) to convert sample counts to activity rates.

Statistics

Unpaired t‐tests or ordinary one‐way ANOVA were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (v.10.4.0). Post‐hoc testing was conducted using the Bonferroni test, and p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
