Planning for Preservation: Feasibility of Erectile Tissue Sparing During Prostate Stereotactic Radiotherapy
Authors: Felicity Hudson, Glen Dinsdale, Tony Young, Anne McMaster, Tania Erven, Olivia Ryan, Sankar Arumugam, Theresa Nguyen, Mark Sidhom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAn estimated one in six Australian men are diagnosed with prostate cancer (PC) by age 85. Cure and quality of life are the modern definitions of successful cancer treatment, and preservation of continence and sexual function relates closely to treatment outcome satisfaction. Erectile tissue sparing (ETS) is not generally standard practice for PC stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT). This retrospective planning study aimed to determine if erectile tissue (ET) could be spared whilst maintaining clinically acceptable doses to target volumes (TV) and critical organs during PC SBRT.MethodsImaging datasets of 30 PC patients treated at our centres were used. Patients underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, with the creation of a substitute CT (sCT) for planning. ET structures, including penile bulb (PB), internal pudendal arteries (IPA) and neurovascular bundles (NVB), were contoured on MRI. Two 40 Gy in five fraction plans were created for each patient, with and without ETS constraints. Plans were compared using Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests.ResultsSignificant dose reduction was observed in bilateral IPAs and NVBs using ETS plans. No significant difference was seen in coverage of the clinical TVD95%, rectum V36Gy, urethra planning risk volume V42Gy, bladder V36Gy, or PB. Changes in coverage of the planning TVD95% and bladder V40Gy were deemed not clinically relevant.ConclusionResults indicate that ETS is feasible in conjunction with a planning MRI during SBRT for PC without compromise to TVs. Further prospective investigation is required to observe whether a decrease in ET dose correlates to a preservation in sexual function and/or improved treatment outcome satisfaction.Keywords: magnetic resonance imaging, organ preservation solutions, prostate cancer, quality of life, stereotactic radiation therapy

Introduction

In Australia, prostate cancer (PC) is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in males, with one in six men diagnosed by the age of 85. The 5‐year survival rates of PC are approximately 96% [1]. Both cure and quality of life are the modern definition of successful cancer treatment [2], and the preservation of continence and sexual function relates closely to treatment outcome satisfaction [2, 3, 4, 5]. New‐onset erectile dysfunction (ED) is a known complication of external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) [6]. The use of stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) to treat prostate cancer has previously demonstrated comparable sexual function outcomes [7], along with overall survival and progression‐free survival to traditional EBRT techniques [8, 9].

The sparing of erectile structures has not generally been standard practice for PC radiotherapy (RT). The penile bulb (PB) has variably been contoured as an avoidance structure in RT to the prostate; however, a definitive relationship between PB and corpus cavernosa dose and new onset ED after EBRT has not been established [6, 10, 11]. Instead, recent literature has suggested that damage to the neurovascular bundles (NVB) adjacent to the prostate is likely to have a greater contribution to ED [10, 12]. Sparing of the NVBs during radical prostatectomy has been shown to directly influence potency after surgery; however, they were previously not able to be spared during RT due to the lack of visualisation on computed tomography (CT) scans [2]. The integration of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) into RT planning has allowed the prostate and surrounding organs at risk (OAR), including the NVB, to be contoured with greater accuracy [13, 14]. NVB sparing may be complicated by their vicinity to the prostate and overlap with planning target volumes (PTV), with a 10 mm PTV expansion from the prostate associated with a 55%–80% overlap with NVBs [2].

The insertion of a rectal hydrogel spacer or endorectal balloon has been associated with increased sparing of the NVB [10] and internal pudendal arteries (IPA) [15]. A multicentre phase II randomised control trial of SBRT with a hydrogel rectal spacer, with or without neurovascular sparing for erectile function preservation in localised prostate cancer, is currently underway (the prostate oncologic therapy while ensuring neurovascular conservation (POTEN‐C)) [16]. This trial tapers the dose to the prostate from 30 Gy near a unilateral ‘spared’ NVB, whilst treating the remaining prostate minus the NVB + 5 mm volume and other erectile tissues (ET). It is hypothesised that the NVB sparing SBRT will reduce the expanded prostate cancer index composite (EPIC) score decline from a control of 20 to 10. Teunissen et al. demonstrated that NVB sparing was technically achievable bilaterally or unilaterally in 20% and 68% of their study cohort respectively [17].

TROG 18.01 Novel Integration of New Prostate Radiation Therapy schedules with adjacent Androgen deprivation (NINJA) Clinical Trial for SBRT is a randomised trial investigating two SBRT regimens in the treatment of prostate cancer [18]. ET constraints are applied for the PB, NVB and IPAs, with results awaited. Hwang et al. provided NVB planning dose constraints for SBRT to assist in creating a rapid dose fall‐off in the structures adapted from long‐course RT [10].

This retrospective planning study aims to determine if ET can be spared with SBRT without compromising the dose to the target volumes and critical OARs using the dose limits prescribed in TROG 18.01. This study also aims to determine achievable planning‐based dose constraints for erectile tissue sparing (ETS) while maintaining target volume dose to the PTV.

Methods and Materials

This study received local ethics approval from the South Western Sydney Local District Human Research Ethics Committee (2019/ETH04391).

Patient Selection

Thirty consecutive patients who underwent simulation for the TROG 18.01 trial at between June 2019 and December 2021 were selected. All patients were recruited to the MRI‐only planning substudy [18]. All included patients had unfavourable intermediate (ISUP 2 and either PSA 10–20 or T2b/c or ISUP 3 and PSA < 20 and T1/2) or low‐high risk PC (ISUP 4 and PSA < 20 and T1/2). Prostate volume was required to be < 100 cc. All patients had a hydrogel spacer and gold seeds inserted prior to undergoing their planning MRI.

Simulation

Patients underwent MRI simulation on a Siemens (Erlangen, Germany) Skyra 3 T MRI scanner on a flat top couch with body coils mounted on coil mounts. Patients were scanned head first supine with a full bladder and empty rectum. The MRI sequences captured during the simulation session are presented in Table 1.

Planning and Contouring

Contouring was previously performed on the planning MRIs by experienced Genito‐urinary (GU) specialist radiation oncologists using MiM v7.3.4 (Cleveland, Ohio, USA) and audited as part of the TROG 18.01 protocol. Planning was undertaken on the synthetic CT (sCT) scan derived from the large field of view MRI sequence as per Dowling et al. [19].

Plans were generated using two full arc 6MV volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) beams in Raystation v10B treatment planning system (RaySearch Laboratories; Stockholm, Sweden) by three experienced pelvic planning radiation therapists. Two plans were created for each patient; ETS plans included soft constraints for the PB, NVB and IPA, whilst the nonerectile tissue sparing (NETS) plans were optimised without these constraints. All planning objectives are detailed in Table 2. Target and OAR doses were based on objectives from TROG 18.01 [18]. Plans were based on the monotherapy arm, dosed to 40 Gy in five fractions to the clinical target volume (CTV) D95%, which encompassed the prostate and proximal 10 mm of the seminal vesicles (SV). 36.25 Gy was prescribed to the PTVD95% (3‐mm isotropic expansion from CTV). NVB contours abutted the prostate and partially overlapped the PTV contour (see Figure 1). Each plan was assessed by a senior pelvic radiation therapy planner and a GU radiation oncologist with extensive experience in prostate SBRT. Plans were rejected if they included four or more minor variations and/or any major variations (not including ETS constraints), outlined in Table 2.

Statistical Analysis

Results were assessed between planning techniques using Wilcoxon rank sum tests with Bonferroni correction. Analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27; New York, USA). Tests were performed on the most clinically significant target and organ at risk constraints as determined by an experienced GU radiation oncologist (Table 2). A p value of ≤ 0.0045 was deemed significant due to the Bonferroni correction.

Planning Target Volumes

Median PTV size was 59.1 cc (IQR 46.3–80.8 cc). All CTV and PTV constraints met the protocol criteria for coverage (CTV D95% > 40 Gy; PTV D95% > 36 Gy; PTV D98% > 34.2 Gy; PTV D2% < 42 Gy). No statistical difference was seen in the coverage of the CTV D95%. A statistically significant difference was seen in the PTV D95% coverage.

Organs at Risk

One ETS plan had a minor variation in the urethral maximum volume (urethra Dmax between 41 and 42 Gy). For this plan, the Urethra PRV was within the protocol range and would have been clinically accepted. All other OAR parameters were met. A statistically significant increase was seen in the bladder V40Gy in the ETS plans, equating to a median 0.03 cc increase in volume.

Penile Bulb

Regardless of technique, the median volume of PB that received above 20 Gy was 0 cc. No statistical analysis was performed on the PB as a result.

Internal Pudendal Arteries

All ETS plans reduced the maximum dose to the IPAs below 20 Gy (see Figure 2). Without ETS sparing, IPA max dose was greater than 20 Gy bilaterally in nine plans, and unilaterally in six plans. The mean IPA dose was reduced by an average of 28.4% with ETS sparing constraints. In two cases, the unilateral IPA mean was increased on the ETS sparing plan; in one case, the maximum also increased.

Internal pudendal artery (IPA) mean and maximum doses between nonerectile tissue sparing (NETS) and erectile tissue sparing (ETS) plans.

Neurovascular Bundles

NVBs overlapped the PTV by a median of 29.1% (IQR 22.3%–35.1%). A statistically significant reduction in dose to bilateral NVBs was seen at all levels, shown in Figure 3a,b. Without intentional NVB constraints, maximum NVB dose breached 40 Gy in 13 NETS plans bilaterally (43.3%) and 10 NETS plans unilaterally (33%). NVB V34.4Gy, V32Gy and V28Gy saw an average reduction of 15.7%, 14.9% and 5.2% respectively.

Neurovascular bundle (NVB) (a) volume and (b) maximum doses between nonerectile tissue sparing (NETS) and erectile tissue sparing (ETS) plans.

Conformity Index, Internal Dose Spillage, MUs

A decrease in the conformity index was seen when ETS constraints were included, while the median internal dose spillage increased. The number of MUs required per plan per fraction increased by an average of 6.4% in the ETS planning arm.

Discussion

Whilst a significant difference was seen in the PTV coverage between the ETS and NETS sparing plans, all plans met protocol guidelines without minor or major deviations, remaining clinically acceptable. Similarly, only one ETS plan breached the remaining OAR guidelines, with one plan seeing a minor variation on the Urethra maximum dose (41.16 Gy). In this case, the Urethra PRV maximum was still within the protocol limits and would have been accepted clinically at our centres. Significant reductions in dose were seen in all ETS structures, excluding the PB. This demonstrates that sparing of the ET is possible without compromising current planning guidelines.

Due to the location of the PB outside of the target area penumbra and modern planning techniques, the median volume that received above 20 Gy was 0 cc, regardless of its inclusion in the planning parameters or not. Considering this, it is no longer necessary for the PB to receive a dose during SBRT treatment of PC using external RT, and it is unlikely to be the cause of current rates of ED after EBRT. Achard et al. found that the Dmean > 4.7 Gy and D2% > 12 Gy received by the crura showed a strong correlation to the onset of ED. To date, this is the first indication of a relationship between the dose received by the crura and the occurrence of sexual dysfunction with EBRT. It has been suggested that the PB has acted as a surrogate for other structures within the area, which have a greater impact on the onset of new ED after EBRT [6, 11]. It is, therefore, feasible that the crura may be one of these structures, as the corpora cavernosa are true ETs as opposed to the corpus spongiosum, but would have been limited by visibility on CT [6]. The crura were not assessed in our study due to the limitations in adequately defining the crura on the sCT. It is important to note that the NVBs were not considered in Achard et al.'s study, which could be a confounding factor as both organs would be impacted by the conformity of the plan around the PTV. Achard also included a 5‐mm expansion of their PTV in comparison to 3‐mm in our study, which would also increase the dose of the crura. With the increasing inclusion of planning MRIs in current treatments, the crura should be considered for inclusion in further prospective work alongside other ETS structures. Based on work by Achard et al., a mean dose of < 4.7 Gy and D2% ≤ 12 Gy should be considered in future planning models for prospective evaluation.

Jaccard et al. showed that IPA sparing with or without an endorectal balloon was possible whilst maintaining target coverage [15]. Whilst our study saw a significant reduction in the Dmax and Dmean of the IPAs, these did not reach the same level. This may have been due to the higher dosing of the CTV in our study, or the prioritisation of the NVBs over the IPAs, which were not included in the former study. In two cases, ET sparing caused a 3.1%–5.7% increase in the unilateral Dmean IPA; in one of these cases, the corresponding Dmax also increased. The dose to the IPAs in these patients was already much lower than the Dmax of 20 Gy protocol (11.7 Gy and 12.4 Gy) and reduction was seen in the contralateral IPA. A maximum equivalent uniform dose of 10 Gy across each IPA was used to help shape the dose in our study. Incorporating a second objective may see further reduction and/or a more even decrease in dose across the right and left IPAs within patients. It is conceivable that further reduction of the IPA dose is possible without compromising target objectives, given their distance from the PTV. Most recently, Achard et al. did not show a significant impact on ED at 3 years when assessing the IPA Dmean (13.7 Gy vs. 14.3 Gy ED− vs. ED+ respectively) and D2% (23.6 Gy vs. 24.8 Gy ED− vs. ED+ respectively) [6]. Our study was able to spare IPA volumes to a greater degree (Dmean 10 Gy, Dmax 13.9 Gy), similar to Jaccard et al. (Dmean 10.5 Gy, Dmax 17.6 Gy) [15] and lower than Teunissen et al. (Dmean 11.9 Gy, Dmax 19.4 Gy) [17]. Previous literature has proposed a Dmax of 20 Gy to the bilateral IPAs. Our study has shown that intentional sparing to the IPAs is possible below this level. Sparing beyond this level may not be necessary as there has not been a proven association between IPA dose and ED in external beam PC SBRT, and other potential causes of ED should be prioritised.

The TROG 18.01 protocol requires a 3‐mm isotropic margin expansion from CTV to PTV, which is slightly less than some other studies in this area [9, 14, 15]. This smaller margin is likely to have decreased the overlap between the NVBs and PTV, which allows greater reduction in dose and improved dose fall‐off to the NVBs. Small CTV‐PTV margins are only feasible in these cases with the use of daily image‐guided RT, with real‐time imaging. Other possibilities for implementation would be the introduction of ETS sparing with MRI‐guided RT (MRgRT), with or without daily adaptive planning [21].

Our study saw a reduction in the NVB V32Gy of 14.88%, similar to that of Hwang et al. who saw a 16% reduction when using both NVB sparing plans and a hydrogel spacer [10]. At V34.4Gy, our reduction in volume was lower (15.67% vs. > 25%). While the PTV in our study was dosed to 36.25 Gy, which was the same as Hwang et al. based on RTOG 0938, our CTV was dosed to 40 Gy, which likely accounts for the lower reduction at higher doses. Definitive maximum doses to the NVBs have not been published, with suggested maximum doses to the NVBs proposed at 32.8 Gy based on literature and experiences with sacral and brachial plexus radiation toxicity [17]. Given the overlap between NVB and PTV contours, these targets would be virtually impossible to meet at this prescription without PTV compromise. This study treated the NVBs as soft constraints, not allowing compromise to the target volumes. Teunissen et al. suggested that unilateral, and in some cases, bilateral sparing of the NVBs to a significantly lower dose than this study, based on the location of the gross tumour volume [17] is feasible. Our study showed that NVB overlap with the PTV varied patient to patient (5.4%–55.9%), and was not uniform bilaterally with an overlap difference between the right and left NVBs in a single patient to be up to 27.6%. Variation in dose sparing was noticed across patients, likely due somewhat to the differing overlap volumes. The clinical implications of reducing the PTV size to exclude the NVBs have been hypothesised not to impact overall or disease‐free survival in that patient cohort. The POTEN‐C trial is currently underway to investigate this area; however, it only incorporates patients with low/intermediate risk PC, without androgen deprivation therapy. To our knowledge, long‐term data are yet to be released to confirm this hypothesis, as well as provide evidence that this sparing is enough to reduce ED in these patients. For higher risk patients, utilising smaller PTV margins to reduce NVB‐PTV overlap, multilevel NVB sparing constraints, and incorporating real‐time IGRT such as seed tracking software [22] or daily adaptive treatments using MRgRT [21] could be used to safely deliver treatment and reduce irradiation of ET structures. Employing multiple planning and treatment strategies has the potential to reduce ED caused by radiation damage to the NVBs, whilst still maintaining target coverage to GTVs located closer to the NVBs. Further study is required to refine dose constraints and priorities for ETS.

This study was a retrospective study conducted at a single site. Whilst statistically significant dose reductions were seen in both IPA and NVB structures, it is feasible that these could have been decreased beyond the suggested planning constraints. Limitations in staff availability, as well as a transition in our treatment planning system during the study, prevented more in‐depth investigations to determine maximum dose reductions possible without CTV/PTV compromise. Due to the transition in planning systems prior to the release of Achard's study in late 2023 [6], we were unable to include the crura as a planning structure as only the sCTs were transferred to the new planning system. The size of the prostate was limited to 100 cc based on TROG 18.01 inclusion criteria; whether the same sparing could be seen with larger target volumes has not been evaluated. Regardless, these constraints provide a starting point to begin implementation of ETS into standard practice, and to begin to assess the clinical impact of these changes on these patients’ QOL.

Conclusion

Sparing of critical tissue associated with erectile function is feasible during prostate SBRT without compromising established target and organ at risk volumes. Further prospective study is required to determine if these measures lead to improved patient outcomes.
