HSP90 inhibition partially rescues alternative splicing dysregulation in cell models of myotonic dystrophy
Authors: Jing Zhang, Amy Mascorro, Humayra Oishi, Asmar Aliyeva, Sharon Shaughnessy, Jia Sheng, Kaalak Reddy, John Douglas Cleary, J. Andrew Berglund
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) and type 2 (DM2) are multisystemic diseases caused by the expression of toxic expansion RNAs that sequester muscleblind-like (MBNL) proteins, resulting in extensive alternative splicing dysregulation. Given that there are no current disease-modifying treatments for DM, we sought to identify compounds that rescue the underlying splicing dysregulation. A medium throughput splicing screen utilizing DM1 patient-derived fibroblasts was developed and used to screen 1584 compounds from the NIH NCI Diversity Set VI, leading to the identification of macbecin II, an HSP90 inhibitor. Macbecin II-mediated HSP90 inhibition corrects several MBNL-regulated splicing events in DM1 myotubes, and a structurally distinct HSP90 inhibitor, CCT018159, produces similar effects. Using RT-PCR splicing analysis, siRNA knockdown, RT-qPCR, immunoblotting, and RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization we examined the effects of HSP90 inhibition in DM models. HSP90 inhibition increases MBNL1 and MBNL2 transcript levels, increases MBNL2 protein, and reduces toxic CUG RNA and nuclear foci in DM1 cell models. An analysis of individual HSP90 isoforms reveals that knocking down HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, and TRAP1 partially improves splicing defects, whereas HSP90B1 knockdown exacerbates mis-splicing. We also show that treatment with HSP90 inhibitors corrects mis-splicing in DM2 myotubes. Together, these data identify HSP90 as a modifier of RNA toxicity and alternative splicing in DM and support further evaluation of HSP90-directed therapeutic strategies.

Microsatellite expansion disorders comprise over 70 neurological and neuromuscular diseases, including Huntington disease (HD), spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs), and the most common cause of adult-onset muscular dystrophy, myotonic dystrophy (DM) (1). DM is categorized into two genetically distinct but clinically similar forms: DM type 1 (DM1), caused by expanded CTG repeats in the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of the DMPK gene (2, 3), and DM type 2 (DM2), caused by expanded CCTG repeats within intron 1 of the CNBP gene (4). Both subtypes manifest overlapping multisystemic clinical features, including but not limited to progressive muscle weakness, myotonia, cardiac conduction disturbances, cognitive impairment, and insulin resistance (5). This clinical complexity coupled to pronounced disease heterogeneity has hampered disease diagnoses and therapeutic development (6).

The pathogenic hallmark of DM is the toxic RNA-mediated gain-of-function mechanism, wherein expanded repeat RNAs sequester the muscleblind-like (MBNL) family of proteins into ribonuclear foci (7). Given that MBNL proteins are crucial regulators of alternative splicing, their sequestration results in global splicing dysregulation, with many individual splicing events tied to specific disease symptoms (8). For example, aberrant inclusion of chloride channel 1 (CLCN1) exon 7a results in a premature stop codon, nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, and reduced CLCN1 protein, which leads to DM’s hallmark myotonia (9). Neutralizing these toxic expanded repeat RNAs has been a therapeutic focus with various moieties including antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), ribozymes, siRNAs, peptides, and small molecules designed to liberate sequestered MBNL proteins or degrade the pathogenic transcripts (10). Several different ASO approaches are currently being evaluated in advanced clinical trials but face challenges with systemic delivery (11). Despite promising outcomes, clinical translation across the different moieties remains challenging due to limitations in systemic delivery, efficacy and safety.

Small molecules offer distinct advantages as therapeutic agents, including oral bioavailability and broad tissue distribution, lower production costs, and rapid medicinal chemistry optimization and repurposing (12). Multiple groups, including our own, have developed small molecule screening platforms based on critical disease hallmarks, some of these are ratiometric expression of expanded versus non-expanded CUG RNAs (13), MBNL levels (14), release of MBNL from CUG repeats (15), small molecules binding to CUG repeats (16, 17) and RNA foci (18, 19). These approaches have established screening as a productive strategy for identifying compounds that modulate RNA toxicity in DM.

In the present study, we develop a medium-throughput splicing screen using patient-derived DM1 fibroblasts. Using this system in a proof-of-concept screen of the NIH NCI Diversity VI, we identify macbecin II as an HSP90 inhibitor that improves mis-splicing. HSP90 proteins are a conserved family of molecular chaperones essential for protein folding, stabilization, and degradation (20, 21) and the HSP90 pathway was recently identified as a modifier of RNA foci in DM1 (18). However, whether HSP90 modulation can correct the spliceopathy that underlies much of DM pathogenesis has not been established clearly. Here we show that macbecin II and a structurally distinct HSP90 inhibitor, CCT018159, both correct splicing defects in DM1 myotubes, suggesting that the effect is related more broadly to HSP90 inhibition. Knockdown of three HSP90 isoforms (90AA1, 90AB1 and TRAP1) partially rescued DM1 splicing events while knockdown of endoplasmic reticulum-specific HSP90B1 worsened splicing. Finally, we extend these findings to DM2 myotubes, supporting HSP90 modulation as a reproducible pathway linked to splicing rescue in myotonic dystrophy.

Results

Development of a screening platform using a DM1 patient cell line for splicing rescue

We previously characterized multiple DM1 patient-derived fibroblast cell lines for use in limited small molecule screens, including cataloging shared and unique aberrant splicing events (22). These cell lines were characterized for splicing rescue using furamidine and other diamidine derivatives (22). To leverage the screening potential of these cell lines, here we developed a robust, splicing-sensitive, medium-throughput screening assay. To aid interpretation of the screening approach, a schematic overview is shown in Figure S1A. As proof-of-concept, DM1 fibroblasts were treated with the structurally diverse NCI Diversity Set VI (comprising 1584 compounds) obtained from the National Cancer Institute Developmental Therapeutics Program (NCI DTP) (23). Following treatment, cells were permeabilized, and RNA from treated cells was directly evaluated using RT-PCR for changes in alternative splicing of INSR exon 11. For each of the 20 screening plates, each compound was assayed at 1 μm in quadruplicate with quercetin (64 μM), which has been shown to rescue splicing (24), serving as a positive control, and DMSO only (0.1%) serving as a negative control. Representative RT-PCR results for control, DM1, and quercetin-treated samples illustrate the dynamic range of the assay (Fig. S1, B and C).

Analysis of the treated cells revealed a robust range of INSR exon 11 splicing response, with compounds both rescuing and exacerbating splicing (Fig. 1A). To normalize values across plates, the change in percent spliced in (ΔPSI) of INSR exon 11 was calculated by subtracting the average PSI of DMSO-only treated DM1 samples from the PSI of compound-treated samples. The mean ΔPSI for DMSO-treated DM1 cells remained at zero, while the positive control quercetin consistently demonstrated a mean ΔPSI rescue of 25 ± 5 across 80 replicates (Fig. 1A). We set the screening threshold for the 1584 Diversity Set VI compounds at a ΔPSI >10 (Fig. 1A), which is consistent with previously reported criteria for splicing modulation in myotonic dystrophy models (25). To validate our primary hits, we performed dose-response assays by treating the DM1 fibroblasts with each compound in a 10-fold serial dilution (10 nM to 100 μM) and assessed splicing correction via RT-PCR analysis for INSR exon 11 as previously described (22). All but two primary hit compounds (CAS131-22-6 & NSC 99756) demonstrated a dose-dependent response (Fig. S2, A–G). In particular, the HSP90 inhibitor macbecin II displayed the best splicing correction after 24 h treatment, with 100% splicing rescue at 1 μm (Fig. 1C). We further investigated macbecin II by assessing its effects on cellular viability using the PrestoBlue assay, which measures metabolic activity as a readout of viable cells (26). Notably, macbecin II showed negligible cytotoxic effects, maintaining cellular viability within the concentration range in which splicing rescue is observed (Fig. 1D). Macbecin II, a macrocyclic ansamycin antibiotic, acts by inhibiting HSP90 ATPase activity through binding to its N-terminal domain (27), with pathway suppression reported at concentrations as low as 1 μm (28). Given that the HSP90 pathway has been previously linked to DM1 (18), the identification of macbecin II provides biological validation of our proof-of-concept screen. This previously reported activity and macbecin II’s ability to rescue splicing at non-cytotoxic concentrations highlights HSP90 inhibition for further investigation.

Macbecin II rescues splicing in DM1 patient-derived myotubes

To further validate our lead hit, we extended our analysis into DM1 patient-derived myotubes. Primary myoblasts derived from a DM1 patient were differentiated into mature myotubes as previously described (13), then treated with macbecin II and assessed for splicing response. We observed a dose-dependent splicing response to macbecin II in DM1 myotubes across four well-established DM1-regulated splicing events: MBNL2 exon 5, ATP2A1 exon 22, NUMA1 exon 16, and SYNE1 exon 137 (Fig. 2). This response was observed across a narrow concentration range (0.1–7 μM, 48 h), with maximal correction at 7 μM (Fig. 2). To assess if macbecin II treatment was dependent on the presence of the CTG repeat expansion, we analyzed splicing response in control myotubes across a similar range of macbecin II concentrations (2–7 μM). Treatment of unaffected cells resulted in modest splicing changes (<10% ΔPSI) at 4 to 7 μM across MBNL1 exon5, MBNL2 exon 5, NUMA1 exon 16, and SYNE1 exon 137 (Fig. S3, A–D). These changes were limited to known MBNL-sensitive targets, and macbecin II did not affect splicing of CAMKK2 exon 15 or VTI1 exon 5, which are not regulated by MBNL (29) in DM1 myotubes (Fig. S3, E and F). That the effects in control myotubes were less pronounced than those observed in DM1 myotubes suggests that HSP90 inhibition has a greater impact on splicing in the presence of an expanded CTG repeat.

HSP90 inhibition by macbecin II modulates DMPK and MBNL transcript levels in DM1 myotubes

To determine if the splicing rescue by macbecin II is associated with DMPK expression changes, we measured DMPK transcript levels. Treatment of differentiated DM1 patient derived myotubes with macbecin II resulted in increased total DMPK transcript levels (Fig. 3A), which aligns with previous findings by other HSP90 inhibitors (18). Interestingly DMPK expression also increased in unaffected myotubes treated with macbecin II (Fig. 3B). To investigate this effect further, we examined macbecin II treatment in the DM1 HeLa screening cell line (13), which stably expresses human DMPK exons 11 to 15 containing either r(CUG)480 or r(CUG)0 repeats. In these cells, treatment with macbecin II significantly reduced the abundance of the expanded r(CUG)480 transcript, while increasing endogenous DMPK mRNA levels (Fig. 3C). A similar increase in endogenous DMPK expression was also detected in the parental HeLa line (Fig. 3D), which lacks the r(CUG)480 or r(CUG)0 repeats. Given that MBNL proteins play a central role in DM1 pathogenesis, the effects of macbecin II on MBNL1 and MBNL2 expression were also examined in DM1 patient-derived myotubes. Following macbecin II treatment, RT-qPCR analysis showed a significant increase in MBNL1 and MBNL2 transcript levels (Fig. 3E). Parallel immunoblot analysis revealed a non-significant trend towards increased levels of MBNL1 and significantly increased MBNL2 protein levels (Figs. 3F and S4–S5). This selective enhancement of MBNL2 suggests that HSP90 inhibition may contribute to splicing rescue via preferentially influencing MBNL1 and MBNL2 protein expression or stability, consistent with prior studies demonstrating HSP90’s role in regulating MBNL proteins in DM1 (18).

To better understand the HSP90 pathway in the context of DM1 splicing, we examined expression, via RT-qPCR, of the four HSP90 isoforms: HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, HSP90B1, and TRAP1 in patient-derived myotubes. Both HSP90AA1 and HSP90B1 were modestly downregulated in DM1 myotubes compared to unaffected myotubes, whereas HSP90AB1 and TRAP1 levels remained stable (Fig. S6, A–D). Next, we examined the isoform-specific response following macbecin II treatment in DM1 myotubes and observed a dose-dependent upregulation of HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, and HSP90B1, with TRAP1 levels remaining unchanged (Fig. 3G). A similar upregulation was also observed in macbecin II-treated unaffected myotubes, except TRAP1, which showed strong upregulation (Fig. S7, A–D). To determine whether this response was also reflected at the protein level, we measured pan-HSP90 protein in DM1 myotubes after macbecin II treatment. Macbecin II increased pan-HSP90 protein relative to DMSO-treated cells (Fig. S8, A–C). These data, which align with previous results for the macbecin II treatment of the prostate cancer cell line DU145 (28), suggest that macbecin II treatment results in compensatory HSP90 isoform upregulation in myotubes.

To explore whether variability in HSP90 isoform expression correlates with the extent of splicing rescue after macbecin II treatment, we performed correlation analyses across multiple DM1-relevant skipped exon events. We examined isoform correlation with both inclusion (MBNL1 exon 5, MBNL2 exon 5) and exclusion (NUMA1 exon 16, ATP2A1 exon 22, SYNE1 exon 137, and CLASP1 exon 19) events. Across biological replicates, modest but reproducible correlations were observed between HSP90 isoform mRNA levels and the degree of splicing correction, with stronger trends for exon exclusion events (Fig. S9). Notably, positive correlations were detected between HSP90AA1 and CLASP1 exon 19 inclusion (p = 0.0042), HSP90AB1 and ATP2A1 exon 22 (p = 0.0061), and HSP90B1 and SYNE1 exon 137 (p = 0.0002). Although these analyses were based on transcript rather than protein levels and do not imply direct regulation, the observed associations suggest that differential HSP90 isoform expression may reflect cellular states that influence MBNL-dependent splicing responsiveness to HSP90 inhibition.

Knockdown of individual HSP90 isoforms alters splicing and MBNL expression in DM1 cells

To directly test the role of individual HSP90 isoforms, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdowns of each isoform in DM1 myoblasts and myotubes. Using a range of siRNA (1-50 nM) against each HSP90 isoform, we treated DM1 myoblasts for 72 h and measured expression of the respective HSP90 isoform. We determined that 20 nM was sufficient to achieve significant knockdown of each isoform without obvious effects on cell health (Fig. S10, A–D). Similar to findings with macbecin II, knockdown of each HSP90 isoform resulted in the compensatory upregulation in the other isoforms (Fig. 4A). To understand the effect of knocking down each HSP90 isoform on splicing dysregulation, we examined a panel of MBNL-regulated exons, including MBNL1 exon 5, MBNL2 exon 5, SYNE1 exon 137, and NUMA1 exon 16 in myoblasts. Interestingly, while knockdown of HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, or TRAP1 modestly improved splicing across all events (Fig. S10, E and H), HSP90B1 knockdown worsened splicing dysregulation for all events but SYNE1 exon 137. These contrasting effects on splicing were also observed in DM1 myotubes, although in the differentiated cells, HSP90B1 silencing did not affect splicing of SYNE1 exon137 and NUMA1 exon16 (Figs. 4B and S11, A and B). Together, these data indicate that knockdown of individual HSP90 isoforms at the mRNA level exerts distinct effects on MBNL-regulated splicing, potentially reflecting HSP90 isoform-specific roles in DM1 cells.

To dissect the contribution of individual HSP90 isoforms to MBNL regulation, we examined MBNL1 and MBNL2 expression in DM1 patient-derived myotubes in which each HSP90 isoform was knocked down (Fig. 4, C and D). Analysis of the MBNL1 and MBNL2 transcript levels by qRT-PCR revealed that siHSP90B1 treatment led to a significant albeit minor reduction in MBNL1 mRNA (Fig. 4C), whereas knockdown of all HSP90 isoforms resulted in a significant decrease in MBNL2 transcript levels (Fig. 4C). When evaluating MBNL proteins, knockdown of HSP90AA1 and HSP90AB1 led to elevated MBNL1 levels, while knockdown of HSP90B1 and TRAP1 showed no effect (Figs. 4D, S12 and S13). MBNL2 protein levels were not significantly altered by knockdown of HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, or HSP90B1, whereas siTRAP1 resulted in a modest decrease (Fig. 4D), despite the reduction in mRNA. These findings suggest that MBNL1 upregulation contributes, at least in part, to the observed splicing rescue upon HSP90AA1 and HSP90AB1 knockdown. However, TRAP1 depletion improved splicing without a corresponding increase in MBNL1, indicating that additional mechanisms may be involved. Interestingly, HSP90B1 knockdown, which significantly worsened splicing (Figs. 4B and S11, A and B) reduced both MBNL1 and MBNL2 transcripts but not their respective proteins (Fig. 4D). Taken together, these findings support a role for HSP90 inhibition in modulating MBNL1 and MBNL2 protein levels, which contributes to rescuing splicing dysregulation in DM1.

A structurally distinct HSP90 inhibitor also rescues splicing in a DM1 cell model

To confirm that our observed splicing correction was attributable to general HSP90 inhibition rather than unique to macbecin II, we tested a structurally distinct HSP90 inhibitor, CCT018159. Treatment of DM1 myotubes with CCT018159 significantly rescued multiple DM1 associated splicing events, albeit to a lesser extent than macbecin II (Fig. 5A), whereas treatment of unaffected myotubes did not show alteration in SYNE1 exon 137, and NUMA1 exon 16 splicing events (Fig. S14, A and B). Similar to macbecin II, CCT018159 also increased total DMPK transcript levels in DM1 myotubes (Fig. 5B), reduced expression of the toxic expanded r(CUG)480 RNA in DM1 HeLa cells (Fig. 5C), and elevated endogenous DMPK expression levels (Fig. 5C). Additionally, MBNL2, but not MBNL1, transcript levels were increased following CCT018159 treatment (Fig. 5D), paralleling the selective upregulation observed with macbecin II (Fig. 3, E and F). While these results suggest a general HSP90 inhibition effect, we also tested if macbecin II directly contributed to splicing regulation by interacting with expanded CUG repeat RNA. We performed isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) using the duplex-forming RNA sequence r(CUG)8 (32) and observed only weak, nonspecific binding (Fig. S15, A–C). HSP90 inhibition modulates RNA foci levels in DM1 cell models

Since HSP90 inhibition has been recently reported to modulate foci in DM1 cells (18), we next examined RNA foci following macbecin II treatment in both DM1 HeLa cells and differentiated DM1 myotubes. Following fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis using a Cy3-(CAG)8 probe, we observed that treatment of DM1 HeLa cells with either macbecin II or CCT018159 markedly reduced the number of nuclear RNA foci compared with DMSO controls (Fig. 6). We observed both a significant decrease in the mean number of foci per nucleus, the proportion of foci-positive nuclei, and the percentage of nuclei containing more than two foci (Fig. 6A). A similar trend was observed in DM1 patient-derived myotubes, where macbecin II treatment significantly reduced RNA foci counts, while CCT018159 treatment showed a more variable reduction (Fig. 6B). Discussion

DM1 is caused by a toxic CUG-repeat RNA that sequesters MBNL proteins and disrupts normal splicing. To identify compounds that correct mis-splicing, we developed a medium throughput screening platform using DM1 patient-derived fibroblasts. This splicing-based platform provides a new tool for the field and in proof-of-concept studies we identify macbecin II, an HSP90 inhibitor, as a small molecule that rescues mis-splicing in DM1 cells. HSP90 inhibitors have recently been shown to modulate DMPK transcript levels and ribonuclear foci in DM1 models (18) thus validating the utility of our screening platform. However, this previous study did not report the effects of HSP90 inhibitors on splicing. Here we demonstrate splicing improvements with two structurally distinct HSP90 inhibitors (macbecin II and CCT018159) and using isoform-specific HSP90 knockdowns reveal that HSP90 plays a previously unrecognized role in modulating the spliceopathy in DM1. Macbecin II also produces modest changes in MBNL-sensitive splicing events in unaffected myotubes, whereas another HSP90 inhibitor has no effect in the same cell line. Given that MBNL independent splicing events were not affected in DM1 myotubes suggests that rather than a broad, nonspecific effect on splicing, macbecin II effects are limited to MBNL-regulated pathways. Notably, our results suggest that upregulation of both MBNL1 and MBNL2 contributes to the observed splicing rescue. We also found that HSP90 inhibition also shows partial splicing rescue in DM2 myotubes, indicating that this pathway may have future applicability across both types of DM. One limitation of the current study is that maximal splicing correction in myotubes required micromolar concentrations of HSP90 inhibitors. Our macbecin II and CCT018159 finding highlight HSP90 modulation as a modifier of DM spliceopathy, rather than as therapeutic leads. Future medicinal chemistry optimization will be required to improve these compounds’ potency and ultimately determine their therapeutic potential.

Johnson and colleagues recently identified HSP90 as a novel mediator of ribonuclear foci levels in DM1 cells (18). Their unbiased small molecule screen identified HSP90 inhibitors (AT13387 & 17-AAG) that led to elevated RNA foci and DMPK RNA levels in undifferentiated DM1 myoblasts. Downregulation of HSP90 upon differentiation was found to be potentially mediated through phospho-STAT3 activity which was previously implicated in a skeletal muscle mouse model of DM1 (33). That two screening platforms based on different outcomes (splicing herein & foci in Johnson et al. (18)) identified HSP90, reinforce this pathway’s role in DM. Here we complement the previous work by demonstrating that HSP90 modulation can also rescue mis-splicing, another pathogenic hallmark of DM. Interestingly in both studies, differential effects were observed between cell type and differentiation state. In Johnson et al., HSP90 inhibition in undifferentiated DM1 cells led to upregulation of DMPK RNA and RNA foci, whereas the opposite was observed in differentiated cells (18). In our study, HSP90 inhibition reduces toxic CUG RNA expression in our engineered HeLa DM1 model yet increases DMPK transcript levels in parental HeLa cells, unaffected and DM1 patient-derived myotubes. The differential effects of HSP90 inhibition on expanded and nonexpanded DMPK transcripts may reflect distinct RNA contexts. Expanded CUG RNA is retained in nuclear foci and assembled into an abnormal ribonucleoprotein complex containing MBNL proteins and other RNA-binding factors. This repeat-containing RNA may therefore respond differently to HSP90-dependent stress-response or chaperone pathways than endogenous nonexpanded DMPK. In contrast, nonexpanded DMPK may be affected through changes in transcription, RNA stability, or broader cellular stress signaling. Given that our data do not distinguish between these possibilities, future studies will be needed to define how HSP90 inhibition differentially regulates expanded and nonexpanded DMPK RNA.

We observed an interesting effect in that HSP90B1 expression is consistently upregulated in conditions when splicing rescue occurs, including macbecin II treatment and siRNA-mediated knockdown of HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, or TRAP1. In contrast, the knockdown of HSP90B1 significantly worsens splicing defects, both in DM1 myoblasts and myotubes. These findings suggest that HSP90B1 may contribute to a compensatory response that supports splicing correction, although the present data do not establish HSP90B1 as the sole mediator of this effect. Consistent with a broader compensatory response, macbecin II treatment also increased pan-HSP90 protein levels. However, because the pan-HSP90 immunoblot does not resolve individual HSP90 paralogs, these data do not define which isoform, or combination of isoforms, is required for splicing rescue. In contrast to HSP90B1, knockdown of HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, or TRAP1 partially improved splicing, suggesting that different HSP90 paralogs have non-equivalent roles in DM1 cells. The effects observed with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) localized HSP90B1 may be related to ER stress pathways, which have been previously implicated in DM1 muscle pathology (34). Taken together, these data support a model in which HSP90 inhibition engages a broader chaperone-network response that influences MBNL availability and splice regulation, rather than acting through a simple increase or decrease in a single HSP90 isoform.

We also observed that HSP90 inhibition rescues splicing defects in DM2 cells, which involve a CCTG repeat expansion in the CNBP gene. Similar to results in DM1 cells, macbecin II treatment significantly increases MBNL1 & 2 RNA levels and MBNL2 protein levels in DM2 myotubes. The consistent upregulation of MBNL2 following macbecin II treatment across both DM1 and DM2 models suggests that MBNL2 may contribute at least in part to the observed splicing rescue. While the precise mechanisms at play require further investigation, these findings raise the possibility that targeting the HSP90 pathway could benefit both DM1 and DM2 by enhancing MBNL2 function and partially rescuing splicing regulation in disease-affected cells. HSP90 modulation has also been connected to splicing outside the context of DM, as the HSP90 inhibitor onalespib alters the splicing of over 500 genes in prostate cancer cells by increasing intron retention and reducing exon skipping (35). Additionally, heat stress has been shown to influence splicing in spinal muscular atrophy through upregulation of the splicing enhancer TRA2β (36). This broader role in splicing may be related to the proposed role of HSP90 in coordinating RNA polymerase II with the spliceosome machinery (37). Taken together, these findings highlight that HSP90 modulation is a reproducible pathway linked to splicing rescue in DM and potentially other diseases, although additional medicinal chemistry optimization will be required to fully understand the therapeutic potential of HSP90 inhibitors.

In conclusion, our study expands the role for HSP90 in DM to regulating alternative splicing, adding to recent findings related to DMPK transcript and RNA foci levels (18). By combining pharmacologic inhibition and isoform-specific knockdown, we show that splicing rescue is associated with compensatory upregulation of HSP90 isoforms, highlighting that the cellular response to HSP90 inhibition actively contributes to splicing rescue. Taken together with previous findings, our work supports HSP90 inhibition increasing MBNL abundance, partially correcting MBNL-regulated splicing, and reducing toxic CUG RNA and nuclear foci (Fig. 8). Experimental procedures

Cell culture and treatment of patient-derived cells

Primary DM1 and unaffected fibroblast lines were obtained from either Coriell Institute (GM08400-unaffected & GM4601-DM1) or the Center for Inherited Myology Research Biorepository (DM2) under VCU IRB_HM300000791. Cells were plated at ∼1 × 104 per well in 96-well plates and cultured to confluence in DMEM with 15% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Once confluent, medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 0.15% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and treated with either structurally diverse compounds in DMSO or DMSO as a control. Cells were treated as described below for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 then analyzed for toxicity and splicing rescue. Primary patient-derived and control myoblasts were obtained from the University of Florida (DM04 & DM05) and Virginia Commonwealth University (Cntrl2 and Myo3) and were derived from muscle biopsies collected under UF or VCU approved IRB protocols. Myoblasts were cultured at ∼1 × 104 cells per well in SkGM-2 BulletKit (Lonza) until >90% confluency. Differentiation to myotubes was induced for 4 days in DMEM/F-12 50/50 (Corning) with 2% donor equine serum (HyClone). On day 4, differentiation medium was replaced with SkGM-2, and cells were treated as indicated.

Diversity set VI library screen

The NCI Diversity Set VI, comprising 1584 compounds, was obtained from the Developmental Therapeutic Program (DTP) of the National Cancer Institute (NCI). Fibroblast cells were grown to confluency as described above, then the next day, medium was replaced with fresh medium containing either 1 μm of library compound in DMSO, 64 μM quercetin as a positive control, or DMSO (0.1%) as a negative control. After ∼24 h of treatment, cells were washed with PBS, then frozen at −80 °C for processing. For processing, plates were thawed, and each well incubated with 0.25% Igepal buffer (10 mM Tris·HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl) followed by gentle lysis on an orbital shaker for 5 min at room temperature. The resulting lysates were used directly for downstream analysis.

Cell viability analysis

Cell viability was assessed using the PrestoBlue HS assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Treated cells were washed with PBS, incubated with a 10:1 mixture of fresh medium and PrestoBlue reagent for 1.5 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2, then fluorescence measured at 560/590 nm (excitation/emission) on a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek) and viability calculated as per manufacturer’s instructions.

siRNA treatment of primary myoblasts and myotubes

Primary DM1 myoblasts were cultured in SkGM-2 (Lonza) to ∼80% confluence. siRNAs were delivered using Polyplus INTERFERin transfection reagent (Polyplus-transfection) in Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Media (Gibco), at 20 nM final concentration. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with SkGM-2 and cells were cultured for 48 h. After 72 h total, cells were washed with 1 × PBS (Corning) and frozen at −80 °C. For myotubes, myoblasts were first differentiated for 7 days and then treated with siRNAs as described.

siRNAs used:

ON-TARGETplus Human HSP90AA1 siRNA (L-005186-00-0005).

ON-TARGETplus Human HSP90AB1 siRNA (L-005187-00-0005).

ON-TARGETplus Human HSP90B1 siRNA (L-006417-00-0005).

ON-TARGETplus Human TRAP1 siRNA (L-010104-00-0005) (All from Dharmacon/Horizon Discovery).

RT-PCR splicing analysis

RNA was isolated using the NucleoSpin 96 RNA kit (Macherey-Nagel) with on-column DNase I digestion per manufacturer protocol. RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). Reverse transcription was performed using SuperScript IV (Invitrogen) with random hexamers (IDT). PCR (32 cycles) was done using primer sets listed in Table S1. PCR products were analyzed on the Fragment Analyzer using the 1 to 500 bp DNF-905 kit (Advanced Analytical). Peak quantification was done with ProSize 2.0 software. Rescue of exon skipping was calculated as:%Rescue=[(PSI_treated−PSI_untreated)/(PSI_non−DM–PSI_untreated)]×100

RT-qPCR expression analysis

qPCR was performed using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) on a CFX96/384 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Expression of MBNL1, MBNL2, HSP90, and DMPK was normalized to GAPDH. Data were expressed as relative mRNA levels (ΔΔCT), setting untreated levels to 1. Toxic CUG repeat levels were quantified using Hot Start Taq 2 × Master Mix (New England Biolabs), primers HT_Forward and HT_Reverse (IDT), and custom fluorescent probes HT_Probe1 and HT_Probe2 (IDT), as detailed in Table S2 and S3.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) microscopy

Myoblasts and HeLa cells (∼5 × 104) were seeded into 8-chamber slides and treated as described. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), permeabilized in 70% ethanol, pre-hybridized at 37 °C, and hybridized overnight at 50 °C with a Cy3-(CAG)8 probe (IDT). After washing with 42 °C pre-warmed 40% formamide in 2 × SSC, samples were mounted using ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Life Technologies). Imaging was performed on a Zeiss Axioscope 5. Foci per nucleus were quantified using CellProfiler. Relative foci counts were normalized to untreated controls.

Western blot analysis

Myoblasts (∼2 × 105) were plated in 6-well plates and treated as described. Protein was extracted using RIPA buffer with 1 mm PMSF and 1 × SIGMAFAST protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein concentration was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher). Ten micrograms of protein were denatured, separated on 4 to 20% SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad), and transferred to low-fluorescence PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad). Blots were blocked with Intercept (PBS) Blocking Buffer (LI-COR), incubated overnight with: MBNL1 (MABE70, Millipore Sigma, 1:500); MBNL2 (3B4, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 1:200); or HSP90 (MBH90AB, Invitrogen, 1:1000); followed by incubation with one of the following secondary antibodies; Donkey anti-mouse IRDye 680 (LI-COR, 1:7500); or Goat anti-rabbit IRDye 800 (LI-COR, 1:7500). Blots were imaged using ChemiDoc (Bio-Rad). Membranes were subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue, and protein signals were normalized to total protein loading. Analysis was performed using Image Lab software, and values were expressed relative to untreated control.
