Superhydrophobic Array Devices for the Enhanced Formation of 3D Cancer Models
Authors: Maria Lopez-Cavestany, Olivia A. Wright, Noah T. Reckhorn, Alexandria T. Carter, Kalana Jayawardana, Tin Nguyen, Dayrl P. Briggs, Dmitry S. Koktysh, Alberto Esteban Linares, Deyu Li, Michael R. King
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
During the metastatic cascade, cancer cells travel through the bloodstream as circulating tumor cells (CTCs) to a secondary site. Clustered CTCs have greater shear stress and treatment resistance, yet their biology remains poorly understood. We therefore engineered a tunable superhydrophobic array device (SHArD). The SHArD-C was applied to culture a clinically relevant model of CTC clusters. Using our device, we cultured a model of cancer cell aggregates of various sizes with immortalized cancer cell lines. These exhibited higher E-cadherin expression and are significantly more capable of surviving high fluid shear stress-related forces compared to single cells and model clusters grown using the control method, helping to explain why clustering may provide a metastatic advantage. Additionally, the SHArD-S, when compared with the AggreWell 800 method, provides a more consistent spheroid-forming device culturing reproducible sizes of spheroids for multiple cancer cell lines. Overall, we designed, fabricated, and validated an easily tunable engineered device which grows physiologically relevant three-dimensional (3D) cancer models containing tens to thousands of cells.

Introduction

Cancer experimentation is typically performed using two-dimensional (2D) tissue culture models where the cells are grown in a monolayer.1 These 2D models are easy to use, cost-effective, uniform, and allow for cells to be readily harvested for downstream testing. Despite these advantages, the 2D approach has major drawbacks given that cell–cell interactions are greatly reduced, which minimizes the physiological relevance. Spheroids are lab-grown three-dimensional (3D) cancer models that contain on the order of thousands of cells and are useful in gaining a better understanding of the primary tumor microenvironment, which are not necessarily spherical in shape and may also grow as ellipsoids and other rounded volumes. They offer increased physiological relevance compared to the 2D models via increased cell-to-cell signaling and diffusion of nutrients through the organized layers of cells.2 A previous study in the King Lab on the efficacy of combination therapies in prostate cancer found that cells grown in 2D were more sensitive to the treatments than those in 3D models.3 Other studies additionally confirm that cells grown in 3D exhibit greater chemoresistance, radioresistance, morphological similarities, and cancer stem cell-like populations than those in 2D, which are closer to what is observed in vivo.4−7 These results highlight the importance of using spheroids over cell monolayers as models to understand the primary tumor.

There are two common laboratory approaches to studying the primary tumor environment. Researchers have previously utilized primary patient blood samples to isolate circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and have cultured these cells into spheroids to gain insight into the primary tumor within the patient.8,9 While such CTC isolation and cell expansion is invaluable to the characterization of the exact tumoral network, genetic makeup, and phenotype, this method can be inconsistent from a high throughput standpoint. For the sake of high throughput experimental research, the use of immortalized cancer cell lines can provide a means to test behaviors, characterize genetic or other markers, and assess drug reactivity in a setting separate from direct clinical reliance while remaining biologically relevant to enhancing the mechanistic understanding of advanced metastatic disease. The corresponding features of primary samples and isolated cell lines can additionally be compared in a corresponding experimental setting in works such as those presented by Mitchell et al. in 2014.10

The primary tumor is not the only instance where cancer cells aggregate into 3D growths in the body. During the metastatic cascade, cancer cells travel through the bloodstream as CTCs to a secondary site.11 Less than 0.1% of these cells survive the circulation due to high shear stress exposure and interactions with immune cells.12 Cancer cells are, however, known to cluster in circulation, conferring higher shear stress and treatment resistance, in addition to increased metastatic potential compared to single cells.13−15 Also, larger CTC clusters may indicate a shorter progression-free survival in patients.16 Therefore, it is increasingly important to have physiologically similar in vitro models of these aggregated cancer cells to better understand their role throughout disease progression and design therapeutics to act against them.

When designing mammalian cell culture surfaces, special attention needs to be given to the surface wettability, as this is one of the key factors that dictates the biological-material interface.17 There is a biphasic response in cell adherence depending on surface wettability where cells easily attach and grow on hydrophobic surfaces, but not on hydrophilic or superhydrophobic ones. Surfaces designed to be hydrophobic easily adsorb extracellular matrix proteins and promote cell adhesion to the substrate and proliferation.18−21 Such surfaces have a water contact angle (WCA) over 90°. In comparison, hydrophilic (WCA < 90°) and superhydrophobic (WCA > 150°) surfaces are not adhesive to cells or bacteria.22 For the dynamic WCA, the advancing and receding water contact angles are captured by pipetting a droplet of water onto and off of the wafer surface.23 For a surface to be classified as superhydrophobic, the receding WCA must be over 90°, the advancing angle must be at least 145°, and the hysteresis must be under 5. Hysteresis is the quantification of the presence of surface defects and roughness at a microscopic level, calculated by subtracting the advancing WCA from the receding WCA. When in contact with cell culture media, most hydrophilic surfaces are not conducive to cell growth due to hydration; however interaction with the cells can occur, and some are able to adhere over time.24 In the case of superhydrophobic surfaces, both a hydrophobic coating and a nanostructured surface are needed to achieve such a high WCA.25 This combination leads to trapped air bubbles underneath a water droplet and ultralow contact area between the surface and the droplet, leading to a self-cleaning surface which repels protein adsorption and does not allow for cell adhesion.

In this study, we successfully designed, fabricated, and validated an easily tunable superhydrophobic array device that can be used to culture a wide range of 3D cancer models (Figure 1). Microwell dimensions can be changed to match the size of the desired 3D model without significantly altering the overall device fabrication protocol. Two device designs were tested as a proof of concept: one to culture spheroids and the other to grow smaller aggregates as a model for CTC clusters, accessible through the use of immortalized cancer cell lines.

Results

Fabrication and Characterization of Superhydrophobic Array Devices

Two superhydrophobic array devices (SHArD) were designed: one to culture small cell aggregates which mimic the clustered CTCs found in patient blood samples (SHArD-C) and the other to grow larger cancer spheroids (SHArD-S). Each device is subdivided into an array of square microwells, similar to the AggreWell 800. Microwell wall height and wall-to-wall distance can be chosen to match the dimensions of the desired cell aggregates or spheroids. This allows for a single device that is easily tunable and streamlines production of a wide range of 3D models using a single protocol.

Both SHArDs were fabricated using a multistep additive process as outlined in Figure 2a in the cleanrooms at the Vanderbilt Institute for Nanoscale Science and Engineering and the Oak Ridge National Laboratories Center for Nanophase Material Science. Importantly, the only step in the microfabrication protocol that is changed between the two devices is the SU-8 lithography, making the device alteration for different 3D model cultures straightforward and simple. The SHArD-C was designed to culture smaller cell aggregates with a wall-to-wall distance of 100 μm and wall depth of 75 μm (Figure S1a). This wall-to-wall distance was chosen to match expected cluster sizes. CTC aggregates in the blood range from 2 to 100 cells in size, with most averaging around 20, and the average cancer cell is about 20 μm in diameter.26 The SHArD-S microwell dimensions were chosen to match those of the AggreWell 800 plates to allow for direct comparison of spheroids between the two culture methods. The dimensions were measured by profilometry and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Microwell wall-to-wall distance was 800 μm and wall depth was 330 μm (Figure S2a). To match these, the SHArD-S was designed to have a microwell wall-to-wall distance of 800 μm and wall depth of over 300 μm (Figure S1b). Wall width is 100 μm to allow for a stable structure for the ultrathick SU-8 walls.

First, a monodispersed ZnO nanoparticle solution in Milli-Q-filtered water was prepared via sonication. Optimal sonication times were validated via dynamic light scattering. Before sonication, the ZnO nanoparticles formed aggregates with an average dynamic diameter of 340 ± 60 nm (Figure S3a). Sonication for 30–180 s broke apart the aggregates leading to more narrow size distributions and a smaller dynamic diameter. The solution with no sonication treatment had an average polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.264 ± 0.046, while sonication for 30–180 s produced PDIs less than 0.14 (Figure S3b). The shortest time to reach minimum PDI and measured dynamic diameter was 60 s. The nanoparticle suspension was spin coated onto the surface of a cleaned Si wafer and then dried at room temperature. The ZnO thin film was confirmed to be highly uniform and reproducible by SEM imaging (Figure 2b,c).

Uniform, contaminant-free, crystalline ZnO thin-films were additionally verified using X-ray diffraction (XRD). XRD data for the thin film before annealing can be observed in Figure S3c. After utilizing annealing as a structure relaxation method, the loss of a small peak intensity near 68 two-theta and increased peak intensity for multiple peaks in the annealed sample were observed (Figure S3d). Additionally, the occurrence of peak broadening in the nonannealed samples was confirmed. The loss of this small peak indicates an increase in crystallinity within a material.27 Annealing is known to relax defects within a given material, and this loss of crystallographic plane (201) yields the exclusion of an atomic plane within the crystal. Proof of defect-loss and therefore increased crystallinity within the sample is further exhibited by increasing peak intensity from nonannealed to annealed samples. Defect loss is characterized by the heightened presence of atoms detected along unit cell planes characteristic of wurtzite ZnO particles. Nanorods were grown after thin film annealing in a bath solution. SEM imaging showed a uniform nanorod layer on the wafer surface. Nanorods were confirmed to be randomly oriented, allowing for greater nanoscale roughness as seen in Figure 2d. This is important as it maximizes surface roughness: an important design parameter for superhydrophobicity. An SEM image of the side-view of the nanorod layer again displays the random orientation of the nanorods (Figure 2e). The average nanorod length was measured to be 469 ± 171 nm and average angle of the nanorods with respect to the wafer surface was 87.8 ± 38.2°.

For the SHArD-C, the wall height was measured to be about 85 μm via SEM (Figure 2f). Utilizing thinner SU-8 structures in the SHArD-C allowed for the use of Omnicoat as an adhesion promoter instead of the SU-8 base layer. This is simpler as there is no additional alignment step required. Structures were also confirmed to be uniform and reproducible via SEM (Figure 2g,h). In the design for the SHArD-S, the required microwell wall height needed to reach at least 300 μm with an aspect ratio of 3:1. To achieve adhesion of a thick SU-8 layer to the nanorods, a 2 μm SU-8 grid was deposited first. Wall-to-wall length was maintained at 800 μm, associated with a width of 200 μm to allow for ease in alignment for the ultrathick step of SU-8 deposition afterward. The SU-8 base layer can be observed in Figure 2i with the black arrow. Ultrathick SU-8 lithography was successfully performed via a two-step SU-8 spin coating protocol. The wall height was measured to be 375 μm via SEM imaging. Additionally, no tapering was observed in the structure (Figure 2j). SEM imaging also revealed uniform walls across the wafer surface (Figure 2k). This method was highly reproducible and produced stable structures that adhered long-term to the wafer surface.

The final step in the microfabrication process was to deposit a nonwetting Teflon-like polymer coating onto the surface of the devices to complete the superhydrophobic surface production.28 This was performed by depositing a thin polymer film via C4F8 plasma polymerization in a reactive ion etcher. The method utilized is the first step of the popularly used Bosch process for deep reactive ion etching, where a polymer film is deposited before etching. This process was chosen, as it reliably layers a thin polymer film in a highly controlled environment. Before coating, wafers were cut into 11 × 11 mm square sections to fit in the wells of a standard 24-well plate for culture (Figure 2l). This was done before coating so that there would not be any bare portions of the device. Polymer deposition via C4F8 plasma polymerization was found to be highly uniform and reproducible in SEM images of the devices (Figure 2m). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were also taken to compare the average roughness of the bare nanorods to the coated nanorods (Figure 3a,b). The mean roughness of the measured sections was 129 ± 8 nm before polymer coating. The mean surface roughness of the measured sections was 121 ± 6 nm after polymer coating. No significant changes to the average surface roughness of the device occurred due to polymer deposition (Figure 3c).

To confirm that the device exhibited ultralow wettability, WCA measurements were made using a goniometer. The static WCA (or Young’s angle of contact) describes the resulting tangential angle that is exhibited from the balance between the solid–vapor, liquid–vapor, and solid–liquid interfaces.23 A WCA under 90° indicates a hydrophilic surface, while a surface with a WCA over 90° is considered hydrophobic.29 A surface is termed superhydrophobic if it exhibits a WCA of 150° or above. First, a piece of polystyrene, the same base material as the Aggrewell 800, was coated with the Anti-Adherence rinsing solution and the WCA was measured to be 6° (Figure 3d). A WCA of 115° was measured for blank, clean silicon wafer coated in the nonwetting polymer, showing that a hydrophobic surface could be achieved via the C4F8 plasma polymerization method (Figure 3e). When a silicon wafer with the ZnO nanorods was coated with the nonwetting polymer, a WCA of 167° was achieved. This confirms that the high surface roughness provided by the ZnO nanorods was necessary to produce a superhydrophobic surface (Figure 3f).

Various different states of water droplets on superhydrophobic surfaces have been described in the literature to better describe the solid–vapor, liquid–vapor, and solid–liquid interfaces which define the contact angle behavior (Figure S4a). This includes the Young’s state, the Wenzel state, the Cassie–Baxter state, and the Marmur state. Using the Young-Dupre equation (eq 1), the solid–liquid adhesion energy per unit area was calculated for each substrate.30 The work of adhesion of a 10 μL droplet of deionized (DI) water to the solid substrates was calculated to be 73.2 dyn/cm for the polystyrene coated in the Anti-Adherence solution, 71.9 dyn/cm for the smooth silicon substrate coated in Teflon-like polymer, and 71.3 dyn/cm for the nanorods coated with the Teflon-like polymer, demonstrating that water droplet adhesion energy decreases with decreasing surface wettability. The roughness factor was then calculated assuming a Wenzel state of the water droplet on our superhydrophobic surface (eq 2).31,32 This was found to be 2.7, which is equivalent to other highly textured surfaces studied in the literature.33 Assuming a Cassie–Baxter state, the fraction of the solid in contact with the liquid was also calculated for the superhydrophobic surface (eq 3).34 Only a fraction of 0.2 of the total area of nanorods coated in the Teflon-like polymer was estimated to be in contact with the 10 μL droplet of water. Therefore, in comparison with a smooth surface (such as the polystyrene coated in the Anti-Adherence solution in the AggreWell 800 plates), there is a highly reduced area where the cells can potentially adhere due to the air pockets created by the complex structure of the ZnO nanorods. This physically reduces interactions with the substrate causing cells to cluster to avoid cell death due to anoikis.

For the dynamic WCA the advancing and receding water contact angles were captured by pipetting a droplet of water onto and off the wafer surface.23 For a surface to be classified as superhydrophobic, the receding WCA must be over 90°, the advancing angle must be at least 145°, and the hysteresis must be under 5. Hysteresis is the quantification of the presence of surface defects and roughness at a microscopic level, calculated by subtracting the advancing WCA from the receding WCA (eq 4). The advancing WCA of our engineered surface was measured as 165.2° and the receding WCA was 168.6°, giving the surface a hysteresis of 3.4 (Figure 3g,h). This confirms the successful fabrication of a superhydrophobic surface at the bottom of our microwells. Additionally, the superhydrophobicity was confirmed to be maintained in cell culture conditions as well. The static WCA was measured to be 158° after the device was submerged in complete media for 6 days (Figure S4b).

Successful Growth of In Vitro Circulating Tumor Cell Model in the Superhydrophobic Array Device

A physiologically relevant method for culturing a model of CTC clusters was developed using the SHArD-C. The average cell clusters found in the bloodstream range from 2 to 100 cells, which was used to determine the range of cluster sizes tested.35−37 Three differently sized aggregates (termed small, medium, and large) were grown in the SHArD-C using the HCT116 and LNCaP cell lines. In each microwell of the SHArD-C, average numbers of 3, 5, and 7 cells were plated to grow small, medium, and large clusters, respectively. Cells were cultured in the device to approximate 3 doubling times. The plating and culture process is represented in the schematic in Figure 4a. A commonly used existing method for growing cancer cell aggregates in vitro was also used for comparison. This common protocol consists of growing cells until they have formed a confluent monolayer, adding trypsin to cleave surface proteins, and then gently pipetting to avoid disrupting floating cell aggregates.35−37 This method was chosen as the cell number required to form such a low-count cluster is too dilute to ensure success using a traditional 3D culture method such as hanging drop or a concave microwell method. In a 96-well plate for 3D culture, the area of the well is so large that a sample with such a low number of cells would likely result in cell death from lack of cell–cell interactions. In the case of the AggreWell, as the wells are so large compared to the cluster size, the resulting cell count in each microwell would be heterogeneous due to the low cell count of the sample. This is a problem that is addressed with the SHArD-C, as the microwells have an area that is 64× smaller than the size of the AggreWell 800. One of the major limitations of the monolayer methods for forming small cell aggregates in vitro is the lack of control over cell cluster size and destabilized cell:cell junctions due to trypsinization.

Cell clusters grown in the SHArD-C were size-controlled based on the average number of cells plated in each microwell. Cluster sizes were analyzed by calculating the theoretical cluster volume. This was calculated by approximating the volume of the cluster to an ellipsoid using the minimum and maximum measured diameters of each cluster (eq 7).38 HCT116 cells clusters grown in the SHArD-C showed significant increments in cluster volume with increasing plating density (Figure 4b,c). The small, medium, and large clusters had an average volume of 22 ± 12, 51 ± 33, and 83 ± 40 μm3, respectively. This closely mimics the sizes of CTC clusters found in the blood of cancer patients.36 In the control monolayer method, cluster sizes were very small, on average 29 ± 25 μm3, and there were many single cells present in the cell suspension. The coefficient of variation was calculated to compare how well the SHArD-C controlled the size of clusters in each group. In the case of the HCT116 cells, the coefficient of variation achieved in the control monolayer method was elevated compared to clusters produced in the SHArD-C, although this difference was not significant (Figure 4d). As CTCs travel through the circulation, they experience low levels of prolonged fluid shear stress (FSS) which can range from 0.4 to 12 dyn/cm2.39 To simulate these shear forces in the laboratory, a cone-and-plate viscometer was used following the protocol described previously by Mitchell and King.40 The modeled clusters were therefore exposed to 2 dyn/cm2 of FSS for 1 h using a cone-and-plate viscometer. The HCT116 clusters grown using the control monolayer method showed significant decreases in volume after exposure to FSS for 1 h. HCT116 clusters grown using the monolayer method showed a 68% decrease in volume on average (Figure 4e). There was no significant change in cluster volume for all 3 sizes of clusters grown in the SHArD-C.

Results for the LNCaP cell clusters grown in the SHArD-C were very similar (Figure 4f). The average cluster volume in μm3 was 12 ± 6, 20 ± 6, and 39 ± 15 for the small, medium, and large clusters, respectively (Figure 4g). The clusters grown using the control monolayer method were quite large with an average of 62 ± 49 μm3. The coefficient of variation of the cluster volume was significantly larger in clusters grown using the control monolayer method compared to those cultured using the SHArD-C (Figure 4h). When exposed to sustained low levels of FSS, the LNCaP clusters grown using the monolayer method exhibited a 68.3% decrease in cluster volume (Figure 4i). In comparison, cell clusters grown using the SHArD-C, regardless of cell type and cluster size, did not have any significant change in volume comparing before and after exposure to FSS. This demonstrates that our method of creating an in vitro model of CTC clusters is more physiologically relevant, as the clusters can withstand forces that CTC clusters would encounter in the circulation, in a manner comparable to clinical CTC clusters present in human blood samples.

SHArD-C Produces a CTC Cluster Model Which Is More Physiologically Relevant

To further investigate the ability of these aggregates to withstand FSS and resemble the collective migration units found in the blood of cancer patients, the expression and localization of the cell junction protein E-cadherin was evaluated via confocal microscopy.43 E-cadherin is important for cell–cell adhesions in epithelial cells and can affect cell proliferation, migration, cell polarization, and survival.44 Additionally, E-cadherin is known to contribute to CTC cluster survival through anoikis prevention and FSS resistance.45−47 Regarding the localization of E-cadherin, clusters cultured using the SHArD-C showed the greatest fluorescent signal at the boundaries shared with other cells (Figure 5a). This suggests that the SHArD-C is capable of growing clusters that are tightly bound internally. In comparison, clusters grown using the control monolayer method displayed only diffuse signal throughout the cytoplasm. Quantification of the E-Cadherin expression showed that both the HCT116 and LNCaP clusters cultured in the SHArD-C expressed significantly more protein than those formed using the monolayer control method (Figure 5b,c). On average, the CTC cluster models increased by 81 and 36%, for the HCT116 and LNCaP cells respectively, compared to the monolayer control method.

Clustering of cancer cells in the circulation is known to confer metastatic advantage to CTCs by protecting against cell death due to anoikis and by shielding portions of the cell surface from exposure to high FSS levels.26,41,42 To further test the clinical relevance of the cultured clusters, the cells were exposed to brief pulses of extremely high levels of FSS using a syringe pump system that can simulate the forces cells experience when passing through the heart. Cell clusters grown in the SHArD-C and the monolayer control method were exposed to 4000 dyn/cm2 in 10 pulses, once every 2 min. This timing between shear pulses simulates the time it would take a cell to traverse the circulatory system and return to the heart.43 Single cells were also exposed to high levels of FSS in this same manner for comparison. Brightfield images were taken before and after FSS exposure to assess the disaggregation of cells throughout the experiment. Interestingly, cells in all groups completely disaggregated after 10 pulses of FSS (Figure 6a). Cell death was quantified via an annexin V and propidium assay on a flow cytometer (Figure 6b). Cell viability in all groups before FSS exposure was high, with an average of 77% of cells identified as healthy (Figure 6c). This also confirmed that the SHArD-C provides an environment that maintains cell viability to the same standard as classic cell culture conditions in a flask or dish (Figure S5). Cell viability decreased significantly after 10 shear pulses for the single cell group, with a 4% coefficient of variation, showing the consistency of the experiments. Cell aggregates grown using the monolayer control method had a highly variable cell viability before FSS exposure, with a coefficient of variation of 16%. The percentage of healthy cells in this group decreased by an average of 16% after the syringe pulses, however this change was not statistically significant. The coefficient of variation for the percent of healthy cells from the monolayer control method after FSS exposure was relatively high, at 30%. Cells from the clusters grown using the monolayer device did not have significantly higher viability than the single cells after FSS. Interestingly, cell clusters which were cultured in the SHArD-C showed no significant change in cell viability after exposure to the high levels of FSS using the syringe pumps. Additionally, the coefficient of variation for cell viability was 5% before FSS and 4% after FSS, demonstrating the consistency of this method. Compared to the single cell group after FSS exposure, the cells cultured into clusters using the SHArD-C showed resistance to cell death and contained a significantly higher percentage of healthy cells. Cell death experiments for clusters were only conducted using LNCaP because HCT116 single cells were found to be resistant to cell death via FSS exposure (Figure S6).

Confirmation of Spheroid Growth Using the Superhydrophobic Array Device

The cancer cell lines HCT116 and LNCaP were utilized throughout this study to validate the spheroid forming capabilities of the SHArD-S compared to the commercially available AggreWell 800 system. For spheroid culture, culture was extended to 6 doubling periods. The HCT116 spheroids were cultured in both the SHArD-S and the AggreWell 800 for 4 days. The LNCaP spheroids were cultured in both the SHArD-S and the AggreWell 800 for 6 days. Three different spheroid plating densities were used for both cell lines: 2500, 5000, and 7500 cells/microwell. Spheroid volume was calculated by following eq 7, as suggested by Talkington and Durrett.38 Spheroid roundness was also quantified and calculated using eq 6.

Representative brightfield images of the HCT116 and LNCaP spheroids cultured in the SHArD-S and the AggreWell 800 can be observed in Figure 7a. HCT116 spheroids cultured using the AggreWell 800 were significantly larger than those cultured in the SHArD-S (Figure S8a). The volume of the HCT116 spheroids grown in the AggreWell 800 was 2.1-fold, 3.4-fold, and 4.5-fold larger than those grown in the SHArD-S when 2500, 5000, and 7500 cells were plated per microwell, respectively. In terms of spheroid roundness, the SHArD-S produced HCT116 spheroids that were significantly less round than those cultured in the AggreWell 800. Interestingly, LNCaP spheroids grown in the SHArD-S were significantly larger than those cultured in the AggreWell 800, a reverse of the HCT116 cells (Figure S8b). On average, LNCaP spheroids had 20% greater volume than those cultured in the AggreWell 800. Similar to the HCT116 spheroids, the LNCaP spheroids grown in the SHArD-S showed reduced roundness compared to those grown in the AggreWell 800, although this difference only reached significance at the lowest plating density.

Spheroid volumes for both cell lines were plotted together to understand how differences in cell type changed spheroid growth using the same device (Figure 7b). Here, we observed that the volume of spheroids grown in the AggreWell 800 depends on the cell type that is plated at all three plating densities. When 2500 cells were plated per microwell, a 7.8-fold difference in spheroid volume was observed when comparing LNCaP and HCT116 cells cultured in the AggreWell 800. The change in volume was larger (9-fold change) at both of the higher plating densities between the prostate and colorectal cancer cells cultured in the AggreWell 800. This result is consistent across multiple prior studies in the literature.44 In contrast, spheroids cultured for 6 doubling periods in the SHArD-S showed no significant differences in volume between the two cell lines. The 3D volume was confirmed through 3D projections of the spheroids stained for F-actin and DAPI (Figure S9a,b). These showcase the irregular shape of the large cell aggregates cultured in the SHArD-S using both the HCT116 and LNCaP cell lines.

When HCT116 spheroids cultured in the AggreWell 800 were exposed to physiological FSS for 30 min, a decrease in the spheroid volume was observed (Figure 8a). The difference was statistically significant in the two spheroids with greater plating densities, where there was a 50% decrease in the mean spheroid volume. Additionally, spheroid roundness was also quantified before and after flow exposure in the cone-and-plate viscometers. Roundness in the HCT116 spheroids grown in the AggreWell 800 with plating densities of 5000 and 7500 cells per microwell increased significantly after FSS exposure. In combination, these results suggest that HCT116 spheroids grown in the AggreWell 800 readily disaggregate and deform during this functional assay. In contrast, HCT116 spheroids grown in the SHArD-S at the two larger plating densities did not change in volume or roundness after exposure to FSS (Figure 8b). LNCaP spheroids cultured in the AggreWell 800 did not exhibit as much sensitivity to FSS exposure (Figure 9a). No significant changes in spheroid volume was observed before and after treatment. This could be due to the smaller size of the LNCaP spheroids grown in the AggreWell 800 compared to the HCT116 cells. LNCaP spheroids grown in the AggreWell 800 at the two higher plating densities had significantly greater roundness, indicating that deformation occurred during the FSS exposure. As before, LNCaP spheroids grown in the SHArD-S did not change in volume or roundness after exposure to FSS (Figure 9b).

Discussion

In this study, a superhydrophobic array device (SHArD) was designed to be easily tunable to culture a wide range of 3D cancer models in vitro with a single protocol. Two devices were fabricated as a proof of concept: the SHArD-C to culture cancer cell aggregates made up of 2–100 cells and the SHArD-S to grow spheroids with thousands of cells. The SHArD devices are comprised of 11 × 11 mm superhydrophobic chips subdivided into square microwells measuring 100 × 100 μm (SHArD-C) or 800 × 800 μm (SHArD-S) (Figure 1). Therefore, the SHArD devices can produce up to 189 spheroids (in the SHArD-S) or 18,906 clusters (in the SHArD-C) in a single well of a 24-well plate. This is comparable to and exceeds the number of 3D aggregates that a single well from the AggreWell 800 can produce (296 spheroids per well of a 24-well plate). Thus, the SHArD-C, the main design being tested, not only provides an optimized surface for 3D cell aggregation and growth, but also has a 65-fold increased capacity to produce cancer cell aggregates compared to the AggreWell 800. Using an alternative method, such as a 96-well plate or hanging drop, would reduce the cluster production capacity by 197-fold compared to the SHArD-C. Additionally, we have developed a physiologically relevant CTC cluster model using a superhydrophobic surface optimized for 3D cell culture. We additionally validated the device for the culture of larger spheroids and demonstrated that these have greater physiological relevance than those grown using commercially available devices.

It is noted that this is not the first hydrophobic surface made for the culturing of spheroids, see, for instance, Ferrari et al.47 The present work does, however, demonstrate the highest throughput method described to date for spheroidal culture based on highly tunable fabrication methods using SU8 photolithography, to the best of our knowledge. It also represents the first cancer cell aggregate modeling platform based on a superhydrophobic surface. Metastatic cancer cluster models often rely on very low throughput, gravity-reliant methods such as the hanging drop method. The SHArD-S not only allows for tens of thousands of realizations in one experiment but also makes aggregates more consistently as a model for the primary tumor by enhancing compactness without increased roundness, a feature not achieved by any other method to date.

In vitro methods for modeling CTC clusters often utilize immortalized cancer cell lines. These protocols consist of growing cells until they have formed a confluent monolayer, adding trypsin, then gently pipetting while avoiding disruption of floating cell aggregates.35−37 While studies have shown that these aggregates share some similarity to primary CTC clusters, there are limitations including a lack of control over the size of aggregates and inability to control the ratios of different cell types for heterotypic cluster formation. Additionally, trypsin works by decreasing cell adherence and damaging the cell–cell junctions, causing the cells in clusters to be only loosely bound to each other.48 Because of this, once they are in a shear stress environment, the clusters formed using the monolayer method completely disaggregate, and they have a similar viability to that of the single cells after 10 pulses. Throughout this study, we demonstrated that the metastatic cluster-modeling aggregates produced in the SHArD-C show higher cell–cell junction protein expression, do not disaggregate under low, laminar FSS, and preserve cell viability after exposure to high levels of FSS (Figures 5 and 6). This makes our device highly appealing, as it produces a physiologically relevant model that enables improved elucidation of the role of collective migration units during the metastatic cascade. Additionally, the viability of the cells from the clusters formed using the monolayer method and the SHArD-C was measured and confirmed to be the same as that of cells that had been cultured in a flask.

Literature such as that presented by King et al. provide key statistics pointing to the importance of cluster model uniformity as it relates to patient sample counterparts.49 In the control method, where the cell aggregates are derived by gently disrupting a cell monolayer via trypsinization, the number of cells per cluster has a high degree of randomness as there is no control over which cells stay bound and which cell junctions are ruptured. Therefore, this results in a wide range of cluster sizes that are not controlled in shape (Figure 4). In contrast, The SHArD-C provides a method to tightly control in vitro cluster model size, a property that is desirable for further investigation of the role of these in the metastatic cascade. Using the methods developed throughout this study, cell aggregates that are uniform in shape and number of cells per cluster can be produced to simulate different types of CTC clusters found in the blood of various cancer patients. CTC clusters range in size and density due to intravasation from a variety of primary tumor sites, metastatic capability according to location of nearby organs, and metastatic potential as immune cells impact cluster behavior in the circulation. In order to better understand the impact of cluster size on the metastatic potential of CTCs, models of CTC clusters need to be cultured in vitro with tight control over the number of cells per cluster. Future in vivo studies will be necessary to confirm whether the small cell aggregates formed using the SHArD-C simulate the metastatic advantages that primary CTC clusters have compared to single cells.

The AggreWell system relies on hydrophilic surfaces to prevent cell–substrate adhesions for the culture of 3D models. However, this is known to be limited in its prevention of protein adsorption onto the microwells, preventing the formation of tight spheroids and producing spheroids that have low size uniformity between cell types when using this system. Additionally, the AggreWell 800 microwells have an inverted truncated pyramid geometry, allowing cells to cluster at the bottom (Figure S2b). This method relies on gravitational pull toward the bottom of the well and a coating of antiadherence solution to avoid cellular protein interactions with the device surface. In comparison, the SHArD uses superhydrophobic surfaces to prevent cell–substrate interactions.

In both SHArD designs, SU-8 negative photoresist was chosen to create the microwell walls, since it is known to be a biocompatible material that causes minimal cytotoxicity for in vitro and in vivo experimentation.50,51 Additionally, SU-8 photolithography is a highly tunable and well-accepted method for easily creating high aspect ratio microscale structures. The fabrication protocol is straightforward to learn, easily scalable to produce large quantities of the devices, and only requires basic equipment that is found in standard cleanrooms. This accessible protocol results in devices with superhydrophobicity as their key characteristic, known to strongly promote cells to grow in 3D aggregates over other surfaces with greater wettability. These surfaces are ultra-water repellent due to their high surface roughness and antiwetting chemistry. Additionally, both SHArD devices have a cube microwell geometry, relying on the shape of a water droplet in contact with a superhydrophobic surface to guide the cells to cluster (Figure S2c). This cubic geometry paired with superhydrophobicity creates a beading effect on the cell media at the bottom of the microwell for cells to not only clump reasonably within an already beaded droplet but additionally rely on cellular adhesions and enhanced E-cadherin signaling for stability in culture due to a lack of microwell surface for proteins to adhere to. There is such little opportunity for protein adsorption and cellular adhesion to the well surfaces that cellular aggregation provides a mechanistic advantage for cells to survive.

Superhydrophobic surfaces have two essential components: a hydrophobic coating and nanoscale surface roughness. These two in combination are necessary to create a surface with a WCA of over 150°. For both devices, creating surfaces with high roughness was the first step of the microfabrication process (Figures 2 and 3). This was achieved using ZnO nanopillars as they are easily fabricated and biocompatible.52 The ZnO nanorods are coated with a thin fluoropolymer film to achieve superhydrophobicity. These films are known in the literature to have long-term stability in liquid interfaces and temperatures up to 400 K and, therefore, are expected to remain intact during the cell culture period.53−55 Both SHArDs are engineered to control the cell-material interactions to minimize cell adhesion to the surface and promote cell-to-cell binding. While they are an improvement over the standard culture methods currently found in the literature, some limitations do exist. First, the devices are fabricated on a silicon wafer, and are therefore not optically transparent. This is a quality that other commercially available products share. Additionally, when the devices are used for cell culture, they are diced to 11 × 11 mm sections and placed into cell culture plates with round wells. This reduces the number of spheroids that can be produced in a single well compared to the AggreWell 800, which has microwells that span the entire bottom of the well.

We also confirmed the enhanced spheroid forming capabilities of the SHArD-S compared to the commercially available AggreWell 800 system. Both HCT116 and LNCaP spheroids had significantly lower circularity when cultured in the SHArD-S compared to the AggreWell 800. A deviation from circularity more closely resembles the disorganized and abnormal shapes of many cancerous tissues.56 Additionally, spheroids grown in the AggreWell 800 plates had large variations in volume between the two cell lines when plated at the same cell density per microwell. This trend was not observed in spheroids cultured in the SHArD-S. This indicates that the SHArD-S provides a biological-material interface that does not alter spheroid growth, a quality that the AggreWell 800 lacks (Figures 7 and 10). Next, the functional integrity of the cell–cell junctions in the spheroids was examined by exposure to FSS using a cone-and-plate viscometer (Figures 8 and 9). The same protocol was utilized to assess disaggregation in the spheroids as the protocol used in the smaller clusters to allow for direct comparison between the two 3D cancer models. Disaggregation was quantified as changes in spheroid volume and roundness. Similarly to the clusters cultured in the SHArD-C, spheroids grown in the SHArD-S resisted disaggregation under FSS conditions better than those cultured in the AggreWell 800. Taken together, these results indicate that the SHArD-S is capable of forming spheroids that are more physiologically relevant than those cultured in the AggreWell 800, as they have a higher density of cells, are more irregularly shaped, and do not disaggregate easily.

Overall, the SHArD-C provides a platform for culturing model clusters that are more similar to the collective migration units found in the blood and can survive high levels of FSS better than current methods in the literature. Future studies using this system will involve the culture of heterotypic clusters to better understand the role of immune and stromal cells during cancer metastasis in the bloodstream. Additionally, the SHArD-S provides a more favorable method for further studies pertaining to the primary tumor microenvironment, drug sensitivity, and interactions with immune cells. By better modeling these 3D systems in the body, more in-depth experimentation can be performed to understand and treat cancer cells throughout the metastatic cascade: from the primary tumor to CTCs.

Materials and Methods

Device Microfabrication Protocol

A 15% (w/v) ZnO nanoparticle (1.5 g) (Zinc oxide NanoTek, 40–100 nm APS powder, Alfa Aesar, 44899) suspension in Milli-Q-filtered water (10 mL) was prepared. The industrial-grade dispersant Sokalan CP10 (BASF, 50072047) was used to stabilize the nanoparticle suspension at 1% (w/v) (0.1 g). Probe sonication (Sonic Dismembrator Model 100, Fisher Scientific) exposure times to produce a uniform and monodisperse solution were evaluated from 0 to 180 s, testing at 30 s intervals. Nanoparticle suspensions were analyzed by dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer, Malvern Panalytical Advanced Series Ultra). The optimal sonication time was determined to be 60 s at power level 5 on ice, to prevent overheating of the mixture.

Silicon wafers were cleaned in acetone then plasma cleaned for 5 min using a Trion Phantom II deep reactive ion etcher. The ZnO nanoparticle suspension (5 mL) was spin coated onto the wafer at 1500 rpm for 30 s, accelerating at 300 rpm/s.57 Wafers were then dried at room temperature (RT). Annealing was performed in a 4” tube furnace (MTI OTF-1200X) at 500 °C for 1 h in argon gas at atmospheric pressure to enhance the ZnO seed crystallinity.58−60 40 min heating and cooling steps were executed before and after annealing to prevent the wafer from shattering. Wafers were then placed in a 500 mL autoclave filled with the nanorod growth solution in Milli-Q-filtered water (500 mL): 0.03 M zinc nitrate hexahydrate (4.46 g) (Sigma-Aldrich, 228737–500G) and 0.02 M hexamethylenetetramine (1.402 g) (Sigma-Aldrich, 398160–250G).58,61,62 Wafers were baked in an oven while submerged in the nanorod growth solution at 90 °C for 4 h. Wafers were angled face down in the growth bath to prevent the formation of large aggregates on the surface. After the baking step, the wafers were washed in Milli-Q-filtered water and dried overnight in a vacuum chamber to eliminate moisture from the surface.

Photolithography masks were designed on the L-Edit software (Siemens) and printed using a Heidelberg Instruments μPG101 laser writer. For the SHArD-S, two masks were used: the base coat mask consisting of an array of 600 μm × 600 μm squares spaced 200 μm apart, and the microwell wall mask consisting of an array of 700 μm × 700 μm squares spaced 100 μm apart. A base coat of SU-8 was used to enhance the adhesion of the ultrathick SU-8 microwells to the nanorod layer.63 SU-8 photolithography for the SHArD-S was performed following the recipes listed in Tables 1 and 2. SU-8 2002 (Y11029–0500L-1GL, Kayaku Advanced Materials) and SU-8 2075 (Y11074–0500L-1GL, Kayaku Advanced Materials) were used for the base coat and microwell walls, respectively. Due to the thickness of the SU-8 layer required for the microwell walls, the photoresist was spun onto the wafer surface in two steps. Edge bead removal was performed before exposure (G042075–4000L1PE, Kayaku Advanced Materials). Exposure to UV irradiation was carried out using a Karl Suss MA-6 mask aligner. Wafers were rinsed with isopropyl alcohol (IPA) and dried with N2 gas between the developer step and the hard bake. For the SHArD-C, the SU-8 mask consisted of an array of 70 μm × 70 μm squares spaced 30 μm apart. For this version of the device, the adhesion promoter Omnicoat (Kayaku, G112850 0500L 1GL) was spin coated onto the wafer surface before the SU-8 step. SU-8 2075 was used to make the microwell walls of the SHArD-C. SU-8 photolithography was performed following the recipes listed in Tables 3 and 4. The wafers were exposed to UV irradiation using a Karl Suss MA-6 mask aligner. SU-8 developer (Y020100–4000L1PE, Kayaku Advanced Materials) was used to reveal the microwell wall pattern for both devices. Wafers were rinsed with IPA and dried with N2 gas between the developer step and the hard bake. The excess omnicoat was removed by submerging the wafer in MF-319 developer (10018042, DuPont) for 30 s.

Device Characterization

The main characterization tool utilized throughout the microfabrication process was scanning electron microscopy (Zeiss Merlin with Gemini II column). All samples were sputter coated in gold before SEM imaging. Thin-films were additionally characterized using a Rigaku Smart Lab Diffractometer with Cu–Kα source to perform in-plane XRD before and after the furnace annealing step. Nanorod surface roughness before and after polymer coating was measured using a Bruker Dimension Icon atomic force microscope on tapping mode in air with a Bruker RTESPA-525 cantilever. For each condition, three 5 μm × 5 μm sections were scanned and the Ra (average roughness) calculated. A 1 μm × 1 μm section of each sample was also imaged. SU-8 structure dimensions for the SHArD-C were measured using a Bruker Dektak 150 stylus profilometer equipped with a 12.5 μm tip while operating in contact stylus profilometry mode. An Ossila Contact Angle Goniometer was used to characterize the wettability of the device surface. For the static WCA, a 10 μL drop of DI water was placed on the surface and an image taken. Static WCA was measured for polystyrene coated with the Anti-Adherence rinsing solution (StemCell Technologies, 07010), a wafer with nanorods, and a wafer with nanorods coated with the Teflon-like polymer. To ensure that the superhydrophobicity of the device is maintained during the culture process, chips with nanorods coated in the nonwetting polymer were submerged in complete media. The static WCA was measured on the sixth day. Images were analyzed on ImageJ using the angle tool.

Cell Culture and Reagents

The human prostate cancer cell line LNCaP (ATCC, CRL-1740) and the human colorectal cancer cell line HCT116 (ATCC, CCL-247) were used throughout the study. HCT116 is a human colorectal carcinoma cell line isolated from the primary tumor of a patient and grow with an epithelial morphology. The LNCaP cell line was isolated from a lymph node lesion of a metastatic prostate cancer patient and is androgen sensitive. These cell lines were chosen as they are representative of two cancer types (colon and prostate) at various stages of the metastatic cascade (primary tumor vs lymph node metastasis) with different doubling periods which are known to have spheroid-forming capabilities. In this way the adaptability of the device to allow for the formation of uniform 3D models using vastly different cell lines is demonstrated.65 The LNCaP cells were cultured in RPMI media (Gibco, 11875–093) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 26140–079), 1% penicillin streptomycin (Corning, 30002CI), 1% (v/v) HEPES buffer (Corning, 25–060-CI), and 1% (v/v) sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360–070). The HCT116 cells were cultured in McCoy’s media (Gibco, 16600–082) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 26140–079), 1% pen strep (Corning, 30002CI). Three biological replicates were performed for each experiment.

For 3D cultures of spheroids (using the SHArD-S or AggreWell 800), cells were grown for approximately 6 doubling periods, as determined from 2D culture experiments. LNCaP cells were incubated for 6 days, while the HCT116 cells were incubated for 4 days. Total cell plating number for an individual well of a 24-well plate was determined by calculating the number of cells per mm2 to achieve an initial plating density of 2500, 5000, and 7500 cells per microwell (eq 5). The surface area of the bottom of each microwell (SAmicrowell) of the SHArD-S is 0.64 mm2, and the surface area of the bottom of a well of a 24-well plate is 190 mm2 (SA24-well). Therefore, a total of 742,000, 1,484,000, and 2,226,000 cells were plated per well in the 24-well plate for the initial plating density of 2500, 5000, and 7500 cells per microwell, respectively. To culture cells on the AggreWell 800 (34815, Stem Cell Technologies), each well was first coated in the Anti-Adherence solution (500 μL) (07010, Stem Cell Technologies) and then rinsed with warmed media. For culture in the SHArD-S, devices were gently placed in wells of a 24-well plate with a pair of forceps and sterilized by UV irradiation for 45 min. Warm media was pipetted vigorously into the microwells to remove any bubbles. For both devices, cells were suspended in 2 mL of media for plating and centrifuged into the microwells at 300g for 5 min. In the SHArD-S, once the cells reached the expected 6 doubling periods, the devices were gently transferred to a clean, empty well in the plate and covered with 2 mL of HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ (14025–092, Gibco). To harvest the spheroids, the media or buffer within the wells was pipetted slowly over the microwells. Spheroid suspensions were then passed through a mesh cell strainer (27215, Stem Cell Technologies) to remove single cells, and rinsed out into a 15 mL conical tube using HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+. LNCaP cells were incubated for 3 days, while the HCT116 cells were incubated for 2 days. Total cell plating number was determined by calculating the number of cells per mm2 to achieve an initial plating density of 3, 5, and 7 cells per microwell (eq 2). For culture in the SHArD-C, devices were gently placed into wells of a 24-well plate using forceps and sterilized by UV irradiation for 45 min. Warm media was pipetted vigorously into the microwells to remove any bubbles. For both devices, cells were suspended in 2 mL of media for plating and centrifuged into the microwells at 300g for 5 min. Cluster Viability Assay

Cells were exposed to high levels of FSS to mimic the forces experienced when circulated through the heart using a syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus) loaded with 5 mL syringes (309646, BD) with a 30-gauge blunt needle (NZ5300505001, Celllink). The syringes and needles were blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (A1470–100G, Sigma-Aldrich) in HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ for 30 min. Single cells and clusters were then suspended in 2 mL of media and loaded into the syringes with the needle detached. Cells were exposed to 10 pulses of FSS using a flow rate of 14 mL/min (equivalent to a wall shear stress of 3950 dyn/cm2) with 2 min between each pulse.39,67,68

Confocal Microscopy

The difference in compactness of spheroids grown in the SHArD-S and the AggreWell 800 were evaluated via confocal microscopy. Spheroids were harvested from each device, fixed in 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA) (40 μL) (15714-S, Electron Microscopy Sciences) in HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ (960 μL) for 15 min, and then permeabilized in 1% (v/v) Triton-X-100 (10 μL) (9002–93–1, Sigma-Aldrich) in HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ (990 μL) for 10 min. Spheroids were then stained with DAPI (2 μL) (D1306, Invitrogen) in HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ (1 mL) at a ratio of 2:1000 for 30 min. Washing steps were performed between each incubation step using HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+. Stained spheroids were resuspended in 1 mL of HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ and placed into the wells of a glass-bottom 12-well plate (P12–1.5H–N, Cellvis) for imaging. The wells were imaged using an LSM 900 Zeiss confocal microscope using a 10× objective. Five images were taken per sample at random positions of the well with a 405 nm laser and the widefield setting. Image analysis was performed using FIJI software. The widefield channel was used to manually contour each spheroid. The mean fluorescence intensity of the ROI and 3 randomly placed square background samples was then measured. Compactness was quantified as the signal-to-noise ratio, calculated as the difference between the mean DAPI fluorescence of the spheroid (FDAPI) and the mean fluorescence of the background (F0) divided by the mean fluorescence of the background (eq 8).8

To investigate E-cadherin expression, the cell clusters were harvested and fixed in 4% (v/v) PFA (40 μL) in HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ (960 μL) for 15 min then permeabilized in 1% (v/v) Triton-X-100 (10 μL) in HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ (990 μL) for 10 min. Clusters were then blocked for 2 h in 5% (w/v) BSA (0.01g) in HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ (100 μL) and 5% (v/v) normal goat serum (100 μL) (50062Z, Invitrogen). The clusters were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2:100 E-cadherin rabbit anti-human antibody (4 μL) (3195S, Cell Signaling Technology) in the 5% BSA and 5% goat blocking serum described above (200 μL). The next day the cells were incubated with the secondary stain 2:500 anti-rabbit IgG Fab2 Alexa Fluor(R) 488 (2 μL) (4412S, Cell Signaling Technology) for 2 h at RT in the blocking serum (500 μL). Then, clusters were incubated in 2:1000 DAPI (2 μL) and ActinRed 555 ReadyProbes reagent (R37112, Invitrogen) cocktail for 30 min at RT in the blocking serum (1 mL). Washing steps were performed in between each step using HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+. The stained clusters were spun onto glass slides using a CytoSpin3 (Shandon) and a drop of antifade mounting media (H-1000, Vectrashield) and coverslips were applied. The slides were imaged on an LSM 900 Zeiss confocal microscope with a 63× oil immersion objective. Five images were acquired per sample. Image analysis was performed in FIJI using a macro to quantify cell nuclei number and E-cadherin fluorescence. Briefly, the “analyze particles” feature was used to count the nuclei on the blue channel (DAPI). The red channel (actin) was used to create a binary mask of the cell cluster that was used to create an ROI. Statistical Analysis

All values are plotted as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Experiments were repeated to obtain three independent replicates for each condition. In the portion of the study investigating cluster formation, 3 different plating densities were tested in the SHArD-C. One plating density was tested for the monolayer cluster method as a control. In the portion of the study assessing spheroid growth, 3 different plating densities were tested on both the SHArD-S and the AggreWell 800 substrates. For microscopy, five images were collected per experiment at random locations on the well plate. Size and fluorescence intensity were quantified using FIJI. Outliers were identified by using a ROUT’s test with Q = 5%. Outliers were removed from the analysis. Data analysis was performed on GraphPad Prism using unpaired, parametric t tests or ordinary one-way ANOVA when appropriate. Statistical significance is shown as * for p < 0.05, ** for p < 0.01, *** for p < 0.001, and **** for p < 0.0001.
