Evaluation of Tumor-Associated Macrophages and Micro-Vessel Density in Verrucous Carcinoma and Squamous Cell Carcinoma of the Oral Cavity
Authors: Hamideh Kadeh, Ghazal Derakhshanfar, Omolbanin Rezaei Mehr
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are a crucial cellular component of human tumors, but their exact roles in tumor growth and angiogenesis during cancer progression are still subject to debate. The present study aimed to explore the impact of TAMs on tumor progression and angiogenesis in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and oral verrucous carcinoma (OVC).Materials and Methods:For this cross-sectional study, 60 samples consisting of 40 OSCC and 20 OVC samples were chosen. Immunohistochemical staining was performed on the samples using anti-CD68 and anti-CD31 monoclonal antibodies to evaluate the frequency of TAMs and micro-vessel density (MVD), respectively. The data were analyzed using statistical software SPSS (v. 23) and through the independent samples Student t-test, one-way analysis of variance, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient.Results:The results of our study revealed that the mean number of TAMs and MVD were significantly greater in OSCC samples compared to OVC samples (P = 0.001) (P = 0.004). Also, the average number of TAMs and MVD increased in the high grade of OSCC; however, the correlation between both parameters and histopathological grades of OSCC was significant only for MVD. Our study found no significant correlation between TAMs and MVD (P = 0.005).Conclusion:Based on the results of our study, both TAMs and MVD were found to be significantly higher in OSCC samples compared to OVC samples. These findings suggest that the progression of oral cancer is associated with an increase in TAMs and vascular density. Additionally, the use of CD68 and CD31 markers may help in differentiating between these two types of tumors.

INTRODUCTION

Oral cancer is the most common malignancy in the head and neck region, presenting a significant metastatic potential and a poor prognosis. Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the most common malignant tumor in the oral cavity. Despite recent therapeutic advances, the 5-year survival of patients has not changed during the past 45 years. This lack of improvement can be attributed to delayed diagnosis due to the lack of diagnostic biomarkers, poor prognosis, and costly therapeutic alternatives.[123]

Oral verrucous carcinoma (OVC) is a low-grade and rare variant of SCC with specific morphological and clinical features. Overall, distant and lymph node metastases are rare in OVC; however, if it is treated inappropriately, involvement of surrounding structures and the large size of this cancer display it as a locally invasive tumor.[4] The treatment of OVC is still controversial as it mimics an invasive tumor, so it is vital to develop a diagnostic biomarker that can reliably differentiate OSCC from OVC.[45]

Studies highlight the essential role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in the development and metastasis of cancer.[2] Tumors constantly communicate with the components of their microenvironment, which house a variety of stromal cells,[6] including cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), mast cells (MCs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), pericytes, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and lymphocytes. These cells contribute to the growth of the tumor, local invasion, cellular proliferation, neovascularization, and immune escape.[78] In fact, mutual cross-talk between TME cells, extracellular matrix (ECM) components, and tumor cells are significantly responsible for stimulating tumor growth via the secretion of various cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and ECM proteins.[910]

Macrophages are widely present throughout various parts of the body. They are involved in inflammatory responses to foreign bodies and microbial invasion. In neoplastic conditions, macrophages often infiltrate the extracellular space and are determined as TAMs.[11] These macrophages show two phenotypes, M1 and M2. M1 cells present antigens, produce immunostimulatory cytokines, and help to kill tumoral cells via secreting mediators such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, IL-12, IL-23, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). On the other hand, it is stated that M2 cells contribute to tumor progression by suppressing the function of T-cells via producing cytokines such as transforming growth factors (TGFs) and IL-10. As a result, it promotes angiogenesis, tumor growth, and metastasis.[61213]

Angiogenesis is characterized as the formation and growth of new vessels from existing ones.[14] This process is well established to contribute to tumor growth as the formation of new capillaries provides tumor cells with the opportunity to enter the blood circulation and metastasize to distant locations.[15] It seems that tumor angiogenesis in the early preneoplastic stage is triggered by tumor development and is regulated by a number of negative and positive mediators produced by leukocytes and malignant cells. Research evidence supports the role of micro-vessel density (MVD) as a powerful prognostic indicator in solid tumors.[161718]

It is believed that TAMs share a role in inducing angiogenesis via secreting several mediators, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), TNF-α, angiogenin, and urokinase.[11] However, the prognostic role of TAMs and MVD in oral cancer is unclear.[1619] Thus, in this study, we examined the distribution of TAMs and MVD in OSCC and OVC to determine the role of TAMs and angiogenesis in the progression of these tumors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The current study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zahedan University of Medical Sciences with codes IR.ZAUMS.REC.1395. 201 and IR.ZAUMS.REC.1398.316. To conduct the study, 60 paraffin blocks were obtained from the archives of the Oral Pathology Department at Zahedan Dental School in Iran. The samples comprised 40 OSCC samples and 20 OVC samples that were selected by the census sampling method between 2006 and 2019. An oral and maxillofacial pathologist reviewed the hematoxylin-eosin-stained sections for reconfirmation and determination of the histopathological grade of OSCC samples. All OSCC cases were categorized into well differentiated, moderately differentiated, and poorly differentiated.[20]

Immunohistochemical staining

Initially, two 4 μm thick paraffin sections were generated from all samples and placed on slides using an appropriate tissue adhesive. The samples were then deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was achieved by microwave heating for 23 minutes using Tris buffer (pH = 7.6 for CD68 and pH = 8.5 for CD31). Subsequently, the sections were exposed to primary mouse monoclonal antibodies CD68 (Clone 514H12, LOT: 50107, Novocastra, United Kingdom) and CD31 (Clone JC70A, LOT: 20035062, Denmark) to detect macrophages and blood vessels, respectively, and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. Following this, the sections were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at room temperature, and a secondary antibody was applied. Diaminobenzidine was used as the chromogen. Immunostaining was examined under a light microscope (Nikon, Type 2, Tokyo, Japan).

Determination of the TAM count

At first, the microscopic regions exhibiting the highest intensity of staining were chosen as hot spots with ×100 magnification. Then in the hot spot areas, ten microscopic fields were randomly examined with ×400 magnification and positive CD68 cells were counted within each field. The average number of TAMs in these ten hot spot areas was reported.[12]

Determination of the MVD

For the determination of MVD, the slides were examined at low magnification (×100) to identify microscopic areas with the greatest concentration of stained vessels, referred to as hot spots. Then ten microscopic fields were randomly examined in the hot spot areas with ×400 magnification. Any cell or cell cluster with or without lumen formation that showed cytoplasmic staining for CD31 was considered as a vessel. The average number of vessels was reported for each sample.[12]

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using SPSS 23 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL). The mean number of TAMs and MVD were calculated as mean ± SD. Comparison between the groups was evaluated using independent sample Student t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pearson’s correlation coefficient was utilized to determine the association between TAMs and angiogenesis. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

This study utilized immunohistochemical staining for CD68 and CD31 markers to investigate the mean number of TAMs and MVD in 40 OSCC and 20 OVC samples.

In the OSCC group, the mean age of patients was 60.92 ± 15.39 years, range 32–90, and 60% of the patients were females. Most of the lesions (47.5%) were located in the mandibular gingiva. Regarding histopathological grading of OSCC samples, 15, 15, and 10 samples belonged to well-differentiated, moderately differentiated, and poorly differentiated OSCC, respectively. The patients in the OVC group had a mean age of 52.8 ± 18.83 years (range 13–86); 65% of the patients were females, and most samples (35%) were located in the lower lip.

In OSCC cases, CD68-positive TAMs were observed to predominantly reside in the stroma surrounding tumoral islands, penetrating into the neoplastic islands in some cases [Figure 1]. In OVC samples, TAMs were mostly observed beneath the wide rete pegs of the epithelium [Figure 1]. As shown in Table 1, the average number of TAMs was significantly higher in OSCC (41.9 ± 31.2) than in OVC samples (16.6 ± 12.9) (P = 0.001).

DISCUSSION

Although remarkable advances have been accomplished during the past 20 years in the early diagnosis of oral cancer, there is an urgent need to understand the molecular basis of oral carcinogenesis and to determine potential molecular biomarkers that can affect the survival and prognosis of patients.[21]

Many studies have shown that TAMs play a significant role in the progression of tumors and predict a poor prognosis in many cancers.[22223] Through secreting factors such as epidermal growth factors (EGFs) and other migratory factors and regulating the hydrolytic remodeling of ECM and collagen fiber production, TAMs can increase tumor invasiveness and promote the mobility of tumor cells.[24]

In our study, the expression of CD68 was significantly higher in the cases of OSCC compared to the cases of OVC, which is consistent with the findings of Chawla et al.,[12] El-Rouby,[11] and Saghafi et al.[16] Chawla et al.’s study reported a significantly higher mean expression of CD68 in samples of OVC, OSCC, and oral mucosa dysplasia compared to normal oral mucosa, suggesting the involvement of TAMs in the cancer-associated inflammatory microenvironment. Chawla et al.[12] also suggested that the increase in TAMs and enhanced angiogenesis in premalignant lesions such as low-grade OVC and OED could be useful for early diagnosis of these lesions which have high potential for malignant transformation.

In the study of El-Rouby, areas with positive CD68 expression were more extensive in OSCC than in OVC, suggesting a possible role for TAMs in modifying neoplastic processes. Also, as OVC is considered a low-grade form of OSCC, this observation indicates a role for TAMs in tumor progression and metastasis.[11]

In another study, Saghafi et al.[16] reported that TAMs were more prevalent in OSCC compared to OVC and ED, highlighting a key role for TAMs in the transformation of premalignant lesions to malignant tumors.

By contributing to the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, TAMs promote tumor progression. It has also been suggested that M2 macrophages can nurture an immunosuppressive microenvironment in SCC and premalignant lesions of the oral cavity, protecting tumoral cells from undergoing apoptosis and being recognized by immune cells, thereby promoting tumor progression.

In another study by Helal et al.,[25] the number of CD68+ macrophages was found to be significantly higher in moderately differentiated tumors than in well-differentiated tumors. This may be attributed to the increased levels of cytokines and chemokines that promote the survival and differentiation of macrophages into the M2 phenotype in higher-grade tumors and the TME. In their study, however, CD68+ macrophages were less frequent in poorly differentiated compared to moderately differentiated tumors, which can be due to the lack of differentiation of M1 macrophages during the high-grade tumor progression. Activation of M1 macrophages occurs through exposure to bacterial-derived products and lipopolysaccharides, resulting in their heightened efficacy in eliminating microorganisms and cancer cells. On the other hand, M2 macrophages are activated by cytokines and promote the growth of the tumor and angiogenesis.[25] In the current study, the number of TAMs increased with the increase in the histopathological grade of OSCC; however, it was not statistically significant, which can be explained by differences in the sample size, techniques, markers used, and methods of counting cells.

CD31 as a mediator participates in the adhesion of adjacent endothelial cells to each other and to leukocytes. It is considered a specific, reliable, and sensitive marker for endothelial differentiation.[11] In our study, CD31 immunohistochemical staining was performed to assess MVD in samples of OSCC and OVC. The results revealed that MVD was significantly higher in OSCC than in OVC. Similarly, studies by Chawla et al.,[12] El-Rouby,[11] and Saghafi et al.[16] reported higher MVD in OSCC compared to OVC.

The growth and proliferation of tumors heavily rely on neovascularization, which is induced by the secretion of angiogenic factors, including VEGF, FGF, TGF-β, and TNF-α, from tumors. These factors interact with their respective ligands on the surface of endothelial cells, leading to a notable increase in vascularity during the progression of normal tissues into carcinomas and dysplastic lesions of varying degrees,[12] an event supported by findings in the present study.

In contrast, Ashkavandi et al.,[26] who investigated CD31 expression in dysplastic and OSCC lesions, reported that the mean expression of CD31 in the intratumor and peritumor stroma of OSCC was not significantly different compared to normal mucosa. In another study by Monteiro-Amado et al.,[27] MVD was reported to be unrelated to clinical and histological parameters such as histological grade, neural invasion, tumor stage, and patient survival. This difference in the results may indicate that tumor physiopathology is much more complex than the presence or absence of vessels alone, and the interaction of various factors that influence the progression of the tumor makes it difficult to assess the effect of each of these factors. In addition, differences in the use of the hot spot method, which is very different for counting of MVD in areas with low vascularity between SCC islands, or the use of nonspecific staining for newly formed blood vessels and pre-existing ones, are also reasons for these different results.

Since TAMs are usually found in the stroma around the tumor, this localization can promote angiogenesis. Within the TME, TAMs release factors such as CCL2, CXCL12, CXCL8, EGF, IL-8, IL-1β, PIGF, TGF-β, and VEGF-A, which contribute to tumor angiogenesis. In multiple studies, the number of TAMs has been noted to positively correlate with MVD, reflecting a link between these macrophages and angiogenesis in OSCC.[112428] Nevertheless, the present study did not reveal a relationship between TAMs and MVD in OSCC and OVC samples, which is consistent with the reports of Chawla et al.[12] and Saghafi et al.[16]

Macrophages can play a dual role in angiogenesis. On one hand, they can produce proangiogenic molecules, while on the other hand, they can produce antiangiogenic factors that disrupt blood vessels. Overall, the proangiogenic function of macrophages is more prominent when they interact with neoplastic cells.[11]

The vascularization observed during the progression of epithelial dysplasia to oral carcinoma is not solely induced by angiogenic factors released by macrophages. It is possible that other cells, such as mast cells, can also influence the tumor environment to promote angiogenesis.[29] Additionally, stromal cells may release factors like VEGF and bFGF, contributing to angiogenesis. The recruitment of endothelial progenitor cells or budding capillaries could also trigger tumor angiogenesis.[30]

Further research is required to fully comprehend the interactions between cancer cells and TAMs. In addition, it is crucial to characterize these cells to reveal the intricate relationships within the TME and identify the cells responsible for the conversion of premalignant lesions to malignant tumors.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this study indicate that the density of blood vessels and the frequency of TAMs were greater in OSCC samples compared to OVC (a lower degree of malignancy) samples. As oral cancer advances, the number of macrophages and the density of blood vessels in the TME increase. CD68 and CD31 markers can be valuable in distinguishing between these tumors and detecting lesions with high potential for malignant transformation at an early stage. The study results suggest that there is no correlation between TAMs and MVD, indicating that TAMs do not play a direct role in angiogenesis in OSCC and OVC.
