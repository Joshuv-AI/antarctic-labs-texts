Hyperacetylation escalates myocardial susceptibility to ischemia reperfusion injury by mediating mitochondrial supercomplexes assembly in T2DM
Authors: Yan Deng, Wei Yang, Jing Yang, Liwei Ji, Maodi Xie, Kuo Tang, Qinqin He, Guochang Hu, Wangning Shangguan, Chunling Jiang, Tao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) markedly increases susceptibility to myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, contributing to elevated mortality. Sirtuin 3 (Sirt3), a mitochondrial NAD+-dependent deacetylase, is downregulated in T2DM and is closely associated with mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation, possibly as a mediator of this effect. This study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying increased susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury in T2DM, with a particular focus on Sirt3 deficiency-mediated mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation.MethodsWild-type (WT) and Sirt3-knockout (KO) mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for 8 weeks and received intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin (STZ, 50 mg/kg for three consecutive days) beginning at week 5 to establish a T2DM model. Both in vitro and in vivo models were used to examine the effects of Sirt3 deficiency-induced hyperacetylation on mitochondrial function, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and myocardial I/R injury. Mitochondrial supercomplex (SC) assembly and the activities of respiratory chain complexes I, II, III and IV were assessed by blue-native PAGE or a microplate assay kit. Protein-protein interactions were analyzed using proximity ligation assay, and Western blot and functional experiments were performed to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms.ResultsIn T2DM, the Sirt3 deficiency-induced mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation significantly increased myocardial susceptibility to I/R injury. Mitochondrial hyperacetylation impaired mitochondrial respiratory function and increased mitochondrial ROS production. Mechanistically, Sirt3 deficiency-induced hyperacetylation of SC-associated mitochondrial proteins disrupted mitochondrial SC assembly, thereby compromising mitochondrial integrity and function.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that the increased myocardial susceptibility to I/R injury in T2DM is driven, at least in part, by Sirt3 deficiency-mediated hyperacetylation of mitochondrial SC-associated proteins. Disruption of SC assembly leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS accumulation, providing a mechanistic link between metabolic dysregulation and heightened cardiac I/R injury in T2DM.Keywords: Diabetes, Heart, Ischemia-reperfusion injury, Protein acetylation, Mitochondrial supercomplexes, Reactive oxidative species

Introduction

The global prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) continues to increase at an alarming rate, affecting hundreds of millions of individuals worldwide and projected to exceed 700 million by 2045 [1,2]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that T2DM is associated with increased susceptibility to myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury [3,4]. Patients with diabetes exhibit poor recovery following acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [5,6], a phenomenon that has emerged as a major public health concern [[7], [8], [9]]. In addition to rendering the heart more vulnerable to I/R injury, diabetes abolishes or markedly attenuates the efficacy of established cardioprotective strategies, including pharmacological preconditioning, ischemic preconditioning, and ischemic postconditioning [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Collectively, this body of clinical evidence strongly supports the concept that T2DM confers heightened susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury [10,11]. Accordingly, there is an urgent need to develop comprehensive therapeutic strategies and delineate the specific pathological mechanisms underlying ischemic heart disease in patients with T2DM.

The heart, characterized by its exceptionally high energy demand, contains a dense population of mitochondria that function as cellular powerhouses and are essential for maintaining cardiomyocyte and organ homeostasis [14]. As the primary source of ATP and a major generator of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in cardiomyocytes, mitochondria play a central role in the pathogenesis of ischemic cardiomyopathy in the context of T2DM [15,16]. Although excessive oxidative stress resulting from a burst of ROS coupled with insufficient antioxidant defenses has been proposed as a key mechanism underlying the increased susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury in the diabetic heart [17], the upstream causes of mitochondrial dysfunction and increased mitochondrial ROS production in T2DM remain incompletely understood. Mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I, III and IV are organized into higher-order structures known as respiratory supercomplex (SC) within the inner mitochondrial membrane. SC are thought to shorten electron transfer distances, increase respiratory efficiency and reduce ROS production [18,19]. However, the extent to which mitochondrial SC contribute to myocardial susceptibility to I/R injury in T2DM has yet to be fully elucidated.

Sirt3, a mitochondrial NAD+-dependent deacetylase, is localized in the mitochondrial matrix and is downregulated in T2DM mice [20]. Previous studies have demonstrated that Sirt3 regulates diverse cellular processes, including energy metabolism, oxidative stress responses, electron transport chain function, mitochondrial membrane potential maintenance, fatty acid oxidation, and antioxidant pathways [21]. Sirt3 deficiency leads to lysine hyperacetylation, disrupts cell energy homeostasis and exacerbates mitochondrial oxidative stress [22], highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for preserving mitochondrial function and mitigating oxidative damage in T2DM. However, whether Sirt3 deficiency-induced hyperacetylation impairs mitochondrial SC assembly, thereby increasing susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury in diabetic hearts, remains unclear.

In this study, we investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying the increased myocardial susceptibility to I/R injury in T2DM. Our findings demonstrated that Sirt3 deficiency-induced hyperacetylation of SC-associated mitochondrial proteins in T2DM mouse hearts disrupted the assembly of complexes I, III and IV into functional mitochondrial SC. This impairment contributed to mitochondrial dysfunction, excessive ROS accumulation, and ultimately heightened susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the policies of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, China. Sirt3-knockout (KO) mice (C57BL/6J background) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. The mice were housed in a pathogen-free barrier facility (12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water, at a constant temperature of 22 °C. For this study, data were collected from independent biological samples or animals. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and investigators were blinded to the experimental groups during the histological analyses, in vivo functional assessments, and outcome evaluations. All the animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol to relieve pain and suffering prior to any invasive procedure, and the mice were euthanized if any irreversible adverse events occurred. No samples or animals were intentionally excluded from the analyses. Each experiment included appropriate control groups, and all comparative samples were collected and processed under identical conditions.

Experimental diabetic model

Eight-week-old male mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD; D12492, Research Diets), supplemented with (kcal) 20% protein, 20% carbohydrate and 60% fat for eight weeks combined with intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, 50 mg/kg dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer, pH 4.5) for three consecutive days on week 5 to establish a T2DM mouse model. After 8 weeks, blood glucose levels were measured in mouse tail vein blood. Mice were included in the study only if they exhibited hyperglycemia (>11.1 mmol/L) concomitant with impaired glucose tolerance [23,24].

Glucose tolerance test

Mice were fasted for 6 h prior to intraperitoneal administration of a bolus of d-glucose at 1 g/kg body weight (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Blood glucose levels were monitored with a glucometer (Roche, USA) at 0, 15, 30, 60 and 90 min via mouse tail vein blood after injection. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated.

In vitro myocardial ischemia and reperfusion protocol

For the in vitro cardiac I/R study, mice were anesthetized with 1-2% isoflurane and administered heparin (1000 IU/kg body weight) via intraperitoneal injection to prevent coagulation. Following thoracotomy, the hearts were isolated and perfused in the Langendorff model with pyruvate/glucose-containing Krebs-Henseleit buffer under a constant perfusion pressure of 80 mmHg. The perfusate was bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 to yield a pH of 7.4 and maintained at 37.5 °C throughout the experiment. After a 20-min stabilization period, the isolated heart was subjected to global ischemia for 30 min and reperfusion for 1 h. Cardiac function was recorded during the period of measurement. At the end of reperfusion, the hearts were removed from the perfusion cannula for triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining.

In vivo myocardial ischemia and reperfusion protocol

Mice were anesthetized via 3% isoflurane inhalation and mechanically ventilated with a mixture of O2 and 1-2% isoflurane using a rodent ventilator (Kent Scientific, Litchfield, CT). A temperature-controlled surgical table was used to maintain the lateral position of the mouse body temperature at 35-36 °C. A left thoracotomy was performed between the third and fourth ribs, the pericardial tissue was removed, and the left anterior descending coronary artery was visualized and ligated with 7-0 silk sutures below the left atrium using a needle. PE-10 tubing was placed between the silk suture and the LAD before ligation. The PE-10 tubing was removed after occlusion for 30 min, and the chest was closed in layers. The mice were gradually weaned from the ventilator once spontaneous respiration resumed and placed on a securely warmed area until fully conscious. After 24 h of reperfusion, echocardiographic measurements were performed, blood samples were collected for creatine kinase-cardiac isoenzyme (CK-MB) and LDH analysis, and the hearts were harvested for infarct size assessment.

Assessment of cardiac infarct size

At the end of the 24-h reperfusion period, the coronary artery was ligated, and 4 ml of 2% Evans blue was injected into the left ventricular cavity. The hearts were subsequently removed and frozen at −20 °C for 15 min, after which the frozen hearts were rapidly sliced perpendicular to the long axis into five to six 1 mm-thick slices. Afterward, the slices were stained with 1% TTC solution for 10 min at 37 °C. Following overnight fixation in 4% formalin at room temperature, the slices were digitally photographed. The infarct size for each slice was quantified using ImageJ software and is expressed as a percentage of the total cross-sectional area of the heart.

Western blotting

Heart tissue and cultured cells were collected and lysed with ice-cold RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors on ice for 30 min. The lysates were centrifuged to collect the supernatant, and the protein concentration was determined using bicinchoninic acid (BCA; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). Protein samples (20-50 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), and blocked with 5% nonfat milk diluted in TBST. The membrane was incubated overnight with primary antibodies targeting Ndufs4 (1:3000, Abcam), SDHA (1:10000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), MT-CO2 (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), UQCRC2 (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), ATPB (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), SOD2 (1:10000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), FoxO3a (1:2000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Sirt3 (1:4000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), acetylation (1:1000, PTM Bio, Hangzhou, China) or GAPDH (1:10000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) at 4 °C with gentle agitation. After the samples were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:10000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), the signal intensities were visualized with a Bio-Rad infrared imaging system. The signals were quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software (NIH), and the results were normalized to the total protein level of GAPDH or to the mitochondrial protein level of SDHA.

DNP immunoblotting

The protein samples were prepared for DNP immunoblotting according to the protocol provided by the OxyBlot Protein Oxidation Detection Kit (S7150 Sigma). SDS-PAGE was performed according to standard procedures. The proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane, and the membrane was incubated in blocking/dilution buffer for 1 h with gentle shaking. The 1° antibody (1:150) was incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle agitation, and the 2° antibody (1:300) was incubated for 1 h at room temperature the next day. The signal intensities were visualized with a Bio-Rad infrared imaging system. The signals were quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software (NIH), and the results were normalized to the GAPDH protein levels.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from heart tissues or cultured cardiomyocytes using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) according to the manufacturer's guidelines. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using 2 × iTaq™ Universal SYBR-Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) on a CFX Connect Real-time PCR Detection system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.). The primer sequences for Sirt3 (mouse) were as follows: forward (F): 5′-ATCCCGGACTTCAGATCCCC-3′, reverse (R): 5′-CAACATGAAAAAGGGCTTGGG-3’. The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated via the ΔΔCt method and normalized to the expression level of GAPDH.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a 13.0-MHz linear transducer (Vivid 7; GE Medical System). The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (2.5% for induction and 1.0% for maintenance, vol/vol), and the heart rate was maintained between 400 and 500 beats per minute. M-mode echocardiography was used to assess left ventricular systolic function. The left ventricle ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricle fractional shortening (LVFS) and other echocardiography parameters were analyzed using Vevo 2100 software (VisualSonics, Inc.). All echocardiographic procedures were conducted by an investigator who was blinded to the experimental group assignments.

Transmission electron microscopy

The left ventricles of mouse hearts were initially perfused with ice-cold PBS to remove blood and then prefixed with 2.5% (wt/vol.) glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4, 4 °C) for 24 h. Subsequently, heart tissues were post-fixed with 1% (wt/vol.) osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4, 4 °C) for 2 h. The samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in epoxy resin (EMbed-812; Electron Microscopy Sciences, USA) and polymerized at 70 °C overnight. All the images were obtained with a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1230, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Cristae abundance was quantified as total crista length per unit mitochondrial area (μm/μm2).

Plasma creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels

After 24 h of reperfusion, blood samples collected in EDTA-containing tubes were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube for the analysis of CK-MB and LDH activity. The levels of CK-MB and LDH were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Multiskan MK3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

Adeno-associated virus 9 preparation and administration

For the Sirt3 overexpression experiments, adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9)-Sirt3 or AAV9-negative control (NC) was purchased from VectorBuilder (Guangzhou, China). Eight-week-old C57/BL6J male mice were administered AAV9-Sirt3 or AAV9-NC via tail vein injection at a dose of 5 × 1011 viral genomes (VG) per mouse prior to the induction of the T2DM model.

Cell culture and treatment

The H9C2 cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA) and cultured in low-glucose DMEM (HyClone Laboratories, Inc.) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biological Industries), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 mg/mL streptomycin (HyClone Laboratories, Inc.) in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After 24 h, the cells were transduced with a Sirt3 knockdown adenovirus (Adv-Sirt3KD) or a control adenovirus (Adv-EGFP, VectorBuilder Inc.) for 48 h according to the manufacturer's instructions. H9C2 cells were subsequently cultured under normal conditions or exposed to palmitate (PA; 0.2 mM) or nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN; 2 mM) for an additional 24 h. The oxygen consumption rate (OCR), mitochondrial membrane potential (assessed by JC-1 staining), and extent of apoptosis were measured following the 24-h treatment period.

Mitochondrial membrane potential

Immunofluorescence assays were used to assess mitochondrial membrane potential by JC-1 staining in H9C2 cells. The cells were seeded in 24-well white plates following the indicated treatments. The plates were subsequently washed twice with PBS and incubated at room temperature for 10 min with 10 μg/mL JC-1 reagent. The fluorescence images were obtained with a Zeiss Axio Observer Z1 fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss). Red fluorescence was detected at an excitation wavelength of 550 nm and an emission wavelength of 600 nm, and green fluorescence was detected at 485 nm excitation and 535 nm emission. The fluorescence was quantified with ImageJ software (NIH), and the results are expressed as the ratio of red to green fluorescence.

Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate

The OCR of isolated intact mitochondria was analyzed using a Seahorse XFe24 analyzer (Agilent Seahorse Bioscience, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Freshly isolated cardiac mitochondria were suspended in mitochondrial assay solution (MAS; pH 7.2). The mitochondrial samples (5 μg) were transferred to an XFe24 microplate and centrifuged at 2000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, all the wells were filled with MAS to a final volume of 500 μL containing pyruvate/malate (10 mM/5 mM) to support complex I-dependent respiration. The microplate was incubated in a CO2-free incubator at 37 °C for 10 min and then loaded onto the XFe24 Analyzer. The OCR was measured following sequential injections of ADP (4 mM), oligomycin (2.5 μg/mL), carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP, 4 μM), and antimycin A (4 μM) at 37 °C. The respiratory rates are expressed as oxygen flux per unit mitochondrial mass and are normalized to the mitochondrial protein content (pmol O2/min/μg mito).

To measure the OCR of H9C2 cells, the cells were seeded in Seahorse XF24 microplates at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells per well on the first day. On the second day, the cells were washed twice with assay buffer, after which 500 μL of fresh assay buffer was added. The plates were then incubated in a CO2-free incubator for 1 h at 37 °C. OCR was measured using a Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer, with three baseline and postinjection readings recorded following the sequential addition of oligomycin (5 μM), FCCP (3 μM), and antimycin A/rotenone (1 μM/1 μM). Data are normalized to 10,000 cells and expressed as pmol O2/min/cells∗104.

CM-H2DCFDA staining

To measure ROS formation, the fluorescent probe CM-H2DCFDA was used. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded 5-μm thick tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a graded series of alcohols. The sections were subsequently subjected to antigen retrieval and blocked with 5% horse serum for 1 h at 37 °C. After being washed three times with PBST, the sections were stained with 5 μM CM-H2DCFDA in PBS for 30 min at 37 °C in a humidified chamber protected from light. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL). All sections were examined via a fluorescence microscope (AX10 imager A2/AX10 cam HRC, Zeiss) and images were captured using ZEN software (Zeiss). For each section, six images were acquired from randomly selected fields of view. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software (NIH). A representative image from each group was selected to reflect the mean or median fluorescence intensity of the group based on quantitative assessment.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

A PLA was performed using the DuoLink® proximity test kit (Sigma Duo92101). H9C2 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with Duolink® blocking solution at 37 °C for 1 h. H9C2 cells were then incubated with primary antibodies against Ndufs4 (1:50, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) and UQCRC2 (1:200) or Ndufs4 and MT-CO2 (1:200) at 4 °C overnight. After being washed three times with wash buffer A, the H9C2 cells were further incubated with anti-rabbit PLUS and anti-mouse MINUS PLA probes (diluted 1:4) for 1 h at 37 °C. After the samples were washed three times with wash buffer A, the ligation reaction was performed at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by amplification at 37 °C for 100 min. The H9C2 cells were then washed with wash buffer B and incubated with DAPI (1 μg/ml) at room temperature for 15 min in the dark. PLA signals were captured with an Olympus automatic inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX83) and analyzed with ImageJ software (NIH).

Detection of mitochondrial ROS

Mitochondrial ROS were detected using the MitoSOX fluorescent probe (5 μM, Invitrogen, Life Technologies Corporation). The collected heart tissue was briefly fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. The tissues were then sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices using a Leica cryostat at −20 °C. Frozen sections were equilibrated at room temperature for 30 min, Antigen retrieval was subsequently performed, and the sections were blocked with 5% horse serum for 1 h at 37 °C. After being washed three times with PBST, the sections were stained with 5 μM MitoSOX for 15 min at 37 °C in a humidified chamber away from light and then washed three times with PBST. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL). Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Observer D1/Axio Cam HRC; Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

To assess ROS formation in H9C2 cells, the fluorescent probe MitoSOX (5 μM, Invitrogen, Life Technologies Corporation) was used. After undergoing the corresponding experimental treatments, the H9C2 cells were washed three times with PBS, incubated with 5 μM MitoSOX for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark and then washed three times with sterile PBS. H9C2 cells were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst (10 μg/mL). Fluorescence images were captured using an Olympus IX83 automated inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed with ImageJ software (NIH).

Isolation of cardiac mitochondria

Mitochondria were isolated from freshly excised mouse hearts. The mouse hearts were rapidly removed, rinsed with ice-cold PBS to remove residual blood, and minced with a blade under mitochondrial extraction buffer containing 0.1 mg/mL trypsin (sigma T1426). After the samples were incubated on ice for 10 min, a trypsin inhibitor (0.5 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich T9128) was added to terminate the enzymatic digestion. The tissue homogenate was then transferred to a glass-Teflon homogenizer and homogenized using a Teflon pestle in mitochondrial extraction buffer. Mitochondria were pelleted by centrifugation at 8000×g for 10 min at 4 °C and collected for further use.

Blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE)

Freshly isolated mitochondrial samples were prepared for analysis. A total of 50 μg of mitochondria was solubilized with 5 μL of sample buffer (BN2003, Invitrogen), 4 μL of 10% digitonin (D141, Sigma) and 11 μL of water. Before loading the samples, 2 μL of Coomassie G-250 (35050, Serva) was added to the sample mixture. BN-PAGE was performed by loading the samples onto a 3-12% gradient gel and running them at 4 °C. Electrophoresis was carried out using light blue cathode buffer (BN2007, Invitrogen) at 150 V for 60 min, followed by clear cathode buffer at 250 V for 90 min.

For immunoblotting, proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane at 4 °C for 2 h and blocked with 5% nonfat milk diluted in TBST. The membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibody solution at 4 °C with gentle agitation. After being washed, the membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG secondary antibody (SA00001-2, 1:10000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 60 min at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH). The results were normalized to the SDHA protein levels in the mitochondria.

Mitochondrial electron transport chain complex activity assays

To assess the in-gel activities of complexes I, II and IV, the gels were incubated in 20 mL of complex I substrate (5 mM Tris pH 7.4, 0.1 mg/mL NADH, and 2.5 mg/mL NTB), complex II substrate (5 mM Tris pH 7.4, 2.5 mg/mL NTB, 20 mM sodium succinate, 0.2 mM phenazine methosulfate), and complex IV substrate (50 mM NaH2PO4 pH 7.2, 0.5 mg/mL DAB, 1 mg/mL cytochrome c) for 20 min at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 10% acetic acid for 10 min. Images were obtained with a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad). The appearance of violet bands is indicative of complexes I, II, and IV activities.

Given the absence of a validated in-gel colorimetric assay for complex III activity under native BN-PAGE conditions, its enzymatic activity was determined by a commercial microplate assay kit (BC3245, Solarbio) followed the manufacturer's instructions using Varioskan™ LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Statistical analysis

The software GraphPad Prism v.8.4.0 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA) was used to perform the statistical analysis. The Shapiro-Wilk normality test was used to determine the distribution of the data. For normally distributed data, values are presented as the means ± SEMs. Unpaired 2-tailed Student's t tests were used to compare two groups, and Tukey's multiple comparisons test was conducted after one-way ANOVA to determine differences among more than two groups. In the case of nonnormally distributed data (n < 5), values are shown as medians and interquartile ranges. For two groups, the Mann-Whitney test was applied, and for more than two groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparisons test was utilized. For experiments with >1 variable, repeated measures two-way ANOVA with Sidak's multiple comparisons was used. No experiment-wide/across-test multiple test correction was applied. When multiple comparisons were tested, multiplicity-adjusted P values are presented. Otherwise, raw P values are reported. P values < 0.05 were considered to indicated statistical significance.

Results

Mitochondrial hyperacetylation induced by Sirt3 deficiency in T2DM increases myocardial susceptibility to I/R injury

As shown in Fig. S1A, T2DM models were established via an 8-week HFD combined with intraperitoneal injection of STZ (50 mg/kg) at week 5 for three consecutive days. Consistent with previous findings [25,26], compared with those in control hearts, the protein levels of Sirt3 in T2DM hearts were reduced, whereas mitochondrial protein acetylation levels were significantly elevated (Fig. S1B). To assess the role of Sirt3 in T2DM mouse hearts, we established a T2DM model in Sirt3-konckout (KO) mice. Compared with wild-type (WT)-T2DM mice, KO-T2DM mice presented higher body weights and more severe glucose intolerance (Fig. S1C–S1F). To further investigate whether Sirt3 deficiency contributes to the increased susceptibility of the hearts to I/R injury in T2DM in vitro, the hearts were isolated and perfused using the Langendorff system, followed by 30 min of global ischemia and, immediately, 60 min of reperfusion. Compared with WT hearts, T2DM and KO hearts exhibited a more pronounced increase in end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) during ischemia, minimal recovery of contractile function during reperfusion, and a larger infarct size; these effects were further exacerbated in the KO-T2DM hearts (Fig. 1A–D).

To determine whether Sirt3 was responsible for the increased susceptibility of T2DM hearts to I/R injury in vivo, the mice were subjected to 30 min of LAD ligation followed by 24 h of reperfusion. Similarly, compared with WT mice, T2DM and KO mice had a lower LVEF, a larger infarct size, and elevated plasma levels of LDH and CK-MB, and these effects were significantly aggravated in KO- T2DM mice (Fig. 1E–H). Taken together, these findings indicate that mitochondrial hyperacetylation induced by Sirt3 deficiency in T2DM increases myocardial susceptibility to I/R injury.

Mitochondrial hyperacetylation in T2DM hearts impairs mitochondrial respiratory function

Mitochondria play a critical role in maintaining cardiac function [27,28]. To assess mitochondrial integrity and respiratory function, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed to examine mitochondrial morphology, and a Seahorse XFe24 Flux Analyzer was used to measure the mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in the hearts of T2DM mice. TEM analysis of the T2DM and KO myocardial ultrastructure revealed increased mitochondrial swelling and reduced cristae density compared with those of the WT, suggesting that the myocardial mitochondria were damaged (Fig. 2A and B). Moreover, glutamate/malate-supported complex I respiration was reduced in T2DM and KO hearts compared with WT hearts, as evidenced by decreased ADP-stimulated mitochondrial OCR and FCCP-stimulated OCR (Fig. 2C–E). As expected, compared with T2DM and KO, KO-T2DM resulted in more severe mitochondrial pathological alterations and mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction (Fig. 2A–E). To mimic the in vitro conditions of cardiomyocytes, H9C2 cells were transduced with Sirt3 knockdown adenovirus for 48 h and then exposed to palmitic acid (PA) for 24 h. Compared with the Ctrl-Adv-EGFP cells, the PA-Adv-EGFP and Adv-Sirt3KD cells exhibited significantly reduced spare respiratory capacity and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, whereas these effects were further exacerbated in the PA-Adv-Sirt3KD cells (Fig. 2F–I). These findings suggest that mitochondrial hyperacetylation induced by Sirt3 deficiency impairs mitochondrial respiratory function in T2DM hearts.

Mitochondrial hyperacetylation leads to excessive ROS production and an inefficient antioxidant response in T2DM hearts

Given that mitochondrial dysfunction can promote the generation and excessive accumulation of ROS, leading to oxidative damage [29], we next examined the ROS levels in our experimental models. We found that protein carbonylation, an irreversible form of oxidative protein modification, was increased in the hearts of T2DM and KO mice compared with WT mice, and that protein carbonylation was further enhanced in KO-T2DM mice (Fig. 3A and B). The fluorescence intensity of DCFDA in cardiac tissue was increased in the T2DM and KO groups, with a more pronounced increase observed in the hearts of the KO-T2DM mice (Fig. 3C and D). Similarly, the MitoSOX fluorescence intensity in cardiac tissue increased in the T2DM and KO groups, with a more pronounced increase observed in the hearts of the KO-T2DM mice (Fig. 3E and F). In H9C2 cells, the MitoSOX fluorescence intensity was greater in PA-Adv-EGFP and Adv-Sirt3KD-treated cells than in Ctrl-Adv-EGFP-treated cells, whereas the fluorescence intensity was further enhanced in PA-Adv-Sirt3KD-treated cells (Fig. 3G and H). ROS accumulation is also closely associated with mitochondrial antioxidant function [30,31]. Next, we examined the effects of Sirt3 on mitochondrial antioxidants. We detected the expression of FoxO3a and SOD2, which are proteins involved in oxidative stress resistance. We found that T2DM and KO mouse hearts presented significantly decreased expression levels of FoxO3a and SOD2 compared with those of WT mice, which were further decreased in KO-T2DM mice (Fig. 3I–K). Taken together, these findings indicate that mitochondrial hyperacetylation induced by Sirt3 deficiency increases mitochondrial ROS production and impairs the antioxidant response in the hearts of T2DM mice.

T2DM-induced Sirt3 deficiency impairs mitochondrial function and promotes ROS accumulation by inhibiting SC assembly

Given that SC, which is primarily composed of complex I (CI), complex III (CIII) and complex IV (CIV), have been shown to increase the efficiency of electron transfer and reduce ROS production [18], we investigated whether Sirt3 deficiency inhibits SC assembly. We first analyzed the expression levels of OXPHOS-related proteins, including Ndufs4 (CI), SDHA (CII), UQCRC2 (CIII), MT-CO2 (CIV) and ATPB (CV), and the results revealed comparable expression levels in cardiac tissues from the WT, KO, T2DM and KO-T2DM groups (Fig. 4A). These data indicate that Sirt3 deficiency may impair the assembly of mitochondrial SC without affecting the expression of mitochondrial respiratory chain (MRC) complex proteins. CI is essential for cardiac mitochondrial OXPHOS and is regarded as the primary site of ROS production within MRCs [32]. Ndufs4 incorporation into SC I + III + IV was reduced in heart mitochondria from T2DM and KO mice, with the most pronounced reduction observed in KO-T2DM mice, as demonstrated by blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE) followed by immunoblotting (Fig. 4B). Consistent with the expression of Ndufs4 in SC, the levels of UQCRC2 and MT-CO2 in SC were also lower in mitochondria from T2DM and KO mouse hearts than in those from WT mice and further decreased in those from KO-T2DM mice (Fig. 4C). The activities of CI, CIII and CIV in mitochondria isolated from T2DM and KO mouse hearts were lower than those in WT mouse hearts and were further reduced in KO-T2DM mouse hearts (Fig. 4D, F and 4G). However, there was no difference in the in-gel activity of CII among the four groups (Fig. 4E). Taken together, our data suggest that T2DM-induced Sirt3 deficiency impairs the proper assembly of mitochondrial SC and decreases CI, CIII and CIV activities in SC, which is critical for mitochondrial function and ROS production.

Deacetylation of mitochondria restores SC formation, decreases mitochondrial ROS levels, improves mitochondrial respiratory function, and attenuates myocardial I/R injury in T2DM mice

Sirt3 is a key mitochondrial deacetylase, and we hypothesize that Sirt3 deficiency impairs the assembly of SC by modulating their acetylation levels. We therefore examined the acetylation level of SC through BN-PAGE followed by immunoblotting and found that it was elevated in T2DM and KO mouse hearts, with a further increase observed in KO-T2DM mice (Fig. 5A). Additionally, we confirmed that mitochondrial acetylation affected the interactions among CI, CIII and CIV via a proximity ligation assay (PLA) in H9C2 cells. We observed that the number of red puncta reflecting CI–CIII or CI–CIV interactions was decreased in PA- and 3-TYP (Sirt3 inhibitor)-treated cells, but was restored in cells cotreated with PA and NMN (NAD+ precursor) (Fig. 5B and C). Our results revealed that hyperacetylation decreased the interactions among the CI, CIII, and CIV subunits, thereby reducing SC assembly. To explore whether mitochondrial complex acetylation (LysAc) affects SC assembly in vivo, we sought to reduce LysAc by supplementation with nicotinamide riboside (NR), a precursor of the sirtuin cofactor NAD+, for 4 weeks in T2DM group; WT-NR mice were used as the control group. As a result, mitochondrial complex protein acetylation was increased in T2DM mouse hearts compared with those of WT or WT-NR mice, but it was restored in T2DM-NR mice (Fig. 5D). The expression levels of CI, CIII and CIV in SC, as well as the activities of CI, CIII and CIV, were lower in T2DM mouse hearts than in those of WT or WT-NR mice; however, these parameters were restored in T2DM-NR mice compared with those of T2DM mice (Fig. 5F–H, 5J and 5K). However, NR-treatment did not affect the overall expression of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex proteins (Fig. 5E) or CII in-gel activity (Fig. 5I). Considering these data, we demonstrated that the deacetylation of mitochondrial complexes restored SC formation.

Furthermore, we evaluated the effects of NR treatment on mitochondrial ROS production and mitochondrial respiratory function. As expected, the protein expression of DNP and the fluorescence intensity of DCFDA were increased in T2DM mouse hearts compared with those in WT or WT-NR mouse hearts, but these changes were reversed in T2DM-NR mice compared with those in T2DM mice (Fig. S2A–S2D). The expression levels of the antioxidant proteins FoxO3a and SOD2 were reduced (Fig. S2E and S2F) and mitochondrial structure and respiratory function were impaired (Fig. S2G and S2H) in T2DM mouse hearts compared with those in WT or WT-NR mice, whereas these parameters were restored in T2DM-NR mice relative to those in T2DM mice (Fig. S2E–S2H). Moreover, NMN treatment also reduced MitoSOX fluorescence intensity (Fig. S2C and S2D) and restored mitochondrial membrane potential in PA-treated H9C2 cells (Fig. S2I and S2J), but the difference was not significant between the control and NMN groups (Fig. S2C and S2D, S2I and S2J).

Moreover, compared with T2DM mice, T2DM-NR mice exhibited decreased fasting glucose levels, improved glucose intolerance without changes in body weight (Fig. S3A–S3D), improved cardiac systolic function without changes in heart rate (Fig. S3I–S3K), decreased cardiac infarct size (Fig. S3E and S3F) and lower plasma cardiac enzyme levels (Fig. S3G and S3H) after myocardial I/R injury, and no differences in any of the above indicators were detected between the WT and WT-NR groups (Fig. S3A–S3K). Collectively, these data suggest that mitochondrial deacetylation decreases mitochondrial ROS production, restores mitochondrial respiratory function, and mitigates myocardial I/R injury in T2DM mouse hearts.

Cardiac-specific Sirt3 overexpression improves SC formation, reduces mitochondrial ROS levels, restores mitochondrial respiratory function and attenuates I/R injury in T2DM mouse hearts

To further understand the role of Sirt3-regulated mitochondrial acetylation in SC formation and I/R injury in T2DM mouse hearts, we generated AAV9 vectors encoding Sirt3 and administered them via tail vein injection. At 4 weeks after injection, the transduction efficiency was confirmed by assessing EGFP expression and Sirt3 expression levels (Fig. S4A–S4C). Compared with T2DM-AAV9-NC mice, T2DM-AAV9-Sirt3 mice exhibited decreased mitochondrial SC protein acetylation levels, increased expression levels of CI, CIII and CIV in SC, enhanced activities of CI, CIII and CIV, upregulated expression levels of the antioxidant proteins FoxO3a and SOD2, reduced DNP protein expression, and diminished MitoSOX fluorescence intensity. No significant differences in these indicators were observed between WT and WT-AAV9-Sirt3 groups (Fig. 6A, 6C-6E and 6G-6M). There were no differences among the groups in terms of the expression of OXPHOS-related proteins in the hearts or the in-gel activity of CII in hearts mitochondria (Fig. 6B and F).

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate that T2DM-mediated Sirt3 deficiency promotes hyperacetylation of SC-associated mitochondrial proteins, thereby disrupting SC assembly. This impairment leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive mitochondrial ROS production, ultimately increasing susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury. Our study investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying increased susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury in T2DM and revealed enhanced mitochondrial SC assembly as a potential therapeutic target.

T2DM is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease. Myocardial I/R injury in patients with T2DM is associated with an increased incidence of complications and mortality [33]. Accumulating evidence indicates that Sirt3 expression is reduced in T2DM hearts [[34], [35], [36]]. Given that Sirt3 deficiency increases the acetylation of multiple mitochondrial proteins, it plays a critical role in regulating myocardial function and modulating the severity of cardiac injury [37,38]. Consistent with these observations, we found that Sirt3 deficiency in T2DM hearts increases susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury and exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction, accompanied by marked increases in mitochondrial protein acetylation. Furthermore, our results demonstrate that reducing mitochondrial protein acetylation through supplementation with an NAD+ precursor decreases the acetylation of SC-associated proteins, promotes SC assembly, restores mitochondrial function and attenuates myocardial I/R injury in T2DM. Together, these findings provide new evidence that hyperacetylation of mitochondrial SC contributes to the increased susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury in diabetic hearts. We acknowledge that a limitation of NAD+ precursor supplementation is that NAD+ repletion also influences additional metabolic pathways, including substrate oxidation and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor signaling in the heart [39], which may independently affect susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury.

Moreover, the data indicate that although Sirt3 deficiency alone induces mitochondrial and myocardial dysfunction compared with WT controls, the presence of T2DM further exacerbates these abnormalities even in Sirt3-deficient mice. This observation likely reflects two inherent limitations of global Sirt3 KO models. First, developmental compensatory mechanisms may be activated during the transition from the embryonic stage to the adult stage, thereby modulating cardiac physiological responses, such as AMPK-mediated enhancement of fatty acid oxidation and glucose uptake or PI3K/Akt-driven promotion of cell survival [[40], [41], [42]]. Second, systemic organ crosstalk may contribute to the observed myocardial phenotypes, as Sirt3 deficiency-induced hepatic and renal dysfunction can influence cardiac function through neurohumoral regulation, introducing confounding factors that obscure the direct impact of T2DM on myocardial mitochondrial injury. To elucidate the direct role of Sirt3 in this process, we overexpressed cardiac-specific Sirt3 in T2DM mice using AAV9. The restoration of Sirt3 expression significantly reversed mitochondrial SC assembly, reduced oxidative stress, and ameliorated myocardial I/R injury. Collectively, these findings establish Sirt3 as a critical mediator of T2DM-induced myocardial dysfunction and underscore its role independent of the developmental and systemic compensatory effects inherent to global Sirt3 KO models.

The formation of mitochondrial SC is essential for efficient electron transport and the regulation of ROS production [32,[43], [44], [45]]. Excessive ROS production leads to oxidative damage to cellular components, which contributes to myocardial injury during I/R [46,47]. Previous studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial complex I is predominantly incorporated into SC and plays a central role in controlling ROS production [32]. Consistent with these observations, our results revealed that Sirt3 deficiency-induced hyperacetylation of SC-associated mitochondrial proteins disrupts SC assembly and reduces the activities of complexes I, III and IV, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and increased ROS production in T2DM. In contrast, both our in vitro and in vivo data show that the deacetylation of SC-associated mitochondrial proteins enhanced the interactions among complexes I, III, and IV, restored mitochondrial respiration and membrane potential, and reduced ROS generation. These findings underscore the importance of mitochondrial protein deacetylation in promoting SC assembly, thereby improving mitochondrial oxidative efficiency and limiting excessive ROS production.

Given that mitochondrial SC assembly and complex I activity, the key determinants of efficient electron transport and ROS homeostasis, are regulated by the acetylation status of mitochondrial proteins, targeting mitochondrial protein acetylation or directly promoting mitochondrial SC assembly may represent a novel therapeutic strategy to mitigate myocardial I/R injury, particularly in high-risk populations such as patients with diabetes. Such approaches may include the use of Sirt3 activators or pharmacological agents that enhance the deacetylation of mitochondrial proteins. Additionally, mitochondria-targeted antioxidants may be explored as potential therapeutic agents for protection against myocardial I/R injury in diabetes.

While this study provides important mechanistic insights, several questions remain to be addressed. First, our study was primarily designed to elucidate the mechanisms by which T2DM increases susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury. Accordingly, we employed a combined HFD and STZ protocol to establish a T2DM model, without systematically examining the individual contributions of a HFD or STZ alone. Whether the mechanisms identified here are also operative in models of obesity induced solely by a HFD or in individuals with type 1 diabetes induced exclusively by STZ administration warrants further investigation. Second, further research is needed to identify the specific mitochondrial proteins affected by hyperacetylation and to elucidate their roles in SC assembly and function. Furthermore, exploring the interplay between Sirt3 and other mitochondrial regulatory pathways may provide a more comprehensive understanding of mitochondrial homeostasis and dysfunction in diabetic cardiomyopathy. Finally, determining whether similar mechanisms are operative in human diabetic hearts will be essential for the translation of these findings into clinically relevant therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

Our study provides evidence that the increased susceptibility to myocardial I/R injury in T2DM is driven, at least in part, by Sirt3 deficiency-mediated hyperacetylation of mitochondrial SC-associated proteins. This mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation disrupts SC assembly, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive ROS accumulation, thereby establishing a mechanistic link between metabolic dysregulation and heightened myocardial I/R injury in diabetic hearts.
