Comprehensive genomic characterization of herpes B virus isolated from a wild long-tailed macaque reveals conserved entry mechanisms and zoonotic potential
Authors: Weena Paungpin, Somjit Chaiwattanarungruengpaisan, Metawee Thongdee, Ladawan Sariya, Krittiga Sapkanarak, Taratorn Kemthong, Worawat Dangsagul, Sarin Suwanpakdee, Witthawat Wiriyarat, Suchinda Malaivijitnond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHerpes B virus (BV; Macacine alphaherpesvirus 1) is a zoonotic alphaherpesvirus endemic in macaques that can cause severe, often fatal encephalomyelitis in humans. However, genomic data from wild macaque populations remain limited. Here, we report the isolation and genomic characterization of a BV strain from a wild long-tailed macaque (Macaca fascicularis) in Thailand and assess its zoonotic potential. Among 138 macaques screened across six populations, BV shedding was detected in a single oral swab (0.72%). The virus (BV/Thailand/Ranong_TPK5/2024) was successfully isolated and propagated in Vero and LLC-MK2 cells, with higher replication efficiency observed in Vero cells. The TPK5 isolate exhibited a high infectious titre (8 × 10⁷ PFU/mL), and transmission electron microscopy confirmed typical herpesvirus morphology. Whole-genome sequencing achieved deep coverage (∼20,974×) and revealed 98.4% identity to a reference strain (E90–136) from captive long-tailed macaques. Phylogenetic analyses of 12 envelope glycoproteins demonstrated host-associated clustering, with the TPK5 isolate grouping within the long-tailed macaque lineage. Key entry glycoproteins (gB, gD, gH, and gL) were highly conserved among macaques but diverged from human herpes simplex viruses. In contrast, host receptors nectin-1 and nectin-2 were highly conserved between macaques and humans (≥95% identity), suggesting preserved receptor compatibility. Despite the low prevalence of shedding, the isolation of an infectious BV strain from an asymptomatic macaque highlights an ongoing zoonotic risk. Conservation of viral entry mechanisms and host receptors supports the potential for cross-species transmission in high human–macaque interface settings.

Introduction

Herpes B virus (BV, Macacine alphaherpesvirus 1 or Simplexvirus macacinealpha 1) is an enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus in the family Herpesviridae, with icosapentahedral virion of about 200 nm in diameter [1]. Its genome ranges from 155 to 157 kbp and encodes proteins involved in viral infection of host cells [2,3]. BV genome structure and antigenicity are similar to those of human herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) and HSV-2 [4]. The process by which BV enters host cells, particularly in humans, is not yet fully understood. Like HSV, BV entry relies on cell fusion between viral envelope glycoproteins (g) and host cell membranes. Four of the 12 envelope proteins, i.e. gB, gD, gH, and gL, mediate this process. The gD binds to specific receptors, such as nectin-1 or nectin-2, on host cells, triggering a conformational change that signals through the gH/gL heterodimer to activate gB for fusion. This coordinated complex enables the viral capsid to enter host cells [5–7].

Zoonotic BV was first identified in 1932, when a physician was bitten by a healthy rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta) during experimental work and died 15 days later from progressive encephalomyelitis [8,9]. To date, 50 human cases have been reported, and 21 patients have died. Human BV infection primarily affected personnel who were exposed to captive macaques [10]. Recently, a potential zoonotic spillover from wild macaques to humans was reported when a man who had direct contact with wild macaques in a country park in Hong Kong became infected with the BV [11]. Although human BV cases are rare, they can be fatal if untreated.

BV mainly infects macaques, especially rhesus and long-tailed macaques (M. fascicularis), which are increasingly utilized in biomedical research [12–14]. As a result, most reported cases of zoonotic BV transmission between captive macaques and humans involve these two species. BV infection in the natural macaque host is typically asymptomatic or mild. Transmission between animals or between animals and humans occurs through direct contact, such as bites, scratches, or sexual reproduction (between animals), or exposure to bodily secretions (saliva or urine) at the mucosal surfaces of infected animals [12,15].

Previous studies demonstrated a high BV seroprevalence in wild rhesus macaques, with rates of 72% in Puerto Rico [16], 64% in Nepal [17], and 82% in Cayo Santiago [18]. Thus, the 13 whole-genome sequences of the BV isolated from captive rhesus macaques have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database [2]. Although the high BV seroprevalence in wild long-tailed macaques (for example, 81.6% in Indonesia [19], and 25%−100% in Thailand [20]) was reported, only one full sequence of BV isolated from a captive long-tailed macaque has been deposited in NCBI. No BV sequence from wild animals is currently available.

The long-tailed macaque is the most frequently encountered and interacted with by humans in urban areas of Southeast Asia, particularly in Thailand, where macaque bites or scratches are often reported [21]. We previously surveyed BV shedding in 864 wild long-tailed macaques across Thailand and detected it in 1.04% of cases [20], while the infectivity of the BV and the risk of zoonotic transmission to humans have not been assessed. Thus, this study aims to isolate the BV from wild Thai long-tailed macaques, culture it in cell lines, perform genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis of the 12 envelope glycoproteins, and deduce the amino acid sequences of the 4 envelope glycoproteins known to be involved with entry into host cells. Furthermore, the amino acid sequences of nectin-1 and nectin-2 from the long-tailed macaque, other macaque hosts, and humans were retrieved from the NCBI database and analysed for sequence similarity.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The Department of National Parks, Wildlife, and Plant Conservation of Thailand approved the capture and collection of specimens from wild long-tailed macaques. The National Primate Research Center of Thailand, Animal Care and Use Committee, approved all animal procedures (protocol no. 2475006). The Faculty of Veterinary Science, Mahidol University, Animal Care and Use Committee approved all animal sample uses (protocol no. MUVS-2024-07-44). The Institutional Biosafety Committee of Mahidol University approved all BV experiments (protocol no. MU 2024-006).

Study sites and specimen collections

We investigated BV shedding in 138 wild long-tailed macaques from 6 populations in central and southern Thailand (Table 1). We captured animals between May and September 2024, identified each animal’s sex, and estimated their age based on dental eruption patterns [22]. We collected oral, urogenital, and rectal swab samples using sterile ultrafine flock swabs and separately preserved each sample in 2 preservation tubes. One was 1.0 mL of DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research, CA, USA) used for BV genome detection by qPCR [23], and the other was 1.0 mL of viral transport media, used for BV isolation by cell culture. We kept all swab samples on ice during transportation to the laboratory within 48 h and stored them at −80°C until use. Detection of BV shedding by qPCR

We extracted DNA from swab samples using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), detected the BV UL29 genes using qPCR as described previously [20,23], and selected the positive sample for virus isolation in cell culture. We established a qPCR standard curve using 10-fold serial dilutions of the UL29 positive control and calculated BV DNA copy numbers for each sample.

Bv isolation by cell culture

Due to the lack of information on BV isolates from wild long-tailed macaques, we utilized two different types of cell lines in this study: (1) Vero cells, which are derived from the kidney of the African green monkey (Cercopithecus aethiops) (ATCC CCL-81, VA, USA), and (2) LLC-MK2 cells, which are derived from the kidney of the rhesus macaque (ATCC CCL-7, VA, USA). We cultured each cell line in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 100 IU/mL of penicillin, 100 µg/mL of streptomycin, and 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone, Cytiva, UT, USA). We seeded each cell line into 12-well plates the day before isolation to allow cell attachment. We inoculated 250 µL of the processed oral swab sample into duplicate wells and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 2 h to allow virus absorption, while the control well was inoculated with 250 µL of sterile DMEM only. We washed the inoculated cells with DMEM, then added 1 mL/well of fresh maintenance medium and incubated the plates at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 7 days. We observed the BV-like cytopathic effect (CPE) daily.

Bv titration by plaque assay

We determined the BV isolate titre by plaque assay. We plated Vero cells at a density of 3.5 × 105 per well in 12-well plates. We performed 10-fold serial dilutions of the BV isolate and incubated the cells with the diluted virus at 37°C for 2 h. We overlaid the cell monolayers with a final concentration of 1.2% Avicel RC-581 agar (Dupont, International Flavors & Fragrances, DE, USA) and 2% fetal bovine serum. We then incubated the plates at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 3 days. We subsequently removed the agar overlays from the cell monolayers, fixed them with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained them with 1% crystal violet. We counted plaques and calculated plaque-forming units (PFU) per millilitre.

Bv particle by transmission electron microscope (TEM)

We infected Vero cells with BV isolates at a multiplicity of infection (ratio of infectious virus particles to Vero cell culture) of 0.003 for 3 days. We collected the infected Vero cells and centrifuged them at 1000 ×g for 20 min at 4°C. We fixed the cell pellets with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h at 4°C, then with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h at room temperature. We dehydrated the fixed cell pellets with a graded ethanol series, infiltrated and embedded them in LR White Resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, PA, USA). We then polymerized the embedded samples at 65°C for 48 h, cut them into 100-nm-thick sections, and stained them with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. We examined the grid under a TEM to observe the structure of the BV particles.

Whole genome sequencing of BV

We extracted BV DNA from the culture supernatant using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit. We constructed the sequencing libraries following the manufacturer’s protocol using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, MA, USA). We sequenced the libraries on a HiSeq platform (Illumina, CA, USA) with a 2 × 150-base read length. We first assessed the quality of the raw next-generation sequencing data in FASTQ format using FastQC [24]. We filtered and trimmed the low-quality reads and adapter sequences using fastp [25] and aligned the clean reads to the reference genome KJ566591.2 using minimap2 [26]. The resulting alignment files were converted to BAM format and sorted with Samtools [27]. We then generated the consensus sequence from the alignments using iVar [28] and finally evaluated the mapping statistics and alignment quality with Qualimap [29].

Phylogenetic analysis of BV envelope glycoprotein sequences

Twelve envelope glycoprotein-coding sequences of BV isolate consisting of gB (UL27), gC (UL44), gD (US6), gE (US8), gG (US4), gH (UL22), gI (US7), gJ (US5), gK (UL53), gL (UL1), gM (UL10), and gN (UL49.5) were compared against reference sequences deposited in the NCBI database including rhesus macaque (GenBank accession nos. KY628984.1, KY628974.1, KY628981.1, KY628976.1, KY628975.1, KY628979.1, KY628977.1, KY628978.1, KY628980.1, KY628973.1, KY628971.1, KY628972.1), Japanese macaque (M. fuscata: KY628982.1), long-tailed macaque (KJ566591.2), bonnet macaque (M. radiata: KY628985.1), southern pig-tailed macaque (M. nemestrina: KY628969.1 and NC_076165.1) and lion-tailed macaque (M. silenus: NC_076164.1). We performed nucleotide sequence alignment and sequence identity calculations in BioEdit Sequence Alignment Editor version 7.7.1.0 [30]. We constructed a phylogenetic tree of whole-genome sequences from BV isolates using Bayesian inference implemented in BEAST X version 10.5.0 [31]. The XML configuration file was generated using BEAUti [32]. The Hasegawa–Kishino–Yano (HKY) substitution model was selected based on the Bayesian information criterion in MEGA version 5.2. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analyses were run for 10 million generations with sampling every 1,000 generations. Convergence and effective sample sizes (ESS > 200) were assessed using Tracer version 1.7.2. After removing the first 25% of samples as burn-in, the maximum clade credibility tree was generated using TreeAnnotator. The tree was rooted using HSV-1 (JQ673480.1) and HSV-2 (NC_001798.2) as outgroups.

We constructed phylogenetic trees for each envelope glycoprotein-coding sequence using Bayesian analysis with the MCMC algorithm implemented in MrBayes 3.1.7. (https://nbisweden.github.io/MrBayes). We selected the HKY model for gH, gI, and gL; the HKY model with a proportion of invariable sites (HKY + I) for gB, gC, gG, gJ, gK, and gN; and the HKY model with gamma-distributed rate variation (HKY + G) for gD, gE, and gM according to the Bayesian information criterion by using MEGA 5.2. Two independent runs were performed for over 10 million generations, sampling every 1,000 generations. Convergence was assessed by ensuring that the average standard deviation of split frequencies was <0.01 and all potential scale reduction factors were approximately 1.0. The first 25% of samples (2,500 trees per run) were removed as burn-in, and the remaining trees were combined to generate a 50% majority-rule consensus tree with posterior probability support. The resulting trees were visualized in FigTree v1.4.4. The tree was rooted using HSV-1 and HSV-2 as outgroups.

We also deduced amino acid sequences of gB, gD, gH, and gL of the TPK5 isolate using BioEdit version 7.7.1.0 and compared the similarity with those of BV isolates obtained from rhesus, bonnet, Japanese, long-tailed, pig-tailed, and lion-tailed macaques, and the corresponding HSV glycoproteins from human isolates.

Retrieving nectin amino acid sequences from GenBank

Nectin-1 and nectin-2 amino acid sequences from macaque species and humans include multiple annotated isoforms/variants, except for those from the rhesus macaque. Therefore, we selected the isoforms with the highest amino acid identity and sequence similarity to the corresponding human nectin reference proteins for comparative analyses to minimize redundancy and improve clarity. Thus, nectin-1 and nectin-2 amino acid sequences retrieved from the NCBI database included those from long-tailed macaque (nectin-1 isoform X1: XP_005579992.1 and nectin-2 isoform X2: XP_005589608.1), rhesus macaque (nectin-1: XP_014971448.1 and nectin-2: NP_001037058.1), southern pig-tailed macaque (nectin-1 isoform X2: XP_011730506.1 and nectin-2 isoform X2: XP_070943218.1), and human (nectin 1 isoform 1: NP_002846.3 and nectin-2 isoform alpha: NP_002847.1). Sequence identity analyses were performed using BioEdit software (version 7.7.1.0).

Results

Bv shedding

The BV shedding detection rate was 0.72% (1 out of 138 animals), with a cycle qPCR threshold of 32.1 and 1,607 DNA copies. A positive sample was detected from an oral swab of a juvenile male (ID: TPK5-O) at Tham Pra Khayang in southern Thailand. Confined to the TPK5 monkey, the urogenital swab and rectal swab samples showed negative qPCR results.

Bv isolate

After BV isolation from TPK5-O, we observed CPEs with balloon-shaped cells in both Vero and LLC-MK2 cells at the first passage, 3 days post-infection (Figure 1). The CPEs progressed more rapidly in Vero cells than in LLC-MK2 cells. The suspected BV isolates were harvested via three freeze-thaw cycles. We quantified BV DNA by qPCR, with cycle threshold values of 20.8 and 13.4 and estimated DNA copy numbers of 844,035 and 111,429,128 in LLC-MK2 and Vero cells, respectively. Thus, only the BV isolate in Vero cells was subsequently tested by TEM and whole-genome sequencing, while the isolate in LLC-MK2 cells was kept as a virus stock and not included in this study. The BV strain was named BV/Thailand/Ranong_TPK5/2024 (hereafter TPK5). The infectious virion titre of the TPK5 isolate, determined by plaque assay from the infected Vero cells, was 8 × 107 PFU/mL. Whole genome sequencing of TPK5 isolate

A total of 37,410,364 reads were generated. Of these, 22,439,058 reads (59.98%) were successfully aligned to the reference genome KJ566591.2, whereas 14,971,306 reads (40.02%) remained unmapped. Paired-end mapping accounted for the majority of alignments, with 22,439,058 reads (59.98%) mapped in proper pairs and 434,318 reads (1.16%) classified as singletons. No secondary alignments were observed, and supplementary alignments constituted 0.16% of total reads. The mean read length was 150.08 bp (range: 25–150 bp). The base composition exhibited a marked GC bias (73.45% GC content), while the proportion of ambiguous bases (N) was negligible (<0.1%).

The mean sequencing depth across the reference was 20,974.67× with a standard deviation (SD) of 5,866.77. Genome-wide coverage was broadly uniform. The average mapping quality was 46.51, indicating a high confidence in read placement. The mean insert size was 588.98 bp, with a wide distribution (SD = 5,281.08 bp). The interquartile range was relatively narrow, with the first, median, and third quartiles of 214, 261, and 312 bp.

A total of 22,346,425 reads (59.73%) were identified as duplicates, corresponding to a duplication rate of 99.68%. Clipping events were observed in 2,944,521 reads (7.87%), indicating moderate sequence trimming during alignment. The overall error rate was estimated at 1.49%. Insertions were detected in 1,135,474 instances (4.6% of mapped reads). Deletions occurred 1,647,909 times (6.15% of mapped reads). Homopolymer-associated indels represented 54.46% of these events, highlighting the susceptibility of such regions to sequencing artefacts. For the reference genome KJ566591.2 (155,157 bp), a total of 3,254,366,768 mapped bases were obtained, yielding an average coverage of 20,974.67× (Supplementary Table 1). The genome sequence of TPK5 was submitted to GenBank (accession no. PX974241).

Discussion

We successfully isolated the BV from an oral swab of a wild long-tailed macaque (TPK5-O) and cultivated it in two different types of kidney cells: African green monkey Vero cells and rhesus monkey LLC-MK2 cells. When comparing the cultivation of the TPK5 isolate in these two cell types, we observed that cell clustering, estimated DNA copy number, and infectious titre were all higher in Vero cells than in LLC-MK2 cells. Based on this isolation system, we can conclude that Vero cells are an optimal cell line for isolating BV from long-tailed macaques, supporting their use for isolating BV from clinical specimens [33]. Previously, it was noted that the BV has co-evolved with its natural macaque host [20,23]. This raised the question of whether in vitro BV cultures with more passages can drive viral evolution toward the host species. To prevent this potential outcome, we harvested the BV isolates at the first passage using three freeze-thaw cycles.

The BV shedding detection rate in wild long-tailed macaques was low, with only 1 of 138 oral swab samples and undetectable in urogenital and rectal swab samples. This low shedding rate might explain why zoonotic BV transmission has been rare in their natural habitats, with only one human case reported so far [10–12,34–35]. Although only one clinical specimen was found to contain the BV genome by qPCR in this study, infectious BV could be isolated from cell lines, confirming its infectivity. Previously, BV seroprevalence of 85.1% (23/27) was observed in the Tham Pra Khayang population, and TPK5 was seropositive [20]. Given that BV can reactivate from latency under various conditions and shed intermittently and unpredictably, zoonotic risk extends beyond the few animals actively shedding at the time of sampling. Since an infected animal (TPK5) was asymptomatic and Tham Pra Khayang is a high human–macaque interface area, this could pose a risk of zoonotic transmission.

After infecting Vero cells with the TPK5 isolate, icosahedral-like BV particles were observed under the TEM. A low multiplicity of infections likely contributed to a longer replication cycle, resulting in only 50–60% CPE. Many viral capsids were found within the nuclei of infected cells, while only a few viral progenies were detected outside the nucleus 3 days post-infection. Previous studies demonstrated that intracellular and extracellular BV isolates in captive rhesus macaques reached plateau levels by 24–28 h post-infection in Vero cells [36]. Since the growth kinetics of the BV isolated from wild long-tailed macaque have not yet been investigated, it may be assumed that the replication cycles of the BV isolated from rhesus macaque might be greater than those of the wild long-tailed macaque in Vero cells.

The mechanism by which BV invades host cells remains elusive. BV shares up to 79% identity with HSV in certain amino acid segments, and the secondary structures of BV glycoproteins are similar to those of HSV glycoproteins [5]. Thus, the mechanism of HSV entry into host cells has been used to explain BV entry, although their entry mechanisms are not identical [5]. HSV entry was proposed to occur via two mechanisms: fusion and endocytosis [37]; however, it is widely accepted that HSV entry is primarily mediated by attachment and fusion [38,39]. Entry of HSV and BV into the host cell is a coordinated process that integrates various glycoproteins. To investigate potential mechanisms by which the TPK5 isolate from wild macaques infects humans, we performed whole-genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis of the 12 envelope glycoproteins and deduced the amino acid sequences of 4 envelope glycoproteins involved in BV entry into host cells. The whole-genome sequence of the BV isolate (TPK5) had high homology (98.4%) to Macacine alphaherpesvirus 1 isolate E90-136 (155,157 bp) (KJ566591.2), which was isolated from a young captive long-tailed macaque [40]. Previous studies have demonstrated that gB, gD, gH, and gL envelope glycoproteins are essential for attachment, membrane fusion, and penetration into the host cytoplasm [6,41,42]. The gD/receptor interaction is the key step that triggers viral entry. The remaining gC, gE, gG, gI, gK, gJ, gM, and gN envelope glycoproteins are non-essential for replication but contribute to optimal viral propagation, immune evasion, and pathogenesis [43]. Noted, the role of gC in herpesvirus entry to host cells may vary depending on the host cell types [6,44].

Previous phylogenetic analyses of either whole genome [2] or glycoprotein sequences [20,23] demonstrated that BV strains co-evolved with their macaque host species. Similarly, a whole genome sequence and 12 envelope glycoprotein sequences from the TPK5 isolate exhibited evolutionary patterns consistent with those of the E90–136 reference virulent strain isolated from a captive long-tailed macaque. The 10 glycoproteins (gB, gC, gD, gE, gG, gH, gI, gK, gL and gM) were highly conserved, while gN and gJ glycoproteins displayed greater sequence variability, suggesting that these genes, while more prone to diversification, continue to follow a shared evolutionary path within long-tailed macaques.

After amino acid sequences were deduced, the gB, gD, gH, and gL envelope glycoproteins of the TPK5 isolate were compared with the E90–136 isolate and showed high sequence conservation of at least 98.7%. In contrast, HSV-1 and HSV-2 exhibited sequence conservation ranging from only 47% to 78% and 45% to 79%, respectively. Consistent with a previous study [3], the gB, gD, gH, and gL envelope glycoproteins of the E2490 isolate (rhesus macaque lineage) were compared with HSV-1 and HSV-2, and the results found that sequence conservation ranged from 57% to 79.9% and 53.4% to 80.4%. Given the low similarity among the four glycoprotein amino acid sequences of BV, HSV-1, and HSV-2, it remains unclear how BV infects humans.

Although alphaherpesvirus tropism depends on entry receptors on host cells, different host cell types express distinct receptors, and their distribution limits viral infection and transmission to specific cell types [5]. The gD protein can bind to at least three classes of host-cell entry receptors, including nectins (nectin-1 and nectin-2), herpesvirus entry mediator (HVEM), and 3-O-sulfonated derivatives of heparan sulfate [5]. Nectin-1 is mainly expressed in epithelial, neuronal, and endothelial cells, as well as keratinocytes, whereas HVEM has broader tissue expression, including T lymphocytes, trabecular meshwork cells, lymphoid tissues, lung, liver, and kidney [39]. Given these distinct receptor expression patterns, viral infection and replication in Vero cells, which are derived from the kidneys of African green monkeys and used in this study, might not fully explain the mechanism of viral entry into human neuronal cells. Moreover, BV can effectively use both nectin-1 and nectin-2 as cellular receptors for entry into human cells, but not the HVEM-mediated entry pathway [38]; however, it is unknown whether HVEM can serve as an entry receptor for BV in monkey (Vero) kidney cells. Given that the complex entry mechanisms of HSV and BV are widely explained by gD binding to nectin-1, our current study focuses solely on nectin receptors, rather than the other two receptor classes, to address a possible pathway for viral transmission between macaques and humans.

Amino acid sequences revealed that both nectin receptors are highly conserved among macaque species, with sequence identities of at least 99.8% for nectin-1 and 99.3% for nectin-2, and a similarly high level of conservation was observed when compared with humans. Long-tailed macaque nectin-1 and nectin-2 share 98.4% and 95.1% amino acid identity with humans, respectively. This may suggest that BV’s glycoprotein-binding ability to the human host remains intact through the conservation of key residues and structural domains crucial for nectin-1 and nectin-2 interactions. This conservation of receptor tropism indicates a strong functional interaction with nectin-1 and nectin-2 despite sequence divergence among related alphaherpesvirus glycoproteins. The three-dimensional structures of the BV gD-human nectin-1 complex were constructed [45]. Although the predicted BV gD-nectin-1 interface was not fully structurally complementary, BV gD remained capable of engaging nectin-1. Such structural conservation likely facilitates BV’s ability to infect human cells and may help explain the virus’s severe zoonotic potential, although the shedding rate in macaque hosts is low (for example, 1.04% in wild Thai long-tailed macaques [20]).

Although we successfully isolated BV from a wild Thai long-tailed macaque and conducted whole-genome sequencing, this work has a limitation: only one positive sample was obtained. Thus, the isolate may not represent the full geographic range of long-tailed macaques and may not capture the full genetic diversity of BV in wild macaques. Nevertheless, BV is known to exhibit host-virus co-evolution with macaque hosts, suggesting that the isolate may still provide meaningful insight into BV’s genetic characteristics in this host population. Moreover, this study relies primarily on sequence conservation to infer cross-species transmission potential; however, sequence homology alone is insufficient to confirm receptor-binding efficiency. Additional host receptors, host factors such as integrins, and interactions between viral glycoproteins and host receptors may also contribute to viral entry and influence cross-species transmission [37]. To address this, we will perform structural modelling to predict the interface between BV gD (TPK5) and human nectin-1 and demonstrate structural complementarity. Additionally, we will test whether nectin or HVEM serves as a potential receptor for BV gD binding in Vero cells. This approach will provide insights into molecular-level interactions and could elucidate the mechanisms underlying BV entry into cells.
