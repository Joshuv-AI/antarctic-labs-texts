Shared Genetics of Kidney Function Traits and Bladder Cancer: A Genome‐Wide Cross‐Trait Analysis
Authors: Yifei Lin, Nanyan Xiang, Yong Yang, Shujun Huang, Shiqi Su, Tingting Fu, Yurui Luo, Zeng Wang, Rui Shi, Tao Zheng, Banghua Liao, Jin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundClinical and epidemiological evidence has suggested a potential association between kidney dysfunction and bladder cancer (BC). One hypothesis for this comorbidity is the presence of a common genetic etiology. However, little is known about the shared genetics and causality of this association. Thus, we aimed to investigate shared genetic architecture and the causal link between kidney dysfunction and bladder cancer.MethodsLeveraging summary statistics from large‐scale genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) conducted on European‐ancestry populations on eGFRcrea (N = 1,004,040), eGFRcys (N = 460,826), BUN (N = 852,678), UACR (N = 288,649), urate (N = 547,361) and BC (N cases = 3357, N controls = 628,027), we conducted a large‐scale genome‐wide cross‐trait study to determine genetic overlap, to identify shared loci, and to infer causal relationships. We evaluated genetic correlation through linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC). Integration of single‐trait GWAS was accomplished by multi‐trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG), which enabled cross‐trait meta‐analysis to unveil overlapping genetic loci between five kidney function traits and BC. Shared genes were further validated through colocalization analysis and transcriptome‐wide association analysis. Bidirectional Mendelian Randomization (MR) was conducted to determine causal inference of kidney function traits on BC.ResultsWe found positive correlations between both BUN and urate and BC at the genome‐wide level. A total of 157 significant overlapping genetic loci (range 7 to 72) were identified across five kidney function traits and BC. Among them, PSCA was prioritized as the strongest shared gene with support from MTAG, colocalization, and TWAS, whereas ZFHX3, TTC33, and ATP2A1 were considered candidates supported only by MTAG and TWAS. MR provided the most consistent support for a potential positive causal effect of BUN on BC, limited support for UACR, and exploratory evidence for urate.ConclusionsOur cross‐trait analysis demonstrated a shared genetic basis underlying kidney function and BC, providing novel insights into the biological functions and molecular mechanisms underlying these complex traits.Keywords: bladder cancer, genetic correlation, genome‐wide cross‐trait analysis, kidney function

Introduction

Ranked as the ninth most prevalent form of cancer worldwide with an estimated 550,000 new cases and 200,000 fatalities annually, bladder cancer (BC) is the most common malignancy of the urinary tract with high morbidity and mortality [1]. The incidence is approximately fourfold higher in men than in women worldwide [2], and urothelial carcinoma constitutes the predominant histological subtype [3]. Researchers have identified numerous risk factors for BC, including environmental exposures such as tobacco smoking and occupational contact with carcinogens, which contribute substantially to the attributable risk [4]. Notably, a growing amount of evidence has suggested that kidney dysfunction might also constitute a risk factor for BC, impacting tumor recurrence, progression, and overall survival [5]. In addition, a comprehensive study involving a total of approximately 100,000 bc patients illustrated that those with end‐stage kidney disease exhibited more advanced stages of bladder cancer compared to counterparts without kidney diseases [6].

Conversely, patients diagnosed with BC were found to have an increased risk of kidney disease. Extensive studies have long demonstrated that up to 46% of bladder cancer patients suffered from deteriorated kidney function [7]. Additionally, studies have highlighted that while treatments such as radical cystectomy were associated with a higher incidence of acute kidney injury and enduring impairment of renal function [8, 9], the commonly used BC drug, cisplatin, would also induce nephrotoxicity, leading to decreased kidney function [10].

Accumulating evidence has suggested that both genetic predisposition and exposure to environmental factors contribute to the development of most kidney diseases and BC [11]. One hypothesis accounting for the association between impaired kidney function and BC posits a shared genetic etiology. For instance, the TP53 gene mutation has been implicated in BC progression and prognosis, while also inducing acute kidney injury via autophagy promotion [12]. However, no comprehensive genetic architecture has been constructed to fully estimate the underlying mechanism of this putative relationship between kidney dysfunction and BC. Similarly, TERT promoter variants, which are among the most frequent alterations in BC, have been associated with CKD susceptibility [13]. The shared risk factors between kidney dysfunction and BC might stem from overlapping genes and biological pathways. Thus, grouping genetic variants common to multiple diseases or traits could elucidate specific processes underlying their comorbidity.

To investigate these shared genetic features, we performed a large‐scale genome‐wide cross‐trait analysis between BC and five established kidney function biomarkers, including serum creatinine‐based estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFRcrea), cystatin C‐based estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFRcys), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR), and urate—under the hypothesis that eGFR‐related traits would be negatively correlated, whereas BUN, UACR, and urate would be positively correlated with BC [14]. Specifically, we conducted post‐GWAS genetic correlation analysis, cross‐trait meta‐analysis, colocalization analysis, and transcriptome‐wide association analysis (TWAS) to identify possible shared genetic variants, and bidirectional two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to explore genetic causal inference. This study may provide genetic evidence for the relationship between kidney dysfunction and BC and offer clues for future mechanistic studies.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

The overall study design was shown in Figure 1. We retrieved summary statistics from publicly available GWAS studies. In terms of kidney function traits, all the summary statistics were based on continuous biomarkers from the Chronic Kidney Disease Genetics Consortium and UK Biobank, encompassing eGFRcrea (N = 1,004,040), eGFRcys (N = 460,826), BUN (N = 852,678), UACR (N = 288,649), and urate (N = 547,361) [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. BC summary statistics were sourced from the Finngen consortium (N case/control: 1,115/217,677), and a meta‐analysis of GWASs (N case/control: 2,242/410,350) including data from the UK Biobank and the Kaiser Permanente Genetic Epidemiology Research on Adult Health and Aging [20, 21]. The LiftOver tool was employed to convert the NCBI36/hg18 reference genome of Finngen BC summary data to GRCh37/hg19. The summary statistics of BC were then combined via inverse variance‐weighted meta‐analysis using METAL software [22]. Details of each dataset can be found in Table S1.

Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression (LDSC) Analysis for Genetic Correlation

We conducted a post‐GWAS genetic correlation analysis for kidney function traits and BC using the LDSC software, alongside the linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure of European ancestry reference data from the 1000 Genomes Project. LDSC capitalizes on the premise that the effect size associated with a specific SNP represents the effects of all SNPs in LD with that SNP [23]. Consequently, SNPs within high‐LD regions exhibit more elevated chi‐squared statistics than SNPs within low‐LD regions, while a similar relationship holds when chi‐squared statistics for a single study are replaced with the product of the z scores from two studies of genetically correlated traits [23]. In addition, LDSC employed a self‐estimated intercept to signify the sample overlap between the studies [24]. p‐values were adjusted for multiple testing across the five kidney traits using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and genetic correlations with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significant.

Cross‐Trait Meta‐Analysis

To identify genome‐wide significant loci between kidney function traits and BC, we used a novel approach named multi‐trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) to conduct joint meta‐analysis [25, 26]. MTAG is a valuable method since it can be applied to summary statistics from GWAS rather than relying on individual‐level data, handling sample overlap across different traits by using bivariate LD score regression, and can accommodate differing sample sizes across traits. The key assumption of MTAG is that all single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) share the same variance–covariance matrix of effect sizes across traits [25]. Each kidney function trait was meta‐analyzed with BC to identify shared independent genetic loci. Significance thresholds for meta‐analysis were set at p < 5 × 10−8, while single‐trait analyses were considered significant at p < 0.05. In addition, to provide functional annotation of all significant SNPs identified, we utilized the Functional Annotation of Variants Online Resource (FAVOR, version 2.0) tool [27], using its default annotation framework without incorporating additional scoring modules.

Colocalization Analysis

For each of the shared loci (index SNP) between kidney function traits and BC that meet the cross‐trait meta‐analysis significance criteria, we performed a colocalization analysis to assess whether two association signals are consistent with one shared causal variant. We first extracted summary statistics for SNPs within a 1000 kb region of the index SNP common to both kidney function traits and BC. Then, using the “coloc” R package with default prior probabilities (p1 = 1 × 10−4, p2 = 1 × 10−4, p12 = 1 × 10−5), we calculated the posterior probability for all variants in the clumping region. By examining the probability results, we aimed to discern whether the index SNP was responsible for both GWAS signals or if there existed a distinct causal variant in close proximity to the index SNP [28]. In cases where distinct causal variants were identified in the clumping region, we further investigated whether their closest genes overlapped with those corresponding to the index SNPs through the FAVOR tool. In the present study, genetic variants with a posterior probability (PP_H4) greater than 0.8 were considered to be colocalized [29].

Transcriptome‐Wide Association Analysis

Transcriptome‐wide association analyses (TWAS) were executed to identify associations of kidney function traits and BC with gene expression in specific tissues. Our TWAS was conducted with the FUSION software package, encompassing data from 49 GTEx (version 8) tissues and 24 The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) tumor expression weights [30, 31]. To mitigate the risk of false positives, multiple testing correction was employed for each trait's gene–tissue pairs on TWAS p‐values using the Benjamini‐Hochberg correction procedure, with a significance threshold set at FDR < 0.05.

Investigation of Biological Pathways and Tissue Expression Patterns

To understand the biological insights of the shared genes between kidney function traits and BC, we employed the Multi‐marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA) [32] to evaluate the enrichment of gene sets based on genes identified as significant in the MTAG analysis in the Gene Ontology (GO) biological process and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway [33]. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was used for correcting multiple testing, ensuring a FDR of less than 0.05. Additionally, utilizing the functional mapping and annotation (FUMA) [34] online tool, we performed GTEx tissue enrichment analysis across 54 distinct tissue types from GTEx version 8 [31]. The above analyses were based on results from the cross‐trait meta‐analysis.

Bidirectional MR Analyses

We performed a bidirectional two‐sample MR framework to identify causal associations between kidney function biomarkers and BC. The MR was designed based on three key assumptions: (1) instrumental genetic variants should be significantly associated with the exposure; (2) the variants should be independent of confounding factors; (3) the variants can only affect the outcome indirectly via the exposure [35]. In the present study, two newly developed methods, namely contamination mixture (ConMix) [36] and Robust Adjusted Profile Score (RAPS) [37] were employed as the primary methods to obtain robust results in the presence of weak and invalid instrumental variables. We also conducted sensitivity analyses employing the Weighted Median, Inverse‐Variance Weighted (IVW), MR‐Egger, Mode‐based Estimate (MBE), MR‐Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR‐PRESSO) [38], and Generalized Summary‐data‐based MR (GSMR) models [35, 39]. For the forward MR, we constructed instruments using genome‐wide significant independent variants with a p‐value less than 5 × 10−8. Given the limited number of instruments meeting the threshold p value at 5 × 10−8 for BC, we set genome‐wide significant p‐value at 5 × 10−5 for reverse MR.

Cross‐Trait Meta‐Analysis

Utilizing MTAG for genome‐wide meta‐analysis, we identified a total of 157 (range 7 to 72) genetic loci demonstrating significant associations with both kidney function traits and BC (meta‐analysis p < 5 × 10−8, and trait‐specific p < 0.05), comprising 72 for eGFRcrea, 42 for eGFRcys, 17 for BUN, 19 for urate, and 8 for UACR (Table S2).

The most significant locus (index SNP: rs267738, P meta = 1.33 × 10−72) associated with eGFRcrea and BC was located near CERS2, a protein‐coding gene influencing cell growth regulation [40]. The index SNP rs113822376 (P meta = 5.08 × 10−152), representing the most significant locus associated with eGFRcys and BC, was located on CST9L, a gene that encoded several proteins with crucial roles in cancer progression [41]. The locus most strongly associated with BUN and BC (index SNP rs13076151, *****P meta = 1.17 × 10−54) lies close to the LRIG1 gene, which encodes a protein that acted as a regulator of stem cell quiescence, a pleiotropic feedback tumor suppressor, and a modulator of autoimmunity [42]. For the 7 loci identified with UACR and BC, the most significant one (index SNP: rs1337526, P meta = 9.56 × 10−29) was mapped on FOXD2‐AS1, an RNA Gene affiliated with the lncRNA class and served as an oncogene [43]. A total of 19 loci were identified to be associated with both urate and BC. The strongest association signal was found at the WDR1 (index SNP: rs56158923, P meta = 2.69 × 10−85) region, known to be involved in cytokinesis and chemotactic cell migration [44].

Colocalization Analysis

With the Bayesian colocalization method, we calculated the posterior probability of all significant SNPs identified in MTAG cross‐trait meta‐analysis (Table S3). We observed only one strong regional evidence of colocalization between BUN and BC (index SNP: rs2976384, PP_H3 = 0.076, PP_H4 = 0.867). Within the ±500 kb region surrounding rs2976384, the top SNP was also rs2976384 (SNP_PP_H4 = 0.120), which is located nearest to JRK and PSCA (Table S3).

Biological Pathway, Tissue Enrichment, and TWAS

The GTEx enrichment analysis found significant tissue‐specific expression for cross‐trait genes across five trait pairs. Specifically, for eGFRcrea and BC, kidney and liver tissues showed enrichment. For eGFRcys and BC, brain tissue exhibited significant enrichment. In the case of BUN and BC, kidney and pancreas tissues were enriched. Since cervix tissue showed enrichment only for urate and BC, and we suspected this may be a chance finding from cross‐tissue testing. Among these, kidney cortex exhibited the most pronounced enrichment (Figure 2). Additionally, GO biological process analysis revealed that the shared genetic background between kidney function and bladder cancer was predominantly enriched in pathways related to kidney and urinary system development, RNA metabolism and transcriptional regulation, as well as signaling pathways involved in cell proliferation and differentiation. KEGG pathway analysis identified significant associations only between eGFR and BC, with enriched pathways broadly implicating growth factor–mediated signaling and immune‐related processes (Tables S4–S9).

The TWAS analysis was conducted to identify genes whose expression was related to both kidney function biomarkers and bladder cancer in specific tissues. In 49 GTEx tissues, bladder cancer shared a total of 497 gene‐tissue pairs with eGFRcrea, 237 gene‐tissue pairs with eGFRcys, 219 gene‐tissue pairs with BUN, 335 gene‐tissue pairs with urate, and 20 gene‐tissue pairs with UACR. In 24 TCGA tumors, bladder cancer was identified to share 75 gene‐tissue pairs with eGFRcrea, 43 pairs with eGFRcys, 35 pairs with BUN, 50 pairs with urate, and 19 pairs with UACR (Tables S10–S16). Among them, MIPOL1 overlapped between BC and 4 kidney function traits (eGFRcrea, eGFRcys, BUN, and urate), which was expressed in adipose subcutaneous, adipose visceral omentum, tibial artery, cells cultured fibroblasts, heart atrial, pancreas, leg, and thyroid cancer (Table S10).

Overlapped Genes

Importantly, the colocalization analysis identified PSCA, which was also identified in the MTAG and TWAS analyses, making it the prioritized shared gene between BUN and BC. PSCA exhibited enriched expression in the brain, stomach, small intestine, colon, esophagus, salivary gland, kidney cortex, prostate, skin, testis, and thyroid. PSCA encodes a glycoprotein significantly expressed in the bladder; it is associated with BC risk [45].

The ZFHX3 and TTC33 genes, expressed in breast cells, cultured fibroblasts, and cervical squamous cell carcinoma, were also identified in MTAG and TWAS. ZFHX3, encoding a transcription factor that functioned as a tumor suppressor in several cancers [46], was shared among eGFRcrea, eGFRcys, UACR, and BC. TTC33, associated with BUN and BC, may regulate renal tubular cell survival by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis [47].

Through TWAS and MTAG analyses, ATP2A1, significantly enriched in the kidney cortex, was highlighted in relation to eGFRcrea, eGFRcys, urate, and BC. This gene encodes a SERCA Ca(2+)‐ATPase, and its dysregulation is implicated in muscle relaxation impairment and poor prognosis in carcinoma patients [48].

Bidirectional MR Analysis

For the forward MR analysis, four MR methods supported a positive association between genetically predicted BUN and BC, whereas support for UACR was observed in three MR methods (all OR > 1, all p < 0.05) (Figure 3 and Tables S17–S18). In addition, the GSMR method also identified a positive association between urate and BC (beta = 0.108, se = 0.055, p = 0.047) (Table S19). For the reverse MR analysis, our results revealed only a weak positive effect of BC on eGFRcrea (Weighted median: OR = 1.0009, 95% CI: 1.0002–1.0016, p = 0.015; MBE: OR = 1.001, 95% CI: 1.0001–1.0019, p = 0.030). None of the other analyses reached statistical significance (Table S17).

Discussion

To our knowledge, it is the first study to explore shared genetic etiology, causal associations, and pathophysiological mechanisms between kidney function and BC, utilizing large‐scale GWAS summary data. In the LDSC analysis, BUN and urate displayed strong positive genetic correlations, indicating common genetic architecture underlying the phenotypic associations. Subsequently, we identified 157 loci shared between kidney function traits and BC. Notably, PSCA was identified as a prioritized shared gene through colocalization analysis and further validated via TWAS analysis. Our MR analysis explored positive causal associations between BUN and BC and provided limited support for the positive causal effect of UACR, and exploratory evidence for urate from GSMR, indicating that the shared genetic influences may be partly attributed to causal etiologies or mechanisms.

Our MTAG cross‐trait meta‐analysis found 157 shared genetic loci and further identified shared genes across five kidney function traits and BC. CERS2 and LRIG1 are involved in crucial processes of cellular growth, proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [49, 50, 51], and have been linked to tumor stage, recurrence, and poor prognosis [41, 52]. Moreover, CST9L plays roles in modulating invasive and metastatic potential of tumors as well as the tumor microenvironment, particularly influencing lymphatic metastasis and the activity of immune cells [53]. Additionally, through their influence on cellular growth, inflammatory pathways, and immune modulation, the genetic loci such as CST9L, LRIG1, and WDR1 also affect renal physiology, particularly in processes such as fibrosis and immune cell mediation within the kidney [54]. These findings highlight the complex biological interactions between renal functions and BC, suggesting a broader systemic influence that extends beyond localized disease mechanisms.

In the colocalization analysis, we only revealed one strong regional colocalization between BUN and BC at locus rs2976384 (PP_H4 = 0.867), indicating a shared genetic signal in this region. The top SNP in this region lies nearest to JRK and PSCA. While the biological functions of JRK remain less well characterized, PSCA encodes a glycoprotein with known roles in epithelial cell proliferation and tumor susceptibility [45], and has been repeatedly associated with BC [55, 56]. However, the modest posterior probability for the lead SNP (SNP.PP_H4 = 0.120) suggests that the association cannot be fine‐mapped to a single causal variant but is instead distributed across multiple correlated SNPs in high linkage disequilibrium, highlighting the need for higher‐resolution fine‐mapping and functional validation.

Our post‐GWAS functional analysis provided biological insights into the shared genes between kidney function traits and BC. GTEx tissue enrichment analysis revealed shared genes were enriched in several tissues, including kidney, liver, pancreas, cervix, and brain. Notably, kidney cortex exhibited the most pronounced and biologically plausible enrichment, suggesting a potentially relevant role of renal cortical biology in the link between kidney dysfunction and BC [57]. Furthermore, GO and KEGG analyses suggested that shared genes may be involved in kidney and urinary system development and growth factor–mediated signaling, aligning with prior evidence of developmental dysregulation in urothelial carcinogenesis [58]. These indicated that developmental and regulatory pathways in kidney could represent potential biological links between kidney dysfunction and BC.

Our TWAS analysis identified 1530 shared gene‐tissue pairs between BC and kidney function. Of them, MIPOL1 was found to be shared between BC and four kidney function traits (eGFRcrea, eGFRcys, BUN, and urate) in multiple tissues and potentially possessed many kinds of biological function. For instance, the enriched expression we found in tibial artery and heart might be consistent with a potential role of MIPOL1 in angiogenesis‐related pathways [59].

Notably, the convergence of evidence from colocalization, MTAG, and TWAS analyses supports PSCA as a prioritized shared gene. Our TWAS results were consistent with previous research that PSCA is expressed in bladder and kidney [60]. Meanwhile, previous studies in both European and East Asian populations have indicated that polymorphisms in PSCA are implicated in BC carcinogenesis [45, 61], supporting our analyses in prioritizing PSCA as a shared genetic signal.

Our analysis also highlighted ATP2A1, which was found in MTAG analysis and significantly enriched in the kidney cortex through TWAS. As a cancer‐associated immunomarker, ATP2A1 showed effects in regulating muscle relaxation and was linked to poor prognosis in carcinoma patients [62]. Its good affinity for HLC‐018, a novel aniline‐linked small molecule, also indicated that ATP2A1 may be biologically relevant in cancer‐related pathways, although its therapeutic relevance requires further validation [63].

In addition, we unveiled ZFHX3 and TTC33 overlapping between the meta‐analysis and TWAS analysis. ZFHX3 has been reported to influence the malignant progression of BC due to alterations in its subcellular localization from protein fragmentation [18], and has been reported as a candidate marker in superficial urothelial bladder carcinoma [64]. TTC33 has been suggested to regulate the apoptosis of proximal tubule epithelial cells, possibly by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby mitigating renal tubular injury in both acute and chronic kidney injury [47]. Moreover, we proposed that TTC33 may promote BC development by negatively modulating estrogen receptor alpha activity [65], however, these mechanisms remain hypothetical and require further validation.

The genetic correlation between kidney function and BC may be attributed to both pleiotropy and causality [66]. Our MR analysis demonstrated the most consistent support for a positive causal effect of BUN on BC, limited support for UACR, GSMR‐dependent evidence for urate, and a weak positive effect of BC on eGFRcrea. Based on the genetic correlation of the previous paragraphs, we would highlight the potential causal effects of BUN and urate on BC, suggesting that individuals with kidney dysfunction characterized by elevated BUN or urate may warrant additional attention regarding BC risk. However, their clinical relevance requires further validation in future studies.

Several strengths have bolstered our study. First, this is the first study to identify shared genetic architecture between kidney function and BC based on summary statistics from the hitherto largest GWASs. Second, through enrichment analysis and TWAS, we demonstrated that shared genes were enriched in several tissues, suggesting potential common mechanisms. Third, we implemented GSMR analysis, a novel causal estimation method that selects SNP instruments more rationally and minimizes bias caused by pleiotropy [39].

However, we acknowledge that potential limitations persist in this study. First, BC cases diagnosed at different stages and grades, as well as across different clinical subtypes (e.g., non–muscle‐invasive bladder cancer and muscle‐invasive bladder cancer), might influence the magnitude of effect sizes in both directions of the MR study, thus limiting our MR estimates to presenting only the overall causal direction for the impact of exposures on outcomes. To minimize the resulting bias, we employed multiple steps of MR to investigate pleiotropy. Second, as both the kidney function traits and BC datasets included participants from UKB, some degree of sample overlap may have inflated the observed associations. Nevertheless, we applied multiple analyses to cross‐validate the findings and mitigate this concern. Third, the limited number of cases means that our use of MTAG may still be subject to the winner's curse, potentially leading to an overestimation of genetic effects, although we have combined two BC GWAS datasets to strengthen the BC signal. Fourth, survivorship and ascertainment bias inherent in BC cohorts could also influence the observed associations. Finally, the study population was primarily of European ancestry, so the results should be cautiously interpreted among populations of other ethnicities. Further replication in non‐European cohorts, such as East Asian [67] and African populations [68], will help to confirm consistency across ancestries and explore ancestry‐specific effects.

Our study, leveraging large‐scale genome‐wide genetic data and advanced statistical genetics approaches, provides novel insights into the associations of kidney dysfunction with BC. Our findings elucidate a genetic correlation between kidney function and BC while identifying specific shared loci between these conditions, highlighting a potential causal effects of kidney function traits on BC. Evaluation of the genetic overlap and post‐GWAS functional analysis help inform shared biological mechanisms underlying this comorbidity.
