Association between dietary intake, anthropometric indices, and diabetes-related foot ulcers in Type 2 diabetes: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Moharam Jalalzadeh, Ensieh Nasli-Esfahani, Amirmasoud Mehrabani, Faezeh Geravand, Mohsen Montazer, Mohammad Heidari Seyedmahalle, Leila Azadbakht
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsThis study explored the association of lifestyle factors and dietary status with foot ulcers and new anthropometric indices in individuals living with diabetes-related foot ulcers.MethodsIn this study, 339 patients with type 2 diabetes were recruited from the Diabetes Research Center of the Tehran University of Medical Sciences using a convenience sampling method. Dietary variables were assessed using questionnaires and 24-hour dietary recalls (one holiday and two working days). Body shape index (ABSI), Body Roundness Index (BRI), and Abdominal Volume Index (AVI) were calculated based on their formulas.ResultsPeople in the highest tertile of foot ulcer area had a higher calorie intake than those in the lowest tertile. In addition, protein and zinc intakes were significantly lower in the third tertile of the foot ulcer area than in the lowest tertile. In the ABSI index, in crude model, there is a borderline association of increasing this index in the third tertile compared to first tertile of lifestyle score, (OR:1.82, 95% CI; 0.99–3.33). There was no significant difference in the BRI and AVI indices between the third tertile compared to first tertile of lifestyle score (OR: 0.70, 95% CI; 0.24–2.08) and (OR: 0.91, 95% CI; 0.40–2.06).ConclusionThe study found that the foot ulcer area was marginally related to energy intake. Protein and zinc intake had a significant inverse relationship with the foot ulcer area. However, no significant associations were found between lifestyle scores and new anthropometric indices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01145-8.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a metabolic disease characterized by excessively high blood glucose levels [1]. One of the most common consequences for people with poorly managed diabetes mellitus is diabetes-related foot ulcers [2]. Poor foot care, peripheral vascular disease, underlying neuropathy, and poor glycemic management are the typical causes of DIABETES-RELATED FOOT ULCERS [3]. Diabetes affects 425 million people worldwide, contributing to four million deaths in 2017 [4]. By 2030, the prevalence will increase to 10.2% (578 million), and by 2045, it will reach 10.9% (700 million) [5]. DIABETES-RELATED FOOT ULCERS occurs in 19–34% of individuals living with diabetes [6]. 20% of those who acquire DIABETES-RELATED FOOT ULCERS will need to have their lower extremities amputated, and 10% will pass away within a year of receiving their first DIABETES-RELATED FOOT ULCERS diagnosis [7, 8]. Controlling blood sugar is one of the main factors preventing the development of diabetes and foot ulcers [9]. In addition, diet is one of the most important factors that can affect blood sugar; therefore, proper nutrition is critical for the prevention and treatment of chronic wounds, including DIABETES-RELATED FOOT ULCERS [9].

Obesity, particularly visceral obesity, is linked to the development of type 2 diabetes [10]. Body Mass Index (BMI) is not a good indicator of obesity because an increase in fat-free mass might contribute to an increase in BMI [11]. To address the shortcomings of BMI, other indices such as the body shape index (ABSI), body roundness index (BRI), and abdominal volume index (AVI) have been developed [12–14]. These markers can provide a more accurate picture of body image and obesity [15].

Diabetes-related foot ulcers have a complicated etiology. However, one of the best methods for management and improvement is a healthy, high-quality diet [16]. Supplying the body with sufficient energy, macronutrients, and micronutrients such as zinc, vitamin C, vitamin D, and protein is vital for the body to repair skin wounds such as diabetes-related foot ulcers [17–20]. Malnutrition and nutrient deficiencies, on the other hand, are associated with delayed wound healing by prolonging the inflammatory phase, reducing fibroblast proliferation, and altering collagen production [21, 22].

There has not yet been a thorough investigation of the association between new anthropometric variables and diabetes-related foot ulcers in Iran. Thus, we conducted this cross-sectional study to determine the association between diet quality, novel anthropometric indices, and diabetes-related foot ulcers in patients with type 2 diabetes.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The main purpose of this cross-sectional study was to investigate the association between nutritional status, the size of diabetes-related foot ulcers (assessed by length, width, and depth, and used to calculate cross-sectional area), and new anthropometric indices in patients with type 2 diabetes. For this purpose, the participants of this cross-sectional study were patients with type 2 diabetes and foot ulcers who were interviewed at the super-specialized Diabetes and Metabolic Diseases Clinic of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, and the necessary data were collected from patients and medical records. The data for the study were collected between January 2022 and March 2023. Participants were selected using a simple random sample from among those referred to the Diabetes and Metabolic Diseases Clinic who met the inclusion criteria. People can opt in or out of the study based on the following criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: type 2 diabetes, diabetes-related foot ulcers, age over 18 years, no pregnancy or breastfeeding, no mental or nervous illnesses, no cancer, no immune system problems, and the ability to answer questionnaire questions. Exclusion criteria: individuals whose medical record information was inadequate and those whose calorie intake was less than 800 kcal/day or more than 4200 kcal/day, as these values are considered physiologically implausible and may reflect reporting errors rather than true intake [23]. The study protocol was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the Tehran University of Medical Sciences [IR.TUMS.EMRI.REC.1401.072].

Data collection

The study participants’ general information, such as first and last name, contact number, weight, height, fasting blood sugar, blood pressure, total cholesterol, and other information, was obtained from their medical records. Also, socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed using a structured and validated questionnaire, including marital status, education, and employment [25]. A nutritionist then performed anthropometric measurements, such as waist and hip circumferences. In addition, the Wagner grade of the diabetes-related foot ulcers [26] and the length, width, and depth of the ulcers were determined from the patients’ medical records.

Assessment of food intake

To assess the type of food intake of the patients, we used a questionnaire with 18 questions on eating habits [27, 28]. The questions in this questionnaire were selected on the basis of previous studies. Its validity and reliability have been tested and confirmed by previous studies [27, 28]. Although the lifestyle questionnaire was originally validated in a population of pregnant women, it was selected for this study due to its demonstrated validity and reliability in Iranian populations. Moreover, its core components (frequency of meals, water intake, and consumption of fried foods, fast food, and red meat) assess general dietary and health behaviors that are relevant and applicable to adult population, including individuals with type 2 diabetes. In this questionnaire, there was a separate question regarding each eating habit and behavior. For each question, 4 options were considered for each question: never, sometimes, often, and always. The type of food intake, frequency of each eating habit, and behavior of the participants were assessed according to the selected options. Finally, these points were added, and the rating of each person’s eating habits and behavior was determined (Supplementary 1). The questionnaire was completed in the form of a personal interview with a nutritionist. Through a 24-hour recall over three days (one public holiday and two working days), the patients’ nutritional intake status was assessed, and the patient’s eating behavior was re-examined. The amount of daily calories, macronutrients, and micronutrients was then determined by entering the food into the Nutritionist IV software (First Databank Division, the Hearst Corporation, San Bruno, CA, United States, modified for Iranian foods). In this study, we focused on dietary intakes obtained from 24-hour recalls. Protein and zinc were selected for further analysis because their intake is more accurately reflected through diet compared to nutrients like vitamin D or omega-3, which are often affected by supplementation and seasonal variation factors not controlled in our cross-sectional design.

Anthropometric indices

Patients’ weight was measured using a digital scale (SECA, Hamburg, Germany) with an accuracy of 0.1 kg while wearing light clothing and being barefoot. The standing height without shoes was measured with a wall-mounted stadiometer with an accuracy of 0.5 cm. For each patient, a tape was used to measure the hip circumference (HC) at the widest point of the hip and waist circumference (WC) at the narrowest point, which was directly above the umbilicus. Patients’ BMI was calculated as the ratio of weight in kilograms to the square of height in meters. Biochemical indices

Total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein, high-density lipoprotein, and triglycerides, as well as fasting blood glucose and HbA1C, assessed after 10–14 h of fasting using standard laboratory methods, were extracted from the medical records. A physician stationed at the center took seated measurements of systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) blood pressure using a sphygmomanometer.

Evaluation of diabetes-related foot ulcers

Using the medical records of individuals with diabetes-related foot ulcers in the study, which were available at the diabetes center, the length (cm), width (cm), and depth (mm) of the diabetes-related foot ulcers as well as infection, and Wagner grade [26] of the ulcer were extracted. Foot ulcer area (cm2) was defined as the ulcer surface area, calculated using the product of length and width (length × width). Other information related to foot ulcers, such as a history of amputations and other chronic illnesses, was obtained through interviews.

Lifestyle score

The total score for eating habits and behaviors, physical activity, and non-smoking constituted the lifestyle score. The scores of eating habits and behaviors (score: 0–54) were calculated as previously described, and non-smoking was given a score of 1 and smoking a score of 0 [27]. Physical activity levels were measured using the short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [32]. The duration and intensity of physical activities over 10 min that were performed in the previous week were reported. The total physical activity of the individuals was determined using (MET1× minutes × days). Eventually, total physical activity levels were recorded in binary form rather than using a specific cutoff point because individuals with diabetes-related foot ulcers were not very active. As a result, individuals with METs-min/week greater than 297 were considered physically active. People above that score was given a score of one and people below that score received a score of zero. A maximum score of 56 (healthy) and minimum of 0 (unhealthy) indicated adherence to lifestyle score. Participants with lifestyle scores below 11 were categorized in the first tertile, those with scores between 11 and 13 in the second tertile, and those with scores above 13 in the third tertile.

Informed consent

Informed consent

was obtained from all patients prior to their participation in this study. The purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits of the study were thoroughly explained to each participant. Patients were given the opportunity to ask questions and were assured that their participation was entirely voluntary. Confidentiality of the participants’ information was maintained throughout the study in accordance with ethical guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Lifestyle scores were calculated as previously described, and each individual received a total score. The participants were divided into three groups based on their lifestyle scores. To better represent the dietary intake of participants across different foot ulcer sizes, they were also divided into three groups based on the size of the foot ulcer area and protein and zinc intakes. Quantitative variables were compared across tertiles of the lifestyle score using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and qualitative variables were compared using the chi-square test. Additionally, continuous variables were reported as mean (SD) and categorical variables as percentages across lifestyle score tertiles. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and visual inspection of histograms. Since most variables followed an approximately normal distribution, descriptive statistics were presented as mean (SD). Also, because the number of participants was based on the sample size and a relatively high number of this population entered the study, we assumed the population to be normally distributed. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was also used to compare dietary intake across tertiles of foot ulcer area and foot ulcer length, width, and depth across tertiles of protein and zinc intakes separately. The total energy and macronutrient intake were adjusted according to age and sex. All other variables were adjusted for age, sex, and energy score. The association between lifestyle scores and new anthropometric variables was examined using binary logistic regression. The limits for ABSI, BRI, and AVI were reported to be 0.091, 4.62, and 24.5, respectively, based on previous research [31, 33, 34]. The association between lifestyle scores and new anthropometric indices was examined using both crude and adjusted models, and odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Two multivariate adjusted models were used for variables. In the first model, confounding variables such as age, sex, and calorie intake were adjusted. Further adjustments were made to the second model for variables such as shoe and sock condition, debridement, probing, BMI, history of foot ulcers and amputations, history of Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events (MACEs), history of diabetes for more than two years, physical activity, smoking, socioeconomic status, and use of insulin. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26 SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 339 individuals who participated in our study Fig. 1, the proportion of women increased from the first tertile (27.5%) to the third tertile (36.6%) of lifestyle scores. The average weight decreased from 86.9 ± 17.0 kg in the first tertile to 81.8 ± 14.6 kg in the third tertile (P = 0.01). Similarly, the proportion of married individuals decreased from 92.2% in the first tertile to 81.7% in the third tertile (P = 0.03). Additionally, people in the third tertile compared to those in the first tertile of lifestyle score were less physically active (P-value = 0.001) and smoked less (P-value = 0.001). The prevalence of a history of foot ulcers was lower in individuals in the third tertile of lifestyle score compared to those in the first tertile, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.06). Table 1 displays the sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants. Although not statistically significant, systolic blood pressure was clinically higher in the third tertile of the lifestyle score compared to the first (P = 0.07), while diastolic blood pressure was lower (P = 0.10). In addition, triglyceride levels decreased clinically in the third tertile compared to the first tertile of the lifestyle score although not statistically significant (P = 0.11). Table 2 shows the biochemical indices for each participant’s lifestyle tertile. In Tables 3 and 4, information on the intake of dietary components by each participant is presented separately. Individuals who were in the highest tertile of foot ulcer area and had the largest foot ulcer size had higher caloric intake than those in the lowest tertile (P = 0.06) although not statistically significant. Protein (P-value = 0.01) and zinc (P-value = 0.001) intakes were significantly lower in the third tertile of the foot ulcer area than in the first tertile. Riboflavin uptake decreased in the third tertile of foot ulcer area compared to the first tertile; however, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.06) (Table 4). In the protein intake tertiles, the foot ulcer length significantly decreased with increasing protein intake (P = 0.001). The length of the foot ulcer also decreased significantly with increasing zinc intake (P = 0.001); however, the decrease in the width and depth of the foot ulcer was not significant (Table 5). In the ABSI index, there was no significant difference in the crude model (OR: 1.82; CI: 0.99–3.33), and after adjusting for possible confounding factors (OR: 1.70; CI: 0.81–3.56). Also, there was no significant difference in BRI and AVI indices between tertiles of lifestyle score (OR: 0.70 CI: 0.24–2.08) and (OR: 0.91 CI: 0.40–2.06), but from a clinical perspective, we see a decline in both of these indices between tertiles of lifestyle score (Table 6).

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that there may not be a strong association between dietary intake status and the new anthropometric indices. However, we found a significant association between tertiles of dietary protein intake, dietary zinc intake, and foot ulcer length, but not with width or depth. These results are similar to previous studies that have reported associations between dietary factors and diabetes-related foot ulcers [35–42].

Several studies have shown that excessive smoking and high systolic blood pressure can aggravate diabetic nephropathy, and diabetic nephropathy itself has been introduced as a factor that aggravates diabetic neuropathy [43]. It seems that a possible reason for the lack of significant improvement in foot ulcer outcomes among participants in the third tertile of lifestyle score may be the elevated systolic blood pressure observed in this group, which is known to impair microvascular circulation and delay wound healing in individuals with diabetes [44, 45]. On the other hand, physical activity can accelerate the healing of diabetes-related foot ulcers through its effect on muscles and blood circulation [46]. However, studies have shown that general physical activity alone may not significantly improve foot ulcer healing unless it is combined with the use of offloading devices, which are essential for reducing pressure on the wound site and facilitating proper healing [46]. It can therefore be said that light physical activity and the use of tools to reduce pressure on the wound site can have a positive effect on wound healing [46, 47]. Insufficient calorie intake has been observed in individuals with diabetes-related foot ulcers [9]. Excessive caloric intake, particularly when it leads to increased body fat and obesity, may negatively impact metabolic health, including insulin resistance, which can hinder the healing process in individuals with diabetes-related foot ulcers [48, 49]. Additionally, unplanned weight loss is a risk factor for malnutrition and delayed healing of pressure ulcers [50].

Previous studies have investigated the association between diet, nutrients, and diabetes-related foot ulcers. One study revealed that a healthy dietary pattern characterized by high consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, and fish reduces the risk of neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy, and cardiovascular disease [35]. Another study found that several nutrients are associated with foot ulcers in patients with diabetes [36]. These included vitamins D, E, and C, zinc, omega-3 fatty acids, antioxidants, and dietary fiber [51–53]. This review found that a deficiency or lower intake of these nutrients is generally associated with an increased risk of foot ulcers [36]. Another study showed that widespread nutrient deficiencies such as vitamin D, vitamin C, vitamin E, zinc, and proteins were predictive of amputation in patients with foot ulcers [37]. Studies have also shown that zinc may improve the management of diabetes and its related complications, through its role in modulating glucose metabolism signaling and regulating insulin receptors [54]. Zinc supplementation has also been shown to mitigate the size of foot ulcers and blood factors (FBS, HOMA-IR, HDL) associated with diabetes [55]. Therefore, based on the statistically significant low zinc intake in the third tertile of the foot ulcer area, it can be concluded that zinc may affect foot ulcers and blood factors through the mentioned pathways due to the moderate low intake of dietary zinc.

Similar to previous studies, our study showed that protein intake can be considered a factor in foot ulcer healing. Previous studies have also shown that in people with foot ulcers, protein intake is lower than the amount necessary for wound healing [56, 57]. This suggests that adequate protein intake can be effective for healing foot ulcers.

ABSI is an anthropometric index that can better predict obesity and sarcopenia than older indices, such as BMI [13]. In the present study, BMI decreased in the third tertile compared to the first, but ABSI increased, suggesting that sarcopenic obesity was more common in this group, and this fat accumulation in the abdominal area can increase insulin resistance and worsen the complications of diabetes and make diabetes-related foot ulcers difficult to heal [50]. Studies have shown that higher BRI and AVI values are associated with an increased risk of the occurrence of diabetes and its complications [58, 59]. Improving these indicators in individuals living with diabetes can be effective in healing diabetes-related foot ulcers by controlling diabetes indicators. Our study did not find significant associations between lifestyle score and novel anthropometric indices. While the use of ABSI, BRI, and AVI may offer unique insights into body composition, our findings suggest that lifestyle-related factors, as measured here, may not be strongly reflected in these indices within this population [31, 60–62]. These indices are relatively new and their clinical relevance and validity are still being evaluated. It is possible that more traditional anthropometric measurements, such as body mass index (BMI) or waist circumference, may have yielded different results.

A notable strength of the current study is the consideration of several novel anthropometric indices, namely ABSI, BRI, and AVI. This comprehensive approach improves our understanding of body composition and distribution and enables a more holistic assessment of the influence of diet on anthropometric indices in patients with diabetes. This study specifically examined the association between lower protein and zinc intake and foot ulcers in type 2 diabetics [63]. By focusing on this specific complication, this study provides valuable insight into the possible role of dietary factors, such as protein and zinc intake, in the development and severity of foot ulcers [64]. This targeted approach contributes to the existing body of knowledge on the management of foot ulcers in individuals living with diabetes. This study had some limitations that should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design of this study limits the ability to establish a causal relationship between dietary factors and foot ulcers. In addition to the complexity of the relationship between dietary factors and anthropometric indices, several other challenges were encountered during the study. These included the time-consuming nature of completing the information and questionnaires, limited ability to gather more detailed data from individuals with diabetes-related foot ulcers due to their condition, and low participant cooperation. Given these limitations and the rarity of information on this population, we designed a cohort study that will involve preparing questionnaires and gathering more data from various referrals. Additionally, we will conduct further follow-up on food intake and foot ulcer status to gain more insight.

Conclusion

Finally, this study did not find a significant association between lifestyle score status and new anthropometric indices but found a significant association between lower dietary intake of protein and zinc and longer foot ulcer length in individuals with type 2 diabetes. Further studies with larger sample sizes, more comprehensive assessments of dietary factors, and longitudinal designs are needed to elucidate the role of diet in the alteration of anthropometric indices and foot ulcers.
