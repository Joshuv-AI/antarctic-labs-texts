Association Between Internet Addiction and Mental Health Problems in Korean Adolescents: A Community-Based Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Min Ah Joo, Un-Sun Chung, Young Sook Kwack, Bung-Nyun Kim, Na Ri Kang, Duk-Soo Moon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to estimate the prevalence of Internet addiction (IA) among Korean adolescents and examine its association with psychiatric comorbidities and mental health symptoms.MethodsA total of 270 adolescents and their parents participated in this study. Parents completed the Internet Addiction Proneness Scale for Youth: Observer Version and the Diagnostic Predictive Scales, whereas adolescents completed the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale for Children, the Screen for Child Anxiety-Related Disorders, and the Youth Self-Report. Group differences in psychiatric disorders and mental health symptoms across IA levels were analyzed.ResultsOf the participants, 86.3% were classified as general, 11.1% as potential high risk, and 2.6% as high risk for IA. The high risk IA group exhibited significantly higher rates of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (42.9%), major depressive disorder (26.3%), and social anxiety (14.3%) as well as greater attentional and social difficulties. Effect sizes for group differences were small to moderate (ε2=0.041 for social problems; ε2=0.033 for attentional problems). IA scores were positively correlated with attentional difficulties and anxiety. Anxiety was a significant predictor of IA in the regression analysis.ConclusionAdolescents with high IA risk exhibited elevated psychiatric comorbidities and mental health difficulties, with anxiety showing an independent association with IA. These findings suggest incorporating anxiety-management components into IA prevention and care. Given the small sample size of the high risk IA group, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings.Keywords: Internet addiction, Adolescents, Mental health, Psychiatric disorders, Depression, Anxiety

INTRODUCTION

The Internet has become an essential part of daily life for many adolescents, providing access to information, socialization, and entertainment. However, excessive Internet use can lead to behavioral patterns associated with Internet addiction (IA), a growing mental health concern. Adolescents are particularly vulnerable to IA owing to their developmental stage, increased online activities, and social influences [1]. IA is typically characterized by excessive or poorly controlled preoccupations, urges, or behaviors related to Internet use, which can significantly impair daily functioning [2]. Although this condition has been widely studied across various age groups, its impact on adolescents warrants particular attention.

Research suggests that adolescents with IA frequently exhibit higher rates of psychiatric comorbidities, including depression, anxiety disorders, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [3]. These comorbidities complicate the diagnosis of IA and potentially worsen its prognosis by negatively affecting adolescents’ psychosocial functioning and academic performance [4]. For example, depression and anxiety may amplify the dependence on online activities as adolescents seek relief from emotional distress, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates IA [5].

Given the high Internet penetration rate in Korea, concerns about IA among adolescents have grown significantly, making it a critical public health issue. Korea has been at the forefront of addressing IA through national initiatives including prevention and screening programs. Several studies have explored the prevalence and psychological impact of IA in Korean adolescents, particularly in relation to psychiatric symptoms, such as depression, anxiety, and attentionrelated difficulties, as well as behavioral issues, such as aggression and social withdrawal [6-8]. Moreover, excessive Internet and smartphone use among adolescents has been associated with a range of emotional and behavioral problems, including internalizing symptoms and an increased risk of psychiatric comorbidities [6].

Despite the growing body of literature, most studies have been conducted in clinical settings, which may not fully capture the broader adolescent population affected by IA [7]. Adolescents who seek clinical treatment represent only a fraction of those experiencing significant Internet-related difficulties, highlighting the need for community-based research [7]. Furthermore, while previous studies have examined IA in relation to specific mental health conditions, a more comprehensive evaluation encompassing multiple psychiatric and behavioral domains is necessary [6,8].

This study aimed to address these gaps by estimating the prevalence of IA in a community-based adolescent population in Korea, and examining its association with various mental health challenges, including depression, anxiety, social difficulties, and other related conditions. By conducting a broad and inclusive assessment, we sought to provide a more nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between IA and psychiatric symptoms. The findings of this study will contribute to the development of targeted intervention strategies tailored to the needs of Korean adolescents, ultimately informing policies and clinical guidelines for IA prevention and treatment.

METHODS

Participants and procedure

This cross-sectional study recruited adolescents aged 12–18 years and their mothers from Jeju, Korea. Participants were required to have been enrolled in school continuously for at least 6 months prior to the study. Data were collected between September and December 2017, in collaboration with local educational institutions. A total of 270 participants completed the assessment.

Parents completed the Internet Addiction Proneness Scale for Youth: Observer Version to classify adolescents into general, potential high risk, and high risk groups based on their Internet use patterns. Additionally, they completed the Diagnostic Predictive Scale (DPS), a screening tool for psychiatric disorders according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV). Adolescents completed the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale for Children (CES-DC) to assess depressive symptoms, the Screen for Child Anxiety-Related Disorders (SCARED) to evaluate anxiety symptoms, and the Youth Self-Report (YSR) to evaluate a broad range of mental health issues. Differences in the prevalence of psychiatric disorders and mental health problems among the three groups were analyzed.

To ensure accurate data collection, trained mental health professionals distributed and collected the paper-and-pencil surveys. These professionals received prior education on the background and methodology of the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians before their participation. The original study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Jeju National University Hospital in 2017 as “prevalence and risk factors of psychiatric disorders in child and adolescent population-school based research-,” and for further research about IA and mental health problems, a separate IRB approval for the present study was obtained in 2022 (IRB No. 2022-10-001).

Assessment tools

Internet Addiction Proneness Scale for Youth: Observer Version

The Internet Addiction Proneness Scale for Youth, developed by the Internet Addiction Prevention Center of the Korea Information Society Agency (https://iapc.or.kr/), is the primary measure for evaluating the risk of IA among adolescents. This tool, verified for its reliability and structural validity [9], assesses factors such as frequency, duration, and lack of control over Internet usage as well as the associated negative consequences. Given the importance of using culturally adapted tools, this scale was developed specifically for Korean adolescents, allowing for a more accurate assessment of IA and its associated risks. This scale provides a reliable framework for identifying IA risk levels. Adolescents were categorized into three groups based on their scores: high risk users (≥35 points), potential risk users (32–34 points), and general users (≤31 points).

Diagnostic Predictive Scales

The DPS is a brief screening instrument derived from the Diagnostic Interview Schedule for Children, Version IV (DISC-IV), a structured diagnostic interview based on DSM-IV criteria [10]. The DPS assesses psychiatric symptoms exhibited by children over the past year, covering a range of disorders, including oppositional defiant disorder, generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), social anxiety disorder, panic disorder, agoraphobia, separation anxiety disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), ADHD, conduct disorder (CD), major depressive disorder (MDD), eating disorder, tic disorder, and elimination disorder. The instrument consists of 92 questions evaluating symptoms and eight additional items assessing children’s mood and behavior in different settings, such as home, school, and social environments. The DPS is a reliable screening tool for identifying DSM-IV disorders, with well-established psychometric validity [11].

A previous epidemiological study of children and adolescents in Korea expanded the DPS by incorporating 33 additional diagnostic items from the DISC-IV that were not originally included. These additional items assess conditions such as selective mutism, eating disorders, elimination disorders, PTSD, tic disorders, nicotine dependence, pica, and trichotillomania [12]. This study used this modified version of the DPS, with mothers serving as primary respondents, to assess their offspring’s psychiatric symptoms. Adolescent-specific cutoff scores were applied based on the criteria established by Cubo et al. [13].

Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale for Children

The CES-DC, developed by the National Institute of Mental Health [14], measures depressive symptoms in individuals aged 6–17 years. A standardized version tailored for the Korean population was used [15], with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.

Screen for Child Anxiety-Related Disorders

The SCARED is a 41-item self-report questionnaire originally developed by Birmaher et al. [16] and later adapted for Korean use [17]. It assesses anxiety levels on a 3-point Likert scale (0=not at all, 1=sometimes, and 2=often). Higher scores indicate increased anxiety severity.

Youth Self-Report

The YSR, initially developed by Achenbach and Edelbrock (1983) [18], is widely used to assess various mental health problems. This study employed the Korean version of the YSR [19], which includes competence and syndrome subscales. The syndrome subscale evaluates eight empirically derived problem areas, including anxiety/depression, withdrawal/depression, somatic complaints, and others.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 18.0 (SPSS Inc.). Descriptive statistics summarized the participant demographics. Chi-squared tests were used to evaluate the prevalence of psychiatric disorders across the three IA groups (general, potential high risk, and high risk). The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess differences in mental health problems. These initial analyses revealed significant disparities in psychiatric symptoms across the groups. Correlation analyses were conducted to explore the relationship between IA severity and various psychometric variables. Finally, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between IA and mental health issues, controlling for age, sex, and socioeconomic status (SES).

RESULTS

The study included 270 adolescents comprising 93 males (34.6%) and 177 females (65.4%). Most participants were high school students (66.3%). Based on the Internet Addiction Proneness Scale, 86.3% of the participants (n=233) were in the general group without IA, 11.1% (n=30) were in the potential high risk IA group, and 2.6% (n=7) were in the high risk IA group (Table 1).

Significant differences in social and attentional problems in the YSR were found among the three IA groups (Table 3). The high risk IA group displayed the highest levels of social and attentional problems compared to the potential high risk IA group and general group without IA. To supplement the interpretation of group differences, effect sizes were calculated using epsilon squared (ε2), which indicated small to moderate magnitudes: ε2=0.041 for social problems and ε2=0.033 for attentional problems. Given the small sample size of the high risk IA group (n=7), the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to account for distributional differences in the data. These results support the association between higher IA severity, increased social difficulties, and attentional deficits in adolescents.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the prevalence of IA among Korean adolescents and examined its association with psychiatric comorbidities and mental health challenges. These findings provide critical insights into the relationship between IA and mental health issues, and emphasize the need for targeted interventions.

The results revealed that 13.7% of participants were classified as either potential high risk or high risk for IA, with 2.6% falling into the high risk category. These findings highlight the significant public health concerns regarding IA among adolescents, particularly in regions with high Internet penetration, such as Korea.

Adolescence is a period of rapid psychological maturation and susceptibility to problematic Internet use [20]. Excessive Internet use has been found to be detrimental to family relationships, self-esteem, life satisfaction, and academic performance [21]. This study revealed that the mental state of adolescents was significantly associated with IA.

Psychiatric disorders including ADHD, MDD, and social anxiety were significantly more prevalent in the high risk group than in the general group. These findings emphasize the importance of considering psychiatric comorbidities in IA, as evidenced by previous research suggesting that such disorders coexist with IA and may exacerbate its severity [22].

For instance, the high prevalence of ADHD (42.9%) in the high risk IA group supports the idea that impulsivity and poor self-regulation contribute to excessive Internet use [23]. Similarly, the increased prevalence of MDD (26.3%) in the high risk IA group suggests that depressive symptoms, such as low motivation and anhedonia, may drive adolescents toward excessive Internet use as a means of emotional escape or coping [2]. Furthermore, the high rate of social anxiety (14.3%) among high risk individuals with IA implies that adolescents with social avoidance tendencies may turn to online interactions as a substitute for real-life social engagement, potentially reinforcing IA behaviors [24].

Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between IA and mental health problems, particularly attentional difficulties and anxiety. These findings are consistent with those of prior studies, indicating that adolescents with higher levels of IA often struggle with attentional control and emotional regulation [3]. The regression analysis identified anxiety as independently associated with IA in adjusted analyses, underscoring its clinical relevance for screening and intervention.

Although anxiety emerged as a significant predictor of IA, the explanatory power of the regression model was relatively low (adjusted R2=0.061), suggesting that other unmeasured factors may contribute substantially to IA.

Notably, in contrast to previous studies, CES-DC scores in this study were not significantly associated with IA. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the roles of depression and anxiety in problematic Internet use. While depression reflects broader emotional distress, anxiety may directly influence compulsive Internet behavior, particularly when adolescents use online activities to cope with stress or social discomfort. Additionally, the CES-DC may not fully capture Internet-specific affect regulation patterns, potentially underestimating the relationship between depressive symptoms and IA.

Previous studies have identified other potential predictors of IA, such as impulsivity, academic stress, peer pressure, family conflict, and a lack of parental supervision [25]. These psychosocial and behavioral factors were not assessed in the current study, which may have contributed to the limited explanatory power of our regression model. Future research incorporating these variables could enhance the predictive validity and offer a more comprehensive understanding of IA risk factors.

Together with our finding that anxiety was independently associated with high risk IA, prior studies suggest that adolescents with elevated anxiety may use the Internet as a maladaptive coping strategy, potentially reinforcing IA-related behaviors [24,25]. This reinforces the importance of the early identification of anxiety symptoms and providing alternative strategies for emotional regulation to mitigate the progression of IA.

Recent research has additionally highlighted significant changes in Internet usage patterns following the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. One study reported that Internet use among adolescents increased notably during the pandemic, with a shift from gaming to video consumption and social media engagement [26]. While problematic Internet use was previously associated primarily with gaming, recent findings suggest that video streaming and social media are major contributors. This shift carries important implications for understanding and addressing IA, as different types of Internet usage may have distinct psychological impacts. Given the growing reliance on digital platforms, future studies should examine the long-term effects of these changes and their associations with adolescent mental health.

This study tentatively suggests that cultural factors in Korea, such as intense academic pressure and societal expectations, may contribute to increased social anxiety among adolescents. The strong emphasis on academic achievement may exacerbate social anxiety by fostering competition, fear of failure, and interpersonal stress [27]. Although these cultural specifics were not directly measured in the current study, the findings indicate potential areas for future research exploring the unique impact of such environmental stressors on IA among Korean youth. Given the significant role of anxiety in IA risk, further investigation is warranted into the processes by which sociocultural stressors influence adolescent mental health and patterns of Internet use in Korea.

This study has several limitations. First, due to its crosssectional design, it was not possible to establish causal relationships between IA and psychiatric comorbidities. It remains unclear whether these symptoms are antecedents or consequences of IA, or whether they mutually reinforce each other over time. Longitudinal studies are necessary to determine whether psychiatric conditions predispose adolescents to IA, or whether IA exacerbates these conditions over time.

Second, the high risk IA group comprised only seven participants, which may have limited the statistical power of the group comparisons and increased the risk of Type II errors. To address this limitation, we reported the effect sizes for key variables showing significant group differences. These effect sizes (ε2=0.041 for social problems; ε2=0.033 for attentional problems) help contextualize the findings despite limited statistical power. They offer supplemental insights into the magnitude of the observed differences and reinforce the need for larger future samples.

Third, this study relied exclusively on subjective reports, including parent-rated and self-reported instruments, without incorporating objective data, such as actual Internet usage time or digital activity logs. This may have introduced reporter bias and limited the precision of the IA assessment. Incorporating objective measures in future research could enhance the validity and accuracy of IA evaluation.

Fourth, the data were collected before the COVID-19 pandemic. The pandemic has significantly altered patterns of internet use [5], with a marked increase in online activities. Future research should consider the impact of these changes on IA and its associated mental health challenges, as understanding the influence of the pandemic is crucial for developing effective interventions.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important insights into IA in Korean adolescents, with a communitybased sample and multiple psychiatric assessments, enhancing our understanding of IA and its mental health associations. The strong association between IA and anxiety suggests that intervention programs targeting anxiety reduction could effectively lower the risk of IA among adolescents. Schools and mental health organizations should implement psychoeducational programs to address IA and its underlying psychiatric comorbidities. Furthermore, screening tools such as the Internet Addiction Proneness Scale for Youth can be integrated into routine assessments to identify at risk individuals early and facilitate timely intervention. Integrating early screening with targeted interventions may help reduce IA risk and support adolescent mental health in the digital era.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the significant prevalence of IA among Korean adolescents and its strong associations with psychiatric comorbidities, particularly anxiety, attentional difficulties, and depressive disorders. Given that anxiety showed a statistically significant independent association with IA in adjusted analyses, targeted interventions focusing on anxiety reduction and healthy coping mechanisms may be crucial. Additionally, as Internet usage patterns have evolved postpandemic, further research is needed to assess their long-term impact on adolescent mental health. Given the small sample size of the high risk IA group, the generalizability of our findings remains limited. Further studies with larger and more representative samples are required to validate these results. Effective IA management requires a multifaceted approach that integrates culturally sensitive tools, early screening, and comprehensive support systems to safeguard adolescent wellbeing in the digital era.
