Lumen-based en bloc extraction of a bound lead pair using a rotational mechanical sheath

Introduction

Atrial lead failure in a dual-chamber system may necessitate ventricular-only pacing, such as ventricle-paced, ventricle-sensed, inhibited mode, thereby eliminating atrioventricular (AV) synchrony and exacerbating symptoms, particularly in patients with heart failure. When a chronic lead becomes nonfunctional, management may require lead abandonment or extraction with system revision.1 A major risk factor for extraction complications is lead-to-lead binding,2 which manifests as unit motion where inseparable leads move in tandem and create a recurrent point of resistance despite standard techniques. We present a case in which early recognition of functional inseparability prompted escalation to single-sheath en bloc extraction using a larger-lumen rotational mechanical sheath to envelop the bound lead pair, enabling successful removal via the superior venous approach.

Case report

A 50-year-old male with hypokinetic nondilated cardiomyopathy, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 41%, and a dual-chamber pacemaker (lead dwell time of ∼5 years) implanted for Mobitz II AV block, presented with heart failure exacerbated by atrial exit block and loss of AV synchrony (Figure 1). Device interrogation confirmed complete AV block and demonstrated a progressive loss of atrial pacing support during the year of extraction: atrial and ventricular pacing were 99.45% and 99.99% in January, with loss of atrial capture. Given atrial lead malfunction with resultant loss of AV synchrony, mildly reduced left ventricular systolic function (LVEF 41%), and complete AV block with a persistent high ventricular pacing burden, the plan was made for transvenous extraction of both leads and replacement with a magnetic resonance imaging–conditional dual-chamber pacemaker incorporating left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP).

The procedure was performed with a defined safety setup including a prepositioned superior vena cava (SVC) occlusion balloon, intracardiac echocardiography, temporary venous pacing, radial and femoral arterial access, a central venous line, and cardiothoracic surgical backup. The preexisting ventricular lead was a Medtronic 3830 originally positioned in the para-Hisian/basal septal region at the index implant; however, paced QRS morphology before extraction was consistent with basal septal myocardial capture rather than true His-bundle or left bundle branch capture, prompting revision to LBBAP. Extraction was initiated via a superior approach using an 11F Shortie sheath followed by an 11F rotational-locking mechanical sheath (Evolution RL, Cook Medical) advanced through an outer SteadySheath Evolution tissue stabilization sheath (Cook Medical), which incorporates a textured, radiopaque stainless-steel distal tip for improved fluoroscopic visibility, tissue engagement, and stabilization during controlled advancement. Despite countertraction, sheath advancement repeatedly stalled at a fixed adhesion plane near the SVC–right atrial (RA) junction. Throughout these attempts, dense interlead binding prevented independent advancement. This translated force into deformation of the 3830 lead complex (Figure 2B and 2C), with subsequent conductor disruption and snowplowing creating a widened tangle. To prevent fragment embolization, rotational activation was discontinued. The strategy was changed by upsizing to a 13F Evolution RL sheath and an 18.1F inner diameter outer SteadySheath. Although the 13F inner sheath failed to envelop the widened tangle, the outer SteadySheath was advanced to fully encompass the bound lead complex for controlled en bloc extraction (Figure 2D and 2E; Supplemental Video 1). As the sheath approached the RA, the bound ventricular lead created an eccentric traction vector on the atrial lead tip, requiring careful manual countertraction to minimize atrial tenting during final release.

A magnetic resonance imaging–conditional system was then implanted. The ventricular lead was positioned for nonselective LBBAP (Figure 3A), and the 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated a morphology consistent with nonselective LBBAP (Figure 3B). Continuous intracardiac echocardiography monitoring showed no pericardial effusion throughout extraction and reimplantation, and the patient remained hemodynamically stable without the need for SVC balloon deployment or surgical intervention. Discussion

Lead-to-lead binding is a recognized technical challenge during transvenous lead extraction and is strongly associated with greater procedural complexity and major complications.2 When binding prevents independent lead mobilization, standard single-lead extraction may become ineffective or unsafe.3 In our case, functional inseparability from interlead binding was recognized by the combination of a fixed point of resistance at the SVC–RA junction and persistent unit motion of both leads under traction. In this setting, continued advancement against a bound lead complex for single-lead extraction can pull the SVC wall into the direct path of the sheath tip, significantly increasing the risk of venous laceration.4

Rather than prolonged separation attempts at a high-risk anatomic location, we transitioned to a lumen-based envelopment strategy. Because the 3830 is a lumenless, small-diameter lead, it may be prone to elongation, buckling, and conductor externalization under high traction when significant lead-to-lead binding prevents coaxial progression. When disruption is recognized, continued rotation or forward advancement of the inner rotational sheath risks further conductor shredding, severing the lead, and embolizing fragments. Therefore, in our case, we ceased rotational activation and advanced only the outer support sheath to encompass the widened lead complex and achieve controlled en bloc removal, avoiding repeated forceful separation maneuvers and lowering the risk of venous injury. Importantly, this principle is not specific to a 13F sheath setup itself, but rather to selecting an outer sheath with sufficient working lumen to envelop the bound lead complex safely. Feasibility depends on the combined diameter and geometry of the leads. Our lead combination (a conventional 6F active-fixation Medtronic 5076 atrial lead and a 4.1F lumenless Medtronic 3830 ventricular lead) likely favored successful envelopment. The same sheath combination may not be suitable for 2 larger conventional active-fixation leads or other bulkier pairings. Furthermore, en bloc extraction must be applied selectively. As the leads diverge in the RA, the presence of a second lead may limit optimal sheath alignment with the targeted lead tip; therefore, sheath trajectory and traction vectors should be reassessed continuously. If coaxial alignment at the myocardial interface is lost or atrial tenting becomes excessive, continued en bloc advancement should be abandoned.

Although en bloc extraction via a femoral approach has been described for overcoiled temporary or deeply fixed septal leads,5,6 our case illustrates an intraprocedural sign of functional inseparability that prompted early lumen-based en bloc extraction for chronic leads from the original implant vein via the superior approach. This course avoided the time, complexity, and potential vascular risk associated with escalation to an inferior (transfemoral) bailout strategy, including femoral snaring or tandem superior/inferior countertraction,7 while facilitating immediate physiological system revision. Given the substantial ventricular pacing and LVEF 41%, we revised the system to nonselective LBBAP to mitigate the risk of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy, in accordance with the 2023 Heart Rhythm Society/Asia Pacific Heart Rhythm Society/Latin American Heart Rhythm Society cardiac physiological pacing guidelines.8
