Optimising bionic pore designs in a spiral blade implant to enhance bone ingrowth and stability: A combined CFD and large-animal study
Authors: Meng-Xuan Yao, Tai-Long Shi, Yu-Qin Zhang, Yi-Fan Zhang, Chuan Ren, Shi-Wu Dong, Zhi-Yong Hou, Ying-Ze Zhang, Hong-Zhi Lv, Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of different Bionic Pore geometries (circular, triangular, and rectangular) and diameters (0.8 mm, 1 mm, 2 mm, and 3 mm) on fluid shear stress (FSS) distribution and bone ingrowth in a bionic spiral blade implant.MethodsTwelve Bionic Pore structures were modeled using CAD software, and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis was performed to simulate the FSS distribution in a bone environment. Based on these simulation results, the circular hole design was selected and manufactured via electron beam melting (EBM). It was then incorporated into a bionic spiral blade (length: 50 mm, outer diameter: 6 mm, and a central canal of 2 mm for surgical pin placement). Four female sheep (weighing 35–40 kg) were implanted with these spiral blades, divided into 3-month and 6-month follow-up groups. After surgery, X-ray imaging, micro-CT, and histological evaluation were used to assess implant stability, bone bridging, and tissue response within the bionic pores.ResultsCFD simulations showed that circular pores generated a more optimal and uniform FSS distribution; the 0.8 mm circular pore produced an average FSS of 0.0086 ± 0.00013 Pa, representing a 79.1% and 68.0% reduction compared to identically sized triangular (0.0412 ± 0.0176 Pa) and rectangular pores (0.0269 ± 0.0056 Pa), respectively. In vivo experiments demonstrated that small-diameter holes significantly accelerated osteogenesis. At 3 months, the 0.8 mm group achieved a bone tissue area ratio (BTA/BHA) of 15.44 ± 1.94%, which was 98.7% higher than that of the 3 mm group (7.77 ± 3.03%). By 6 months, the 0.8 mm group reached a BTA/BHA of 39.79 ± 7.80%, demonstrating a 131.2% greater bone occupancy compared to the 3 mm group (17.21 ± 3.97%, p < 0.01), alongside significantly higher bone volume fractions (BV/TV, p < 0.0001). Larger holes (2 mm and 3 mm) initially exhibited fibrous tissue formation, but as the pores gradually narrowed over time, local FSS increased, supporting later-stage bone formation. Radiographic and histological analyses confirmed that the bionic spiral blades remained stable in vivo.ConclusionThis study indicates that optimizing the geometry and size of bionic pores can improve FSS conditions and promote bone ingrowth. Small-diameter circular holes, in particular, favor early and robust osseointegration. Moreover, this work underscores the potential of combining advanced manufacturing techniques with mechanobiological principles to design customized bionic implants for enhanced orthopedic outcomes. Importantly, the close agreement between CFD predictions and in vivo outcomes validates computational fluid dynamics as a practical prescreening tool for optimizing pore designs in load-bearing implants.The translational potential of this articleThis "bench-to-bedside" research has established a robust framework for clinical translation. Leveraging the aforementioned optimized parameters, a novel bionic internal fixation device has been developed, which has successfully secured a U.S. utility patent and obtained a medical device registration certificate. This bionic device facilitates trabecular bone ingrowth and remodeling in parallel with cortical bone healing. Consequently, the findings of this study provide a validated, evidence-based foundation for the future design and development of bionic orthopedic implants.

Introduction

Orthopaedic implants play a critical role in restoring mobility and function for patients with skeletal injuries or degenerative conditions [1,2]. Despite significant advancements in implant materials and designs, improving implant–bone integration remains a persistent challenge. Mechanical stability alone is insufficient for long-term success; rather, robust and stable bone ingrowth around the implant is essential for preventing loosening and failure [[3], [4], [5]]. In particular, proximal femoral fractures, which often occur in elderly populations or those with compromised bone quality, frequently require secure fixation to promote healing and prevent complications such as avascular necrosis or non-union [6,7]. Achieving stable fixation in the femoral neck region is especially difficult due to its unique biomechanical demands, including load-bearing and range of motion requirements [[8], [9], [10]]. Consequently, further innovation in implant architecture is crucial for enhanced therapeutic outcomes and patient quality of life.

In recent years, researchers have turned their attention to Bionic Pore designs—strategic internal cavities or pores intended to mimic naturally occurring structures in bone. Such designs have shown promise in improving osseointegration through two principal mechanisms. First, porous architectures can facilitate fluid flow and nutrient transport, creating microenvironments conducive to osteoblast adhesion and vascular infiltration [[11], [12], [13]]. Second, these internal features can reduce stress shielding by better matching the implant's stiffness to that of the host bone. This improved load sharing helps maintain the physiologically relevant mechanical stimuli that are essential for continuous bone remodeling [[14], [15], [16]]. Nevertheless, optimal pore geometries, orientations, and sizes remain an area of ongoing debate, with evidence suggesting that smaller pore diameters might encourage faster bone bridging, while larger pores could support the development of more mature and interconnected trabecular structures over time [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. However, more studies are needed to verify the specific effects of different pore sizes on the process of bone regeneration and their internal mechanisms, and the experimental results are not consistent. Li et al. [21] reported in their study that, compared with smaller pore sizes (200 μm and 400 μm), the larger pore size (600 μm) reduced the foreign body response and enhanced bone regeneration. Chen et al. [22] studied the effects of pore size and porosity on cell proliferation, osteogenesis, and bone ingrowth in selective laser melted Ti6Al4V ELI porous scaffolds. Scaffolds with pore sizes of 500 μm, 600 μm, and 700 μm, and porosities of 60% and 70% were tested in vitro and in vivo. The results showed that a pore size of 500 μm and a porosity of 60% facilitated optimal cell proliferation, osteogenic differentiation, and bone ingrowth. Smaller pore sizes and lower porosities were associated with increased cell adhesion, differentiation, and bone formation.

Among the diverse factors driving bone tissue growth and regeneration, fluid shear stress (FSS) has gained particular attention for its effects on osteoblast function and extracellular matrix deposition [[23], [24], [25]]. As interstitial fluids circulate through bone or biomimetic pores, they stimulate mechanosensory pathways that can promote cell differentiation and new bone formation [26]. However, the distribution of shear stress depends heavily on the pore geometry, surface roughness, and local flow dynamics. For instance, Ali et al. [27] studied the permeability and shear stress distribution of bone scaffolds with different geometric structures (such as TPMS and lattice architectures) through computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis. They found that scaffolds with less pore size variation exhibited a more uniform shear stress distribution, while high-variability architectures (e.g., Schwarz primitive models) resulted in a less uniform shear stress distribution. Additionally, Moradkhani et al. [28] analyzed the impact of different geometric architectures of bone scaffolds on shear stress distribution using the finite element method. Their research revealed that complex implicit surfaces (such as Gyroid and HP architectures) provided a more uniform shear stress distribution compared to simpler architectures (e.g., IWP). Thus, understanding and controlling FSS via carefully engineered bionic pores has become central to the design of next-generation orthopaedic implants.

While previous studies have demonstrated the promise of Bionic Pore designs in promoting osseointegration, several gaps remain. First, there is limited understanding of how hole geometry (e.g., circular, triangular, rectangular) and diameter influence local fluid dynamics and subsequent bone ingrowth in load-bearing situations. Second, few studies provide a direct comparison of these variables in a controlled in vivo model to observe how early-stage bone formation progresses into more mature tissue. Therefore, a systematic approach combining CFD simulations and large animal testing is warranted to comprehensively evaluate the performance of such Bionic Pore designs. To our knowledge, no prior study has employed CFD-guided pore geometry screening as a predictive tool followed by prospective validation in a large-animal intramedullary fixation model. Existing CFD studies of bone scaffolds remain purely computational or validated only in vitro, whereas the present study closes this translational loop by demonstrating that CFD-derived fluid shear stress predictions can prospectively identify optimal pore parameters whose superiority is subsequently confirmed in a clinically relevant ovine model.

In summary, porous and biomimetic designs have emerged as pivotal trends in the evolution of internal fracture fixation devices. However, the underlying mechanisms by which biomimetic pores influence bone regeneration—specifically through the modulation of the local mechanobiological microenvironment—remain to be fully elucidated. Against this backdrop, the present study utilizes a helical blade intramedullary nail as a research platform to systematically investigate the regulatory mechanisms of various biomimetic pore morphologies and geometric parameters on fracture fixation and osseointegration.Accordingly, it is hypothesized that the geometric configuration and spatial dimensions of the pores fundamentally regulate the local FSS microenvironment, which in turn dictates early osteogenic differentiation and longitudinal bone ingrowth. To test this hypothesis, a functional biomimetic strategy was implemented by selecting fundamental topological primitives—namely circular, triangular, and rectangular geometries—to evaluate their efficacy in promoting local trabecular remodeling. These specific geometries were selected as they represent the fundamental and standardized structural elements in additive manufacturing. Furthermore, a systematically defined size range (0.8–3.0 mm) was established to encompass critical physiological thresholds, ranging from initial cellular adhesion to macroscopic bone bridging. To validate the aforementioned hypothesis, a comprehensive research framework was developed, integrating computer-aided design (CAD), CFD, electron beam melting (EBM) additive manufacturing, and large-animal trials. Bone healing was comprehensively evaluated through the quantitative analysis of intra-channel fluid flow and shear stress distribution, complemented by post-implantation X-ray imaging, micro-CT, and histological examination in a sheep model. The present study seeks to provide a theoretical foundation for the optimization of porous internal fixation devices and to offer actionable clinical solutions for enhancing fracture healing efficiency.

Methods

Parameter design of bionic pores

In this study, we used CAD software (e.g., SolidWorks) to design 12 different bionic pore structures on a cylinder with a diameter of 6 mm and a height of 5 mm. The bionic pore shapes included circular, triangular, and rectangular, with pore sizes (d) of 3 mm, 2 mm, 1 mm, and 0.8 mm. For circular pores, d represents the diameter; for triangular pores, d/2 refers to the distance from the center to the apex; and for rectangular pores, d denotes the side length. All pores were arranged perpendicular to the cylinder's longitudinal axis (Fig. 1). After finalizing the design, the model was exported in STL format and refined using software such as MeshLab to ensure that the size and shape of each pore met the requirements for additive manufacturing.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of different bionic pore shapes and sizes. CFD analysis of interstitial fluid flow in bone with various bionic pores for evaluating reconstruction performance

We conducted a fluid-mechanical analysis of different bionic pore models within a bone tissue environment. To accurately simulate the internal conditions after implantation, both the fluid and solid domains were designed as cylinders. The designed bionic pores were placed within a cylindrical fluid domain (diameter 10 mm, height 10 mm), which was divided into fluid and solid domains (Fig. 2). A coupled fluid-solid model and numerical simulation were then established using the ANSYS 2022 R1 platform. Fluid entered through an inlet, flowed through the bionic pores, and exerted stress on the pore walls. Given the low inlet velocity and an expected Reynolds number below 10, we adopted a laminar flow model and employed the steady-state Navier–Stokes equations for calculations. The fluid was modeled with a density of 1060 kg/m3 [29,30] and a viscosity of 0.003 kg/(m·s) [29] to simulate blood properties; the inlet velocity was set to 0.002 m/s [31], the walls were set as no-slip boundaries, and the outlet was set to 0 Pa [32,33]. The fluid domain was meshed with a combination of hexahedral and tetrahedral elements. After initialization, iterative calculations were performed while monitoring the residuals (e.g., velocity and pressure). Once the residuals dropped below 10−4 and the flow field distribution became stable, convergence was considered achieved. Finally, post-processing was conducted to evaluate shear stress on the pore walls and its potential effects on osteocyte adhesion and osteoblast activation.Fig. 2Schematic representation of the fluid and solid domains used for CFD analysis of bionic pores with different diameters (3 mm, 2 mm, 1 mm, and 0.8 mm) and geometries (circular, triangular, and rectangular). Each model consists of a cylindrical fluid domain (10 mm in diameter × 10 mm in height), with the Bionic Pore embedded perpendicularly to the cylinder's long axis. The inlet and outlet boundary conditions are applied at the top and bottom, respectively, to simulate fluid flow through the internal pore structure. The solid portion (green) represents the implant material, while the transparent region indicates the fluid domain.

Fabrication of bionic helical blades

Based on the above fluid-mechanical analysis, circular bionic pores were selected for further study. Using SolidWorks (Dassault Systèmes), we designed circular bionic pores with diameters of 3 mm, 2 mm, 1 mm, and 0.8 mm along the long axis of a PFNA (proximal femoral nail antirotation) helical blade, with each pore oriented perpendicular to the blade's axis. The helical blade was designed to be 50 mm in length, 6 mm in outer diameter, and contained a through-hole of 2 mm in diameter to accommodate a 2 mm K-wire during surgery for precise placement (Fig. 3A). The design was then exported in STL format, and the blade was manufactured using Arcam EBM Technology's electron beam melting technique, which selectively fuses Ti6Al4V Grade 23 ELI alloy powder layer by layer in a vacuum. After printing, support structures were removed, and the blade underwent heat treatment to relieve residual stress, followed by sandblasting to increase surface roughness. Quality control was performed via micro-CT scanning and visual inspection to ensure that the size, shape, and distribution of the pores matched the design specifications. Thus, bionic helical blades with optimized structural and biological properties were successfully obtained (Fig. 3B).Fig. 3Design and fabrication of the bionic helical blade. Large animal experiments

To further verify bone regeneration within the bionic pores on the helical blade, we used sheep as a large-animal model. Seven healthy adult female sheep (35–40 kg) underwent comprehensive preoperative examinations to confirm their health status. The experiment was divided into two duration groups: a 3-month group (n = 3) and a 6-month group (n = 4) to observe bone integration at different healing stages.

The intact bone model was deliberately selected as a first-principles approach to isolate the independent effects of pore geometry on bone ingrowth, without the confounding variables introduced by fracture healing—including fracture gap variability, periosteal disruption, callus formation, and micromotion magnitude. This methodological approach is consistent with established paradigms in translational porous implant research, wherein osteoconductive capacity is first validated in intact bone prior to evaluation in fracture models [4,5]. The sample size (n = 3 at 3 months; n = 4 at 6 months) conforms to the minimum requirements stipulated in ISO 10993-6 (Biological Evaluation of Medical Devices—Tests for Local Effects After Implantation) for large-animal studies. Critically, each animal received implants incorporating all four pore diameters, thereby serving as its own internal control and substantially reducing inter-animal variability while increasing statistical power.

All sheep were fasted for 12 h before surgery. They were first sedated with an intramuscular injection of 2% xylazine hydrochloride (0.05 mg/kg; Huavet Animal Health Products Co., Ltd., China), followed by intravenous administration of 20 mL of 2% propofol (Fresenius Kabi, Germany) and 1.4 mg/kg carprofen (Zoetis, Belgium), after which endotracheal intubation was performed. During surgery, anesthesia was maintained with 1.5% isoflurane (Baxter, USA) and supplemented with additional 2% propofol as needed.

With the sheep under deep anesthesia, they were positioned laterally on the operating table. After routine shaving, disinfection, and sterile draping, a small incision was made on the lateral side of the greater trochanter to expose the bone surface. A 2 mm K-wire was inserted approximately 1 cm below the greater trochanter into the femoral neck; its position and orientation were verified via X-ray. A hollow drill was then used to create a cortical opening compatible with the diameter of the bionic helical blade, after which the blade was slowly screwed into the femoral neck. Once the blade was confirmed to be stable and no significant soft tissue damage was present, the incision was closed in layers. Postoperatively, penicillin was administered for 5 days to prevent infection, and the wound was checked daily for about 1.5 weeks. Three sheep were sacrificed at 3 months and four at 6 months post-surgery. The proximal femurs were harvested, wrapped in saline-moistened gauze, and stored at −20°C until further analyses.

All animal experiments (permit number: BMWFW-66.010/0073-WF/V/3b/2015) were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University. Procedures involving animals were carried out in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Chinese Ministry of Public Health and the U.S. National Institutes of Health, and were reviewed and approved by the Animal Experiment Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Baotou Medical College. The “3Rs” principle—Reduction, Replacement, and Refinement—was strictly followed throughout the study.

Radiological analysis

X-ray examinations were performed at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively to preliminarily assess the stability of the bionic helical blade within the femoral neck and observe any potential bone resorption. Subsequently, the samples were scanned using a Bruker Skyscan 1276 (Bruker, Germany) micro-CT system. To prevent changes in bone microarchitecture due to dehydration, specimens were kept moist during scanning. The raw projection data were reconstructed into 3D volumes using NRecon (Bruker, Germany), followed by post-processing and quantitative analysis with MIMICS and CTAn. To ensure reproducibility and minimize potential bias, bone tissue within the pores was quantified using a semi-automatic segmentation workflow. First, a specific Region of Interest (ROI) corresponding strictly to the internal geometric volume of each bionic pore was manually defined by a blinded investigator to exclude the titanium implant and the external host bone. Subsequently, an automated global thresholding algorithm, based on predefined grayscale values, was applied within this ROI to accurately segment the mineralized newly formed bone from soft tissue and void spaces. The main parameters measured included bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) and bone mineral density (BMD) within the bionic pores, providing a comprehensive evaluation of new bone formation and changes in bone quality.

Histological analysis

After being fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 2 weeks, the specimens were rinsed in running water for 24 h. Gradual dehydration was then performed using graded ethanol (70%–95%, each step for 24 h). The specimens were subsequently immersed in a series of ethanol-Technovit 7200 resin mixtures at ratios of 3:7 (2 days), 1:1 (2 days), pure resin I (7 days), and pure resin II (3 days), followed by light-curing for embedding and polymerization. After polymerization, the embedded blocks were sectioned to a thickness of approximately 200 μm using an EXAKT 300CP cutting system (EXAKT, Germany). The sections were then ground and polished using an EXAKT 400S grinding system with 320-, 800-, and 1200-grit sandpaper until the thickness reached around 20 μm, and were further polished with 4000-grit sandpaper. The sections were subsequently stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson's trichrome.

Images of the stained hard-tissue sections were imported into ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) for quantitative analysis. Through grayscale processing and threshold segmentation, the bone tissue area within the bionic pore and the total pore area were measured. The ratio (Bone Tissue Area/Bionic Pore Area × 100%) was then calculated to assess the degree of bone regeneration within the pore.

Fig. 6 further provides quantitative comparisons of FSS under various pore shapes and diameters. For circular pores (Fig. 6A), FSS increased with pore size, measured at 0.0086 ± 0.00013 Pa for 0.8 mm, 0.0125 ± 0.00005 Pa for 1 mm, 0.0214 ± 0.00011 Pa for 2 mm, and 0.0329 ± 0.00010 Pa for 3 mm. In triangular pores (Fig. 6B), FSS was highest at 0.8 mm (0.0412 ± 0.0176 Pa), dropped to the lowest at 1 mm (0.0216 ± 0.0082 Pa), and then maintained intermediate values at 2 mm (0.0378 ± 0.0123 Pa) and 3 mm (0.0379 ± 0.0109 Pa). This indicates larger fluctuations in FSS at smaller triangular pore sizes, stabilizing at medium and larger sizes. For rectangular pores (Fig. 6C), the highest FSS (0.0558 ± 0.0182 Pa) was observed at 1 mm, followed by 3 mm (0.0478 ± 0.0100 Pa), 2 mm (0.0360 ± 0.0094 Pa), and 0.8 mm (0.0269 ± 0.0056 Pa), showing significant differences in FSS across mid-to-small pore sizes. Overall, the circular pores achieved the most uniform FSS distribution and thus may provide a more stable flow environment.Fig. 6Quantitative analysis of FSS distributions for bionic pores with various shapes and sizes under constant flow. Micro-CT cross-sectional images (Fig. 8) and their corresponding quantitative metrics (Fig. 9) complementarily demonstrate the progression of bone ingrowth within bionic pores of all diameters (3 mm, 2 mm, 1 mm, 0.8 mm). Specifically, at 3 months, larger pores (3 mm, 2 mm) visually showed nascent trabeculae primarily along the pore edges, forming relatively sparse bridging structures (Fig. 8A). This visual evidence directly translates to the quantitative outcomes, where these larger pores exhibited relatively low bone volume fractions (BV/TV). In contrast, smaller pores (1 mm, 0.8 mm) visually exhibited higher levels of bone filling at 3 months (Fig. 8A), which was statistically corroborated by the 0.8 mm group exhibiting a significantly higher BV/TV than both the 3 mm (p < 0.01) and 2 mm (p < 0.05) groups (Fig. 9A). Other microstructural parameters at 3 months, including Tb.Th, Tb.N, Tb.Sp, and BMD, showed no significant differences across pore sizes (Fig. 9B–E). By 6 months, the visual evidence indicates that the porous network became more interconnected across all pore sizes. Smaller pores (1 mm, 0.8 mm) displayed denser and more branched trabecular structures (Fig. 8B), a trend strongly reflected in the 6-month quantitative data where BV/TV further separated among groups (Fig. 9F). The 0.8 mm group maintained the highest BV/TV, significantly greater than the 3 mm (p < 0.0001) and 2 mm (p < 0.001) groups, while the 1 mm group was also higher than the 3 mm group (p < 0.01). Trabecular microarchitecture displayed pore-size–dependent differences at this 6-month stage: Tb.Th was significantly higher in the 2 mm group than in the 1 mm group (p < 0.05; Fig. 9G), Tb.N was significantly higher in the 0.8 mm group than in the 3 mm group (p < 0.05; Fig. 9H), and Tb.Sp was significantly lower in the 1 mm group than in the 2 mm group (p < 0.001; Fig. 9I). Longitudinal comparisons confirmed that the volume of newly formed bone increased over time. BV/TV increased from 3 to 6 months with statistical significance in the 1 mm and 0.8 mm groups (both p < 0.0001; Fig. 9K), while the 3 mm and 2 mm groups showed an increasing trend without significance. Tb.Th increased significantly over time in all pore sizes (p < 0.0001; Fig. 9L), whereas Tb.N showed no significant time-dependent change (Fig. 9M). Tb.Sp increased significantly only in the 2 mm group (p < 0.0001; Fig. 9N). Notably, BMD decreased from 3 to 6 months in the 2 mm, 1 mm, and 0.8 mm groups (p < 0.05; Fig. 9O), with no significant change in the 3 mm group. Overall, the combined visual and quantitative assessments demonstrate that all pore sizes supported sustained bone ingrowth, with smaller pores (particularly 0.8 mm and 1 mm) achieving both visually denser trabecular integration and quantitatively higher bone volume fractions by 6 months.

Discussion

An ideal orthopedic implant should not only match the anatomical structure of natural bone tissue but also feature interconnected pores to support inward cell growth and vascularization [11,34,35]. At the same time, it must offer sufficient mechanical strength to provide reliable support and avoid fatigue fracture. Moreover, its surface should facilitate cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, while exhibiting favorable biocompatibility and robust osseointegration [[36], [37], [38]]. The design of porous structures primarily revolves around three factors: porosity, pore size, and shape. These parameters affect not only the transport of nutrients and oxygen but also critical biological responses—such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and signal transduction—which ultimately influence the mechanical behavior of the scaffold [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. Since the introduction of porous Ti6Al4V implants for bone repair, researchers have been striving to identify designs that better promote bone tissue regeneration and integration. With advancements in additive manufacturing, emerging porous structures have demonstrated progressive optimization in both structural parameters and biomimetic features, offering more flexible strategies for customizing and enhancing the functionality of bone implants.

The primary aim of this study was to investigate the effect of different Bionic Pore designs (circular, triangular, and rectangular) and diameters (ranging from 0.8 mm to 3 mm) on bone ingrowth and implant performance, particularly when integrated into a spiral blade for proximal femoral fixation. The CFD analysis showed that circular holes displayed a more uniform distribution of FSS compared to triangular and rectangular holes, whose stress values tended to fluctuate more significantly across the hole wall. In vivo tests on a sheep model further demonstrated satisfactory fixation and progressive bone integration for all hole designs, with micro-CT and histological analyses confirming new trabecular bone formation within the bionic pores at both 3 and 6 months. Notably, smaller holes (0.8 mm and 1 mm) consistently exhibited higher rates of bone ingrowth earlier, as reflected by higher BV/TV, denser trabecular patterns, and greater BTA/BHA. These findings collectively suggest that hole geometry and diameter play a significant role in modulating the local biomechanical and biological environment conducive to bone ingrowth.

Under combined mechanical loading and muscle contraction, interstitial fluid within scaffold pores flows and generates FSS, which directly influences osteogenic differentiation [30,43]. Previous studies have shown that FSS <10 mPa favors bone formation; when 10 mPa < FSS <30 mPa, cartilage-like tissue tends to form; at 30–60 mPa, fibrous tissue formation is promoted; and FSS >60 mPa generally fails to induce effective tissue differentiation [[44], [45], [46]]. In our CFD simulations, circular pores exhibited a more uniform and, in certain cases, relatively low FSS. Notably, at a diameter of 0.8 mm, the average FSS ranged between 8 and 9 mPa, which falls into the bone-favoring region (<10 mPa). In contrast, 3 mm circular pores displayed FSS values of approximately 30–33 mPa in certain areas, more likely to induce fibrous tissue. Meanwhile, the sharp corners in triangular and rectangular pores frequently led to local flow separation or vortex formation, causing higher and more uneven FSS levels—often within 10–30 mPa or 30–60 mPa, favoring chondrogenic or fibrogenic differentiation. For instance, the FSS for a 0.8 mm triangular pore was around 40 mPa, and that for a 1 mm rectangular pore reached approximately 55–56 mPa. Prior work also suggests that a uniform and moderate FSS enhances osteogenic signaling, whereas an excessively high or localized FSS may induce microdamage or suboptimal cellular responses [30,47].

These findings were corroborated by large-animal experiments and subsequent histological and Micro-CT analyses: scaffolds with smaller pore diameters (0.8 mm and 1 mm) not only had higher BV/TV values and denser trabecular structures but also exhibited significantly increased BTA/BHA. In contrast, the 3 mm pores showed a propensity for early fibrous tissue accumulation; only as the effective pore space narrowed and local FSS decreased did bone gradually grow in, eventually forming a composite of bone and fibrous tissue. Furthermore, the relatively uniform FSS distribution in circular pores translated into a more stable and pronounced osteointegration in vivo, especially when the diameter approached 0.8 mm. Consistent with this pattern, Wang et al. [48] designed Ti6Al4V scaffolds with pore sizes of 800, 900, and 1000 μm, finding that larger pores led to higher levels of F-actin and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, alongside upregulated gene expression for BMP2, OCN, RUNX2, and ALP. These observations align well with our findings on how pore size and geometry shape mechanical microenvironments and osteogenic potential, underlining the critical role of pore design in bone tissue engineering scaffolds.

Moreover, small pore sizes have often been considered vital for promoting cell adhesion, vascularization, and early osteoconduction in scaffold design. Ratheesh et al. [49] reported that dual-pore scaffolds combining small and large pores significantly enhanced cell adhesion and proliferation, particularly in the small-pore regions, thus facilitating osteogenic differentiation. Han et al. [17] employed melt electrospinning to fabricate scaffolds with different pore sizes and observed that in the 100–200 μm range, chondrocytes and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells exhibited optimal proliferation and gene expression. Similarly, Ai et al. [12] found that silk fibroin scaffolds with a pore size of about 173.8 μm, when coated with fibronectin/gelatin, further improved cell attachment and osteogenic differentiation. Notably, vascularization within the scaffold also depends on pore distribution. Li et al. [50] used a sugar-leaching technique to create two types of gradient porous scaffolds: one with a central region of large pores (1100–1250 μm) and peripheral small pores (500–650 μm) (HALS), and another with reversed architecture (HASL). After one month of implantation, HASL exhibited significantly larger peripheral blood vessels compared to the center, whereas in HALS, only a few blood vessels formed in the central region, suggesting a heterogeneous pattern of vascular infiltration. Thus, although no universal consensus exists regarding the “optimal” pore size, sub-millimeter ranges generally favor in vitro osteogenic differentiation, necessitating a balance between small pores for bone formation and larger pores for vascular ingrowth.

Finally, X-ray imaging at 1, 3, and 6 months confirmed the overall stability of the helical blade with bionic pores, indicating that incorporating well-designed internal architectures into orthopedic implants can maintain mechanical support while enhancing biological fixation. This design concept may offer new avenues for customizing implant properties, striking a balance between surgical operability and efficient bone healing. The proposed hole design strategy has direct clinical relevance because early bone ingrowth and interface stability are key determinants of long-term fixation performance. By promoting a more favorable mechanobiological microenvironment for osseointegration, optimized holes may reduce early micromotion at the implant–bone interface, which is expected to lower the risk of progressive loosening and improve implant longevity, particularly in osteoporotic bone.

The results of this study underscore the potential of using Bionic Pore structures to enhance osseointegration within load-bearing orthopaedic implants. By systematically evaluating hole shapes and diameters, the study provides a methodology for tailoring implant microarchitecture to balance mechanical requirements—such as structural rigidity and fatigue strength—with biological imperatives like fostering stable fluid conditions and effective bone ingrowth. This knowledge may inform the design of next-generation implants in the fields of hip arthroplasty, trauma fixation, and bone defect reconstruction.

Moreover, the approach employed here, including computational simulation, additive manufacturing, and in vivo assessments, aligns with the broader trend toward personalised medicine and patient-specific implants. As imaging and modelling technologies advance, clinicians could theoretically customise Bionic Pore size and distribution to suit individual patient bone quality or healing capacity, potentially leading to more predictable outcomes in high-risk patients or complex orthopaedic cases.

The translational potential of this study is highlighted by its direct progression from theoretical biomechanics to clinical application. Historically, the role of trabecular bone reconstruction in fracture healing has often been underappreciated in implant design. By demonstrating that specific bionic pore parameters can effectively facilitate trabecular ingrowth during cortical healing, this study provides a robust framework for next-generation orthopaedic devices. More importantly, the translational value of this research has been successfully realized: based on the optimal parameters screened via our CFD and large animal experiments, a novel bionic internal fixation device has been developed and has recently obtained a medical device registration certificate for clinical use. This successful bench-to-bedside translation not only proves the clinical viability of the bionic pore concept but also establishes a strong foundation for the future research and development of customized, highly integrative bionic implants. This end-to-end validation—from computational screening through additive manufacturing and large-animal confirmation to regulatory approval—represents, to our knowledge, the first reported instance of a CFD-guided bionic pore design achieving clinical translation in the field of intramedullary fixation devices. The integrated methodology presented herein provides a replicable framework for future implant optimization efforts.

Limitations

Despite these promising findings, several limitations warrant discussion. Several limitations should be acknowledged. (1) While the ovine model is a standard for translational orthopedic research, inherent species differences in bone metabolism and loading patterns may affect direct clinical extrapolation. Furthermore, the use of an intact bone model rather than a fracture model served to isolate the effects of pore geometry but did not account for the significant micromotion and fluid velocity spikes—potential drivers of early fibrous tissue formation—that occur during actual fracture healing. (2) The experimental scope was constrained by a moderate cohort size (n = 7) and a specific anatomical site (femoral neck). Consequently, the results may not fully represent the long-term remodeling, fatigue performance, or mechanical responses at other implantation sites with different loading environments. (3) The CFD simulations assumed steady laminar flow rather than transient, dynamic loading, and the micro-CT analysis remains subject to metal artifacts and minor manual intervention during ROI segmentation, which could influence bone volume quantification. (4) Although micro-CT findings were corroborated by histology, the lack of direct mechanical testing (e.g., push-out or torsion) limits our ability to definitively link morphological ingrowth with actual interfacial strength. Future investigations incorporating standardized osteotomy models and formal biomechanical evaluations are required to further optimize these bionic designs under more rigorous physiological demands.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that incorporating strategically sized and shaped bionic pores into a spiral blade design can modulate local fluid shear stress distributions, enhance early-stage bone ingrowth, and promote long-term osseointegration in a large animal model. Smaller circular holes (especially 0.8 mm and 1 mm in diameter) appear most favorable for early and robust bone bridging, although larger holes eventually achieve substantial ingrowth over time. These findings highlight the promise of Bionic Pore architecture in orthopaedic implant design, offering a viable route towards implants that integrate more seamlessly with host bone while maintaining mechanical stability. Future investigations, including more complex simulations, extended follow-up periods, and clinical trials, will help refine these designs and evaluate their full translational potential.
