Role of maternal health-seeking behaviour on complete measles vaccination coverage in Bangladesh: evidence from Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey
Authors: S M Raysul Haque, Md Rabiul Islam, Shabareen Tisha, Sakina Sultana Swarna, Kamran ul Baset, Wasimul Bari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the prevalence and contributing factors of complete measles vaccination with key attention to maternal health-seeking behaviour.DesignThe study used secondary data from the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) 2017–2018, which was a cross-sectional study.SettingData extracted from the BDHS 2017–2018 dataset. It is the latest available nationally representative dataset containing measles vaccination data.ParticipantsThe sample comprised 2651 children aged 15–59 months in Bangladesh.Outcome measuresThe study analysed participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, maternal health-seeking behaviour and measles vaccination coverage. Frequency distributions, χ2 tests, and stepwise binary logistic regression analysis were performed.ResultsThe prevalence of complete (first dose at 9 months and second dose at 15 months or later) measles vaccination coverage was 80% among Bangladeshi children. Complete measles vaccination coverage was significantly higher among babies of mothers who had completed at least an antenatal visit (AOR 1.71, p<0.001), undergone an institutional delivery process (AOR 1.36, p=0.017), and completed at least one postnatal visit (AOR 1.27, p=0.050). In addition, maternal health-seeking factors, notably educated mothers (secondary educated: AOR 2.34, p<0.001; higher secondary and above AOR 2.58, p<0.001), had more vaccination coverage for their children. However, children living in urban areas had significantly lower vaccination coverage (AOR 0.68, p=0.001).ConclusionsThe study recommends strengthening the complete measles vaccination coverage in Bangladesh, which is still behind the target. Specific measures should be taken to increase antenatal care and postnatal care coverage and provide institutional delivery facilities. The study’s findings would contribute to attaining the Sustainable Development Goals for children’s health in Bangladesh by mitigating measles-related morbidity and mortality.

Introduction

Vaccination is considered one of the safest and most economical approaches to protecting children from communicable diseases.1 However, every year, vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) infect up to 30 million children, many of whom could have been prevented with proper vaccination.13 Approximately 1.5 million childhood deaths occur due to VDPs, and most of these deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries.1 4

Despite significant progress and global initiatives, more than 14 million infants remained unvaccinated worldwide as of the end of 2024, leaving them vulnerable to VPDs such as measles.5 6 Consequently, measles outbreaks have affected multiple regions globally in recent years. Measles was responsible for an estimated 107 500 deaths globally in 2023 and 95 000 deaths in 2024, with the majority occurring among children under 5 years of age, even though a safe and cost-effective vaccine is available.7 8 In 2025, measles incidence reached its highest level since 1991. About 93% of confirmed measles cases occurred among unvaccinated individuals or among those with unknown vaccination status. Vaccine coverage of at least 95% with measles-containing vaccine-1 (MCV1) and measles-containing vaccine-2 (MCV2) can prevent outbreaks and protect communities.9 However, the milestones for MCV1 and MCV2 coverage remain below the recommended levels. The WHO estimated that about 84% of children globally received the MCV1 and around 76% received MCV2 in 2024.10

Recent evidence suggests that measles is still a public health concern in many African and Asian countries, including Bangladesh. A total of 1724 measles cases were reported in 2017 and approximately 1319 cases in 2018.11 According to the estimation of WHO, in 2019, there were 4181 measles cases in Bangladesh.12 The government of Bangladesh initiated the Expanded Program on Immunisation (EPI) in 1979 with the support of UNICEF and the WHO and made substantial progress toward increasing immunisation coverage and decreasing childhood morbidity and mortality.13 Although Bangladesh has achieved a high vaccination status against VPDs, coverage for the measles vaccine specifically is still falling behind its desired target level of >90% set out in the 4th Health, Nutrition, and Population Sector Program (HPNSP).2 14 The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MOHFW) has strategically provided vaccines to children aged 9 months to 15 years through both routine immunisation centres and educational institutions,15 and around 1.2 million VPD deaths in Bangladesh were prevented between 1987 and 2007.16 Bangladesh introduced MCV1 (administered at age 9 months) in 1989, and MCV2 (administered at age 15 months) in 2012.17 18 To increase measles vaccination coverage, the MOHFW launched a campaign in January and February 2014,15 resulting in an increase in MCV1 coverage from 74% in 2000 to 94% in 2016 and an increase in MCV2 coverage from 35% in 2013 to 93% in 2016.17

Several factors, such as maternal education, mass media exposure, having vaccination cards and accessibility of antenatal care (ANC), were identified as predictors of overall vaccination in Bangladesh.1921 Some studies reported that maternal health care-seeking behaviour, most notably ANC accessibility, community ANC utilisation and postnatal care (PNC) have a significant role in total vaccination coverage.202225 Mothers who completed adequate ANC and PNC visits and used the ANC facility had increased awareness of vaccination22 26 and were more likely to have their children immunised.27 Although institutional delivery is another important factor for child vaccination, it was not particularly explored for measles vaccination coverage, along with either ANC or PNC coverage or both.

Previous studies1921 have explored the socioeconomic, demographic and healthcare utilisation factors that impact childhood vaccination. However, no study has exclusively focused on the impact of maternal health-seeking behaviour on measles vaccination coverage in Bangladesh. Therefore, our aim was to determine the prevalence and contributing factors of maternal health care-seeking behaviours, including ANC, PNC and institutional accessibility, on completing the childhood measles vaccine schedule in Bangladesh using the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) 2017–2018 data, which is the most recent BDHS dataset containing measles vaccination information.

From the dataset, children aged 15–59 months were selected to ensure a valid assessment of full measles vaccination coverage in Bangladesh. The country’s typical immunisation schedule sets the first dose of MCV1 at 9 months and the second dose of MCV2 at 15 months of age.17 18 Children younger than 15 months may not yet be eligible for the second dose of MCV2. Although measles vaccination coverage is frequently assessed among children aged 12–35 months, these age ranges may underestimate full measles vaccination coverage in Bangladesh, where vaccination delays are common due to socioeconomic and demographic factors.19 By restricting the lower age limit to 15 months and extending the upper limit to 59 months, this study ensured that all included children were age-eligible for MCV2 and had sufficient opportunity to complete the recommended measles vaccination schedule. A concurrent previous study in Bangladesh also set the upper age range of 59 months to consider full immunisation coverage.19 In addition, information on ANC, PNC and childhood vaccination is routinely collected in BDHS surveys for the most recent live birth, which minimises recall bias and ensures data completeness. Thus, this wider range (from 15 to 59 months) allows us to capture the full picture of measles vaccination coverage and how it is attributable to the maternal health-seeking behaviours in Bangladesh.

Methods

Data source and study population

The BDHS 2017–2018 was a cross-sectional survey, which was performed under the authority of the National Institute of Population Research and Training, the Medical Education and Family Welfare Division and the MOHFW. The survey collected data on different sociodemographic, fertility, nutrition and health status of the population following a two-stage stratified cluster sampling technique. It covered a nationally representative sample of 20 100 ever-married women aged 15–49 years and their children born 0–59 months prior to the survey date. Additional details of the survey methodology and strategies are described elsewhere.28 Children aged 15–59 months from the most recent births were included in the study. The total sample size for the study was 2651 children.

Outcome variable

The status of complete measles vaccination among children aged 15–59 months was the outcome variable of this study. It was dichotomised as either fully vaccinated (‘Yes’) or not vaccinated (‘No’). Following the standard DHS protocol, information on childhood vaccination was collected in two ways: (a) from vaccination cards shown to the interviewer during the interview and (b) from mothers’ verbal response when the card was not available or when a dose was not recorded on the card. If a vaccination card was available, the interviewer transcribed the dates for each vaccine dose directly from the card. If no card was available or a vaccine dose was not recorded, the mother was asked to report whether the child had received that dose. Our outcome (complete measles immunisation) was derived from the BDHS ‘card or mother’s report’ variables, which classify a dose as received if it is documented on the card or reported by the mother, consistent with the definitions used in the BDHS final report.29

Independent variables

We included various explanatory variables in the analysis. The explanatory variables and their category are as follows: education level of the mother and father: ‘no education’, ‘primary education’, ‘secondary education’ and ‘higher education group’; place of residence: ‘rural’ and ‘urban’; mother’s access to media exposure (includes watching TV and/or listening to radio and/or reading newspapers): ‘exposed’ and ‘non-exposed’; wealth index: ‘poor’, ‘middle class’ and ‘rich’; place of delivery: ‘non-institutional’ and ‘institutional delivery’; age of mother at 1st birth: ‘18 and above’ and ‘below 18 years’; birth order of index child: ‘1st birth’, ‘2nd to 4th birth’ and ‘5th and above’; sex of index child: ‘female’ and ‘male’; mother’s employment status: ‘not employed’ and ‘employed’; mother’s awareness of the community clinic: ‘not aware’ and ‘aware’; divisions: ‘Dhaka’, ‘Barisal’, ‘Chittagong’, ‘Khulna’, ‘Mymensingh’, ‘Rajshahi’, ‘Rangpur’ and ‘Sylhet’; religion: ‘Muslim’ and ‘non-Muslim’; ANC visit during pregnancy: ‘ANC visit’ and ‘no visit’; PNC check-up within 2 months: ‘PNC checked’ and ‘not checked’.

Statistical analysis

We used SPSS V.27 to analyse the data. Design weights were not used in the analysis because the main objective was to estimate associations between key determinants, the maternal health-seeking behaviours and complete measles vaccination coverage rather than to generate national-level estimates of indicators.3032 Both the descriptive statistics and inferential statistics were carried out in the study using three consecutive steps. The analysis was done in three steps. First, univariate analysis in terms of frequency and percentage was done for both dependent and independent variables. Second, bivariate analysis (χ2 test) was performed to examine the association between the dependent variable and all other independent variables separately. Finally, binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the adjusted association of ANC visit, place of delivery and PNC, which collectively are termed maternal health-seeking behaviours, with complete measles vaccination. Sequential logistic regression models (a total of four models) were also considered to observe how the association of maternal health-seeking behaviour changes with the different types of covariates. In all analyses, we considered the significance level to be at p value <0.05.

Results

Distribution of the sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

Table 1 shows the distributions for the sociodemographic variables of the study participants. The prevalence of measles vaccination was 80% among the children. Almost half (48.5%) of the total mothers completed their secondary level of education, and only 17.8% completed their higher education. Nearly one-third (32.3%) of the total fathers completed their secondary level of formal education and around 20% have a higher level of education. Nearly two-thirds (65.5%) of the study participants lived in rural areas in Bangladesh and 41% of participants were in the poor wealth index group. Approximately half of the mothers did not have access to any sort of media exposure. It is evident from the study that the non-institutional delivery (50.1%) rate was slightly higher than the institutional delivery rate. The study found that around 40% of mothers experienced their first birth at age 17 years or younger. The majority (52.5%) of the index children were male. Two-thirds of the total number of mothers were unemployed. Most mothers (56.9%) were aware of the community clinic. It was also found that 17.1% of the participants were mainly from the Chittagong division and the lowest proportion (9.8%) was from the Barisal division. The study outcome also shows that more than 90% of respondents were Muslim, with the remaining participants (9.1%) being part of another religion. Out of 2651 mothers, 92.6% of mothers completed at least one ANC visit during pregnancy, and 65.8% of mothers completed the PNC check within 2 months of delivery.

Model II

In model II, after adjusting for maternal education, place of residence, wealth index and maternal employment, the association of measles vaccination coverage with ANC (OR=1.738, p=0.001), place of delivery (OR=1.437, p=0.005) and PNC (OR=1.279, p=0.045) remained significant. Higher education of the mother is associated with 3.066 times higher vaccination coverage (p<0.001), while secondary and primary education levels show 2.460 (p<0.001) and 1.375 (p=0.085) times higher coverage, respectively. In addition, urban mothers have lower odds of vaccination coverage (OR=0.697, p=0.002). However, the wealth index is not associated with measles vaccination coverage.

Model III

of the logistic regression further includes mother’s age at first birth, birth order of the child and sex of the index child. ANC and institutional delivery are associated with 1.741 (OR=1.741, p=0.001) and 1.387 (OR=1.387, p=0.012) times higher odds of vaccination coverage. PNC is not found to be significantly (OR=1.262, p=0.060) associated with vaccination coverage. Maternal education remains significant, with higher education (OR=2.709, p<0.001), secondary education (OR=2.391, p<0.001) and primary education (OR=1.369, p=0.098).

Additionally, living in urban areas (OR=0.698, p=0.002), mothers with employment (OR=1.252, p=0.033) and mothers who gave birth before the age of 18 are significantly (OR=0.809, p=0.042) associated with measles vaccination coverage.

In the final logistics regression model (Model IV), the results show that ANC visits continue to have a strong predictor (OR=1.711, p=0.001) of measles vaccination coverage, indicating that even after adjusting for maternal media exposure and religion, along with all variables considered in model III, mothers who receive ANC care during pregnancy are much more likely to vaccinate their child. Similarly, institutional delivery also remains significantly associated with higher measles vaccination coverage (OR=1.364, p=0.017). The OR stabilises at 1.274 (p=0.050), suggesting that PNC visits still contribute to measles vaccination coverage but have a weaker association than ANC and institutional delivery.

The model also illustrates that education remains highly significant, with higher education, secondary and primary education groups showing ORs of 2.693, 2.672 and 1.474, respectively (p<0.001 for secondary and higher education). Furthermore, the likelihood of measles vaccination coverage increases among mothers with middle (OR=1.123, p=0.048) and rich wealth index (OR=1.097, p=0.048), and with media access (OR=1.291, p=0.040). Conversely, the chance of vaccination coverage is reduced among mothers living in urban areas (OR=0.683, p=0.001). Mother’s employment status, age of mother at her first birth, birth order of index child, sex of the index child and religion are not significantly associated with vaccination coverage.

Discussion

Identifying the factors influencing measles vaccination coverage is crucial for developing targeted interventions. This study used the nationally representative BDHS dataset and performed a stepwise logistic regression model to determine the factors of complete measles vaccination. Complete measles vaccination coverage was 80% among Bangladeshi children. Maternal health-seeking behaviours, including ANC, place of delivery and PNC, are significant predictors of complete measles vaccination coverage. In addition, the level of maternal education, wealth index, media access of the mother and area of residence of the mother are significant determinants of measles vaccination coverage.

The HPNSP has established a national goal for measles vaccination coverage at 90%, which is 10% higher than the current prevalence of 80%. However, a prior study indicated that the prevalence was 89.7% in 2011 and 88% in 2014.13 This trend clearly shows that the measles vaccination coverage in Bangladesh has slightly declined after 2011. Furthermore, Bangladesh has the lowest coverage for the measles vaccination among all routine vaccines.13 It is imperative to achieve >90% routine vaccination coverage in all children to reduce the deaths associated with VDPs.33 Evidence suggests that ≥95% coverage with both the MCV1 and MCV2 of 95% is required to achieve herd immunity, essential to protect the community.9 Hence, Bangladesh’s immunisation programme set a national target of 90% coverage, which falls short of the international recommended standard. Therefore, Bangladesh must undertake essential measures to enhance its measles immunisation coverage by at least 10% to achieve the target established by HPNSP.

Coverage of MCV1 typically exceeds that of MCV2, representing a persistent programmatic challenge. Compared with the WHO estimates for 2024, MCV2 coverage in our study was higher than the global estimate of 76%.10 However, this study did not assess MCV1 and MCV2 coverage separately; future research should examine dose-specific coverage patterns. This study shows there is a significant positive association between measles vaccination coverage and maternal ANC visit, and mothers who had used ANC during pregnancy were 1.711 times as likely to immunise their children as mothers who had not used. Other studies conducted in Bangladesh,1 as well as Congo,34 Ethiopia35 and Ghana,36 also had similar findings. Additionally, a qualitative finding of a study in Ethiopia found a significant association of measles vaccination coverage with maternal ANC coverage during pregnancy.37 This finding was also aligned with studies done in Ethiopia and Mozambique.38 39 These findings may be explained by the fact that the promotion of EPI vaccines often takes place among women in ANC facilities.14 Furthermore, maternal health programmes, including ANC visits, can provide mothers with knowledge about the advantages of vaccination and result in increased satisfaction with healthcare accessibility, potentially leading to greater vaccination coverage.40

The measles vaccination status of children is also significantly associated with the place of delivery. Children born in an institutional setting have a 36% higher likelihood (OR=1.364, p=0.017) of complete measles vaccination coverage than their counterparts. This finding is consistent with the findings of a study conducted in India by Dhalaria et al in 2024.41 Previous studies conducted in Bangladesh, Nigeria and Ethiopia similarly showed that the children of women who delivered at health facilities were more likely to receive full immunisation compared with those of women who had home delivery.4247 This might be due to the fact that some vaccines, like BCG and oral polio vaccine, the first dose is often given immediately after birth at health facilities. These studies did not yield any specific evidence regarding measles vaccination coverage and the place of delivery. However, another study in Bangladesh determined a significant association (adjusted OR (AOR)=1.69, p<0.001) of measles vaccination status with the place of birth delivery. These findings indicate that institutional birth delivery should be ensured to promote measles vaccination of children.14

Mothers who have completed PNC visits within 2 months of delivery have higher odds of completing measles vaccination for their children. This association has also been shown in other studies.48 Attending PNC may represent health-seeking behaviour, and women who seek professional healthcare for a check-up may be more likely to seek care for their child. Furthermore, additional information about vaccination may be provided during the PNC visit, and a positive relationship with the healthcare system may be established.49 These findings reveal that attending PNC may be positively associated with measles vaccination coverage, and future interventions may benefit from encouraging mothers to attend PNC visits.50

Maternal education level is a significant determinant of measles vaccination coverage. The children whose mothers completed at least a secondary level education were approximately two times as likely to be vaccinated. This finding is corroborated by the research conducted by Jahan et al in 2019, which found a positive correlation between the level of maternal education and the likelihood of measles vaccination.14 Our findings are also supported by the global pattern; children of highly educated mothers have higher vaccination rates.5154 This may be attributed to the fact that educated mothers are more aware of vaccinations and are more capable of adhering to their children’s vaccination schedule.

We also found that measles vaccination coverage was slightly higher among mothers from families with middle (OR=1.123) and rich wealth index (OR=1.097). This may be due to the fact that children from lower socioeconomic strata failed to use the immunisation services of health systems in Bangladesh due to a lack of awareness or other problems related to the financial constraints of mothers. Thus, the children of lower socio-economic strata often tend to be deprived of the benefits of vaccination.14

Vaccination coverage exhibits substantial disparities between rural and urban areas. While several studies have reported higher vaccination coverage in urban settings,14 46 55 emerging evidence suggests that this pattern is changing. This study showed that vaccination coverage is significantly higher in rural areas. The finding is consistent with more recent national and regional analyses. Urban area’s vaccination coverage is 5.6% lower than rural area (79.0% vs 84.6%).56 This could be attributed to the fact that rural Bangladesh has benefited substantially from the expansion of community clinics that focus on one-stop primary healthcare service in rural communities, including EPI and home visits.57 Urban areas exhibit significant population mobility, informal housing (particularly in slums) and weaker community engagement, whereas rural areas benefit from organised and consistently used primary healthcare services, resulting in this discrepancy.

There is a significant correlation between the employment of mothers and the level of measles vaccination coverage. Children with employed mothers have 1.247 times higher vaccination coverage. Similarly, children of unemployed mothers had 1.46 times higher odds of not receiving the measles vaccines.58 A consistent finding from other studies stated that children of working mothers have 1.14 times higher odds of having measles vaccination.59 Conversely, another study in Bangladesh found that vaccination coverage was notably higher among the children of unemployed mothers.13 In addition, another study in different settings also reported findings contradictory to this study.60 One possible reason may be that most employed mothers are educated and are aware of the importance of childhood vaccinations. Their employment is associated with greater autonomy and improved decision-making power, all of which positively influence child healthcare utilisation. On the other hand, unemployed mothers are typically busy with household and other unpaid work, resulting in a tendency to overlook their children’s vaccination schedules. Finally, these women lack financial freedom, which could be another contributing factor for not visiting the vaccination site.

We observed 1.29 times higher odds of measles vaccination among the children from families who were exposed to the media compared with those who were not exposed. Similarly, exposure to mass media has been observed to be positively associated with the vaccination of children in previous studies.6163 This observation reflects that immunisation campaigns through radio, television and other culturally acceptable low-cost means of reaching the community can help people understand the beneficial role of measles immunisation.14

This study hypothesised that maternal age at first birth is a more significant determinant of measles vaccination than the mother’s current age because it reflects when a woman entered motherhood, and the social and health conditions shaping her health-seeking behaviour. In addition, early age at first birth in countries like Bangladesh negatively affects childhood vaccination coverage, and it is attributable to lower education, reduced autonomy and limited use of ANC and PNC.64 In contrast, a mother’s current age mainly reflects biological age and parity and does not capture these early disadvantages. Therefore, maternal age at first birth was considered a better predictor of measles vaccination coverage than the current age of the mother. However, although the association was in the expected direction, maternal age at first birth was only marginally above the threshold for statistical significance (p=0.053). Further research is warranted to explore the reasons for this discrepancy and to better understand the underlying mechanisms.

Although the birth order of the children,58 65 economic status and geographic location were found significantly associated with measles vaccination coverage in previous studies,58 we did not find any significant association with these factors. This can be attributed to the variability in the execution of the study period. We used the latest dataset, whereas these studies employed outdated datasets.

The strong associations between complete measles vaccination coverage and ANC, institutional delivery and PNC underscore the need for integration of routine immunisation within the continuum of maternal and child health services in Bangladesh. Policymakers should prioritise strengthening ANC and PNC platforms to systematically deliver immunisation counselling, ensure timely registration of newborns into immunisation programmes, and implement reminder and follow-up mechanisms. In addition, expanding community-based outreach, improving access to immunisation services and scaling up mass-media campaigns may help address persistent inequities in measles vaccination coverage.

Limitation

There are several limitations in the study. Due to the nature of a cross-sectional study, we could not establish a causal association between the outcome and explanatory variables. The measles vaccination history was recorded either from the immunisation cards or by relying on the mothers’ self-reporting. Additionally, there was a gap between the period of measles data collection and the administration of measles vaccinations to children, resulting in potential recall bias. It could potentially impact the validity of the outcome measure. However, integrating card data with maternal recall constitutes the standard methodology employed by DHS. Card plus history estimates can offer reliable population-level coverage assessments while circumventing the selection bias that would result from entirely excluding children without cards. This study did not apply sampling weights in the descriptive or regression analyses because the primary objective was to estimate associations between key determinants and complete measles vaccination coverage rather than to generate nationally representative prevalence estimates. As such, the results presented are unweighted.

Conclusion

The prevalence of measles vaccination was less than the recommended level in Bangladesh. The study determined some important barriers to completing measles vaccination in Bangladesh. Policy makers should consider these obstacles when implementing policies aimed at improving measles vaccination coverage. Targeted interventions should be taken, with a particular emphasis on increasing and improving ANC visits, institutional deliveries and PNC check-ups. Other potential factors that need to be considered include maternal education, employment and the residential location of the children.
