Reverse Tesla valve modulated efficient water evaporation and cooling
Authors: Wensheng Wang, Shouwei Gao, Han Wu, Pengyu Chen, Dongnan Xu, Haoyan Xu, Mingxing Jing, Qi Sun, Shaocong Chang, Zhuangzhi Sun, Haipeng Yu, Zuankai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water evaporation, a physical process effectively driving natural hydrological cycles, has long suffered from rate limitations in industrial applications. Particularly in scenarios such as solar steam generation and evaporation cooling, the evaporation rate of conventional evaporators struggles to meet the practical request, due to the unresolved boundary layer resistance at the liquid-air interface. Here, we reinvent Nikola Tesla’s fluidic diode into a reverse Tesla valve modulated bulk-effect evaporation system (BEES). By leveraging structure-induced vortex airflow to disrupt thermal and mass transfer boundary layers, BEES unlocks previously unattainable evaporation dynamics at the inner liquid-air interface and achieves an evaporation rate of 21.29 kg m−2 h−1, with a total energy/solar efficiency of 627%. Our BEES enables all-day freshwater harvesting and efficient evaporative cooling, achieving energy savings of 414 GJ m−3 month−1. This work establishes a bulk-effect evaporation framework complementary to traditional interfacial engineering for addressing the water-energy nexus.

Introduction

Water evaporation is one of the most ubiquitous surface physical phenomena in our daily lives. Its significance extends across various domains, contributing substantially to the thermoregulation of mammals, the metabolic processes of plants, and the intricate dynamics of the hydrological cycle on Earth1–4. From the historical development of the steam engine in the 18th century5 to the modern advancements in technologies like solar vapor generation6,7 and evaporation cooling technologies8,9, understanding, manipulating and harnessing water evaporation have consistently commanded a prominent position at the vanguard of scientific investigation10.

Despite the diversity, enhancing the evaporation rate remains the primary focus of research. Fundamentally, achieving high evaporation rates hinges on either a strong and stable thermal dynamic driving force, the chemical potential differences between the surface and environment, or a quick kinetics diffusion of vapor11–13. For the former, the specific strategies include broadening the energy source, incorporating heat management, and modifying the material to reduce the evaporation enthalpy14–16. For the latter, increasing the evaporation area17,18, and introducing forced convection19,20 are the two commonly used methods. The most comprehensive technology is called cold evaporation, which features a black top light-absorbing layer for sunlight harvest and a large side surface for harnessing low-grade thermal energy from the environment for evaporation21–24. Especially, when combined with heat pumping and forced convection, the corresponding device can realize a total energy collection of more than 300% of the single-sun irradiance, achieving an evaporation rate of more than 9 kg m−2 h−1 25–27. Despite these advancements, such an evaporation rate still falls short of meeting the increasing demands for practical applications.

One potential strategy involves expanding the evaporation zone within the bulk evaporator from its external surface towards the interior, which can increase the available evaporation surface area by creating numerous internal gas-liquid contact regions28–30. Developing such a bulk effect evaporator is challenging, owing to the significant hindrance to vapor diffusion kinetics within the confined space of the bulk evaporator31. As a result, the internal vapor tends to saturate rapidly, causing the disappearance of chemical potential differences and prematurely halting the evaporation process. Although in conventional design, this issue can be mitigated by introducing airflow, its effectiveness is strongly limited due to the existence of the heat and mass transfer boundary layer near the surface exerted by the viscous effect of flow32–34.

Tesla valves can enable fluid to flow predominantly in one direction. This unique hydrodynamic regulation ability stems from specialized periodic structures that promote smooth fluid flow in the forward direction, while a velocity reduction when operated in reverse by redirecting a portion of the fluid to interact with the primary flow and the valve wall35,36. Traditionally, Tesla valves have been used in the forward direction for flow cooling due to their one-way rectifying characteristics. Differently, here, we innovatively integrated the reverse Tesla valve structure into an evaporation system and proposed a bulk-effect evaporator system (BEES). The incorporation of the reverse Tesla valve within the bulk evaporator not only expands internal gas-liquid contact areas for enhanced evaporation but also destroys the boundary layers through the robust interaction between the airflow and the valve wall, boosting the heat and mass transfer. Additionally, the design enables us to synergistically optimize efficient solar energy conversion, environmental heat input, and heat loss recovery, all of which are pivotal for the thermal dynamics of evaporation. Ultimately, our innovative bulk-effect evaporator, BEES-4v (with 4 valves), achieved an evaporation rate of 21.29 kg m−2 h−1 under 1 solar irradiation and a low-velocity airflow of 2.5 m s−1 airflow. Furthermore, it boasts a multi-source energy power harvest of 135.8 kW m−3, equivalent to 627% of one-solar and over 6.4 times the efficiency of conventional solar evaporators. Notably, our bulk-effect evaporation system also demonstrates significant potential for evaporative cooling, offering monthly energy savings exceeding 414 GJ m−3 when the solar absorption layer is omitted. This work represents a complementary advancement in enhancing traditional solar evaporation methods and opens up important avenues for technological innovation in freshwater harvesting, energy-efficient cooling, and various evaporation-related applications.

Results

Structural design of BEES

The design of the bulk-effect evaporation system (BEES) is outlined in Fig. 1a–c and the Supplementary Fig. 1, comprising three distinct functional layers. The first layer constitutes a light-absorbing structure, featuring a multilayer configuration of biocarbon particles and electrostatically spun filaments (Supplementary Fig. 2). The second layer functions as a transitional zone, characterized by a columnar array structure (Supplementary Fig. 3) aimed at enhancing heat utilization by expanding the heat transfer area between the light-absorbing layer and the primary evaporation component. The mechanical interlocking between the columnar structure and the electrospun network enables stable interfacial adhesion between the light-absorbing layer and the substrate (Supplementary Fig. 4). The third layer encompasses the primary evaporation part, constructed from carbonized wood integrated with a reverse Tesla valve structure (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 5). An in-situ fabricated SiC layer embellishes the surface of this layer (Supplementary Fig. 6), which can enhance heat transfer due to its high thermal conductivity and ensure the stability and sufficient water delivery of the evaporator leveraging its hydrophilic properties (Supplementary Figs. 7, 8). Our device is named BEES-Xv, with ‘X’ representing the count of integrated reverse Tesla valve channels (Supplementary Fig. 9). Multi-energy synergistic characteristics and evaporation performance of BEES

Our BEES demonstrates superior water evaporation performance in comparison to conventional 3D evaporators, exemplified by the 3D wood evaporator and BEES-0v. As illustrated in Fig. 1d, the 3D wood evaporator and BEES-0v achieve evaporation rates of 1.96 and 2.88 kg m−2 h−1, respectively. Both rates surpass the 1.47 kg m−2 h−1, the theoretically limited rate for solar vapor generation under one solar irradiation, showcasing typical cold evaporation characteristics. In contrast, our BEES-4v achieves a significantly higher evaporation rate of 4.49 kg m−2 h−1 under the same conditions. Particularly noteworthy is that when airflow is incorporated into the system, an evaporation rate of 21.29 kg m−2 h−1 can be attained with an airflow velocity of 2.5 m s−1. This rate is 10.8 times greater than that of the 3D wood evaporator and 7.3 times greater than that of BEES-0v. The remarkable water evaporation rate of our BEES stems, in part, from its exceptional energy harvesting capability, crucial for ensuring a significant chemical potential difference between the evaporator and its surroundings. The corresponding energy input power is calculated at a high 135 kW m−3 (see Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Fig. 10), which amounts to 627% of a single-solar energy input.

Such an exceptional energy harvesting capability of our system is a result of the elaborately designed multilayer structure. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, in conventional 3D evaporators, the limited energy transfer and utilization lead to a top surface temperature higher than the ambient environment, resulting in constrained energy absorption from the surroundings and also substantial heat losses through convection and radiation. Specifically, in the top layer, a porous, anti-reflective Biochar/E-Spin structure functions as a light-absorbing layer, adept at efficiently capturing sunlight (refer to Supplementary Fig. 2). As displayed in Fig. 2b, the Biochar/E-Spin layer with 8-layer alternating structure achieves an impressive average light absorption of 97.5% across the UV-Vis-NIR range (350–2500 nm), significantly surpassing that of the Biochar layer and natural wood. Under standard one-sun illumination, the temperature of the dried photothermal layer can reach 51 °C within 5 min (see Supplementary Fig. 12), providing a robust thermal driving force for the evaporation process. Moreover, in the primary evaporation layer, the incorporation of in-situ stacked SiC substantially enhances the thermal conductivity of our evaporator (refer to Supplementary Figs. 6, 13). As illustrated in Fig. 2c, the thermal conductivity of the bulk evaporator measures 8.12 W m−1 K−1, an impressive 70.3 times higher than that of natural wood. Simulation results indicate that 92.5% of the photothermal layer’s total heat is transported through the SiC thermal interface layer (Supplementary Fig. 14). This design ensures the efficient directional flow of energy from the top photothermal layer, the surroundings, and the water to the bulk evaporator, benefiting from sustaining the evaporation process23(refer to Supplementary Figs. 11, 15).

Reverse Tesla valve for enhancing heat and mass transfer in BEES

More importantly, our BEES is able to accelerate vapor diffusion kinetics by integrating reverse Tesla valve channels, thereby significantly improving water evaporation rates. On the one hand, by incorporating these channels, the evaporation zone can extend from the external surface of the evaporator into its interior, thus creating numerous internal gas-liquid contact regions that enhance the evaporation process. This effect is demonstrated in Fig. 2d, where an increase in the number of channels results in a rise in the evaporation rate from 2.88 kg m−2 h−1 for BEES-0v to 4.49 kg m−2 h−1 for BEES-4v, which is 2.2 times greater than that of the wood evaporator. This enhancement can be further supported by the observation that the average equilibrium temperature of the top surface of the evaporator gradually decreases to 24.9 °C with an increasing number of channels, falling below the ambient temperature (Tambient = Twater = 25.5 ± 0.5 °C) (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 16). This reduction is attributed to heat loss resulting from rapid evaporation, specifically arising from the synergistic interaction of improved thermal conductivity in SiC, an increased evaporation area, and enhanced convective heat transfer (Supplementary Fig. 17). This highlights the effectiveness of the reverse Tesla valve channel in facilitating quicker evaporation.

On the other hand, the significance of the reverse Tesla valve channel in enhancing water vapor diffusion becomes more apparent when it interacts with airflow. For traditional bulk evaporators, vapor diffusion kinetics are severely impeded by confined spaces (refer to Supplementary Fig. 18). As water evaporates, these channels gradually transform into dead zones where vapor saturates, leading to a reduction in the chemical potential difference and a halt in water evaporation (see Supplementary Fig. 19). Introducing airflow not only aids in removing saturated vapor and eliminating stagnant dead zones but also notably reduces the distance for vapor diffusion, minimizing the thickness of the diffusion layer to a level comparable to that of the flow viscosity boundary layer, which will effectively boost the evaporation rate.

The left part of Fig. 3a illustrates the simulation of wet vapor distribution in forward and reverse Tesla valve channels when airflow is introduced. As shown, for the forward Tesla valve channel, airflow tends to follow paths with minimal pressure drop, resulting in the persistence of many dead zones. In contrast, during reverse operation, the reverse Tesla valve channel efficiently eliminates stagnant dead zones by redirecting branch flows to ensure complete airflow filling. Consequently, saturated vapor in the reverse Tesla valve channel can be carried away by the airflow, leaving more areas with shorter vapor diffusion lengths compared to those in the forward channels. The advantage of reverse Tesla valve vapor diffusion is further corroborated by the evaporation performance difference between BEES-4v and other evaporators integrated with some representative structures, including forward Tesla valves (same total inner surface area), tic-tac-toe (higher total inner surface area), rectilinear (same inner surface area) channels, and various other advanced microchannel designs (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 20 and Note 2). The number of branch channels in the Tesla valve structure is optimized to four to ensure optimal overall performance (see Supplementary Fig. 21). As depicted in Fig. 3c, Supplementary Figs. 22a, 23, all these evaporators exhibit higher evaporation rates with increasing airflow due to the reduction of stagnant dead zones and the narrowing of the vapor diffusion boundary layer. Among them, BEES-4v consistently outperformed the other evaporators, reaching an impressive 21.29 kg m−2 h−1 at a low convective velocity of 2.5 m s−1. This can be attributed to the lower average relative humidity and removal of stagnant dead zones in the reverse Tesla valve channel (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 24). The counterintuitive phenomenon can be elucidated by the improved heat transfer facilitated by the interaction between airflow and the reverse Tesla valve. As shown in the left part of Fig. 3e, Supplementary Movie 2, the flow is smooth and stable during forward operation. In contrast, there are plenty of characteristic vortices formed within the channel during reverse operation. As the wind speed increases further, although the vortices persist, their shape becomes distorted, and the rate of increase in heat transfer efficiency begins to slow (see Supplementary Fig. 25). The right part of Fig. 3a displays the simulated temperature and fluid field distribution in the Tesla valve channels when airflow is blowing in both forward and reverse directions. These vortices, following the field synergy principle38,39, decrease the angle between temperature gradient and flow velocity within the channel, enhancing heat exchange with the channel wall by disrupting thermal and flow viscosity boundary layers. (Supplementary Note 3). Demonstrated results show that our BEES-4v boasts a high heat transfer coefficient (HTC) at the gas-liquid interface, achieving 43.56 W m−2 K−1 with an airflow rate of 2.5 m s−1 (Fig. 3f), representing a 143% enhancement compared to the average performance of various high-performance microchannels (Supplementary Fig. 22b). Meanwhile, its system pressure drop is only slightly higher than the average for various high-performance microchannel (Supplementary Fig. 22c). This indicates that the advantage of the reverse Tesla valve configuration lies in the synergistic improvement of effective evaporation area, convective heat transfer efficiency, and vapor diffusion within the channel, rather than merely increasing local turbulence or pressure drop (Supplementary Fig. 22d). This confers upon our BEES-4v an excellent energy harvesting capability, yielding 9.7 W under a 2.5 m s−1 airflow (Fig. 3g).

Considering the bulk evaporation characteristics of our BEES, we introduce the concept of ‘energy conversion power per unit volume’ to objectively evaluate the performance of our 3D evaporator, moving away from the conventional metric of projected area per unit. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 26, with increased convection, our system’s bulk evaporation potential can be fully harnessed. Under an airflow of 2.5 m s−1, our BEES-4v can achieve 135.8 kW m−3 in multi-source energy conversion power per unit volume, with 81.6% attributed to ambient heat contribution (refer to Fig. 3h, Supplementary Tables 4-7). Benchmark plots in Fig. 3i demonstrate that both the evaporation rate and the total energy-to-solar conversion efficiency of our system significantly surpass those of existing devices. Notably, the total energy-to-solar conversion efficiency of our BEES-4v reaches up to 627%, exceeding average values reported for 3D evaporators by more than 6.4 times.

BEES-based all-weather freshwater production test

Thanks to its exceptional evaporation performance, our BEES can be seamlessly integrated into solar-driven freshwater harvesting systems (Fig. 4a). A superhydrophilic top cover was employed to prevent condensed droplets from accumulating, thereby maintaining high solar transmittance (refer to Supplementary Fig. 27). Illustrated in Fig. 4b, the device, with our BEES at its core, showcases effective water collection and anti-fogging capabilities. Notably, unlike traditional systems limited to daytime operation, the BEES-based freshwater harvesting device can continuously harness energy from sunlight, the surroundings, and water day and night, courtesy of the cold evaporation capacity of our BEES (see Fig. 4c). This not only underscores its great potential for uninterrupted, all-weather freshwater collection, but also demonstrates that the solar-cold evaporation synergy enhances vapor condensation by promoting mass and energy transfer processes within the system (refer to Supplementary Note 4, Supplementary Fig. 28). As depicted in Fig. 4d, the prototype device achieves a daily freshwater collection rate of 28.8 kg m−2, marking a 4.5-fold enhancement over single solar evaporation and meeting the daily drinking water needs of nine adults. Its solar-to-water production efficiency remains superior even against advanced condenser designs, exceeding that of conventional systems by more than fivefold (Supplementary Fig. 29). The collected water maintains high quality standards with minimal organic pollutants and low ion concentration, complying with WHO drinking water guidelines (Supplementary Fig. 30). Additionally, the device exhibits excellent salt resistance ability, as evidenced by the absence of visible salt crystallization after extended evaporation in seawater, nor was there any noticeable decrease in evaporation rate (Supplementary Fig. 31), ensuring its longevity in practical applications. Demonstration of scalable BEES-based cooling system

Due to the significant latent heat absorption involved in water evaporation, our BEES presents a notable advantage in facilitating efficient evaporative heat dissipation. To leverage this capability, we developed an evaporative cooling system with the light-absorbing layer removed, BEES serving as the central component, aiming to expand its application potential in large-scale industrial evaporative air-cooling equipment (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 33). As depicted in Fig. 5b, Supplementary Movie 4, the designed thermostatic evaporative cooling system achieves a temperature decrease of 28.4 °C in just 15 min, surpassing heat pipe-based cooling systems by more than 180% in speed. We also quantify the cooling performance of our device by calculating the electricity consumption of the thermostatic heating device. Additionally, benefiting from the direct evaporative cooling effect between the evaporative system and the surrounding environment37,49 (Supplementary Fig. 34), a global evaluation of the cooling energy saving performance of BEES-4v was conducted based on typical meteorological year data from 2013–2022 (Supplementary Note 5). This potential for sustainable economic benefits is expected to deliver critical technical support across a broad spectrum of cutting-edge fields, including building energy conservation, data center cooling, and industrial refrigeration, thereby playing a pivotal role in mitigating global carbon emissions and enhancing economic efficiency (see Supplementary Fig. 35).

Discussion

We have developed a space-integrated bulk-effect evaporation system, named BEES, capable of achieving an ultra-high evaporation rate of 21.29 kg m−2 h−1. This remarkable evaporation rate is primarily attributed to its excellent energy harvesting capability and its ability to enhance the kinetics of vapor diffusion through the integration of reverse Tesla valve channels. Furthermore, when introducing airflow, a unique interaction between the reverse Tesla valve channels and the airflow can effectively eliminate vapor dead zones, reduce vapor diffusion lengths, and significantly improve heat transfer between the channels and the environment due to the formation of special vortices. Notably, though our BEES performs best in evaporation compared to existing devices with various microchannel structures. Given the vast number of unexplored structures, our BEES design may not always be optimal. Nevertheless, as an advanced study emphasizing the modulation of convective transport at the gas-liquid interface in bulk evaporation systems to enhance evaporation and cooling efficiency in solar-driven desalination, we hope this work will inspire further exploration of higher-performance evaporation structures.

Methods

Materials

Sodium chlorite and acetic acid used in the experiment were purchased from Aladdin Co., Ltd, China. Silica powder (particle size 13 μm) and polyacrylonitrile (molecular weight 150,000) were purchased from MacLean Co., Ltd., China. N, N-dimethylformamide was purchased from Yongchang Reagent Co., Ltd., China. Biochar powder was purchased from Longxin Environmental Technology Co., Ltd., China. Basswood was purchased from Mingli Co., Ltd., China.

Preparation process of the BEES

First, the columnar array was constructed on the upper surface of the wood using laser engraving. Tesla valve channels were constructed on the lower surface of the wood by precision milling. Subsequently, the wood was placed in a 2.5 wt% sodium chlorite solution (acidic) at 80 °C to remove lignin. The freeze-dried delignified wood was thermostated at 500 °C for 3 h to obtain the carbonized wood precursor. After mixing the carbonized wood precursor with pure silicon powder, it was placed in an argon atmosphere and heated to 1600 °C at 10 °C min−1 for 2 h to perform a molten silicon reaction. Finally, the polyacrylonitrile powder and N, N-dimethylformamide solution were prepared as an electrostatic spinning precursor solution in a ratio of 1:9. Biochar powder, polyacrylonitrile powder, and N, N-dimethylformamide solution were prepared as a biochar electrostatic spray precursor solution in the ratio of 3:1:6. The solution was stirred and injected into a JDF05 electrostatic spinning machine with a voltage of 20 kV and a pressure speed of 0.1 mm s−1. Biochar/E-Spin layers were constructed on the wood surface by alternating electrospinning and electrostatic spraying. BEES with a Tesla valve and a multi-level hierarchical structure were fabricated.

Materials characterization

The structural characteristics of BEES were observed using scanning electron microscopy (JSN-7500F, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. The reflectance spectrum and optical transmittance of BEES were measured in the range of 350–2500 nm using a spectrophotometer (LabSpecPro FR/A1142, UK). Thermal conductivity was measured using a laser flash thermal conductivity meter (LFA 467, Germany) to determine the thermal conductivity parameters of BEES and the other materials. Digital images of the infrared temperature of the BEES were taken using the HT-18 infrared camera. Continuous temperature measurement was performed using an ST1004 thermocouple thermometer. Evaporation experiments were carried out under a solar simulator with a CEL-SA500/350 xenon lamp. The light absorbance of the methylene blue solution was measured using a Cary 100 UV-visible spectrophotometer.

Measurement of solar vapor generation

The solar vapor evaporation unit was placed on an electronic balance, and the evaporation was monitored under solar simulator irradiation (25.5 °C, 40% RH, Harbin, China). The BEES was placed in an experimental water cistern, and the mass change of water in the solar evaporator was recorded by the electronic balance.

Measurement of energy-saving cooling power of evaporative cooling units

The water pump and thermostatic heater were placed in the water tank of the evaporative cooling unit, and evaporative cooling was maintained by intermittently spraying water onto the unit. The outdoor environment was simulated by supplementing wind energy on the outside (25 °C, 40% RH, 2 m s−1 airflow, Harbin, China). The thermostatic heater was connected to a power meter, and the energy-saving cooling power was obtained by measuring the power consumption of the evaporative cooling unit.

Wind speed measurement in experiments

In this study, the airflow direction was consistently aligned perpendicularly with the surface containing the microchannel inlets of each evaporation system (Supplementary Fig. 36). Wind speed and temperature were measured using a thermal anemometer (TESTO 405 V1, accuracy: 0.01 m s−1). The sensor was positioned 1.5 cm in front of the windward center of each evaporation system, outside the system boundary. An airflow control hood was mounted at the front end of the stepless-speed fan. Wind speed at the measurement point was adjusted by varying the fan power, ensuring consistency across all evaporation systems. The airflow hood between the fan and the sample was used to maintain steady and uniform flow conditions, thereby ensuring the validity and rigor of the comparative experiments.

Airflow channel aerodynamics characterization

The specimen was horizontally mounted in a custom wind tunnel (Supplementary Fig. 37). A smoke generator was set up on one side of the wind tunnel device to generate colored smoke. An infinitely variable fan creates directional and stable convection inside the wind tunnel device and drives the smoke from inside the airflow channel of the prototype. A high-speed camera (Photron, FASTCAM Mini AX200) was set up directly above the wind tunnel unit to record the airflow movement inside the convection channel in real time. Simulations involving heat transfer, dehumidification, and similar phenomena are solved using multiphysics models (Supplementary Note 6).
