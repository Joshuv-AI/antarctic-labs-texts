Investigation of cellulose nanocrystals fabricated via sulfuric acid combined with deep eutectic solvent route
Authors: Jianli Yang, Siyu Yao, Zuguo Song, Chengchun Wu, Hanghang Han, Jiahao Gong, Chen Hu, Yuhan Liu, Yanran Wang, Lijuan Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo make full use of tobacco waste in cigarette production and enhance resource utilization efficiency, cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) were prepared by a method of sulfuric acid combined with a deep eutectic solvent. This method was compared with conventional methods, and the CNCs were thoroughly characterized. The results indicated that the yield of CNCs prepared by the sulfuric acid combined with a deep eutectic solvent method exceeded 26 %, with the highest yield reaching 29.5 %, surpassing the yields from two other methods of 12.5 %, 17.2 %, corresponding to sulfuric acid and sulfuric acid-oxalic acid, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy revealed a laminar morphology of CNCs, in which the CNCs maintained a cellulose type I structure with well-preserved crystallinity. In addition, CNCs prepared by the sulfuric acid combined with a deep eutectic solvent method exhibited good thermal stability, with maximum mass loss at temperature of 342.8 °C during third pyrolysis stage, higher than that of sulfuric acid at 316.7 °C and sulfuric acid-oxalic acid at 335.9 °C. Steady-state rheological testing revealed that the viscosity of the CNCs suspension decreased with an increasing shear rate, which is characteristic of pseudoplastic fluid shear thinning behavior. CNCs prepared by the sulfuric acid combined with DES method reduced the amount of sulfuric acid, and the yield was much higher than that of the other two methods. Moreover, the method using sulfuric acid combined with DES method was simple and safe, and the obtained CNCs had high thermal stability and viscosity, which expands the application prospects in biocomposite material fields.Keywords: Tobacco waste, Sulfuric acid combined with deep eutectic solvent, Cellulose nanocrystals, Characterization

Introduction

Cellulose is globally recognized as the most abundant and widely distributed renewable biological resource, known for its low cost and exceptional biophysical properties. Nevertheless, due to its dense structure and intermolecular hydrogen bonding, cellulose is insoluble in most solvents, which impedes its efficient utilization [1]. Chemical, physical, and biological methods can reduce cellulose to the nanoscale dimension along one axis, resulting in nanocellulose with dimensions up to a few micrometers. Nanocellulose combines the physiochemical attributes of nanomaterials with the physiological properties of cellulose, to exhibit exceptional characteristics including a large specific surface area, high strength, and a significant aspect ratio [2,3]. Nanocellulose holds significant application value, encompassing fields such as composite materials, biomedicine, food science, and chemical engineering. Based on its morphological structure, nanocellulose can be categorized into four main types: cellulose nanocrystalline (CNCs), fibrous nanocellulose, spherical nanocellulose, and sheet-like nanocellulose [4].

The primary methods for preparing CNCs involve the degradation of cellulose molecules, utilizing chemical approaches such as acid treatment [[5], [6], [7]], ionic liquids, and deep eutectic solvents (DESs) [8,9], along with physical methods such as ultrasound and high-speed homogenization [10]. Biological methods such as enzymatic hydrolysis [11], are employed to selectively degrade the non-crystalline regions, thereby preserving the short rod-shaped or needle-like crystalline structures [1,12]. DESs typically consist of two or three components, comprising at least one hydrogen bond acceptor and one hydrogen bond donor. DESs have numerous advantages over ionic liquids (ILs), because they are low cost, nontoxic, and recyclable. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of utilizing DESs instead of conventional reaction systems for producing and dispersing nanomaterials. CNCs were prepared from microcrystalline cellulose by combining chemical hydrated DESs with mechanical high shear force methods, and the yield of CNCs increased from 27.2 % (0.5 h) to 65.2 % (2.5 h) with extended treatment time [9]. Sulfuric acid hydrolysis is an efficient method that allows obtaining high-purity cellulose nanocrystals in a relatively short time. While the pure sulfuric acid hydrolysis method remains the most commonly employed technique, it requires a significant amount of concentrated sulfuric acid and poses challenges such as acid recovery difficulties and equipment corrosion. For example, studies have proposed a method for sulfuric acid combined with organic acid catalyzed hydrolysis to produce spherical CNCs, primarily utilizing a mixture of anhydrous oxalic acid [13] or maleic acid [14] with concentrated sulfuric acid for CNC extraction, although this process still requires concentrated sulfuric acid. Another approach involves preparing DESs by mixing oxalic acid dihydrate with choline chloride or citric acid with betaine [15], or combining the resulting DES with concentrated sulfuric acid for CNC preparation [16]. However, CNCs synthesized using these two DESs exhibit low pyrolysis temperatures and poor thermal stability. However, preparation of CNCs using sulfuric acid combined with DES was rarely reported.

China is a significant producer in the domain of tobacco cultivation, yielding about 3 million tons of tobacco leaves yearly. Unfortunately, between 25 % and 30 % of these leaves and remnants are unusable in cigarette production. Consequently, the nationwide volume of tobacco waste is estimated to exceed 1.5 million tons. This waste abundant in cellulose, usually contains approximately 35 % raw fibers [17]. Through careful management, this waste can be converted into natural chemical raw materials meeting the highest environmental standards. This practice not only mitigates alterations to soil composition and structure caused by tobacco waste but also prevents pollution from hazardous constituents. Hence, the strategic utilization of tobacco waste, a plentiful byproduct resource, offers significant economic, societal, and environmental benefits by converting waste into valuable resources.

In this study, a coupled method involving dilute sulfuric acid and a DESs was employed to hydrolyze tobacco waste for the preparation of CNCs. A subsequent characterization analysis was conducted with the aim of incorporating CNCs into heat-not-burn tobacco products, thereby enhancing their mechanical and liquid-holding properties. This endeavor seeks to broaden the application value and scope of CNCs.

Materials and methods

Tobacco waste was provided by Shaanxi Tobacco Industrial Co., Ltd. Other reagents including sulfuric acid, oxalic acid, lactic acid, and glycerol are all of analytical grade.

Preparation of cellulose

To prepare the cellulose, 100 g of pulverized tobacco waste powder was taken and added to 2000 mL of 5 % NaOH solution. Extraction was conducted at 80 °C for 60 min, followed by the addition of 100 mL of 5 % H2O2 and continuous reflux for 1 h. The supernatant was removed by centrifugation, and the residue was washed with deionized water until a neutral pH was obtained. Subsequently, the material was subjected to freeze-drying under vacuum to obtain the cellulose.

Preparation of CNCs

Sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis method

A certain amount of cellulose was weighed, and then added to a coupling solution of sulfuric acid combined with DES according to the liquid-to-solid ratio for catalytic hydrolysis (Table 1). After mixing with deionized water and centrifugation, the process was repeated 3–5 times. Subsequently, dialysis using dialysis bags (Sangon Biotech, China) with a molecular weight cut-off ≥2000 Da was carried out until the pH of the cellulose nanocrystalline suspension was approximately 7. Following centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min, CNCs were obtained through vacuum freeze-drying. The method for preparing the sulfuric acid combined with DES coupling solution involved mixing oxalic acid and glycerol in a specific mass ratio, heating them in an ultrasonic bath for 30 min, and then sulfuric acid was added in a specific mass fraction.

Ultrasound-assisted inorganic acid hydrolysis method

Ten grams of cellulose was weighed and added to 80 mL of a 35. wt% sulfuric acid solution with a liquid-to-solid ratio of 1:8. Catalytic hydrolysis was conducted at 90 °C for 4 h under ultrasound assistance. After mixing with deionized water and centrifugation, the process was repeated 3–5 times. Subsequently, dialysis using dialysis with a molecular weight cut-off ≥2000 Da was carried out until the pH of the suspension was approximately 7. After centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min, CNCs were obtained through vacuum freeze-drying.

Sulfuric acid-oxalic acid hydrolysis method

Briefly, 10 g of cellulose was weighed and added to 80 mL of a sulfuric acid–oxalic acid mixed solution. Catalytic hydrolysis is conducted at 90 °C for 4 h. After mixing with deionized water and centrifugation, the process was repeated 3–5 times. Subsequently, dialysis using dialysis bags with a molecular weight cut-off ≥2000 Da was carried out until the pH of the suspension was approximately 7. Following centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min, CNCs were obtained through vacuum freeze-drying. The preparation of the sulfuric acid-oxalic acid mixed solution involved dissolving oxalic acid in pure water at a ratio of 1:4, followed by mixing with 35. wt % sulfuric acid at a ratio of 1:3.

Characterization of cellulose nanocrystalline

SEM analysis

The microscopic morphology was observed at 5000× magnification using a JSM-7001F field emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan) after the sample coated with gold.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) testing

Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Vertex 70, Bruker, Germany) was employed to analyze the samples with a detection range of 4000–400 cm−1 for transmittance with a resolution of 4 cm⁻1.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) testing

The crystalline structure of the samples was observed using a D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (Bruker, Germany) with CuKα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) in the scanning range of 2θ = 10°–90°.

Thermogravimetry-derivative thermogravimetry analysis (TG-DTG)

Sample analysis was performed using an STA449F5 simultaneous thermal analyzer (Netzsch, Germany) with a gas flow rate of 20 mL/min, temperature ranging from 30 to 800 °C, and a heating rate of 10 °C/min.

Rheological behavior characterization

Steady-state rheological testing of a suspension of the solid mass fraction (0.72 %) of the CNCs was conducted using a rotational rheometer (Discovery HR-1, TA, USA). A 1.5 mL suspension was utilized with a 25 mm fixture, and shear rates ranging from 10−1 to 10−3/s were applied at 25 °C.

Results and discussion

Visual comparison of CNCs obtained from the three preparation methods

The CNCs obtained through the sulfuric acid hydrolysis method exhibited a dark brown fibrous appearance after freeze-drying (Fig. S1). By contrast, CNCs prepared via sulfuric acid–oxalic acid hydrolysis appeared darker brown with a similar fibrous texture post-freeze-drying. Conversely, CNCs synthesized using the sulfuric acid combined with DES method presented as fluffy powders with a slightly lighter coloration. It is noteworthy that the sulfuric acid hydrolysis method required a large amount of sulfuric acid, whereas the sulfuric acid combined with DES method reduced sulfuric acid consumption and minimized waste liquid recovery.

Characterization results

SEM characterization

The morphology of CNCs obtained through scanning electron microscopy is depicted in Fig. 1. At the micrometer scale, CNCs appeared as flakes with significant interconnecting adhesion between them. Notably, CNCs from the sulfuric acid-oxalic acid mixed hydrolysis group and the sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis group exhibited a more porous structure compared with the other groups. The freeze-dried CNCs depicted in Fig. 1c and d resulted in the formation of large porous amorphous irregular pieces in various sizes, while there were almost no porosity and small pores in Fig. 1a and b. Blue arrows indicated the porous regions within the material. The particles of CNCs collided, and agglomerated during the freeze-drying process. The robust hydrogen bonds between crystallite sites acted to bind the CNCs together during sample drying for SEM imaging [6].

SEM characterization of tobacco powder and CNCs prepared by three methods. a tobacco powder, b sulfuric acid hydrolysis, c sulfuric acid-oxalic acid hydrolysis, and d sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis.

FT-IR analysis

As shown in Fig. 2 and Table 3, it was observed that CNCs prepared using all three methods exhibited hydroxyl (–OH) vibration absorption peaks in the range of 3200–3600 cm−1, characterized by broad peaks that indicated a high quantity of hydroxyl groups and the formation of hydrogen bonds [18]. Peaks around 1334 and 1436 cm−1 corresponded to characteristic peaks of cellulose molecules. Absorption peaks were also observed at 3410, 2902, 1630, 1430, 1163, 1110, 1060, and 898 cm−1, indicating the presence of cellulose I. Peaks at 1510 and 1246 cm−1 represented the C=C vibration of lignin. The peak at 1739 cm−1 corresponded to the stretching vibration of carbonyl groups in carboxylic acid or ester groups in hemicellulose and lignin [19]. Notably, this peak was absent in the spectra of CNCs prepared via sulfuric acid–oxalic acid hydrolysis and sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis, indicating effective removal of lignin and hemicellulose by NaOH treatment.

XRD analysis

The XRD patterns of the four samples are depicted in Fig. 3. CNCs prepared by three methods exhibited major diffraction peaks around 15.3°, 21.5°, and 34.6°, corresponding to the diffraction peaks of the three crystal planes in cellulose I [20]. This indicates that treatments with sulfuric acid, sulfuric acid–oxalic acid, and sulfuric acid combined with DES did not alter the crystalline structure of cellulose. Furthermore, the diffraction peaks of CNCs obtained from sulfuric acid and sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis exhibited sharp peaks, while the crystallinity of CNCs obtained from sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis method was well-maintained.

Thermogravimetric analysis

The TG-DTG curves of CNCs prepared using the three methods are presented in Fig. 4a and b. The first stage of degradation occurring between 30 °C and 100 °C was primarily attributed to the evaporation of free moisture. The second stage, observed between 100 °C and 210 °C, involved the disruption of intermolecular hydrogen bonds or the degradation of low molecular weight compounds present in the sample. The third stage, occurring between 210 °C and 400 °C, mainly involved the dehydration, decarboxylation, and depolymerization of glycosidic bonds within the CNCs chains. The fourth stage involved further oxidation and decomposition of carbon residues into low molecular weight components such as hydrogen, ethylene, ethane, and other volatile substances. The fragmentation stage was fundamentally consistent with the extraction of cellulose nanocrystals from alternative sources as reported in the literature [14,21].

TG curves (a) and DTG curves (b) of CNCs prepared by three methods.

The initial degradation began at a temperature of 196 °C for the tobacco powder, while CNCs were obtained through sulfuric acid hydrolysis at 155 °C, through mixed acid hydrolysis at 238 °C, and through sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis at 235 °C. CNCs prepared using three methods exhibited three thermal degradation intervals before 500 °C. The with maximum mass loss at the third stage of thermal decomposition temperature was 317.1 °C, 330.9 °C, and 342.8 °C for CNCs prepared via sulfuric acid hydrolysis, sulfuric acid–oxalic acid hydrolysis, and sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis, respectively. Tobacco powder exhibited two peaks during the pyrolysis stage, with no peak ranging from 300 °C to 400 °C. Comparatively, CNCs prepared via sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis showed the highest thermal stability among the three preparation methods, also higher than tobacco powder.

Furthermore, CNCs prepared via sulfuric acid sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis exhibited two additional thermal decomposition intervals between 500 °C and 700 °C, possibly attributed to ester bond cleavage of cellulose in oxalic acid and hydroxyl group cleavage. The weight loss of CNCs prepared via sulfuric acid–oxalic acid and sulfuric acid combined with DES method hydrolysis was higher than that of tobacco powder and CNCs prepared via sulfuric acid hydrolysis. This may be due to the presence of oxalic acid in both sulfuric acid–oxalic acid and sulfuric acid combined with DES method hydrolysis samples, in which some carboxyl groups were grafted onto the hydroxyl groups of CNCs, resulting in lower residual rates due to decarboxylation and depolymerization. Compared with CNCs prepared from tobacco stalks (with an initial decomposition temperature of 252.36 °C) [17], the thermal stability of CNCs in this study was significantly improved.

Rheological behavior study

From Fig. 5, it can be observed that the viscosity of CNCs gradually decreased with an increasing shear rate. When the shear rate was less than 10 s−1, the viscosity decreased rapidly with an increasing shear rate. Subsequently, as the shear rate further increased, the apparent viscosity tended to level off, indicating the shear-thinning behavior characteristic of pseudoplastic fluids. Among the three methods of CNCs preparation, CNCs obtained via sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis exhibited the highest initial viscosity, but the final viscosity converged to a similar level as the other two preparation methods. Generally, a higher viscosity of CNCs indicates stronger fluid-holding properties. Therefore, CNCs produced via sulfuric acid combined with DES hydrolysis were more suitable for the production of new types of tobacco sheets.

Rheological behavior diagram of CNCs prepared by three methods.

High shear rates easily disrupted the entanglement between cellulose molecular chain segments, consequently reducing flow resistance. In Addition, strong macromolecular entanglement could decrease the suspension relaxation time, thereby enhancing shear-thinning behavior [22]. At equivalent concentrations and shear rates, CNC suspensions exhibited greater viscosity owing to enhanced macromolecular interaction, resulting in a more rigid network structure [23].

Conclusions

In this study, a sulfuric acid combined with DES method was employed to prepare CNCs from tobacco waste. CNCs prepared via the sulfuric acid combined DES method exhibited significantly higher extraction yield rates of 29.5 %, compared with two other methods. Structural characterization indicated that CNCs prepared by all three methods exhibited a flake-like morphology under scanning electron microscopy and retained the cellulose I structure. The removal of lignin was more effective with the sulfuric acid-oxalic acid and sulfuric acid combined with DES method, leading to improved crystallinity. Notably, CNCs prepared using the sulfuric acid combined with DES demonstrated superior thermal stability with the initial degradation temperature 342.8 °C of the third stage and viscosity. This suggests that CNCs prepared using this method are more suitable for processing heat-not-burn products, offering enhanced stability and fluid-holding properties. This study established the extraction method for cellulose nanocrystals, laying the foundation for subsequent synthesis of thermally conductive composite materials with carbon nanoparticles, and expanded the application in the development of biodegradable film materials.
