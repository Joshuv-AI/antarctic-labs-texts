Balloon dilation of the para-device space to alleviate severe flow restriction in an oversized pulmonary flow restrictor: a case report
Authors: Raymond N Haddad, Hanan Faraji, Ahmed Adel Hassan, Mahmoud Al Soufi, Mohamed Kasem
Published: 2025

Background

Managing pulmonary artery (PA) pressure and blood flow is critical in treating infants with congenital heart disease (CHD) and pulmonary overcirculation. A promising approach utilizes manually modified microvascular plugs (MVPs), which are FDA-approved and CE-marked devices from Medtronic Inc., USA. These MVPs, serving as pulmonary flow restrictors (PFRs), offer a less invasive alternative to conventional surgical PA banding [1, 2]. Further studies are needed to fully explore potential procedural complications, refine techniques, and optimize the long-term use of PFRs [1–3]. Herein, we present a safe and effective new strategy of dilating the para-device space to relieve severe PFR restriction.

Case presentation

A full-term newborn, delivered via cesarean section with a birth weight of 2300 g, was antenatally diagnosed with tetralogy of Fallot, later refined to a double outlet right ventricle, featuring a large, malaligned subaortic ventricular septal defect, unrestricted pulmonary blood flow, small arterial duct, and dilated left heart chambers. The infant experienced persistent respiratory distress from birth, requiring oral antifailure therapy, including ACE inhibitors and diuretics, and 7 days of non-invasive ventilation. Upon admission for worsening respiratory distress, the infant was stabilized in an incubator and placed under continuous cardio-respiratory monitoring.

Following a multidisciplinary discussion, the decision was made to control pulmonary over-circulation by implanting bilateral microvascular plug (MVP)-based pulmonary flow restrictors (PFRs) on day 24 of life (weight: 2.4 kg, length: 50 cm). The reasons for favoring endovascular banding over traditional surgical banding include the desire to avoid a sternotomy before the planned complete repair at a later stage, our positive cumulative experience with this approach [1], and the generally favorable surgical outcomes after PFR removal [4]. This decision was made despite the known advantages of surgical banding, which include the absence of a need for 24-hour anticoagulation, no requirement for ongoing antiplatelet therapy, and no associated risk of intimal damage.

After obtaining informed parental consent, the procedure was performed in a biplane catheterization laboratory under general anesthesia, with prophylactic antibiotics and systemic heparinization. Arterial and venous accesses were secured under ultrasound guidance. The infant’s hemoglobin was 16 g/dL, baseline oxygen saturation was 96–97%, and the Qp: Qs ratio was calculated as 2.3:1.0 under an inspired fraction of oxygen (FiO2) at 21%. On baseline angiography, left and right pulmonary arteries measured 3.8 mm and 4.1 mm, respectively, with evidence of pulmonary congestion (Fig. 1A, Vid. 1).

(A) Baseline PA angiography. (B) Angiography showing significant flow restriction to the right lung (white arrow) after implantation of an MVP-7Q. (C) Para-device dilation with a 2.0 mm semi-compliant MINI TREK coronary balloon (white triangle). (D) Repeat angiography showing improved flow to the right PA with better pulmonary venous return (white arrows)

To address this, MVPs from Medtronic were manually fenestrated by removing one triangle with a surgical scalpel at the most proximal inflow V-line of the nitinol wire (Fig. 2). As our routine practice, we initially attempted to implant an MVP-5Q in the left PA using a Progreat 2.7 Fr microcatheter from Terumo but faced technical difficulties in avoiding jailing the left upper lobe branch without having the device protruding in the PA bifurcation (Vid. 2). This was due to the short anatomic distance between the left upper PA and the main PA bifurcation. Therefore, we revised the approach and started by successfully implanting an MVP-7Q in the right PA via a 4Fr diagnostic glide catheter, followed by the placement of the MVP-5Q in the left PA (Vid. 3, 4). Although the diameter difference between the two branch PAs was minimal, we chose an oversized MVP-7Q for the right side based on our previously published protocol, which recommends an MVP-5Q for vessels with diameters up to 4 mm [1]. This decision was based on the understanding that excessive oversizing of the octagon-shaped MVP within a circular vessel could prevent proper expansion of both the device and its PTFE covering, potentially causing a para-device leak [5]. However, leaving the device in place for an extended period increases the risk of intimal injury.

Manually modified MVP-7Q (Medtronic) featuring a single fenestrated triangle (black arrow) at the most proximal inflow V-line of the nitinol wire (black V-shaped marking). Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) excision was performed using a surgical scalpel

Post-implantation of both PFRs, angiography, and transthoracic ultrasound revealed significant flow restriction to the right lung, with oxygen saturation dropping to 60% (Fig. 2B, Vid. 5). To improve right lung flow, we crossed next to the right PFR with a combination of microcatheter and 0.014-inch Whisper coronary wire from Abbott (Vid. 6), and the para-device space was gently dilated using a 2 × 15 mm MINI TREK and subsequently a 3 × 15 mm TREK semi-compliant coronary dilation catheters from Abbott (Fig. 2C, Vid. 7, 8). Repeat angiography and echocardiography confirmed improved flow to the right PA with better pulmonary venous return (Fig. 2D, Vid. 9). There were no signs of distal thrombosis, device migration, or fracture. At the conclusion of the procedure, oxygen saturation improved to 87%, and the Qp: Qs ratio was reduced to 1.1:1.0. The patient was transferred to the ICU for 24-hour monitoring and intravenous heparin. Oral aspirin and clopidogrel were started.

Twenty-four hours post-catheterization, the patient was stable, not requiring inotropic support, with a sinus rhythm of 125–135 bpm and a mean arterial pressure of 40–50 mmHg. Peripheral pulses were palpable, with a capillary refill time of < 2 s. The patient was breathing spontaneously, maintaining oxygen saturations between 90 and 93% on room air. There were no signs of stridor or retractions. A follow-up ultrasound at 48 h confirmed that both PFRs were in place, with gradients of 55 mmHg on the left and 48 mmHg on the right, and a small arterial duct was noted (Vid. 10).

One week later, the patient was discharged on aspirin, clopidogrel, diuretics, and beta-blockers. At the one-month follow-up, the patient was off medications, gaining weight, and had an oxygen saturation of 92% on room air. A Doppler ultrasound showed a gradient of 70 mmHg on the left side and 45 mmHg on the right, with no evidence of ductal flow. By the 12-week follow-up, the patient’s weight had increased to 4.8 kg.

The patient underwent biventricular repair and PFR removal nine months post-intervention at a weight of 7.9 kg. Both PFRs were extracted in one piece via the main PA using combined blunt and sharp dissection. On the right side, the surgeon observed endothelial reaction with fibrotic areas and wall thinning, necessitating autologous patch repair. Post-procedure assessment with Hegar dilators confirmed appropriate size and patency of both branch PAs, with no residual stenosis. The patient was discharged on postoperative day five. At two-week follow-up, echocardiography confirmed patent branch PAs and satisfactory surgical outcomes.

Discussion and conclusions

This case highlights the successful use of MVP-based PFRs to control pulmonary overcirculation in a critically ill newborn with a double outlet right ventricle and unrestricted pulmonary blood flow, delaying the need for corrective surgery until the patient reaches a larger weight [1, 2]. The anatomy of the infant, characterized by a short left PA hilar segment and a longer right PA segment, presented notable challenges. To avoid jailing the right PA and preserving access to the left PA upper lobe branch, we modified our initial approach by implanting the right PFR first, ensuring its proper positioning without compromising the left PA.

After positioning the right PFR, a Doppler gradient above 4 m/s was observed, indicating a tighter restriction than usual. However, the oxygen saturation dropped only by 5–8%, as one-lung restriction typically doesn’t cause significant desaturation. Since the left PA had a generous flow, we proceeded to implant the left PFR to evaluate the overall effect. After both PFRs were placed, it became apparent that the right PA was compromised, despite some distal filling. This suspicion was confirmed by a drop in oxygen saturation to 60%. The problem appeared to stem from the oversized MPV-7Q in the relatively long right PA, an anatomy that eliminated the awaited para-device leak, and the device’s cross-sectional fenestrated area was likely compressed or partially jailed by the PA wall. At this point, we had two options: snare the right PFR, modify its fenestration, and re-implant it, or replace it with a smaller MVP-5Q. The latter, however, would have increased the risk of para-device leak and migration [2, 3]. Instead of removing the device, which would have lengthened the procedure and increased radiation exposure, we opted to cross beside the PFR and dilate the para-device space using semi-compliant coronary balloons, carefully adjusting the space without dislodging the device. We believed that the contact area between the oversized PFR and the right PA wall would be sufficient to prevent device dislodgment or embolization, and this was confirmed after two balloon dilations, during which the device remained completely stable.

We were initially concerned that using a 3 mm balloon for dilation might lead to a loss of the restrictive effect, potentially resulting in overcirculation during follow-up, as seen in earlier cases [3]. However, it was clear that inducing a para-device leak was necessary to reduce tension on the inner PA wall and address the extremely tight initial fenestration, as the patient experienced a 30% drop in oxygen saturation, reaching 60–65%. To address this, we opted for a gradual balloon dilation, starting with a 2 mm balloon, which improved saturation by 15–20%. Following this, a re-dilation with a 3 mm balloon was deemed appropriate to achieve the target saturation levels of 85–90%. Fenestrating one triangle in the MVP-7Q device results in an equivalent circular surface area with a 2.8 mm diameter [1]. Given that the initial fenestration was minimal, we determined that dilating with a 3 mm balloon would best meet the patient’s needs. Post-procedural ultrasound confirmed a satisfactory restrictive effect, and oxygen saturation returned to the desired range. We were also concerned that the metal frame deformation caused by balloon dilation would revert over time due to the Nitinol memory shape of the MVP device. However, follow-up revealed no increase in restriction, confirming that these devices are highly fragile and can be easily distorted after manipulation [6]. We were also concerned about the risk of device fracture, given its inherent fragility, which makes it prone to structural failure. Aggressive, rapid, or non-compliant balloon dilation can easily lead to fractures and should be approached with caution. In fact, fractures have been observed with certain implantation techniques, especially when the device is positioned at the take-off of the right upper PA, where increased stress on the device frame can occur [2]. However, in our case, the careful use of semi-compliant coronary balloons with incremental diameters likely helped mitigate this risk effectively.

The patient underwent a successful complete repair nine months after PFR implantation, at a weight of 7.9 kg, confirming that the PFRs served their intended banding purpose. Despite the positive surgical outcome, consistent with our previous reports on successful PFR removal [4], endothelial damage was observed on the right side, requiring patch repair. This highlights the importance of carefully evaluating device oversizing and closely monitoring when the PFR is left in place for several months.

In conclusion, balloon dilation of the para-device space was a safe and effective solution for severe PFR restriction, restoring adequate flow without dislodging the device. It provided a quick, safe alternative to device removal, fenestration adjustments, and MVP size modifications. This case underscores the trade-off between sizing and embolization risks, where oversizing hinders expansion and damages the PA wall, while undersizing raises embolization and leaking concerns. Device size selection should be flexible, particularly in cases of borderline PA diameters. While excellent surgical outcomes were achieved, intimal scarring remains a concern with oversized, long-standing devices, necessitating patch repair.
