Direct Oral Anticoagulants Versus Low‐Molecular‐Weight Heparin for Treating Venous Thromboembolism in Patients With Gastrointestinal Cancer
Authors: Wei‐Ting Chang, Mei‐Chuan Lee, Hui‐Wen Lin, Sheng‐Hsiang Lin, Yi‐Heng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDirect oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have demonstrated non‐inferiority to low‐molecular‐weight heparin (LMWH) for preventing recurrent venous thromboembolism (VTE) in cancer‐associated thrombosis. However, patients with gastrointestinal (GI) cancer receiving DOACs have reported increased major bleeding events. Hereby, we aim to compare the risks of recurrent thromboembolism and bleeding between those treated with DOACs and LMWH.MethodsUsing Taiwan's National Health Insurance Research Database, we retrospectively identified GI cancer patients with newly diagnosed VTE from 2011 to 2020. Outcomes were recurrent VTE and major bleeding. Covariate balancing was performed using inverse probability weighting, and Cox proportional hazards models compared risks, with a competing risk model for death. Follow‐up was 12 months.ResultsAmong 1895 patients (1038 on DOACs, 857 on enoxaparin), recurrent VTE occurred in 20.71% of DOAC and 19.25% of enoxaparin patients. Competing risk analysis showed relatively higher recurrent VTE risk with DOACs (subdistribution HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.09–1.48; p < 0.01). Regarding composite bleeding risk, DOACs were similar compared with LMWH (subdistribution HR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.68–1.45; p = 0.97). However, DOACs presented with an increased GI bleeding risk (subdistribution HR, 2.59; 95% CI, 1.48–4.54; p < 0.01) but a reduced intracranial hemorrhage risk (subdistribution HR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.16–0.78; p = 0.01).ConclusionsCompared with DOACs, LMWH expressed a lower risk of recurrent VTE in GI cancer patients. However, DOAC presented with a lower intracranial hemorrhage risk but a higher GI bleeding risk. Further research is needed to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Cancer‐associated venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a frequent and life‐threatening complication in cancer patients, contributing substantially to morbidity and mortality [1, 2, 3, 4]. Tumor‐derived procoagulant factors and cancer‐directed therapies, such as surgery and chemotherapy, markedly increase thrombotic risk. Among malignancies, gastrointestinal (GI) cancers carry the highest VTE burden, particularly in pancreatic, gastric, and hepatic cancers [3, 4, 5]. Management of cancer‐associated thrombosis (CAT) is complex, as treatment must balance the elevated risk of recurrence with heightened bleeding susceptibility [3].

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) such as HOKUSAI Cancer, SELECT‐D, and CARAVAGGIO have compared direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) with low‐molecular‐weight heparin (LMWH) [6, 7, 8]. While efficacy in preventing recurrent VTE was largely similar, GI bleeding risk was higher with edoxaban and rivaroxaban, whereas apixaban demonstrated a safer bleeding profile [6, 7, 8]. Meta‐analyses have further supported DOACs as effective alternatives to LMWH, though bleeding risks, particularly in patients with unresected GI tumors, remain a concern [9, 10]. Importantly, RCTs often underrepresent advanced cancer patients who experience high mortality, making traditional hazard ratio (HR) estimates potentially misleading [6, 7, 8]. Death is a common competing risk in oncology, as patients who die cannot subsequently experience VTE or bleeding [11]. The Fine–Gray competing risk model thus provides a more accurate estimate of cumulative incidence than conventional Cox regression [11, 12]. Also, large‐scale real‐world evidence specifically addressing GI cancer–associated VTE remains scarce. Therefore, we aimed to investigate recurrent VTE and bleeding risks with DOAC versus LMWH in patients with GI cancer, incorporating death as a competing risk to reflect clinical reality more accurately.

Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective cohort study using data from Taiwan's National Health Insurance Research Database. Patients were categorized based on their post‐diagnosis anticoagulation therapy into two groups: those receiving DOACs, including rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, and dabigatran; and those treated with LMWH. Eligibility for inclusion required patients aged 20 years or older with a diagnosis of GI cancer between 2011 and 2020 who had complete clinical data available. From this cohort, those with newly diagnosed VTE were included. To ensure accurate assessment of anticoagulation outcomes, only patients who received anticoagulants or LMWH after VTE diagnosis were eligible. Patients were excluded if they had a history of atrial fibrillation or end‐stage renal disease. Additional exclusions included patients with prior joint replacement or valvular surgery within 1 year before the index date, those with index dates after December 31, 2020, and those with unused prescriptions or improperly defined medication pick‐up. In compliance with Taiwan's National Health Insurance guidelines, patients with VTE were required to undergo LMWH treatment for a minimum of 5 days before transitioning to edoxaban or dabigatran [13]. Consequently, we categorized patients who received LMWH within a 14‐day period prior to initiating DOAC treatment as part of the DOAC group [14]. Comorbidities were defined as having at least two outpatient visits or one hospitalization within 1 year prior to the index date [14]. Concomitant medications were defined as those prescribed within the 3 months prior to the index date [14].

Outcome Measures

Primary outcomes assessed the recurrence of VTE. Secondary outcomes included composite bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, gastrointestinal bleeding, other critical site bleeding (defined in International Classification of Disease in Table S1) [15], which were diagnosed during hospitalization. The ICD coding was selected and listed in Table S1 according to previous literature [14, 15]. The accuracy of identifying VTE using ICD‐10 codes has been validated in systematic reviews and meta‐analyses [16, 17]. Also, anatomical therapeutic chemical (ATC) codes for antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, and LMWHs were listed in Table S2 [14, 15]. The follow‐up duration was 12 months.

Statistical Analysis

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to balance baseline covariates between the DOAC and LMWH groups [18]. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression to adjust the covariables listed in Table 1 including year, sex, age, subgroup of VTE, cancer grade, comorbidities (including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, peripheral arterial occlusion disease, chronic kidney disease, and chronic liver disease), history of bleeding, concurrent antiplatelet therapy, and cancer treatments (chemotherapy/radiotherapy) [18]. The index date was defined as the date of the first prescription of either LMWH or DOAC post‐VTE diagnosis. Only outcomes occurring within 1 year of the index date were included in the analysis. Patients were followed from this index date until the occurrence of any of the study outcomes. Kaplan–Meier (K‐M) survival curves were generated to visually represent the time‐to‐event data for VTE and composite bleeding. Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to estimate HR for both primary and secondary outcomes [12, 15]. Meanwhile, using Fine–Gray competing risk model, subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) were carefully considered to ensure the robustness of the outcome assessments [11, 12]. Recognizing that death may preclude the occurrence of the primary outcomes such as VTE recurrence and major bleeding, we used methods appropriate for competing risks [19]. By incorporating these competing mortality risks, we aimed to provide a more accurate estimate of the treatment effects, reflecting the real‐world implications of using DOACs and LMWH among patients with GI cancer and VTE. In addition to our primary analysis that followed patients for a 1‐year period, we conducted a sensitivity test extending the follow‐up duration to the maximum allowable tracking period within our database. This extended follow‐up aimed to assess the long‐term outcomes and potential late effects of treatment with DOACs versus LMWH. We conducted all statistical analyses using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina). A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant in our study.

Subgroup Analysis

To further refine our understanding of the treatment effects across different patient demographics and clinical conditions, we conducted subgroup analyses based on baseline characteristics. These analyses allowed us to explore potential variations in treatment outcomes among distinct patient subgroups. We categorized patients by factors such as age, sex, type of malignancy, and severity of the initial VTE event. Each subgroup was analyzed to calculate the HR values for primary and secondary outcomes, providing insights into the efficacy and safety of DOACs versus LMWH in these specific populations.

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of National Cheng Kung University Hospital (Approval Number A‐EX‐111‐003) and is adherent to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. The retrospective nature of the study justified waiving the need for individual patient consent.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Patients

As Illustrated in Figure 1, the broader cohort initially consisted of 274, 392 patients diagnosed with GI cancer from 2011 through 2020, all of whom were at least 20 years old with complete data records. From this pool, exclusions were applied to 232 patients with a past history of atrial fibrillation, 50 with end‐stage renal disease, and 149 who had undergone valve surgery prior to the index date. Additionally, 324 were excluded for having an index date after December 31, 2020, and 2591 for not meeting the anticoagulant medication criteria or not using anticoagulants at all. The remaining 1895 eligible patients were subdivided based on their use of anticoagulants following a VTE diagnosis, resulting in a subgroup of 1038 patients using DOACs: specifically, 58 using dabigatran, 639 using rivaroxaban, 184 using apixaban, and 157 using edoxaban. The LMWH treatment group comprised 857 patients.

Flowchart of study design.

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of 1895 patients (DOACs = 1038 and LMWH = 857) diagnosed with GI cancer who were treated with either DOACs or LMWH before and after IPTW. Demographic data showed slight differences in sex distribution between the groups, with men comprising 57.10% of the total before IPTW adjustments. Post‐IPTW, the sex distributions were nearly balanced. Age analysis revealed that the mean age of DOAC users was slightly higher compared with that of LMWH users (68.18 ± 12.72 years vs. 65.40 ± 13.28 years, respectively) before IPTW, a difference that was statistically significant (p < 0.01). The median ages and interquartile ranges (IQR) were similarly adjusted post‐IPTW. The distribution of VTE subgroups (PE, DVT and combined PE and DVT) also displayed initial differences that were minimized after IPTW. The cancer grading scale showed a higher proportion of advanced grades (grade 3 and 4) among DOAC users before IPTW. The prevalence of comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, peripheral arterial occlusion disease or coronary artery disease, acute ischemic stroke, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, and a history of bleeding was documented. The statistically significant higher prevalence of hypertension in the DOAC group before IPTW was effectively adjusted post‐IPTW. A similar significant adjustment was observed in the history of chronic liver disease. Finally, treatment modalities including chemotherapy and radiotherapy were closely matched between the groups both before and after IPTW.

Efficacy and Safety Outcomes Comparing DOACs and LMWH Therapy with 1 Year Follow‐Up Duration

Among 1895 patients (1038 on DOACs and 857 on enoxaparin), recurrent VTE occurred in 20.7% of DOAC users and 19.3% of enoxaparin users. Figure 2A shows the unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curve for VTE risk, indicating no significant difference in survival probability between the two groups during 1 year of follow‐up. Consistently, the hazard ratio (HR) for recurrent VTE did not differ significantly between DOAC and LMWH users (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.79–1.07; p = 0.29) (Table S3). However, when accounting for death as a competing risk, the sHR for recurrent VTE was significantly higher among DOAC users compared with LMWH users (sHR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.09–1.48; p < 0.01) (Table 2).

With respect to bleeding outcomes, DOACs were associated with a lower composite bleeding risk than LMWH (HR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.42–0.90; p = 0.01) (Table S3), although the unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curve for composite bleeding showed no significant difference between the groups (Figure 2B). Similarly, after accounting for death as a competing risk, the risk of composite bleeding did not differ between DOAC and LMWH users (sHR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.68–1.45; p = 0.97) (Table 2). In further analyses, DOACs were associated with a higher risk of gastrointestinal bleeding (HR, 2.59; 95% CI, 1.48–4.54; p < 0.01), but a lower risk of intracranial hemorrhage (HR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.16–0.78; p = 0.01). Additionally, DOACs reduced the risk of other critical‐site bleedings (sHR, 0.10; 95% CI, 0.02–0.54; p = 0.01) compared with LMWH users (Table 2).

Efficacy and Safety Outcomes Comparing DOACs and LMWH Therapy with the Maximal Follow‐Up Duration

Furthermore, we extended the follow‐up period to the maximum duration permitted by the database to evaluate the long‐term effects of DOAC therapy compared with LMWH in patients with GI cancer. The extended follow‐up results were consistent with our initial findings, showing no significant difference in VTE outcomes between the two groups (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.86–1.16; p = 0.96) (Table S4). However, when accounting for death as a competing risk, DOAC users demonstrated a higher risk of recurrent VTE than LMWH users (sHR, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.25–1.67; p < 0.01) (Table 3). In contrast, while DOAC use was associated with a reduced risk of other critical‐site bleeding (sHR, 0.17; 95% CI, 0.05–0.58; p < 0.01), it was linked to an increased risk of gastrointestinal bleeding (sHR, 3.00; 95% CI, 1.94–4.64; p < 0.01), alongside a nonsignificant trend toward lower intracranial hemorrhage risk (sHR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.47–1.33; p = 0.37) compared with LMWH use.

Subgroup Analyses Focusing on Recurrent VTE and Bleeding

As illustrated in Figure 3A, our subgroup analysis of recurrent VTE associated with GI cancer highlights that patients under 65 years of age demonstrated a reduced risk of VTE when treated with DOACs. Similarly, patients with more advanced cancer (grades 3 and 4) also showed a statistically significant benefit from treatment with DOACs. Moreover, a notably significant improvement was observed in patients with a history of antiplatelet agent use. Figure 4A presents the K‐M curve for VTE risk, illustrating no statistically significant differences in VTE occurrence between patients treated with different anticoagulants over the study period (p = 0.17).

Discussion

In this study, we observed that among 1895 patients with GI cancer–associated VTE, one‐year outcomes showed no significant difference in recurrent VTE between DOAC and LMWH users (HR, 0.92; p = 0.29). However, when accounting for death as a competing risk, DOACs were associated with higher VTE recurrence (sHR, 1.27; p < 0.01). Regarding safety, although DOACs showed no significant difference in composite bleeding risks compared with LMWH (sHR, 0.99; p = 0.97), further analyses revealed increased gastrointestinal bleeding but reduced intracranial and other critical‐site bleeding with DOACs. Extended follow‐up confirmed these findings with similar directional effects. The major findings were summarized in Figure 5.

Major findings of this study.

Although currently, guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) have gradually shifted toward accepting DOAC use as an alternative to LMWH, this trend also underscores the importance of individual patient assessment, particularly considering factors like bleeding risk and life expectancy [3, 20, 21]. In contrast to previous reports [6, 14, 22, 23], we observed that DOACs may carry a higher risk of GI bleeding while relatively reducing the risk of intracranial hemorrhage. Of note, our subgroup analysis of younger patients (aged under 65 years) and those with advanced cancer (grades 3 and 4) showed a reduced risk of recurrent VTE with DOAC treatment. The observed reduced VTE risk in younger patients aligns with the previous body of evidence, reinforcing the idea that age is a critical factor in anticoagulant therapy, and younger patients with cancer may derive a greater benefit from DOAC use with lower associated risks. In terms of advanced‐stage cancer, which is often associated with a hypercoagulable state [2], leading to higher rates of VTE, the reduction in VTE risk in patients with advanced GI cancer treated with DOACs suggests that these medications may offer a protective effect even in more aggressive disease stages, which is a positive finding given the challenging nature of managing thrombosis in this patient cohort. Our present results may help refine patient selection for DOAC therapy, suggesting that these subgroups could be prioritized for DOAC treatment to maximize benefit and reduce VTE recurrence. However, our data also underscore the need to continue monitoring for bleeding complications, especially in patients with GI cancers.

Among LMWHs, tinzaparin has been extensively evaluated in cancer patients and offers several practical and pharmacological advantages, including once‐daily dosing, predictable pharmacokinetics, and a lower potential for drug–drug interactions [24, 25]. Importantly, tinzaparin has demonstrated favorable safety and efficacy profiles across diverse oncology settings, making it particularly relevant for patients with renal insufficiency, polypharmacy, or heightened gastrointestinal bleeding risk. While DOACs provide convenience and are increasingly used in routine practice, LMWHs such as tinzaparin remain crucial for individualized anticoagulation strategies in complex cancer populations [24, 25]. Nevertheless, our study did not specifically evaluate different LMWH agents, including tinzaparin, and therefore cannot directly compare their relative effectiveness or safety. Future studies incorporating agent‐level analyses are warranted to better define optimal anticoagulant selection in oncology patients.

Our study differs from prior randomized trials and meta‐analyses that suggested DOACs reduce the risk of recurrent VTE in cancer patients [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. In our GI cancer cohort, we observed no reduction in recurrent VTE with DOACs compared with LMWH, and competing risk analysis revealed a relatively higher risk of recurrence among DOAC users. Several factors may explain these discrepant findings. First, the patient population is an important distinction. RCTs typically enrolled heterogeneous cancer cohorts with relatively preserved functional status, whereas our study focused on GI cancers, which are strongly prothrombotic and carry a higher risk of both recurrence and bleeding [6, 7, 8]. Second, mortality was high in our cohort, with nearly half of patients dying within 1 year [26]. HR from Cox models estimate relative risk under the assumption of independent censoring [27]. In cancer populations with high competing mortality, this assumption is violated, as death precludes subsequent events such as recurrent VTE or bleeding [11]. sHRs from Fine–Gray competing risk models incorporate death as a competing event, providing risk estimates more reflective of real‐world patient trajectories [11, 12]. In our analysis, while HRs suggested no significant difference in VTE recurrence between DOACs and LMWH, sHRs revealed a higher recurrence risk with DOACs, underscoring the importance of competing risk adjustment in oncology populations. Finally, real‐world factors including older, frailer patients, comorbidities, and variability in adherence may further contribute to outcome differences [26]. Clinicians should carefully individualize anticoagulant choice in GI cancer patients, balancing recurrence and bleeding risks.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, retrospective studies are subject to inherent biases, such as selection bias, information bias, and potential misclassification of exposure and outcomes [28]. Our findings may have been influenced by factors not fully captured or controlled for in the database, such as variations in cancer severity, differences in concurrent treatments, and patient adherence to prescribed therapies. Second, the study is based on a Taiwanese population, and this may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations with different ethnic backgrounds, healthcare systems, and treatment practices. Third, although our study used stabilized IPTW to balance baseline covariates, there may still be unmeasured confounding factors that could affect the outcomes, such as detailed cancer staging, specific tumor characteristics, nutritional status, and the presence of other comorbidities. Fourth, although we carefully selected ICD codes based on prior literature and validated coding accuracy, misclassification remains a limitation in real‐world database analyses [14, 29]. To note, our study demonstrated a recurrent VTE rate of 21.3%, which is substantially higher than the recurrence rates reported in pivotal RCTs of NOACs in cancer‐associated thrombosis (ranging from 0.7% to 7.9%) [6, 7, 8]. This discrepancy likely reflects differences between trial populations and real‐world practice, including more advanced cancer stages, higher comorbidity burden, and less stringent follow‐up in routine care. For example, Chen et al. reported recurrent VTE or major bleeding in 14.1% of NOAC users [14]. These findings underscore the need for careful patient selection and vigilant monitoring. Fifth, the CARAVAGGIO trial found that all GI bleeding in GI cancer patients occurred in those with unresected tumors but in our study, we could not determine tumor resectability [8, 30]. The presence of unresected or advanced tumors may sustain hypercoagulability and diminish the protective effect of DOACs observed in broader populations [8, 30]. Also, hereby bleeding complications were restricted to ICH, GI, and other bleeding, whereas important sites such as genitourinary bleeding, a common source of bleeding in this population, were not captured. Last, for anticoagulant drugs ATC codes are required to ensure precision [14]. In contrast, for chemotherapy or radiotherapy, ICD codes such as Z51.11 (antineoplastic chemotherapy) and Z51.0 (radiotherapy) are generally sufficient, as they capture treatment encounters across both inpatient and outpatient settings, consistent with previous studies [14, 31]. However, when distinguishing between specific chemotherapeutic agents (e.g., cisplatin vs. oxaliplatin), ATC codes provide greater specificity and are therefore more appropriate.

Conclusions

In this large real‐world cohort of patients with gastrointestinal cancer–associated VTE, DOAC use was not associated with a lower risk of recurrent VTE compared with LMWH when accounting for death as a competing risk. Meanwhile, DOACs were associated with increased gastrointestinal bleeding but reduced intracranial and other critical‐site bleeding compared with LMWH. However, due to the study design and other limitations, further prospective studies or randomized controlled trials are needed to validate these findings and better inform clinical practice.
