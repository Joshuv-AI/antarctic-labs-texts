Women’s empowerment and barriers to healthcare access in Burkina Faso: an ordinal logistic regression analysis of the 2021 DHS
Authors: Narges Ebadi, Souleyman Diabate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough free healthcare policies for women have been implemented in Burkina Faso, access to essential services remains severely hindered by insecurity, climate shocks, and widespread displacement. As one of the most affected countries in West Africa by internal conflict and fragility, Burkina Faso provides a critical context for examining gendered health disparities. Yet, studies that address the aspects of women’s empowerment in this country remain rare, despite their importance for understanding healthcare access. Against this backdrop, the present study investigates how women’s empowerment dimensions, such as decision making, information and communication technologies (ICT) access, bank account ownership, and perceptions of justified violence, shapes healthcare access in Burkina Faso.MethodsA nationally representative sample of 12,868 partnered women aged 15–49 years was analyzed. Healthcare access was measured using a composite score based on four barriers: needing permission, financial constraints, distance to health facilities, and reluctance to seek care alone. Each barrier was coded as 1 (present) or 0 (absent), generating a total score from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating greater barriers. Key predictors included decision-making power, ICT access, bank account ownership, and acceptance of justified violence. To examine associations between healthcare access barriers and women’s empowerment indicators, both unadjusted and adjusted ordinal logistic regression models were employed.ResultsAmong the sampled women, 29.6% reported no barriers to healthcare access, while 5.8% experienced all four. In adjusted ordinal logistic regression models, women with moderate autonomy in decisions had significantly lower odds of experiencing high healthcare barriers (OR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.66–0.93), and those with high decision-making power had even lower odds (OR = 0.47; 95% CI: 0.28–0.78). Acceptance of justified violence was associated with increased odds of high barriers (OR = 2.14; 95% CI: 1.72–2.66). Women with the highest level of ICT access had markedly lower odds of facing high healthcare barriers (OR = 0.18; 95% CI: 0.14–0.22).ConclusionThis study shows that women’s empowerment dimensions, especially decision-making power and ICT access, substantially reduce healthcare barriers, while acceptance of justified violence increases them. Financial inclusion and sociodemographic factors such as higher education, urban residency, and household headship also improve access. These findings underscore the need to address both structural inequalities and restrictive norms to expand women’s healthcare access in Burkina Faso. Limitations include the cross-sectional design, reliance on self-reported data, and lack of qualitative insights.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

One important measure of social and economic growth is the health of women [1]. However, women still encounter obstacles such as restricted autonomy, financial reliance, constrictive gender norms, and limited access to information and transportation in many low- and middle-income nations [2, 3], These limitations make it more difficult for them to get timely medical attention, which increases the risk of avoidable disease and death 4). Therefore, enhancing health outcomes requires promoting women’s empowerment through education, income management, and decision-making participation [4, 5].

In Burkina Faso, persistent gender inequalities and weak health infrastructure further restrict women’s access to healthcare [6]. Despite national initiatives such as community health insurance and maternal health programs, utilization remains low due to structural and cultural barriers [7, 8]. Understanding how different dimensions of women’s empowerment, decision-making authority, financial inclusion, and social attitudes, affect healthcare access is crucial for guiding gender-responsive health policies in the country [9–11].

Burkina Faso not only faces poor health indicators but also contends with overlapping humanitarian crises that further undermine women’s access to care. Since 2015, insecurity [12] and recurrent climate shocks [13] have intensified vulnerabilities, forcing over one million people, mostly women and children, into displacement by 2020. Many lost access to essential health services due to facility closures, staff shortages, and limited resources [14].

In 2016, Burkina Faso introduced free healthcare policies to improve service access, including maternal and child health services [15, 16]. However, women continue to face substantial barriers to benefiting from these initiatives. Economic hardship and entrenched gender norms often restrict women’s autonomy, leaving many dependent on male relatives for healthcare decisions ([17–18]). Structural barriers, such as long distances to facilities, lack of transportation, shortages of trained personnel, insecurity-related disruptions, and negative provider attitudes, further limit women’s ability to obtain timely care, particularly in rural areas [12–14, 19]. In this context, promoting women’s empowerment is both a matter of equity and a practical strategy to enhance healthcare access and utilization [20].

Although previous studies have explored individual features of women’s empowerment in relation to healthcare access [17–40], there remains a notable gap in the evidence specific to Burkina Faso. Limited studies have used nationally representative data to analyze this relationship [17, 19].

Existing research often focuses on single aspects of empowerment, such as education or decision making, but rarely considers how multiple dimensions, financial autonomy, gender attitudes, and access to information, interact to influence healthcare access in fragile and resource-constrained settings. This knowledge gap limits the development of interventions that are both context-sensitive and effective [20, 21].

This study seeks to address that gap by providing a comprehensive analysis of dimensions of women’s empowerment and healthcare access in Burkina Faso, using data from the 2021 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS). By capturing the combined effects of multiple empowerment dimensions, this study contributes new evidence to inform policies and interventions tailored to Burkina Faso’s unique context, where poverty, insecurity, and gender inequalities converge to create persistent barriers to women’s health [12–14, 17–19]. Such context-specific insights are critical to designing gender-sensitive health strategies that move beyond policy promises and translate into real improvements in women’s lives [20, 21].

Review of literature

A growing body of evidence underscores the importance of women’s empowerment in improving healthcare access, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Empowerment is multidimensional, with economic independence often intertwined with decision-making autonomy, as women’s ability to generate income frequently enhances their participation in household decisions. Alongside access to information and control over resources, these factors shape women’s health-seeking behavior.

Economic empowerment has been consistently correlated with improved maternal and child health outcomes, emphasizing the critical role of financial resources in health- decision-making. Financial autonomy, often facilitated through income generation or bank account ownership, enables women to make independent decisions and invest in healthcare [22, 23]. Studies show that women with greater financial control are more likely to utilize maternal and reproductive health services [24, 25]. Yet, economic empowerment alone does not fully overcome structural and social barriers. Gender-based violence, restrictive cultural norms, and institutional biases continue to constrain women’s access to care, as demonstrated in research from India and Pakistan [26–28].

Access to information and communication technologies (ICTs) has also emerged as a significant dimension of empowerment. ICTs provide women with vital health information and platforms for awareness-raising, particularly in resource-constrained contexts. Mobile phones, the internet, and traditional media, radio, television, and newspapers, serve as important channels for disseminating knowledge and encouraging positive health behaviors. Evidence from Iran and Uganda links media exposure and ICTs access to improved maternal healthcare utilization [29, 30]. Similarly, in Pakistan, women’s literacy, labor force participation, and decision-making autonomy have been associated with greater use of reproductive health services [31].

Leadership and representation within healthcare systems further influence equity and access. Women’s presence in decision-making positions, whether in community health initiatives or institutional governance, has the potential to reduce gender-based disparities in service delivery and ensure that health systems better reflect the needs of women and children [32].

While women’s empowerment is critical for improving health access, its impacts are highly context-dependent ([33–34]). For instance, studies in Indonesia and Bangladesh show that women’s decision-making autonomy significantly improves reproductive healthcare utilization [35–37]. However, entrenched social and gender norms often continue to restrict access, even in settings where economic empowerment is present, as seen in Malawi [38]. At the same time, research from Guinea, Bangladesh, and sub-Saharan Africa demonstrates that empowerment through education and antenatal care awareness significantly enhances skilled birth attendance and overall maternal care [39–41].

Beyond autonomy and education, financial and informational resources also play a critical role. Micro-credit programs, for example, have been shown to strengthen women’s healthcare autonomy in Bangladesh [42]. Similarly, access to ICTs provides transformative opportunities, enabling women to obtain life-saving health information during pregnancy and beyond, thereby enhancing both access and quality of care [43–45]. To conceptualize these diverse influences, this study employs Kabeer’s empowerment framework [46, 47], which structures empowerment across three interrelated dimensions: resources (e.g., education, employment, wealth), agency (decision-making power), and achievements (outcomes such as healthcare access). This framework clarifies the complex ways empowerment operates. It is not simply about possessing assets [48, 49], but about the ability to make meaningful choices [50, 51]. Among these dimensions, agency plays a central role, as it reflects women’s capacity to act in line with their goals, directly shaping health behaviors ([52–53]) Yet, in Burkina Faso, women’s agency remains constrained by traditional norms, limited education, and weak legal protections [54]. Unequal distribution of resources, such as health information or financial assets, further restricts empowerment [46, 55, 56], constraining health-related autonomy in decisions and outcomes [47].

This study also incorporates ideas from “Cockerham’s health lifestyle theory” to support Kabeer’s empowerment framework [57]. Although Kabeer views women’s empowerment in terms of the interconnected aspects of accomplishments, agency, and resources, her paradigm mostly concentrates on the ways in which women’s access to and control over resources allow them to exercise agency and accomplish desired results. On the other hand, Cockerham’s (2005) theory places more emphasis on how social structure and agency interact dynamically to shape people’s habitual health behaviors. Cockerham contends that structural factors like class, gender, and social environment shape healthy lifestyles rather than only being the product of individual decisions.

In contrast, both theorists emphasize the importance of agency as a key component that connects personal potential to larger social systems. But whereas Kabeer’s model places agency in a framework that is focused on empowerment and development, emphasizing how women mobilize resources to attain well-being, Cockerham’s approach places agency in the sociology of health, showing how social hierarchies both enable and constrain it. Therefore, whereas Cockerham’s theory aids in explaining how structural disparities influence the conversion of empowerment into actual health behaviors, Kabeer’s framework offers a basis for comprehending how empowerment emerges and manifests. By incorporating different viewpoints, the study is able to investigate not only whether women have power but also how empowerment and structural context interact to affect their ability to make decisions about their health.

Localized evidence is still scarce despite worldwide study advancements. These dynamics have not received much attention in Burkina Faso, where women’s lives are still shaped by structural limitations and gender conventions. Thus, while controlling for important sociodemographic variables, this study investigates the relationship between women’s empowerment indicators, such as decision-making authority, ICT use, bank account ownership, and views of justified violence, and healthcare access.

Methods

This study utilized data from the 2021 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) of Burkina Faso. The dataset specifically focused on women of reproductive age. A separate SPSS dataset was used for women, focusing on a nationally representative sample of 12,868 women (15–49 years of age) who were either married or living with a partner. Women’s empowerment is particularly relevant among those who are married or cohabiting. Accordingly, this study focuses on women in marital or partner relationships to examine how different dimensions of empowerment are associated with barriers to the healthcare access.

The Complex Samples module in SPSS was used to correctly apply the survey weights and account for the DHS sampling design. The analysis incorporated the complex sampling structure, including weights (v005), clustering (v021), and stratification (v022), to ensure valid statistical inferences. Understanding the DHS sampling approach is critical for interpreting the results accurately. DHS employs a stratified, two-stage probability sampling method, with stratification based on geographic regions and urban-rural residence. In the first stage, primary sampling units (PSUs) are selected using probability proportional to size (PPS), followed by systematic sampling of households within each PSU. This design results in unequal selection probabilities, requiring the use of sampling weights, along with stratification and clustering variables, to produce unbiased and nationally representative estimates [58].

Dependent variable

A healthcare barrier refers to any factor that hinders individuals from utilizing health services or limits their access to appropriate, evidence-based care [59]. To measure women’s healthcare access, a composite ordinal variable was constructed by summing responses to four standard DHS questions: (1) needing permission to seek care, (2) lack of money, (3) distance to a health facility, and (4) reluctance to go alone [22]. Each item was coded as 1 (perceived as a problem) or 0 (not a problem), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 4. This scoring approach allows for a nuanced assessment of barrier severity. Higher scores indicate more reported barriers and, therefore, lower access to healthcare. This ordinal variable was used as the main outcome in the regression models.

Indepenedent variables

Two decision-making indicators were used: who decides on major household purchases and who manages the husband’s earnings. Each variable was recoded into three categories: 0 = “No power” (decisions made by husband/others), 1 = “Shared power” (joint decision-making), and 2 = “Sole power” (respondent alone). The two recoded variables were then summed to create a composite score ranging from 0 to 4, with each component contributing equally to the total score. Higher scores indicate greater decision-making autonomy.

Women’s economic empowerment and participation were measured by including a variable indicating bank account ownership, recorded in binary format as “yes” or “no.” Access to financial resources is a key component of empowerment.

A composite information and communication technology usage or ownership (ICTs) (range: 0–4) was created by combining four binary-coded items: frequency of watching TV, listening to radio, internet use, and mobile phone ownership. Responses were recoded so that any usage or ownership was scored as 1, and no usage/ownership as 0. The sum reflected overall ICTs.

Attitudes toward gender norms were also assessed, as they influence empowerment dynamics. Perceptions of justified violence sums five scenarios where wife-beating is perceived as justified: [1] going out without informing her husband [2], neglecting the children [3], arguing with her husband [59], refusing sex, and [4] burning food. Scores range from 0 (no justification) to 5 (high acceptance).

Socio-demographic characteristics

In addition to key independent variables, a range of socio-demographic factors were included in the ordinal logistic models to control for potential confounding. The selection of these characteristics was carefully guided by existing theory and prior research. These factors were selected based on prior literature. While no formal directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed, the selection was theory-informed and grounded in existing empirical studies to improve model specification and interpretability.

The study utilized the DHS wealth index, which ranks households into quintiles based on asset ownership and housing characteristics, with scores derived through principal component analysis. This divides the population into five wealth groups [60].

Other important demographic factors were similarly classified using standardized methods. Residence was classified as rural (0) or urban [1], and household headship was coded as 0 for female-headed and 1 for male-headed households, based on survey responses. Household size was classified using the Emergencies Monitoring Household Survey [61], which assigns scores based on the number of household members, ranging from 1 to 45, with categories from 4 for households with 1–7 members to 0 for those with 29–45 members. Age was categorized into five groups: 15–21, 22–28, 29–35, 36–42, and 43–49 years. Educational attainment for women and their husbands was grouped into four levels: no education, primary, secondary, and higher education. Women’s employment status was classified as currently working or not working. The “husband/partner’s occupation” variable was recoded into three categories: not working, blue-collar, and white-collar. Blue-collar included agricultural self-employment, manual labor, domestic work, and service/sales, while white-collar covered professional, technical, managerial, and clerical roles.

Drinking water sources were categorized as “Improved” or “Unimproved” based on DHS classifications, with improved sources including piped water, public taps, boreholes, protected wells, bottled, and sachet water. Unimproved sources included unprotected wells, surface water, rainwater, tanker trucks, and unspecified sources, with “Not a de jure resident” responses treated as missing [62]. Sanitation facilities were categorized similarly to ensure consistency in assessing living conditions. Toilet facilities were similarly classified as “Improved” or “Unimproved” based on sanitation hygiene, with improved facilities including flush toilets connected to a piped sewer or septic tank, pit latrines with slabs, VIP latrines, and composting toilets. Unimproved facilities included toilets flushing to unspecified locations, pit latrines without slabs, bucket toilets, hanging toilets, and no facilities (bush/field). “Other” responses were categorized as unimproved, and “Not a de jure resident” entries were treated as missing [63].

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS (Complex Sample) version 27. Specialized procedures were employed to account for the complex survey design. In this study, there were no missing data across the variables included in the analysis. All observations contained complete information for the selected indicators of women’s empowerment and the outcome variables. Descriptive analyses were also carried out to summarize the characteristics of the study sample. To examine associations between healthcare access barriers (the dependent variable) and women’s empowerment indicators, both unadjusted (separately) and adjusted (simultaneously) ordinal logistic regression models were employed. This method was selected because the outcome variable is categorical with more than two levels and the proportional odds assumption was not violated (refer Table 5). Results from these models were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For adjusted model, collinearity among independent variables was assessed. To verify the proportional odds (parallel lines) assumption required for ordinal logistic regression, the Test of Parallel Lines was conducted. This test examines whether the relationship between each independent variable and the dependent variable is consistent across all thresholds of the ordinal outcome. Model fit was evaluated using classification accuracy and pseudo-R-squared statistics. Finally, to assess the internal consistency of composite measures, Cronbach’s alpha reliability tests were performed for both the healthcare access barriers scale and the perceived justified violence scale.

Ethics and privacy

This study uses publicly available secondary data from the DHS Program, which follows strict ethical standards to protect the privacy and confidentiality of all participants. Survey protocols and questionnaires are reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of ICF (International Coaching Federation) and, when applicable, by national ethics committees in each participating country. The ICF IRB ensures compliance with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services regulations (45 CFR 46) for the protection of human subjects. For this secondary analysis, we obtained permission to use the DHS data through the formal data request process. Because the data are publicly accessible, no additional institutional ethical approval was required for this analysis.

Results

Table 1 presented women’s socio-demographic factors. Wealth was evenly spread across quintiles. Most women lived in rural areas (72.9%) and in male-headed households (91.9%), with over half in households of ≤ 7 members. Women aged 22–35 made up 51.6% of the sample. Education levels were low: 70.8% of women and 72.9% of husbands had no formal schooling, and only 1.6% of women had higher education. Most women (61.3%) were employed. Access to improved water (72.6%) was higher than access to improved sanitation (60.7%).

Table 2 summarizes women’s empowerment indicators in Burkina Faso using 2021 DHS data. Most women lacked autonomy in decisions regarding large purchases (78.5%) and their husband’s earnings (85.8%), with 71.5% having no power in either domain and only 1.7% reporting high autonomy (Fig. 1). Just over half (52.5%) rejected all justification for domestic violence. Financial empowerment was low, with only 7.7% owning a bank account. ICTs access varied, with 11.3% having none and 7.9% reporting high access, indicating moderate exposure overall.

The unadjusted ordinal logistic regression showed key findings on women’s access to health services (Table 4). Women with lower autonomy had higher odds of facing barriers (OR = 0.62; 95% CI: 0.54–0.71). Women in the richest quintile (OR = 0.12; 95% CI: 0.10–0.15) and urban residents (OR = 0.32; 95% CI: 0.28–0.37) faced fewer obstacles. Education had a strong effect, secondary (OR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.31–0.426) and higher (OR = 0.092; 95% CI: 0.05–0.15) levels lowered barriers. Employed women also had lower odds (OR = 0.75; 95% CI: 0.64–0.87).

Discussion

This study’s main goal was to investigate the relationship between Burkina Faso’s healthcare system and women’s empowerment aspects, as determined by decision-making authority, ICT access, bank account ownership, views of justified violence, and important sociodemographic characteristics. This method offers a thorough understanding of the obstacles affecting women’s use of healthcare.

In April 2016, Burkina Faso introduced free healthcare for pregnant women, new mothers, and children under five. However, 2022 data show that maternal and child mortality remain high, and healthcare services are still limited in many areas [16]. Although maternal mortality declined by 49% between 1990 and 2015, from 727 to 371 deaths per 100,000 live births, significant maternal and child health challenges persist [64]. Descriptive results from this study further reveal that only about 30% of women in Burkina Faso reported no barriers to accessing healthcare.

Despite reforms such as free maternal care and the rollout of universal health insurance (RAMU), healthcare access and utilization remain constrained by sociodemographic inequalities and systemic barriers [65]. These findings are particularly relevant to Burkina Faso’s ongoing gender and health reforms. They highlight the importance of integrating empowerment strategies into broader health policy. They underscore the need to complement national policies with targeted strategies that strengthen women’s autonomy, digital access, and protection from violence. Aligning gender policy with healthcare delivery is essential for achieving equity [15, 66].

This study found that women with greater decision autonomy were less likely to face impediments to healthcare service use, reinforcing the importance of autonomy and financial control in improving health outcomes. These findings align with a growing body of literature emphasizing women’s agency as a critical determinant of healthcare access. A study in SSA [67] showed that women’s independent decision authority and financial access enhance their ability to seek care. Similarly, a study in Burkina Faso [17] emphasized that in patriarchal communities, restrictive gender norms and limited autonomy continue to hinder access to healthcare, even in the presence of supportive policies. Such constraints underscore the complex interplay between societal norms and individual empowerment. Empowering women in healthcare decisions not only increases their access but also gives them a voice often denied in traditional settings [68]. However, decision-making involvement is influenced by the type of healthcare service, a woman’s age, her status within the household, and prevailing community norms. Consequently, full autonomy remains elusive for many women, despite policy efforts. Even with the elimination of direct payments, many women still lack full autonomy in accessing reproductive health services [69].

Perceptions of justified violence were associated with healthcare barriers, highlighting the role of social attitudes and norms in limiting access to care. This aligns with a qualitative synthesis [70] that identified stigma, fear of discrimination, and inadequate provider responses as major barriers. Chronic health consequences from domestic violence also reduce women’s ability to manage their health and access services [28].

Women’s financial inclusion, measured by bank account ownership, remained significantly associated with reduced healthcare barriers. Financial empowerment thus emerges as a vital pathway to overcoming access challenges. This reinforces evidence that socioeconomic empowerment through tools like micro-credited programs can improve access to healthcare services by enhancing financial independence and autonomy power [42]. A qualitative study in Burkina Faso found that while micro-credit can help women access healthcare by shifting household dynamics, challenges like increased burdens, loan delays, reduced spousal support, and economic shocks may limit its health benefits [71].

In this study, ICTs emerged as a key facilitator of healthcare access, highlighting its potential to transform healthcare delivery by delivering life-saving information and helping women prevent avoidable health problems [72]. This underscores the growing importance of digital tools in public health strategies. It aligns with earlier research demonstrating a correlation between access to health information and improved care quality [44]. Additionally, exposure to mass media, such as listening to the radio, reading newspapers, and watching television, was found to boost health literacy, which is widely recognized as a critical driver of healthcare utilization [73].

This study also found that urban residence was linked to fewer healthcare barriers compared to rural settings. Environmental and infrastructural differences play a significant role in shaping these disparities. Women in urban areas benefit from better infrastructure and access to services, supporting findings from both developed and developing contexts [74]. In contrast, women living in rural areas often face greater poverty, limited insurance coverage, and delayed care due to cost and access barriers [75].

Education was a strong predictor of women’s access to health services in this study, with both women’s and their husbands’ education levels significantly related to improved access. This finding reinforces the critical role of education in shaping health behaviors and service utilization. This aligns with prior research demonstrating that higher education tied to healthcare utilization and outcomes worldwide [76, 77]. Conversely, lower education levels are consistently associated with reduced access to healthcare and lower use of services [78]. For example, literate women are more likely than illiterate women to deliver in health facilities. Socioeconomic inequalities affecting healthcare access have also been documented in earlier studies from Burkina Faso [79].

Household headship also showed a significant association. The structure and dynamics of the household can influence healthcare priorities and resource allocation. Female-headed households were more likely to incur healthcare expenditures [80], possibly due to differing priorities or reduced household support. Broader gender norms may contribute to underinvestment in women’s health, as families tend to prioritize male health needs [81].

Employment was associated with fewer reported healthcare barriers, although previous work [82] suggests employment is a relatively weak determinant of maternal healthcare use. This indicates that while employment can influence access, its impact may be mediated by other factors.

The type of household water source was not significantly associated with women’s access to healthcare services in this study. Nevertheless, environmental conditions like water accessibility may have indirect effects on health-seeking behaviors. A study from 41 countries [83] found that improving water accessibility may indirectly support healthcare utilization, particularly for antenatal care.

Finally, the model showed a modest fit, with pseudo R² values of 0.046 (Cox and Snell) and 0.135 (Nagelkerke). These statistics indicate limited but acceptable explanatory power, which is common in logistic regression models in social and health research. The results suggest that, while the included predictors are relevant, other unmeasured factors may also influence the outcome.

Strengths and weaknesses

This study’s multifaceted methodology, which looks at the interactions between several facets of women’s empowerment and healthcare access, is one of its main strengths. This viewpoint offers a sophisticated comprehension of the ways in which empowerment could influence health-related behaviors. Contextual relevance is increased by placing findings within current literature, and depth is added by include traits of both women and their spouses.

However, because unobserved factors may influence both empowerment and healthcare habits, the cross-sectional approach restricts causal inference and raises issues with endogeneity and omitted variable bias. Therefore, it is important to interpret associations carefully. The accuracy of reported healthcare access and empowerment may be impacted by recollection or social desirability biases introduced by relying solely on self-reported data. Results may also be impacted by unmeasured variables such neighborhood influences, cultural norms, and healthcare quality, underscoring the challenge of fully capturing the picture with quantitative surveys alone. Understanding of intricate social and gender-specific circumstances is further hampered by the lack of qualitative data.

A key limitation of this study is that it does not include subgroup analyses for populations specifically targeted by Burkina Faso’s 2016 free healthcare policy, such as pregnant women and recently delivered mothers, which may mask important heterogeneity in associations. In addition, although grounded in Kabeer’s broader conceptualization of women’s empowerment, the analysis is constrained by DHS data and therefore captures only limited individual- and household-level dimensions, particularly related to healthcare decision-making. Broader civic, political, and legal aspects—such as land and property rights, legal literacy, freedom of movement, and political participation—are not included, and may be better captured by alternative sources such as Afrobarometer or the OECD Social Institutions and Gender Index (SIGI). Furthermore, ICT access was modeled as a composite indicator of overall information and communication technology exposure; however, the analysis does not disaggregate its components (e.g., television, radio, mobile phone, and internet), which may have distinct pathways and differential contributions to healthcare access. These limitations are particularly relevant in the context of Burkina Faso, where structural constraints on women’s agency remain substantial.

This work has clear policy implications for Burkina Faso and similar situations. By integrating women’s empowerment into gender and health policy through better decision-making, financial inclusion, and ICT use, access to healthcare can be enhanced. Education of healthcare professionals about gender inequality and community activities that address the norms that justify gender-based violence also boost women’s agency. Collectively, these policies promote public health, equitable healthcare, and gender equality.

Conclusion

This study provides both academic and policy value by applying a multi-dimensional framework of women’s empowerment to nationally representative data from Burkina Faso, an underrepresented context in the literature. It highlights the significant role of empowerment dimensions, particularly autonomy, financial inclusion, and access to information, in reducing barriers to healthcare. Socio-demographic factors such as education, urban residence, and household headship also emerged as important determinants. Methodologically, the study advances prior work by analyzing multiple empowerment dimensions simultaneously, offering a more comprehensive understanding than studies focusing on isolated aspects. Theoretically, it demonstrates how different facets of empowerment influence healthcare access in a specific cultural and socioeconomic setting. These findings underscore the need for context-specific interventions and provide actionable insights to guide policies aimed at improving women’s healthcare access in Burkina Faso and similar low-resource settings. To better capture the complexity of gender norms and structural impediments influencing women’s healthcare experiences, future research should use qualitative and longitudinal methodologies. Furthermore, analyzing how different aspects of women’s empowerment and healthcare access relate to one another in rural and urban areas would offer insightful information for developing policies and programs that are tailored to the local environment.
