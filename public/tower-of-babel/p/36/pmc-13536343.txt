Biomechanical effects of titanium alloy removable partial denture framework designs fabricated by selective laser melting in mandibular Kennedy class II partial edentulism: A finite element study
Authors: Min-Chieh Chang, Ping-Ching Wu, Wei-Shao Lin, Yung-Chung Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/purposeThe biomechanical effects of framework design and material interactions in mandibular free-end saddle removable partial dentures remain unclear. This study evaluated framework variables using finite element analysis.Materials and methodsA mandibular Kennedy Class II model was digitized to create a cobalt-chromium (CoCr) and sixteen Ti–6Al–4V (Ti64) frameworks varying by major connector dimension, clasp configuration, and lingual reciprocal assembly (LRA). Periodontal ligament (PDL) and mucosa layers were incorporated. Contacts were defined, and materials were assumed homogeneous and isotropic. Vertical and oblique loads were applied, and von Mises stresses were analyzed. Model validation was performed using digital image correlation.ResultsTi64 frameworks reduced overall stress by 21%–43% compared with CoCr. Under vertical loading, PDL stress decreased by 40% and framework stress by 32%–46%. Under oblique loading, direct retainer, PDL, and mucosal stresses increased by 3%, 6%, and 13%, respectively. Framework stress distribution was governed by clasp–LRA interaction, whereas increasing connector rigidity reduced stress magnitude and variability rather than peak stress. Supporting tissue responses were primarily determined by major connector dimension, with LRA and clasp effects being design- and loading-dependent.ConclusionTi64 frameworks improved load distribution with limited supporting tissue stress increases under oblique loading. Framework behavior was primarily governed by clasp–LRA interaction, while connector dimension determined supporting tissue response. Increasing major connector dimension attenuated LRA-related stress variations, particularly under oblique loading. Coordinated control of clasp configuration, LRA incorporation, and major connector dimension is required for biomechanical optimization.

Introduction

Compared with cobalt-chromium (CoCr) removable partial denture (RPD) frameworks, titanium frameworks offer lower density, improved biocompatibility, and a lower elastic modulus, which may reduce stress transfer to abutments.1,2 However, commercially pure titanium is difficult to cast and may exhibit inferior mechanical performance, including deformation and clasp fracture.1,3 These limitations have led the development of alternative titanium alloys and selective laser melting (SLM), which improve fabrication accuracy, enable complex geometries, and enhance mechanical and fatigue properties compared with cast counterparts.4–6 Despite these advancements, biomechanical optimization remains essential for achieving favorable stress distribution.

Free-end saddle (FES) RPDs exhibit differential displacement due to mismatched compliance between the periodontal ligament (PDL) and mucosa, leading to stress concentration at terminal abutments, distal extension mucosa, and framework transition zones, with peak stress occurring in the distal-apical PDL under occlusal loading.7 Stress distribution is influenced by FES length and mucosal compliance, with displacement increasing with saddle length.8 Stress concentrations are commonly observed at clasp shoulders, minor connectors adjacent to mesial rests, and major connector junctions, under oblique loading.8,9 While these stresses generally remain within the yield strength of CoCr alloys, limited evidence on how SLM-fabricated titanium frameworks alter these patterns.

The biomechanical behavior of RPDs is influenced by abutment conditions,10–12 mucosal resiliency,13,14 FES length,15 and ridge contour or inclination.13,14 Design is a critical determinant of long-term success,10,16 with rest configuration and positioning,8,10 rigidity of the major connector and retainer,17,18 clasp configuration19,20 and framework geometry,8,10,14,20–22 governing load transfer to supporting tissues.8,13,23 Material properties and loading conditions further affect biomechanical response.6,8,13,23,24

Direct retainer morphology, including arm length, cross-sectional shape, curvature, and taper, affects stress distribution within the framework, abutment, PDL and mucosa. 10,14 Structural transition zones, such as the clasp arm–body junction, are regions of stress concentration under functional loading.9,22 Arm length influences the balance between retention and flexibility, with shorter circumferential arms increasing retention but also stress.25,26 Rigid retainers reduce buccolingual displacement of abutments and vertical movement in FES RPDs.17,27 Optimized retainer design is essential to reduce stress concentration and torque on abutments, preserving periodontal and mucosal health.20,28

Major connector configuration also governs load distribution across the arch. Adequate rigidity promotes more uniform load transfer, reduces abutment distal displacement, 8,16,23 and limits flexure associated with mucosal compression and bone resorption.8,23 However, increased rigidity may elevate PDL and mucosal stress, reflecting a trade-off between abutment protection and denture base stability.13,22,23,27,29 In FES RPDs, greater rigidity reduces saddle deflection, although displacement remains dependent on saddle length.15 No clear interaction has been reported between major connector rigidity and direct retainer design.27 Evidence regarding mandibular connector design remains limited.8,30 Therefore, computational analysis is essential for optimizing these variables and achieving balanced stress distribution.

Given that the elastic modulus of Ti–6Al–4V (Ti64) is approximately half that of CoCr, it remains unclear whether increased framework flexibility compromises structural integrity or alters stress distribution within the PDL and mucosa. This study integrated digital image correlation (DIC) with finite element analysis (FEA) to evaluate the biomechanical behavior of SLM RPD frameworks with variations in material, major connector rigidity, lingual reciprocal assembly (LRA), and direct retainer configuration in mandibular Kennedy Class II conditions, and to determine whether increased structural rigidity can compensate for the lower elastic modulus of Ti64 without exacerbating stress on abutments and supporting mucosa. The null hypothesis is that these variables do not affect biomechanical performance of SLM RPD frameworks or supporting tissue response.

Materials and methods

A mandibular model missing the right second premolar to third molar was scanned (Vinyl; Smart Optics, Bochum, Germany), and root contours were refined to define the PDL (Fig. 1A). Occlusal rests were placed on the distal fossa of the left second premolar and the mesial fossae of the left first premolar, first molar, and right first premolar. 0.01-inch retentive undercuts and a 3-mm distal guiding plane on the right first premolar were prepared. The model was imported into CAD software (exocad DentalCAD; exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) for framework design.

Experimental design and group allocation

The standard framework featured an open-lattice minor connector, embrasure and Aker’s clasp, and a lingual bar with a half-pear cross-section measuring 4.2 × 2.0 mm. Sixteen experimental designs varied according to the presence of an LRA on the left first premolar, major connector dimensions including standard, thickened at 4.2 × 2.5 mm, widened at 5.0 × 2.0 mm, or 20%-enlarged at 5.0 × 2.5 mm, and direct retainer configurations using either an Aker’s clasp or a round cross-section (RCS) clasp. Denture bases and record rims were digitally constructed (Fig. 1).

Finite element model construction

Assemblies were imported into finite element software (HyperMesh 13.0; Altair Engineering Inc, Troy, MI, USA). Uniform 0.3-mm PDL and 2.0-mm mucosal layers were generated. Models were meshed with 4-node tetrahedral elements (C3D4), yielding approximately 5.6 × 105 elements and 1.0 × 105 nodes. Local mesh refinement was applied to high-stress regions, with a minimum element size of 0.05 mm in the PDL. A mesh convergence analysis was performed using mesh sizes of 0.12, 0.1, 0.08, and 0.05 mm. The finest mesh of 0.05 mm was used as the reference, and variations within 5% were considered acceptable. Stress values stabilized from 0.1 mm. Therefore, 0.05 mm was selected to ensure mesh-independent results. Surface-to-surface contact with finite sliding was defined at framework-tooth and denture base-mucosa interfaces. A penalty friction coefficient of 0.3 and hard contact were assigned. Initial interferences were resolved using the “adjust only to remove overclosure” option. Fixed contact was defined among the mucosa, bone, PDL, and teeth. All components were assumed homogeneous and isotropic with simplified elastic behavior (Table 1),23,31–36 consistent with previous RPD finite element studies.8,19,23

Quasi-static finite element analysis

Both condyles were fully constrained (ENCASTRE) to prevent rigid body motion. A 400 N vertical load, reflecting typical posterior bite forces,37 and a 40 N oblique load at 45° were applied at the second molar region (Fig. 2A). The oblique load, 10% of vertical load, simulated functional horizontal forces reported in previous studies.8,23 The framework, PDL, and distal extension mucosa were evaluated. As quasi-static FEA produced deterministic outputs without sample variability, results were compared descriptively.

Validation using in vitro mechanical test and digital image correlation

A reference model based on the finite element design was fabricated using a PolyJet 3D printer (J55 Prime; Stratasys

The assembly was vertically loaded to 200 N at the right second molar region using a universal testing machine (AG-1; Shimadzu Corp, Kyoto, Japan) at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min (Fig. 2B), for validation with a corresponding FEA model under the same loading condition. Stereoscopic images of the speckle-patterned base were captured using two synchronized CMOS cameras (Ximea; Ximea GmbH, Münster, Germany), and 3D displacement fields were analyzed with DIC software (Ncorr; Georgia Institute of Technology, Atlanta, GA, USA).38 Validation compared deformation and displacement trends between FEA and DIC. Following validation, simulations were conducted under the predefined conditions with 400 N vertical and 40 N oblique loading.

Results

Overall stress distribution and material comparison

Under vertical loading, stress concentrated at the direct retainers and adjacent minor-to-major connector junction (MMJ) near the FES, whereas oblique loading extended stress along the major connector (Fig. 3). Compared with CoCr, Ti64 reduced overall average stress by 21%–43% and decreased critical-region stress by 32%–46% under vertical loading. Mesh convergence confirmed mesh-independent results within 5% tolerance. Under oblique loading, major connector stress decreased by 17%, while direct retainer stress increased slightly by 3% compared with CoCr, indicating only a minor difference between the two materials. Ti64 reduced PDL stress at the direct abutment by 40% under vertical loading but increased it by 6% under oblique loading. In contrast, mucosal stress showed minimal change under vertical loading but increased by 13% under oblique loading (Table 2).

Stress distribution in framework-related regions

The effects of substituting Aker’s with RCS clasp, LRA incorporation as a structural reinforcement, and major connector rigidity were summarized (Tables 3 and 4). For the direct retainer on tooth 44 under vertical loading, stress was minimally affected by clasp type, generally reduced with LRA incorporation, and increased with greater connector rigidity. The increase was most pronounced when Aker’s clasp was combined with LRA incorporation and increased connector rigidity. Under oblique loading, both clasp substitution and LRA incorporation individually increased clasp stress, but their combined effect was attenuated. Increasing major connector rigidity generally reduced overall stress and decreased variability across designs but did not affect peak stress, which remained governed by the clasp–LRA interaction (Fig. 4). Clasp configuration and LRA incorporation determined the pattern of stress redistribution, whereas connector rigidity mainly modulated stress magnitude and variability rather than peak values.

For the MMJ near the FES and major connector, MMJ stress under vertical loading showed minimal variation, with only slight reductions as connector dimensions increased. Under oblique loading, major connector stress followed a similar pattern to clasp stress. Both clasp substitution and LRA incorporation individually increased stress, but their combined effect was attenuated. Increasing connector rigidity generally reduced stress, with more pronounced reductions in the 20%-enlarged designs (Fig. 5).

Distal extension mucosal strain and stress

Under vertical loading, mucosal strain and stress both remained stable, showing only minimal variation across designs (Fig. 6A, Table 5). Under oblique loading, mucosal responses became more sensitive to design variables. Without an LRA, clasp substitution slightly increased mucosal stress, whereas with an LRA it reduced stress by 23.55%–30.69% in the standard and thickened groups. LRA incorporation increased mucosal stress by approximately 42% in the standard and thickened Aker’s designs, but this effect was attenuated with increasing connector dimensions. Overall, the 20%-enlarged design showed the lowest mucosal strain and stress (Fig. 6B, Table 6). Major connector dimension was the primary determinant, whereas LRA effects were design-dependent.

Stress distribution in the periodontal ligament of tooth 44

Under vertical loading, PDL stress predominantly concentrated distally (Fig. 7A). Clasp substitution increased stress mainly in the widened and 20%-enlarged designs with an LRA. LRA effects were design-dependent, increasing stress primarily in the thickened Aker’s design and across all RCS designs. Increasing connector dimensions was associated with a 5%–16% increase in stress (Table 5). Under oblique loading, stress extended from distal to buccal and mesial aspects (Fig. 7B). Clasp substitution reduced stress, particularly in the standard and thickened designs with an LRA. LRA effects increased stress in the standard and thickened Aker’s designs but showed minimal effects in the other designs. Increasing connector dimensions reduced stress by approximately 15%–28%, with the greatest reduction in the 20%-enlarged design with an LRA (Table 6). The enlarged configuration showed the lowest PDL stress and attenuated LRA-related differences as connector dimensions increased.

Discussion

The null hypothesis was rejected, as framework rigidity and related design variables influenced biomechanical behavior and supporting tissue response. Unlike previous studies focusing on isolated parameters,19,20,22,23 framework stress was governed primarily by clasp-LRA interactions, with stress magnitude modulated by major connector rigidity. In contrast, supporting tissue stress was mainly governed by major connector dimension, with LRA and clasp effects being design- and loading-dependent. Increasing framework rigidity did not uniformly reduce stress but redistributed it in a loading-dependent manner between the framework and supporting tissues.

Consistent with previous studies, stress concentrated at the clasp shoulder and MMJ near the FES, with oblique loading extending stress along the major connector.9,19,22 Compared with CoCr, Ti64 reduced overall stress by 21%–43% and decreased stress at the direct retainer and MMJ by 32%–46% under vertical loading. Under oblique loading, major connector stress remained reduced, with only a 3% increase at the retainer. Despite its lower elastic modulus, Ti64 maintained adequate mechanical strength and favorable stress distribution compared with cast titanium frameworks. The maximum von Mises stresses remained below the reported elastic limits of cast and SLM titanium alloys (900 and 950 MPa, respectively),39 indicating an adequate mechanical safety margin.

Ti64 reduced PDL stress at the direct abutment by 40% with minimal change in mucosal stress under vertical loading, whereas oblique loading increased PDL and mucosal stress by 6% and 13%, respectively. These findings indicate load redistribution rather than uniform stress reduction. Increasing major connector dimensions reduced stress under oblique loading, highlighting its role in partially mitigating stress increases in supporting tissues. The oral mucosa is a nonlinear, vascularized, viscoelastic tissue in which excessive pressure may impair blood flow. In this study, peak mucosal stresses ranged from 0.15 to 0.55 MPa, exceeding capillary perfusion pressure but approaching reported pressure–pain thresholds of 0.102–0.405 MPa.40 However, clinical relevance should be interpreted with caution, as finite element–derived von Mises stress does not directly reflect physiological conditions and is influenced by modeling assumptions. Therefore, these results should be considered comparative indicators of load distribution rather than absolute predictors of biological damage. Clinically, appropriate material selection and framework design, along with minimizing excessive distal and oblique loading, may help reduce unfavorable stress on supporting tissues.

Compared with a previous study,27 the present results demonstrate interactions among clasp configuration, LRA incorporation, and major connector rigidity, particularly under oblique loading. Under oblique loading, stress redistribution followed a non-additive clasp–LRA interaction, with clasp substitution and LRA incorporation individually increasing stress, but their combined effect was attenuated. This interaction pattern was also observed at the major connector. Increasing connector dimensions generally reduced overall stress and variability, especially in the 20%-enlarged design, indicating a stabilizing effect on stress distribution within the framework.

Supporting tissue response differed from framework behavior, with major connector dimension as the primary determinant of stress magnitude, whereas LRA and clasp effects were design- and loading-dependent. For the distal extension mucosa, responses remained stable under vertical loading but became sensitive to design variables under oblique loading. LRA-related stress increases in standard and thickened Aker’s designs were mitigated by increasing connector dimensions, with the 20%-enlarged design showing the lowest response. For the PDL, stress increased with connector dimension under vertical loading, whereas under oblique loading it extended distally, buccally, and mesially. LRA-related increases were similarly attenuated with increasing connector dimensions, and the 20%-enlarged design showed the lowest stress and reduced LRA-related differences. Overall, increasing connector rigidity mitigated LRA-related stress variations and stabilized supporting tissue responses, particularly under oblique loading. These findings suggest the need for coordinated control of clasp configuration, LRA, and connector rigidity rather than isolated design modifications.

This study is limited by simplified geometry, quasi-static loading, idealized contact, and elastic assumptions that may not fully reflect clinical conditions. Although fatigue performance of SLM-fabricated Ti64 has been reported to be improved compared with cast alloys,5 it cannot be directly evaluated in this quasi-static analysis. Model outcomes are sensitive to assumptions of PDL thickness and the penalty friction coefficient and are further influenced by framework geometry and material properties under different loading conditions. Although mesh convergence was confirmed, the absence of sensitivity analysis limits generalizability. Nevertheless, Ti64 frameworks improved load distribution without marked increases in supporting tissue stress. Framework behavior was governed by clasp–LRA interaction, whereas connector dimension determined supporting tissue response. Increasing connector dimension alone did not provide consistent biomechanical benefit, indicating that effective design requires coordinated control of clasp configuration, LRA, and connector geometry. These interaction patterns provide a basis for rational framework design and future AI-assisted RPD optimization.
