An SPR-Based In Situ Methane Sensor for the Aqueous and Gas Phase
Authors: Arne Estelmann, Ralf Prien, Willem Marz, Mark Elbing, Philipp Harz, Gregor Rehder
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a fast, low-cost, precise methane sensor based on refractive index changes in a cryptophane A (CryptA)-doped polystyrene membrane. For the realization of this sensor, we built a surface plasmon resonance sensor with a refractive index resolution of 4.31 × 10–6 and investigated the optimal membrane thickness, i.e., a polymer layer of sufficient sensitivity with the lowest response time. For a membrane thickness of 760 nm, a limit of detection of 135 ppm and a response time constant of 45 s were found. Despite a comparable refractive index resolution and a higher CryptA content, the limit of detection is 3 orders of magnitude larger than that of a reported prototype. The sensor is capable of quantifying methane in the gas or aqueous phase. However, temperature, humidity, and ethanol vapor highly influence the signal. Although the principle of CryptA-based methane measurement is a promising, fast, and low-cost method, it will not be able to compete with state-of-the-art sensing in its current state.

Introduction

Methane (CH4) is the second most important greenhouse gas on earth. Its atmospheric concentration of 1.9 ppm (2021) has nearly tripled since preindustrial times, making it responsible for at least 16% of atmospheric warming to date.1−3 Methane emissions continue to rise. If sources are not reduced, international climate action goals may not be reached.4 Unfortunately, there is great uncertainty when quantifying the contribution of methane emission sources.1 About half of the global CH4 emissions originate from aquatic sources. However, due to a lack of observations, estimations vary from 41% to 53% depending on the method, making it challenging to quantify key emitters for effective mitigation.2

Simultaneously, methane is a flammable and abundant fossil fuel broadly used as an energy source or as a raw material for chemical processes. Due to the lower CO2 emissions and lower costs compared with coal combustion, methane as the principal component of natural gas is an indispensable part of the energy supply of many western countries with increasing importance.5 Unfortunately, the odorless and colorless gas can be explosive when mixed with air, thus possibly endangering people’s safety at work or in everyday life.

Consequently, there is an urgent need for in situ methane sensors for aqueous and gas phases for environmental purposes and safety reasons. While environmental sensing applications have higher demands regarding detection limit, accuracy, and precision, both fields require a fast response and high selectivity. These sensors should come at a low cost and be small in size, making it easy to implement methane quantification into technical devices or measurement campaigns, thus increasing the number of possible methane measurements.

In 2005, Benounis et al. proposed a novel methane sensing system that could potentially fulfill these demands.6 It was based on a polymer layer doped with cryptophane A (CryptA), a molecular cage with a cavity. Methane molecules can enter the cavity and build host–guest complexes based on London dispersion interactions with the cage molecule.7 By this, methane alters the density, the weight, and, most relevant for this study, the optical refractive index of the polymer. The synthesis of CryptA can be conducted within two steps from the inexpensive starting materials vanillyl alcohol and ethylene glycol (see the Methane-Sensitive Polymer Layers section).

In 2008, Boulart et al. presented a surface plasmon resonance (SPR)-based methane sensor using CryptA-doped polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) membranes.8 Their sensor could measure dissolved methane with a remarkable limit of detection (LOD) of only 0.1 ppm and a fast response time, especially for aqueous media, of only 40 s. Later, Boulart proved its operativeness in a field campaign on the Baltic Sea.9 The simple but effective design makes this prototype convenient for broad use in many applications, especially since the polymer-based measurement principle should work in both the gas and aqueous phase.

By now, the results by Boulart et al. have been neither used for further studies nor reproduced. Furthermore, neither the SPR setup nor the used PDMS type is available anymore. Surprisingly, in a previous study, we found currently available PDMS species to be unsuitable for methane sensing.10 Instead, polystyrene (PS) was found to be a possible matrix for CryptA-based methane sensing. Based on these findings, we planned to assess the applicability of CryptA-based methane sensing following Boulart’s method. Therefore, we built an SPR sensor of comparable resolution and investigated the optimal PS layer thickness. The performance of our polymer layers is tested in gas and aqueous phases, including the influence of humidity and other possible cross sensitivities. Finally, we discuss and compare the obtained results with those of published methane sensors based on refractive index measurements of CryptA-doped polymers.

Materials and Methods

Surface Plasmon Resonance

SPR is based on the excitation of surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs) by an incident light beam. SPPs are longitudinal oscillations of electrons at the interface of a highly conductive material, usually a metal and a dielectric. The electric field component along the surface normally decays exponentially within the metal and dielectric media. Consequently, the surface plasmon mode highly depends on the dielectric’s refractive index (RI) and, thus, on any chemical or physical changes on the metal surface.

To excite SPPs, the incident light wave vector component parallel to the surface must equal the wave vector of the surface plasmon mode. Two essential conditions follow from this: first, the electric field of the incident light beam must be within the incident plane, i.e., the light beam needs to be p-polarized. Second, due to the different dispersion relations of SPPs and electromagnetic fields in dielectric media, SPPs cannot be excited directly by a light beam. Instead, coupling methods are required to match the wave vectors along the surface.

The Kretschmann coupled surface plasmon resonance used in this publication was described first by Kretschmann in 1968.11 Since then, it has become the most common SPR setup for analytical purposes. The Kretschmann coupling uses another high RI medium on the other side of the metal for the incident light beam (see Figure 1). The light beam undergoes total internal reflection at the newly introduced dielectric/metal interface. If the metal is sufficiently thin, then the evanescent field of the light beam will reach the second metal/dielectric surface. There, the wave vector of the low RI surface plasmon mode and the evanescent field component along the surface may match and excite SPPs. For the specific wavelength or angle at which the wave vectors match, the light beam’s energy is transferred, and a reflection minimum occurs. In the case of a monochromatic light source, this angle is called the resonance angle θR.

Cross section of a Kretschmann coupled SPR setup consisting of two dielectric media and a metal in between. The incident light wave is p-polarized; hence, its electric field E is limited to the x- and z-dimensions, while its magnetic field H occurs along y. If the incident angle equals the resonance angle θR, then surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs) are excited on the far side of the metal. In the case of best coupling conditions, the reflected light intensity R declines to zero.

Multilayer Fresnel equations and finite-difference frequency-domain (FDFD) methods were used to simulate the behavior of the SPR setup (Supporting Information (SI), S-1).

SPR Prototype

We built an angular-based SPR Kretschmann sensor for laboratory use (Figure 2). Such devices comprise a light source, a prism, a gold surface for plasmon coupling, and a detector to trace the resonance angle. In this work, the gold surface was not applied directly to the prism but instead to thin glass slides that were put on the prism. By doing so, different coatings could be examined using the same setup and prism. The collimated light beam of an LED (SFH 4544, 950 nm) was led through a bandpass (FWHM of 10 nm) and a polarization filter. The resulting p-polarized light converged within a semicylindrical prism (Schott SF10, Fleige Optik). A monochrome global shutter camera sensor (Omnivision OV9281, 1280 × 800 pixels) detected the reflected light intensity. The light source and the camera position could be repositioned to place the SPR resonance angle in the center of the camera detector.

SPR setup used in this study. The setup is placed in a closed box to ensure complete darkness (see SI, S-5). A Matlab interface permits live tracking of the SPR signal, temperature, and camera metadata.

Thin, one-sided gold-coated SF10 glass plates (PHASIS BioNano, 5 nm Ti, 48 nm Au) were placed on the prism so that the gold surface faced the sample. A refractive index matching fluid (Cargille Series B-1.7000) prevented refraction and reflection at the glass/glass interface. The prism and glass slide were pressed against the O-ring sealing of a microfluidic flow cell (see SI, Figure S3). The flow cell comprised a volume of less than 1 mL and was equipped with a three-wire PT100 temperature sensor.

A Raspberry Pi 4 collected measurement raw data. Each data point consisted of 10 averaged images and temperature values. The averaged pixel intensity matrices were summed along one dimension to obtain an angular light intensity distribution vector of 1280 values. Resulting data were sent to a Matlab interface that calculated the minimum position and enabled real-time monitoring. The light intensity minimum position Pmin was determined by an asymmetric fit function.12 The resulting measurement frequency was 1.1 Hz.

The refractive index resolution of the setup was quantified by measuring the resonance angle of Milli-Q ultrapure water (specific resistance of ≥18 MΩ cm) and solutions of 5, 10, 15, and 20 g kg–1 NaCl (PanReac ITW Reagents) in Milli-Q water at temperatures varying between 18 and 26 °C. Refractive indices for each data point were calculated from known salinity and measured temperature.13

Methane-Sensitive Polymer Layers

Cryptophane A was synthesized in the laboratory from vanillyl alcohol in a two-step synthesis according to a modified literature procedure (see Figure 3).14 First, ethylene glycol (Roth) was tosylated by means of tosyl chloride (J&K). Then, vanillyl alcohol (Aldrich) was reacted with ethylene glycol ditosylate to form the corresponding diol. This diol was treated with formic acid (Chemsolute) in high dilution to deliver raw cryptophane A. Purification was achieved by column chromatography (silica gel; dichloromethane/ethyl acetate) and subsequent washing of the solid with diethyl ether. Cryptophane A was obtained as a colorless solid in 5% yield. Analytical purity was confirmed by NMR spectroscopy (SI, S-2).

Synthesis of CryptA from ethylene glycol and vanillyl alcohol. a: TsCl, Et3N, MTBE (55 °C, 16 h, 81%); b: K2CO3, acetone (50 °C, 16 h, 70%); c: HCO2H (55 °C, 3 h, 5%).

Then, CryptA was embedded in a polymer matrix. Polystyrene (PS) pellets (BASF Polystyrene 143E) were dissolved in tetrachloromethane (Sigma-Aldrich) to obtain polymer solutions of 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 wt % (see Figure 4, left graph, and SI, S-3). Eventually, the solution was mixed with the maximum amount of CryptA to create a uniform, homogeneous polymer (mCryptA = 0.45mPS). These solutions were spin-coated onto the gold-coated SPR glass slides to give evenly distributed polymer layers of different thicknesses. The remaining solvent was removed at 80 °C for 2 h.

PS/CryptA solutions used to coat the glass slides were of different polymer concentrations, which gave different PS layer thicknesses. The mass concentration of CryptA was 0.45βPS for all slides.

The glass slides appeared to be evenly coated. The PS layers were homogeneous and free of any precipitant. PS layer thicknesses were measured with a Bruker DektakXT stylus profiler at the edge of each slide (Figure 4, right graph). With increasing PS/CryptA content, the solutions used for spin coating gave larger polymer layers, varying from 100 to 1400 nm. Surprisingly, the layer of slide 4 (βPS = 2.5 wt %) was found to be the thickest, although it was not made using the solution with the highest concentration.

A protective PDMS layer was coated on top of the PS layers to avoid any influence from the sample matrix on the SPR signal. The influence of these PDMS layers on the response time can be neglected (SI, S-4). Therefore, the polymer surfaces underwent plasma treatment (ambient air plasma, 0.4 mbar, 30 W, 2 min), were functionalized with (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES, VWR, 1 vol % in Milli-Q water, 45 min), and were spin-coated with PDMS DOW Sylgard 184. The adhesion of PDMS on PS due to the APTES layer was of remarkable strength. Although the slides were used over a period of more than six months and were partly exposed to water for multiple weeks, no layer started to detach or peel off.

Methane Signal Quantification

Before any measurement, the polymer-coated glass slide of interest was placed on the prism. Then, the camera angle was slightly adjusted (≤4°) to position Pmin at its center. N2 (5.0) and 1% CH4 in N2 (Linde) were dosed by mass flow controllers (MFCs, Aera FC-7700CD for 10, 50, and 100 sccm). To achieve a broad range of different CH4 concentrations at a constant gas flow of 40 sccm, two different MFCs could be used for the methane gas flow. MFCs were controlled by digital potentiometers and manually switched by T valves (see SI, S-5). Before any measurement, the setup was flushed with N2 for 1 h.

In the first set of experiments, the gas phase response of the slides was tested. Step-wise decreasing CH4 concentrations were fed directly into the microfluidic SPR cell for 750–1000 s, depending on the response delay of the slide (Figure 5). In between, the system was flushed with N2 for 750–1300 s. During the measurement, temperature fluctuations were dampened by a water circuit of a constant temperature. At the end of each measurement, the temperature variation was recorded to quantify a possible temperature effect. Surprisingly, a slight signal overshoot was observed for the first signal after the CH4 MFC was changed (Figure 5). Thus, the first signal after the MFC change was disregarded.

Processing of the raw Pmin data, here shown for slide 3 (gas phase). CH4 and N2 alternated (red/green areas). Lighter areas were not used for signal processing due to response delay. The CH4 concentration is shown in the upper plot. At 8500 s (dotted line), the CH4 MFC was changed, leading to a slight signal overshoot. A temperature variation at the end of the measurement was used for an T-correction of Pmin (second plot). The remaining signal drift was removed by interpolating the N2 signal (red line). CH4 signals were calculated from the T- and baseline-corrected minimum positions (final plot, white bars).

The aqueous phase methane signal was assessed by a similar procedure in a second set of measurements. For this purpose, the gas stream was fed into an equilibrator consisting of a Milli-Q water-filled four-valve glass bottle. Instead of a cooling circuit, the water circuit itself was placed in a thermostat to avoid strong temperature drifts. To shorten the equilibration time, the gas stream was pushed through a glass frit while the Milli-Q reservoir was heavily stirred. A peristaltic pump (Boxer 9QQ) then pumped the Milli-Q solution through the SPR system. As the gas/water equilibration time was elevated, the CH4 concentration was changed every 2500 s only. By consequence, only concentrations of ≥1000 ppm were investigated.

In a third set of experiments, the repeatability and cross sensitivities of water and ethanol vapor were assessed in the gas phase. For these experiments, glass slide 3 was chosen. For this purpose, a three-valve glass bottle completed the gas phase measurement setup as an injection chamber and by an air humidity/VOC sensor (BME 688). Water and ethanol were purged with N2 and inserted into the chamber using a syringe.

Raw Data Processing

The raw minimum position data Pmin were temperature-corrected using a linear regression for each measurement. However, the signal still drifted significantly at the beginning of each measurement. More significant drifts were observed for thin layers and gas phase measurements (Figure 5). Thus, for each measurement a baseline was calculated. Stable N2 signals, i.e., the N2 signal obtained 500 s (gas phase) or 2000 s (aqueous phase) after a gas composition change, were interpolated using the Matlab Modified Akima algorithm.15 CH4 signals and response time constants were then calculated from the corrected Pmin.

Results

SPR Prototype Resolution

The sensitivity S of an SPR sensor is defined as the ratio of the sensor’s signal change, i.e., the minimum position change ΔPmin, to the change in refractive index Δn (eq 1).161The noise σ60 is defined as the standard deviation of Pmin subtracted by a running mean over 60 s. Assuming a required signal-to-noise ratio of 3, the limit of detection (LOD) can be calculated (eq 2).2Because the relation of the resonance angle and the RI of the measurement medium behave linearly for small RI changes such as saltwater solutions (nsolutions ∈ [1.332, 1.336]), a linear regression was used to determine S.13 A refractive index sensitivity of 3.34 × 104 pixels with σ60 = 4.8 × 10–2 pixels was reached. Conclusively, the SPR sensor has a detection limit of 4.31 × 10–6 for aqueous solutions.

The RI of PS is about 0.2 times greater than that of water.13,17 At higher RI, the theoretical sensitivity of the SPR increases. However, the full width at half minimum (FWHM) increases as well, which may confuse the determination of Pmin. For this reason, the resolution of SPR resonance is often compared by a figure of merit (FOM, eq 3).163

For a conservative estimate of the RI sensitivity at n(PS/CryptA), a potential loss of RI resolution should be considered. The FOM of different refractive indices can be simulated using multilayer Fresnel equations (see Figure 6, left graph, and SI, S-1). Unfortunately, the RI of the CryptA-doped PS layers was not known and could not be measured. Hence, the refractive index of pure PS (nPS = 1.57) was chosen, which is close to the RI at the minimum FOM (Figure 6, right graph).17

Left: normalized theoretical light intensity distribution of the setup for the measurement of Milli-Q water (blue) and PS (orange), including the respective resonance angles θR (dotted lines). Right: dependence of the figure of merit (FOM) of the refractive index. The FOM for PS is about 74% of the FOM of Milli-Q.

The light source and camera need to be repositioned to realize the larger resonance angle for PS. By that, the minimum width takes up approximately the same number of pixels, making the FOM a suited method to estimate the resolution at different angles. The theoretical FOM is calculated by a multilayer Fresnel equation respecting the 10 nm bandwidth of the light source and the wavelength-dependent sensitivity of the camera (Figure 6).18 The theoretical FOM of PS is about 74% of the FOM of Milli-Q. By consequence, we roughly estimate a sensitivity of SRI = 2.48 × 104 pixels and a refractive index LOD of 5.86 × 10–6 for the CryptA-doped PS layers.

Characterization of Cryptophane-A-Doped PS Layers

Methane Sensitivity

The methane sensitivity of the investigated polymer layers can be derived from eq 1 by replacing Δn with the change in CH4 concentration Δ[CH4]. For all slides, a linear correlation of signal ΔPmin and Δ[CH4] was seen (Figure 7). To compare the aqueous and gas phases, each sensitivity was calculated for the concentration range from 1000 to 7500 ppm by linear regression. Generally, almost all regressions were of excellent quality (R2 ≥ 0.99), had low noise, and had a gas phase sensitivity of about 3.5 × 10–4 pixels ppm–1 (Figure 8, upper left and central plots). The sensitivity of the aqueous phase measurements was significantly lower than that of the gas phase measurements (70% of S(g) for slides 3–5). For slide 1, the sensitivity was about two-thirds lower than that of the other slides. Besides, the validity of its aqueous phase regression is questionable (R2 ≈ 0.5).

CH4 signals and concentration for each polymer coating in the gas (darker colors) and aqueous phase (lighter colors). For each slide, a linear regression in gas and aqueous phases was calculated (dotted lines).

Performance of the slides with different PS layer thicknesses. Smaller figures show the sensitivity (upper left), noise (upper center), response and recovery constants (lower left), and temperature cross sensitivity (lower center) of the slides. On the right, the RRMSE is plotted against the LOD. Aqueous phase measurements (shown in lighter colors, “+”) were in general less sensitive, showed a stronger temperature dependency, and gave a larger RRMSE than gas phase measurements (darker colors, “x”). Due to the equilibration delay of the aqueous phase, response and recovery times were determined for the gas phase only (smaller value: response time constant; larger value: recovery time constant).

The detection limit for each slide in both phases can be derived from eq 2. Unfortunately, this does not include the quality of the linear regression performed to derive the sensitivity. On the other hand, standard statistic measures such as the summed square error or the root-mean-square error (RMSE) are biased by the difference in the signal values. In such situations, the relative root-mean-square error (RRMSE), defined as the RMSE divided by the mean expected value, is preferable.

LOD and RRMSE permit a sensitivity assessment among the slides (Figure 8, right graph). All slides performed better in the gas phase than in the aqueous phase. While the difference between slides 2–5 is small, slide 1 shows a very high LOD and a larger RRMSE, especially in the aqueous phase. Apart from slide 5’s gas phase measurement, the LOD improved with the PS/CryptA layer thickness.

A further important measure regarding the sensitivity quantification is the linear range. Because we did not measure low concentrations in the aqueous phase (<1000 ppm), the linear range can only be assessed for the gas phase. In the gas phase, all slides showed a linear behavior down to 350 ppm, which is remarkable, especially for slide 1, considering its LOD. Slide 5 gave a linear behavior down to its LOD of about 150 ppm.

Estimation of the Polymer RI Change

The CH4 sensitivity of the RI of the polymer layer can only roughly be estimated from the approximated RI sensitivity SRI. To complicate matters further, the penetration depth of the surface plasmon into the polymer is not reached for all of the slides. The evanescent plasmon field thus may reach beyond the actual polymer layer into PDMS.

The evanescent electric field intensity along z has been calculated using an FDFD approach (Figure 9, left plot, and SI, S-1). Again, the refractive index of the doped PS membrane was assumed to be 1.57. If the RI of PS is increased by doping, the graphs shown on the right in Figure 9 are slightly compressed along the abscissa, while the discontinuities keep their position. The impact of the CryptA/PS layer on a change in θR can be estimated by the ratio of the integral of |Ez|2 of the PS layer and the integral of |Ez|2 within both dielectrics (Figure 9, right graph). From these calculations, it can be determined that from a wavelength of approximately 500 nm, which is equivalent to λ/2, the layer thickness no longer affects the signal height. In practice, slide 4 exhibits a slightly higher signal height than slides 3 and 5. Otherwise, the observed sensitivities align with the calculated theoretical behavior.

Left: impact of different PS layer thicknesses on the evanescent field intensity. Slides 4 and 5 gave virtually similar functions of |Ez|2. The layer thickness has a direct influence on the sensitivity, as for layers thinner than λ/2, a significant part of the evanescent field intensity is susceptible to changes in the PDMS RI. Right: impact of layer thickness on the electric field intensity within the doped PS layer compared to measured sensitivity and thickness.

With respect to the FOM, the refractive index sensitivity of the setup for the RI of PS was estimated to be about 2.48 × 104 pixels (see the SPR Prototype Resolution section). The mean methane sensitivity of slides 2, 3, and 5 is 3.53 × 10–4 pixels ppm–1. As a consequence, the RI sensitivity of the doped PS polymer can be estimated to be about 1.42 × 10–8 ppm–1.

Response Times

The sensor response can be quantified by a response time constant τresp and a recovery constant τrec (eq 4). The 90% response/recovery time corresponds to about 2.3 times the response time constant.4Time constants were calculated for the gas phase only, as response times in the aqueous phase were heavily biased due to the Milli-Q/gas equilibration (Figure 8, lower left plot). Additionally, response times were altered by the CH4 concentration setting because the MFCs were controlled manually by potentiometers. Thus, we calculated the constants for the concentration that was set the fastest (2000 ppm, about 20 s for CH4 and 10 s for N2).

For all slides, the response times were smaller than the recovery times. On average, the recovery was 37% slower than the response. The relative difference was most prominent for slide 1, which might be impacted by the slow concentration setting at such a fast response. In general, the recovery and response times increased with layer thickness. Surprisingly, the longest response time was not observed for slide 4 but for slide 5.

Temperature Cross Sensitivity

All experiments’ temperatures ranged from 19 to 24 °C, with a maximum temperature variation of 2.5 °C per experiment. The SPR signal of all polymer layers was heavily influenced by temperature changes (Figure 8, central lower plot). A change in temperature of 1 °C corresponded to a methane concentration change of more than 1% for most slides.

In the gas phase, temperature effects did not follow a clear pattern, whereas the temperature drift in the aqueous phase decreased with increasing thickness. Again, like for the response time constants, slide 4 perfectly fits between slides 3 and 5, although its thickness was determined to be larger.

Repeatability and Selectivity

In a final experiment, the impact of relative humidity and ethanol vapor, as an example of the chemical selectivity of the sensor, was assessed. Slide 3 was used for this purpose. It is the slide with the thinnest PS layer for which the evanescent field does not reach the PDMS layer (Figure 9). Also, it gave a low RRMSE and LOD in both the aqueous and gas phases, while its response and recovery time constants were below 60 s. Three gas phase methane concentrations were measured in a dry gas stream, at almost 100% air humidity, in a dry gas stream again, and in a gas stream passed over an ethanol reservoir (Figure 10).

Repeatability and Reproducibility

Both measurements in the dry gas stream gave a similar sensitivity and LOD, indicating a reproducible method despite the disturbance by high relative humidity in between (Figure 11, second and third graphs). However, although their LOD agreed with the LOD of the first gas phase measurement, their sensitivity was 45% larger. Likewise, the sensitivity in humid air showed an LOD comparable to that of the aqueous phase. However, its sensitivity slope was 36% steeper.

Impact of humidity on the sensor signal and comparison with the former results. The relative humidity highly impacts the temperature-corrected sensor signal (top left). If the sensor signal is also corrected for baseline drift and humidity, its sensitivity can be determined (bottom left). Although the sensitivity seems higher than in previous experiments, the noise also increased, leading to a comparable detection limit (bottom right).

During the implementation of the BME 688 sensor for VOC content and relative humidity, the setup was entirely disassembled and eventually reassembled. The setup had not been disassembled to such an extent in previous experiments. Consequently, the light source and camera position might have significantly differed from prior experiments in position, tilt, and distance from the prism. Such possible changes in the geometry affect the sensitivity. For example, a larger distance between the camera and the prism results in a higher resolution due to the diverging light beam. However, it has been shown that for a classic SPR setup, the noise increases with the camera distance.16 Increasing noise and sensitivity compensate each other, resulting in a reproducible LOD.

Relative Humidity

Despite the PDMS cover of the sensitive layer, the relative humidity leads to a higher resonance angle and thus a shift of minimum position. The humidity signal is about an order of magnitude larger than the highest methane concentration measured (7500 ppm). Because the humidity sensor was located in the exhaust of the gas flow, the signal shift plotted against the humidity percentage gave a hysteresis loop (Figure 11, first graph).

At almost 100% relative humidity, we still observed methane sensitivity. However, the sensitivity was lower than the dry gas phase’s, while the LOD increased. Concerning the reproducibility (Repeatability and Reproducibility section), it can be stated that the saturated gas phase humidity and aqueous phase measurements behaved similarly. As mentioned before, the humidity perturbation was completely reversible.

Ethanol

Introducing ethanol vapor in the gas phase severely affected the minimum position (Figure 10). The resonance angle highly increased. The minimum position shifted by about 2 orders of magnitude compared to the maximum methane signal shift observed. While the minimum position was dominated by ethanol vapor, a methane signal was observed neither in the raw data nor in the data corrected for temperature, humidity, baseline drift, or all these parameters together.

Discussion

Kretschmann SPR Setup

The built SPR sensor is a further developed version of a system described in a previous publication.10 The most distinct changes in the setup are the camera, the Raspberry Pi, and the bandpass filter. These changes, combined with improved raw data treatment, permit superior RI resolution while collecting data at twice the measurement frequency (4.31 × 10–6 at 1.1 Hz instead of 2.4 × 10–5 at 0.4 Hz). Increased computational power, smaller pixel sizes, and temporal and spatial averaging are primarily responsible for such an enhanced performance, which comes at the cost of a higher power demand. With less than €500 overall costs, the new version is at least as low-priced as the previous setup.

An important reason for the improved resolution is the low noise σ(Pmin) below 0.05 pixels, mostly less than 0.02 pixels. The unit pixel originates from the used camera and is more reliable than RI noise, as it is independent of the sample’s refractive index. The noise level reduction is a consequence of averaging. The white noise level of SPR light intensity distributions is inversely proportional to the exposure time or, in case of a fixed exposure time, to the number of images averaged.16,19 The previous work used a single image of a line camera. A single-pixel array of the 2D camera used in this work is, on its own, of inferior quality. Nevertheless, the noise level is reduced notably by averaging the image signal of 10 images that include 800 pixel arrays. For saltwater experiments, a single pixel corresponded to only 1.8 × 10–3°. For these measurements, the angular resolution of the setup is consequentially about 2.6 × 10–4 ° pixel–1 (S/N = 3).

In general, classic Kretschmann SPR sensors reach an LOD of 10–5 to 10–7. With respect to the broad RI range and its low-cost design, the resolution of this work’s setup may not be extraordinary but is more than satisfactory for CryptA measurements and lies within the range of other reported SPR prototypes.20

Methane Sensitivity

With CryptA-doped PS layers, methane concentrations can be measured successfully in the gas and aqueous phases. Slides with different polymer layer thicknesses were tested and compared. The results of the slides are comparable, as they were obtained under similar conditions and with only slightly adjusted incident angles (±2°). The measurements were found to be repeatable and, for specific parameters, reproducible. It was shown that relative parameters such as RRMSE or the resolution are suited for comparison (see the Repeatability and Reproducibility section).

For layers with a thickness of about 0.5λ or more, LOD and RRMSE were of similar size. This agrees with the character of the evanescent fields calculated in the Estimation of the Polymer RI Change section. As expected, response times and recovery times increased with layer thickness, while the sensitivity did not increase significantly for slides 2, 3, and 5. Thus, when it comes to the best performance of the CryptA/PS layers, the thinnest slide with a thickness larger than 0.5λ should perform the best. In this work, this applies to slide 3, although the difference in the LOD and RRMSE of slides 2 and 3 is small.

Slide 4 marks an exception, as it showed the thickest PS/CryptA layer despite not containing the highest CryptA concentration. Still, its response time fit the observed dependence of response time constants on CryptA content. In a previous work, we concluded that the CryptA complexation may be the response time governing step, which may explain the lower τ of slide 4.10 However, the better sensitivity of slide 4 cannot be explained. Thus, slide 4 should rather be treated as an outlier.

In the last 20 years, several methane sensor prototypes based on an RI change of CryptA-doped polymer layers have been published (see Table 1). Apart from Boulart et al. in 2008 and 2013, Jágerská et al. in 2017 (CH4 in water), and Wang et al. in 2024 (CH4 in transformer oil), sensors were developed for atmospheric measurements.8,9,31,32 All these sensors rely on different polymer types that mainly comprise a large amount of delocalized π-electrons, in which a large amount of CryptA can be dissolved.10 Polycarbonate (PC), PS, and styrene-acrylonitrile (SAN, a PS copolymer) are the most common. Furthermore, it has been shown that the methane-induced change in RI increases for higher CryptA concentrations. Unfortunately, higher CryptA content usually comes at the cost of a higher response time.10,31

Although this work has, to our knowledge, the second-highest known CryptA concentration and a thick polymer layer when compared to cited sensors, its response time is within the range of reported prototypes. However, our sensor did not show the highest resolution. The by far highest resolution was reported for the SPR prototype by Boulart et al. in 2008, albeit its polymer layer had the lowest CryptA content.8 Boulart used the Spreeta SPR chip, a low-cost SPR chip developed by Texas Instruments in the early 2000s, which is not available anymore. However, the Spreeta is of similar RI resolution (5 × 10–6) as the device presented in this work. Surprisingly, its operational range of n ∈ {1.32, 1.37} is not suited for the RI of PDMS (n = 1.412). Boulart’s prototype remains the only device ever tested in the field.9 There, its performance was not as high as in the laboratory (LOD ≈ 2.2 ppm assuming 20 °C and a salinity of 20 g kg–1) but still remarkable.

While Boulart observed methane sensitivities between 3.2 and 9.4 nmol kg–1 for the refractive index of his PDMS coatings in the laboratory (corresponding to 3.7 × 10–6 to 1.1 × 10–5 ppm–1 for 35 g kg–1 practical salinity, 20 °C), his field experiments gave a methane sensitivity of 7 × 10–6 nmol kg–1 (corresponding to 1.4 × 10–5 ppm–1 for 10 g kg–1 practical salinity, 5 °C).8,9 A further polymer sensitivity estimate was stated by Yang et al., who gave a value of approximately −3.8 × 10–7 ppm–1 for UVCFS.28 Yang’s polymer layer had a slightly higher CryptA content than this work. Interestingly, they observed a decrease in the RI, which contradicts the findings from Boulart as well as our previous and current work. Nevertheless, Yang did not investigate whether their PCF was sensitive to gas phase changes when no CryptA was used. The polymer layer by Wang et al. in 2024, which comes closest to the LOD of Boulart, gave a methane sensitivity of 1.9 × 10–7 ppm–1.32 Dullo et al. observed a methane sensitivity of 3.2 × 10–9 ppm–1 for their SAN/CryptA polymer blend.27,34 Based on the RI resolution estimate (SPR Prototype Resolution section), the PS/CryptA polymer in this work may reach a sensitivity of 1.4 × 10–8 ppm–1. These sensitivities are all multiple orders of magnitude smaller than those observed by Boulart. By consequence, the difference between Boulart’s and other works such as this lay mainly in the methane sensitivity of the polymer layer, not in the refractive index resolution of the applied method.

We cannot explain and only speculate about why our methane sensitivity is below that of Boulart to such an extent. Our CryptA content is 20 times higher, and both SPR devices have similar RI resolution. Both polymers showed no methane sensitivity without CryptA.8,10 At least for laboratory experiments, the influence of possible cross sensitivities can be excluded. Consequently, we can only speculate whether some CryptA cages were unavailable for host–guest interaction with methane. Either strong interactions such as π-stacking of PS chains and CryptA, may have blocked the entrance and, in this way, kinetically hindered some CryptA cages or some of the molecular cages collapsed; another possibility might be an irreversible interaction of some CryptA molecules with an unknown host, leading to a lower “active” CryptA content. However, it must be noted that other prototypes achieve neither the methane sensitivity nor the LOD of Boulart despite having higher RI resolution and CryptA content.

Temperature Cross Sensitivity

The refractive index of a dielectric is highly dependent on the temperature. This also applies to the CryptA-doped polymer layers, the gold layer, and the glass itself. However, we were not able to measure the temperature of these very thin slides. Instead, the temperature was measured in the microfluidic cell. The temperatures of the water samples and the polymer layers can be assumed to be approximately similar due to the high specific heat capacity of water. For gas phase samples, the measured flow cell temperature and actual temperature of the polymer layer may differ.

In the aqueous phase, the temperature dependence of slides 3–5 corresponded roughly to a change in RI of 1.61 × 10–4 °C–1. From this, an increase by 1 °C triggered the same signal as a decrease in methane concentration by 1100 ppm, almost 9-fold of the gas phase LOD. Even Boulart et al. stated that “temperature influences dramatically the RI measurement,” although their setup was about 1000 times less prone to temperature changes.8 Furthermore, Boulart points out that the temperature affects the chemical equilibrium and thus the signal height, which we have not considered yet.

Inevitably, if not used in highly stable thermal conditions, the temperature variation impacts this setup’s SPR signal more than the actual methane concentration. Consequently, carefully conducted temperature compensation is required. Exact temperature measurements as close to the SPR active surface as possible are needed for future work. Furthermore, the temperature dependency was estimated only on a slight temperature variation. A more detailed temperature characterization over a larger temperature interval would be indispensable if a slide was used for actual methane measurements outside the laboratory.

Selectivity

The methane sensitivity is based on a host–guest interaction between CryptA molecules and methane. The interaction depends on the size of the guest molecule and the CryptA cavity, the cavity entrance size, the symmetry of the guest molecule, and the capability of the guest to undergo van der Waals interactions.7,35 For cryptophane A, CH4 is among the best-suited guest molecules, but due to the host’s nature, cross sensitivities by xenon and halogen-substituted methane derivatives are expected.7 Moreover, cavities are constantly occupied and abandoned by other small molecules such as N2, O2, CO2, or H2O that can enter the cavity. A weak interaction of CryptA and H2O has been reported.36 These might not be the best-suited guest molecules and thus do not build as stable of complexes as methane. However, a concentration change of these may alter the SPR signal despite a constant methane concentration. In 2011, Yang et al. assessed the influence of N2, O2, CO, CO2, and H2 on their sensor signal.24 They found it to be of minor extent or, for O2 and N2, even negligible.

Nevertheless, Yang also reported that the impact of relative humidity was relatively small compared to the detected methane signal. This was not the case for our observations. An increase in the relative humidity increased the RI of our polymer layer and affected the methane signal height. At 100% relative humidity, the methane signal was dampened to approximately 75% of its initial size. The reduction in the signal at 100% relative humidity corresponded precisely to the reduction observed in the aqueous phase. Consequently, the methane signal loss implies that the humidity effect does not or only partially comes from changes in the polymer matrix RI. Water molecules must enter the cavity, interact with CryptA, and displace some methane guests. An increase by 10% in relative humidity corresponded to an increase in the detected methane signal by almost 1% (dry gas signal). In 2016, Wang et al. published a methane sensor that comprises CryptA-doped polymer layers.37 Although it was based on surface acoustic wave sensing instead of electromagnetic evanescent waves, the sensor was susceptible to moisture to a similar extent as our prototype.

Moreover, Sun et al. presented a similar sensor for ethanol quantification six years before.38 Because the sensor consisted of a CryptA-doped ethanol channel and a CryptA-free reference channel, the signal observed by Sun must have come from a CryptA–ethanol interaction. In this work, we could show such a high affinity for ethanol vapor. The ethanol signal was multiple times larger than the relative humidity signal. In the case of an ethanol-saturated gas phase, the methane sensitivity of the sensor had vanished.

Finally, an interaction with CryptA is not mandatory to impact the sensor signal. Anything that swells the polymer and changes its RI can alter the methane sensitivity. Benounis et al. found their sensor susceptible to larger concentrations of higher hydrocarbons that theoretically do not fit the CryptA cavity but swell the polymer membrane.6 In general, membrane swelling and aging are familiar sources of sensor drifts. It could also happen to the presented PS membranes if deployed in the environment.

Possible Application Areas

The published works on CryptA-based methane sensors of the last 20 years were proposed for environmental chemistry, explosive mining gas warning, and transformer aging. The principal idea of such sensors was to design cost-efficient, small, and simple but high-resolving devices. Unfortunately, except for Boulart, all sensors fail to resolve the atmospheric methane concentration by far. Boulart’s findings could not be reproduced, although later prototypes had larger RI resolution, higher CryptA content, or larger evanescent field sections. Moreover, we have demonstrated that, at least for the CryptA-doped PS polymer layer, it is necessary to consider a high level of interference by small molecule compounds, temperature, and humidity. A higher RI resolution of the sensing setup would not solve these difficulties.

In this case, we could only conduct measurements with a N2 baseline. While all polymer matrix effects, such as temperature, may be deduced using a second, CryptA-free reference channel, large impact disturbances, such as those observed for humidity or ethanol vapor, come from an interaction with CryptA. A possible reference system cannot consider these disturbances. Nonetheless, they must be addressed to enable CryptA-based methane sensing in air or water.

The best possible application areas for CryptA-based methane sensors would be sampling sites of high methane concentration, temperature stability, and low change in humidity. Methane is a flammable gas which can build explosive mixtures with air at concentrations above 4.3%. Our prototype can readily resolve such concentrations if air humidity and temperature are compensated and no further disturbing substances like ethanol vapor are present. Yang found their prototype to be “ideal for the detection of methane in coal mines.”24 Already, many commercially available methane sensors exist for gas phase measurements. These are based on absorption, pyroelectric, calorimetric, and semiconducting oxide sensing.39 The latter two measurement methods are destructive, while the first two can become costly. Still, these sensor principles have matured and can reach LODs of below 10 ppm. Due to the reported lack in selectivity and resolution, CryptA-based sensing cannot compete with them.

The remaining advantage of our sensor design is that it is submersible. Usually, dissolved methane is measured by equilibration with a gas phase, in which the actual measurement is conducted. The small diffusion path through the polymer layers avoids the need for such an equilibration since the signal comes from the membrane itself. Besides, the nondestructive CryptA method does not require heating or the presence of oxygen. If the sensor periphery (Raspberry Pi, light source, camera) were placed in a pressure housing, the sensor could measure methane concentrations directly in the water column. In environmental chemistry, such properties would be helpful to quantify methane concentrations in underwater vents, deep-sea plumes, freshwater anoxic waters, or pore water of sediment. Unfortunately, variations in dissolved methane concentrations in the environment are small, requiring a resolution of a few ppb.

Conclusion

In this work, we successfully built a low-cost SPR sensor prototype for methane quantification with a refractive index resolution of 4.31 × 10–6 for water and of at least 5.86 × 10–6 for PS. Combining it with self-made CryptA-doped PS layers, we measured methane concentrations in the gas and aqueous phase repetitively.

In the process, for slides with a sensitive layer thickness of more than λ/2 (475 nm), the sensitivity was not dependent on the thickness anymore. Such slides gave the best sensitivity. The response time was lowest for the thinnest polymer layers. Hence, the slide with the thinnest thickness that matched the λ/2 criterion, i.e., slide 3 (760 nm), was the best suited of the here-tested polymer layers. This slide’s detection limit was 135 ppm, while its response time constant was 45 s (62 s for recovery). The best-observed resolution was found for slide 4 (1400 nm, 125 ppm). The methane-induced change in the PS layers was estimated to be 1.42 × 10–8 ppm–1. The polymer layers responded quickly to methane concentration changes.

The methane signal is highly influenced by temperature, humidity, and ethanol. Further cross sensitivities by small molecules are expected. We miss the resolution of Boulart’s prototype by several orders of magnitude, although our prototype performed better than many other CryptA-based methane sensors. In summary, CryptA-based sensing is a promising alternative concept for methane measurement. However, it fails to reach a higher technical readiness level, despite rigorous investigation by different groups using different approaches. As long as the challenges highlighted in this work are not addressed, it cannot compete with state-of-the-art methods due to insufficient resolution and chemical selectivity.
