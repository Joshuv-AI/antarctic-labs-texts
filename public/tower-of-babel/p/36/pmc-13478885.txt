Periostin and airway mucus plugs in nonasthmatic former smokers with and without COPD

To the Editor:

Airway mucus plugs are increasingly considered key pathology in smokers and patients with COPD. Mucus plugs on computed tomography (CT) are associated with lower forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), exacerbations, health-related independence loss, and mortality in patients with COPD [1–3]. Understanding of mucus plugging factors is needed to establish them as a treatable trait in smokers without asthma.

COPD is predominantly characterised by neutrophilic and macrophage inflammation. Type 2 (T2) inflammation contributes to disease pathology in a COPD subgroup, as blocking interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13 reduces exacerbation [4, 5]. IL-13 stimulates airway mucus production and is upstream of periostin and exhaled nitric oxide fraction (FENO) [4, 6, 7]. Nonetheless, a CT study showed that a combination of blood eosinophil count (BEC) ≥300·μL−1 and FENO ≥25 ppb was associated with mucus plugs in asthma and asthma-COPD overlap, but not COPD [8]. Since current smoking impairs FENO production in the airways [9] and mixed eosinophil-neutrophil inflammation can cause mucus plugs in airway disease with moderate T2 inflammation [6], we hypothesised that combined IL-13 dominant T2 and neutrophilic inflammation may induce mucus plug formation in former smokers with and without COPD. This study examined associations of mucus plugs with serum periostin and YKL-40, reflecting IL-13-based and neutrophilic inflammation [4, 6, 10], respectively, along with BEC and FENO in non-asthmatic former smokers.

This study used baseline data from a cohort of smokers with and without COPD at Kyoto University Hospital to examine frailty-associated factors, approved by the ethics committee (R3747-1) and registered with the UMIN (University hospital Medical Information Network) Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000049755). Participants provided written informed consent. COPD was diagnosed with post-bronchodilator FEV1/forced vital capacity <0.7. Exclusion criteria were asthma history or diagnosis, regular oral corticosteroid, and biologics use. FENO measurement (NIOX VERO, NIOX Group, Oxford, UK), spirometry (CHESTAC-8900, Chest M.I., Inc Tokyo, Japan), and blood tests, including white blood cell differential, serum periostin (SHINO-TEST, 326070442, Shino-test Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and YKL-40 (Chitinase 3-like 1, R&D Systems, RSD-DY2599-1, Bio-Techne Corporation, Minneapolis, MN, USA), were conducted. Full-inspiratory ultra-high-resolution chest CT was performed during exacerbation-free periods using an Aquilion Precision scanner (Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Tochigi, Japan). Mucus plug scores by bronchopulmonary segments (0–18) were obtained by two CT-experienced analysts and averaged [3]. R software version 4.3.0 was used for statistics, including Spearman's correlation and logistic regression for mucus plug presence (score >0).

Of 189 smokers with ≥10 pack-years without oral corticosteroids or biologics, 54 with asthma history were excluded, leaving 107 former and 28 current smokers. Age, FENO, and serum periostin were higher in former than current smokers (age: 74.8±7.3 versus 70.0±7.4 years, p=0.002; FENO: 23.7±17.4 versus 15.9±7.2 ppb, p=0.02; periostin: 55.3±17.2 versus 45.5±11.5 ng·mL−1, p=0.005). There was no significant difference in male rate (94 versus 82%), body mass index (23.4±3.2 versus 22.3±3.2 kg·m−2), % of predicted FEV1 (81.6±21.6 versus 73.3±23.3%), BEC (208.0±199.8 versus 217.1±142.2·μL−1), serum YKL-40 (median (interquartile range) 295.5 (191.2–484.0) versus 259.1 (174.3–405.6) ng·mL−1), mucus plug score (0.0 (0.0–1.3) versus 0.0 (0.0–1.6)), or mucus plug presence rate (40 versus 29%). The interaction between periostin and smoking status (current versus former) on mucus plug presence showed a trend in a multivariable logistic model (p=0.052).

Based on the median YKL-40 level (287.9 ng·mL−1), former smokers were divided into the high and low YKL-40 groups (n=55 and 52), of which 20 (36%) and 23 (44%) had mucus plugs, respectively. As shown in figure 1a, serum periostin was significantly associated with mucus plug score in the high YKL-40 group (rho=0.46, p=0.0004), but not in the low YKL-40 group (rho=0.11, p=0.45). There was a significant interaction between periostin and the YKL-40 groups on the mucus plug presence in a multivariable logistic model adjusted for age (p=0.04). In contrast, neither FENO (figure 1b) nor BEC was significantly associated with mucus plug score in the high YKL-40 group (FENO, rho=0.12, p=0.46, and BEC, rho=0.11, p=0.41) or the low YKL-40 group (FENO, rho=0.01, p=0.93, and BEC, rho=0.22, p=0.11). As shown in figure 1c, multivariable logistic regression models showed that periostin levels, age, and FEV1 % predicted were independently associated with mucus plug presence in the high YKL-40 group, while FEV1 % pred was independently associated with mucus plug presence in the low YKL-40 group. Additionally, the significant association between periostin and mucus plug presence in the high YKL-40 group was confirmed in separate multivariable models, each adjusted for one covariate (either age, or FEV1 % pred). In sensitivity analysis, a significant correlation between serum periostin and mucus plug score was detected in former smokers with COPD and high YKL-40 (n=44, rho=0.46, p=0.002), but not in former smokers with COPD and low YKL-40 (n=35, rho=0.16, p=0.36).

Neither periostin nor FENO was associated with mucus plug score in the low YKL-40 group (n=52). c) Forest plots for multivariable logistic models assessing the presence of mucus plugs were constructed by including serum periostin, age, and % of predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1 % pred) as independent variables. In this study, among former smokers with high YKL-40 levels, higher serum periostin levels, but not BEC or FENO, correlated with higher mucus plug scores. Conversely, no associations between T2 markers and mucus plug scores were observed in former smokers with low YKL-40 levels. To our knowledge, this is the first study to link periostin to mucus plugs in non-asthmatic former smokers.

Serum periostin and FENO were higher in former than current smokers, aligning with previous findings of reduced FENO in current smokers with COPD [9]. Among the high YKL-40 former smokers, serum periostin, but not FENO, was associated with mucus plugs, despite both periostin and FENO being indicative of IL-13 related T2 inflammation [4, 6]. Serum YKL-40 reflects sputum neutrophil presence, oxidative stress, and non-T2 inflammatory status in airway disease [10, 11]. The lack of association between FENO and mucus plugs may result from impaired upregulation of inducible nitric oxide synthase and nitric oxide consumption through oxidant reactions in COPD airways, as previously proposed in cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis [12]. Together with the reported association of serum periostin with mixed eosinophil–neutrophil inflammation in asthma [13], our findings suggest that serum periostin may reflect complex airway inflammatory status involving both T2 and non-T2 inflammation more sensitively than FENO in former smokers with neutrophilic inflammation.

The periostin-mucus plug association exclusively in the high-YKL-40 group suggests that in former smokers with COPD, IL-13 dominant T2 inflammation may drive mucus plug formation only with concurrent airway neutrophilic inflammation, representing mixed granulocytic inflammation. IL-13 activation of human airway epithelial cells releases peroxidase and catalyses the formation of oxidant acids in oxidative stress condition, facilitating pathogenic mucus formation [14]. The mixed eosinophilic–neutrophilic inflammation may be driven by airway bacteria of the phylum Proteobacteria in chronic airway diseases with moderate eosinophilic inflammation [6]. Further research should explore drivers of mixed inflammation in non-asthmatic smokers.

While blood biomarkers may not fully capture airway neutrophilic and type 2 inflammation [15], serum YKL-40 and periostin remain clinically valuable due to their accessibility and ability to reflect certain inflammatory components [4, 7, 10].

There are limitations. First, due to the small sample size of current smokers, we could not perform correlation analysis between T2 markers and mucus plugs in the high and low YKL-40 current smokers, which limits periostin utility across different smoking statuses. Second, the small sample size of females restricts the applicability of the current findings to females. Third, serum periostin and FENO levels were frequently within the normal range, requiring further study of serum periostin as a mucus plug biomarker in non-asthmatic former smokers. Fourth, mucus plug cases were relatively few in both the high and low YKL-40 groups. Thus, the multivariable model results should be considered hypothesis-generating and require larger study verification. Finally, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causal relationships between periostin and mucus plugs.

Mucus plugs were associated with higher periostin levels in the high, but not low, YKL-40 former smokers with and without COPD, indicating that the superimposition of IL-13-mediated T2 inflammation on neutrophilic inflammation may facilitate airway mucus plugging.
