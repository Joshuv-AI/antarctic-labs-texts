The effects of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy-related frailty by analytic morphomics in esophageal cancer
Authors: Chien-Hung Chiu, Jules Lin, Peng Zhang, Andrew C Chang, Brian A Derstine, Brian E Ross, Binu Enchakalody, Nidhi V Shah, Yun-Hen Liu, Stewart C Wang, Yin-Kai Chao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery offers potential cure for locally advanced esophageal cancer. Yet, frailty often emerges post-therapy, heightening risks of delayed surgery and complications. Analytic morphomics, derived from computed tomography scans, quantifies body composition, reflecting nutritional and functional status. Our study delineates morphomic shifts post-neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and their impact on short-term outcomes.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients with esophageal cancer treated with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy followed by esophagectomy between 2007 and 2016. Clinical and morphomic data pre- and post- neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy were collected, and changes were identified using paired comparisons. Delayed surgery and postoperative complications were evaluated using univariate and multivariable analyses.ResultsThe study included 181 patients with esophageal cancer who received neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery. Decreased bone mineral density, dorsal muscle group area and visceral adipose tissue area (all p < 0.001) were noted after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. On multivariable Cox regression, a greater increase in subcutaneous fat area after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (p = 0.04) was associated with delayed surgery. Increasing age was associated with overall (p = 0.03) and medical (p = 0.006) postoperative complications while a greater decrease in dorsal muscle group area after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (p = 0.046) was associated with surgical complications.ConclusionsBody composition changes significantly after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy for esophageal cancer, indicating increasing frailty. Advanced age correlated with medical complications, while reduced dorsal muscle group area post-neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy was linked to surgical complications. Utilizing analytic morphomics to evaluate post-neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy body composition changes helps predict short-term outcomes and may refine esophagectomy patient selection.Keywords: Esophageal cancer, Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, Analytic morphomics, Body composition, Delayed surgery , Postoperative complications

Background

Malnutrition and weight loss are common in patients with malignant diseases, especially in esophageal cancer where patients may have limited oral intake due to dysphagia [1]. For locally advanced esophageal cancer, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by esophagectomy is potentially curative [2]. However, adverse events during nCRT have a negative effect on survival [3]. Studies have reported that sarcopenia increases after neoadjuvant chemotherapy for esophageal cancer [4, 5]. Limited studies have also associated sarcopenia with delayed surgery and postoperative complications [5–8].

Analytic morphomics provides quantitative body composition data using standard computed tomography scans which have been associated with outcomes in various malignancies [9, 10]. Changes in analytic morphomics may represent the combined effects of disease severity and a patient’s nutritional or functional status. While previous studies have associated decreased muscle area after nCRT with worse outcomes in esophageal cancer [4–8], analytic morphomics provides a more comprehensive, quantitative evaluation of body composition including muscle, fat, bone, and body dimensions [9, 10].

The goal of this study was to characterize changes in body composition after nCRT and to evaluate their associations with short-term surgical outcomes following esophagectomy. The primary outcomes were delayed surgery after nCRT and the occurrence of postoperative complications. Secondary outcomes included overall, medical, and surgical complication subgroups and their relationships with specific morphomic changes. We hypothesized that nCRT would result in significant frailty-related changes in body composition and that these changes would be associated with delayed surgery and an increased risk of postoperative complications.

Methods

Patients

Ethical approval for this retrospective analysis was obtained from the Chang Gung Medical Foundation Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 201701548B0) on December 26, 2017. Patients deemed fit for surgery by their thoracic surgeon underwent esophagectomy at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (Linkou, Taiwan) from March 2007 to March 2016. Out of 467 patients, 251 underwent esophagectomy post-nCRT, excluding upfront surgery and salvage esophagectomy cases. After removing sarcoma, inadequate imaging, and incomplete clinical data cases, 181 patients were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Flowchart outlining the patients who satisfied the study’s inclusion/exclusion criteria

The staging workup included biopsies, endoscopic ultrasound, barium esophagram, chest and abdominal computed tomography, and positron emission tomography scans. Restaging computed tomography scans were performed 4–6 weeks after the completion of nCRT. Tumors were staged according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer Staging Manual, 7th edition.

Treatment

During the study period, two chemotherapy regimens were used concurrently. The first regimen included cisplatin (75 mg/m2) given via intravenous infusion over 3 h on day 1 and day 29, along with 5-fluorouracil (1000 mg/m2 per day) continuously infused over 96 h from days 1 to 4 and days 29 to 33. The second regimen involved weekly carboplatin administration (doses adjusted to achieve an area under the curve of 2 mg/mL/min) alongside paclitaxel (50 mg/m2) over 5 weeks. Chemotherapy dose reduction was defined as early termination or receiving less than 80% of the full dose. The choice of chemotherapy regimen was determined by the treating clinicians based on clinical considerations and patient tolerance. The radiation protocol evolved during the study. Before July 2014, most patients received 3000–3999 cGy, while after, it was 4000–5600 cGy. The radiation field encompassed the primary tumor and regional/involved lymph nodes.

Scheduled esophagectomy was performed within 8 weeks after nCRT [2, 11]. Delayed surgery was defined as surgery performed more than 8 weeks after nCRT. A McKeown procedure was performed for upper esophageal lesions while an Ivor Lewis esophagectomy was performed for middle and lower esophageal tumors. We performed at least a two-field lymph node dissection. The surgical approach were included in open surgery, minimally invasive surgery or hybrid surgery.

Postoperative complications after esophagectomy were recorded and classified according to the Esophagectomy Complications Consensus Group (ECCG) definitions [12]. In addition, the severity of complications was graded using the Clavien–Dindo classification [13]. In this cohort, complications were categorized into surgical and medical complications based on ECCG criteria. Surgical complications included esophagoenteric anastomotic leak, conduit necrosis or failure requiring intervention, feeding jejunostomy–related complications, wound infection or dehiscence, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, chyle leak, and reoperation for reasons other than anastomotic leak or conduit necrosis. Medical complications encompassed gastrointestinal complications such as ileus and delayed conduit emptying, pulmonary complications including pneumonia, pleural effusion requiring intervention, pneumothorax, atelectasis requiring bronchoscopy, respiratory failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and aspiration, as well as cardiac, thromboembolic, renal, infectious, neurologic or psychiatric, and other systemic complications as defined by the ECCG framework.

Analytic morphomics

All pre- and post-nCRT computed tomography scans were processed using a semi-automated program in MATLAB R2016b [14]. All morphomic variables were measured at the bottom of the second lumbar vertebral cross-sectional level (L2) except bone mineral density (BMD) which was calculated as the average pixel intensity of trabecular bone at the mid-L2 level (Fig. 2). Subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) area was defined as the area between the skin and fascia. Visceral adipose tissue (VAT) area was the cross-sectional area of fat-intensity pixels (-205 to -51 Hounsfield units) inside the visceral envelope. The dorsal muscle group (DMG) area was defined as muscle-intensity pixels (0 to 100 Hounsfield units) within the triangular boundary between the spinal canal and bilateral lateral seams. The DMG density was the median intensity of muscle pixels inside the DMG boundary.

Bone mineral density was measured in trabecular bone. Subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) area was the area between the fascia and skin (yellow and purple lines). Visceral adipose tissue (VAT) was the area of fat-intensity pixels within the visceral cavity (yellow line). The dorsal muscle group (DMG) area was the total area of normal-density muscle in the triangular region between the spinal canal and bilateral lateral seams (pink line)

Statistical analysis

Counts and percentages were used for summarizing categorical variables while median and interquartile values were presented for continuous variables. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess the difference between pre- and post-nCRT variables, which was a non-parametric hypothesis test to one-sample paired Student’s t-test [15]. We used percent of changes in the mrophomic variables (defined as the difference between the post-nCRT and pre-nCRT values divided by the pre-nCRT value) as predictors to develop regression models on short-term outcomes.

Univariate analysis was conducted to determine the differences between patients with and without any delayed surgery or postoperative complications. Fisher’s exact test was used to investigate binary variables. For continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney test was used. To investigate independent associations among morphomic changes and short-term outcomes, we employed a multivariable logistic regression with morphomics changes (BMD, SAT area, VAT area, DMG area and DMG density) and clinical variables (age, clinical stage, chemotherapy regimen, decreased chemotherapy dose, radiation dose and Charlson comorbidity index). In addition, we further utilized a forward and backward selection procedure to select a reduced number of variables for building the best performing logistic regression model. The final selected model was optimized with the best Akaike information criterion. All statistical analyses were conducted in R 3.4.2 (http://www.r-project.org).

Most patients received a cisplatin-based chemotherapy regimen (82.9%) and had a radiation dose < 4000 cGy (72.4%). Reduction of the chemotherapy dosage due to toxicity was required in 18.2%.

Body composition changes after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy

For clinical factors associated with body composition, body weight (p = 0.019), BMI (p = 0.019), and albumin (p < 0.001) significantly decreased after nCRT. Among the morphomic factors, patients had decreased BMD, DMG area, and VAT area post-nCRT (all p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Delayed surgery

Most patients (70.7%) underwent scheduled esophagectomy after completing nCRT. In the full model, no specific variable was associated with delayed surgery. However, in the parsimonious model, a greater increase in SAT area after nCRT (OR 1.01, p = 0.04) was independently associated with delayed surgery (Table 3; Fig. 3). Information regarding the specific reasons for delayed surgery was not systematically or uniformly documented in the medical records and therefore could not be reliably categorized or analyzed.

Postoperative complications

Postoperative complications occurred in 65.2% (118/181) of patients. Among these patients, 49.7% (90/181) had medical complications while 23.8% (43/181) had surgical complications (Table 4). In the parsimonious model, increasing age (OR 1.04, p = 0.03) was significantly associated with postoperative overall complications (Table 5). Upon specific complications subgrouping, increasing age (OR 1.05, p = 0.006) was associated with medical complications (Table 6), whereas a greater decrease in DMG area after nCRT (OR 0.96, p = 0.046) was significantly associated with an increased risk of postoperative surgical complications (Table 7; Fig. 3).

Discussion

While neoadjuvant chemoradiation has improved oncological outcomes for esophageal cancer, side effects associated with chemotherapy and radiation could lead to more frailty and deconditioning increasing the possibility of delayed surgery and the risks of postoperative complications [2]. In the current study, body weight, BMI, and albumin, surrogates for malnutrition and frailty, were significantly decreased after nCRT. Applying analytic morphomics to pre- and post-nCRT computed tomography scans demonstrated marked corresponding changes in body composition with decreased BMD, DMG area, and VAT area.

Malnutrition in esophageal cancer patients receiving chemoradiotherapy is not uncommon due to a combination of mechanical obstruction, adverse effects of chemotherapy or radiation, and cancer cachexia [5–8]. In patients receiving nCRT, mean reductions of 9.59 cm² in skeletal muscle area and 31.5 cm² in adipose tissue area have been reported [4]. Similarly, a decrease in the psoas muscle index was noted in 64% of patients after nCRT [6]. Loss of both muscle and fat mass has been strongly associated with malnutrition [16, 17], and a reduction in skeletal muscle index exceeding 2.98% has been linked to poorer prognosis [7]. In the present study, in addition to a reduction in DMG area after nCRT, we also observed that fat loss predominantly occurred in the visceral compartment rather than in subcutaneous fat. This pattern aligns with previous findings indicating that malnutrition is more closely associated with decreased visceral fat content [18]. Pinho et al. also found that visceral fat decreased more than subcutaneous fat in adults with weight loss [19].

Previous studies revealed that delayed surgery after nCRT for esophageal cancer had negative effects on survival and was associated with increased perioperative mortality [20, 21]. Surgical delays can arise from a combination of clinical and systemic factors. On the clinical side, malnutrition and impaired functional status may necessitate postponement of surgery to allow for patient optimization. System-level contributors include institutional limitations such as restricted operating room availability and shortages in inpatient bed capacity. Patients with a lower BMI were more likely to undergo delayed surgery [22]. However, BMI does not distinguish between fat and muscle mass. Several authors have found that differences in body composition are associated with chemotherapy-related toxicities including not only lower muscle mass but higher fat mass [23, 24]. Subcutaneous fat area may also be increased due to chemotherapy-induced fluid retention and edema, and docetaxel has been known to induce fluid retention due to capillary protein leakage [25]. Prado et al. hypothesized that patients with sarcopenic obesity, with higher fat relative to a lower muscle mass, would receive a larger chemotherapy dose based on their body surface area that would then be distributed throughout a lower lean body mass, increasing the incidence of toxicity [26].

The study of Tessier et al. showed that there tended to be more patients with malnutrition had delayed surgery more than 7 weeks after nCRT [27]. Shaikh et al. reported that toxicity and decondition after nCRT were the reasons for longer delayed surgical interval [28]. In our study, we discovered that a greater increase in SAT area after nCRT was linked to delayed surgery. Moreover, patients needing a reduced chemotherapy dose also tended to experience delayed surgery. We hypothesized that these patients might encounter increased toxicity from nCRT, necessitating a longer recovery period and raising the likelihood of delayed surgery. Personalizing neoadjuvant chemotherapy doses based on these morphomic features could potentially lower toxicity and minimize the risk of delayed surgery.

Complications after esophagectomy have a negative impact on survival [29]. Previous studies revealed several predictors for post-esophagectomy complications including comorbidities, poor nutrition, and impaired functional status [30, 31]. Other studies have reported that sarcopenia after neoadjuvant chemotherapy for esophageal cancer was associated with postoperative complications [4–8]. Analytic morphomics can provide a reproducible, comprehensive overview of an individual patient’s body composition which reflects their general condition, such as nutritional stuatus and performance status [32]. Our study investigated how clinical and morphomic changes relate to post-esophagectomy complications. We found that increasing age correlated with overall complications, with more medical complications occurring as age advanced. In addition, because changes in DMG area after nCRT were predominantly negative, a more negative DMG area change represents greater muscle loss, which was associated with an increased risk of postoperative surgical complications.

Other studies have also found that higher age was associated with postoperative complications after esophagectomy [30]. Older patients likely have less physiological reserve and require longer recovery times. Malnutrition, frailty and cachexia have been associated with postoperative complications after esophagectomy [30, 31], and several studies have shown that muscle loss is common with these conditions [16]. Ida et al. found that decreased protein levels, skeletal muscle, body mass or fat-free mass after neoadjuvant chemotherapy was associated with postoperative complications in patients with esophageal cancer [5]. In the current study, we found that greater decrease in DMG area after nCRT, a potential sign of malnutrition and frailty [32], was related to post-esophagectomy surgical complications. Thoracic duct injury, anastomotic leak, and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury were the most common postoperative surgical complications. It is well known that malnutrition was associated with anastomotic leak [33]. In addition, we hypothesized that patients with malnutrition have less mediastinal fat between the esophagus and adjacent structures, such as the thoracic duct and recurrent laryngeal nerve. Although reduced mediastinal fat may facilitate identification of these structures, it may also result in diminished thermal buffering, potentially increasing susceptibility to direct energy-related injury during dissection. Consequently, chyle leak and laryngeal nerve injuries may be more likely to occur during radical esophagectomy in malnourished patients. Analytic morphomics can help clinicians to quantify these changes and to identify high risk patients preoperatively and potentially reduce these risks through prehabilitation and aggressive nutritional intervention.

This retrospective study has limitations. It primarily included squamous cell carcinoma patients for surgery, potentially limiting generalizability. Additionally, using reduced chemotherapy dose as a toxicity indicator lacked specific side effect data. Reasons for delayed surgery, including patient deconditioning, chemotherapy toxicity, scheduling issues, were not systematically recorded. Consequently, we were unable to quantify or compare specific causes of surgical delay, which may have limited interpretation of the observed association between body composition parameters and surgical timing. In our previous study, the surgical approach was not found to be associated with post-esophagectomy complications [34]. Moreover, the logistic regression model assessing post-nCRT frailty and surgical outcomes did not incorporate specific surgical procedures. Although prior evidence suggests limited impact, the absence of surgical approach as a covariate in the present analysis may still influence the results and should be interpreted with caution. Incorporating predictors of postoperative complications can help clinicians personalize preoperative management. Lastly, the relatively small sample size underscores the need for larger prospective multicenter studies to validate findings.

Conclusions

Notable decreases in BMD, DMG area, and VAT area following nCRT, as identified by analytic morphomics, indicate frailty. A greater decrease in DMG area post-nCRT was associated with postoperative surgical complications. Utilizing analytic morphomics may help pinpoint high-risk patients who could benefit from prehabilitation and intensive nutritional support.
