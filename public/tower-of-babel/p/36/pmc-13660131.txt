Cost-effectiveness of percutaneous epidural adhesiolysis for lumbar spinal stenosis in Thailand
Authors: Suttinee Chaikaew, Nattiya Kapol, Kanchanok Sirison, Nuj Tontisirin, Pramote Euasobhon, Yot Teerawattananon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous epidural adhesiolysis (PEA) is an alternative intervention used to relieve pain in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis who do not respond to standard treatment and epidural steroid injection (ESI). Although it provides better and longer pain control, it comes at a higher cost. This study aimed to evaluate cost-effectiveness in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis in Thailand from a social perspective.MethodsA model-based cost-utility analysis was performed using a Markov model to evaluate the costs and quality-adjusted life years of PEA in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis, aged 50 years. The analysis compared patients who received a single ESI prior to PEA (ESI-PEA), with those receiving two ESIs (ESI-ESI) and the standard treatment. The Markov model simulated patient transitions between health states, including improved, unimproved, and death states, over a 24-month time horizon. One-way sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the effect of single variables, whereas probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted using Monte Carlo simulations to account for uncertainty across multiple parameters.ResultsThis study revealed that the standard treatment had the lowest cost of THB 242,101, whereas the ESI-PEA had the highest cost of THB 274,250 and the highest QALY of 0.8542. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of ESI-ESI and ESI-PEA compared to standard treatment were 961,014 and 1,891,117 THB per QALY, respectively, which exceeded Thailand’s willingness to pay threshold of 160,000 THB/QALY. Uncertainty analysis indicated that the most influential variable was the utility value of non-improved patients.ConclusionsESI-PEA and ESI-ESI are not cost-effective compared to standard treatment in the Thai context. These findings provide valuable insight for policymakers when considering treatment options for lumbar spinal stenosis in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15033-9.Keywords: Percutaneous epidural adhesiolysis, Lumbar spinal stenosis, Epidural steroid injection, Cost-effectiveness, Low back pain

Background

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a condition caused by narrowing of the spinal canal and is often accompanied by narrowing of the neural foramina and compression of the nerve roots. According to the Framingham study [1], the prevalence of LSS increased with age. For those aged 60–69 years, the prevalence rates were 47.2% and 19.4%, respectively. LSS is a common illness in the elderly, causing pain in the lower back and buttocks, sciatica, numbness, tingling, and loss of sensation [2, 3]. These conditions impact mobility and work performance, leading to decreased productivity and disability. Patients with LSS frequently undergo spine surgery [4], which significantly increases the cost of care. In South Korea, the average monthly direct medical cost for the surgical cohort was $68.28 per patient, compared to $40.78 per patient for the non-surgical cohort [5].

Thailand has been classified as an aged society as people aged 60 years and above account for 10% of the population since 2005 [6]. In 2023, the country became a complete-aged society when older persons accounted for 20% of the population and it is expected to become a super-aged society in the next 15 years. Consequently, it is anticipated that a significant number of senior citizens in Thailand will encounter the impact of LSS, leading to an increase in healthcare expenses and a decline in their overall well-being.

Conservative treatment, or non-operative treatment, such as oral medication, physical therapy, and injection intervention (epidural steroid injection and percutaneous epidural adhesiolysis) is the most common alternative for LSS patients [7]. The clinical guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis by the North American Spine Society (NASS) [8], as well as the recommendation of pain intervention for chronic low back pain by the Thai Association for the Study of Pain [9], advocate for non-operative treatment as the first line therapy or standard treatment for LSS.

Epidural steroid injection (ESI) and percutaneous epidural adhesiolysis (PEA) are two injection interventions showing promise for patients with LSS [10–12]. ESI is widely utilized and has a temporary effect on reducing pain and improving patients’ ability to engage in daily activities. However, empirical data on patients with spinal canal stenosis are limited, and long-term effectiveness studies have yielded inconsistent findings. Research suggests that PEA offers better pain control than ESI does [13, 14]. Moreover, adverse reactions to the PEA procedure are mild and self-limiting. However, the cost-effectiveness of PEA is constrained. A single U.S. study reported that the cost-utility of PEA in LSS patients was $2,652 per quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) [12]. Nevertheless, the applicability of this information to Thailand may be limited due to contextual differences.

Although the Thai Association for the Study of Pain recommends PEA for LSS patients who are unable to manage pain with ESI, patients are still responsible for out-of-pocket costs. The Universal Health Coverage Benefit Package Committee is deliberating whether to include two ESI injections (ESI-ESI) or a single ESI prior to PEA (ESI-PEA) in the health benefit package for LSS patients. Economic evaluation plays a crucial role in providing decision support for policymakers. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the cost-utility of ESI-ESI and ESI-PEA compared to standard treatment in patients with symptomatic LSS in the Thai context.

Methods

Study design and participants

A model-based economic evaluation was conducted to compare the costs and health outcomes of injection treatment (ESI-PEA or ESI-ESI) with those of standard treatment in Thai LSS patients from a societal perspective. The evaluation followed the Thai Health Technology and Assessment Guidelines [15] and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Ramathibodi and Siriraj hospitals, Thailand (COA: MURA2022/240). The results were presented in terms of the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), expressed as the monetary value per quality-adjusted life year.

Model structure

Three separate Markov models were developed based on the natural disease progression and treatment (Fig. 1A-C). The first model represented the standard treatment which included physical therapy and medication. The second model depicted treatment with two ESI injections (ESI-ESI). The third model represented PEA treatment for patients who did not improve after receiving an ESI, then followed by PEA (ESI-PEA). The analysis used a 24-month time horizon based on Manchikanti’s study [4], with 2-week cycle lengths determined through model validation.

A Symptomatic LSS with standard treatment; B Symptomatic LSS with 2x ESI (ESI-ESI); C Symptomatic LSS with ESI then PA (ESI-PEA)

For all the models, a hypothetical 50-year-old patient with LSS and low back pain for at least 6 months was used. Treatment outcomes were categorized as either improved or unimproved. Patients whose condition did not improve continue receiving treatment and could subsequently improve, and vice versa. In the second model, patients could receive up to two ESI injections (ESI-ESI) within 2 years if there was no response. In the third model, patients receive an initial ESI injection, and if they do not respond within two years, a subsequent PEA injection is administered.

Input parameters

Effectiveness parameters, such as treatment outcomes, and death rates, were examined through international literature and national databases. Initially, treatment outcomes retrieved from the literature were considered underestimated and subsequently revised based on expert validation. However, in order to ascertain the difference, we performed a scenario analysis utilizing treatment results from the literature.

Health utility data were collected from 51 LSS patients treated at Ramathibodi and Siriraj Hospitals. The inclusion criteria required a diagnosis of lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) and treatment with either ESI or PEA within the past six months. The mean age of participants was 67.8 ± 9.5 years, and 64% were female. The most common underlying conditions were hypertension (38%) and diabetes mellitus (24%). Additionally, 67% of the patients were unemployed. Fifty-one patients with LSS were evaluated and classified into improved and unimproved groups by a specialized physician. The Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I) Thai version was used to evaluate the treatment outcome. A positive response of 1–3 points indicates that the treatment improved; a response of 4–7 points indicates that the treatment unimproved. The European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions 5 Level Version (EQ-5D-5L) was used to assess health utility for improved and unimproved patients.

Direct medical costs were retrieved from two hospital databases, whereas direct non-medical costs were obtained from interviews with the same 51 patients and their families at Ramathibodi and Siriraj Hospitals. Direct non-medical costs included travel, food, accommodation, and home renovation costs. In addition, income loss incurred by caregivers was included. To avoid double counting, indirect costs were not included in the analysis because productivity losses may already be partially captured in the health utility values used to estimate QALYs. All costs were adjusted to the year 2022 values using Thai consumer price index (CPI) from the Bureau of Trade and Economic Indices (US$1 = 34.45 THB approximately). Model validation

Face validation was conducted through two stakeholder meetings. These meetings involved clinical specialists, policy makers, and health economists. The main goals of the first meeting were to determine the research questions, policy options, and data sources. The following gathering aimed to validate the model structure and the findings of the research. Clinical specialists were consulted during the development of the treatment pathways and model assumptions. In addition, internal validation was performed by comparing our estimated effectiveness parameters with those of the study carried out by Manchikanti et al. [14].

Data analysis

A discount rate of 3% was applied to both costs and outcomes, in accordance with the Thai Health Technology and Assessment Guidelines [15]. The total costs and outcomes among the three model options were compared in terms of the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). A treatment option was considered cost-effective if the ICER was lower than the cost-effectiveness threshold in Thailand, which is 160,000 THB per QALY [15].

To assess parameter uncertainty, sensitivity analysis was performed using both one-way and probabilistic methods. For the one-way sensitivity analysis, each parameter was varied one at a time while keeping the other parameters constant. The mean values of the parameters were varied using their standard errors (SE). A tornado diagram was used to illustrate how the ICER changes when a parameter is at its minimum or maximum value. Only the most impactful parameters were presented in the tornado diagram. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted to consider the uncertainty of all the parameters simultaneously by changing their values at the same time. Monte Carlo simulations were run 1,000 times based on the data distribution and the results were depicted in a cost-effectiveness plane and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves.

Results

Base case

Table 2 illustrates the total costs, life years, QALYs for standard treatment, ESI-ESI, and ESI-PEA. The total cost per patient was THB 242,101 for standard treatment, THB 256,532 for ESI-ESI, and THB 274,250 for ESI-PEA. All strategies resulted in the same life expectancy (50.9446 years) but differed in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs): 0.8372 for standard treatment, 0.8517 for ESI-ESI, and 0.8542 for ESI-PEA. The total costs and QALYs were the highest for ESI- PEA, and the lowest for standard treatment. However, the ICERs for ESI-ESI and ESI-PEA, at 961,014 THB per QALY and THB 7,369,982 per QALY respectively, exceeded THB 160,000 indicating that neither strategy was considered cost-effective compared to standard treatment. Figure 2 shows a tornado diagram from the one-way sensitivity analysis, revealing that the most sensitive variable was the utility value of unimproved patients. The minimum and maximum values of this variable influenced the ICER changes with a range of -29.8% and 72.3%. The next sensitive variables were the probability of improvement after receiving the standard treatment and the cost of PEA.

Sensitivity analysis

The results of the PSA were illustrated by a cost-effectiveness plane (Fig. 3) and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (Fig. 4). The cost-effectiveness plane revealed that ESI-PEA resulted in greater costs and higher QALYs when compared to standard treatment. According to the willingness to pay threshold in Thailand, the treatment with ESI-PEA was not cost-effective unless the cost-effectiveness threshold exceeded 1,050,000 Baht per QALY. ESI-PEA is always inferior to standard treatment and ESI-ESI at every cost-effectiveness threshold (Fig. 4).

Scenario analysis

In the scenario where data from the literature were used instead of expert opinion, the findings indicate that while both ESI-ESI and ESI-PEA offer modest improvements in QALYs, they are associated with substantially higher costs. As in the base case analysis, the resulting ICERs exceed the commonly accepted cost-effectiveness threshold in Thailand. These results are presented in the supplementary materials.

Discussion

This was the first economic evaluation comparing standard treatment for patients with LSS with two injection interventions: PEA and ESI. The injection treatments, ESI-ESI or ESI-PEA provided higher QALYs but also incurred higher costs compared to the standard treatment. However, neither alternative was cost-effective compared to standard treatment in the Thai context.

The findings of this study differed from those of an earlier study carried out in the U.S [12]. The ICER of the ESI-PEA in this study was 7,369,982 THB (US$ 213,933), which is significantly higher than the US$2,649 per QALY reported in the U.S study. However, there were certain methodological variations. This study included direct medical costs outside of the hospital as well as direct non-medical costs, whereas the U.S. study included only the costs of ambulatory surgery and physician visits within the hospital. Additionally, our model assumed that over the period of two years, patients with LSS would receive ESI-ESI or ESI-PEA once, whereas patients in the U.S. study received PEA six times. Therefore, the effectiveness of the interventions may differ according to the number of treatments administered. Moreover, the standard treatment costs, particularly medication costs, are lower in Thailand than in the United States.

Although PEA demonstrated greater effectiveness in reducing pain in patients with LSS compared to steroid injections or physical therapy [19] in the short term, one notable finding was that the PEA group required retreatment more frequently than did those receiving steroid injections alone [4, 14]. This implies that while the higher rate of retreatment in the PEA group offers better short-term pain relief, the necessity for multiple treatments may lead to only slight benefits in utility, while substantially increasing the overall expenses associated with the PEA group.

The one-way sensitivity analysis, as illustrated in the tornado diagram, identified the utility value of unimproved patients as the most sensitive variable affecting the ICER. However, our sensitivity analysis findings indicate that even though the most sensitive variable varied from minimum to maximum and had an impact on the change in the ICER, this did not affect the conclusion. PEA was found to be less cost-effective than the standard treatment.

The findings of our study should be interpreted with caution, given the following limitations. Owing to the limited number and short duration of clinical trials involving patients with LSS, this study compared costs and outcomes at 24 months. This time horizon may not be sufficient to capture the long-term effects of all interventions, particularly PEA. Despite conducting a systematic review of the efficacy of injection interventions in patients with LSS, we found only two direct comparisons: standard treatment vs. ESI and ESI vs. PEA [14, 19]. The experts involved in the model validation disagreed with the pooled efficacy data from the literature, and consequently, the effectiveness values derived from expert data were ultimately applied in this study. This may affect the strength of the evidence. Finally, variability in healthcare delivery, learning curves associated with the use of ESI and/or PEA, patients’ health state preferences, and financing mechanisms across settings can make it challenging to generalize the study findings in Thailand to other contexts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while ESI-PEA offers greater pain relief and improved quality of life for patients with lumbar spinal stenosis, it is not a cost-effective option compared to ESI-ESI and standard treatment in the context of Thailand’s healthcare system. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of ESI-PEA significantly exceeds the country’s willingness-to-pay threshold, suggesting that despite its clinical benefits, ESI-PEA may not be a feasible alternative from an economic perspective.
