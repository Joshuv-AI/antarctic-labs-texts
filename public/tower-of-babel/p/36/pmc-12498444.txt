Oral contraceptive use and intraocular pressure: findings from a population-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Rozhin Kasiri, Zahra Rahimi, Farideh Moramezi, Elham Zanganeh Yousefabadi, Abbas Mohammadi, Ali Kasiri, Mohammad Armin Kasiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the association between oral contraceptive pill (OCP) use and intraocular pressure (IOP) among women aged 35–50 years, considering confounding factors such as age, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, and family history of glaucoma.MethodsThis population-based cross-sectional study included 100 women aged 35–50 years. Participants were categorized into OCP users (n = 50) and non-users (n = 50). Ocular examinations, including Goldmann applanation tonometry for IOP measurement, were conducted between 7:30 a.m. and 9:00 a.m. Detailed medical histories were obtained, and relevant covariates such as age, BMI, blood pressure, and family history of glaucoma were recorded. Statistical analysis involved descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to assess the relationship between OCP use and IOP, adjusting for identified confounding factors.ResultsThe mean duration of OCP use in the user group was 6.57 ± 5.21 years. After adjusting for age, BMI, hypertension, and family history of glaucoma, no statistically significant difference in mean IOP was found between OCP users (14.2 ± 2.8 mmHg) and non-users (14.5 ± 3.1 mmHg) (p = 0.592). The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference in IOP was − 1.24 to 0.73 mmHg.ConclusionThis study found no statistically significant association between oral contraceptive pill use and intraocular pressure in women aged 35–50 years after controlling for key confounding variables. While the difference was not statistically significant, the findings suggest that OCPs, as used by this cohort, do not lead to clinically meaningful alterations in IOP that would be of concern in routine ophthalmologic practice, considering the Minimal Clinically Important Difference for IOP is typically 2–3 mmHg. Further research with larger sample sizes and detailed OCP specifications is warranted.Keywords: Oral contraceptive pills, Intraocular pressure, Glaucoma, Women's health, Epidemiology

Introduction

Intraocular pressure (IOP) is a critical ophthalmological parameter, as its elevation is the primary modifiable risk factor for glaucoma, a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide [1]. IOP refers to the fluid pressure inside the eye, maintained by a balance between the production and drainage of aqueous humor [1, 2]. Understanding factors that influence IOP is crucial for early detection and management of ocular diseases [1].

Beyond well-established risk factors such as age, race, and family history of glaucoma, a growing body of research explores systemic factors that may impact IOP [1, 3]. Hormonal influences, particularly sex hormones, have garnered attention due to their widespread physiological effects, including their potential role in ocular physiology. Sex hormone receptors are widely distributed in various ocular tissues, including the conjunctiva, cornea, ciliary body, trabecular meshwork, and lacrimal glands, suggesting a direct pathway for hormonal modulation of ocular functions, including aqueous humor dynamics [4, 5].

Oral contraceptive pills (OCPs) are widely used hormonal medications, primarily by women of reproductive age. Their systemic effects are extensive, and given the presence of sex hormone receptors in ocular tissues, there is a theoretical basis for OCPs to influence IOP [5–10]. Previous studies on the association between OCP use and IOP have yielded inconsistent results, with some reporting an increase [6], others a decrease [7], and many finding no significant association [8–10]. These discrepancies may be attributed to variations in study design, population characteristics, duration and type of OCP use, and control for confounding variables [9, 10].

This study aims to investigate the association between oral contraceptive pill use and intraocular pressure among women aged 35–50 years. Recognizing that other systemic factors can influence IOP, we also accounted for key covariates such as body mass index (BMI), hypertension, and smoking status. These factors were included not as primary outcomes of interest, but as crucial confounding variables known to affect IOP, thus ensuring a more accurate assessment of the independent effect of OCPs. By rigorously controlling for these variables, our goal is to provide a clearer understanding of the relationship between OCP use and IOP in this specific age demographic.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional, population-based study involved women aged 35–70 who participated in the Hoveyzeh Cohort Study (HCS), a subset of the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran [PERSIAN] [11, 12]. The HCS, conducted in southwest Iran, initially enrolled 10,009 adults from May 2016. For this analysis, from the 5,983 women aged 35–70 years in HCS, we applied exclusion criteria, resulting in a final sample of 2,272 women (Fig. 1). Data collection protocols, including questionnaires and biological sampling, were meticulously implemented following rigorous Quality Assurance (QA) and Quality Control (QC) procedures at multiple levels, ensuring data integrity throughout all study phases that were previously published (11, 12).

Participants were divided into two groups: OCP users (n = 50), defined as women currently using oral contraceptive pills for at least six consecutive months, and OCP non-users (n = 50), defined as women who had never used oral contraceptive pills or had discontinued use for at least 12 months prior to the study. Information regarding OCP use, including duration of use, was collected through a detailed questionnaire.

Data collection

All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination. Ocular measurements, including IOP, were consistently performed by the same trained ophthalmologist between 7:30 a.m. and 9:00 a.m. to minimize diurnal variation.

Intraocular pressure measurement

IOP was measured using Goldmann applanation tonometry (Haag-Streit AG, Koeniz, Switzerland). To ensure accuracy and consistency, two consecutive IOP readings were taken for each eye. If the difference between the two readings was greater than 2 mmHg, a third reading was taken, and the average of the two closest readings was used for analysis. The mean IOP from both eyes was used as the primary outcome variable.

Anthropometric and Clinical Data.

Height and weight were measured to calculate BMI (kg/m²). Blood pressure (systolic and diastolic) was measured using an automated sphygmomanometer after a 5-minute rest period. Information on smoking status (current smoker, ex-smoker, never smoker), history of hypertension, and family history of glaucoma (defined as a first-degree relative diagnosed with glaucoma) was collected through a standardized questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS statistical software, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics of the study population, presented as means ± standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables.

Independent t-tests were used to compare continuous variables between OCP users and non-users. Chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables.

The primary analysis involved an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to compare mean IOP between OCP users and non-users, while controlling for potential confounding variables. Based on existing literature and biological plausibility, age, hypertension (yes/no), family history of glaucoma (yes/no), and BMI were included as covariates in the ANCOVA model. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 746 women (90 OCP users and 656 non-users) successfully completed the study. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences between OCP users and non-users regarding age, BMI, or blood pressure. The mean age of the participants was 42.1 ± 4.5 years.

The mean duration of oral contraceptive pill use in the user group was 6.57 ± 5.21 years (ranging from 1 to 14 years).

Mean intraocular pressure for OCP users was 14.2 ± 2.8 mmHg, while for non-users it was 14.5 ± 3.1 mmHg. An independent t-test revealed no statistically significant difference in mean IOP between the two groups (p = 0.592).

To account for potential confounding factors, an Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was performed, with age, BMI, hypertension, and family history of glaucoma included as covariates. After adjusting for these variables, there remained no statistically significant difference in mean IOP between OCP users and non-users (F(1, 95) = 0.288, p = 0.592). The estimated marginal means for IOP were 14.3 mmHg for OCP users and 14.4 mmHg for OCP non-users, with a 95% confidence interval for the mean difference of −1.24 to 0.73 mmHg (Fig. 2; Table 2).

Regarding the age distribution of OCP use within the sample, the prevalence of OCP users among the age subgroups was as follows: In the 35–40 age group, 40% were OCP users; in the 41–45 age group, 60% were OCP users; and in the 46–50 age group, 100% were OCP users. Discussion

This study investigated the association between OCP use and IOP in a cohort of 100 women aged 35–50 years, meticulously controlling for key confounding variables such as age, BMI, hypertension, and family history of glaucoma. Our primary finding is that there was no statistically significant difference in mean IOP between OCP users and non-users after adjusting for these important covariates. This result suggests that, within the context of this study’s design and population, OCP use does not exert a clinically meaningful effect on IOP.

Our findings align with several previous studies that also reported no significant association between OCP use and IOP [8, 9, 10]. For instance, Tint et al. similarly found no difference in IOP between OCP users and non-users in a large population-based cohort [13]. This consistency across diverse populations and methodologies strengthens the argument that OCPs, in their general application, may not significantly alter IOP. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference in IOP (−1.24 to 0.73 mmHg) further underscores this point, as it comfortably falls within the range typically considered to be clinically insignificant for IOP fluctuations. Considering that the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for IOP is generally accepted to be around 2–3 mmHg [14–16], our observed mean difference of 0.3 mmHg (non-users 14.5 mmHg - users 14.2 mmHg = 0.3 mmHg) is well below this threshold, indicating that even if a subtle effect exists, it is unlikely to have clinical relevance for glaucoma risk or management.

Conversely, some studies have reported conflicting results, with some suggesting a potential increase [6] or decrease [7] in IOP with OCP use. These discrepancies might be attributable to several factors. Variations in study design, including participant age ranges, sample sizes, duration, and type of OCP formulations (e.g., estrogen-progestin combinations vs. progestin-only pills, dosage levels), and differences in controlling for confounding variables, could all contribute to disparate outcomes [14, 15]. For example, studies focusing on specific OCP formulations or durations might reveal subtle effects not captured in a general assessment of OCP use [16]. Furthermore, genetic predispositions or interactions with other systemic conditions might modulate the hormonal impact on IOP [14–18], which were not explored in detail in our study.

The strengths of our study include its population-based cross-sectional design, the careful selection of a homogenous age group (35–50 years), and the rigorous control for known IOP-influencing factors such as age, BMI, hypertension, and family history of glaucoma using ANCOVA. The consistent time of IOP measurement (7:30 a.m. to 9:00 a.m.) also minimized diurnal variations, enhancing the reliability of our IOP measurements.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes the establishment of a causal relationship between OCP use and IOP. Longitudinal studies are needed to observe changes in IOP over time concerning OCP initiation, cessation, or changes in formulation. Second, while we controlled for OCP use, we did not differentiate between specific types of OCPs (e.g., combined oral contraceptives vs. progestin-only pills), their dosages, or the precise hormonal composition. Future research should investigate whether specific hormonal components or dosages within OCPs might differentially affect IOP. Third, although we collected information on the duration of OCP use, the analysis did not stratify by duration or cumulative exposure, which could be relevant for long-term effects. Lastly, while our sample size was adequate for detecting a statistically significant difference if one existed, a larger sample might reveal very small, yet potentially relevant, associations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides further evidence that general oral contraceptive pill use in women aged 35–50 years is not associated with a statistically significant or clinically meaningful difference in intraocular pressure, after accounting for important confounding variables. These findings are reassuring for women using OCPs in terms of their ocular health regarding IOP. Future research should consider prospective designs, detailed OCP specifications, and the exploration of genetic or other systemic interactions to further elucidate any subtle hormonal influences on IOP [13].
