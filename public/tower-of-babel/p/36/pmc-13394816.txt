SERPINE1 Alternative Polyadenylation Influences Gastric Cancer Prognosis via N6‐Methyladenosine Modification
Authors: Zhuoyi Wu, Ruyun Xu, Ju Fang, Bing Liu, Cao Zhang, Shangxin Zhang, Jing Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlternative polyadenylation (APA) and N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) methylation are critical post‐transcriptional regulators in cancer, yet their synergistic role in gastric cancer (GC) remains poorly understood. By integrating RNA‐seq and MeRIP‐seq data from paired GC tumor and adjacent normal tissues, we identified 9769 distinct APA events and 3117 differentially methylated sites. Concurrent 3′UTR length and methylation alterations were observed in 165 genes. Among these, SERPINE1 emerged as an independent prognostic factor; its high expression correlated with advanced TNM stages and poor survival across multiple independent cohorts. Moreover, elevated plasma SERPINE1 protein levels in an in‐house cohort of 263 GC patients were significantly associated with worse overall and disease‐specific survival. Mechanistically, the APA factor CSTF2 and the m6A reader IGF2BP1 were found to physically interact and cooperatively regulate SERPINE1 3′UTR selection. Silencing either factor shifted expression toward the long 3′UTR isoform and reduced SERPINE1 protein levels. Functional assays further confirmed that the short SERPINE1 isoform promotes GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion more potently than the long isoform. Collectively, this study reveals that CSTF2 and IGF2BP1 synergistically drive SERPINE1 3′UTR shortening to enhance GC malignancy, highlighting SERPINE1 as a promising prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target.Keywords: alternative polyadenylation, biomarker, gastric cancer, m6A methylation

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most common malignant tumors globally and poses a serious threat to human health. In 2022, GC ranked fifth in both incidence and mortality among all cancers globally, posing a severe disease burden in China with 358,700 new cases and 260,400 deaths recorded [1, 2]. Due to the lack of effective early diagnostic markers, a significant proportion of GC patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage [3, 4]. Consequently, the 5‐year survival rate remains below 35%, and the median survival time for patients with advanced disease is less than 16 months [5]. Genetic factors serve as key prognostic indicators in GC, with their expression governed by complex factors at multiple levels. Such as, epigenetic [6, 7] and splicing regulation [8, 9]. Understanding these mechanisms is vital for advancing precision medicine and improving survival outcomes for GC patients.

Alternative polyadenylation (APA) is a pervasive post‐transcriptional regulatory mechanism in eukaryotic cells. By selecting distinct polyadenylation sites (PAS), a single gene can generate multiple mRNA isoforms with different 3′untranslated regions (3′UTRs) [10]. This process plays a pivotal role in RNA metabolism, including mRNA maturation, stability control, and translation efficiency, ultimately contributing to proteome diversification [11, 12, 13]. Recent studies have established that dysregulated APA acts as a critical driver in cancer progression. For example, Tan et al. demonstrated that in hepatocellular carcinoma, high expression of CPSF6 promotes the usage of the proximal poly(A) site in the NQO1 3′UTR, thereby facilitating HCC progression [14]. Similarly, another study reported that the usage of the proximal poly(A) site resulted in a shorter 3′UTR transcript, which led to the loss of miRNA binding site. This mechanism affected lung adenocarcinoma malignant phenotype by regulating the expression level of NIT2 [15]. However, despite these advances in other malignancies, the specific regulatory roles of APA in the progression of GC remain largely unexplored.

N6‐methyladenosine (m6A), a pivotal epitranscriptomic modification, dynamically regulates gene expression by modulating RNA metabolism—including splicing, stability, and translational efficiency. Emerging evidence reveals that aberrant m6A deposition is critically linked to tumor survival and progression [16, 17, 18]. Recent evidence has revealed a functional crosstalk between m6A modifications and APA machinery in gene expression regulation. The m6A reader YTHDC1 recognizes methylated adenosines in the FSP1 3′UTR, recruiting the CSTF3‐containing cleavage complex to generate shortened isoforms with reduced mRNA half‐life [19]. Luo et al. provided direct evidence that IGF2BP3‐dependent m6A recognition recruits NUDT21 to enforce proximal PAS selection in SPTBN1 transcripts, thereby enhancing oncogenic progression in ovarian cancer [20]. Likewise, another study in the same malignancy found that IGF2BP2 recognizes m6A‐modified PUM2 transcripts and recruits CPSF6 to the proximal PAS, generating shortened 3′UTR transcripts that drive tumor progression [21]. Taken together, the m6A‐APA axis plays a significant biological role in tumor initiation and progression; however, whether similar regulatory mechanisms contribute to the progression of GC remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we integrated multi‐omics analyses to screen for critical prognostic targets in GC. We elucidated a regulatory mechanism driven by the crosstalk between m6A modification and APA, wherein an m6A reader binds to its downstream target mRNA and facilitates the recruitment of a key polyadenylation regulator. This interaction alters 3′UTR processing and promotes tumor progression, thereby providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying GC prognosis.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Tissue Specimens

An in‐house cohort of 324 GC patients (designated as the AhGC cohort) was recruited from the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between August 2020 and December 2023. For plasma protein level validation, a sub‐cohort of 263 patients was selected strictly based on the availability of adequate preoperative blood samples and complete clinicopathological and follow‐up records. None of the patients had received preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy, and all patients provided written informed consent. Detailed patient characteristics are provided in Table S1. RNA sequencing was performed on 61 paired GC and adjacent normal tissue samples from the AhGC cohort using the DNBSEQ‐T7 platform (MGI, China) (Table S2). This study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. 20210333) and followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Gene expression data and clinical information for four public GC cohorts were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), namely TCGA‐STAD (n = 482), GSE63089 (n = 90), GSE54129 (n = 132), and GSE65801 (n = 64). To prevent analytical bias driven by cross‐platform batch effects, each public dataset was analyzed independently for external validation.

APA Analysis

APA usage was quantified using Dapars2 (Python 2.7.2), with the Percentage of Distal Poly(A) Site Usage Index (PDUI) serving as a metric for APA regulation. PDUI values range from 0 (exclusive proximal polyadenylation site usage) to 1 (exclusive distal site usage), reflecting the relative preference for distal poly(A) signal (PAS) utilization. APA events were validated using data from The Cancer 3′UTR Atlas (TC3A) (http://tc3a.org/). The difference in median PDUI scores between tumor and normal samples was calculated for each gene (ΔPDUI = MedianPDUItumor—MedianPDUInormal) to evaluate 3′UTR changes. Genes with |ΔPDUI| > 0.05 were identified as having shortened or lengthened 3′UTRs. Statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, with a False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05 considered significant. The association between PDUI scores and gene expression was evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis.

Differential 3′UTR Usage and Hierarchical Clustering Analysis

The PDUI value of each transcript with significant 3′UTR alterations in tumor samples was compared with its median value in normal samples (ΔPDUITumor = PDUITumor—MedianPDUINormal), followed by hierarchical clustering analysis. To quantify the relative proportions of infiltrating immune cells, we utilized the CIBERSORT algorithm with the LM22 signature matrix as a reference.

MeRIP‐Seq Analysis

MeRIP‐seq profiling of three paired tumor and adjacent tissues. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Scientific, USA) and treated with DNase I (NEB, USA) to remove genomic DNA. RNA purity, integrity, and concentration were assessed using a Nanodrop OneC, LabChip GX Touch, and Qubit 3.0, respectively. Polyadenylated RNA was enriched with VAHTS mRNA Capture Beads (Vazyme, China) and chemically fragmented into ~100‐nt pieces (20 mM ZnCl2 at 95°C). A 10% aliquot was saved as input, while the remainder underwent immunoprecipitation with an anti‐m6A polyclonal antibody and Protein‐G magnetic beads (Thermo Scientific, USA). After washing and TRIzol extraction, both input and m6A‐IP RNA were used to construct libraries with the KC Digital mRNA Library Prep Kit (Seqhealth), incorporating unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) to minimize PCR bias. Libraries (200–500 bp) were quantified and sequenced on a DNBSEQ‐T7 platform (MGI, China) using the PE150 mode.

MeRIP‐seq data processing and analysis were performed using the exomePeak2 package. Raw sequencing reads underwent quality control and adapter trimming, followed by alignment to the human reference genome using STAR. m6A peak identification and differential methylation analysis were conducted with exomePeak2, applying default parameters unless otherwise specified. The resulting peak level quantification matrix was reshaped and formatted using the reshape2 package to facilitate downstream statistical analyses and visualization. Differentially methylated peaks were filtered based on a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Identification of APA and m6A Regulators

Potential regulators of APA events and m6A modifications were identified using the POSTAR3 database (http://111.198.139.65/). Specifically, the “RBP Binding Sites” module was employed to predict regulators that potentially influence the mRNA of target genes.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Plasma SERPINE1 concentrations were measured using a commercial ELISA kit (FineTest, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were diluted 1:10 prior to analysis, and optical density was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Single‐Cell RNA‐Sequencing Data

Single‐cell RNA‐sequencing data from 26 GC tissue samples were obtained from the GEO database (accession number GSE183904) to characterize immune cell populations. A Seurat object was generated using the CreateSeuratObject function, and cell clusters were annotated based on canonical marker genes curated from published literature.

Cells and Culture Conditions

Human GC cell lines (HGC‐27, AGS) were purchased from Shanghai Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Vivacell, China), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Apexbio, USA) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere.

Western Blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease/phosphatase inhibitors (Apexbio, USA), and protein concentrations were determined via BCA assay (Sangon Biotech, China). Equal amounts of proteins were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA), probed with specific primary antibodies, and visualized using an ECL substrate (Zenbio, China).

Reverse Transcription and Quantitative PCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the SevenFast Total RNA Extraction Kit (Seven, China), followed by cDNA synthesis with SPARK script II All‐in‐one RT SuperMix (Sparkgade, China). Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was performed using 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Sparkgade, China) following the manufacturer's instructions.

3′RACE Amplification and Product Validation

The target gene transcripts were amplified using the 3′‐Full RACE Core Set with PrimeScript RTase kit (Takara, Japan) following the manufacturer's protocol. PCR products (5–10 μL) were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm amplification.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation Assay

HGC‐27 and AGS cells were lysed in lysis buffer supplemented with inhibitors (Apexbio, USA). Total protein (1,000 μg) was incubated with 5 μg of anti‐IGF2BP1 antibody or normal IgG overnight at 4°C, followed by a 4‐h precipitation with Protein A/G magnetic beads. After thorough washing, the immunoprecipitated complexes and whole‐cell lysates (Input) were eluted and analyzed by Western blotting.

Colony Formation Assays

Pretransfected cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/mL and cultured for 14 days, with medium replaced every 4 days. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and visible colonies (defined as ≥ 50 cells) were counted microscopically.

Migration and Invasion Assays

Pretransfected cells (5 × 104 cells) suspended in serum‐free medium were seeded into uncoated (for migration) or Matrigel‐coated (for invasion) Transwell inserts. RPMI 1640 medium containing 20% FBS was added to the lower chambers as a chemoattractant. After 24–48 h of incubation, the migrated or invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted microscopically.

Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK‐8 Assay)

Two types of GC cells were seeded into 96‐well plates and subjected to serum starvation for 24 h. Cell viability was evaluated at 24, 48, and 72 h using the CCK‐8 assay. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and cell viability was calculated relative to the sham group, with baseline adjustments made for the starvation absorbance. Cell viability (%) was calculated as: [(ODtest—ODstarvation) / (ODsham—ODstarvation)] × 100%.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 and R (version 4.4.3). For functional analysis, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) gene sets were analyzed using the R package “ClusterProfiler”. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments. Differences between two groups were analyzed using the two‐tailed Student's t‐test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one‐way ANOVA. Survival probability was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log‐rank test. To maintain statistical robustness and avoid overfitting, continuous variables were dichotomized into ‘High’ and ‘Low’ groups based strictly on their respective median values within each cohort. To identify independent prognostic factors, multivariate Cox regression models were employed, adjusting for covariates including gender, age, and TNM stage. Correlations were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis. Unless otherwise specified, a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

Identification and Characterization of APA Events in GC

In this study, we characterized the landscape of APA in GC by analyzing the PDUI derived from RNA‐seq data of 61 patients from the AhGC cohort. In total, we identified 9769 APA events with 70.01% (n = 6847) validated in TC3A (Figure 1A). Specifically, these events included 740 cases of 3′UTR lengthening and 408 cases of shortening (Figure 1B,C), the majority of which exhibited length deviations exceeding 500 bp compared to normal tissues (Figure 1D). Functional enrichment analyses revealed that these APA‐altered genes were enriched in multiple tumor‐related pathways, including p53 signaling and Rho protein signal transduction (Figure 1E). Furthermore, an analysis of PDUI shifts from median normal levels successfully identified two distinct APA patterns (Figure 1F). Subsequent CIBERSORT analysis underscored unique immune landscapes within these subgroups, most notably marked by a significant differential infiltration of Natural Killer (NK) cells (Figure 1G). Collectively, we established a comprehensive APA landscape in GC, elucidating the link between significant 3′UTR alterations and key oncogenic signaling pathways, and identifying distinct patient subgroups characterized by unique immune microenvironments.

Aberrant m6A Methylation Patterns in GC

MeRIP‐seq profiling characterized the typical m6A peaks around stop codons and 3′UTRs (Figure 2A), while genomic annotation revealed a marked abundance of m6A in intronic regions (Tumor: 68.16%; Normal: 67.96%) (Figure 2B). This distinct intronic pattern suggests the involvement of m6A in early pre‐mRNA processing, including splicing and APA. Additionally, we identified 3117 differentially methylated peaks (DMPs) (p < 0.05), comprising 1700 hypermethylated and 1417 hypomethylated peaks (Figure 2C). These DMPs mapped to 2040 genes, which were significantly enriched in RNA splicing and critical oncogenic signaling pathways, notably Ras, Rap1, and TNF signaling (Figure 2D). These findings underscore the multifaceted role of m6A dysregulation in driving GC tumorigenesis.

Integrative Analysis Identifies Prognostic Genes Driven by Concurrent m6A and APA Events

We investigated the potential mechanisms linking m6A and APA by integrating their respective profiles, identifying 165 genes characterized by concurrent alterations in both 3′UTR length and methylation status (Figure 3A, Table S3). These genes predominantly participate in cell‐matrix interactions and central oncogenic signaling, such as the NF‐κB, Hippo, and small GTPase‐mediated pathways, highlighting their critical roles in GC progression (Figure 3B). We further assessed the correlation between mRNA expression levels and PDUI values for these candidate genes. Our analysis showed that 78 genes exhibited significant correlations (p < 0.05), indicating a potential functional link between 3′UTR usage patterns and transcript abundance (Table S4). Subsequently, multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis within the TCGA‐STAD cohort identified the PDUI values of SERPINE1 (HR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.51–0.98, p = 3.61 × 10−2) and ZNF480 (HR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.51–0.99, p = 4.24 × 10−2) as robust, independent prognostic factors (Figure S1A).

SERPINE1 is a Prognostic Biomarker Under the Regulation of m6A and APA in GC

Although both genes exhibited prognostic value at the PDUI level, only SERPINE1 mRNA overexpression significantly predicted poor survival (HR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.10–1.39, p = 2.33 × 10−3), and the finding was successfully validated via the KM‐plotter database and another independent cohort (GSE62254) (Figure 3C,D and Figure S1B). Moreover, a significant negative correlation was observed between its PDUI value and mRNA expression level (r = −0.16, p = 1.50 × 10−3) (Figure 3E). Consistent upregulation of SERPINE1 in gastric tumors was confirmed across multiple independent datasets, including TCGA‐STAD, GSE63089, GSE65801, and GSE54129 (Figure 3F and Figure S1C).

Within our in‐house GC cohort, elevated plasma SERPINE1 protein levels were associated with both worse OS (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.09–1.31, p = 9.65 × 10−5) and DSS (HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.05–1.29, p = 2.01 × 10−3) (Figure 4A,B). Subgroup analyses demonstrated that this association was significant only in advanced‐stage patients, with an HR of 1.56 (p = 1.13 × 10−2) (Figure S2). Furthermore, single‐cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that SERPINE1 expression was predominantly enriched in fibroblasts. Subsequent sub‐clustering of fibroblast populations demonstrated that high SERPINE1 expression was specifically concentrated in cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) (Figures S3 and S4).

CSTF2 And IGF2BP1 Cooperatively Influence 3′UTR Selection in SERPINE1 Transcripts

To characterize the APA‐mediated regulation of SERPINE1, 3′‐RACE assays were conducted in AGS and HGC‐27 cells, confirming the presence of SERPINE1 transcript isoforms with distinct 3′UTR lengths (Figure 4C). Computational analysis using the POSTAR3 database identified the APA regulator CSTF2 and the m6A reader IGF2BP1 as potential RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs) targeting SERPINE1 mRNA, with notable overlap in their binding sites (Figure 4D).

Next, to investigate their functional relationship, we performed siRNA‐mediated knockdown of CSTF2 and IGF2BP1 (Figure S5). Western blot analysis revealed that the knockdown of either factor did not significantly alter the protein level of the other (Figure 4E), suggesting a lack of mutual regulation at the expression level. Moreover, Co‐IP assays confirmed a direct physical interaction between CSTF2 and IGF2BP1 in the HGC‐27 cell line (Figure 4F).

Finally, RT‐qPCR analysis was employed to evaluate the usage of long and short SERPINE1 transcripts following individual or simultaneous knockdown (Figure 4G). The results demonstrated that silencing either CSTF2 or IGF2BP1 shifted the preference toward the long SERPINE1 transcript, with simultaneous knockdown exerting the most pronounced effect. Consistently, western blot analysis revealed that the knockdown of CSTF2 or IGF2BP1 resulted in a concomitant reduction in SERPINE1 protein expression (Figure 5A).

Functional Characterization of SERPINE1 3′UTR Isoforms in GC Progression

The functional contributions of SERPINE1 3′UTR isoforms were elucidated by selectively silencing the long or short transcripts in AGS and HGC‐27 cells. Transwell assays revealed that the knockdown of either isoform significantly impaired the migratory and invasive potential of GC cells compared to control groups, with the short‐transcript knockdown producing a more robust inhibitory response (Figure 5B). These findings were corroborated by colony formation assays, in which downregulation of either isoform markedly curtailed clonogenic capacity (Figure 5C). Moreover, CCK‐8 proliferation assays demonstrated that while silencing either variant hindered cell growth, the suppressive effect was notably more pronounced upon targeting the short isoform (Figure 5D), consistent with the observations from the migration and invasion assays.

Based on these findings, we propose that CSTF2 and IGF2BP1 cooperatively shorten the SERPINE1 3′UTR, leading to the accumulation of a truncated isoform that promotes GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (Figure 6). The graphical abstract is shown as Figure 7.

Discussion

By integrating MeRIP‐seq with APA profiling, we identified 165 genes with concurrent alterations in 3′UTR length and m6A methylation. Among these, SERPINE1 was prioritized as a prognostic biomarker in GC. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that the APA factor CSTF2 physically interacts with the m6A reader IGF2BP1 to cooperatively regulate SERPINE1 3′UTR selection. Functional assays further revealed that the short 3′UTR isoform of SERPINE1 exerts a more pronounced pro‐tumorigenic effect than its long isoform. Together, these findings map APA dysregulation in GC and establish SERPINE1 as a key prognostic factor, with its short isoform representing a mechanistic rationale and therapeutic candidate.

As a pivotal serine protease inhibitor, SERPINE1 regulates the plasminogen activation system by inhibiting the activities of PLAT and PLAU [22]. This inhibitory mechanism is essential to fibrinolysis regulation and cell‐matrix dynamics [23]. Beyond its canonical roles, SERPINE1 promotes cell migration via both protease‐dependent and protease‐independent pathways [24, 25]. In malignancy, SERPINE1 acts as a multifaceted orchestrator of tumor progression, driving proliferation, migration, and EMT [26, 27, 28]. In our study, SERPINE1 plasma protein levels closely correlated with poor clinical prognosis in GC patients. In subsequent subgroup analyses, while high SERPINE1 expression did not show a statistically significant trend of decreased survival in Stage I–II patients, it became a highly significant independent predictor of poor overall survival in the Stage III–IV group. This phenomenon also suggests that SERPINE1 may exert its oncogenic role by facilitating tumor migration and invasion in late‐stage gastric cancer. Crucially, our single‐cell transcriptomic analysis uncovered a preferential enrichment of SERPINE1 within CAFs. This aligns with evidence that CAF‐derived SERPINE1 reshapes the stroma by inducing M2 macrophage polarization and endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition, thereby facilitating metastasis [29].

Furthermore, SERPINE1 serves as a cornerstone of therapy resistance, with its elevation in the tumor niche fostering chemoresistance and radioresistance [30, 31]. However, the intrinsic regulatory mechanisms governing SERPINE1 overactivation have remained elusive. Our study fills this gap by demonstrating that in the GC landscape, SERPINE1 expression is not solely driven by transcriptional upregulation but is finely tuned by an APA‐m6A synergistic axis. Specifically, the high levels of CSTF2 and IGF2BP1 in GC promote the formation of the short SERPINE1 3′UTR isoform, which we have proven to be the more potent driver of malignancy compared to its long counterpart. Taken together, these findings underscore the clinical significance of SERPINE1 as a pivotal biomarker.

APA serves as a fundamental post‐transcriptional regulator in diverse physiological processes; it is frequently co‐opted in malignancy to drive oncogenesis. Dysregulated APA events reconfigure the expression profiles of target genes by modulating mRNA stability, translational efficiency, and protein subcellular localization [10, 32, 33]. While certain APA shifts represent pan‐cancer signatures, growing evidence highlights their profound tumor‐type specificity; indeed, a multitude of cancer‐specific APA alterations have been identified that remain virtually absent in adjacent normal tissues [34, 35]. Consistent with this specificity, our observation that differentially APA‐regulated genes are significantly enriched in core oncogenic pathways and correlate with immune infiltration patterns underscores the multifaceted role of APA in orchestrating the GC landscape. Crucially, this specificity is not governed by APA factors alone but rather mediated through a sophisticated crosstalk with the epitranscriptomic machinery. As a key driver of tumor progression, m6A methylation is known to functionally interface with APA to coordinate gene expression [36, 37]. Our findings provide new evidence for this synergistic model; specifically, we observed that the simultaneous silencing of the APA factor CSTF2 and the m6A reader IGF2BP1 elicited the most pronounced shift toward the long SERPINE1 transcript. This cooperation may represent a prevalent mechanism by which GC cells achieve exquisite control over gene expression.

Nevertheless, there are several limitations in our study that should be acknowledged. First, functional experiments were restricted to in vitro GC cell lines without in vivo confirmation, while the exact molecular mechanisms and crosstalk explaining the short SERPINE1 isoform's heightened oncogenicity remain poorly understood. Additionally, our APA and m6A profiling was limited to a small cohort of bulk tumor tissues, obscuring cell‐type‐specific regulatory dynamics and reducing overall epigenomic representation and peak detection sensitivity. Although single‐cell data suggested SERPINE1 enrichment in CAFs, the precise contribution of distinct cell populations to APA regulation remains to be resolved using spatial transcriptomics or single‐cell APA analysis.

In summary, this study reveals widespread APA dysregulation in GC and its functional crosstalk with m6A modification. Specifically, we demonstrated that CSTF2 and IGF2BP1 synergistically drive SERPINE1 3′UTR shortening to enhance GC malignancy, with the short isoform promoting tumor progression more potently than the long isoform. Collectively, these findings highlight SERPINE1 as a promising prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target for GC.
