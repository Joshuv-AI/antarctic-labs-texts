Public views of forensic science: An intersection of science and policing?
Authors: Naomi Kaplan-Damary, Tal Jonathan-Zamir, Gali Perry, Eran Itskovich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Consistent with the growing interest in public attitudes toward forensic science, the present study focuses on the potential antecedents of trust in forensics. Collecting and analyzing forensic evidence involves scientific methods, and thus trust in science as a whole may influence trust in forensics. At the same time, because the police are responsible for collecting and analyzing forensic evidence, public trust in the police may affect perceptions of forensic science. These hypothesized relationships align with theories of generalized trust, which suggest that individuals apply broad trust orientations across different authorities and social institutions. To test the role of these views as antecedents of trust in forensics, 1,342 Israelis were surveyed about their views of forensic science, the police, and science as a whole. A cluster analysis identified three groups of people: those expressing high levels of trust in all three institutions; those expressing low trust in all three; and those expressing high trust in science and forensics but low trust in the police. The clusters were associated with several socio-demographic characteristics. Moreover, correlations reveal that trust in forensics is associated with trust in science and, to a lesser extent, with trust in the police, and may be embedded within broader patterns of institutional trust.

Introduction

Following a century in which forensic science was generally accepted as a reliable and trustworthy way of establishing guilt or innocence in legal proceedings, the early 2000s witnessed a radical change [1]. Forensic methods came under growing scrutiny, with concerns raised about carelessness, cognitive bias, and incompetence. Studies commissioned by the National Academy of Sciences [2] and the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology [3] voiced numerous concerns over the scientific foundations of many forensic disciplines.

In light of these developments, scholars (e.g., Refs. [[4], [5]]) have expressed interest in the way forensic evidence is perceived and understood by the general public: is it still considered trustworthy and reliable? Alternatively, does the public recognize the significant limitations of forensic evidence? These views are considered important due to the inherent connection between forensic science and the police (the police gather forensic evidence and build criminal cases; e.g., Ref. [6]), and are thus linked to the broader issue of public trust in the police [[7], [8], [9]]. Moreover, inaccurate perceptions of forensic evidence may influence jury decisions (e.g., Ref. [10]), and/or give rise to significant public pressure on the criminal justice system, especially in high profile cases [11].

In turn, a growing body of research reveals considerable variation in individuals’ trust in the ability of forensic science to reliably identify the perpetrator of a crime. This was found for forensic science as a whole, as well as for specific forensic disciplines, (e.g., Refs. [[4], [5], [12], [13], [14]]). At the same time, the sources of this variation remain unclear. The present work seeks to address this gap, and illuminate the factors that influence (or at the very least are correlated with) public trust in forensic science. As the analysis of forensic evidence involves the use of scientific methods, it is reasonable to expect that trust in science as a whole impacts trust in forensics (e.g., Ref. [15]). At the same time, since forensic evidence is collected and analyzed by the police, and is, in essence, a form of police service, trust in the police (e.g., Ref. [[16], [17], [18]]) may also influence trust in forensic evidence. Notably, these expected relationships are consistent with theories arguing that trust is projected and generalized across authorities and social institutions (e.g., Ref. [19,20]).

We begin by arguing for the importance of public views of forensic science. We then present the hypotheses of the present study: trust in forensic evidence is associated with trust in science as a whole, as well as with trust in the police. We continue with a description of our methodology—a community survey carried out in Israel—and our analysis, which reveals that while trust in forensics is indeed associated with both types of trust (in science and in the police), there are three clusters of people, each demonstrating a different distribution of these three main variables. These clusters are also associated with numerous socio-demographic characteristics. We conclude that public trust in forensic science does not stand alone, but tends to reflect broader orientations toward science and the police, as well as socio-demographic differences.

Public views of forensic evidence

Why are public views of forensic evidence important?

Kaplan-Damary et al. [5] discuss several reasons why public attitudes toward forensic science matter, and are thus an important topic of research. First, they argue that views of forensic science may well be associated with the public image of the police, and specifically with its legitimacy, because the (perceived) quality of the collection, storage, analysis, and implementation of forensic evidence in criminal investigations reflects one aspect of (perceived) police effectiveness, which is an important antecedent of police legitimacy (although secondary in its importance to procedural justice; for more information on the concept of “police legitimacy” see Refs. [7,[21], [22], [23]]).

Second, public perceptions of forensic science can play an important role in jury decision-making. Cognitive models of juror decision-making suggest that jurors’ knowledge, expectations and attitudes influence how they evaluate the evidence presented to them [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]], which, in turn, affects their verdicts [10]. Studies show that pre-trial expectations regarding forensic science can impact verdicts [29], making it crucial to understand these perceptions as part of broader efforts to reduce bias in criminal trials [4].

Finally, even in bench trials or non-jury systems, public sentiment can influence legal outcomes [11,30]. High-profile cases, such as Joan Little's 1974 acquittal driven by public support [31,32], or, more recently, the 2018 debate over reopening the Roman Zadorov case in Israel [33], illustrate the power of public opinion in shaping judicial outcomes. These dynamics can be viewed in the context of penal populism, a concept depicting the adoption of criminal justice policies based on political appeal rather than actual effectiveness [34]. For example, studies indicate that in the U.S., public demand for harsher punishments has influenced judicial decisions [35], underscoring the impact of public sentiments on the processes and outcomes of the criminal justice system.

What do we know about public views of forensic evidence?

One strand of research in the area of public attitudes toward forensic evidence has focused on the so-called “CSI effect” [36,37]. This term, inspired by the television series “CSI” and similar crime fiction dramas, reflects the concern that idealized portrayals of forensic evidence in these series may create unrealistic expectations regarding its accuracy and infallibility. It has been argued that this effect places pressure on prosecutors to provide forensic proof of guilt (“no forensic evidence = not guilty”), while at the same time disadvantaging defendants when forensic evidence is presented, as jurors may assume that any such evidence is conclusive (“forensic evidence = guilt”). Despite widespread interest in the CSI effect, studies have not established a consistent link between exposure to forensic crime drama and juror decision-making (e.g., Ref. [29,37]), with recent research finding no evidence for such effect [38,39].

Some studies have gone beyond the potential impact of the media to explore broader public perceptions of forensic science. Smith and Bull [13] found that the British public places only moderate trust in forensic science, rating it just below 2.5 on a 5-point scale. Ribeiro et al. [12] investigated how people perceive the likelihood of errors and the degree of human judgment involved at various stages of the forensic analysis, including evidence collection, storage, testing, analysis, reporting, and presentation in court. Their findings indicate that forensic work is widely seen as involving substantial human discretion and prone to error. Building on Ribeiro et al. [12], Kaplan et al. [4] found that participants held somewhat pessimistic views of forensic investigations, estimating that errors could occur in roughly half of all forensic procedures (see Ref. [5] for a comprehensive review).

More recently, Kaplan-Damary et al. [5] explored public confidence in forensic science, revealing mixed views: strong beliefs were expressed in the ability of forensic science as a whole, as well as of specific forensic disciplines, to reliably identify perpetrators. At the same time, trust declined when respondents were asked to consider specific stages of the forensic process. Furthermore, while the favorable evaluations of DNA and fingerprint analysis correspond well with systematic, objective assessments of these forensic disciplines, respondents tended to overestimate the reliability of bite mark and hair analysis, both of which have been deemed insufficient by the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology [3], and rated them as more trustworthy than shoeprint analysis which “lacks sufficient support” (but is not discredited) according to PCAST.

These studies reveal considerable variation in the public's trust in the ability of forensic science as a whole, as well as of specific forensic disciplines, to reliably identify the perpetrator of a crime. At the same time, to the best of our knowledge, no study to date has sought to investigate the sources of this variation—the factors that influence public views of forensic evidence. The present study begins to address this gap by investigating the correlates of trust in forensic science. Given that forensic science is both a scientific discipline and a policing practice, as detailed in the next section, our “immediate suspects” were trust in science and trust in the police.

Potential antecedents of public trust in forensic evidence

Trust in science as an antecedent of trust in forensic evidence

Research on public attitudes toward science illuminates the critical role of these attitudes in shaping societal engagement with scientific knowledge. Durant et al. [40] argue that science represents one of humanity's greatest cultural achievements, making public understanding of science an essential component of cultural literacy. They further argue that as scientific advancements increasingly influence various aspects of daily life, individuals must possess a foundational understanding of science to make informed decisions. They note that public attitudes toward science are also significant in the context of democratic governance, where policy decisions on issues such as climate change and public health rely on scientific expertise. An informed public can ensure that such policies align with both scientific consensus and societal values.

Accordingly, skepticism or rejection of scientific findings can lead to significant societal consequences [41]. Controversial issues such as climate change, vaccination, and genetic engineering rely on scientific research, yet resistance to scientific consensus in these areas poses substantial risks. For example, although vaccines are among the most effective tools available for preventing infectious diseases, individuals who distrust the science or the scientists behind them often perceive vaccines as dangerous and may expose themselves or their loved ones to unnecessary health risks [42]. Similarly, climate change denial continues to hinder global policy efforts to address environmental sustainability [43].

Rutjens et al. [44] provide an integration of recent research on how people evaluate science, and note that as science continues to advance, attitudes toward it appear to have become increasingly divided: while some place their trust in science, others consistently refuse and disregard scientific evidence. They highlight that attitudes toward science are not solely driven by knowledge, but also by cognitive ability, political ideology, religious beliefs, and moral concerns. These scholars also emphasize that different domains of science provoke different responses, with climate science, vaccines, and genetic engineering eliciting varying degrees of skepticism across ideological and cultural groups.

As noted above, trust in science may influence evaluations of science-based practices. A notable example is the study by Maciuszek et al. [15], which found that individuals with pro-vaccine attitudes were also more likely to hold generally pro-science views. Therefore, as forensic analysis relies on scientific principles, we hypothesize that trust in science is correlated with trust in forensic science.

Trust in the police as an antecedent of trust in forensic evidence

Public trust in the police is arguably one of the most researched areas in policing in recent decades, often as a component of the broader concept of “police legitimacy” (see reviews by Ref. [7,[21], [22], [23],[45], [46], [47], [48]]). “Trust” represents an emotional dimension in a relationship between two parties, and in the context of policing refers to the bond between citizens and the police. The degree of trust placed in the police reflects citizens' subjective belief that officers will act in ways that are favorable and desirable [49]. As noted by Tyler and Huo [50], trust in an individual's motives or character pertains to internal, unseen qualities that are inferred from their observable behavior.

In democratic societies, where citizens are policed by consent and are the source of police powers, broad trust in the police is an important normative standard (e.g., Ref. [51]). But on the more practical level, trust in the police is critical due to its influence on citizens’ behavior through the expectations it establishes. When citizens trust the police, they anticipate that officers will uphold key values such as protecting rights, combating crime, and adhering to the law. These expectations, in turn, foster broad cooperation with the police, even in situations where there are no immediate, tangible benefits for doing so (e.g., Ref. [23,52]).

Research on trust in the police (independently or as part of “police legitimacy”) generally assumes that citizens form this macro-level evaluation based on micro-level assessments of how the police perform concrete tasks (see review by Ref. [53]). Analyses predicting trust in the police consistently find that it develops from both outcome-based evaluations (the instrumental outcomes the police deliver—the “what”) and process-based evaluations (the fairness of police procedures—the “how”), with the latter typically playing a more critical role (e.g., Ref. [7,23]).

Since forensic evidence is identified, collected, stored, analyzed and used as evidence in criminal cases by the police, effectively making it a form of police service, our second hypothesis suggests that trust in the police may shape trust in forensic evidence. This assumption is supported by the “halo effect” [54,55], according to which the positive or negative qualities of a certain object or body are applied to its other traits. Thus, trust in the police may lead one to view forensic evidence as trustworthy, and vice versa.

Generalized trust and institutional confidence

The third hypothesis underlying the present study is that trust in all three institutions—science, police, and forensic science—are interrelated, based on the assumption that individuals who have a general tendency to trust will likely trust all three institutions (and vice versa). This general trust hypothesis is rooted in “relational justice schemas” [19] which refer to cognitive frameworks that individuals develop about fairness and justice, based on early interactions with authority figures, such as parents and teachers. In turn, these early schemas are generalized, and shape how they interpret and trust other social actors. For example, Pickett et al. [19] find that perceptions of police procedural justice extend beyond specific encounters, affecting perceptions of fairness in broader social contexts. Thus, individuals who develop trust in authority figures during childhood may apply this generalized schema to other authorities; in our context—the police, science, and forensics.

Building on Rothstein & Stolle [56], Van Craen & Skogan [20] make similar arguments. They claim that judges and police officers play a critical role in shaping public perceptions of societal moral standards. Their behavior serves as a signal to the public regarding the prevailing ethical norms in society. When police officers and judges act in a trustworthy manner, citizens extrapolate this trust to other societal actors, reinforcing broader social cohesion. This suggests that trust in law enforcement is not merely a product of direct/vicarious interactions with police officers, but also of the general view that authority figures adhere to appropriate ethical standards. It is plausible to extend this argument to scientists, who, like police officers and judges, function as authority figures in society. If scientists are perceived as trustworthy, this may influence trust in law enforcement and in forensic science (and vice versa). Similarly, public confidence in law enforcement and forensic science may shape perceptions of trust in science, creating a reinforcing cycle of generalized trust.

In sum, trust in forensic science, trust in the police, and trust in science are expected to be interrelated. Whether trust (or distrust) originates from early socialization [19] or is reinforced by institutional behavior [20], there is reason to believe that individuals who trust either science, the police, or forensics, are more likely to also trust the other two institutions, and vice versa.

Research design and methods

Study context

A few words should be noted regarding Israel as the site of the present study. First, with regard to the way forensic evidence is collected and analyzed, the Israel Police Division of Identification and Forensic Science (DIFS) is responsible for most forensic investigations across the country (other agencies may handle cases in specific areas, such as the National Center of Forensic Medicine1 and the Clinical Toxicology and Pharmacology Laboratory2; [57]). DIFS is a globally recognized institution and an active member of the European Network of Forensic Science Institutes (ENFSI).

With regard to the Israel Police (IP) more generally, as noted by Jonathan-Zamir and Harpaz [58], the IP is a national and centralized police agency with broad responsibilities, including internal security. This sets it apart from the more decentralized and locally governed police systems common in North America and in some European countries. At the same time, the IP shares important features with other democratic police agencies. Israeli police officers operate under legal constraints similar to those common in other Western democracies, including clear restrictions on the use of force, detention, and arrest, and they are required to provide equal, non-discriminatory treatment. The daily responsibilities of local police stations in Israel, such as handling crime, managing traffic, and serving the public, closely resemble those of police agencies in other democratic societies. In addition, over the years, the IP has incorporated international policing strategies such as community policing, Compstat, and hot spots policing. Public expectations regarding procedural fairness are also similar to those in the United States and Europe.

Despite these similarities, public trust remains a major challenge for the IP. According to the 2022 (the year of our study) annual survey conducted by the Israel Democracy Institute,3 only 35% of Jewish respondents and 17% of Arab respondents reported trusting the police [59]. The European Social Survey of 2011 reveals that of 20 European countries, Israel, Bulgaria and the Russian Federation had the least favorable views regarding police treatment of the population [60].

Another relevant aspect of the Israeli context concerns the judicial system. There are no juries in Israel; professionally trained judges preside over bench trials and handle all aspects of the administration of justice [61]. Despite this fact, public attitudes toward forensic evidence in such legal frameworks may still exert significant pressure on the criminal justice system [11,30]. A notable example is the high-profile debate surrounding the reopening of the Zadorov murder trial in 2018. In response to growing public pressure, the Israel State Attorney emphasized that prosecutorial decisions would be guided solely by the evidence, not by public sentiment [33]. The very need to issue such a statement underscores the potential influence of public opinion, even in a system without juries.

[13,14]; e.g., Every crime can be solved with forensic science), about their trust in the police (based on [[16], [17], [18],65]; e.g., Police officers are often dishonest), and about their trust in science in general (based on [66]; e.g., Science is the best way to improve people's lives). Analytical strategy

To assess the underlying structure of the attitudes measured, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed. As shown in Table 2, three expected factors were identified: trust in forensic science, trust in science (generally), and trust in the police. Then, three indices were constructed by averaging the survey items in each factor (see Table 2): “Trust in the police” (M = 2.77, SE = 0.03, range: 1–5, Cronbach's Alpha = 0.81), “Trust in science” (M = 3.52, SE = 0.02, range: 1–5, Cronbach's Alpha = 0.75), and “Trust in forensics” (M = 3.33, SE = 0.02, range: 1–5, Cronbach's Alpha = 0.77). The PCA and Cronbach's Alpha estimates confirmed the discriminant validity and internal consistency of the scales.

Second, cluster analysis (which groups objects so that those within the same cluster are more similar to each other than to those in other clusters) was conducted. Specifically, a hierarchical cluster analysis was performed to identify the optimal number of clusters, followed by k-means clustering to assign observations to clusters [67,68].

Subsequently, a multinomial regression model [69] was used to investigate how socio-demographic variables influence the probability of belonging to any particular cluster (i.e., in this model the dependent variable is the cluster allocation and the independent variables are the socio-demographic characteristics).

This analytic strategy was chosen because there does not appear to be a unidirectional relationship between the three main variables of interest: it is possible that public trust in the police and/or trust in science influence trust in forensics, but in line with the literature reviewed above, a reverse hypothesis could also be posited. Thus, this appears to be a case of simultaneous equations, in which some of the explanatory variables are jointly determined with the dependent variable, rather than the “classic” case in which the dependent variable is solely a function of independent variables. This case of simultaneity poses challenges for the estimation of the regression coefficients because the Gauss-Markov assumption of strict exogeneity of the regressors is violated [70]. To address this, our main approach relies on cluster analysis, which makes no assumption regarding the relationship between the variables [68]. At the same time, we also estimate an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression model, which allows us to examine the associations between trust in forensics and the other two forms of trust, while explicitly noting its limitations. This analysis is therefore presented not as evidence of causal direction, but as a complementary way to illustrate the robustness of the observed associations.

Findings

Three clusters (N = 542, 395, 371) were identified on the basis of the three indices (trust in the police, trust in science, trust in forensics). Cluster 3 is characterized by relatively high levels of trust in science and in forensics, but relatively low trust in the police, with mean levels of 3.59, 3.76 and 2.05 respectively. It is evident that Clusters 1 and 3 are characterized by relatively high levels of trust in science and in forensics, while Cluster 2 is characterized by relatively low levels of trust in both variables. This finding, along with the corresponding Pearson correlation of 0.35 (p ≤ 0.05), suggests that there is a moderate relationship between these variables, which supports our first hypothesis. It is also apparent that in Clusters 1 and 2, there are similar mean levels of trust in the police and in forensics, but in Cluster 3, there are relatively high levels of trust in forensics and low levels of trust in the police. This finding, along with a Pearson correlation of 0.18 (p ≤ 0.05), indicates that, as hypothesized, there is a modest relationship between trust in forensics and trust in the police.

Clusters 1 and 2, which reflect similar levels of trust across all three variables, may be explained by theories of generalized trust reviewed above [19,20]. This pattern of trust supports the third hypothesis of the study and reflects a broader tendency to evaluate different institutions/authorities through a common lens. Another explanation may involve a halo effect [54,55], in which positive or negative perceptions of one domain influence perceptions of another.

Following the identification of the clusters, the question becomes what characterizes the individuals in each cluster? We tested this question using a multinomial regression model in which the effect of socio-demographic characteristics on the probability of belonging to clusters is investigated. Discussion

Public attitudes toward forensic science are meaningful because they may influence the popular legitimacy of the police and trust in the criminal justice system more generally. They may also shape jury decision-making and pressure on the judicial system, which could, in turn, impact judicial decisions and criminal justice policies. Research has shown substantial variation in public trust in forensic science, both in terms of its overall reliability and in terms of the ability of specific forensic disciplines to accurately identify perpetrators. Thus, an important question becomes how public attitudes toward forensic evidence develop. Accordingly, the present study sought to explore potential sources of variation in trust in forensics. Given that forensic science exists at the intersection of scientific inquiry and law enforcement, we examined trust in both science and the police as potential antecedents of trust in forensics.

On the basis of the three indices (trust in the police, trust in science, trust in forensics), three clusters were identified. Cluster 1 is characterized by relatively high levels of trust in the police, science, and forensics; Cluster 2 by low levels of trust in all three institutions, and Cluster 3 by relatively high levels of trust in forensics and in science, but relatively low levels of trust in the police. The correlations between the indices suggest that trust in forensics is indeed associated with trust in science and, to a lesser extent, with trust in the police, a pattern also reflected in the supplementary OLS regression. These findings are consistent with propositions of generalized trust [19,20], which suggest that individuals who tend to trust a particular authority are more likely to trust other authorities and public institutions. They may also imply that trust in forensic science is influenced by trust in science as a whole and trust in the police, thereby providing a partial answer to the question of what underlies public attitudes toward forensic science.

In addition, since forensic science is a form of police service, the relationship observed between trust in forensic science and trust in the police is in line with the large corpus on police legitimacy, which identifies police performance/effectiveness as an important antecedent of legitimacy. At the same time, the limited magnitude of the correlation is also in line with this literature, since other antecedents of legitimacy (procedural justice) were identified as a stronger predictor of legitimacy (see reviews by Ref. [7,21,22,45,46,48]).

Cluster 3, characterized by high levels of trust in both forensic science and science more generally, but low levels of trust in the police, may reflect a perception of forensic science as an objective and epistemically neutral field, distinct from law enforcement. For individuals in this cluster, trust in forensics may stem from a belief in its potential to uncover the truth and promote justice [71], even in the face of a lack of trust in the police. This configuration may arise from direct or vicarious experiences of discrimination, marginalization, or perceived police misconduct, leading to skepticism about the trustworthiness of law enforcement [50], while maintaining confidence in the perceived objectivity and impartiality of science, particularly when it is viewed as a safeguard against wrongful convictions or abuse of power [72].

The broader patterns of trust, as reflected in the identified clusters, raise the question of who is most likely to exhibit them. The analysis reveals that compared to individuals in Cluster 2 (characterized by low trust across all domains), older people, as well as those who identify as secular, traditional, or modern Orthodox (compared to ultra-Orthodox), are more likely to belong to the high-trust cluster (Cluster 1). A higher likelihood of membership in Cluster 1 is also associated with having completed high school or a non-academic post-high school program (compared to up to 10 years of education), and with being separated or divorced rather than single. Compared to Cluster 2, older individuals are also more likely to belong to Cluster 3, which reflects high trust in science and forensics but low trust in the police.

This association between age and institutional trust aligns with previous research on age-related differences in trust, showing that older individuals tend to express greater trust in general [73]. The lower likelihood of ultra-Orthodox individuals to belong to the high-trust cluster likely emanates from their world view that “real wisdom lies not in science and technology but in the study of Torah and the religious achievements” ([74], p. 37), an approach that also distances this population from mainstream society. These findings are consistent with research on trust among minority groups, which indicates that members of these groups report substantially less trust [75,76]. The fact that individuals with intermediate levels of education were more likely to belong to the high-trust cluster aligns with studies showing that trust tends to increase with education [77]. However, no such pattern was found for those with a bachelor's or more advanced degrees, consistent with research showing that the education-trust link varies across institutional contexts [77]. The finding that separated or divorced individuals were more likely to belong to the high-trust cluster raises intriguing questions that to the best of our knowledge have not yet been investigated. One possible explanation is that exiting complex personal relationships may increase reliance on, or openness to, formal institutions as sources of order and support, particularly when legal proceedings are involved.

To trust or not to trust?

While our empirical analyses focused on trust in science and trust in the police as antecedents of trust in forensics, the topic itself raises a broader, normative question: what level of public trust in forensic science should be considered “desirable?” Clearly, this question cannot be answered with the present data, but it is central to ongoing debates about science, policing, and their role within the criminal justice system.

From a normative standpoint, more trust in forensic science is not necessarily better. Excessive trust in forensics may amplify the persuasive power of methods that lack scientific validity, leading to miscarriages of justice (e.g., the CSI effect; [36,37,78]). Conversely, insufficient trust—even in well-established methods such as DNA—may hinder accurate fact-finding and just outcomes [72]. Thus, the optimal stance appears to be one of calibrated trust: confidence that is proportionate to the strength of the scientific evidence [2,3].

Interestingly, studies suggest that this is often not the case: while DNA and fingerprint analysis, both regarded by PCAST as foundationally valid, are generally viewed as trustworthy, there is a much weaker correlation between objective data and public assessments of other forensic methods [[4], [5]]. For example, dental and hair analysis are perceived to be trustworthy despite serious objective reservations, while firearm, toolmark and shoeprint analysis are trusted (moderately) despite the fact that their accuracy and reliability remain a subject of ongoing research [[3], [4], [5]]. As objective evaluations of forensic techniques continue to develop, future research should examine how these methods are perceived and trusted by the public, and work to understand and address the discrepancies that may arise.

Science education can play an important role in fostering more informed attitudes toward forensic science. Trust in forensics can be seen as an extension of trust in science more broadly, shaped by factors such as understanding of scientific processes [44,79]. If science education can shape general attitudes toward science [80,81], it may also influence how people perceive forensic evidence and how they weigh it in legal contexts [10]. Taken together, these insights highlight the need to foster both sustained science education and accessible public outreach. Promoting public understanding of forensic science requires clear and transparent communication about which forensic methods are supported by strong scientific evidence and which remain under review. Additionally, public educational efforts should aim to develop “critical consumers of science” [82], who can engage with forensic claims thoughtfully, ask questions and apply judgment, rather than accepting or rejecting them unquestioningly.

Implications and limitations

The present study also points to implications for the criminal justice system more broadly. Given that trust in forensic evidence may be associated with factors unrelated to the case at hand, and that such evidence may carry significant weight in court proceedings, it is crucial that judges, prosecutors, defense attorneys and juries recognize both the risks of overreliance on weak forensic methods and the missed opportunities that stem from undervaluing scientifically sound evidence. Expert witnesses should therefore be required to present not only their conclusions, but also the scientific limitations and uncertainty associated with their methods (e.g., Ref. [2], p. 22). Courts may need to take a more active role in guiding jurors’ interpretation of forensic testimony. This could involve pretrial education, or more detailed jury instructions regarding the scientific status of different forensic techniques (e.g., Ref. [2], p. 237).

Before concluding, several limitations of the present study should be noted. First, our sample represents the adult Jewish population in Israel, as national and religious minorities are, unfortunately, significantly underrepresented in online survey platforms. This limitation, along with the fact that our study is based on Israeli panelists, suggests that the findings may differ in other countries or populations. Additionally, as a cross-sectional study, it was not designed to establish causal relationships, but rather to identify associations, leaving questions about causality and directionality for future research.

Conclusions

Trust in forensics is related to both trust in science and, to a lesser extent, to trust in the police. It appears to be associated not only with perceptions of its scientific quality, but also with general trust in related institutions. Patterns of institutional trust were associated with several socio-demographic characteristics, including age, religious affiliation, education, and marital status. These findings highlight the importance of ensuring that public trust in forensic science is grounded in the actual scientific strength of the methods being used. Achieving this requires continued efforts by the forensic science community to strengthen and assess the scientific foundations of its methods, along with clear communication about their validity. It is also necessary for public education to promote scientific literacy and critical thinking, enabling individuals to evaluate forensic evidence thoughtfully and responsibly.
