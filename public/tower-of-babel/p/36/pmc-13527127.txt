Community-aware biased random walks for community detection in attribute networks
Authors: Jin Zhang, Hailu Yang, Jun Li, Qi Zhen, Shuo Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunity detection aims to identify a collection of closely connected nodes in a network, which is an important task in network analysis. In recent years, network embedding techniques based on random walks have been widely applied to community detection tasks. However, such methods suffer from numerous issues, such as ignoring node attributes and requiring the tedious process of manually setting random walk parameters. To address these issues, this paper proposes a network embedding method based on community-aware biased random walk for community detection in attributed networks. The method jointly leverages both the topological similarity and attribute similarity of nodes to guide the random walk process. When the random walk reaches the community boundary, the walker reduces the ambiguity of communities by enhancing the probability of walking toward the interior of the community. Compared with assigning unified walking parameters to all nodes, we adaptively set the number and length of random walks based on node degrees and the number of attributes. This strategy avoids the problem of oversampling nodes with low degrees and fewer attributes. Finally, we learn the node embeddings from the random walk sequences using the skip-gram model, and employ the clustering algorithm to obtain the community detection results. The effectiveness of the proposed algorithm is verified by conducting experiments on real-world and synthetic datasets.Keywords: Community detection, Random walk, Network embedding, Attributed networkSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Networks, as a common form of data in the real world, play a crucial role in the study of relationships between entities. In reality, the connections between entities in many domains can be modelled as networks and contain rich attribute information, such as citation networks, protein networks, social networks, etc., which are called attribute networks1–3. For attribute networks, it is not enough to use the topological information of the nodes; the rich attribute information contained in the nodes is also crucial for community detection1,2,4. Community detection in attribute networks aims to exploit the community structure in the network5, so that nodes within the same community are not only tightly connected structurally, but also have similar attributes, while nodes in different communities are sparsely connected structurally and have dissimilar attributes6,7. How to effectively use the topological and attribute information of nodes for community detection of attribute networks is currently popular research.

In recent years, the network embedding technique8 has been widely used in community detection tasks to achieve community delineation by clustering the embedded vectors, with the advantage that not only the topological information of the nodes but also the node attributes can be used to embed the high-dimensional sparse information of the nodes into the low-dimensional dense vector space and preserve the original topological and attribute features of the nodes9. Among them, random walk-based network embedding methods are effective in community detection tasks. For example, the DeepWalk algorithm proposed by Perozzi et al.10 pioneered the use of random walking to generate a corpus, obtain the embedding vectors of the nodes through the Skip-Gram model11, and combine it with a clustering algorithm to obtain community detection results. However, some of these random walking methods only use the topological information of the nodes, ignoring the importance of the node attribute information, and some do not consider maintaining the community structure of the nodes for the community detection task at the time of embedding, which may lead to inaccurate community detection results.

To address the above problems, this paper proposes a network embedding method based on community-aware random walk for community detection in attributed networks (RWACD). First, the topological similarity and attribute similarity of the nodes are calculated to construct the fusion similarity matrix; then, the centrality and boundaries of the nodes are considered in constructing the transition probability matrix using the fusion similarity matrix, and the transition probability matrix for nodes with a bias towards walking within the community and a bias towards walking at the community boundaries are constructed. Finally, the number of walks and the length of walks for the nodes are set according to the degree and the number of attributes of the nodes, to simplify the process of setting the walking parameters under the premise of guaranteeing the accuracy of the algorithm, and to avoid the overfitting and oversampling of nodes caused by manually setting the walking parameters. Through the above strategy, highly cohesive community detection results are obtained.

The main contributions of this paper are threefold:

Our proposed algorithm effectively leverages both the topological structure and attribute information of nodes, enabling each node to distinctly capture both the interior and boundary characteristics of communities during random walks. This dual mechanism allows for more accurate and robust community identification compared to existing random walk-based approaches.

Related works

Traditional community detection methods

Traditional community detection methods include label propagation, seed expansion, spectral clustering, graph segmentation, modularity optimisation, etc., and the representative algorithms are GN (Girvan-Newman)12, LPA (Label Propagation Algorithm)13, FN (Fast Newman)14, CPM (Cluster Percolation Method)15 and Blondel et al.16 and Ding et al.17 heuristically considered the effect of low-quality seeds and communities on the expansion process and proposed to discard low-quality seeds and communities during the expansion process, and finally obtained the community detection results through the optimization process. Zhao et al.18 reduced the graph size by compressing nodes with degree 1 or 2 to height nodes and filtered the seed nodes using a quality function and node density, and obtained community detection results through seed expansion. Berahmand et al.1,2 constructed an affinity matrix using topological similarity and attribute similarity of nodes through a parameter-free method and obtained community detection results through spectral clustering. Zhe et al.19 mapped attributes into nodes through node-attribute relationships and added edges between two types of nodes for community detection of the augmented graph through the modularity maximisation model. Meng et al.4 proposed a coupled similarity calculation method to capture node-to-node, node-to-attribute, and attribute-to-attribute similarity to generate the weights of edges between nodes and obtained community detection of a weighted graph through a clustering algorithm delineation. Berahmand et al.1,2 added weights to edges through topological similarity and attribute similarity and calculated node influence using Laplace centrality to improve the selection step of the label propagation process.

Deep learning-based community detection methods

Among the deep learning based approaches, Huang et al.20 proposed a deep neural network-based attribute network embedding framework that preserves the community structure by combining the network topology, node attributes, and community structure to obtain a vector representation of the nodes in a low-dimensional space. Jin et al.21 exploited the salient features of graph convolutional networks and Markov random fields in a complete end-to-end deep network architecture to solve the semi-supervised community detection problem in attribute networks with semantic information. Guo et al.22 proposed the AWBA (Attentional-Walk-Based AutoEncoder) algorithm, which learns the attention coefficients through the encoder, thus guiding the nodes in the decoder to randomly walk to neighbouring nodes and capture the relationships between high and low order nodes. Cui et al.23 used a Laplace smoothing filter to mitigate high-frequency noise in the node features, followed by an adaptive encoder that iteratively enhances the filtered features, which can be better embedded in the nodes for better application in downstream tasks such as community detection.

Furthermore, several recent works have advanced attributed network embedding specifically for clustering tasks. Zhang et al.24 jointly train a neighbor enhancement autoencoder and an attribute-aware skip-gram model to capture both structural and attribute information. Zhang et al.25,26 leverage adaptive high-order graph convolution to smooth node features and perform spectral clustering directly on the filtered representations without neural network training. Wang et al.27 proposed DAEGC (Deep Attentional Embedded Graph Clustering), which employs a graph attention network to learn node embeddings and jointly optimizes a self-training clustering objective in an end-to-end manner. While these deep learning-based methods achieve strong performance, they typically involve higher computational complexity and more hyperparameters. In contrast, our RWACD provides a lightweight, adaptive random walk alternative that explicitly models community boundary structures without requiring deep architectures.

Network embedding-based community detection methods

Existing network embedding methods include matrix decomposition, random walks, and deep learning. Community detection is achieved by combining them with clustering algorithms.

Among the matrix decomposition-based methods, Huang et al.28,29 proposed an attribute network embedding framework to jointly characterise topological and attribute information as embedding vectors, and proposed a distributed algorithm to speed up the operations of matrix decomposition. Xu et al.3 proposed the CDANE algorithm to jointly model the attribute and structural similarities of nodes, obtain node embedding vectors through matrix decomposition, and obtain community delineation results through non-parametric clustering. parametric clustering to obtain community detection results. Ye et al.30 proposed a new matrix updating method to deal with the optimisation problem of non-negative matrix decomposition, which uses the non-negative matrix decomposition framework to identify the community structure in the network. Gao et al.31 used the LDA topic model to generate the initial community to guide the random walking, and to control the walking bias towards the inside of the community. Lu et al.32 proposed the topology and attribute combination of non-negative matrix decomposition and non-negative matrix decomposition with topology and attribute symmetry, two non-parametric models to divide the network into communities. Wang et al.33 proposed the M-NMF model to maintain the community structure of the nodes by adding modularity constraint terms to their objectives.

Among the random walk-based methods, the LINE (Large-scale Information Network Embedding) algorithm proposed by Tang et al.34 improves the embedding effect by optimising the first-order similarity and second-order similarity of the nodes. The Node2Vec algorithm proposed by Grover et al.35 is based on DeepWalk by controlling the walking direction and sampling strategy with parameters. Zhang et al.25,26 proposed the DiaRW algorithm, which designs a backtracking strategy and variable length strategy during walking to reduce redundant information, considering the scale-free nature of the network. Mohammed et al.36 set the number of walking times for the nodes proportionally according to the degree of the nodes to reduce the amount of computation and unbalanced data. Berahmand K et al.37 proposed the CSADW algorithm, which uses the topological similarity and attribute similarity between the nodes to construct the weight matrix and obtain the transmission probability matrix for random walking by normalisation. Wei et al.38 proposed the IANRW algorithm, which defines the topological neighbours and attribute neighbours of the nodes and controls the walking between the two types of nodes by parameters. Yang et al.39 proposed the TADW (text-associated DeepWalk) algorithm, proving that the DeepWalk algorithm is equivalent to matrix decomposition and extending it to perform supervised random walking using text attributes of nodes. Dou et al.40 perform walking on structural and attribute bipartite graphs to maintain network structure and attribute homogeneity, respectively. In recent research, attribute community detection methods have been widely applied to location-based social recommendation tasks41–43.

Some of the above methods only use the topological information of the nodes, some require extensive experimentation to determine the walking parameters, and some do not take into account the community structure of the nodes, resulting in inaccurate delineation of the community structure for various reasons. Unlike these methods, our method uses both topological and attribute information of the nodes, emphasises the community structure during random walking, and eliminates the tedious process of determining the walking parameters, allowing for more accurate identification of the community structure.

Community-aware transition probability matrix

When designing the community-aware transition probability equation, we primarily considered the following three factors: (1) Structural Characteristics of Communities. A community is a substructure characterized by dense internal connections and sparse external connections. Therefore, the number of edges pointing from community boundary nodes to the interior is significantly larger than those pointing outward, meaning that a random walk located at a boundary node has a higher probability of returning to the inside of the community; (2) Attribute Correlation. Nodes within the same community exhibit high attribute correlation; thus, a random walk will transition to highly correlated nodes with a higher probability; (3) Core Node Attraction. As the core of a community, high-degree nodes exert an attractive effect on random walks, biasing the walker to transition toward high-degree nodes. The integration of the above three aspects enables the random walk process to be community-aware, thereby facilitating more accurate community detection. Walking parameters and sampling strategy

Traditional methods use parametric experiments to determine the walking parameters, which not only increases the degree of experimental tedium, but can also lead to overfitting problems. Moreover, these methods set uniform walking parameters for all nodes, which may lead to oversampling of low-degree nodes, thus generating a large amount of redundant information and causing problems such as poor embedding effect. We propose a method to set the number of walking times and walking lengths for nodes based on the degree and number of attributes, which are calculated as shown in Eqs. (13) and (14).

In the above two equations, Num is the number of node walks times, Length is the walking length of the node, is the degree of the node, is the number of attributes contained in the node, and is a hyperparameter representing the upper limit of the walking length to prevent the random walker from traversing too deep into the network, which could introduce noise from irrelevant communities and increase computational cost. For the number of walks, we adopt the maximum value between the node degree and the attribute count. A node with a high degree is structurally significant, requiring more walk sequences to fully capture its diverse topological neighborhood. Similarly, a node with a large number of attributes possesses rich semantic information, necessitating adequate sampling to explore attribute-based connections. By taking the maximum value, we ensure that a node playing a crucial role in either the structural or attribute domain is sufficiently sampled, thus avoiding the loss of critical information. For the walk length, we calculate the average of the node’s degree and attribute count, bounded by an upper limit. This design scales the exploration depth proportionally to the node’s overall information richness. Nodes with dense connections and rich attributes warrant a deeper walk to uncover extended community structures. However, we cap this length at Lmax to prevent the random walker from traversing too deep into the network, which could introduce noise from irrelevant communities and increase computational cost.

When dividing the boundary nodes and internal nodes, we sort the nodes by degree from largest to smallest based on the point-degree centrality and slice the list by the parameter , and take the first nodes as the internal nodes of the community, which are subjected to walking sampling through the biased internal walking transition probability matrix. When a node is walking, according to the corresponding transition probability matrix, the nodes with transition probability with the current node are sorted in ascending order of transition probability, from which the nodes in the top tenth of nodes are taken out as the set of nodes to be jumped, and a node is randomly selected from the set to join the walking sequence as the next walking node until the length of the sequence reaches the end of the corresponding walking length.

In Phase 1, the algorithm initializes by fusing the structural and attribute information of the network. It computes the Topological Similarity Matrix and Attribute Similarity Matrix, respectively. These two matrices are then linearly combined via the balance parameter α to form the Fusion Similarity Matrix. Based on this unified representation, the algorithm constructs two distinct transition probability matrices: the internal-biased transition matrix and the boundary-biased transition matrix. This dual-matrix design is the cornerstone of the algorithm, ensuring community-aware transition probabilities that accurately guide the subsequent random walk process depending on the spatial role of each node.

In Phase 2, all nodes in the network are sorted in descending order based on their degree centrality. The node set is divided into two mutually exclusive subsets: internal nodes and boundary nodes. For each target node, the algorithm automatically calculates the necessary number of walks and the maximum walk length based on its individual degree and attribute count. This adaptive calculation mitigates the risk of oversampling low-degree nodes and undersampling high-degree nodes, effectively eliminating the need for tedious manual parameter tuning. During the iterative walk generation, the algorithm checks the spatial affiliation of the current starting node. If the node belongs to the internal node set, the walker utilizes the internal-biased transition matrix to step toward highly cohesive neighbors; conversely, if it is a boundary node, the walker employs the boundary-biased transition matrix.

In Phase 3, the algorithm maps the generated corpus of walk sequences into a continuous vector space. The Skip-Gram model is deployed to learn the low-dimensional embedding representations of all nodes by maximizing the co-occurrence probability of nodes within a fixed sliding window. These dense, continuous vectors effectively preserve both the complex topological structures and the rich attribute homophily of the original network. Ultimately, the k-means clustering algorithm is applied to the learned embeddings to partition the nodes into distinct groups, yielding the final community detection results.

Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the effectiveness of the algorithm by conducting experiments on real-world and synthetic datasets using three evaluation metrics. First, we investigate the effect of the parameters α, β, and d on the accuracy of the algorithm. Parameter experiments

We study the effect of important parameters α, β and d on the accuracy of the algorithm on real-world and synthetic datasets. In our experiments on citation and social networks (e.g., Citeseer, BlogCatalog), we empirically set as the default value. This indicates that a significant portion of nodes in these medium-sized networks exhibit clear community core characteristics. However, for large-scale networks with power-law degree distributions, the proportion of “core” nodes might be much smaller. In such cases, a fixed β may not be optimal. A potential adaptive strategy is to determine β based on the statistical distribution of node degrees. For instance, one could set the threshold at the “elbow point” of the degree distribution curve or use a statistical quantile (e.g., top-k percentile where the cumulative degree reaches a certain percentage of total edges).

Specifically, for a network with N nodes, β could be dynamically adjusted as or determined by the Gini coefficient of the degree distribution, ensuring that only truly influential nodes are treated as internal cores.

Ablation study

To comprehensively verify the effectiveness of the core methods proposed in this paper, we conducted a rigorous ablation study. By isolating these components, we aim to clarify the individual impact of each mechanism on the final community detection performance. We designed three degraded variants of our proposed algorithm to compare against the full RWACD model:

RWACD-B (Bacis): This is the most degraded variant. It removes both proposed mechanisms. It performs a standard random walk directly on the Fusion Similarity Matrix (without community-aware bias) and assigns identical, fixed walking parameters (number of walks and length) to all nodes regardless of their topological degree or attribute diversity.

RWACD-B (Bacis): This is the most degraded variant. It removes both proposed mechanisms. It performs a standard random walk directly on the Fusion Similarity Matrix (without community-aware bias) and assigns identical, fixed walking parameters (number of walks and length) to all nodes regardless of their topological degree or attribute diversity.

RWACD-FP (Fixed Parameters): This variant preserves the community-aware biased random walk strategy but removes the adaptive parameter calculation. Instead, it uses unified, fixed walk parameters for all nodes. The fixed parameters were empirically set to the optimal manual values explored in Section "Comparison of Walking Parameter Setting Methods".

RWACD-FP (Fixed Parameters): This variant preserves the community-aware biased random walk strategy but removes the adaptive parameter calculation. Instead, it uses unified, fixed walk parameters for all nodes. The fixed parameters were empirically set to the optimal manual values explored in Section "Comparison of Walking Parameter Setting Methods".

RWACD-NB (No Bias): This variant retains the adaptive determination of walk parameters but removes the “community-aware bias” module. It does not distinguish between internal and boundary nodes, performing random walks based purely on the un-biased Fusion Similarity Matrix.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a network embedding based on community-aware and random walk for community detection in attributed networks. Firstly, we construct the community similarity matrix using the topological similarity and attribute similarity of nodes, which ensures the effective use of node topological and attribute information. Second, we construct transition probability matrices that favour walking within the community and at the community boundary, respectively, to enhance node walking within the community and at the community boundary to better maintain and identify the community structure. Finally, we set the walking times and lengths for nodes according to their degree and attribute numbers to simplify the process of setting the walking parameters and to avoid the problems of overfitting as well as oversampling of nodes caused by manually setting the walking parameters, under the premise of guaranteeing the accuracy of the algorithm. Experimental results on real-world and synthetic datasets show that our algorithm outperforms other baseline methods. In the future, based on the existing work, we consider the development of a community detection algorithm that automatically determines the number of communities by integrating density-based clustering techniques directly into the embedding space, which can naturally infer the number of clusters from the data distribution.
