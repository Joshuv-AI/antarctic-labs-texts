A Novel Dysferlin‐Binding Kinase CK2α Promotes Plasma Membrane Repair in Dysferlinopathy
Authors: Naoko Nakamura, Naoki Suzuki, Shin‐ichiro Kanno, Rei Yamanaka, Hiroya Ono, Rumiko Izumi, Rui Muliang, Christian Borgo, Akiyuki Ohno, Ryuhei Harada, Saki Saito, Yukino Funayama, Kensuke Ikeda, Shio Mitsuzawa, Yasuaki Watanabe, Tomomi Shijo, Tetsuya Akiyama, Toshiaki Takahashi, Makoto Kanzaki, Shion Osana, Hitoshi Warita, Yoshitsugu Aoki, Satoru Ebihara, Mauro Salvi, Ryoichi Nagatomi, Akira Yasui, Katsuya Miyake, Masashi Aoki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDysferlinopathy is an adult‐onset form of muscular dystrophy caused by mutations in the dysferlin gene and is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. Dysferlin is primarily known for its role in plasma membrane repair. Although several proteins associated with dysferlin have been identified, many aspects of its signaling pathways and protein–protein interactions remain unclear. Here, we focused on the region between the third and fourth C2 domains, where frequent genetic mutations occur and functional domains are concentrated, and identified the protein kinase CK2α (formerly known as casein kinase 2) as a novel dysferlin‐binding protein. CK2α was found to accumulate at membrane injury sites along with dysferlin in mouse skeletal muscle, and membrane repair was delayed in CK2α knockout cells. Furthermore, overexpression of CK2α in dysferlin‐deficient mouse muscle led to improved membrane repair. Additionally, we revealed that CK2α plays a role in phosphorylating annexin A1, which is known to bind to dysferlin and is involved in plasma membrane repair. Our results indicated that CK2α controls membrane repair by participating in the phosphorylation of annexin A1. The molecular interplay among dysferlin, CK2α, and phosphorylated annexin A1 represents a novel therapeutic target for promoting membrane repair.Keywords: annexin A1, Dysferlinopathy, plasma membrane repair, protein kinase CK2, skeletal muscle

Introduction

Dysferlinopathy is an autosomal recessive muscular dystrophy caused by mutations in the dysferlin gene, which result in reduced or absent levels of dysferlin protein. Three distinct phenotypes, namely, limb‐girdle muscular dystrophy type R2, Miyoshi muscular dystrophy 1 [1, 2], and distal myopathy with anterior tibial onset [3], have been identified.

Dysferlin is involved in plasma membrane repair and accumulates at the site of membrane damage upon influx of Ca2+ ions due to membrane injury [4]. Furthermore, in recent years, dysferlin‐binding proteins involved in membrane repair, including MG‐53/TRIM72 [5], caveolin‐3 [6], annexins [7], AHNAK [8], Fam65b [9], HDAC6 [9], and AMPK [10], have been gradually identified. Proteins involved in membrane repair are thought to interact and mobilize vesicles to the site of damage, forming a “patch” to fill the damaged area, and repair is believed to occur following influx of Ca2+ ions [11, 12]. However, the details regarding the interactions and pathways leading to the “patch” remain unclear.

Skeletal muscle is constantly exposed to mechanical stress and damage. Effective repair of the plasma membrane is a critical and indispensable process for mitigating muscle injuries, complementing the muscle regeneration facilitated by myoblasts. This restorative mechanism is active in individuals with normal muscle function and even in those afflicted with muscle disorders. Therefore, the development of therapeutic approaches to promote plasma membrane repair is considered applicable not only to dysferlinopathies but also to other muscle disorders in which muscle regeneration is impaired, potentially alleviating their pathophysiology.

The dysferlin gene is located on chromosome 2 (2p13.3‐p13.1) and is a large gene consisting of 55 exons and 6243 base pairs. Its encoded protein, dysferlin, localizes to the muscle cell membrane [13]. Dysferlin comprises seven C2 domains, three Fer domains (FerI, FerA, FerB), three DysF domains (DysFN, IDysF, DysFC), and a single C‐terminal transmembrane domain (Figure 1). Most of the known functional domains are concentrated between the C2C and C2D domains (region II) [14, 15]. Furthermore, mutations observed in dysferlinopathy are found throughout the dysferlin gene, but when these mutations are categorized by frequency, region II is most commonly affected. The p.W999C mutation, which is the most common mutation in the Japanese population, is also located within this region [16, 17]. Previously, we focused on this region and reported that the AMPK complex binds to dysferlin and is involved in membrane repair [10].

Domain Structure of dysferlin. Dysferlin consists of seven C2 domains, three Fer domains, and three DysF domains, with a transmembrane domain at the C‐terminus. For convenience, the regions between C2 domains are divided into regions I–VI, with a focus on the second region where the Fer and DysF domains are concentrated.

We hypothesized that proteins that bind to the functionally important region II of dysferlin regulate the plasma membrane repair of skeletal muscle. The present study aimed to identify another protein that binds to region II of dysferlin. We analyzed the functions of these genes in plasma membrane repair and signaling pathways to elucidate the molecular pathogenesis of dysferlinopathy.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Mouse C2C12 myoblasts [American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and Department of Biomedical Sciences, University of Padova], CK2α, α′, b‐knockout C2C12 myoblast cells (Department of Biomedical Sciences, University of Padova) and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) with high glucose (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biosera, Cholet, France) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical) at 37°C and 5% CO2. The immortalized dysferlin‐reduced and control human myoblast lines (Table 1) (Jain Foundation, Seattle, WA) were cultured using a SkGM‐2 BulletKit (Lonza, Basel, Swizerland). Differentiation into myotubes was initiated at approximately 70%–80% confluence in DMEM supplemented with 2% horse serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

Plasmid Construction

Dysf‐DsRed and Dysf‐Halo were constructed using the In‐Fusion HD Cloning Kit (TaKaRa Bio, Shiga, Japan). PCR fragments of dysferlin (NM_003494) from DYSF‐Venus (#29768 Addgene, Watertown, MA) were generated. The DsRed vector utilized the pDsRed‐Monomer‐N In‐Fusion Ready Vector (TaKaRa Bio), and the Halo tag vector was prepared by digesting the pFC14K HaloTag CMV Flexi Vector (Promega, Madison, WI) with Carboxy Flexi Enzyme Blend (Promega) at the SgfI and EcoRI sites, followed by linearization. Amplified fragments of CK2α with additional XhoI at the N‐terminus and NotI at the C‐terminus were incorporated into pEGFP‐N1 (TaKaRa Bio). CK2α‐HA (NM_177559.3) was custom‐made by VectorBuilder (Chicago, IL). The GFP vector used as a control was the pCAG‐EGFP vector (Addgene). Gene transfection was performed on HEK293T cells using PEI max (Polysciences, Warrington, PA).

BcGST‐DYSF‐II Pulldown and nanoLC/MS/MS

The RH30 cell pellets were suspended in cell extraction buffer [50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 0.3 M NaCl, 0.2% NP40, and protease inhibitor cocktail cOmplete (Roche, Basel Swizerland)] and sonicated for 15 s. The suspension was centrifuged again (12 000 g, 10 min), and the supernatant was used as the cell extract.

BcGST‐DYSF‐II was cloned and inserted into pFastBac1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by PCR amplification from the DYSF‐Venus vector (Addgene) and infected Sf9 cells. BcGST‐ DYSF‐II‐expressing cell lysates were incubated with glutathione magnet beads and MagneGST Glutathione Particles (Promega) (30 μL volume) for 1 h at 4°C and washed three times with washing buffer A (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5; 0.3 M NaCl; 0.1% NP‐40). RH30 cell extracts were incubated with GST (control) and BcGST‐DYSF‐II‐bound magnetic beads for 12 h in the presence of benzonase nuclease (Novagen, Darmstadt, Germany) at 4°C. After three washes with washing buffer (0.15 M NaCl. 0.1% NP‐40, 50 mM HEPES pH 7.4), GST (control) and BcGST‐ DYSF‐II binding proteins were eluted twice with 300 μL of elution buffer (1.2 M NaCl. 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4). The eluates were concentrated and desalted by the centrifugal filter unit Amicon Ultra4‐10 k (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA), suspended in SDS–PAGE sample buffer and boiled for 5 min. The samples were resolved by SDS–PAGE. The gel was stained using a Wako Silver Stain MS Kit (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical). Gel fragments were reduced with 100 mM DTT and alkylated with 100 mM iodoacetamide. After washing, the gels were incubated with trypsin overnight at 30°C. Recovered peptides were desalted by Ziptips c18 (Merck Millipore). Samples were analyzed by nanoLC/MS/MS systems (DiNa HPLC system KYA TECH Corporation/QSTAR XL Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA). Mass data were acquired via Mascot software.

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Wako) supplemented with protease inhibitor (cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail: Roche), sonicated for 2 min, and centrifuged at 4°C and 15 000 rpm for 15–30 min to collect the supernatant. After the lysates were processed, the protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Laemmli buffer was added to the lysates, followed by SDS–PAGE, and the proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (PVDF, Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA). The membrane was blocked with PVDF Blocking Reagent for Can Get Signal (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), and primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C in Can Get Signal. After the membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, the bands were detected using Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Merck Millipore) and then detected using ChemiDocMP (Bio‐Rad).

Co‐Immunoprecipitation

We used a Halo Tag Mammalian Pull‐Down System (Promega). HEK293T cells transfected with 10 μg of dysferlin‐Halo or control Halo vector and 10 μg of CK2α‐HA were seeded in a 15 cm dish and harvested 48 h later. The cells were washed twice with ice‐cold PBS, scraped, and centrifuged at 4°C and 2000× g for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was stored at −80°C for more than 24 h. The stored cells were lysed in mammalian lysis buffer supplemented with 50× protease inhibitor cocktail and incubated at room temperature. The lysate was pipetted 10 times with a 27‐gauge needle, followed by centrifugation at 4°C and 14 000× g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. The lysate was added to HaloLink Resin and incubated at room temperature on a rotator for 60 min. After centrifugation at 800× g for 2 min, the supernatant was removed, and the resin was washed five times with resin equilibration/wash buffer. SDS elution buffer was added, and the mixture was shaken at room temperature for 30 min to elute the proteins.

We also used GFP‐trap Magnetic Particles M‐270 (GTD‐20, Proteintech) for Co‐immunoprecopitation. HEK293T cells transfected with GFP‐dysferlin II or control GFP vector and CK2α‐HA were seeded in a 15 cm dish and harvested 48 h later. Cells were washed twice with PBS(−), harvested using a cell scraper, and pelleted by centrifugation at 200× g for 5 min at 4°C. The pellets were washed once more with PBS(−) and centrifuged again. The resulting cell pellets were lysed in 200 μL of lysis buffer (10 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP‐40 substitute (9016‐45‐9, Wako)) supplemented with protease inhibitor (cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail: Roche), and incubated on ice for 30 min. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 17000× g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Dilution buffer (10 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA) containing protease inhibitor (cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail: Roche) was added. The samples were incubated with GFP‐trap at 4°C for 1 h with rotation. The beads were washed four times with wash buffer (10 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.05% NP‐40 substitute), and bound proteins were eluted with 2× SDS sample buffer.

Antibodies

The antibodies used for immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation were as follows: HA tag (51064–2‐AP; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL), Halo tag (G9281; Promega), dysferlin (NCL‐Hamlet, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), CK2α/α′ (MCA3031Z; Bio‐Rad), CK2β (ab76025; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), annexin A1 (21990–1‐AP, Proteintech) (#610067, BD Bioscience, Franklinlakes, NJ), pAkt (S127) (ab133458; Abcam), Akt (#9272; CST, Danvers, MA), GFP (M048‐3; MBL, Tokyo, Japan), GAPDH (#2118; CST), Vinculin (#13901; CST), and HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (mouse: #7076; CST, rabbit: #7074; CST).

Sex as a Biological Variable

Our study examined male and female animals, and similar findings were reported for both sexes.

Mouse Skeletal Muscle Harvesting and Protein Extraction

Mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia by cervical dislocation, and the anterior tibialis muscle, gastrocnemius muscle, and soleus muscle were collected. The collected tissues were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen muscles were then crushed using a mortar and pestle, further homogenized in RIPA buffer (Wako) supplemented with protease inhibitor using a BioMasher II (Nippi, Tokyo, Japan), and centrifuged at 4°C and 15 000 rpm for 30 min to collect the supernatant.

Transfection Into Mouse Flexor Digitorum Brevis (FDB) Muscles

Under anesthesia, 20 μg of plasmid was injected into the FDB muscles of 6‐week‐old C57BL/6J mice or 18‐month‐old Bla/J mice (Jain Foundation). Electroporation was performed using a Pulse Generator CUY21EDIT (Bex, Tokyo, Japan). The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation 7–14 days after electroporation, and the FDB muscles were collected and used for the membrane repair assay.

Membrane Repair Assay

FDB muscles harvested from mice were treated with collagenase A (6 mg/mL) (Roche). Subsequently, membrane injury was induced using a two‐photon laser (820 nm) (Coherent, Saxonburg, PA), and the accumulation or influx of the fluorescent dye FM4‐64 or FM1‐43 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at the site of injury was observed via confocal microscopy (LSM880, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Videos were recorded for 3 min starting 2 s before the injury. The fluorescence intensity was measured using ZEN imaging software (Zeiss). The fluorescence intensity of GFP at the site of injury was measured, and the change in fluorescence intensity from F0 was quantified (ΔF/).

Luminometer Quantification of Plasma Membrane Injury

This experiment was modified from a previously reported method using the release of ATP from cells as an indicator of damage [18, 19]. Cultured cells (approximately 20 000 cells) were treated with PBS (−) as a negative control, PBS (+) containing 1 mM Ca2+ ions as a positive control. After damage was induced by one stroke of syringe loading or scraping, 10 μL of luciferin/luciferase solution (CellTiter‐Glo 2.0 Cell Viability Assay, Promega) was added to the cell suspension, and the luminescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HTX multimode reader, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Luminescence intensity was also measured for samples without induced damage (no wound: nw) as a background. The membrane repair rate was calculated using the following formula: (1—(sample luminescence intensity—nw)/(negative control luminescence intensity—nw)) × 100.

Evans Blue Dye Assay

Bla/J mice were used for in vivo experiments. CK2α‐GFP was electroporated into the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle (5 pulses × 2 sets; 75 V, pulse on 20 ms, pulse off 200 ms). One week after electroporation, Evans Blue dye (EBD) (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical) was administered via intraperitoneal injection. Muscle damage was induced 10–18 h later by downhill treadmill exercise (15° decline, 60 min). TA muscles were collected immediately after exercise, rapidly frozen, fixed in acetone, and examined by fluorescence microscopy.

SGC‐CK2‐1 Treatment

70% confluence C2C12 cells were treated with 0, 2, 4, 10 μM of SGC‐CK2‐1 (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ) for 20 h. After two washes with PBS (−), lysed in RIPA buffer (Wako) containing protease inhibitor (cOmplete EDTA‐free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail: Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor (PhosSTOP: Roche), sonicated for 2 min, and centrifuged at 4°C and 15 000 rpm for 15 min to collect the supernatant.

In mice skeletal muscles, FDB was collected and treated with collagenase, then immersed in 10 μM SGC‐CK2‐1 solution for membrane repair assay.

Preparation of Cell Samples for Phosphoproteomic Analysis

Wild‐type and CK2α KO C2C12 myoblast cells were harvested using TripLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific), washed three times with PBS (−), pelleted, and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen cell pellets were transported to Medical Proteoscope Corporation for analysis.

Purification of Phosphorylated Peptides

To each sample, 500 μL of buffer (9 M urea, 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 2 solution, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 3 solution) was added, followed by ultrasonication on ice. The mixture was then centrifuged at 15000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, after which the supernatant was collected. The total protein concentration was measured using the BCA assay, and an equivalent of 100 μg of total protein was aliquoted. DTT was added, and the samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 min, followed by alkylation with IAA. The urea concentration was decreased to 2 M by adding 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), and then, 5 μL of trypsin solution (1 μg/μl) was added. The samples were then incubated at 37°C for 16 h for enzymatic digestion. The resulting peptide solution was desalted using a STAGE Tip (SDB + C18) and dried under vacuum [20]. Specific adsorption of phosphorylated peptides was performed using the Titansphere Phos‐TiO Kit (GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan) [21]. The eluate was dried under vacuum. After the reaction, the peptide solution was desalted again using a STAGE Tip (SDB + C18) and dried under vacuum.

LC–MS/MS Analysis of Phosphorylated Peptides

The peptide samples recovered after titania treatment were dissolved in a solvent consisting of water, acetonitrile, and trifluoroacetic acid (at a volume ratio of 98:2:0.1). The total solution was subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis.

[LC]: Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano (Thermo Fisher Scientific). [MS/MS]: Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Data Processing and Amino Acid Sequence Database Search for Phosphorylated Peptides

Proteome Discoverer (ver. 3.0) (PD 3.0) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was utilized for peak alignment, peak intensity normalization, and sequence database searches for each LC–MS/MS measurement dataset. The amino acid sequence database was constructed by combining the following two databases: Swiss‐Prot protein entries derived from mouse available in UniProt (http://www.uniprot.org/) version 2020_1 (total 17 029 entries) and amino acid sequences of protein contaminants obtained from The Global Proteome Machine Organization (http://www.thegpm.org/crap/index.html) (total 116 entries). The search criteria for the database were as follows: enzyme: trypsin, maximum missed cleavage: 2, peptide tolerance: ±5 ppm, MS/MS tolerance: ±0.02 Da, mass: monoisotopic mass, fixed modification: carbamidomethyl (C, +57.021), variable modification: oxidation (M, +15.995), and phospho (ST, +79.966). Significant peptide identification was determined with a false discovery rate (FDR) as the criterion, and a threshold of FDR < 0.05 was set for identification.

PhosTag

The cell pellet was dissolved in RIPA buffer (Wako) containing protease inhibitor (cOmplete EDTA‐free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail: Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor (PhosSTOP: Roche), followed by incubation on ice for 30 min. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 15000 rpm at 4°C for 15 min, after which the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 10 μg of sample supplemented with Laemmli buffer was applied to a SuperSepTM Phos‐tag gel (Wako) and electrophoresed using running buffer (100 mM Tris, 100 mM MOPS, 0.1% (w/v) SDS, 5.0 mM sodium bisulfite). The proteins were then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF, Bio‐Rad) semidry blotting system after they were immersed in transfer buffer containing 5 mM EDTA for 10 min twice. Band detection was performed using the same procedure used for immunoblotting.

Results

CK2α Bind to Region II of Dysferlin

To explore protein binding to region II of dysferlin, we selected an expression system in insect cells using a baculovirus vector, which allows post‐translational modifications similar to those in animal cells and provides relatively large amounts of proteins. We expressed and extracted the dysferlin region II protein tagged with BcGST from Sf9 cells. Subsequently, lysates prepared from RH30 cells, a human rhabdomyosarcoma cell line, were applied to magnetic beads conjugated with the purified protein, and the bound proteins were eluted and separated via SDS–PAGE. By comparing the bands corresponding to putative binding proteins to those of the controls, we successfully identified multiple candidates binding proteins via mass spectrometry. Among these proteins, we further examined the protein kinase CK2α (formerly known as casein kinase 2), which has been reported to be involved in myogenesis. (Figure 2A,B and Figure S1) [22].

(A) The left panel shows SDS–PAGE (CBB staining) confirming the expression of BcGST‐DYSF‐II and BcGST. (B) The right panel shows silver staining, where bands considered to be bound proteins were excised and identified as CK2α and CK2α′ by LC–MS/MS (arrows 1, 2, 3). Coimmunoprecipitation of Halo‐tagged dysferlin and HA‐tagged CK2α was performed using cell lysates from HEK293T cells, which do not express endogenous dysferlin, overexpressing both plasmids. We revealed that CK2α‐HA bands were detected only in the cell lysates with overexpression of dysferlin‐Halo, demonstrating the binding of dysferlin to CK2α (Figure 3A). Furthermore, co‐immunoprecipitation was performed in HEK293T cells expressing GFP‐tagged region II of dysferlin (GFP‐Dysf II) and CK2α‐HA. CK2α‐HA was co‐precipitated when GFP‐Dysf II was pulled down by GFP‐trap, confirming that CK2α binds to region II of dysferlin, although the possibility remains that it may also bind to other regions (Figure S2).

Next, we investigated the expression level of CK2α during muscle differentiation and in living organisms. We assessed endogenous CK2α in mouse myoblasts and myotubes, and its expression was verified in the skeletal muscles of both normal mice and dysferlin‐deficient mice (Bla/J) (Figure 3B). Semiquantitatively, there was no significant difference in expression between myoblasts and myotubes (p = 0.6179) or between wild‐type and Bla/J mice (p = 0.6569) (Figure 3C). These findings suggest that the expression level of CK2α in muscle cells remains consistent, regardless of the differentiation status or the presence of dysferlin.

CK2α and CK2α′ Accumulate at the Injury Site and Colocalize With Dysferlin

Because the primary function of dysferlin is membrane repair, we investigated the behavior of CK2α during plasma membrane repair using laser membrane injury experiments [4]. Ex vivo laser membrane injury was induced in mouse skeletal muscle fibers electroporated with CK2α‐GFP. We observed fluorescence localization, confirming that CK2α accumulated at the site of injury (Figure 4A,B). Furthermore, in the muscle fibers electroporated with both CK2α‐GFP and dysferlin‐dsRed, we observed colocalization of CK2α and dysferlin at the injury site (Figure 4C, Pearson's correlation coefficient 0.42). Similar colocalization with dysferlin was also observed for the other CK2 catalytic subunit, CK2α′ (Figure 4D). We found that both CK2α and CK2α′ accumulate at the site of membrane damage and colocalize with dysferlin. Collectively, these observations indicate an important role for CK2α and CK2α′ in the plasma membrane repair process, with both subunits not only accumulating at sites of membrane injury but also colocalizing with dysferlin. This colocalization suggests a potential synergistic action or direct interaction between these proteins in facilitating membrane repair.

Plasma Membrane Repair Is Impaired in CK2α Knockout C2C12 Myoblast Cells

To investigate whether CK2α is involved in plasma membrane repair, we obtained CK2α, CK2α′, and CK2β knockout (KO) C2C12 myoblast cells [22]. We confirmed the actual knockout of each protein by immunoblotting (Figure 5A). We conducted laser membrane injury experiments with the cells and found that compared to the wild‐type cells, the CK2α KO myoblast cells showed influx of FM4‐64 for more than 120 s (Figure 5B,C), indicating decreased plasma membrane repair.

Furthermore, to quantify the plasma membrane repair function, we measured the amount of ATP released from cells subjected to membrane injury by performing a single syringe loading stroke. We compared ATP release in wild‐type C2C12 myoblast cells, where membrane repair is absent in a Ca2+‐free PBS solution, with that in cells undergoing membrane repair in a Ca2+‐containing PBS solution. The objective was to calculate the rate of plasma membrane repair. The findings indicated a significant decrease in the repair rate for the CK2α KO and CK2α′ KO myoblast cells compared to that for the wild‐type myoblast cells (Figure 5D; WT: mean 59.91% ± 2.49, CK2α KO: mean 36.91 ± 10.75, CK2α′ KO: mean 36.07 ± 3.27, CK2β KO: mean 53.79 ± 1.97, WT vs. CK2α KO: p = 0.0095, WT vs. CK2α′ KO: p = 0.0073). Consequently, knocking out one or more CK2 catalytic subunits reduces the capacity for plasma membrane repair.

Overexpression of CK2α Promotes Plasma Membrane Repair in Dysferlin‐Deficient Mice

As CK2α positively regulates membrane repair, we investigated membrane repair upon overexpression of CK2α‐GFP in dysferlin‐deficient Bla/J mice. In muscle fibers lacking CK2α‐GFP expression or expressing GFP‐tag alone, continuous influx of FM 4–64 was observed for an extended period (Figure 6A and Figure S3). Conversely, in muscle fibers expressing CK2α‐GFP, we observed a reduced and early cessation of FM 4–64 influx, indicating increased membrane repair (Figure 6B,C and Figure S3). These results indicate that CK2α overexpression can significantly promote plasma membrane repair, even in the absence of dysferlin.

To assess whether CK2α improves membrane repair in vivo, we performed the Evans Blue dye (EBD) assay using Bla/J mice. Although there was no statistically significant correlation, fibers with high EBD uptake, indicative of muscle damage, tended to show weaker GFP‐CK2α expression (Pearson's correlation coefficient 0.01). When fibers were categorized into high CK2α‐GFP expression (fluorescence intensity ≥ 30) and low or no expression groups, the EBD intensity was significantly different between the groups (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.003). Although the statistical power of this analysis is limited due to the low transfection efficiency and the small sample size, these results do not exclude the possibility that CK2α may improve membrane repair (Figure S4).

Plasma Membrane Repair Decreases With a CK2 Inhibitor

CK2α has been shown to positively regulate membrane repair. Next, to investigate whether this function is mediated by kinase activity, we examined membrane repair using the CK2 inhibitor SGC‐CK2‐1. In C2C12 cells, Phosphorylation of S129 of Akt was significantly reduced when reacted with up to 10 μM SGC‐CK2‐1, confirming that it reduces the kinase activity of CK2 (Figure 7A,B; 0 vs. 2 μM p = 0.0027, 0 vs. 4 μM p = 0.0004, 0 vs. 10 μM p = 0.0002). Then, we performed the laser membrane injury experiment with FDB of WT mice immersed in 10 μM SGC‐CK2‐1 solution, and the membrane repair function was reduced compared to the control (Figure 7C,D). These results suggest that CK2 kinase activity is involved in plasma membrane repair.

Annexin A1 Was Identified via Comprehensive Analysis of CK2α Phosphorylation Substrates

Given that CK2α controls plasma membrane repair through its kinase activity, we next identified its substrates. Although CK2 is ubiquitously expressed in cells throughout the body and has a large number of intracellular substrates [23], there have been no comprehensive analyses of the intracellular substrates of CK2α in skeletal muscle tissues or muscle cells. Therefore, we employed phosphoproteomic techniques to identify proteins with altered phosphorylation levels by comparing wild‐type C2C12 myoblast cells with CK2α knockout myoblast cells. Proteins were extracted from each cell pellet, and phosphorylated peptides within the peptide mixture obtained by trypsin digestion were purified by adsorption onto titania beads, followed by mass spectrometry analysis (Figure 8A). A total of 4871 phosphorylated peptides were identified. For identification of proteins with reduced phosphorylation in CK2α KO myoblast cells, peptides with less than half as much phosphorylation in the CK2α KO cells as in the wild‐type cells were extracted, resulting in the identification of 1247 phosphorylated peptides (Figure 8B). The known substrates of CK2α include the DNA replication licensing factor MCM2 and protein phosphatase inhibitor 2 [24]. Furthermore, among the proteins previously reported to be involved in membrane repair [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10], the number of phosphorylated peptides of annexins A1 and 3 was less than half that of the wild‐type proteins in the CK2α KO strain (Figure 8C). Focusing on annexin A1, which has been reported to be involved in membrane repair, along with dysferlin [7], we confirmed that the detected peaks were indeed phosphorylated peptides of annexin A1 (Figure 8D and Figure S5). Furthermore, evaluation of the phosphorylation status of annexin A1 using PhosTag technology revealed a decrease in the number of phosphorylated bands of annexin A1 in the CK2α KO myoblast cells (Figure 8E). Therefore, CK2α is presumed to be involved in the phosphorylation of annexin A1.

Phosphorylation of Annexin A1 Is Decreased in Human Myocytes With Dysferlin Mutations

Since phosphorylation of annexin A1 is decreased in CK2α KO C2C12 cells, we examined whether phosphorylation of annexinA1 is altered in human myocytes with dysferlin mutations. There was no difference in CK2 and annexin A1 expression in immunoblots of cells with dysferlin mutations (Figure 9A,B and Figure S6). However, in PhosTag, the phosphorylation band of annexin A1 was reduced in cells with dysfelrin mutations, suggesting that annexin A1 phosphorylation is decreased (Figure 9C).

Discussion

In this study, we used a classic laser membrane injury assay and a novel luminometer analysis to quantify cell injury and demonstrated that CK2α positively regulates membrane repair by binding to dysferlin. Additionally, we revealed that annexin A1, which has been reported to be associated with membrane repair, is phosphorylated by CK2α. It was suggested that phosphorylation of annexin A1 may also be decreased in patients with dysferlin mutations. These findings indicate that upon membrane damage, CK2α, along with dysferlin, accumulates at the site of injury and promotes its membrane repair function by participating in the phosphorylation of annexin A1 (Figure 10).

Schematic diagram of plasma membrane repair mediated by dysferlin, as elucidated in this study. (A) At steady state, dysferlin spans the membrane at the C‐terminus, and CK2 binds to dysferlin at region II. (B) When the cell membrane is injured, extracellular calcium ions influx, leading to the accumulation of dysferlin at the injury site, where it is believed to bind to the cytoplasmic side of the membrane via its C2 domain. In the presence of dysferlin, CK2 also efficiently distributes to the site of membrane injury, maintaining its kinase activity by interacting with dysferlin. Phosphorylated annexin A1 recruits intracellular vesicles to the injury site. (C) The mobilized intracellular vesicles are hypothesized to serve as a source of phospholipids, which act as material for the patch that seals the damaged site in cellular membrane repair.

CK2 is a tetrameric serine–threonine kinase composed of two catalytic subunits (α, α′) and two regulatory subunits (β) that are highly conserved across species and ubiquitously expressed in cells throughout the body. This protein has numerous intracellular substrates, and the phosphorylation of these substrates is known to play roles in cell growth, proliferation, and suppression of apoptosis [25]. CK2 overexpression in malignant tumor cells indicates that CK2 is a potential therapeutic target [26, 27]. In skeletal muscle, CK2 subunits play distinct roles: β in myoblast proliferation, α in myotube differentiation, and α′ in cell membrane fusion during differentiation [22].

The antitumor peptide CIGB‐300 and the orally available small molecule inhibitor CX‐4945 (silmitasertib), both of which target CK2, have demonstrated antitumor effects in animal models, leading to clinical trials being conducted on various malignancies [28, 29]. Furthermore, increased CK2 activity has been reported in patients with COVID‐19 [30]. Common side effects include diarrhea, nausea/vomiting, and fatigue, while no skeletal muscle‐related adverse events, such as hyper‐CK‐emia, myalgia, or muscle weakness, were reported [31]. However, given the involvement of CK2α in membrane repair, skeletal muscle dysfunction may be a potential side effect. On the other hand, if therapeutic strategies to activate CK2 for dysferlinopathies are developed, an increased risk of carcinogenesis may be a concern, indicating the need for careful and long‐term evaluation. The practical application of CK2 activators is expected to face substantial challenges. Therefore, we hypothesized that targeting substrates of CK2α during membrane repair can indirectly complement the function of CK2α. To explore this possibility, we conducted target discovery through phosphoproteomics.

Annexin A1, identified through phosphoproteomics, belongs to the annexin A family. Annexins are a protein family characterized by their binding to calcium or phospholipids and possessing a unique core domain structure at the C‐terminus (see Figure S3B) [32]. Annexin A1 is rapidly recruited to the site of membrane damage upon influx of Ca2+ ions [33], and annexin A1, A2 and A6 have been reported contributing to membrane repair, particularly by mobilizing vesicles to the cell membrane and participating in the formation of a “patch” to fill the damaged site [7, 34]. The expression of annexin A1 and A2 is upregulated in the skeletal muscles of patients with dysferlinopathies [35, 36]. Furthermore, surface plasmon resonance analysis revealed that annexin A1 binds to the C2A domain of dysferlin in a Ca2+‐dependent manner [37]. Regarding the relationship between annexin A1 and CK2α, while there are reports suggesting the involvement of CK2α in the phosphorylation of α‐E‐catenin during annexin A1 activation in BV‐2 microglia [38], no direct evidence of CK2α phosphorylating annexin A1 has been found in the literature. Furthermore, the sequence surrounding the newly identified candidate phosphorylation site, S27, does not conform to the canonical phosphorylation motif of CK2α [39]. This suggests that CK2α may be involved in the phosphorylation of S27 indirectly rather than directly. Additionally, there are no reports of phosphorylated annexin directly leading to membrane repair in skeletal muscle. However, annexin A1 is known to undergo phosphorylation at S27 within its functional domain located at the N‐terminus (see Figure S3B) by protein kinase C, and this phosphorylation is associated with its translocation to the cell surface and aggregation of phospholipid vesicles [40, 41, 42]. Given that residue S37 identified in this study also resides within this domain, phosphorylation of S37 may induce translocation to the cell membrane and vesicle accumulation, leading to “patch formation”.

Furthermore, in dysferlin‐deficient Bla/J mice overexpressing CK2α, while CK2α accumulation at the site of membrane damage decreased, we observed an increase in membrane repair, although not to the extent observed in wild‐type mice. Annexin A1, in contrast, accumulates at sites of membrane injury in a Ca2+‐dependent manner and shows similar behavior even in dysferlin‐deficient muscle cells [43]. Therefore, CK2α must interact with dysferlin to accumulate at the site of damage and efficiently promote membrane repair. Similar to our previous study of the role of AMPK in dysferlinopathy [10], we speculated that excess CK2α may increase the membrane repair capacity through the phosphorylation of membrane repair molecules such as annexin A1, even if they are not necessarily localized to the cell membrane.

These findings suggest that upon membrane damage, CK2α and annexin A1, along with dysferlin, accumulate at the site of damage, where annexin A1 is phosphorylated, facilitating “patch formation”. Ultimately, we aimed to advance therapeutic strategies based on the pathological hypothesis that increasing the level of phosphorylated annexin A1 promotes membrane repair.

The limitations of this study include the fact that only proteins binding to the second domain of dysferlin were examined, the inability to investigate the changes in CK2α localization in human patient samples due to the lack of suitable antibodies for immunostaining, and the inability to verify the relationship with AMPK, which also binds to domain II of dysferlin [10].

Does CK2 mediate the phosphorylation of AMPK? Although no differences in phosphorylation were observed in the current phosphoproteomic analysis, this finding might be attributed to the fact that the measurements were carried out under steady‐state conditions. It is possible that there are proteins related to membrane repair that are phosphorylated only during membrane damage, and AMPK may be one of these proteins. In the future, we will try to establish a method for recovering cells undergoing membrane repair and conduct phosphoproteomic analysis to identify proteins specialized in membrane repair. Moreover, AMPK is positioned downstream of the formylpeptide receptor 2 (FPR2/ALX) receptor, which is activated by annexin A1 [44, 45]. During skeletal muscle damage, annexin A1 indirectly activates AMPK, shifting the balance of macrophages toward M2 dominance, suppressing inflammation, and promoting muscle regeneration [45]. In this study, CK2α is presumed to be upstream of annexin A1, and a working hypothesis suggests the existence of a CK2α‐annexin A1‐FPR2/ALX receptor‐AMPK pathway, which is involved in membrane repair. Validating this hypothesis is also a crucial future task for the comprehensive elucidation of membrane repair mechanisms.
