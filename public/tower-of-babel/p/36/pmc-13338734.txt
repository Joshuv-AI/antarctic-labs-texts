Re-evaluation of the term “subchondral cyst” in the temporomandibular joint
Authors: Isadora Zuppo Drumond, Giulio Cesar Moreira Manzi, Karolina Kristian Aguilar Seraidarian, Diogo de Azevedo Miranda, Izabella Lucas de Abreu Lima, Paulo Isaias Seraidarian, Flávio Ricardo Manzi
Published: 2026

Dear Editors,

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) represent a complex and multifaceted group of conditions affecting the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), masticatory muscles, and associated structures, with degenerative processes being among their most challenging and prevalent aspects. These processes, often initiated by biomechanical alterations, such as articular disc displacement, trigger an inflammatory cascade and bone remodeling, significantly impacting patients' quality of life. Radiological and clinical literature, corroborated by various imaging and histopathological studies,1346 which investigate everything from the distribution of degenerative features to the relationship with bone density and the use of artificial intelligence in diagnosis, classically describes distinctive tomographic signs of TMJ degeneration. These include articular surface flattening, the presence of erosive processes, osteophyte formation, and, notably, the presence of subchondral cysts (Fig. 1). However, it is precisely in the terminology employed to describe these subchondral cavities that a conceptual imprecision lies, demanding critical attention.

Delving deeper into the terminological issue, the designation "subchondral cyst," widely used to describe one of the TMJ's bone alterations, warrants reconsideration. Despite being widely referred to as “subchondral cysts,” the term is imprecise because histopathological analyses demonstrate that these lesions lack an epithelial lining and liquid content, thus precluding the classic definition of a cyst. Instead, they consist of areas of necrotic bone, fibrous connective tissue, adipocytes, and foci of active osteoblastic remodeling, characterizing localized bone repair phenomena.1 The formation of these lesions has been explained by two main theories. The Synovial Rupture Theory proposes that microfractures resulting from cartilage thinning would allow synovial fluid to enter the subchondral bone, triggering an inflammatory process with the development of fibromyxoid tissue. Conversely, the Bone Bruise Theory suggests that joint wear and irregular load distribution lead to microfractures, medullary edema, and subsequent bone remodeling, without direct communication with the joint. Regardless of the etiopathogenic pathway, their development occurs in two phases: a destructive phase, characterized by osteonecrosis, resorption, and cavity formation, followed by a productive phase, marked by peripheral sclerosis and possible lesion expansion. Histologically, they exhibit predominantly fibrous content, neovascularization, nerve fibers, and bone spicules, along with increased mineralization of the adjacent bone and thickening of the overlying cartilage. Furthermore, the cavity secretes mediators such as nitric oxide, prostaglandin E2, and metalloproteinases, which intensify bone resorption and perpetuate the process of pathological remodeling.2

Radiographically, subchondral cavities appear as well-defined radiolucent areas beneath the articular surface, often bordered by a sclerotic margin.3 They are closely associated with osteoarthritic changes and frequently arise in regions of the joint subjected to mechanical stress.45 Longitudinal MRI studies have shown that many of these lesions remain stable or even spontaneously regress, reinforcing their non-progressive and self-limiting nature.1

This histopathological profile distinguishes TMJ subchondral lesions from periarticular cysts, such as synovial or ganglion cysts, which are true cystic entities containing a lining membrane and serous or mucinous fluid.6 In contrast, TMJ subchondral lesions are intraosseous cavities, without epithelium or synovial fluid, and are better described as cavitary defects or pseudocysts resulting from inflammatory and degenerative processes. The World Health Organization already emphasizes this distinction by defining idiopathic bone cavities of the jaws as pseudocysts due to the absence of epithelial lining,7 but this same conceptual rigor has not been consistently applied to TMJ subchondral lesions.

This can negatively impact the interpretation of imaging examinations, hinder the establishment of precise diagnostic criteria, and even influence the patient's perception of their own condition, leading to unrealistic expectations or unnecessary concerns. Additionally, recent longitudinal studies have demonstrated that many of these lesions exhibit spontaneous resolution or remain stable over time, a behavior that is much more consistent with bone remodeling and repair phenomena than with the progressive growth characteristic of a true cystic formation.1

In light of these considerations, we advocate replacing the term “subchondral cyst” with “subchondral bone defect,” as this designation more accurately reflects the underlying pathological condition. The proposed terminology acknowledges the absence of epithelial lining and fluid content, unequivocally excluding the definition of a true cyst. By contrast, the term “defect” appropriately characterizes a structural alteration or localized loss of bone tissue, thereby avoiding any implication of cystic etiology.

Furthermore, this terminology emphasizes the intrinsic association of these lesions with inflammatory and degenerative processes, particularly within the context of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis, which frequently originates from articular disc displacement followed by inflammatory changes. In this scenario, the so-called lesion represents a consequence or manifestation of the etiopathogenic cascade rather than an independent cystic entity.

Additionally, substantial evidence from cross-sectional, longitudinal, and histological investigations has consistently demonstrated active bone remodeling and repair in these areas. A “defect” denotes a structural alteration subject to biological repair mechanisms, which aligns closely with the dynamic changes observed in clinical and experimental studies. Importantly, maintaining the term “subchondral” preserves the necessary anatomical precision while improving conceptual accuracy.

The adoption of a more precise term is not merely a semantic refinement but represents a crucial advancement towards diagnostic accuracy, the indispensable harmonization between radiographic and histopathological findings, and consistency in interdisciplinary communication among healthcare professionals. We believe that the expression "subchondral bone defect" avoids misunderstandings, accurately reflects the structural alteration observed, and establishes a clearer framework for future research and for defining more appropriate and effective clinical management.

We respectfully submit this proposal for the scientific community's consideration, with the primary aim of stimulating constructive debate and consolidating a nomenclature more faithful to the biological reality of TMJ subchondral alterations.
