Thresholds and emergent processes of visual consciousness on color and category in preschoolers and adults
Authors: Ryoichi Watanabe, Yusuke Moriguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how perception transitions from an unconscious to conscious state is a central aim of visual consciousness research. The threshold is the boundary at which consciousness arises, and the process from unconsciousness to consciousness is the emergent process. Although studies have investigated the thresholds and emergent processes of visual consciousness in adults, how these processes develop during childhood remain unknown. Seventeen 5–6-year-old preschoolers and 16 adults completed two discrimination tasks (color and category) with varying stimulus onset asynchronies (SOAs; 16.7–216.7 and 16.7–83.3 ms respectively). Objective discrimination accuracy was recorded, and subjective awareness was assessed via a four-point Perceptual Awareness Scale. Data were fitted with a four-parameter non-linear psychometric function to estimate each participant’s threshold and gradualness (center and range of the slope, respectively). Thresholds of objective discrimination and subjective awareness were significantly larger in preschoolers, without the main effect of task or interaction between age and task. Furthermore, both emergent processes were significantly more gradual in preschoolers. Therefore, visual consciousness of color and category emerged at larger thresholds and more gradually in preschoolers, with no differences in task performance. Hence, preschoolers experience consciousness of colors and objects more gradually and slowly than adults.Keywords: Preschoolers, Visual consciousness, Backward masking, Awareness, Level of processingSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

Thresholds and emergent processes of visual consciousness

Visual consciousness, the subjective and phenomenal experience of seeing, encompasses the perception of visual images1. The shift from unconscious to conscious visual experiences has been a significant focus of visual consciousness research2–5. Although extensive research has examined the thresholds and neural mechanisms of visual consciousness in adults, studies on how these thresholds develop are lacking6,7. However, the developmental trajectory of visual consciousness is gaining understanding and recognition owing to its potential to enhance theoretical models8–10. This study examined the thresholds and emergent processes of visual consciousness among preschoolers and adults.

Subjective awareness and objective discrimination

The shift from unconsciousness to consciousness has been examined through emergent processes and thresholds of subjective awareness and objective discrimination5–7,11,12. Subjective awareness is typically evaluated through binary responses from participants (e.g., aware vs. unaware) or the Perceptual Awareness Scale (PAS; 1: no experience, 2: brief glimpse, 3: almost clear experience, and 4: clear experience), which assesses perceptual awareness on a gradient13. It allows researchers to gauge the existence and strength of a participant’s awareness. Research has indicated that longer stimulus presentation durations generally increased the intensity of subjective awareness5–7,11,14. In contrast, objective discrimination is quantified using task accuracy or metrics, such as d’, from the signal detection theory15. Similar to subjective awareness, objective discrimination performance tended to improve with increased duration of stimulus presentation5–7,11,14. This combined approach, which analyzes both the subjective and objective aspects, offers a comprehensive method to investigate the emergent processes and thresholds of visual consciousness.

Development of the emergent process and threshold of consciousness

Research on preschoolers has primarily examined objective discrimination performance and the thresholds for visual stimuli via backward masking techniques. Previous studies demonstrated that preschoolers exhibited inferior objective discrimination performance and higher discrimination thresholds for various stimuli, which included letters7,16–18, figures6, and colors7, compared with older children or adults. However, studies on the development of subjective awareness yielded conflicting results. Although preschoolers had higher thresholds for subjective awareness of shapes than adults6, those of colors and numbers were similar to those of adults7. These contrasting findings indicate that subjective awareness may develop differently from objective discrimination abilities among children. The development of visual consciousness is related to the development of the frontal lobe and frontal-parietal network6,7 and ventral visual stream involving the recognition of colors and categories19,20. However, no studies have directly examined the development of visual consciousness and these brain development.

Disparities between these studies could be owing to variations in type of stimuli and experimental methodologies. Previous studies employed an in-person experiment that used shape stimuli6, and also an online study that used color and number stimuli7. To examine these factors, this study employed an in-person experimental design based on previous research, which investigated differences in processing levels of stimuli within the same task framework7.

Theory of consciousness

The Level of Processing Hypothesis (LoPH) offers a reconciliation between the seemingly contradictory all-or-none and gradual emergence perspectives21–23. It proposes that the process of consciousness varies according to the level of stimulus processing; low-level tasks (e.g., shape or color judgments) exhibit gradual emergence and high-level tasks (e.g., meaning interpretation or stimulus categorization) follow an all-or-none trajectory11,24–29. This hypothesis is consistent with the hierarchical processing of visual information in the brain, where low-level processing involves basic visual features and high-level processing pertains to semantic content30, similar to the depth of processing31. Previous research examined the differences in performance slope steepness between low-level (color judgments) and high-level (number magnitude judgments) tasks; results demonstrated a more gradual and steeper emergent process for low-level and high-level tasks, respectively11. Other empirical studies have supported this hypothesis. Tasks that required high-level processing, such as judgments regarding stimulus meaning, aligned with the all-or-none processes14,32. In contrast, tasks that focused on perceptual features, such as stimulus color or shape, aligned with the gradual processes5,33.

However, a previous study examined the developmental aspect of the LoPH and revealed that the emergent process of higher-order processing of number magnitude judgments was more gradual than that of lower-order processing of color judgments in preschoolers compared with adults7. This suggested that the level of processing differed between preschoolers and adults. However, the magnitude of the numbers may be more difficult to judge than color judgments. No studies have examined the direct relationship between numerical magnitude and color discrimination. Nonetheless, previous studies revealed that preschoolers (especially second graders) were less accurate in estimating numerical magnitude than schoolchildren34, while little difference was observed in color discrimination between 3-year-olds and older children and adults9. Studies on the LoP examined various combinations of low-order and high-order processing tasks21, such as color and numbers11,25,26, color and categories23,28, colors and phonemes24, letter detection and phonemes35, and letter and shape location and identification27,29. Preschoolers could perform category judgments, such as animals or other objects36. Therefore, in our study, we assume that there is no significant difference in the difficulty level between a color and a category judgement tasks. We employed colored illustrations as stimuli for the level of stimulus-processing hypothesis and administered color and category judgments as a lower-order and higher-order processing tasks, respectively. We chose images considered familiar to preschoolers as well as adults37; subsequently, we checked whether preschoolers could name them via a pilot study.

Purpose

To summarize previous findings, adults tended to have a more all-or-nothing (that is, binomial manner around threshold) and gradual emergent process in the higher-order and lower-order processing tasks, respectively. Meanwhile, preschoolers were more gradual in the higher-order processing task than lower-order processing task. Previous studies on adults revealed that performance on higher-level tasks, such as category judgment, improved rapidly as the stimulus onset asynchronies (SOA) between the target and mask stimulus increased compared with lower-level tasks, such as color judgment; however, this was not the case for preschoolers. This result may be owing to the possibility that the number magnitude judgment task used in the higher-order processing task in the previous study was difficult for preschoolers. This study set the category judgment task, which was easy for preschoolers to answer, as the higher-order processing task. Difficulty levels of the higher-order and lower-order processing tasks were aligned. Furthermore, we set the SOA to < 100 ms for adults based on previous studies11,14; however, it was difficult to use the same conditions for preschoolers6,7. Hence, we set the SOA to more than twice that of adults based on a previous study6. We measured the objective discrimination accuracy as the objective measures and Subjective Perceptual Awareness Scale (PAS; 1: not visible, 2: slightly visible, 3: almost visible, and 4: clearly visible) rating as the subjective awareness measures. We used the word “visible” instead of “experience” to make it easier for participants to understand6,7.

To examine the threshold and emergent processes of visual consciousness, we measured the midpoint and range of the model’s slope via the psychometric function5. This study aimed to identify differences in the level of processing between preschoolers and adults via an in-person experiment. Since the tasks were designed for preschoolers, direct comparison of these results with those of adults was difficult. Therefore, we focused on the interaction between age and task to identify differences in the thresholds and emergent processes of higher- and lower-order processing tasks in preschoolers and adults. We predicted the results of the in-person experiment based on a previous online experiment7.

If the level of processing was the same for preschoolers and adults, we expected no interaction effects between age and task in the emergent processes and thresholds.

Conversely, if the level of processing differed, we expected interaction effects between age and task in the emergent processes and thresholds.

We further measured the proportion of responses for middle PAS ratings (2: slightly visible and 3: almost visible) near the threshold to examine the intermediate experience. If the emergent processes were gradual, the intermediate responses would increase. Previous research revealed that middle PAS ratings were more often used in a lower-order processing task around the threshold for conscious perception24.

The hypotheses and predictions of this study have been discussed.

Hypotheses

All hypotheses and methods were preregistered (https://osf.io/mzryn). We clarified the hypotheses to eliminate ambiguity during the revision. We hypothesized that the level of processing pattern would differ between preschoolers and adults and predicted an interaction between age and task. We assessed this prediction across three outcome domains. First, for subjective awareness, we predicted a significant interaction between age and task for the threshold and emergent process of the PAS (Hypothesis 1). Second, for objective discrimination, we predicted a significant interaction between age and task for objective discrimination accuracy (Hypothesis 2). Third, for intermediate awareness, we predicted a significant interaction between age and task for the proportion of intermediate PAS ratings (scores 2–3) at near threshold (Hypothesis 3).

Methods

Participants

Table 1 presents the participants’ information. Seventeen 5–6-year-olds preschoolers (mean age = 6 years, SD = 0.29, ten females) and sixteen adults (mean age = 23 years and 6 months, SD = 2.37, five females) without any eye-related diseases (e.g., color vision or low visual acuity) or neuro-developmental disorders completed the tasks. The teachers or parents of the child participants, and the adult participants themselves, declared whether they had been diagnosed with eye diseases or neuro-developmental disorders prior to the experiment. Two additional preschoolers participated in the experiment; however, they did not complete the tasks. We decided on the sample size based on previous research, where the typical sample size for a visual awareness task for adults and preschoolers was approximately twelve–twenty6,7,11,14. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Unit for Advanced Studies of the Human Mind, Kyoto University (No. 1-P-21). Written informed consent was obtained from the adult participants and parents of all the child participants. Experiments were conducted in a spare room at a nursery school or university laboratory. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Stimuli & apparatus

We used 60 line-drawing illustrations constructed from 30 animals (e.g., cow or deer) and 30 objects (e.g., sofa or envelope) from an image set from a previous research37, colored red or blue (RGB values of red = 255, 0, 0; blue = 0, 0, 255) as target stimuli (e.g., red lined cow or blue lined sofa; Fig. 1). We used illustrations that preschoolers could recognize in the pilot study. Random multicolored patches from the red and blue colors and letters “##” were used as a backward mask stimulus. Stimuli were presented on a gray background (RGB values of gray = 125, 125, 125). Participants viewed the stimulus approximately 60 cm from their PC on a 14-inch monitor size, 60 Hz refresh rate and 1920 × 1080 pixel resolution. We set the size of the target and mask stimuli to 5 cm × 5 cm on the screen (Fig. 1). The tasks were performed via PsychoPy (v2022.2.1).

Design of the stimuli and procedure of the task. Target stimuli were colored illustrations and masking stimuli were randomly multicolored patches. Participants responded to the objective discrimination questions, “Was the illustration red or blue?” and “Was the illustration an animal or object?” and subjective awareness questions “How clearly did you see the illustration?” on the arranged 4-scale Perceptual Awareness Scale (PAS; 1: not visible, 2: slightly visible, 3: almost visible, and 4: clearly visible.)

Procedure

Participants completed the two tasks within approximately 20–30 min. Figure 1 illustrates the design of the procedure, which was similar to our previous studies6,7. Participants completed two judgement tasks, color and category judgement, in separate five blocks. Task order was counterbalanced across participants; that is, some performed the color judgement blocks first, whereas others began with the category judgement blocks. Participants judged whether the illustration was red or blue and an animal or object in the color and category judgment tasks, respectively. In both the tasks, each trial began with a black fixation cross displayed centrally for 1000 ms. Subsequently, the target stimulus appeared for one of six durations (0, 16.7, 66.7, 116.7, 166.7, and 216.7 ms in preschoolers; 0, 16.7, 33.3, 50, 66.7, and 83.3 ms in adults). Previous studies revealed that SOA conditions of 100 ms and subsequent conditions were difficult for preschoolers6,7. Hence, we set the SOA for young children higher than that for adults. Participants judged whether the illustration was red/blue or animal/object by touching one of the two panels (red and blue or animal and object). They evaluated the target stimulus on the 4-scale PAS by touching one of the four panels (1: not visible, 2: slightly visible, 3: almost visible, and 4: clearly visible). Participants conducted five blocks and each block consisted of 12 trials, including 2 catch trials. In the catch trial, no target stimulus was presented (SOA = 0 ms) to assess whether the participants responded correctly (e.g., participants who answered that the stimulus was “not visible” were coded as correct). Thus, participants conducted 50 main and 10 catch trials in five blocks for each task, respectively.

Data analysis

Data exclusion

We excluded participants if their performance in the catch trials was below 50% (i.e., fewer than 5 correct out of 10) and if the model fitting resulted in a decision coefficient of R² < 0.5.

Statistical analysis

First, we analyzed the task and SOA differences in objective discrimination accuracy and subjective awareness ratings between the preschoolers and adults. We conducted a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on discrimination accuracy as objective discrimination performance and PAS for each SOA. Independent variables were task (category and color judgment task) and SOA (16.7, 66.7, 116.7, 166.7, and 216.7 ms in preschoolers and 16.7, 33.3, 50, 66.7, and 83.3 ms in adults).

Second, we fitted a four-parameter non-linear model (Eq. (1)) to objective discrimination performance and subjective awareness ratings via MATLAB (MATLAB R2024b).

Parameters a and b reflected the lower and upper boundaries of the psychometric function, respectively (i.e., a = 0 and b = 1 for objective discrimination performance and a = 1 and b = 4 for subjective awareness ratings). Parameter c denoted the center of the slope of the psychometric function; therefore, we considered it as the threshold. Parameter d was the measure of the steepness of the slope; therefore, we considered it as the emergent process of consciousness. A larger parameter d indicated a more gradual model slope. Subsequently, we analyzed task and age differences in the threshold and gradualness of the slope of the objective discrimination and subjective awareness. We conducted a model analysis and two-way ANOVA on the threshold and gradualness of the slope of the objective discrimination and subjective awareness. Since children and adults had different SOAs, direct examination of the effect of age was not possible; subsequent analysis examined the effect of task and the interaction between task and age.

For the model analysis, we formulated a general linear model using the Bayesian estimation through the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method (family = gaussian, set prior = “”, class = “Intercept” and “sigma”, iter = 10000, warmup = 1000, total post-warmup samples = 4000, thin = 1, adapt delta = 0.95) using brms package38,39 of R programming language (version 4.0.3). Subsequently, we judged whether the upper and lower 95% CI included 0 or not. If the upper and lower 95% included 0, there would be no significant difference. The model for the threshold was “Parameter c ~ task * age + (1|Participant)” and gradualness of the slope was “Parameter d ~ task * age + (1|Participant).” We set the participants as random effects.

We conducted a two-way ANOVA on the threshold and gradualness of the slope. The independent variables were task and age group.

Furthermore, we examined the proportion of intermediate PAS ratings (values 2–3) near the threshold as an indicator of the graduality of the emergent process. We predicted that if the emergence process of consciousness were gradual, their proportion would be higher. For SOAs around the threshold in both adults and preschoolers, we used the proportion of the PAS ratings with values of 2–3 as the dependent variable and age and task as the independent variables.

We conducted a post-hoc analysis if a significant difference was observed in the interaction. In the pre-registration phase, we planned to use only the model analysis via the Bayesian estimation. We also conducted a repeated ANOVA for the analyses as the range of the 95% confidence interval of the model analyses was too large and results could be underestimated.

Discussion

This study examined the thresholds and emergent processes of objective discrimination and subjective awareness in preschoolers and adults with color and category judgement via a backward masking task. We hypothesized that the level of processing pattern would differ between preschoolers and adults and predicted an interaction between age and task. Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3 were based on subjective awareness, objective discrimination, and intermediate awareness, respectively. Results revealed the main effect of age; however, no main effect of task and interaction between age and task were observed in the thresholds and emergent processes in subjective awareness and objective discrimination and proportion of intermediate awareness. Therefore, no hypotheses were supported. Results indicated that the center of the slope of the model fitted with subjective awareness and objective discrimination performance was larger for preschoolers than for adults for both color and category judgments. Furthermore, the range of the slope of the model was also larger. This suggested that visual awareness in preschoolers occurred more gradually with larger thresholds than in adults for both color and category judgments. Our finding that preschoolers’ thresholds were larger than those of adults was consistent with those of previous research6,7.

Age differences in the gradualness of the emergent process could be interpreted as follows. Adults tended to be more bivalent and preschoolers tended to be more gradual and changed from gradual to bivalent with age. According to the global neuronal workspace theory, the global workspace—closely related to working memory—and the fronto-parietal network are critical for the emergence of consciousness, and the theory predicts that consciousness is bivalent3,14,40–43. Working memory and other executive functions develop dramatically in preschool and keep improving into late childhood44–52. During this time, the fronto-parietal50–55 and ventral visual pathway56–58 for color and category are still maturing. Since these systems are not yet fully developed, preschoolers may experience visual consciousness more gradually and slowly, rather than in the “all-or-none” way of visual consciousness in adults. It should also be noted that the SOA for children was set longer than that for adults, which may have influenced the results, although this adjustment was necessary to ensure that children could adequately perform the task. Moreover, a previous study using an online paradigm reported age-related differences only in objective discrimination, but not in subjective awareness7. In contrast, the present study and another previous study using an in-person paradigm6 revealed differences in both objective discrimination and subjective awareness. Taken together, these findings suggest that while objective discrimination may be relatively robust across online and in-person contexts, subjective awareness appears more sensitive to the conditions under which the experiment is conducted.

We also found that the frequency of the use of intermediate PAS values near the threshold was higher in adults than in preschoolers; however, no main effect of task and interaction between age and task was observed. This indicated that adults responded with more graded responses, such as slightly visible, almost visible, and near the threshold, compared with preschoolers. Hence, preschoolers could be more immature in metacognition than adults and exhibit a tendency toward binary responses59–64.

This study examined the level of processing hypothesis in preschoolers via category and color judgments as the higher-order and lower-order processing tasks, respectively. Our results did not support the LoPH, which indicated that the emergent process of lower-order processing was more gradual than that of higher-order processing. This result was consistent with those of previous studies, which used number magnitude and color judgments as a higher-order and lower-order processing tasks, respectively7. These findings suggested that the emergent process of consciousness did not differ significantly between higher- and lower-order processing in preschoolers. The emergent process of visual consciousness may shift from a gradual to a dichotomous one as the frontal-parietal networks develop in preschoolers. However, the development may be more salient in higher-order processing. Thus, higher-order processing may be more dichotomous in adults than lower-order processing in the emergent process of consciousness.

This study has several limitations. First, we considered a limited relationship between color and category when assessing the stimulus processing level hypothesis, which must be considered along with other stimuli, such as in previous research6,7. Second, the preschoolers’ age, 5–6 years old, is also limited. Although age differences between preschoolers and adults were identified, we did not examine age-related changes, such as in older children or adolescence. Future studies should examine the trajectory by which their developmental process of the threshold of visual consciousness reaches the same level as adults. Third, preschoolers’ SOA was larger than that of adults; therefore, it constrains the interpretation of age-related differences. Preschoolers’ thresholds could be larger and the slopes gradual. However, based on previous studies and preliminary experiments6,7, preschoolers had difficulty performing tasks set at the same SOA as adults; hence, the results can be considered robust at least regarding the threshold. Future studies should consider the related factors for development of visual consciousness. Relevant factors include cognitive and cerebral development.

Summarizing the findings of previous studies and this study (e.g., the color and category judgement tasks as the lower-order and higher-order processing tasks, respectively) on visual consciousness in preschoolers and adults revealed no differences in the emergent processes or thresholds of visual consciousness based on the level of processing of stimuli in preschoolers. However, preschoolers’ emergent processes were more gradual and thresholds higher compared with adults, regardless of the level of processing of stimuli. Furthermore, the development of visual consciousness may be related to cognitive and cerebral development. Future studies should examine the underlying mechanisms of the development of visual consciousness.
