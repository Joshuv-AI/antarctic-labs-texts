A survey of deep learning techniques in detecting neurological disorders using MRI
Authors: Deepak Mane, Ranjeet Bidwe, Rivan Shetty, Atharva Shinde, Gaurav Shende, Vishal Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is widely regarded as the most reliable non-invasive imaging modality for detecting neurological disorders. However, manual interpretation of MRI scans is often time-consuming and prone to inter-observer variability, which can lead to inconsistencies in diagnosis. The global burden of neurological disorders—including Alzheimer’s disease, brain tumors, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and schizophrenia—continues to increase, creating an urgent demand for accurate, scalable, and automated diagnostic solutions. In recent years, deep learning (DL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for medical image analysis, enabling automated feature extraction and improved diagnostic performance in neuroimaging applications. This survey provides a comprehensive analysis of deep learning approaches for MRI-based detection of neurological disorders. A systematic review of 47 research articles published between 2019 and 2025 is conducted, covering over 40 deep learning architectures evaluated on 34 publicly available and clinical datasets. The study categorizes and critically examines convolutional neural networks (CNNs), Vision Transformers (ViTs), hybrid CNN–Transformer models, and other specialized deep learning frameworks developed for neurological disease detection and classification. Comparative analyses are presented across different neurological conditions, highlighting model performance, dataset characteristics, evaluation protocols, and computational requirements. Furthermore, the survey identifies emerging architectural trends and evaluates the relative strengths and limitations of existing approaches with respect to generalization, interpretability, and clinical applicability. Key research gaps are highlighted, including limited cross-institutional validation, dataset heterogeneity, insufficient explainability, and challenges in real-world clinical deployment. Finally, the paper outlines promising research directions such as multimodal learning, self-supervised representation learning, and robust cross-domain generalization to enhance the reliability and clinical translation of MRI-based deep learning systems.

Introduction

Health systems worldwide face a major challenge from neurological disorders because these conditions stand as the leading reasons for disabilities and fatalities across all countries. The Global Burden of Disease Study has published new epidemiological research which demonstrates that both the occurrence and effects of nervous system disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, have reached higher levels than before [1, 2]. The medical field requires accurate diagnostic methods to detect diseases at their initial stage because these methods improve patient outcomes and enable medical professionals to start treatment earlier while minimizing future healthcare requirements.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) serves as the primary non-invasive brain imaging technique which medical professionals use to detect structural brain abnormalities. The high spatial resolution and soft-tissue contrast capabilities of MRI enable its primary use in detecting brain tumors, neurodegenerative atrophy, demyelinating lesions, and structural changes in psychiatric disorders. Experts in radiology spend a lengthy time to evaluate MRI scans because traditional MRI analysis methods require their expertise, but this system suffers from differences in assessment results between different observers. The initial studies on artificial intelligence use in neurology demonstrated that automated neuroimaging analysis could produce useful results yet encountered various operational challenges for implementation [3, 4].

The development of traditional machine learning methods began with their application in MRI diagnosis, which used standardized feature extraction methods to create texture descriptors and volumetric measurements and region-based statistical measurements. The methods provided better results than manual operations, yet they needed specific domain knowledge to create features and their performance decreased when applied to different types of datasets [4].

Deep learning (DL) emerged as a field which made place for Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to improve automatic MRI analysis through its capacity to extract features from unprocessed imaging data. CNN-based architectures have been widely applied across neurological disorders, including pediatric brain tumor detection and segmentation [5–8], Parkinson’s disease classification using 3D MRI volumes [9–12], schizophrenia detection [13], and Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis [14]. The models decrease their reliance on manual feature creation while achieving effective results in both classification and segmentation activities. The brain regions show sustained structural dependencies which develop over extended distances, yet CNNs use local receptive fields as their main processing method. This dependency limits their ability to model essential brain connections, which become critical in studying multiple neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders.

The medical image analysis field has gained access to Transformer-based architectures as a solution for its existing limitations. Vision Transformers (ViTs) and their variants use self-attention mechanisms to create global contextual relationships between the image patches and volumetric tokens. The combination of Transformer-based models and hybrid CNN–Transformer systems has demonstrated effective performance for Alzheimer’s disease classification [15–25], brain tumor detection and segmentation [26–29], and multimodal dementia diagnosis using MRI and PET data [30, 31]. The approaches work to establish global feature modeling while they strengthen strength across different patient imaging groups.

Recent research studies investigate multimodal fusion, graph-based reasoning, and self-supervised pretraining approaches to create better generalization and diagnostic reliability for pure Transformer frameworks and their advanced applications [32–36]. The methods work to solve real-world problems which involve both insufficient labeled data and class imbalance and cross-institutional variation and multimodal data that remain incomplete. Deep learning methods for all neurological disorders have expanded their research field [37–39].

The existing research literature shows incomplete development because new medical knowledge has advanced faster than scientists can create comprehensive studies about different diseases and architectural systems. Existing surveys have either provided early overviews of deep learning in neurology [3, 4] or focused on specific disorders. There exists a gap in research that needs to be filled through a study which compares CNN-based systems with Transformer-based systems and hybrid systems across various neurological disorders which include Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease and brain tumors and multiple sclerosis and schizophrenia.

Literature survey

Recent progress in neuroimaging involves more and more applications of deep learning (DL) and Transformer-based models in early detection, classification, and segmentation of neurological conditions, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), brain tumors, multiple sclerosis (MS), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and schizophrenia. The CNNs were learned with hybridization into CNN–Transformer models, and fully Transformer-based models were proposed; cross-interplays, therefore, started to happen between multimodality, longitudinal data and explainability–generalization–clinical translation.

Architectural evolution: from CNNs to transformers

The current research demonstrates that deep learning methods for detecting neurological disorders through MRI imaging have progressed from using traditional Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to adopting both hybrid CNN–Transformer and entirely Transformer-based system designs. Early studies established CNNs as dominant models for feature extraction and classification in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) brain tumors and multiple sclerosis (MS) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) and schizophrenia because their local spatial representation capability [3–5, 40, 41] proved to be effective. The technology suffered from restricted receptive fields which prevented it from capturing long-range anatomical connections through volumetric and longitudinal MRI data. Hybrid architectures were developed to combine convolutional inductive bias with self-attention mechanisms, which achieved better classification results because attention maps and region-of-interest analysis provided interpretable results in AD and tumor diagnosis [15, 18–21, 23]. The interaction among features reached its peak enhancement through bottleneck transformers and fusion transformers, which accomplished this while keeping computational expenses at a lower rate [14, 22, 42]. The development of fully Transformer-based frameworks enabled researchers to conduct longitudinal multimodal learning through temporal progression modeling and MRI–PET integration for dementia classification [16, 24, 30, 31, 34, 35, 43]. The models achieve better context recognition and diagnostic accuracy but they remain resource demanding because their operational needs exceed conventional computational limits and they depend on extensive high-quality training data for successful system performance.

Disease-specific applications and challenges

Researchers use different research methods to study specific diseases. Longitudinal modeling and graph-based learning methods were used to study subtle neurodegenerative disease progression in AD research [16, 25, 33, 34]. Scientists in brain tumor research use BraTS-PEDs standard benchmarks to evaluate CNN-based segmentation methods, which include nnU-Net and DeepMedic, and they found that Vision Transformer variants provide better performance against rotational difficulties and increased system reliability [6–8, 26, 27, 29, 44]. The MS studies use self-ensembling and synthetic lesion augmentation techniques to study lesion segmentation and inter-dataset generalization through the analysis of PediMS and MSLesSeg data [36, 45–48]. Researchers used 3D CNNs and multimodal MRI data to develop PD classification methods, which predict subtypes and forecast outcomes through analysis of multi-center datasets [9–12]. Scientists discovered four main research patterns, which include the use of attention-based architectures for global context modeling and researchers who study multiple disorders through their application of multi-center standardized datasets, which became more important for their research needs, while scientists achieved better results through improved explainability methods. Future research needs to solve four major problems, which include computational scalability, scanner variability, dataset harmonization, and clinical translation, while researchers need to create diagnostic procedures that combine architectural complexity with efficient systems and cross-site generalizations.

Biological and imaging constraints by disorder

The disproportionate research focus on Alzheimer’s disease and brain tumors, relative to Parkinson’s disease and multiple sclerosis, reflects biological signal visibility and dataset infrastructure rather than purely scientific prioritization. Alzheimer’s disease benefits from large standardized repositories which include ADNI and OASIS because the disease manifests as distributed cortical atrophy that structural MRI can detect, which deep CNN and Transformer architectures can process although researchers may overestimate their generalization capabilities through repeated benchmarking in shared datasets. Brain tumors produce high-contrast space-occupying lesions that have well-defined boundaries which naturally lead to better results with convolutional segmentation models, which explains the high Dice score results that show the ability to separate lesions. Parkinson's disease shows weak detection capacity through standard structural MRI because its pathological changes affect dopaminergic pathways that anatomical assessment cannot detect while its smaller and more diverse research groups diminish the strength of detection systems. Multiple sclerosis introduces additional difficulty through small irregular and radiologically ambiguous lesions which create major difficulties for raters because they produce large differences that limit the maximum accuracy of segmentation work regardless of which advanced technologies are used. The deep learning performance evaluation across different disorders gains methodical bias because researchers need to disclose all biological and annotation restrictions which they must include in their study.

Summary

The survey results reveal that research activities show uneven distribution across different neurological disorders. Researchers conduct most of their studies on Alzheimer’s disease because they can access well-organized public databases while clinicians require early detection methods to treat patients. Researchers conduct fewer studies on Parkinson's disease and schizophrenia because they face challenges with existing imaging databases, patients show more diverse symptoms and the diagnostic process involves multiple complex methods.

This difference shows that there is a big gap in research in the field of neuroimaging-based artificial intelligence. Future research should focus on underrepresented disorders by facilitating the creation of extensive, multi-institutional datasets that incorporate multimodal imaging and clinical biomarkers, as well as developing disorder-specific modeling frameworks that can encapsulate intricate disease variability. To make sure that AI-driven neurodiagnostic have a bigger impact on the whole clinical picture, we need to fix this imbalance.

Methodology

Study design and protocol

This systematic review was performed in accordance with the 2020 PRISMA guidelines. An a priori protocol was determined prior to literature screening and strictly adhered to. Procedures, such as search structure, screening rationale, data extraction templates, and risk-of-bias criteria, were all established before the full-text review to avoid any retrospective decision-making. There was a considerable amount of heterogeneity across the datasets, model architectures, tasks, and metrics for reported scores, making quantitative meta-analysis unfeasible.

Data sources and search strategy

The data search was performed through Pubmed, Scopus, IEEE xplore, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, arXiv, and SpringerLink, ranging from January 2019 to February 2025. The search was implemented by using controled vocabulary and free-text terms into deep learning, structural MRI, and neurological disorders without restrictions for language or geography. The studies were all manually screened by reference and were included in the list of grey literature included.

Boolean template: (“magnetic resonance imaging” OR MRI) AND (“deep learning” OR “neural network” OR CNN OR transformer OR “transfer learning” OR autoencoder) AND (neurological OR brain OR glioma OR tumor OR glioblastoma OR Alzheimer* OR dementia OR meningioma OR stroke OR Parkinson* OR sclerosis OR epilepsy) AND (classification OR detection OR segmentation OR diagnosis).

Small-sample retention

Studies with small neurological MRI datasets, such as PediMS (n = 45), were retained if they provided interpretable metrics and met risk-of-bias criteria. Sensitivity analyses excluded studies with fewer than 100 subjects to assess distortion from small-sample effects, with the threshold chosen based on common practice in imaging-AI literature to mitigate model instability.

The measures of Accuracy and AUC were placed at the forefront of evaluation metric considerations, while Precision, Recall, and F1-score played a complementary role. An inflated measure of Accuracy can be deceptive for datasets having imbalanced class distributions; hence, extra metrics were considered for a fair appraisal.

For segmentation tasks, Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) was taken to be the primary metric, while Intersection-over-Union (IoU) served as a secondary measure. These metrics prioritize spatial overlap between predicted and ground-truth regions rather than class ratios.

For detection tasks, Sensitivity was placed at the forefront in order to emphasize the identification of cases declared as positive with high certainty, while F1-score and mean Average Precision (mAP) gave a secondary consideration to the balance of precision and recall. The bias for true positive ratio therefore acts to prioritize clinically relevant detections.

For the prediction of progression, which is described as a regression or a continuous outcome-based problem, the metrics of AUC and correlation coefficients were set to function as the primary metrics, while the root mean square error (RMSE) tried to function as a secondary metric for quantifying the deviation from the actual ground truth values of the progression. The combination accounts for both ranking performance and absolute prediction error in truly continuous prediction settings.

Observation

This section provides a summary report of the main results of the 47 studies concerning the aspect of detecting neurological disorders through MRI with the use of the deep learning methods. These findings have been organized in such a way that they are comparatively analyzed with previous studies in the area. Based on the methodology described in “Literature survey” section, the discussion starts with graphical visualization of the most famous deep learning models and architectures used in neuroimaging. Following figures and tables result in following trends: dataset distribution, relative performance compared with models and performance lineups by model category and disease type. Such information-based methodology enables to directly compare the best research models, techniques, and datasets using the right data, revealing methodology weaknesses and gaps in research. In every image, a specific discussion is provided to emphasize the strengths and weaknesses as well as the emergent trends, such as the recent tendency toward more and more hybrid structures and the failure to adequately represent multiple neurological conditions in the studies conducted so far.

The comparison metrics of classification and segmentation studies were considered strictly within task categories, without any comparisons across the domains. For example, one could not compare classification Accuracy directly with segmentation Dice values. The chosen classification frame ensured that the performance comparison was methodologically sound so as to account for disparities in datasets, output types, and class prevalence.

Discrepant accuracy can be misleading when looking at medical datasets with classes that are unbalanced. For instance; in the classifications of Alzheimer's or in the segmentation of tumors, a model can have an accuracy of 99% by misreporting almost all clinically relevant positive cases while assuming predictions of the majority class. Hence, emphasis was given to metrics of Sensitivity, Specificity, F1, and Dice for proper evaluations.

The evolution of methodological development of clinical neuroimaging with deep learning shows a gradual transition from local feature extraction models on architectures with the ability to detect both short- and long-range dependencies as shown in Fig. 3. The medical imaging as compared to the natural image tasks has distinct challenges like low number of annotated datasets, high inter-patient variability, and noise that varies with modality. Timeline of progression of deep learning in neuroimaging

CNN era (2019–2020)

Earlier implementations in neurological imaging analysis: early implementations of deep learning in the area were primarily based on CNNs, including 3D CNNs, ResNet and Bayesian-optimized versions. Analogies: these models demonstrated great learning ability of local patterns including edges, textures, and boundaries of the tissue that greatly outpaced the established machine learning processes. They however, worked more locally and therefore they rarely could generalize their findings to produce more complex neurological disorder/diseases due to global structural relationship within the brain thus restricting their extrapolation to more complicated neurological diseases. The relatively low amount of large-scale medical imaging data also limited their capacity to scale, in comparison to CNN advances in natural image applications such as ImageNet.

Transformer emergence (2022)

The introduction of attention mechanisms to transformers brought a paradigm shift as models taking the whole scan at once now had a chance of consideration. Jaiswal et al. [42] and Architectures Bottleneck Transformer and Kushol et al. [22] AddFormer capitalized on this capability by attempting to capture long-range dependencies in forecasting more robust representations of neuroimaging data. However, as indicated, this potential was negated by the data-hungry character of the transformers since medical data are small, skewed and heterogeneous most of the time. Unlike natural vision, which can use millions of samples due to natural data, clinical images data tend to be just under a few thousand patients, and it is hard to pretrain and generalize effectively because of the small scale.

Advancement of hybrids and advanced ViTs (2024)

Combined with the limitations of CNNs and transformers, hybrid learning styles were developed, including the 3D Hybrid Compact Convolutional Transformer, rotation-invariant forms of ViT. These models of CNNs were based on finer spatial details in the structure of the majority of datasets, fused with a feature of transformers to model the global context, delivering the state-of-the-art accuracy across various datasets. Their flexibility and greater strength put them a next step ahead in real-world applications of neuroimaging in practice. However, their performance is still (in part) limited by the bottlenecks of annotation since natural image-labeling is crowdsourced unlike annotation of either MRI or PET scans which need clinical expertise.

Ensembles and specialized architectures (2025)

Embarking on ensembles and multimodal structure, the latest trends include Swin-UNETR and multimodal vision transformers. Ensembles help to increase the reliability of diagnostic tests through the aggregation of varying model estimates, whereas multimodal systems strive to incorporate imaging with other additional sources of data (e.g., fMRI or clinical metadata). Despite the above promising directions reported about models, they are still highly computationally expensive, and can be frequently limited by the difficulty of aligning heterogeneous data modalities. Lack of large standardized and multimodal benchmarks also makes the reproducibility and comparability of such models difficult.

Underperforming approaches

Some of the architectures have failed to materialize their expected potentials. GNNs have been found to work poorly because they are ill-posed to brain connectivity modeling, with noisy graphs construction and few available labeled graph-based data. On the same note, multimodal methods were particularly underperforming due to lack of uniformity or complete dataset in clinical cohorts thereby decreasing their generalization capability. Medical data heterogeneity can confound representation learning unlike the case of natural images where variability enhances representation learning.

Summary

The path of CNNs through transformers, hybrids, and ensembles signifies the change in the methodology tilted toward localized or holistic expression of neuroimaging data. Every next step was aiming to correct the error of its predecessor and also setting in new challenges of inability to compute and randomness of data scarcity. The natural factors of medical imaging such as scarcity of annotations, inter-cohort variability, and privacy of medical data prevent its faster advancement than that of mainstream computer vision. The future development of research is probably concerned with the solutions to those issues, which are scalable, explainable, and have many modes to define multimodal requirements in the broad settings of a clinical setting. Overall developing a model is not only issue but to train it needs large amount of valid medical data for better results.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs)

The operational backbone of MRI analysis currently depends on CNNs because they can extract local spatial features from volumetric data with high efficiency. The actual level of their success gets overstated in most cases. The deeper CNNs increase their receptive field coverage through brute-force parameter scaling instead of using essential brain-to-brain relationship modeling which connects different brain regions. The majority of studies use depth expansion and ensemble stacking to achieve slight accuracy gains but they fail to disclose their actual computational requirements and training process stability and their points of system failure. The practice of scaling in small-cohort clinical datasets leads to overfitting because it fails to provide actual benefits in representational accuracy.

Transformers

Transformer-based models are increasingly adopted to model long-range dependencies via self-attention. In theory, this aligns well with distributed neurodegenerative patterns. In practice, their performance gains are often incremental relative to optimized 3D CNN baselines, while computational cost rises substantially. Few studies provide FLOPs, memory usage, or inference latency, despite proposing deployment-oriented claims. Moreover, Transformer performance is highly sensitive to dataset size and annotation quality, yet most neuroimaging datasets remain limited in scale. The result is an architectural–data mismatch that is rarely acknowledged.

U‑Net and variants

The U-Net variants dominate segmentation tasks because their design matches the requirements of these tasks. The majority of reported improvements in research studies show that actual progress results from two factors which include specific dataset tuning methods combined with extreme data augmentation techniques and preprocessing systems. The current methods for testing across different datasets show limited capabilities which results in doubts about the system's ability to function in real-world scenarios. The reported improvements in Dice scores only provide minimal benefits which do not result in significant changes to medical imaging segmenting results.

Hybrid deep learning models

The strong performance results of CNN–Transformer hybrids and graph-enhanced frameworks demonstrate that their combination of local and global feature modeling delivers effective results. The architectural complexity of these systems together with their interpretability challenges, create obstacles that organizations must address to achieve their benefits. The hybrid system requires additional resources which ablation studies cannot validate in many situations. The field now considers architectural fusion as a measure of progress, despite the fact that this practice produces only minor statistical improvements.

Generative adversarial networks (GANs)

GAN-based methods are used to solve problems which occur when working with limited sample sizes. There is need to perform stringent validation of synthetic data because it has the potential to increase dataset bias under specific circumstances. Self-supervised learning shows potential benefits yet multi-site testing and domain-shift studies do not demonstrate its alleged better generalization capacity. Scientists assess representation quality through downstream accuracy measurements rather than using direct evaluation methods.

Graph-based/hypergraph models

Graph neural networks together with hypergraph models work to model the regional connectivity patterns which correspond to the mechanisms that cause neurological diseases. The graph construction pipelines require preprocessing work which creates two essential dependencies that determine all downstream results. The performance differences exist because different methods define graphs which creates a confounding factor that researchers do not usually control.

The data in Table 1 show that segmentation and classification research exhibit strong architectural gaps, with Transformer hybrids now predominantly utilized in Alzheimer's models while nnU-Net variants, self-ensembled fusion, and others based on U-Net still form the solid backbone in tumor and MS lesions segmentation. The metrics demonstrate a strong relationship with the structural requirements which each task presents. Classification pipelines achieve 95–99% accuracy on both ADNI and OASIS databases yet Dice scores for lesion segmentation reach between 0.55 and 0.85 which demonstrates the difference between two difficulty levels because underlying anatomical accuracy needs to be improved at a voxel level and biomedical imaging segmentation needs to be solved through normalizing spatial variations between advanced encoders. The advantages which transformers show in performance become obvious yet their benefits do not apply to all situations and all use cases. The spatiotemporal transformer LongFormer demonstrates superior performance to static 3D CNNs because it uses longitudinal features for AD classification which enables it to detect disease progression better than anatomical measurements. Additional Transformer encoders provide no benefits for pediatric tumor and MS segmentation tasks since U-Net performance matches or exceeds Dice scores through its classical architecture and residual-attention variants. Transformers demonstrate their optimal performance through disease processes which lead to major changes in macrostructural geometry while they do not perform well in situations where sparse and irregular spatial targets require dense morphological priors to identify lesions.

The reported accuracy is influenced by cohort structure. If we take multi-institutional and especially pediatric cohorts, we get a number of lower Dice and accuracy values than those of the single-source ADNI/OASIS classification pipeline. It is not a fault of the model architecture that has been designed; what has happened here is an environmental limitation that has been masked by the other words in the homogeneous ADNI pipelines—population, scanner, and protocol. All these strikes us like impossibly high accuracies (> 98–99%), which seemed to be valid mostly in classification studies based on curated or internally balanced datasets indicating possible dataset bias and ceiling effects. Again, models are called top-line metrics for multimodal MRI + PET fusion or augmented OASIS subsets, but such generalizability is mathematically exaggerated due to the lack of true out-of-domain validation.

The selection of evaluation strategies tends to diverge widely, compromising interpretability. There is usually no guarantee of separate subject-level evaluation, especially in slice-based pipelines, which puts the processes at risk of possible information leakage and statistical inflation. Cross-validation protocols could be standard fivefold, challenge type with fixed splits, but only a handful among those mentioned here included external validation. A clearer pattern evolved from the studies that reported lower metrics and higher reliability, thanks to the use of true external validations, which speaks to the fact that within-dataset success is not a stable surrogate for clinical generalizability.

The disease targets of study also shape the outcome profiles. The classification of Alzheimer's disease is the methodological outlier while contains several Transformer hybrids, big cohorts, and public benchmarks, leaving it with the highest reproducibility and the most aggressive SOTA claims. Tumor and MS segmentation studies have shown a slower evolution of architectural techniques and better support for robustness, using explicit lesion maps, multi-sequence MRI, and rigorous spatial metrics. The studies on Parkinson's disease are fewer in number and methodologically less robust and largely depend on T1-MRI alone, lacking multimodal or deep longitudinal integration reflecting performance that is comparatively weaker.

In synthesis, Table 1 demonstrates that dataset structure influences performance claims more than the novelty of the model. The strong evidence arises, for example, when models are subjected to real cross-distribution evaluation within true subject-level separation and report measures across different spatial or probabilistic metrics instead of a single accuracy score. The other strong detriment, one which can be seen across the entire dataset, is the absence of any rigorous cross-domain benchmarking, especially when it comes to the more advanced AD models.

In Table 2, the screening results identify a pool of studies unambiguously within the confines of this review—intelligibly targeting neurological disorders using MRI-based deep learning models, with no exclusions at the eligibility stage—this apparent homogeneity masks deeper variance in evidential strength. This near-universal match between populations, modality, disease domain, and approach speaks to a highly filtered set of evidence, one that was defined by very rigorous inclusion criteria, the very nature of which was such that it was likely to favor those pipelines and approaches that had obvious dependencies on MRI and maturing DL pipelines. This, again, speaks to an unavoidable structural bias wherein the literature included was not representative of the field at large, but rather that segment of it that was most methodologically Another quality of evidence axis was full-text accessibility. Although all of these ultimately qualify, a significant heterogeneity exists in the amount of details given, between one extreme where the results of the Dice trajectories are given in full detail (such as papers on the segmentation method giving the results of the Dice trajectories), and the other extreme where only bare descriptions of the work are presented. Such heterogeneity produces a multi-layered evidence terrain whereby the evidence with complete procedures and quantitative outcomes to evaluate and synthesize, compared to evidence with abstracts as the sole source of information where analysis of directional outcomes can be offered, causes a problem of permitting inspection but decreasing transparency and generate uncertainty to internal validity of outcomes. The non-symmetry of depth in the text will have an inevitable effect on not only the degree of trust in the findings but also on the spread of the interpretative load throughout the dataset.

As opposed to MRI being an insignificant addition to the input structure of the model, multimodal MRI/PET studies were exploited, and this is an indicator that the problem is starting to be framed differently. The observable tendency is unimodal to integrated architectures (fMRI connectivity, PET fusion, cognitive risk prediction workflows), which indicates the onset of a shift in the definition of the problem, which implies the growth of the field. The trend is also indicative of clinical relevance, however the parallels with the past are hard to draw as the multimodal synergy may artificially overstimulate performance in comparison with MRI-only methods.

Quantitative reporting appeared in almost every study, but the comparability among them is weaker than this table may suggest. Tumor segmentation papers report overlap metrics; dementia classifiers report AUC, sensitivity and specificity; MCI conversion models rely on risk stratification accuracy; and aging or progression models report regression-derived error metrics. Thus while "Yes" in the quantitative column confirms numerical results, it does not guarantee metric coherence. The widely varying targets of disease (tumor, MS, AD/FTD/MCI, PD, general neurological categories) and task variability (classification, segmentation, progression prediction) must therefore be taken into consideration in the interpretation of cross-study synthesis in light of intrinsic heterogeneity.

Overall, the eligibility table denotes a field both maturing and fragmenting. Consistency in methodology at the structural level, which MRI modality and DL method matches provide, while it is the differences in accessibility of data, task framing, granularity of disease, and reporting depth that create layers of heterogeneity impacting the analysis downstream.

In Table 3, as many as 47 studies met the inclusion criteria; as such, most of the methodological quality was considered to be generally good, according to the QUADAS-2 assessment, with majority of the studies having low concern in the Index Test and Applicability domains. Most studies were at low risk in terms of bias in Index Test because most of them described the model architecture, training protocol, and evaluation workflows well, accompanied by a transparent report of their validation strategies. Specifically, in the case of the Reference Standard and Flow/Timing domains, the weightage of the Unclear was quite high, predominantly because there was poor reporting on the process in which the ground truth was truly labeled (e.g., number of) radiologists, inter-rater agreement, etc.), timing, imaging versus diagnosis and inadequate information given on how to divide the data set or the participants. These are not the problems of low quality but the problems of inadequate transparency in the conveying of the methodology, which restricts reproducibility as well as credibility to internal Risk of bias and applicability assessment (QUADAS-2)

The segmentation and multi-label classification studies carried out by most researchers were found to be inconsistent with the ones carried out. Research on classification of Alzheimer and glioma was reported. A minority of the research reported was as follows moderate risk of bias regarding the selection criterion of patients, like the use of a retrospective or convenience sampling technique. Nevertheless, the risk of bias with regard to the applicability criterion was regularly low, as the specifications to the models, the disease target and the clinical settings were all in accordance with the extent of the present time review. Moreover, any disparities observed in the population level can hardly have any effect on the interpretation of the data. In sum, the research had a low to moderate risk of bias, although the research gaps were in the reporting particularly with respect to the standard of references and time, generate uncertainties, which should be taken into account when analyzing the trends in performance.

Strengths and weaknesses of various deep learning models

But uniform trade-offs arise. In fact, the majority of the architectures based on the transformer require extensive computational resources as well as annotated datasets in order to be scalable and deployed. Generalizability is a recurring drawback: models which are trained on ADNI or BraTS can tend to underperform when transferred to other sites or modalities. Classical CNNs are effective in detecting the spatial features and fail to capture the global context, whereas RNN/LSTMs are effective in detecting time variations but fail to detect spatial complexity. GAN-based methods enhance data augmentation but are unstable and maybe biased.

Two clear insights emerge:Systematically, hybrid architectures (CNN–Transformer, CNN–RNN, graph-based hybrids) prove better than standalone models, due to the necessity to trade between local and global feature encoding.Computational overhead and sensitivity of the methods to the domain balance out the strengths of most of them, meaning that future studies should focus on the efficient training, multi-site validation and explainability.

In general, the table summarizes the trade-space of evolution: trade-offs in accuracy due to increasingly elaborate architectures are compensated by increased computation, increased annotation, and decreased cross-dataset generalization.

Table 6 displays a structural imbalance which exists in the current research about MRI-based deep learning methods for treating neurological disorders. Although 3D hybrid CNN–Transformer models and large-scale Vision Transformers drive architectural innovation forward, their computational transparency remains inconsistent and incomplete throughout their development process. The research only shows parameter counts and FLOPs in a minority of studies, while even fewer studies provide essential information about their training duration and inference latency. Temporal efficiency reporting remains sparse: training time (T) was provided in only six studies [7, 18, 20, 26, 34, 35] and inference time (I) in five [7, 18, 32, 34, 37], reinforcing the observation that computational practicality is secondary to accuracy-driven reporting. The researchers provide GPU information for more than half of their studies, but this information fails to enable reproducibility because VRAM constraints and batch size and optimization settings determine overall feasibility. All studies lack energy reporting, which demonstrates a complete disregard for sustainability requirements, even as researchers increasingly depend on high-end accelerators like A100 and RTX A6000 The second problem arises from increasing computational requirements which do not match the existing standards for reporting accuracy. The large Transformer-based models which contain more than 60 million parameters show performance improvements but their operational costs need assessment through actual deployment conditions. Researchers use batch sizes which range from 1 to 4 as their smallest size and 1024 as their largest size without providing any methodological explanation which creates difficulties for study comparison. The research field suffers from variable access to open-source materials which prevents researchers from conducting independent validation. The emerging pattern shows that researchers are developing methods which require more complex procedures than existing transparency standards allow. Researchers emphasize accuracy improvements but they consider computational expenses and scalability and cross-institutional reproducibility to be less important. The current situation requires researchers to stop developing new architectural designs and instead focus on creating products that meet efficiency standards while following existing reporting requirements and assessment methods for actual product performance.

Outcome of survey

This paper aims mainly to discuss the use of deep learning models to detect successfully and successfully neurological disorders based on the MRI imaging and image processing modalities. This review summarizes the findings of 48 research articles, claiming the developments made in neuroimaging by various technical models. To improve readability, the results have been provided in tabular and graphical forms where readers can draw important resolutions easily. The survey underlines the variety of models and methods used with the corresponding datasets, as well as the maximum reported performance rates, thus offering a comparative impression of the use of the datasets and accuracy achievements in the domain.

Synthesis of metric trends

Further, when the results are grouped by the type of metrics used, the studies on Alzheimer’s and tumor classifications show high values of Accuracy, Sensitivity, and AUC due to the large amount of data used and the distinctive features of the images. The Dice metrics for the segmentation results vary greatly due to the irregular shapes of the lesions, the inconsistencies of the human raters, and the class imbalance. This shows that the architectural excellence of the models cannot be determined solely by the values of the metrics used.

The difference in the performance as presented in the studies can be explained in terms of the three interacting factors: the anatomical peculiarities of each particular disorder, the size and content of available datasets, and the computation requirements of both architecture configurations. Tumor segmentation is always high. Dice effectivity as tumors develop intense contrast gradients and well stipulated lesion margins that are concomitant and with the spatial receptive fields of CNN. Also, Alzheimer’s disease classification achieves both high accuracy and high area under curve (AUC) values as a result of having an enormous amount of data with many aspects of anatomy represented by the images; thus providing sufficient data for the Global Attention model in the Transformer and Hybrid Architectures to learn and represent anatomically complex scenes. Conversely, MS Segmentation results demonstrate the volatility of Dice Scores as a result of being unable to identify the very small and irregularly shaped plaques in radiology images, resulting in even expert raters being in disagreement. Therefore, on average, Parkinson’s disease classification has been moderate in its ability to classify diseases regardless of architecture configuration; which represents the clinical setting’s reality—gross differences in MRI Intensity levels, combined with variability in whether the diagnostic information was structural, functional, molecular or anatomical. Therefore, these findings indicate that performance metrics cannot be comprehended without understanding biological visibility of signals, richness of data sets and strategies used in designing models simultaneously.

On the other hand, the contributions of small data sets such as PediMS, HMS, Cheng, Biocog and OpenNeuro Japan are minimal at only 1.3% indicating that they are either very rare or there has been little research into these diseases. This disproportionately suggests that even though there have been major developments in the diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease and tumors primarily based on the use of accessible data sets, the development of data sets in other neurology fields was not completely supported with better accuracy models.

Despite the clinical necessity of tracking disease progression in neurodegenerative disorders, the current literature exhibits a heavy reliance on cross-sectional datasets. Out of the 47 studies reviewed, only 6 (12.7%) utilized longitudinal data [16, 18, 35, 36, 45, 46] to model temporal trajectories, such as Alzheimer's progression or MS lesion evolution. The remaining 41 studies (87.3%) relied exclusively on cross-sectional data. This severe imbalance inherently restricts the ability of most current deep learning models to forecast patient-specific health changes or clinical conversion rates.

Conclusion

The deep learning techniques which include CNN–Transformer structures resulted in significant advancements between 2019 and 2025 for neuroimaging-based disease detection according to the survey results. The performance of the studies depends on which materials the researchers choose to use because the studies on Alzheimer disease and tumor research outnumber the studies on Parkinson multiple sclerosis autism and schizophrenia. The observation that accuracy rates exceed 99 percent in overrepresented conditions demonstrates structural bias instead of proving the universal models function effectively. The clinical environment needs to handle model complexity issues because they reduce both model interpretability and model usefulness. Hybrid models reach high performance levels, but they need more computational resources, and they become likely to overfit when used with small datasets, while simple graph-based methods can be executed but they fail to work in situations where diseases have low priority or show multiple diverse medical conditions. The above observations dictate the role of standardized benchmarking, external validation, and multi-center datasets in guaranteeing that evaluation of separate centers can be reproducible and meaningful to the clinics. The next generation of research aims at creating large heterogeneous balanced data on various neurological disorders to develop explainable AI, which will help researchers understand clinical models and develop ensemble and federated learning methods that improve resilience against data scarcity. The design of the studies should also possess computational efficiency, generalizability, and ethics. The field would achieve greater fairness and scientific rigor through a comprehensive systematic approach to handling dataset bias and methodological constraints in clinical applicability, which enables deep learning to apply across different neurological disorders beyond the artificial intelligence applications that currently exist for common diseases.
