Stereo-EEG associated anti-GAD65 autoimmune encephalitis – A report of two cases
Authors: Claude Steriade, Andrew Christiana, Giovanna Dane, Peter A. Rozman, Daniel Friedman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•SEEG can be followed by a subacute encephalopathy with GAD intrathecal synthesis.•An autoimmune workup should be considered prior to SEEG in TLE of unknown cause.•SEEG may induce autoimmune encephalitis through BBB disruption in prone individuals.

Introduction

Autoimmune encephalitis (AE) has been increasingly recognized as an uncommon cause of seizures that holds the potential for etiology-targeted immunotherapy, if identified early. Glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 (GAD65) antibodies are associated with autoimmune encephalitis associated epilepsy (AEAE), that can present with a temporal lobe epilepsy (GAD-TLE) phenotype. GAD-TLE is typically refractory to not only immunotherapy, likely due to its association with cytotoxic T cells and early structural injury, [1] but also antiseizure medications (ASMs) and epilepsy surgery [[2], [3], [4]]. Sometimes, a diagnosis of AEAE is made after epilepsy surgery [5]. The identification of neural autoantibodies prior to surgery has been emphasized by the development of clinical prediction tools aimed at the diagnosis of patients with neural autoantibodies prior to surgical intervention [6]. The surgical outcomes of persistent seizures after AE [5] and GAD-TLE [7] are poor, such that an early diagnosis prior to surgery may be preferable in order to effectively counsel patients on expected prognosis. The role of epilepsy surgery itself as a provoking factor for encephalitis has been confined to a case report of post-DBS LGI1 antibody encephalitis associated seizures [8].

Here we report on two cases who developed an AE syndrome after undergoing stereo-EEG (SEEG), with associated GAD antibody intrathecal synthesis. We hypothesize a role for blood brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction as a trigger for AE in susceptible individuals with adult onset TLE of unknown cause.

Methods

We collected the clinical characteristics of two subjects who developed an AE syndrome in the postoperative period, after SEEG explantation. GAD65 antibodies were tested in both subjects at Mayo Clinic Laboratories, through radioimmunoassay, as previously described [9]. GAD antibody index was calculated as follows: (CSF GAD antibodies / Serum GAD antibodies) / (CSF albumin / Serum albumin) [10]. A GAD antibody index of >4 was considered consistent with intrathecal synthesis [10]. The following antibodies were also tested in both patients: AMPA-R, CASPR2, DPPX, GABA-B, IgLON5, LGI1, NMDA-R (cell-based assay (CBA) confirmed with Immunofluorescence (IF)), AGNA-1, ANNA-1, ANNA-2, ANNA-3, CRMP-5, GFAP, mGluR1, Neurochondrin, NIF, PCA-1, PCA-2, PCA-Tr., PDE10, Septin-7, TRIM46 (IF followed by immunoblot if positive IF) at Mayo Clinic Laboratories. Both patients consented to their case being reported in this publication.

Clinical characteristics.

Case 1

A 61-year-old woman with drug-resistant TLE since age 44 underwent SEEG. She had tried and failed 11 ASMs. Electroclinical and noninvasive evaluations were consistent with dominant TLE, although discordant neuropsychological testing indicated nondominant temporal dysfunction. She underwent SEEG sampling of the left temporal, perisylvian, and orbitofrontal regions. Several subclinical seizures with left hippocampal and parahippocampal gyrus onset were recorded. She had no spontaneous electroclinical seizures, but several habitual seizures were provoked by bedside cortical stimulation of the left amygdala, hippocampus, and middle temporal gyrus. Routine head CT immediately after removal demonstrated two small (1.2 cm x 1.0 cm x 0.7 cm and 0.5 cm x 0.5 cm x 0.7 cm, AP x ML x CC) superficial intraparenchymal hematomas in the left middle temporal gyrus with associated subarachnoid hemorrhage along the prior electrode tracts. About one week after electrode removal, she developed a low-grade fever and headaches. A repeat MRI showed expected evolution of the hematomas. She received a presumptive diagnosis of aseptic meningitis and was treated with a short course of dexamethasone. Concurrently, she began experiencing worsening cognitive dysfunction. Toxicology and hepatic panels were negative, suggesting relapse of alcohol misuse was unlikely. She stopped having seizures for one year, before their recurrence with unchanged semiology. During that year, no changes to her ASMs were made. Due to ongoing encephalopathy, she underwent evaluation for AE. Low titer serum GAD antibodies were identified, without other antibodies, and with a GAD antibody index of 5.8. She received treatment with intravenous steroids, rituximab, IVIg, and then tocilizumab without significant improvement. Her seizures returned after about one year, with similar semiology, and she remained cognitively impaired. Given that her neuropsychological profile places her at high risk of memory decline with a dominant temporal destructive procedure, and in light of the prior encephalitic illness following a neurosurgical intervention, a definitive neurosurgical intervention has not been undertaken, after careful shared decision-making with the patient.

Case 2

A 38-year-old woman with drug-resistant TLE since age 27 underwent SEEG. She had previously undergone a bilateral temporal SEEG, 4 years after her epilepsy onset, at another institution, with reported bilateral independent temporal onset, after which she underwent vagus nerve stimulation. She continued to have seizures despite multiple ASMs, so a repeat SEEG was planned, with sampling of bilateral temporal lobes and perisylvian regions. SEEG captured multiple habitual electroclinical seizures all arising from the left amygdala with early spread to the left mid-hippocampus, then further spread to the left temporal pole, and occasional spread to the right amygdala, hippocampus, and inferior and middle temporal sulcus. Bedside cortical stimulation elicited seizures in the right amygdala and right middle hippocampus. Routine head CT immediately after removal demonstrated an area of subacute diffusion restriction in the posterior right temporal lobe. Subsequent MRI was suggestive of a small subacute venous infarct over the right posterior middle temporal gyrus, without hemorrhagic transformation. Two months after SEEG electrode explantation, she and her family noted a worsening of her memory. While the exact onset of the worsening was unclear on history at that time, it had occurred sometime between explantation and the two month follow-up visit. She then underwent responsive neurostimulation (RNS) with depth electrodes targeting the bilateral amygdalo-hippocampal areas. Soon after RNS implantation she experienced further worsening of her memory. She also started exhibiting poor judgment (e.g., falling victim to telephone scammers). Her seizure frequency increased to almost daily, with unchanged semiology. Her subacute cognitive and behavioral disturbance led to investigations for AE. Low titer serum GAD antibodies were identified, without other antibodies, and a GAD antibody index was calculated to be 10.7. She experienced improvement in her cognitive dysfunction and 75 % reduction in clinical seizures with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg). Escalation to rituximab was ineffective, and seizures worsened with withdrawal of IVIg, then improved with reinitiation of IVIg (Fig. 1). All seizures have been left hippocampal with or without concurrent right hippocampal involvement on RNS electrocorticography. Discussion

We present two cases of AE emerging after SEEG in two patients with adult onset TLE of unknown cause, low titer serum GAD antibodies and evidence of GAD antibody intrathecal synthesis. In both cases, a clinical change, characterized primarily by cognitive dysfunction and encephalopathy, emerged within weeks of SEEG explantation. While neither patient had been investigated for an autoimmune cause of TLE prior to SEEG, the clinical course suggests that SEEG was associated with a change in neurologic course. The change may have represented an exacerbation of an insidious GAD-TLE syndrome, which could have been previously undiagnosed, or an entirely de novo autoimmune neurologic syndrome.

Low titer GAD antibodies have inconsistent associations with GAD neurologic syndromes and frequent alternative diagnoses [11]. Case series of GAD-TLE have not included low-titer GAD antibodies [2,4]. Evidence of GAD intrathecal synthesis does not predict immunotherapy response, [12] but has been proposed as a core step in demonstrating neurologic autoimmunity, particularly in the cases of low-titer GAD antibodies [13]. One may hypothesize that low titer GAD antibodies had little to no clinical significance before SEEG, but that, due to BBB disturbance during the neurosurgical procedure, gained entry to the CNS and led to a cascade of events ultimately leading to intrathecal synthesis of GAD antibodies. Of note, both cases had asymptomatic hemorrhages along electrode tracts, a known complication of SEEG in up to 19 % of implantations, [14] which may have correlated acutely with greater BBB disruption. When CSF sampling was performed, weeks after explantation, there was no evidence of BBB dysfunction as measured by the albumin index, so it is unlikely that ongoing BBB dysfunction played a role in perpetuating AE [15]. We also deem it unlikely that intracerebral hemorrhage led to inaccurate CSF antibody synthesis calculations, since CSF red blood cell counts were low (Table 1). The venous infarct in the second patient is unlikely to have led to the seizure exacerbation and encephalopathy, given its small nature, right posterior middle temporal gyrus location, while all RNS seizures were left hippocampal in onset. Nevertheless, given the lack of neural autoantibody testing prior to the cases and the presence of hemorrhages, there remains some uncertainty on the definitive nature of the GAD antibody mediated encephalitis diagnosis we argue for, and it is possible that these patients experienced protracted encephalopathy post SEEG due to competing causes including hemorrhage related structural injury.

Both cases primarily presented with encephalopathy and cognitive dysfunction, but one case interestingly experienced new seizure freedom after SEEG, lasting about one year before re-emergence of seizures, with the same semiology as that before SEEG. Transient seizure improvement has been reported after SEEG and is posited to relate to a microlesional effect, [16] akin to that seen in neuromodulation [17]. Since seizure exacerbation would be expected with an AE syndrome, it is hard to reconcile this feature of the clinical course in this case with the AE diagnosis, but the subacute encephalopathy, not explained otherwise given the limited nature of the intracerebral hemorrhage, was felt to be secondary to GAD antibody mediated autoimmune encephalitis.

Since neither case was tested for autoimmune causes of epilepsy prior to SEEG, it is difficult to know whether their course would have been different with autoimmune presurgical investigations. In our center, we have implemented systematic discussion of whether an autoimmune workup should be considered in our presurgical conference discussions, and perform antibody testing in many refractory adult onset TLE of unknown cause. If low-titer GAD antibodies had been identified in serum and no GAD intrathecal synthesis had been identified, we would have likely similarly proceeded to SEEG. Periprocedural dexamethasone was recently shown to correlate with improved seizure outcomes after resective epilepsy surgery [18]. In cases with uncertain low-titer GAD antibodies without intrathecal synthesis, we believe SEEG should still be undertaken, but consideration of periprocedural anti-inflammatory treatments may be given. At our center, we have implemented perioperative dexamethasone in all SEEG procedures.

We conclude that SEEG may rarely be associated with a postsurgical AE characterized by encephalopathy, with or without seizure exacerbation, and GAD antibody intrathecal synthesis, in patients with epilepsy of unknown cause and low titer serum GAD antibodies. The targeted investigation of autoimmune causes of seizures in the presurgical evaluation of patients with TLE of unknown cause is paramount to mitigating risks associated with SEEG.
