Optimizing Education to Improve COVID-19 Vaccination Rates in a Federally Qualified Health Center
Authors: Joshua R Perkins, Ecler E Jaqua, Van T Nguyen, Daniel A Franz, Joseph Elkins, Kelly R Morton
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCOVID-19 vaccination hesitancy is prevalent in underserved communities, and family medicine clinics can combat hesitancy with vaccine education. However, due to general misinformation, physicians hesitate to educate patients because doing so can create conflict.MethodsA series of resident-run, team-based quality improvement projects were conducted at a federally qualified health center every 4 months between June 2021 and May 2022. First, staff documentation of vaccine status was addressed. Second, physician and staff education about COVID-19 vaccines was completed along with motivational interview training to avoid conflict with patients. Third, patient COVID-19 vaccine education was addressed.ResultsAfter Cycle 1, COVID-19 vaccine documentation status increased the number of patients who completed the vaccination series from 1% to 22%. Cycle 2 showed an increase in COVID-19 vaccination rate after health care team education. This reflected an increase from 35% to 76% of residents reporting that they discussed COVID-19 vaccines with unvaccinated patients after the intervention. Cycle 3 fought vaccine misinformation by educating patients. Most patients heard information about COVID-19 vaccines from friends and family (95%), social media (90%), and the news (80%). Physician confidence in providing COVID-19 vaccine education to patients increased from 2.8 (< somewhat confident) to 4.3 (moderately confident) out of 5 over 3 plan-do-study-act cycles.DiscussionVaccination rates were tracked alongside physician surveys regarding the experience of offering the vaccine to patients. Vaccination rates steadily increased over time, and physicians became more confident in COVID-19 vaccine discussions with patients.ConclusionPrimary care physicians are needed to approach public health concerns, such as vaccination completion, but ongoing education is also needed to promote confidence in health care pathways.

Introduction

At the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, maximal resources were deployed to develop a safe and effective vaccine as quickly as possible. Once developed, however, vaccination acceptance was tenuous in many communities. As of May 2023, only 55.2% to 61.3% of Californians had completed the primary COVID-19 vaccination series, and only a third of those completed the additional bivalent booster. 1 Vaccine hesitancy and delay in vaccination are ranked as the foremost threat to global health by the World Health Organization. 2 The most common reasons for COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among the general population include safety, efficacy, lack of insurance or financial resources, having contracted COVID-19, and religious reasons. Perceived lack of disease risk, limited information, and lower trust in national health authorities are also associated with vaccine hesitancy. 2–4

The 2 demographic characteristics most associated with COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy are being female and Black. 2 Women are more likely to be concerned about vaccine safety due to a lack of data regarding pregnancy and effects on fertility. 5,6 Vaccine hesitancy in Black Americans has been linked to historical distrust in the medical community following past abuses by researchers. 7–9 Unfortunately, Black Americans have a high incidence of COVID-19–related mortality. Specifically, Black Americans comprise only 13% of the US population, but 21% of the COVID-19 related deaths. In addition, only 3% of the participants in the COVID-19 vaccine trials were Black individuals. 10 It is clear that mistrust and communication regarding vaccination is important and can put public health at risk if not done well.

Primary care physicians (PCPs) are in a unique position to educate and vaccinate patients as a trusted source of health information. 11,12 Although physicians report general confidence in vaccine knowledge and counseling skills, nearly half report discomfort discussing COVID-19 vaccination with patients due to time, potential for conflict, and feeling patients had all decided on vaccination without PCP input. 13

However, according to Finney Rutten et al, 14 COVID-19 vaccination status should be addressed by all members of the health care team to educate and encourage vaccination. The health care team can use information about the benefits of vaccination vs risks, as well as altruism, to promote vaccination—similar to the methods used in discussions of other vaccines. For example, there is evidence that providing novel information about the vaccine or the illness helps to decrease vaccine hesitancy whereas confronting the patient and creating conflict is not effective. If misinformation and misperceptions are noted, physicians are urged to affirm patient values and provide an alternative, factual narrative with motivational interviewing techniques that include open-ended questions, asking the patient’s permission to the discuss the issues further, and helping the patient articulate the costs and benefits of vaccination. 14,15

The present quality improvement project aimed to improve vaccination workflows and education of staff, resident physicians, and patients in 3 plan-do-study-act (PDSA) cycles in an underserved community-based federally qualified health center. The PDSA series occurred during the federal vaccine rollout to complement community efforts at improving vaccination rates in a Medicaid managed care–insured patient population.

Methods

The Loma Linda University Health Institutional Review Board determined that this activity did not meet the definitions of human participant research because it was not a systematic investigation and this project was not designed to develop or contribute to generalizable knowledge. Thus, patient consent was not required.

A series of 3 4-month-long team-based quality improvement projects were conducted in a family medicine residency clinic at an urban federally qualified health center in a high-demand, high-need area via PDSA cycles between June 2021 and May 2022. The team included medical assistants, licensed vocational nurses, resident physicians, psychology doctoral students, and attending physicians. Vaccines were provided in the clinic and interventions were applied to patients who were eligible for COVID-19 vaccine administration per the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommendations at that point in the pandemic. In June 2021, only persons 12 years and older were eligible for the COVID-19 vaccine. In November 2021, midway through the 3 PDSA cycles, COVID-19 vaccines became eligible for persons older than 5 years. 16 Because patients could receive vaccination at both the clinic and other public health sites, the primary outcome measure of all 3 cycles was total number of patients with at least one COVID-19 vaccination dose, with a secondary outcome of total number of vaccination doses provided at the clinic.

Each PDSA cycle identified a specific intervention to improve overall rates of COVID-19 vaccination, as follows.

PDSA Cycle 1 June to September 2021

Electronic medical record reports provided baseline vaccination levels in the authors’ clinic population. The PDSA aim was to improve identification of COVID-19 primary vaccine status so that evidence-based education could be provided as needed. Every age-eligible patient was asked about COVID-19 vaccine status and the electronic medical record was manually updated by the medical assistants at each clinic visit. A chart audit of all clinic visits was conducted August 23 to 27, 2021, to establish baseline patient vaccine status. After the medical assistants were provided education regarding appropriate diagnosis codes and documentation of vaccine status, another manual review of all clinic visits was conducted September 7 to 10, 2021, to assess improvement.

PDSA Cycle 2 October 2021 to January 2022

PDSA Cycle 1 revealed that COVID-19 vaccine education was needed for the entire health care team given the rapid development and provision of information regarding the vaccine and vaccine eligibility. Thus, the aim for PDSA Cycle 2 was to improve education of the health care team regarding COVID vaccinations. A pretest survey of PCPs determined the most common patient questions and hesitations regarding the COVID-19 vaccine. Due to the overwhelming and rapid release of new information, and misinformation, the authors provided a multimodal education series for the health care team as learners retain information more effectively if information is received across different modalities. The multimodal education series included 1) a widely distributed handout of common COVID-19 questions and answers (Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia), 2) weekly clinic emails with common COVID-19 myths and misinformation, 3) a video by an infectious disease physician at the authors’ institution shown at clinical staff meetings explaining the development and mechanism of mRNA vaccines, and 4) a didactic piece by a prominent vaccine expert who started the #thisisourshot campaign describing how to approach the COVID-19 conversation with patients and how to address common misconceptions. A posttest survey assessed which educational modality was most helpful to improve COVID-19 vaccine patient counseling, as well as the team’s overall confidence recommending COVID vaccines across the PDSA Cycle 2.

PDSA Cycle 3 February to May 2022

Building upon previous cycles, this PDSA cycle provided resources for the team to educate patients regarding the COVID-19 vaccine with the goal of increasing confidence in patient education. The authors streamlined clinic workflow and emphasized vaccine status inquiry and timing of vaccine education introduction (Figure 1). A multimodal approach to patient education was introduced, including 1) a patient handout, 2) viewing of vaccine informational videos from the CDC in the waiting rooms to the clinic, and 3) an easily accessible document regarding common COVID-19 vaccine myths to be added to the patients’ after-visit summary that was shared with all practitioners. A posttest survey assessed medical assistants’ reactions to the patient education materials and resident physician confidence in providing patient vaccine education in addition to balancing measures of effects on the amount of time it takes to see the patient in the clinic.

Results

Vaccine documentation PDSA Cycle 1

From August 23 to 27, 2021, 621 patients who were seen in the clinic had a chart audit to assess vaccine status documentation. Of these, 99% had no documentation on COVID-19 vaccine status. After the documentation workflow intervention, 424 charts of patients seen in the clinic from September 7 to 10, 2021, were audited. Of these, 61% had no documentation on COVID-19 vaccine status. However, 22% had complete documentation on COVID-19 vaccine status; of these, 3% had started the series, 11% declined, and 8% were counseled.

PCP and health care team vaccine education PDSA Cycle 2

COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy reported by resident physicians were a positive history of COVID-19 infection, possible susceptibility to infection regardless of vaccination, the vaccine’s rapid development, and fear of vaccine effects on fertility (Figure 2). Resident physicians reported that their patients received COVID-19 education from friends, family, or social media, and only 65% reported vaccine education from their health care team.

Resident physicians were surveyed regarding the most effective educational strategy for their own vaccine education. The survey was completed by 21 residents and the most-viewed methods were rated as email and video, 81% and 76%, respectively, followed by handouts and didactics, 71% and 57%, respectively. COVID-19 vaccine counseling confidence increased from 2.8 to 3.2 on a 1- to 5-point rating scale (not confident to extremely confident), improving from somewhat to moderately confident (P < 0.05). 17

Patient vaccine education PDSA Cycle 3

Resident physician surveys indicated an increased rate of providing patients with COVID-19 vaccine information from less than half to more than half the time (P < 0.05). The most commonly used patient educational resources were the CDC website (66.7%), after-visit summary patient handout (57.1%), and the clinic website (41.7%). Resident physician confidence in providing COVID-19 vaccine education increased throughout all 3 cycles from less than moderately confident (2.8 on a 5-point rating scale) to confident (4.3, P < 0.05; Figure 3). However, resident physicians were less likely to counsel patients regarding COVID-19 vaccination (76% to 54%). It could be that less counseling happened because resident physicians also reported an increased clinic visit cycle time when counseling patients about the COVID-19 vaccines from neither agree nor disagree (3.1) to somewhat agree (4, P < 0.05) over the course of the pandemic.

Overall vaccination rates

The number of patients with at least one COVID-19 vaccine dose increased from 26% to 48% over the academic year in the authors’ patient population; however, this rate was below the county rate of 69.1% (Figure 4). As a secondary measure, the total number of COVID vaccines provided at the family medicine clinic was assessed longitudinally across all 3 PDSA cycles. As expected, it improved over time and at the end of all 3 cycles was 8931 (Figure 5). Given that the clinic had 29,377 patient visits that academic year, this would mean that vaccinations were made in 30.4% of these visits.

Discussion

On average, the development of a vaccine through obtaining US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval takes 10–15 years. 18 The urgency of the COVID-19 pandemic led to the rapid development of the mRNA vaccine starting in January 2020 to FDA-authorized use by December 2020; however, mRNA vaccines had been under development since 1970. 19 Introduction of this new vaccine led to well-documented vaccine hesitancy and misinformation in local communities. Unfortunately, misinformation impacted not only the authors’ clinic’s patients but also members of the health care team. The authors uncovered substantial discomfort of staff and resident physicians in their knowledge and confidence to counsel patients on the COVID-19 vaccine.

Vaccine hesitancy and misinformation was addressed, but the authors quickly realized they had to more fully understand the discomfort in the team and how to address knowledge gaps and confidence in providing patients with counseling about the vaccine. The COVID-19 vaccine was novel to both patients and the health care team, and, as such, established workflows regarding vaccine status documentation, vaccine availability in the clinic and administration, as well as vaccine education, were underdeveloped. PDSA Cycle 1 established a method to determine who had and had not completed their primary vaccination series. This allowed resident physicians to decide who needed information on the vaccine. PDSA Cycle 2 then created physician information and education about how to counsel, educate, and dialogue with patients regarding the COVID-19 vaccine. Following this clinic team education, PDSA Cycle 3 moved forward to offer the information to patients and to complete the workflow to improve vaccine rates. This was not completely successful due to the conflicts that followed each new vaccine rollout phase in the public sector. These conflicts resulted in longer conversations that delayed clinic visit completion times and deterred vaccine counseling in a busy primary care clinic.

This quality improvement series identified the need for accurate immunization records for all patients in a national database that can complete electronic medical records. It was difficult to assess the true vaccine rate of the clinic due to a delayed state registry communication with the electronic medical record. Moreover, the need for rapid dissemination of accurate vaccine education and information was identified for both the patient and the health care team. The authors posit that improved education for health care team members may have improved the efficiency of vaccine counseling by decreasing the apprehension primary care practitioners felt about the time needed to counsel patients and the fear of conflict that could result in the visit.

Overall, the 3 PDSA cycles did improve resident physician confidence with vaccine counseling via a multimodal approach with information in email, videos, handouts, and didactics. The most utilized patient education modalities referenced were the CDC website, after-visit summary patient education, and information from the clinic website, although these sources often became outdated in the rapidly developing landscape surrounding the COVID-19 vaccine.

Conclusion

With the rapid changes in the COVID-19 vaccines, treatments, and prevention guidance, the need to combat misinformation and hesitancy taught important lessons about how PCPs remain informed in the authors’ training programs regarding new interventions and how this information is offered to patients. To remain a trusted source for patient education, primary care practitioners can use their unique position to leverage an established and trusted relationship with patients to disseminate accurate and current information that improves the shared decision-making process in making health care decisions.
