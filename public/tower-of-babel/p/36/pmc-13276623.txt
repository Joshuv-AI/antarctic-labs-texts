Automated Text Messages to Link Emergency Department Patients With Public Benefits Navigators
Authors: Erica L. Dixon, Joseph Harrison, Aliza Haider, Melissa Berkowitz, Lauren Southwick, Charles Rareshide, Conor Carroll, Clayton Kaledin, Wendy De La Rosa, Raina M. Merchant, Austin S. Kilaru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study describes the outcome of 2 text messaging interventions to reach patients eligible for government benefits programs.

Introduction

Public benefits help families obtain food, housing, and financial support that can address health-related social needs (HRSNs).1 In the US, despite recent funding cuts and eligibility restrictions, many households remain eligible to receive benefits but are not enrolled.2 Health systems increasingly screen and refer patients for assistance with HRSNs in various clinical settings.3

A previous study demonstrated that in-person screening with postdischarge text messages connected emergency department (ED) patients to benefits navigators.4 In the present study, we tested a more scalable approach that did not involve in-person screening but instead used automated text messages to contact ED patients following discharge. We also examined 2 intervention designs—opt-out and active choice—to assess the effectiveness of different strategies for patient engagement.

Methods

We conducted a prospective cohort study in 4 hospitals located in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, in partnership with Benefits Data Trust (BDT), which operated a telephone hotline staffed by benefits navigators (eTable in Supplement 1). Participants were adult patients residing in Philadelphia who were discharged from the ED, had Medicaid or Medicare insurance, had a mobile telephone, and reported English as their primary language. Eligible patients were identified via each hospital’s electronic health record and automatically enrolled. The University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board approved this study and waived the informed consent requirement because it posed minimal risk to participants. We followed the STROBE reporting guideline.

Participants were nonrandomly assigned to 1 of 2 interventions that sent text messages via the Way To Health platform (Penn Medicine).5 The opt-out approach (May 2024) sent patients an initial text message indicating their potential eligibility for benefits and asked them to opt out if they did not want to receive 3 subsequent text messages with contact information for benefits navigators (eMethods and eFigure in Supplement 1). The active choice approach (June 2024) sent patients the initial text message with potential eligibility information and instruction to either opt in or opt out of subsequent text messages.

Primary outcome was the number of logged calls to benefits navigators, collected via telephone records. The 2-phased approach allowed us to attribute calls to the correct intervention arm, as BDT ascertained but was unable to share patient-level outcomes due to confidentiality limitations. We also assessed the number of benefits applications submitted with assistance from navigators and the number of participants in each arm who opted out, opted in, or declined to receive text messages. We recorded the number of participants who did not receive messages due to delivery failures, such as spam filters or incorrect telephone numbers (eFigure in Supplement 1). All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

We enrolled 1429 participants, of whom 643 (45.0%) were assigned to the opt-out group and 786 (55.0%) to the active choice group. Their mean (SD) age was 46.1 (18.6) years, and they included 877 females (61.4%). There were 1041 (72.8%) patients with Medicaid and 388 (27.2%) with Medicare (Table 1).

Of 786 patients in the active choice group, 70 (8.9%) called navigators and 27 (3.4%) submitted applications. Sixty-eight patients (8.7%) did not receive any text messages, 167 (21.2%) opted in, 25 (3.2%) opted out, 3 (0.4%) discontinued later, and the remainder did not respond. There was no significant difference in call rate between groups (0.9 [95% CI, –0.8 to 2.7] percentage points).

Discussion

Automated text messages prompted 1 in 12 contacted ED patients to seek assistance in applying for public benefits, even without in-person screening or counseling. This broad approach to contacting patients generated benefits applications at a rate comparable to that reported in other settings.6 Only a small proportion of participants opted out or later discontinued receiving text messages. Similar outcomes for both interventions suggest that, while both are viable, active choice might be preferred given that it is potentially less intrusive and a better indicator of call volumes. However, this study did not randomize patients between approaches to definitively identify the better option.

Study limitations are the inclusion criteria that potentially excluded some patients eligible for benefits, geographic requirements for benefits eligibility that reduced generalizability, and inability to attribute benefits applications to specific participants. Due to these limitations, we could not determine how many participants were actually eligible for benefits, although this proportion was estimated in a previous work.4 Finally, our findings may be unique to the Philadelphia region and less transferrable to areas with lower Medicaid penetration or less robust resource navigation. Nonetheless, text messages may represent a low-cost, scalable intervention to connect patients with resources for addressing HRSNs.
