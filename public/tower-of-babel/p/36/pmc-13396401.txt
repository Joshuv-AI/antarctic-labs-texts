Prenatal valproic acid exposure alters striatal proteomic signatures associated with autism spectrum disorder in mice
Authors: Eun Hwa Jo, Yunjung Choi, Han-Byeol Kim, Ran-Sook Woo, Dohyun Han, Won-Cheol Kim, Seong June Park, Mina Kang, Yoori Choi, Keon Wook Kang, Hye-Sun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrenatal exposure to valproic acid (VPA), a widely prescribed antiepileptic and mood‑stabilizing drug, is a well-established environmental risk factor for autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Although behavioral and anatomical abnormalities have been reported in VPA-exposed animal models, the underlying molecular mechanisms within specific brain regions remain unclear. In this study, we used tandem mass tag (TMT)-based quantitative proteomics to profile protein expression in the striatum of 9–10-week-old mice prenatally exposed to VPA. Behavioral assessment confirmed core ASD-like phenotypes, including reduced body and brain weights and increased repetitive self-grooming behavior. Proteomic profiling identified 101 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), with 47 upregulated and 54 downregulated in VPA-exposed mice. Functional enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of pathways related to synaptic transmission, neuronal development, metal ion homeostasis, oxidative stress response, and excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance. Notably, proteins such as parvalbumin (PVALB), NR2F1, and metallothioneins (MT1, MT2, MT3) were markedly downregulated, implicating impaired inhibitory signaling and redox regulation. Importantly, quantitative PVALB immunofluorescence analysis provided histological validation of the proteomic findings, revealing a significant reduction of PVALB immunoreactivity in the dorsolateral striatum, with a non-significant trend toward reduction in the dorsomedial striatum. Additionally, protein–protein interaction network analysis identified PVALB and MT2 as central hub proteins linking synaptic, glial, and oxidative stress-related modules, highlighting disrupted striatal network organization. Collectively, these findings provide subregion-specific molecular and histological insight into how prenatal VPA exposure alters striatal neurobiology and contributes to ASD-like behavioral phenotypes. Proteomic data are available via ProteomeXchange (PXD067574).Subject terms: Molecular neuroscience, Diseases

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental condition characterized by deficits in social communication and repetitive behaviors [1, 2]. While genetic factors contribute significantly to ASD risk, environmental insults during early development are increasingly recognized as critical contributors to disease pathogenesis [3]. Among these, prenatal exposure to valproic acid (VPA), an antiepileptic and mood-stabilizing drug, has been consistently linked to increased ASD incidence in both humans and ASD-like behaviors in animal models [4, 5].

Prenatally VPA-exposed rodents recapitulate core ASD-like behaviors, including increased stereotypy and social deficits, and are widely used as an environmental model of ASD [6]. Mechanistically, VPA exerts broad epigenetic and signaling effects, functioning as a histone deacetylase inhibitor and a suppressor of glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK-3β). These actions lead to enhanced Wnt signaling and β-catenin accumulation, which alter neuronal differentiation and cortical patterning [7]. VPA also disrupts gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) metabolism and calcium homeostasis, perturbing excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance—an essential property of functional neural circuits [8, 9]. Moreover, VPA exposure has been associated with dysregulation of oxytocin signaling, neuroinflammatory cascades, and metal ion detoxification pathways [10–12].

The striatum, a key forebrain structure involved in motor control, reward processing, and social behavior, is increasingly implicated in ASD pathophysiology [13]. Striatal dysfunction is supported by neuroimaging findings in ASD patients and by evidence of altered dopaminergic and GABAergic signaling in animal models [14–17]. Notably, changes in striatal interneuron markers such as parvalbumin (PVALB) and choline acetyltransferase suggest circuit-level E/I imbalance [18, 19]. Our previous work further demonstrated that prenatal VPA exposure increases the expression of the transcription factor Nurr1 in the striatum, which was associated with reduced dendritic spine density and impaired social interactions, reinforcing the notion that VPA disrupts striatal circuitry [20]. However, despite the known relevance of the striatum, comprehensive molecular profiling of this region in the VPA model remains limited. Based on these prior observations, we hypothesized that the reductions in dendritic spine density and mature spine number previously observed in prenatally VPA-exposed mice [20] may arise from altered expression of synaptic proteins. We further reasoned that the well-documented E/I imbalance in ASD models could be reflected in the striatal proteome through coordinated changes in inhibitory and excitatory synaptic pathways.

In this study, we performed a tandem mass tag (TMT)-based quantitative proteomic analysis of the striatum in VPA-exposed mice to uncover region-specific protein alterations associated with ASD-like behaviors. We identified differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) and conducted functional enrichment analyses to explore disrupted biological pathways. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate protein alterations in the striatum of a VPA-induced ASD mouse model using proteomic analysis. By applying a proteomics-based approach, we aimed to identify molecular alterations that may underlie ASD-related pathophysiological mechanisms in a more specific and comprehensive manner. Our findings provide novel molecular insights into how prenatal VPA exposure reconfigures the striatal proteome, potentially contributing to the behavioral phenotypes observed in ASD.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male and female BALB/c mice were purchased from KOATECH (Pyeongtaek, Republic of Korea) and maintained under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Timed mating was performed by pairing one male with one or two females, and vaginal plugs were checked daily. On embryonic day 12.5 (E12.5), pregnant dams received a single subcutaneous injection of valproic acid sodium salt (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at a dose of 600 mg/kg (100 mg/mL solution) dissolved in 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM phosphate, pH 7.4). The injection volume was adjusted according to body weight. Control animals received an equivalent volume of PBS. A total of 19 pregnant dams were used (PBS n = 5; VPA n = 14). Five VPA-treated dams experienced complete pregnancy loss, resulting in no viable offspring. The average number of viable pups per dam was 5.2 in the PBS group and 3.4 in the VPA group. All viable pups survived to weaning (P21), and no postnatal mortality occurred prior to behavioral testing or tissue collection. No animals were excluded after weaning. Only male offspring were used for subsequent experiments and housed under standard conditions (24 ± 1 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle) with food and water ad libitum. Behavioral analyses included 15 PBS and 11 VPA offspring derived from multiple independent litters. For proteomic analysis, striatal tissues were randomly selected from offspring. Data analysis were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Seoul National University (SNU-220426-3-4) and were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Behavioral assessment

Repetitive behavior was assessed using self-grooming assays at 9–10 weeks of age. Mice were habituated to the testing room for 1 h while remaining in their home cages, and were then individually placed in a polycarbonate cage (20 × 30 × 15 cm). Behavior was video-recorded for 15 min, and grooming duration was manually scored during the final 10 min. Behavioral scoring was conducted by an observer blinded to treatment group. All experiments were conducted in a dedicated testing room maintained at 24 ± 1 °C and 50 ± 10% humidity. Light intensity was maintained at 30–40 lux under consistent sound conditions.

Tissue collection

Following behavioral testing, mice were sacrificed within 3 to 4 days. Animals were anesthetized with 3–5% isoflurane and perfused transcardially with ice-cold PBS. Brains were rapidly removed, weighed, and placed on an ice-cold dissection plate. The whole striatum was dissected following a previously described procedure [21]. Specifically, the olfactory bulb was first removed, after which the anterior 1 mm of the brain was coronally sectioned and discarded. The subsequent 2 mm segment was then coronally sectioned, and from this section, the striatum was carefully separated from the adjacent cortex. Because the striatum and cortex are clearly delineated by the external capsule, the possibility of contamination from neighboring regions was minimized. In addition, the dissected striatal tissue was washed with PBS prior to freezing to ensure sample purity, immediately placed on ice, and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Protein extraction and digestion

Striatal tissues were washed with PBS and lysed in buffer containing 4% SDS, 10 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), 50 mM chloroacetamide (CAA), and 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.5). Lysates were sonicated and incubated at 95 °C for 30 min. Protein concentration was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Proteins (200 µg) were precipitated with cold acetone and resuspended in buffer containing 2% SDS, 10 mM TCEP, 50 mM CAA, and 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.5) for processing by filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) as previously described [22], using 30 kDa spin filters (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The filters were washed with UA buffer (8 M urea in 0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) followed by exchanges with 50 mM HEPES via centrifugation. Trypsin/Lys-C (1:100) was used for overnight digestion at 37 °C.

TMT labeling and peptide fractionation

Tandem mass tag (TMT) 6plex reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were dissolved in 100% acetonitrile (ACN) and added to 30 µg of peptides at 30% ACN. Samples were spiked with chicken ovalbumin peptides (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and labeled for 90 min. Reactions were quenched with 5% hydroxylamine. Labeled peptides were pooled, dried, and desalted using Oasis HLB columns (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Peptides were fractionated using an Agilent 1290 HPLC system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) on a ZORBAX 300Extend C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 3.5 µm). Separation was performed under high-pH reversed-phase conditions with a 40-min gradient from 5 to 35% Solvent B (15 mM ammonium hydroxide in 90% ACN) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. A total of 96 fractions were collected, concatenated into 24 fractions, dried, and stored at –80 °C until LC–MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Peptides were analyzed using a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Ultimate 3000 RSLC system (Dionex, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Separation was performed on an Aurora Ultimate column (60 cm × 75 µm, 1.7 µm; IonOpticks, Fitzroy, VIC, Australia) at 60 °C with a 150-min gradient (5–45% B; 0.1% formic acid in 80% CAN) at 230 nL/min. MS1 scans were acquired at 120,000 resolution (200 m/z) with data-dependent MS/MS scans (top 20) at 45,000 resolution using a normalized collision energy of 32.

Data processing

Raw TMT data were processed in Proteome Discoverer v3.1.1.93 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the SEQUEST HT algorithm against the UniProtKB Mus musculus database (UP000000589, April 2024, 17,217 entries). Search parameters allowed up to 2 missed cleavages, peptide length 6–30, dynamic modifications of Met oxidation (+15.995 Da) and protein N-terminal acetylation (+42.011 Da), and static modifications of TMT6plex (+229.163 Da, N-term and Lys) and carbamidomethylation (+57.021 Da, Cys). Peptide spectrum matches were filtered at a 1% FDR.

Bioinformatic analysis

Protein quantification tables were processed in R (v4.4.0) using Bioconductor packages. Protein intensity values were normalized by total intensity, log2-transformed, and analyzed in R v4.4.0 using the genefilter package (v1.86.0) [23]. DEPs were determined using Student’s t-tests with statistical significance defined as those with p < 0.05 and | fold change| >1.2, a threshold chosen to ensure both statistical significance and biologically meaningful magnitude of change. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using DAVID (version 2021) [24, 25], and visualized in R. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was conducted using the STRING database (version 12.0), with a confidence score threshold of >0.4. Interactions among downregulated DEPs were visualized to identify functional modules and hub proteins. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [26] partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD067574 (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pride/archive/projects/PXD067574).

Immunofluorescence staining of the striatum

Following deep anesthesia, mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS, and brains were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 24 h. Tissues were then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose solution for 3 days at 4 °C until fully equilibrated. Brains were embedded and sectioned at a thickness of 30 µm using a cryostat, and the resulting sections were stored in cryopreservation solution until immunostaining. For antigen retrieval, sections were incubated in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 85 °C for 10 min, followed by washing with PBS. Sections were permeabilized and blocked in PBS containing 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C using rabbit anti-parvalbumin antibody (Proteintech, 29312-1-AP) diluted in blocking buffer. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 555–conjugated donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, 31752) for 3 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and sections were mounted using fluorescent mounting medium.

Image acquisition and quantification

Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a Leica THUNDER imaging system, and tiled images of the striatum were obtained using a manually defined tiling protocol with a motorized stage under identical acquisition settings across samples. For quantitative analysis, regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to the dorsolateral striatum (DLS) and dorsomedial striatum (DMS) were manually defined based on anatomical landmarks according to a previously published striatal subregional map [27]. Because identical ROIs were applied across all sections and animals, PVALB-positive cells were directly counted within fixed areas and compared between groups. Quantification was performed by an investigator blinded to experimental group using ImageJ software (NIH).

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of body weight, brain weight, self-grooming behavior, and PVALB-positive cell counts in the DLS and DMS between treatment groups (VPA and control) were conducted using unpaired Student’s t-tests. Only male offspring were analyzed; thus, sex was not included as a factor. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Reduced body and brain weights in VPA-exposed mice

Our experimental design involved a single subcutaneous injection of VPA (600 mg/kg) or saline at embryonic day 12.5 to pregnant mice (Fig. 1A). To validate the effects of prenatal VPA exposure, body and brain weights were measured at 9–10 weeks of age. VPA-exposed mice exhibited significantly lower body weight (21.4 g ± 1.077) compared to control mice (24.87 g ± 0.265; p < 0.01) (Fig. 1B). Brain weight was reduced by a similar proportion (0.399 g ± 0.0084 vs. 0.451 g ± 0.0053; p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C).

Increased repetitive behavior

Repetitive self-grooming behavior was significantly increased in VPA-exposed mice relative to control mice. Grooming duration increased by approximately 142% to 26.11 s ± 3.58 s in the VPA group and 10.78 s ± 1.51 s in the control group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1D).

Striatal proteomic alterations following VPA exposure

To examine the molecular effects of prenatal VPA exposure, we performed TMT-based quantitative proteomics using striatal tissue from VPA-exposed and control mice (Fig. 2A). To ensure data quality, we performed QC analyses, which demonstrated highly consistent distributions, high correlations (>0.997), and clear separation between groups in PCA, indicating that the major source of variance was biological rather than technical (Supplementary Fig. 1). Proteomic profiling identified 9309 proteins, among which 101 were differentially expressed in the striatum (47 upregulated, 54 downregulated; p < 0.05, |fold change| >1.2). Notably, upregulated proteins included PER2, MCHR1, CACNG3, and NRXN2, whereas PVALB, NR2F1, and metallothioneins MT1, MT2, MT3 were significantly downregulated. These proteins are functionally associated with neurodevelopment and synaptic function, E/I balance, oxytocin signaling, metal homeostasis, and calcium signaling, based on previously reported links to ASD-related mechanisms (Fig. 2B, Table 1). Enriched biological pathways among DEPs

GO enrichment analysis revealed that upregulated proteins were enriched in biological processes such as vasoconstriction, neuron migration, and intracellular pH regulation. Downregulated proteins were associated with GABAergic synaptic transmission, ion homeostasis, oxidative stress response, and nervous system development—pathways broadly relevant to ASD (Fig. 3A, B). To further explore the functional organization of these DEPs, a PPI network was constructed using the STRING database. The network of downregulated proteins revealed several hub nodes, including PVALB, GFAP, and MT2, which formed interconnected modules related to synaptic signaling, glial function, and antioxidant defense (Fig. 3C). These findings highlight coordinated molecular disruptions in striatal circuits affected by prenatal VPA exposure.

Subregion-specific reduction of PVALB interneurons in the striatum

To validate the proteomics-based downregulation of PVALB at the cellular level, we performed immunofluorescence staining for PVALB in the striatum of saline- and VPA-exposed mice. Representative images revealed a reduction of PVALB-positive interneurons in the DLS of VPA-exposed mice, whereas no apparent change was observed in the DMS (Fig. 3D). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant decrease in PVALB-positive cell counts specifically in the DLS of VPA-exposed mice compared with controls (p < 0.05), while the difference in the DMS was not statistically significant (Fig. 3E). These findings provide histological validation of the proteomic results and indicate subregion-specific vulnerability of PVALB interneurons within striatal microcircuits following prenatal VPA exposure.

Discussion

The VPA animal model generated by prenatal exposure during pregnancy is a widely used environmental model for studying ASD, displaying core behavioral phenotypes and neurodevelopmental disorders. In line with prior search, we observed reduced body and brain weights following prenatal VPA exposure [28, 29]. In this study, we extended these findings by demonstrating that VPA exposure induces molecular alterations in the striatum, a brain region increasingly recognized for its role in social behavior and repetitive actions.

Importantly, a focused literature search using the terms striatum, ASD, and proteomics identified multiple prior studies, indicating that proteomic profiling of the striatum in ASD models is not unprecedented [30–32]. Several of these studies were conducted in Shank3B–/– mice, a genetically driven ASD model. For example, Peixoto et al. examined synaptosomal fractions of the dorsal striatum from P8–P18 mice using LC–MS/MS and demonstrated extensive remodeling of the striatal synaptic proteome during a critical window of corticostriatal circuit maturation [30]. Their findings revealed a developmental switch in glutamatergic plasticity driven by cortical hyperactivity in striatal spiny projection neurons, with behavioral abnormalities emerging during the same postnatal interval. These phenotypes were ameliorated by normalizing excitatory synaptic connectivity through protein kinase A (PKA) downregulation.

Similarly, Wang et al. demonstrated that Shank3 protein isoforms are differentially expressed in direct versus indirect pathway medium spiny neurons (MSNs) and that Shank3B–/– mice exhibit selective alterations in the postsynaptic proteome of indirect-pathway MSNs [31]. This cell-type–specific postsynaptic vulnerability highlights the essential role of Shank3 isoform expression in synaptic maturation and ASD-associated synaptopathies. Because Shank proteins are multidomain postsynaptic scaffold molecules that organize glutamatergic synapses and regulate dendritic spine development, proteomic changes in these models primarily reflect the consequences of ASD-linked Shank gene mutations.

In contrast to these genetic models, far fewer studies have applied proteomics to brain regions in environmentally induced ASD models such as prenatal VPA exposure. Several groups have reported brain-wide or region-specific proteomic changes in rodent VPA models [33–39]. For instance, Zhu et al. identified 329 DEPs in the hypothalamus of VPA-exposed rats, revealing extensive alterations in metabolic, neuronal, and signaling pathways [39]. However, none of these studies specifically investigated the striatum, despite its central role in ASD-related repetitive behavior and cortico-striatal circuit dysfunction.

Thus, although previous work has characterized striatal proteomic alterations in genetic ASD models—most notably Shank3B–/– mice—our study provides the first proteomic characterization of the striatum in an environmental ASD model induced by prenatal VPA exposure. This distinction is critical because VPA and Shank3 mutations represent fundamentally different etiological pathways, and comparing their molecular signatures offers insight into convergent vs. divergent mechanisms in ASD pathophysiology. Building on this distinction, our quantitative proteomic analysis identified 101 DEPs in the striatum of VPA-exposed mice, revealing disrupted pathways that collectively reflect regionally selective molecular vulnerability relevant to ASD (Fig. 2).

GO analysis of downregulated DEPs strongly suggested the impairments in neurodevelopment, oxidative stress regulation, and synaptic signaling (Fig. 3B). Of particular interest, several proteins involved in metal ion homeostasis—notably the metallothionein isoforms MT1, MT2, and MT3—were markedly reduced, with fold changes of –0.399, –0.484, and –0.317, respectively (p < 0.05 for all; Fig. 2B, Table 1). This finding is consistent with clinical reports of altered copper/zinc balance in individuals with ASD [40, 41], suggesting that disrupted detoxification pathways may render the striatum vulnerable to oxidative damage. Given the antioxidant and buffering functions of metallothioneins [42], their coordinated downregulation may signify impaired neuroprotective capacity and contribute to the redox imbalance increasingly implicated in ASD pathophysiology.

We also observed downregulation of key neurodevelopmental regulators, including NR2F1, ELAVL2, and SCGN. Notably, NR2F1 has been implicated in Bosch-Boonstra-Schaaf optic atrophy syndrome, a syndromic form of ASD [43], supporting the idea that prenatal VPA exposure may recapitulate molecular features observed in genetically driven ASD. Additionally, decreased expression of PVALB highlights deficits in inhibitory interneurons critical for E/I balance. This aligns with prior evidence from postmortem ASD brains and PVALB-deficient mouse models [44–46]. Importantly, our immunofluorescence validation demonstrated that the reduction of PVALB-positive interneurons occurred selectively in the DLS, whereas the DMS was relatively spared. Given the established contribution of the DLS to habitual motor control, such subregion-specific vulnerability provides a mechanistic link between inhibitory dysfunction and the emergence of repetitive behaviors in the VPA model.

Conversely, dopamine D1 receptor (DRD1) was upregulated, suggesting increased activity of direct pathway MSNs. Given that D1 signaling facilitates motor output and habit formation, heightened DRD1 expression may bias striatal circuits toward overactivation of the direct pathway, thereby promoting repetitive behavioral output. Taken together, the combined diminution of inhibitory interneuron integrity and enhancement of direct pathway signaling supports a model in which prenatal VPA exposure shifts striatal circuit dynamics toward the generation of repetitive motor programs.

Importantly, several core molecular alterations identified in our striatal proteomic dataset show direct concordance with findings from human ASD transcriptomic studies. Large-scale postmortem cortical transcriptomic analyses have consistently demonstrated convergent dysregulation of synaptic and interneuron-related gene modules in ASD, accompanied by reduced expression of PVALB and disruption of E/I balance [47, 48]. Consistent with these transcriptomic findings, postmortem histological analysis further confirmed a reduced number of PVALB-expressing interneurons in the prefrontal cortex of individuals with ASD [49]. In parallel, human blood RNA sequencing has revealed significantly reduced MT2A expression in children with ASD, indicating impaired metallothionein-dependent metal buffering and redox regulation [50]- a pattern that closely mirrors the concurrent reduction of MT1, MT2, and MT3 observed in our striatal proteomic dataset. Collectively, these cross-species transcriptomic and cellular signatures indicate that prenatal VPA exposure recapitulates fundamental molecular dimensions of human ASD, particularly at the levels of inhibitory circuit integrity, synaptic network organization, and redox vulnerability.

Interpretation of these proteomic results requires consideration of the striatum’s complex cellular and anatomical composition. Although MSNs constitute the majority of striatal neurons, the dissected striatal tissue includes diverse afferent projections (corticostriatal, thalamostriatal, and nigrostriatal axons) as well as glial populations such as astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes. Thus, the observed proteomic alterations likely reflect not only intrinsic striatal changes but also perturbations in presynaptic inputs and glial regulatory processes. For example, the downregulation of GFAP suggests astrocytic involvement, while immune-related DEPs (e.g., MYD88) point to microglial contributions. Similarly, synaptic proteins may originate from both striatal neurons and incoming glutamatergic or dopaminergic terminals, highlighting the need for circuit-level interpretation.

Consistent with this multifactorial interpretation, PPI network analysis revealed hub proteins such as PVALB, GFAP, and MT2, which formed interconnected modules linked to synaptic signaling, glial regulation, and oxidative stress defense. The coordinated downregulation of these hub proteins suggests destabilization of striatal microcircuit integrity, offering a unifying framework for multiple observed alterations. At a broader system level, DEPs were distributed across pathways related to neurotransmission (DRD1, ADRA2A), calcium signaling (CACNG3), oxytocin signaling (MCHR1, PDE11A), and innate immunity (MYD88), indicating that prenatal VPA exposure perturbs multiple, interconnected signaling networks rather than isolated molecular processes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only male offspring were analyzed to minimize hormonal variability and reflect the higher prevalence of ASD in males; however, sex-specific molecular responses remain an important consideration. Second, although stringent statistical thresholds were applied, the proteomic analysis was performed with a relatively small sample size (n = 3 per group), and thus independent validation in larger cohorts will be required to confirm the robustness of these molecular signatures. Third, although our findings correlate with previously reported ASD-related molecular changes, additional functional studies are required to establish causal relationships between specific protein alterations and behavioral phenotypes. Fourth, our analysis was restricted to the striatum, while other regions such as the cortex and cerebellum also play critical roles in ASD and warrant future investigation.

Despite these limitations, our findings highlight the striatum as a key site of molecular vulnerability to prenatal environmental insult. In particular, the selective disruption of PVALB interneurons in the DLS provides a plausible circuit-level explanation for the emergence of repetitive behaviors. Given the heterogeneity of ASD and the lack of reliable biomarkers, proteomics may provide valuable insights into molecular signatures of disease. The alterations observed here—involving oxidative stress, metal homeostasis, E/I balance, and neurodevelopment—represent candidate mechanisms with translational relevance and potential therapeutic value. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that prenatal VPA exposure induces widespread yet regionally selective proteomic and cellular alterations in the striatum. These results provide mechanistic insight into the molecular consequences of environmental risk and support the utility of proteomics for identifying candidate molecular signatures and therapeutic targets in ASD.
