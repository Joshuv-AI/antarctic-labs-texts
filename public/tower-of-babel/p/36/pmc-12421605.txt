Limited utility of faecal immunochemical testing in a colorectal cancer screening programme for adults with cystic fibrosis: insights from a prospective UK study
Authors: Aqeem Azam, Karuna Sapru, Andrew M Jones, Dipesh H Vasant, Anne-Marie Kelly, Graham Horsman, Rowland J Bright-Thomas, Javaid Iqbal, Peter J Barry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePeople with cystic fibrosis (pwCF) are at significantly increased risk of colorectal cancer (CRC), prompting international recommendations for earlier screening with colonoscopy. The utility of faecal immunochemical testing (FIT) as a screening adjunct in pwCF remains unclear. This study evaluates FIT’s diagnostic performance and uptake within a CRC screening programme in a UK CF centre.MethodsPwCF aged ≥40 years were invited in person to participate in a screening protocol including FIT and colonoscopy. FIT results were interpreted using three thresholds: ≥10 µg Hb/g (primary), ≥80 µg Hb/g and ≥120 µg Hb/g. Colonoscopy findings and polyp histology were recorded.ResultsOf 113 eligible patients, 66 (58.4%) returned FIT, 49 (43.4%) had FIT and colonoscopy, and 27 (23.9%) underwent colonoscopy only. Colonic polyps were detected in 27.6% (21/76), which were predominantly adenomatous, and no CRCs were detected. For polyp detection, FIT demonstrated poor sensitivity (14%) and modest specificity (86%) at the 10 µg Hb/g threshold. Quantitative FIT values did not correlate with polyp presence (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.48). Bowel preparation was generally adequate (mean Boston Bowel Preparation Scale 6.8), with a low repeat colonoscopy rate (14.5%).ConclusionIn this study, no cancer was detected in 76 consecutive eligible individuals who underwent colonoscopy. In 49 patients who had both FIT and colonoscopy, FIT did not aid colonic polyp detection, showing low sensitivity and no correlation between faecal haemoglobin and adenoma detection. Given the high adenoma prevalence, the limitations of FIT and the poor FIT return rate, colonoscopy should remain the preferred modality for CRC screening in pwCF.

Introduction

Advancements in the management of cystic fibrosis (CF) have significantly improved health outcomes, resulting in an increasingly ageing population of people with CF (pwCF). However, this demographic shift brings new challenges, acknowledging higher rates of colorectal cancer (CRC) among pwCF. CRC rates are up to 10 times higher than the general population,1 with incidence occurring at a younger age compared with the general population and predominantly right-sided tumours.2 The reasons behind these changes are less known, with theories attributing chronic gut inflammation, fatty acid malabsorption and altered expression of the MUC gene and the CFTR gene as tumour suppressor genes as the main procarcinogenic drivers in the CF bowel.24 Various studies have shown that tubular and tubulovillous adenomas are the most common histological findings of precancerous polyps.58

Recommendations for earlier CRC screening in pwCF compared with the general population have been driven by several studies showing increased incidence of adenomatous polyps (up to 50%) in pwCF aged >40 years,3 6 9 with another study suggesting screening at the age of 40 years could potentially prevent 79% of deaths due to CRC.10 A group from Minnesota found that the adenomatous polyp formation is accelerated in pwCF, as evidenced by increasing adenoma detection rates on subsequent short-interval colonoscopies in patients over the age of 40 years.6 Therefore, the US Cystic Fibrosis Foundation (CFF) recommends that screening for CRC begin at the age of 40 years, using colonoscopy as the screening modality.2 This is to be repeated every 5 years if results are normal and 3 years if polyps are detected.

Various methods have been employed for colorectal screening around the globe for the general population. In Canada and the USA, the screening tool is an amalgamation of a stool-based test such as the faecal immunochemical test (FIT) and direct visualisation; flexible sigmoidoscopy in Canada and flexible sigmoidoscopy, colonoscopy or CT colonography in the USA, targeting ages between 45 and 74 years in the USA and between 50 and 74 years in Canada.11 12 England and Australia have adopted a non-invasive approach by using a stool-based test as their initial screening examination and targeting people of age 45–74 years in Australia and 54–74 years in England, with plans to lower the age to 50 years in the coming years and reserving colonoscopy as an initial screening tool in high-risk groups.1316 In the UK, CF is not currently included in the high-risk group for CRC, as per the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence.17 Currently, there are no established guidelines in the UK specifically for CRC screening in pwCF. Some CF centres have adopted the guidelines provided by CFF to inform their practices, and some centres incorporate FIT in their practice.18 Although FIT may appear to be a cost-saving option, its accuracy and clinical utility in the CF population are uncertain due to a lack of data.2 10 Furthermore, the cut-off value for pwCF will need to be established. Different FIT thresholds are used in CRC screening and diagnosis in the general population. The UK National Health Service bowel cancer screening programme (BCSP) applies a faecal haemoglobin (fHb) threshold of 120 µg Hb/g in England and Northern Ireland and 80 µg Hb/g in Scotland (soon to be adopted in Wales) to screen asymptomatic individuals.17 Conversely, for symptomatic patients presenting to primary care, a threshold of 10 µg Hb/g is recommended to guide triage for onwards specialist referral.19 The objective of this study is to assess the clinical utility of FIT as an additional diagnostic tool within our CRC screening protocol for pwCF.

Methods

After consideration of study data and consensus agreement between the CF medical team and local gastroenterologists, from June 2022, all non-lung-transplanted pwCF aged ≥40 years attending a large adult CF centre in the UK were invited to participate in a BCSP, which included (1) FIT testing and (2) a referral for screening colonoscopy. Symptoms were not recorded as part of the screening protocol. Data were collected prospectively as part of a registered quality improvement project and received institutional approval (registration number HL091). All FIT testing kits were given in person during clinic attendance, and the rationale for FIT and colonoscopy was explained. The faecal sampling was performed at home, with samples returned via post to the hospital laboratory. Patients who had not returned their FIT kits were reminded during routine follow-up appointments.

In this study, we examined the diagnostic performance of different cut-off points. For primary analysis, we used fHb <10 µg Hb/g as indicative of a negative FIT result, and for secondary analysis, we used thresholds of fHb <80 µg Hb/g and <120 µg Hb/g. All colonoscopies were carried out at our institution, with patients receiving a CF-specific bowel preparation regimen (online supplemental file 3). The adequacy of bowel preparation was assessed using the Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS), with a score of ≥6 considered adequate (see online supplemental file 1).20 Colonoscopy reports were generated using the Lumens Systems endoscopy software. Data, including demographics and relevant clinical information, were compiled into a dedicated hospital database. Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio V.2024.04.0+735, with a two-tailed p-value of <0.05 considered statistically significant. The χ2 test of independence (or the alternate Fisher’s exact test), logistic regression, receiver operating characteristics (ROC) and its area under the curve (AUROC) were used where appropriate. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research, as this part of the study is a continuation of an already established CRC screening programme in our centre. A Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology checklist for this study is available as an online supplemental file 4).

Results

Of the 129 patients initially considered, seven declined the screening invitation (both FIT and colonoscopy), six had colonoscopies within the year of the screening programme due to clinically symptomatic indications (not screening purposes), two deferred to be included in the screening programme and one was not appropriate for screening due to comorbidities, leaving 113 patients who were eligible for the initial screening invitation. The demographic baseline of our patient cohort is shown in table 1, and the consort diagram of our study is presented in figure 1. There was no statistically significant association between the baseline demographics and the presence/absence of colonic polyps. All 113 eligible patients received a FIT kit during their clinic appointment, and 66 (58.4%) returned their completed tests. Among these 113 patients, 76 (67.3%) underwent colonoscopies, with 10 patients still awaiting a date for the procedure. The remaining 27 patients did not have a colonoscopy due to various reasons (see figure 1).The breakdown of FIT results and the presence of colonic polyps identified during colonoscopy are shown in table 2. A total of 49 (43.4%) patients completed both FIT and colonoscopy, while 27 (23.9%) patients underwent only colonoscopy without a corresponding FIT result. The median time (in weeks) from an FIT resulting to having a colonoscopy was 30 weeks (IQR 18–49).

There were five patients who had positive FIT results (cut-off value of fHb >10 µg Hb/g) with values ranging from 14 to 70 µg Hb/g, with no polyps detected on colonoscopy. Alternative diagnoses for a high FIT result in these patients included proctitis, haemorrhoids, diverticulosis and benign mucosal haemorrhage. One patient who had a positive FIT of 21 µg Hb/g is still undergoing investigation with a repeat colonoscopy (the first colonoscopy attempt was inconclusive due to difficulty in manoeuvring the scope, and subsequent CT colonography was also inconclusive). Two patients with FIT-positive results (23 and 139 µg Hb/g) made an informed decision to decline colonoscopy (and also declined CT colonography as an alternative). Only two patients with a FIT positive result (25 and 55 µg Hb/g) had colonic polyps on inspection. One of the patients had a large (32 mm×45 mm), complex-appearing polyp that was suspicious and not amenable to endoscopic resection. A laparoscopic right hemicolectomy and anastomosis were undertaken, with histology showing tubulovillous adenoma with low-grade dysplasia. The other patient had a 12 mm tubular adenoma in the descending colon with an uncomplicated polypectomy.

When using the cut-off of fHb 10 µg Hb/g, Fisher’s exact test showed no association between the presence of a polyp and a positive FIT result (p=1). Further logistic regression evaluating the quantitative value of FIT (higher FIT value vs polyp detection) also showed no association (p=1), with an AUROC of 0.48. When analysed using the cut-off of fHb 80 µg Hb/g and fHb 120 µg Hb/g, our cohort only had one patient with fHb exceeding those values, at fHb 139 µg Hb/g. However, the patient declined a colonoscopy or other alternative diagnostics such as CT colonography.

The overall prevalence of colonic polyps in this cohort was 27.6% (21/76 patients). 30 polyps were identified in these 21 patients, with six patients presenting with multiple polyps. 14 polyps were right-sided, with eight located on the left side and eight in other sites such as the rectum or anus (see tables3 4). All polyps were resected and sent for histological examination, except for one benign-appearing polyp and one insufficient sample. Tubular adenoma was the most common histological finding (10 cases). No high-grade dysplasia or malignancy was detected in our cohort.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that the adenoma detection rate in our cohort is high at 27.6%. There were no CRCs in our cohort, and we found that FIT, at a cut-off value of fHb >10 µg Hb/g, has no clinical value in our screening programme. Our FIT return rate of 58.4% is lower than other BCSPs, despite employing a more direct invitation approach to screening. There were no adverse outcomes related to colonoscopies in our cohort.

FIT in detecting CRC versus colonic polyps

The UK national BCSP uses FIT as a first-line screening tool, with a positive FIT result (fHb >80 µg Hb/g or fHb >120 µg Hb/g, depending on the region) followed by a diagnostic intervention, typically a colonoscopy. In our study, no cases of CRC were detected, but 21 patients had colonic polyps. It is important to note that FIT is a test for CRC, not for detecting colonic polyps. This raises two key questions: Is there a role for FIT in detecting colonic polyps in pwCF, and what is the most appropriate fHb threshold in pwCF?

We examined data for a lower fHb threshold of 10 µg Hb/g, as pwCF often experience abdominal symptoms as part of their typical CF-bowel manifestation, which can be difficult to distinguish from CRC-related symptoms. At this 10 µg Hb/g threshold, the diagnostic performance of FIT for detecting colonic polyps in our study was poor, with a sensitivity of 14%, a specificity of 86%, a positive predictive value (PPV) of 27.6% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 72.4%. This low sensitivity makes FIT unsuitable as a primary screening tool for polyps.

Increasing the threshold to either 80 µg Hb/g or 120µg Hb/g would result in 0% sensitivity and 100% specificity (it is important to note that one patient in this range declined colonoscopy). In the general population, FIT’s diagnostic performance varies more widely when detecting precancerous polyps than CRC. Studies on FIT’s performance in CF are limited. A study (conference abstract) of 39 patients with CF quoted a PPV of 55.6% and NPV of 51.7% for detecting an ‘abnormal colonoscopy finding’, defined as precancerous lesions or CRC.21 However, their prevalence was much higher compared with ours, with a prevalence of polyps of 45% and CRC of 5%. This discrepancy may possibly be attributed to the inclusion of lung transplant recipients who are on immunosuppressive medications, which have a known association with an elevated cancer risk.22 23 Online supplemental file 2 compares the diagnostic performance of FIT in our study to various general population studies.2432

In this study, FIT results were interpreted as binary outcomes (positive/negative) using an fHb threshold of <10 µg Hb/g to indicate a negative result. There was no statistically significant difference between the binary FIT results (positive/negative) and the presence of colonic polyps. To further evaluate FIT’s potential role in detecting polyps, we examined whether higher quantitative FIT values (µg Hb/g) correlated with increased polyp detection. Linear regression analysis showed no significant association between FIT values and the presence of colonic polyps. Additionally, ROC analysis of FIT values yielded an area under the ROC curve of 0.48, indicating poor performance of quantitative FIT results in detecting colonic polyps in our CF cohort. These findings align with another study, which also found no significant association between FIT and colonic polyps.21 33

The prevalence of colonic polyps in our study was 27.6%, which is comparable to a recent Danish study in pwCF (24%).34 Given FIT’s poor diagnostic performance in detecting colonic polyps, the high prevalence of polyps and the accelerated adenoma formation in pwCF aged over 40 years,6 we are concerned that relying on FIT alone may not be appropriate or safe in this high-risk group. Therefore, we support the CFF’s recommendation of using colonoscopy as the first-line tool for detecting precancerous polyps and CRC in pwCF.

Polyp histology

From the patients who had polyps removed, the histology was mostly precancerous adenomas (tubular adenoma and tubulovillous adenomas). This is in keeping with other CF studies.5735 Different studies quote the natural trajectory of tubular adenomas to be up to a 1.5%–9.4% risk of developing into malignant lesions in the following 8–10 years.36 37 We observed more right-sided polyps in our study, which is similar to other epidemiological studies.2 38 There is growing evidence that FIT is less sensitive at detecting right-sided polyps compared with left-sided polyps.3942 This may explain the low sensitivity of FIT in our study.

FIT uptake

The uptake or return rate of FIT is another important factor in determining the effectiveness of a screening programme. In our centre, 113 patients were identified as eligible for the screening invitation. We explained the rationale of the screening programme, which consists of an FIT test and a screening colonoscopy. After the patient has been consented, we would simultaneously request the colonoscopy and provide the patients with physical FIT kits to take home, along with instructions on how to obtain a sample and how to post the samples over to the hospital’s laboratory. We observed a 58.4% (66/113) return rate of the FIT kit in our cohort. This is slightly less compared with other BCSPs. The uptake of FIT in the UK BCSP is around 68.9% and 66% in the USA.43 44 Interestingly, despite our more ‘direct’ screening invitation method (face-to-face consult), our FIT return rate is poorer compared with an ‘indirect’ screening invitation, that is, postal invitations employed by the UK BCSP.

Bowel preparation

Poor bowel preparation is concerning, as it can result in missed polyps, with a study reporting up to a third of missed adenoma rates.45 It is known that pwCF need an intensive bowel prep regimen due to the increased mucus production around the bowel mucosa and the slower transit time.2 46 We evaluated the adequacy of our bowel prep regimen by using the BBPS score (see online supplemental file 1), with a score of ≥6 considered adequate.20 In our cohort, the mean BBPS score was 6.8 (6.9 in the group with polyps and 6.7 in the group without polyps). There were two cases of missed polyps due to poor bowel prep. 81.6% (62/76) of the patients had at least adequate bowel prep, with 11 patients (14.5%) needing repeat colonoscopies due to poor bowel prep and three patients (3.9%) needing repeat colonoscopies due to the initial colonoscopy being a complicated procedure (not due to poor bowel prep). In the general population, the rate of inadequate bowel preparation is reported to be 24%, with approximately 17% of repeat colonoscopies attributable to poor bowel prep.47 The repeat colonoscopy rate in our cohort is better than the general population, which is noteworthy given the more intensive regimen required for pwCF.

Study limitations

One of the main limitations of this study is the relatively small sample size of pwCF who underwent both FIT and colonoscopy, which limits the robustness of the sensitivity analyses for FIT in detecting colonic polyps and CRC. To contextualise our findings, we have included comparative diagnostic performance data from other studies in online supplemental file 2). Another limitation is that abdominal or bowel-related symptoms were not recorded as part of the screening protocol. While this information could provide further insights into the relationship between colonic polyps and pwCF, we considered it more appropriate to reflect other screening programmes, which aim to detect abnormalities in asymptomatic individuals. Moreover, gastrointestinal symptoms related to the CF bowels may be difficult to distinguish from those caused by other bowel pathologies. Therefore, the decision to omit symptom recording was a pragmatic approach.

Conclusion

The prevalence of colonic polyps in this cohort of pwCF over 40 years was 27.6%. No CRC was found, but FIT testing was not of value as a screening tool for colonic polyps in this group. At the 10 µg Hb/g threshold, FIT demonstrated poor sensitivity (14%) but high specificity (86%), making it unreliable for primary screening. Furthermore, quantitative FIT values did not correlate with polyp detection and were associated with poor diagnostic performance. These findings should be interpreted with caution due to the relatively small number of pwCF who had both FIT and colonoscopy data.

Histological analysis confirmed precancerous adenomas as the most common polyp type, with a predominance of right-sided lesions—a known limitation of FIT detection. Bowel preparation was generally adequate when an intensive regimen was used, and the rate of repeat colonoscopies due to poor bowel preparation is also lower than that observed in the general population.

We observed a 58.4% FIT uptake rate, slightly lower than national BCSPs, despite our direct invitation to screening via in-person communication. As uptake is a critical determinant of a screening programme’s success, further research is needed to explore behavioural factors influencing engagement with different screening methods. Understanding these behaviours will be essential to maximise the benefit of screening at a population level. Our data supports the need for colonoscopy as the preferred screening modality in pwCF as per recommendations of the US CFF.
