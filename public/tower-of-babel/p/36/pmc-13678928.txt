Measuring what matters- patient and clinician priorities for clinical decision support interactions: a modified Delphi study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient-centered clinical decision support (PC CDS) is a spectrum of digital tools intended to support patient and clinician decision making. Patient-clinician interaction is key to PC CDS, and patient-clinician interaction measurement areas assess how PC CDS impacts the care process and experiences a patient has accessing and receiving healthcare. Identifying measures that assess if and how PC CDS influences outcomes is essential to measuring impact, but it is unclear what outcomes patients and physicians consider important. This study aimed to develop a prioritized list of measurement areas informed by patients and primary care physicians (PCPs) to inform considerations for measuring patient-clinician interaction for PC CDS.MethodsWe conducted a modified Delphi study to understand patients’ and PCPs’ perspectives on the importance of fifteen previously identified patient-clinician interaction measurement areas for PC CDS. We conducted a three-round process with nine patients and nine PCP participants: (1) rating importance of measurement areas, (2) online discussion of ratings, and (3) forced-choice ranking activity of measurement areas.ResultsPatients and PCPs generally agreed about the importance of communication quality and trust in clinicians, and the interconnectedness of these areas. They additionally agreed that the relative importance of measurement areas depends on context and described contextual factors such as care expectations and patient complexity. Patients and PCPs also agreed that satisfaction, decision regret and discharge preparedness are of lower importance for PC CDS measurement. However, patients and PCPs had differing views on the importance of measuring access to information, decision quality and self-management. Patients ranked access to information higher than PCPs, while PCPs ranked decision quality and self-management higher than patients.ConclusionsAssessments of PC CDS effectiveness should consider what outcomes are most important to CDS end-users. Measurement of PC CDS is limited by logistics and feasibility considerations, and researchers should examine the outcomes assessed in PC CDS studies and identify opportunities to assess measurement areas important to key stakeholders. The results of this activity highlight areas for future work, including the importance of understanding how measurement of these areas may be impacted by context (e.g. patient complexity, disease status).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03715-7.

Background

In recent years, healthcare organizations have increasingly focused on providing patient-centered care by honoring patients’ preferences, needs, and values [1]. Patient-centered care establishes a partnership between patients and clinicians, allowing patients to make informed decisions about their care [2, 3]. Alongside this push has been the rise of patient-centered clinical decision support (PC CDS), a spectrum that reflects the degree to which the knowledge base, data, delivery, and use of digital decision support tools (e.g., patient portals, digital decision aids, applications) focus on patient-centered factors [4]. PC CDS can provide timely information for healthcare decision making for patients, caregivers, and clinicians and promotes active engagement by facilitating patient-provider interactions [5, 6].

Although there is a growing interest in digital health tools that support patient decision-making and self-management, the impact of these tools on patient-clinician interactions is less studied and understood. Measuring the impact of PC CDS on patient-centered outcomes is a critical part of evaluating whether a tool or intervention has achieved its intended purpose [7, 8]. While CDS has been shown to improve quality of care, health outcomes, clinical processes, information sharing, and patient-provider communication [9–11], it also must align with patient needs and desired outcomes to be more effective [12–14]. In addition, as PC CDS is intended to support patient and clinician decision making, it is essential to identify measures that assess if and how PC CDS influences outcomes related to patient-clinician interaction and the importance of these outcomes to both groups [7, 8, 15]. However, there is limited literature on PC CDS outcomes that are most meaningful to patients or clinicians. Prior studies have primarily focused on implementation and systems-level factors like cost, with the assessment of clinician-specific outcomes limited to adherence or acceptance [16, 17]. Likewise, assessments of clinicians’ perspectives of and experiences with CDS have tended to focus on impact on workflows or workload and tool usability [18], rather than outcomes related to patient-clinician interactions facilitated by CDS. Various studies highlight the importance of understanding patients’ and clinicians’ perspectives in measurement, often as part of the development and validation process of measures [19, 20]. Examining the similarities and differences in the perspectives of patients and clinicians can inform design, implementation and evaluation of PC CDS, and better understanding these similarities and differences can improve the provision of concordant care advancing patient-centered care [21]. Understanding where patients and clinicians agree regarding these measurement areas may indicate where standardization or incorporation of consistent measurement is most needed. Areas of disagreement can highlight where there is a disconnect in perspectives, which may have implications for measuring the effectiveness of PC CDS tools.

In this study, we aimed to develop a prioritized list of measurement areas to inform potential priorities when measuring patient-clinician interaction for PC CDS according to primary care physicians (PCPs) and patients. Given that evaluation resources and respondent burden limit the number of outcomes that can feasibly be assessed in PC CDS evaluations, we aimed to provide guidance for researchers and implementers of PC CDS on which measurement areas related to patient-clinician interaction are most salient to end users. This information can help researchers and others involved in PC CDS prioritize what they measure to understand the impact of PC CDS, to make sure it is of most value to those involved or using a tool.

Methods

This modified Delphi study sought to understand patients’ and PCPs’ perspectives regarding the importance of fifteen patient-clinician interaction areas for PC CDS. Modified Delphi is a method of building consensus and has been used for various purposes including prioritization of measures and outcomes [22–24]. It is expected that an individual’s responses change based on group feedback through participating in a Delphi study, and this is considered a key component of the consensus building approach [25]. In this modified Delphi study we conducted a three-round process: (1) a rating of the importance of the previously identified measurement areas, (2) online discussions of ratings, and (3) a final forced-choice ranking activity to prioritize the measurement areas. We elaborate on each round of the modified Delphi, hereafter referred to as “the prioritization activity.” Separate prioritization activities were held for patients and PCPs to understand their distinct perspectives.

Participant recruitment

Convenience samples of nine patients, patient partners and patient advocates (referred to as patients in this paper) participated in March 2024, and nine PCPs from November 2024-January 2025. Participants were identified through snowball sampling and recruited via email. For patient advocates, we started with an initial list of patient advocates who participated in the Clinical Decision Support Innovation Collaborative (CDSiC), an initiative funded by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. We then expanded that outreach list by (1) asking these advocates to share the participation invitation with other advocates and (2) conducting online searches for patient advocates. For PCPs, we also started with an initial list of clinicians who had participated in the CDSiC, and conducted online searches for additional PCPs.

To be eligible, patient and patient advocates had to be older than 18 and self-identify as a patient or patient advocate. PCPs needed to be practicing family or internal medicine doctors in the United States (US) who regularly interact with patients. All participants received gift cards based on the requested time commitment for prioritization activities (patients received $100, PCPs received $75). The NORC at the University of Chicago Institutional Review Board (FWA00000142) reviewed all methods and deemed this work not human subjects research.

Identification of outcomes domains

Prior work from the CDSiC, an initiative funded by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, identified 15 measurement areas that reflect patient-clinician interaction and patients’ experiences with care (Table 1) [7]. These measurement areas were identified by the Outcomes and Objectives Workgroup within the CDSiC based on members' expertise in PC CDS, shared decision-making (SDM), and patient-centered care, and refined through a synthesis of 31 systematic reviews on patient-centered measures as well as four key informant interviews. These measures fall across three domains that assess how PC CDS impacts care processes and captures the range of experiences a patient has accessing and receiving healthcare, including interactions and engagement with clinicians and systems.

Initial rating of importance

In the first round of the prioritization activity, participants completed an online form rating the importance of each of the 15 measurement areas (Table 1). Participants received definitions for each measurement area (Appendix A) and rated their importance using a 5-point Likert scale from “Not at all Important” to “Extremely Important.” A response option of “I don’t know” was included, acknowledging individuals may be uncertain about some areas. Aggregated results, descriptive statistics, and charts were developed for each participant group.

Online panel discussion

In the second round, participants discussed initial ratings results to gain a deeper understanding of decisions regarding the importance of the measurement areas. Discussion questions were distributed before each session. Virtual discussions were held via Zoom for approximately 90–120 min and recorded after obtaining participant consent. To accommodate practicing PCP schedules, three separate 90-minute discussions were held (n = 4, 3, and 2 PCPs). Two research team members took notes which were used to help verify the Zoom-generated transcript. Notes were used to correct errors in the transcript as well as document observations not captured in the transcript (e.g., nonverbal behaviors, raised hands). Both the notes and transcript were analyzed using qualitative content analysis, with the transcripts primarily used and supplemented by the notes as needed [26]. Deductive analysis was conducted using Excel. An initial coding framework was developed based on the measurement categories and discussion panel guide and applied to the data. Two members of the research team independently coded the data to identify content related to connections between measurement areas, areas of agreement and disagreement between participant groups (patients and PCPs) and additional themes that emerged during analysis. Coding focused on describing and categorizing the data, and the research team reviewed coding decisions as a group to reach consensus. Because the qualitative analysis was intended to support interpretation of participant perspectives and prioritization decisions, formal inter-rater reliability statistics were not calculated. Excel was used to track coded data, compare content across participant groups, and summarize thematic patterns.

Final prioritization

Following the online discussion, participants ranked all 15 measurement areas against each other in order of importance (1: most important, 15: least important). Participants could additionally respond to two optional open-response questions included in the form on additional factors related to caregivers, and social determinants of health (SDOH) and health-related social needs (HRSNs). Including open-ended response questions that allow participants to explore new and developing ideas is a common component of Delphi panels [27]. The first asked if participants’ rankings would change if a caregiver was involved in patient care, and the second asked about emergent areas that may influence patient-clinician interactions. These topics were first raised in the patient discussion and asked of the patient group. These topics did additionally emerge in the PCP group, who completed the prioritization activity second, and the PCPs were provided with these questions as well. Ranking responses were analyzed by participant group and compared across groups. Disagreement in results between patient and PCP panels were observed when there were differences in the final rankings, and agreement was observed when the final rankings were the same. We did not establish a formal quantitative consensus threshold or calculate panel agreement statistics such as Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) given the exploratory nature of this study. Instead, convergence and divergence in perspectives were assessed descriptively through analysis of rankings and discussion panel findings [28]. Qualitative responses were analyzed in Excel using a content analysis approach to identify emergent themes.

Results

This section summarizes findings from the prioritization activity for both patient and PCP groups.

Findings from the initial rating of importance

Nine patients and nine PCPs participated in the first round of the prioritization activity. Figure 1 depicts the results for both groups. Results are grouped by domain. All measurement areas were rated between “Extremely Important” and “Slightly Important” by both groups; no measures were rated as “Not Important.” Patients tended to rate a greater number of measurement areas as “Extremely Important” compared to PCPs.

Patient ratings

Communication quality was the only measurement area rated as “Extremely Important” by all patients. Other areas highly rated by patients included knowledge, decision quality, coordination of care, and SDM experience. Adherence, decisional conflict, and discharge preparedness were the only measurement areas where less than half of patients selected “Extremely Important.”

PCP ratings

PCPs rated decision quality highest, though responses were split between “Extremely Important,” “Very Important,” and “Moderately Important.” Other areas highly rated by PCPs included access to information, communication quality, coordination of care, and self-management. Timeliness was the only measurement area rated as “Very Important” by all PCPs. Discharge preparedness, decision regret, and satisfaction were the only measurement areas where less than half of PCPs selected “Extremely Important” or “Very Important.”

Qualitative themes from the online discussion panels

Eight patient participants and nine PCPs attended virtual discussion panels in Round 2 of the prioritization activity. Of the eight patients and patient advocates who attended, five were self-reported cancer survivors working with cancer advocacy or research organizations, two worked with rare disease patient advocacy organizations, and one was a patient partner for multiple chronic conditions. Six of the PCPs specialized in internal medicine and three in family medicine. Five had between 1-5 years’ experience, two between 6-20 years’ experience, and two greater than 20 years’ experience. Four PCPs were trained clinical informaticists, and two provided addiction medicine. Seven PCPs were affiliated with academic medical centers, one with a non-profit healthcare system, and one provided primary care via telehealth. Of the types of insurance or payment accepted, 8 PCPs reported accepting private insurance and Medicare, 7 accepted Medicaid/Children’s Health Insurance Program (CHIP), 6 accepted self-payments, 1 accepted U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) insurance, and 1 PCP reported being unsure of the type of insurance or payment accepted.

Below, we describe key qualitative themes from the discussions conducted separately with patients and PCPs. There were two common themes between patients and PCPs, along with one patient-specific theme, and two PCP-specific themes.

Common themes

All measurement areas are important, but relative importance depends on context. Both patients and PCPs noted the importance of all measurement areas in patient-clinician interactions but described various contextual factors that influence relative importance. Patient participants noted expectations and priorities for care vary, and the importance of different measurement areas may be impacted by timing, such as within a person’s health journey, or their relationship with a given provider. Patients discussed how information needs can change over time, and as one patient noted, “When you first get your diagnosis, communication, knowledge and shared decision making […] can be very important, but it could also change over time depending on where you are in a process.” Additionally, patients may have different expectations for trust and communication quality depending on the type of clinician they interact with (e.g., emergency medicine versus primary care) and relationship length, influencing the importance of these areas in different patient-clinician interactions.

Both the patient and PCP groups discussed how various patient factors influence the relative importance of measurement areas. For instance, PCPs noted that SDM experience may be more relevant for patients with multiple conditions, polypharmacy, or complex or life-threatening conditions. One PCP highlighted the impact of generational differences and demographics on the patient-clinician interaction, noting older patients may feel more comfortable with their PCP being more involved in decision making. PCPs further discussed how often measurement areas arising in their practice impacted their rankings, with areas more frequently relevant (e.g., communication quality) more highly ranked than those that occur less frequently in primary care (e.g., discharge preparedness). Similarly, patients highlighted that certain measurement areas, such as knowledge, may be situationally important, such as in the case of a rare disease or complex medical situation. Patients further described how caregiver involvement in complex cases may impact ratings. For example, people with an intellectual or developmental disability or dementia may require caregiver involvement, and priorities around self-management and knowledge likely differ for patients and their caregivers.

There are several relationships and interdependencies among the measurement areas. The majority of patient and PCP participants agreed that communication quality and trust in clinician are central to successful patient-clinician interactions and highlighted the relationship of these to other measurement areas. For instance, patients described how communication quality influences SDM experience, which is difficult to achieve without trust in your clinician. Patients also emphasized the relationship between communication quality and knowledge, as patients do not get needed information when communication is poor. PCPs discussed the importance of communication quality and trust in clinician, and most noted these are critical in primary care because of their impact on other areas. However, a few PCPs noted that a patient’s trust in their clinician is developed over time, and even if trust is not present, quality communication and knowledge can ensure care is adequately delivered.

Patient and PCP participants additionally highlighted relationships among other measurement areas. Specifically, patients noted dependencies between SDM experience and knowledge, as well as timeliness and decision quality. One patient highlighted that information access could be considered an aspect of communication quality. PCPs also noted connections between many of the top ranked measurement areas and discussed how access to information, self-management, communication quality, decision quality and coordination of care are central to patients staying engaged in their care. Several PCPs highlighted that some measurement areas may act as “root causes” for other areas (i.e., they significantly influence other measurement areas). For example, PCPs noted that a root cause of decision quality is if the options considered reflect patient preferences. Most PCPs stated it would be important to measure these “root cause” measurement areas and explore the interconnectedness between areas. For instance, knowledge partially depends on the information accessed, and access to information supports knowledge gathering. Adherence was seen as the outcome of factors such as SDM experience or trust in clinicians. PCPs commented that the dependencies and connections between areas may complicate measurement of the topics, but understanding the relationships is key.

Additional themes from patient discussions

It is important to capture HRSNs and SDOH as these influence access to care and patient-clinician interaction. HRSNs are the individual-level factors that affect a person’s ability to maintain their health [29], and include social and economic needs such as financial instability, food insecurity, and access to housing, healthcare, and transportation [29]. Participants did not view these areas as patient-clinician interaction outcomes, but rather as inputs into healthcare decision making that influence these outcomes. While patients acknowledged difficulties defining and capturing HRSN information in healthcare settings, they reiterated clinicians should consider these factors before making recommendations. As one patient noted, if these factors are acknowledged by the clinician, “that becomes part of the communication and decision making.” Several patient participants discussed how patient costs are not explicitly mentioned in any of the existing measurement areas, but influences access to care, decision making, adherence, and other factors. Likewise, access to care can be extremely important to consider for rural patients who may travel further to appointments or not have access to the same services.

Additional themes from PCP discussions

Ratings were influenced by what measurement areas PCPs can individually impact in their clinical practice. PCP participants expressed interest in measuring areas they felt were actionable or within their locus of control, noting many areas are influenced by factors outside of their personal control. For instance, patient satisfaction, activation, decision regret, decisional conflict, timeliness, and adherence are influenced by patients’ internal drivers (e.g., patient motivation, confidence, psychosocial factors) and external factors outside of clinician control (e.g., reception staff, organizational factors, financial incentives). Several PCPs reflected that satisfaction is the only area typically measured in healthcare encounters, but the current operationalization of satisfaction focuses on factors beyond physician control (e.g., ease of parking, interaction with front desk staff). One PCP noted measuring areas outside their control seems less important than assessing the quality of a decision and how it aligns with patients’ preferences. PCPs also emphasized the importance of timing in measurement, highlighting that measures that are actionable within a visit are more helpful than retrospective assessments. For example, measuring decision quality in real time can help them improve care delivery.

PCPs noted there is a tension in prioritizing measurement areas that reflect following evidence-based practice guidelines compared to those that measure patient preferences. PCPs discussed that their priority is providing evidence-based care while balancing patient preferences and needs. One participant reflected that part of a PCP’s role is to balance the correct clinical decision with the patient’s best interest by helping them prioritize options based on their individual context. This prioritization may result in following evidence-based practices not aligned with patient preferences and can impact decision quality, decisional conflict, and SDM experience. PCPs further discussed how factors that limit patients’ trust in their clinician influence willingness to follow evidence-based advice. Some PCPs emphasized the importance of patient clinical outcomes over process measures that describe the patient’s experience of an interaction.

Patient-clinician interaction measurement areas rankings

Patient rankings

Communication quality, knowledge and SDM experience remained the top measurement areas for patient participants in the final round of this activity. Trust in clinician and access to information, moved from the middle to the top tier of importance. Discharge preparedness remained at the bottom of the rankings, but three areas originally considered of lower importance (adherence, patient activation, and decisional conflict) moved up to the middle in the final ranking.

PCP rankings

The top four measurement areas considered most important in Round 1 (decision quality, communication quality, trust in clinician, and self-management) remained the top measurement areas in the final round. In the final ranking, coordination of care, which was originally highly ranked, fell to the middle, while knowledge moved into a higher rank. Of the areas initially rated lowest, satisfaction, discharge preparedness, and decision regret remained at the bottom, while access to information, which had originally been rated in the middle, fell to the lowest priority group and timeliness moved up to the middle priority group, in the final round.

Emergent measurement areas

Four patients and all PCP participants provided additional insight on the importance of caregivers and emergent measurement areas. Half of patient participants reported caregiver involvement would change how they ranked specific measurement areas such as knowledge, self-management, and trust, and several PCPs noted that involvement of caregivers would increase the importance of coordination of care and communication quality but may lower aspects of decision quality and self-management. Two patients and four PCPs stated involving caregivers would not change their rankings. Regarding emergent areas of HRSNs and cost, the majority of participants noted HRSNs are a significant barrier to quality healthcare, with some PCPs drawing a connection to their influence on decision quality and coordination of care. Patient participants explicitly discussed these as factors that influence decision making and other outcomes, while PCPs noted that HRSNs do not impact care for all patients but can be extremely important to measure and discuss as they influence the feasibility of recommendations. Additionally, the majority of PCPs noted cost of care as important, and for some, it is increasingly part of their patient discussions.

Discussion

Through the three rounds of this modified Delphi prioritization activity, patient and PCP participants assessed the importance of previously identified patient-clinician interaction measurement areas to develop a prioritized list of measurement areas. Understanding patient and PCP views of these measurement areas is vital as evaluators must balance thoroughness with real-world practicalities (e.g., respondent burden) and ensure that evaluations of PC CDS provide meaningful results. Ultimately, this work provides actionable guidance about PC CDS measurement. The areas of agreement between patients and PCPs suggest measurement areas that may warrant prioritization when measuring patient-clinician interaction for PC CDS, and the qualitative nature of this work provides context as to why PC CDS end-users see these areas as important. Assessing the prioritized measurement areas can help to inform more patient- and clinician-relevant PC CDS moving forward.

Areas of agreement and disagreement between patients and PCPs

There was general agreement in the perspectives of patients and PCPs who participated in this prioritization activity regarding areas of highest importance and reasons for these decisions. Participants in both groups highlighted the importance of communication quality and trust in clinician, which were ultimately highly ranked in the final activity. Both groups discussed the interconnectedness of these areas, with patients highlighting that all other areas flow from these, and PCPs describing these as “root causes” of some of the other measurement areas. Several recent studies report similar findings, including evidence that communication quality is linked to greater trust in clinicians, and breakdowns in communication can undermine this trust [30–32]. These areas that may exert influence over others were noted to be particularly important and connected to other areas. The importance of communication quality and trust in providers is well documented, as is the influence of these areas on care experience [33–35]. Patients and PCPs further highlighted other connections between measurement areas as well as the importance of context in determining relative importance of areas. While this activity sought to identify a prioritized list of measurement areas, both groups of participants highlighted factors that could impact what is measured in different settings.

There was further agreement between patients and PCPs about areas that were lowest ranked, including satisfaction, decision regret, and discharge preparedness. Both patients and PCPs noted discharge preparedness was an outlier in this set of measurement areas, and may be prioritized in certain settings, but not in primary care. Additionally, both groups of participants noted satisfaction and decision regret are impacted by other measurement areas and external factors. Satisfaction is often considered a proxy measure for areas such as health care quality [36], but failure to understand how satisfaction influences other areas may complicate interpretation of outcomes. Several studies have identified limitations with measures of satisfaction, and a recent 2023 review identified varied aspects of satisfaction in medical contexts that can limit the utility of satisfaction measures [37, 38]. PCPs highlighted the potential importance of measuring satisfaction if measures were more focused on factors related to the patient-clinician interaction and less focused on external factors that may be beyond the physician’s control (e.g., parking, wait times) [39]. These perspectives about what is within the physician’s control, however, may differ for individuals outside of academic medical centers, such as those in private practice who may have more control over external factors. Both patients and PCPs noted a preference for measuring areas seen as more influential or “root causes” (e.g. access to information, self-management, communication quality, decision quality and coordination of care).

Several areas of disagreement were noted in the prioritization of access to information, decision quality, and self-management. In the final ranking, patients highly ranked access to information, while PCPs ranked this area among the lowest, although they noted it is of greater importance for patients with complex needs. While access to information was prioritized by patients, PCPs may not have the bandwidth to routinely deliver health and wellness information unless a patient presents with a complex medical situation [40, 41]. A recent study quantified how long it would take PCPs to provide guideline-based care to the average US adult panel, finding that it would take 26 h a day with the majority of time spent on counseling tasks [42]. Another study found that on average three clinical decisions are made during office visits, with a range of one to eight, though few were preceded by extensive discussion. The authors highlighted that few decisions included elements of SDM, and that time needed for these discussions may be a contributing factor [43]. Research on the implementation of CDS and other workflow-integrated interventions have identified time pressure and competing demands as key determinants of what is feasible to do during visits and, consequently, what outcomes clinicians view as realistic to measure [44, 45].

PCPs additionally highly ranked decision quality and self-management in the final activity while patients ranked these areas lower. Decision quality was the highest ranked measurement area by PCP participants, who noted it reflects alignment of decisions with patient preferences and may influence other measurement areas. PCPs additionally discussed how in their opinion self-management is a major factor in the ability of patients to continue with their care plan outside a visit, reflecting PCP interest in measures that assess clinical outcomes. Self-management is considered a key component of patient-centered care for clinicians, and can improve patients’ self-care and appropriate utilization of the healthcare services [46]. The difference in the perspectives about the importance of these measurement areas may reveal how structural barriers in the healthcare system, including time limitations, that are ever-present considerations for clinicians [47], but not necessarily patients, impact what is considered a priority in measurement.

Additional areas of disagreement were identified around the importance of cost, access to care and HRSNs. PCPs noted HRSNs and other external factors impact care but may not be actionable or addressable in the primary care context. This tension has also been noted in the broader literature about integrating this type of data into clinical workflows, which emphasize that while screening and documentation are increasing, actionability depends on local resources, referral pathways, and role clarity [48, 49]. A recent qualitative study assessed how PCPs can address HRSNs, but the majority of patient participants highlighted addressing these needs is not the role of a PCP [50]. Additional studies have noted while clinicians understand the importance of HRSNs, structural barriers prevent discussing and addressing these needs during clinical encounters [51].

Implications for PC CDS measurement

This activity highlights the value of understanding what outcomes patients and PCPs find important, as the areas most important to patients or physicians may not be what researchers and implementers first think to assess for PC CDS. The measurement areas often noted in the literature, such as adherence and satisfaction, do not align with those either group identified as most important as currently operationalized. Our goal was to identify what is important to patients and PCPs, and while there were differences, the final rankings from this activity suggest researchers and implementers may consider prioritizing other outcomes in their research and implementations of PC CDS that may be more important to key stakeholders when assessing patient-clinician interaction (e.g., communication quality, trust in clinician, decision quality). In practice, not all areas can be measured due to logistics, feasibility considerations, resources, and the burden of providing and reviewing data. Prioritization of what to measure is vital. This activity identified several areas where patients and PCPs agreed on the importance of measurement areas which can inform future focus of measurement in this field, with additional measures considered depending on the context and goals of the specific tool. Measuring outcomes patients and PCPs find most important can better allow researchers and implementers to measure the value of PC CDS in the context of important patient-clinician outcomes. Researchers should examine the outcomes assessed in PC CDS studies and identify opportunities to assess measurement areas that may be of more importance to key stakeholders in their specific setting, which may differ from those included in this study. Additionally highlighted were considerations around healthcare cost, access to care, and HRSNs, which while not explicit in the patient-clinician interaction measurement areas, can influence many areas. A growing body of evidence suggests HRSNs significantly impact healthcare outcomes, and there is ongoing work to incorporate HRSNs and SDOH factors into PC CDS [49, 52]. Evolving terminology standards for SDOH and HRSNs can support standardized collection of this data for use in CDS [52–54], but a lack of agreed-upon categorization creates measurement issues, as measurement requires an understanding of the concept being measured. More work is needed to understand how these factors can impact PC CDS outcomes, as well as how best to measure these outcomes and when is most appropriate and actionable.

Future directions

Our findings highlight several important areas for future work to further PC CDS measurement. First, a vital next step is understanding what measurement instruments are most applicable or appropriate and operationalizing these within clinical encounters and EHR enabled PC CDS workflows. Establishing agreement about specific measures to use, and developing measures where current measures are insufficient, is key. The CDSiC previously developed a measurement inventory, but these measures may not be applicable in all situations, valid for use with certain populations, or rely on differing definitions for domains, which could be further explored [7]. Prior work also highlights the dearth of instruments for certain measurement areas, including communication quality and access to information, so additional measures may need to be developed in these areas [7]. Mapping prioritized measurement areas to specific instruments, structured EHR elements, and implementation strategies could support greater consistency in PC CDS evaluation. Second, while emerging measurement areas identified by patients included SDOH and HRSNs, more work is needed to understand how these areas can be integrated into measurement as inputs influencing other outcomes. Finally, both participant groups described various contextual factors that influence relative importance of measurement areas, such as expectations, timing, and patient-related factors. Future research should seek to understand how these context-specific factors and the relationship between measurement areas (e.g., root causes) may influence the relative importance of measurement areas. As participants in both groups noted, the importance of some of these measurement areas depends on the disease stage or where an individual is in their health journey (e.g., if they are newly diagnosed), where they are receiving care (e.g., specialist vs. primary care), and who is included in that care (e.g., caregivers or other support). These differences may also impact the decision making processes that clinicians need to make when interacting with patients. These are important considerations that could be further explored.

Limitations

This was formative work with a sample of patients, patient advocates, and PCPs, and is not generalizable to all experiences using PC CDS. Participants were selected because of their experience and interests in PC CDS, and they may not be representative of the broader patient and PCP populations. Several were patient advocates, and several PCP participants had training in clinical informatics, which may have influenced prioritization decisions. Additionally, several patients and patient advocates were cancer survivors and may not represent the views of the general patient population, newly diagnosed individuals or individuals receiving care for other conditions. The majority of PCPs who participated in the activity worked at large academic medical practices and may not represent the views of providers who work in other types of practices. Additionally, due to scheduling constraints we had to hold separate panels with the PCP participants. While having a combined panel may have had an impact on prioritization, it was a necessary choice given the busy schedules of practicing clinicians. Additionally, there was attrition in the patient group, with nine patients completing the first round and seven completing the final ranking. Participants in both groups also noted opportunities to improve clarity in the definitions of measurement areas, highlighting how differences in interpretation can impact prioritization.

There are also a few limitations due to the modified Delphi panel study design. Unlike a traditional Delphi panel, a modified Delphi panel does not typically involve continuous rounds until consensus is achieved and further rounds of ranking could have elicited additional discussion of rankings and clarification where measurement areas were closely ranked. In addition, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting patterns of agreement and disagreement, rather than consensus, given that we did not use a pre-determined consensus threshold and did not assess inter-rater reliability for transcript coding.

Conclusion

Our findings identify preliminary measurement priorities reported by patients and PCPs, and highlight several important considerations. Specifically, both groups of participants discussed the importance of communication quality and trust in clinicians. In addition, both groups noted connections and dependencies between several measurement areas. The results from this activity further point to several areas for future work, including emergent areas in PC CDS related to SDOH and HRSNs, such as cost and transportation. In particular, future work can explore how various contextual factors may influence the relative importance of SDOH and HRSNs to patient-clinician interaction areas, and the need to identify instruments for the prioritized measurement areas.
