The Synergy Between a Silver‐Ruthenium Antimicrobial and Aminoglycosides Is Based on Severe Macromolecular Damage
Authors: Emmanuel P. Oladokun, Gracious Y. Donkor, Julius K. Narh, Grady D. Jacobson, Cade Ward, Patrick O. Tawiah, Lisa K. Polzer, Kevin A. Edwards, Kyle A. Floyd, Jan‐Ulrik Dahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe rise of multidrug‐resistant (MDR) bacterial pathogens, including uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC), highlights the urgent need for alternative treatment strategies to restore antibiotic efficacy. The silver‐ruthenium antimicrobial AGXX exerts potent bactericidal effects through the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS); however, its potential synergy with antibiotics has not been thoroughly investigated. Here, we show that sublethal concentrations of AGXX strongly enhance aminoglycoside‐mediated killing across a diverse panel of Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive MDR clinical isolates, including highly aminoglycoside‐resistant strains. Combinational treatments significantly reduced the effective concentrations of gentamicin, tobramycin, kanamycin, and amikacin required to kill bacteria. Mechanistic analyses revealed that AGXX/aminoglycoside co‐treatments induce pronounced intracellular ROS accumulation, resulting in an imbalanced proteostasis due to extensive protein aggregation and DNA damage. Scavenging ROS abolished synergistic killing, establishing oxidative imbalance as the primary driver of the synergy between both antimicrobials. We further identified polyphosphate as a key bacterial defense mechanism that mitigates ROS accumulation, proteotoxicity, and genotoxic stress during combinational treatment. Moreover, AGXX–aminoglycoside synergy was preserved in an artificial urine medium and across clinical UPEC isolates, underscoring its relevance to urinary tract infections. Together, these findings position AGXX as a potent aminoglycoside adjuvant that restores antibiotic efficacy through ROS‐driven macromolecular damage, supporting its development for combination therapies against MDR bacterial infections.

Introduction

Originally isolated from Streptomyces spp. and Micromonaspora, respectively, aminoglycoside antibiotics have now also been developed as semisynthetic derivatives (Tawiah, Sultana, and Dahl 2025). These bactericidal antimicrobials are polycationic and enter bacteria in three steps. Firstly, establishing ionic interactions between the polycationic moieties of aminoglycosides and negatively charged components of the cell surface, including those found in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Saika et al. 1998; Martin and Beveridge 1986) displaces divalent cations from aminoglycoside binding sites. This, in turn, results in the destabilization of LPS components and the disruption of outer membrane integrity in an energy‐independent way (Joshi et al. 2015). Driven by an increased proton motive force, particularly in aerobically respiring bacteria, aminoglycosides cross the plasma membrane and enter the cytoplasm, where they bind to the aminoacyl‐tRNA recognition site and interact with the 16S ribosomal RNA, ultimately causing protein mistranslation that can lead to protein misfolding [recently reviewed in (Tawiah, Sultana, and Dahl 2025)]. Misfolded proteins can be inserted into the plasma membrane and form irregular membrane channels that further exacerbate aminoglycoside uptake and subsequent bactericidal effects (Kohanski et al. 2008; Davis et al. 1986). Although controversial (Liu and Imlay 2013), production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) as a result from hyperactivation of the electron transport chain in aerobically respiring bacteria has been proposed as a potential contributor to the bactericidal activity of aminoglycosides (Kohanski et al. 2008; Davis et al. 1986; Dwyer et al. 2014). Likewise, aminoglycoside resistance observed in most anaerobic bacteria could be explained by a lack of sufficiently high proton motive force that aminoglycoside uptake depends on (Kohanski et al. 2008; Ramirez and Tolmasky 2010). Moreover, Ezraty et al. (2013) showed that iron–sulfur cluster‐containing proteins play an important role in aminoglycoside uptake, ultimately contributing to the bactericidal effects of these antibiotics.

Resistance to aminoglycosides has now been reported in all ESKAPEE pathogens ( Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter species, and Escherichia coli ) (Oyenuga et al. 2024). E. coli colonizes the gastrointestinal (GI) tract of human newborns shortly after birth, where they establish themselves as part of the commensal gut microbiome. Beyond commensal symbiosis, additional E. coli serotypes can be pathogenic, resulting in life‐threatening diarrheal disease, including enteropathogenic, enterohemorrhagic, enterotoxigenic, enteroinvasive, enteroaggregative, and diffusely adherent E. coli serotypes (Kaper et al. 2004). Moreover, extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli serotypes (ExPEC) can often still colonize the GI tract and serve commensal functions. It is only after dissemination from the GI tract and entry into their primary site of infection that ExPEC strains become pathogenic. The most common ExPEC serotype is uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), the etiologic agent of most urinary tract infections (UTIs) (Kaper et al. 2004). UPEC traverse the urethra to establish infection within the bladder (cystitis) and can further ascend the ureters to cause infection within the kidneys (pyelonephritis), which, if left untreated, can result in the development of potentially life‐threatening septicemia (Klein and Hultgren 2020; Flores‐Mireles et al. 2015). UTIs account for approximately 20%–50% of all nosocomial infections (Saleem et al. 2019), many of which occur in catheterized patients. Biofilm formation on indwelling urinary catheters is the primary driver of catheter‐associated UTIs (CAUTIs) and serves as a reservoir for continued seeding of infection within the urinary tract (Klein and Hultgren 2020; Flores‐Mireles et al. 2015; Andersen and Flores‐Mireles 2020). There is also increasing evidence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR)‐related genes in UPEC (Timm et al. 2024). The inherent infection mechanisms of UPEC, its ability to form catheter‐associated biofilms, and the rising prevalence of AMR highlight the urgent need to develop improved antimicrobial compounds and catheter surface coatings to treat or prevent UTIs (Lansdown 2010; Grass et al. 2011).

Despite its long‐standing history and high efficacy against bacteria, the antimicrobial mode of action of silver (Ag) remains poorly understood, although changes in DNA condensation, membrane alteration, and protein damage have been associated with Ag (Barras et al. 2018). Ag+ ions interact with cysteine thiols, destabilize iron–sulfur clusters, replace metal‐containing cofactors, and elicit ROS generation, thereby damaging a wide range of proteins (Barras et al. 2018). Over the last two decades, silver derivatives have received increased attention for use as antimicrobial surface coatings that can protect from biofilm‐forming bacteria and reduce the risk of nosocomial infections (Nolan et al. 2015). One such silver‐containing surface coating is AGXX (Sobisch et al. 2019; Loi et al. 2018). All AGXX formulations consist of a silver‐ruthenium complex, but differ in the silver‐ruthenium ratio, particle size, and production procedure, which may affect their antimicrobial activity (Clauss‐Lendzian et al. 2018; Vaishampayan et al. 2018). AGXX has been shown to be more potent than silver alone, inhibiting the growth of a range of pathogenic bacteria by eliciting a thiol‐specific oxidative stress response (Loi et al. 2018; Guridi et al. 2015; Donkor et al. 2023; Tawiah, Gaessler, et al. 2025). We recently reported that AGXX potentiates the efficacy of aminoglycoside antibiotics when used in combination against the opportunistic pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Donkor et al. 2023). The synergistic bactericidal effects against P. aeruginosa are mediated by elevated ROS levels in the cells, which disrupt Fe/S‐clusters in metabolic enzymes, lead to elevated free iron, trigger hydroxyl radical formation, and result in a significant increase in outer and inner membrane permeability to exacerbate aminoglycoside influx into the cell (Donkor et al. 2023).

In this study, we provide novel insights into the cellular consequences of the synergy between AGXX and aminoglycosides across a variety of Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative pathogens, including UPEC and highly aminoglycoside‐resistant clinical isolates of various ESKAPEE pathogens. This synergy appears to rely primarily on ROS generation, as co‐supplementation with an antioxidant or the lack of oxygen abolishes AGXX/aminoglycoside‐induced cell death. For the first time, we provide direct evidence that AGXX/aminoglycoside co‐treatments promote extensive protein aggregation and induce DNA damage. Despite the potent toxicity of AGXX/aminoglycoside co‐treatment, we found that UPEC can activate sophisticated defenses by expressing molecular chaperones, such as IbpA, and by producing the chemical chaperone polyphosphate (polyP). We observed that polyP reduces intracellular ROS accumulation, thereby decreasing DNA damage and protein aggregation, and positively affects UPEC survival, underscoring polyP's significant role in oxidative stress defense.

Results

AGXX Enhances the Bactericidal Effects of Aminoglycoside Antibiotics in Various Multidrug‐Resistant Pathogens

The rapid emergence of multidrug‐resistant pathogens (MDR) requires alternative treatment options, including therapies that enhance the effectiveness of existing antibiotics. The silver‐ruthenium antimicrobial AGXX, which was shown to be effective against laboratory strains of both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative pathogens (Loi et al. 2018; Clauss‐Lendzian et al. 2018; Vaishampayan et al. 2018; Guridi et al. 2015; Tawiah, Gaessler, et al. 2025; Linzner and Antelmann 2021), may provide such an opportunity (Donkor and Dahl 2026). As previously reported by us, AGXX potentiates the activity of aminoglycoside antibiotics in the laboratory P. aeruginosa strain PA14, which is due to elevated aminoglycoside uptake as a result of ROS‐induced membrane damage (Donkor et al. 2023). To exclude strain‐specific effects and assess the effectiveness of AGXX/aminoglycoside combinations against other bacterial pathogens, we examined the impact of AGXX/aminoglycoside combinations on a panel of Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive clinical isolates obtained from burn‐wound patients. With minimal inhibitory concentrations (MIC) between 1 and > 2000 μg/mL, these isolates differ quite significantly in their susceptibility to either gentamicin (Gm) or tobramycin (Tob) (Table S1). We exposed P. aeruginosa strains 1, 36, and 40; Klebsiella pneumoniae strain 18; Acinetobacter baumannii strain 99; and Staphylococcus aureus strain 3 to the indicated sublethal concentrations of AGXX720C, aminoglycosides, or their combination and monitored bacterial survival for 4 h by enumerating colony‐forming units (CFUs). Individual treatments with sublethal concentrations of AGXX720C or aminoglycoside had minimal ( P. aeruginosa strains 36 and 40) to no bactericidal effects across these strains (Figure 1A–G). In contrast, combinational treatments with AGXX720C and aminoglycosides resulted in rapid synergistic killing, with ~3‐log or greater reductions in CFUs observed in all tested strains after 4 h. This effect was particularly pronounced against P. aeruginosa strain 1 and A. baumannii strain 99, which both have Gm/Tob MICs > 500 μg/mL (Table S1). Notably, sub‐MIC concentrations of Gm/Tob, combined with AGXX720C, significantly reduced survival by more than 3‐log, thereby rendering these highly resistant isolates sensitive to aminoglycosides again (Figure 1C,E). These findings demonstrate the therapeutic promise of AGXX as a broad‐spectrum compound that enhances aminoglycoside activity against both Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive MDR pathogens.

AGXX/Aminoglycoside Co‐Treatment Causes Severe Oxidative Stress in Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC)

Silver and silver‐containing compounds have become increasingly popular as surface coatings on medical devices, such as catheters, to prevent bacterial attachment and reduce the risk of developing CAUTIs, a significant health problem for hospitalized patients and those living in long‐term care facilities (Andersen and Flores‐Mireles 2020). The most prominent causative agent of CAUTIs is UPEC, which readily adheres to catheters and forms stress−/treatment‐resistant biofilms. To examine the efficacy of AGXX/aminoglycoside combinational treatment of UPEC, we treated mid‐log phase CFT073 cells with sublethal concentrations of Tob, amikacin (Ami), Gm, or kanamycin (Kan), alone and in combination with 30 μg/mL of a more recent AGXX formulation (i.e., AGXX394C), and quantified bacterial survival via CFU enumeration after 240 min. Individual treatments of AGXX394C and the four aminoglycosides had minimal effects on CFT073, only reducing survival by less than 1‐log (Figure 2A and Figure S1). However, combination treatments of AGXX394C and any aminoglycoside decreased CFT073 survival by up to 4‐logs (Figure 2A and Figure S1). Similar synergistic effects were observed for AGXX394C/Tob treatments of two UPEC clinical isolates VUTI156 (pyelonephritis) and VUTI207 (cystitis) after 60 min, which further demonstrates combinatorial effects with clinically relevant UPEC isolates (Figure 2B). These experiments were conducted in artificial urine media (AUM), to more closely mimic conditions found within the urinary tract (Brooks and Keevil 1997). Next, we sought to characterize the strength of the synergistic interaction between AGXX and aminoglycosides in CFT073. We performed a checkerboard assay at varying concentrations of Gm, Kan, Ami, and Tob, with or without 30 μg/mL AGXX720C. While both AGXX formulations consist of the galvanized silver‐ruthenium complex, they differ in various aspects, such as the silver ratio, particle size, and production procedure, with consequences for their antimicrobial activity. Compared to AGXX394C, AGXX720C is characterized by larger particle size and lower bactericidal activity. We observed that the addition of AGXX720C reduced the aminoglycoside concentrations required for complete growth inhibition by 16.7‐fold for Gm, 3‐fold for Kan, 4‐fold for Ami, and 3.8‐fold for Tob (Figure S2), indicating that AGXX potentiates aminoglycoside antibiotics, independent of the type of aminoglycoside and/or AGXX formulation used.

In P. aeruginosa , we previously showed that the synergistic effect between AGXX and sub‐lethal aminoglycoside concentrations is attributed to increased accumulation of ROS (Donkor et al. 2023). We observed a similar ROS accumulation in UPEC strain CFT073, where neither AGXX394C nor Tob alone increased ROS levels, but AGXX394C co‐treatment with a sub‐lethal Tob concentration resulted in a ~13‐fold increase in ROS levels relative to untreated controls (Figure 2C). However, fluorescent dyes for ROS detection have recently been found unsuitable by Korshunov and Imlay (2025). To determine if AGXX394C/Tob co‐treatment elicits increased responses indicative of oxidative stress, we determined the fold‐changes in transcript levels of soxS compared to untreated control cells by qRT‐PCR. SoxS is a transcriptional regulator that activates the expression of genes required in the removal of superoxide, a ROS member recently shown to be generated by AGXX (Tawiah et al. 2025). Expression of soxS was not significantly induced by individual exposure to AGXX394C or Tob, respectively, but was 50‐fold elevated upon exposure to the combinational treatment (Figure 2D). Likewise, UPEC CFT073 strains defective in gshA (involved in glutathione biosynthesis) and trxA (encodes thioredoxin) show impaired survival compared to the WT upon AGXX394C/Tob treatment (Figure 2E), suggesting that the synergy between both antimicrobials impairs the cellular redox homeostasis in CFT073.

The Synergy Between AGXX and Aminoglycosides Is due to Increased Proteotoxic Effects, Evoking Elevated Expression of Molecular Chaperones

Increased ROS levels pose a risk to cellular macromolecules, especially proteins and nucleic acids (Ezraty et al. 2017; Cooke et al. 2003). However, bacteria have evolved intricate systems to cope with the detrimental effects of ROS and mitigate ROS‐mediated damage, including transcriptional upregulation of heat‐shock and SOS responses (Dahl et al. 2015; Sultana et al. 2020; Brand et al. 2025; Sultana and Dahl 2023; Sen and Imlay 2021). To determine whether AGXX394C/Tob co‐treatment elicits increased responses indicative of macromolecular damage, we examined fold‐changes in transcript levels of hallmark genes for protein aggregation (i.e., ibpA, dnaK) relative to untreated control cells. Expression of ibpA, encoding a small heat shock protein that protects against proteotoxic stress (Miwa and Taguchi 2025), was induced 2.5‐fold and 58‐fold by individual exposure to AGXX394C or Tob, respectively (Figure 3A). However, AGXX394C/Tob co‐treatment resulted in over 1000‐fold increase in ibpA (Figure 3A). Similarly, expression of dnaK, a primary molecular chaperone that prevents protein aggregation and guides proper folding during stress and regular growth (Wickner et al. 1991), shows little to no increase upon individual exposure to AGXX394C or Tob (Figure 3A). Treatment with AGXX394C/Tob reveals transcriptional upregulation in dnaK over 30‐fold (Figure 3A), suggesting that the synergy between AGXX and aminoglycosides in CFT073 may be based on a more pronounced protein aggregation. Next, we assessed the extent of protein aggregate formation in vivo in the presence and absence of individual or combined treatments with sublethal concentrations of AGXX394C and/or Tob. We utilized a reporter strain harboring a chromosomal fusion between ibpA and monomeric superfolder green fluorescent protein under the control of the native ibpA promoter. IbpA expression is induced under stress conditions that induce protein unfolding (Ratajczak et al. 2009), and IbpA‐msfGFP has been used previously to monitor and quantify protein aggregation in vivo (Tawiah, Gaessler, et al. 2025; Govers et al. 2018). We exposed E. coli cells expressing IbpA‐msfGFP to sublethal concentrations of AGXX394C (15 μg/mL), Tob (0.3 μg/mL), or their combination for 1 h, and then measured IbpA‐msfGFP fluorescence by flow cytometry. As a positive control, we induced protein aggregation via heat shock by exposing cells to 50°C for 60 min, which resulted in the most pronounced IbpA‐msfGFP fluorescence (Figure S3). The combination treatment of AGXX394C and Tob induced a substantial shift in fluorescence intensity, representing a ~2‐fold increase in IbpA‐msfGFP fluorescence (Figure 3B), comparable to the 50°C heat‐shock control, while there was no significant change in IbpA‐msfGFP fluorescence observed for the individual treatments and untreated controls (Figure 3B). Visualization of IbpA‐msfGFP fluorescent foci by confocal microscopy showed a similar trend for cells exposed to either individual or combined treatments for 60 min (Figure 3C,D). Approximately 25% and 30% of cells showed a single IbpA‐msfGFP focus per cell upon individual treatment with AGXX394C or Tob, respectively, which is comparable to the 20% of cells with a single focus observed in untreated cells (Figure 3C,D). However, IbpA‐msfGFP foci formation was significantly higher upon AGXX394C/Tob combinational treatment, where 85% of cells had foci, and approximately 30% of those cells harbored multiple foci per cell (Figure 3C,D). Overall, our results provide direct evidence of increased proteotoxicity due to the synergy between AGXX394C and Tob.

The AGXX and Aminoglycoside Synergy Exacerbates DNA Damage

Likewise, qRT‐PCR analysis revealed transcriptional upregulation of sulA in response to AGXX394C/Tob co‐treatment (Figure 4A). Next, we sought to further determine if co‐treatment induces significant DNA damage. To monitor and quantify DNA damage, we used E. coli cells that overexpress the bacteriophage protein Gam fused to GFP. Gam‐GFP has been previously shown to specifically bind to DNA double‐strand breaks in both bacterial and mammalian cells (Shee et al. 2013). Gam‐GFP expression was induced by the addition of doxycycline, and cells were either left untreated or treated with AGXX394C and Tob, either individually or in combination. Only ~15% and ~18% of cells treated with Tob and AGXX394C showed one detectable Gam‐GFP focus, respectively, which did not differ significantly from the ~5% observed in untreated cells (Figure 4B,C). Combinational AGXX394C/Tob treatment induced a significant increase in Gam‐GFP foci, with ~50% of cells having at least one focus, which was comparable to the positive control treatment with ciprofloxacin, a DNA‐damaging fluoroquinolone (Figure 4B,C).

The ROS‐Mediated Effects Generated by AGXX and Tob Synergy Can Be Suppressed by Antioxidants or Oxygen Limitation

Our data suggest that the increased sensitivity of UPEC to the synergy between AGXX and aminoglycosides results from augmented intracellular ROS accumulation, which promotes protein aggregation and DNA damage. It has previously been proposed that the bactericidal effects of aminoglycoside antibiotics are at least in part due to the induction of protein misfolding and the insertion of misfolded proteins into the plasma membrane, creating membrane channels and uncontrolled drug uptake (Davis et al. 1986; Bryan and Kwan 1983). Having established that AGXX394C/Tob‐treated cells exhibit increased endogenous ROS levels (Figure 2C), we further investigated whether this oxidative stress directly contributes to AGXX394C/Tob lethality. To test this, CFT073 cultures were pretreated with the ROS quencher thiourea (or left untreated) before exposure to AGXX394C/Tob co‐treatment, and bacterial survival and intracellular ROS levels were determined. In the absence of thiourea, cells subjected to the combined treatment exhibited a ~3‐log reduction in survival (Figure S4A) and a ~25‐fold increase in ROS production (Figure S4B). Thiourea pretreatment restored bacterial survival almost completely, and significantly reduced ROS levels by ~50% (Figure S4A,B). Furthermore, pretreatment with thiourea also mitigated the induction of IbpA‐msfGFP fluorescence to levels observed under unstressed conditions (Figure S4C,D). These findings further point towards a key contribution of ROS to the bactericidal effects of the AGXX/aminoglycoside combinational treatment.

However, in a recent study from the Imlay lab it became evident that antioxidants such as thiourea may not be well suited to prove that the bactericidal effects of select stresses are ROS‐based (Korshunov and Imlay 2024). To provide additional evidence that the synergistic effect of AGXX/aminoglycosides is ROS‐based, we exposed exponentially growing UPEC cells that were either left untreated or pretreated with exogenous catalase, an antioxidant enzyme that degrades hydrogen peroxide (Yuan et al. 2021), to AGXX/Tobramycin for 1 h and then serially diluted for CFU counts. Consistent with Figure S1, AGXX394C/Tobramycin co‐treatment resulted in an approximately 3‐log reduction in survival compared to untreated cells (Figure 5A). Pre‐treatment with catalase abolished this phenotype, as the antioxidant significantly restored bacterial survival to levels comparable to those in untreated controls (Figure 5A), suggesting that hydrogen peroxide plays a substantial role in bacterial killing. Likewise, when UPEC cells were grown anaerobically in MOPSg media supplemented with 1% KNO3, the antimicrobial effects of AGXX/Tobramycin were largely abolished (Figure 5B). Consistent with this data, flow cytometric analysis revealed little to no change in IbpA‐msfGFP fluorescence after treatment with AGXX, tobramycin, or their combination, compared with the untreated control. In contrast, heat shock treatment (50°C) strongly induced IbpA expression. Altogether, these findings further suggest that ROS production is a key contributor to the bactericidal effects of the AGXX/aminoglycoside combinational treatment but requires the availability of molecular oxygen.

Polyphosphate Protects UPEC From AGXX/Aminoglycoside Co‐Treatment Induced Proteotoxicity

In addition to activating protein‐based molecular chaperones such as IbpA, many Gram‐negative pathogens can also respond to proteotoxic antimicrobials by converting cellular ATP into polyP, a chemical chaperone that helps to prevent protein aggregation (Groitl et al. 2017; Gray et al. 2014). We recently showed that UPEC cells lacking the enzyme for polyP production (i.e., ∆ppk) are more susceptible to AGXX (Tawiah, Gaessler, et al. 2025). To test whether polyP protects UPEC from the proteotoxic effects of AGXX/Tob co‐treatment, we analyzed the ability of CFT073, with and without the polyP kinase Ppk1‐encoding gene, to grow and survive upon exposure to the AGXX394C/Tob combination. A growth comparison of AGXX394C/Tob‐treated WT, ∆ppk, and ∆ppk cells complemented with plasmid‐encoded Ppk1 showed a significantly longer lag phase of polyP‐deficient cells, which was complemented by plasmid‐encoded Ppk expression (Figure 6A). Notably, when keeping the concentration of one stressor constant while varying the concentration of the other, it became apparent that polyP protects cells from both AGXX394C and Tob (Figure S5). In our survival studies, we exposed exponentially growing WT and ∆ppk cells to AGXX394C/Tob for 1 h, then serially diluted them for CFU counts. In the absence of Ppk1, AGXX394C/Tob co‐treatment resulted in a ~2.5‐log lower survival compared to the wild‐type, indicating an increased sensitivity to AGXX394C/Tob, which could be fully restored by complementation with plasmid‐encoded Ppk1 (Figure 6B). E. coli produces polyP in response to AGXX394C but not to tobramycin (Figure 6C,D). When treated with the AGXX/Tob combination, we observed polyP production, which was significantly higher than the corresponding AGXX alone would elicit (Figure 6F and Figure S6C,D). Moreover, ∆ppk cells exhibited much higher ROS levels upon treatment with Tob alone and when combined with AGXX394C (Figure 6C). We then sought to analyze potential polyP‐dependent differences in macromolecular damage using IbpA‐msfGFP‐expressing WT and ∆ppk cells. IbpA‐msfGFP‐expressing WT and ∆ppk cells were exposed to a combination of 10 μg/mL AGXX394C and 0.2 μg/mL Tob for 1 h, which is a combination of AGXX394C and Tob concentrations that resulted in the highest IbpA‐msfGFP expression in ∆ppk (Figure 6A,B). Confocal microscopy showed that IbpA‐msfGFP foci in untreated WT and ∆ppk cells were comparable with 12% and 6% of all cells, respectively (Figure 6D,E). While in WT cells, the combined treatment caused IbpA‐msfGFP foci formation in ~20% of the cells, foci were counted in ~85% of ∆ppk cells (Figure 6D,E). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that AGXX394C/Tob induced IbpA‐msfGFP expression was much more prominent in polyP‐deficient cells (Figure 6G,H). The analysis also revealed a significant IbpA‐msfGFP expression in polyP‐deficient cells as compared to WT cells during heat stress (Figure 6E,F), which is consistent with previous reports (Yoo et al. 2018). Taken together, our data point towards a critical role for polyP in protecting UPEC from the deleterious effects of AGXX/aminoglycoside‐induced macromolecular damage.

Discussion

AGXX/Aminoglycoside Combinations Exhibit Broad‐Spectrum Antimicrobial Activity

The emergence of antibiotic resistance is an escalating global public health concern. Given the lack of new antimicrobials in the industrial pipeline, novel approaches are desperately needed to combat bacterial pathogens that are otherwise difficult to eradicate Donkor and Dahl (2026). The threat of growing antimicrobial resistance is further compounded by the ability of many pathogenic bacteria to form protected multicellular biofilm communities. Not only does biofilm formation provide defense against antimicrobials, which struggle to diffuse through the extracellular matrix, but it also increases the likelihood of the spread of antimicrobial resistance genes between bacterial species via horizontal gene transfer. Biofilm‐mediated resistance, along with antimicrobial resistance, is especially problematic in device‐associated nosocomial infections (e.g., catheter‐associated UTIs, intravenous catheter infections, and medical implant‐associated infections). To combat these ever‐evolving threats, a promising area of research has focused on compounds that enhance antibiotic efficacy by targeting metabolic processes and/or cellular networks (Wright 2016). Compounds with synergistic effects have been reported for aminoglycosides (Tawiah, Sultana, and Dahl 2025), as well as beta‐lactams (Bilal et al. 2019; Giamarellou et al. 1984; Pankuch et al. 2011), rifampicin (Poole et al. 2016), and macrolides (Tré‐Hardy et al. 2010). Here, we report on the synergistic interaction between aminoglycosides and AGXX, a ROS‐generating surface coating comprised of the two transition metals Ag and Ru. The antimicrobial activity of different AGXX formulations is attributed to their specific coating composition, including the silver‐ruthenium ratio, particle size, and production procedure (Tawiah, Gaessler, et al. 2025). Silver and silver‐containing derivatives, such as silver nanoparticles, have been previously reported as potent enhancers of aminoglycoside activity in P. aeruginosa and other pathogenic bacteria (Barras et al. 2018; Herisse et al. 2017; Morones‐Ramirez et al. 2013; Dove et al. 2022; Habash et al. 2017). These synergies have been shown to have especially meaningful potential clinical implications, with their use as antimicrobial surface coatings on catheters and other medical implants (Lansdown 2010). Moreover, silver derivatives, such as silver sulfadiazine, are currently used in topical applications as a standard for treating and preventing wound infections, despite being associated with side effects, including allergic reactions to the sulfadiazine moiety (Fuller 2009). Intriguingly, AGXX, which has been shown to be significantly more potent than classical silver and silver sulfadiazine (Carr et al. 1973), is predicted to cause minimal toxicity to human cells due to minimal release of Ag even after several weeks (Guridi et al. 2015; Linzner and Antelmann 2021), and, to our knowledge, antimicrobial resistance to AGXX has not been reported yet. Taken together, these characteristics make AGXX potentially well‐suited for use as a novel antimicrobial surface coating for medical devices.

Following up on our previous study, showing that AGXX potentiates the bactericidal activities of aminoglycosides in the Gram‐negative opportunistic pathogen P. aeruginosa (Donkor et al. 2023), we now provide evidence that the activity of AGXX/aminoglycoside co‐treatments extends to other planktonically growing ESKAPEE pathogens, including MDR‐resistant K. pneumoniae , A. baumannii , S. aureus , and UPEC strains (Figures 1 and 2A). Our killing assays revealed that, regardless of the strain tested, exposure to combinatorial AGXX/aminoglycoside treatment rapidly causes a 3‐ to 5‐log reduction in bacterial survival compared to the individual treatments. Most importantly, AGXX/aminoglycoside co‐treatments reduced the antibiotic concentrations required to efficiently kill clinical ESKAPEE pathogen isolates, some of which are characterized by high gentamicin and tobramycin resistance, by 8‐ to 16‐fold, respectively (Figure 1 and Figure S1). This may minimize the risk and/or severity of antibiotic‐induced side effects and may also delay or prevent resistance development (Rosenberg et al. 2020). These data are consistent with our previous observation that AGXX re‐sensitizes an intrinsically resistant P. aeruginosa strain to sub‐MIC concentrations of kanamycin (Donkor et al. 2023), and underscore the broad‐spectrum antimicrobial potential of AGXX. However, it is important to note that all our studies were performed in planktonic bacteria, requiring the assessment of the efficacy of AGXX/aminoglycoside co‐treatment on bacterial biofilms in the future.

The Synergy Between AGXX and Aminoglycosides Is Mediated by the Induction of Severe Oxidative Stress, Which Impairs Cellular Redox Homeostasis

Our current study further demonstrates that ROS accumulation is the central mechanism underlying the synergistic interaction between AGXX and aminoglycoside antibiotics. Consistent with our previous observation in P. aeruginosa (Donkor et al. 2023), fluorescence‐based assays in UPEC revealed that AGXX/aminoglycoside co‐treatments induce a ~13‐fold increase in intracellular ROS levels compared to individual treatments alone (Figure 2). These elevated ROS levels were tightly linked to the induction of canonical stress response pathways, such as the transcriptional upregulation of soxS, a transcriptional regulator that activates the expression of genes required in the removal of superoxide, as well as dependence on functionally active glutathione and thioredoxin systems. Furthermore, ibpA, which encodes a small heat‐shock protein involved in proteostasis, and sulA, a marker of SOS response to DNA damage, were induced upon AGXX/aminoglycoside co‐treatment. Thus, our data point towards significant proteotoxic and genotoxic challenges that AGXX/aminoglycoside combinations impose on UPEC, likely exceeding the threshold that a functional redox homeostasis can buffer. Flow cytometry and confocal microscopy further demonstrated that AGXX/Tob co‐treatment induced IbpA protein foci to levels comparable to those observed in heat‐shocked bacteria (Figure 3). These observations are consistent with established models in which aminoglycosides promote mistranslation and the accumulation of misfolded proteins (Ezraty et al. 2013), while AGXX independently induces oxidative protein damage through thiol oxidation, leading to widespread protein aggregation (Loi et al. 2018; Tawiah, Gaessler, et al. 2025; Sultana et al. 2021). As a result, expression of molecular chaperones and proteases is highly upregulated in AGXX‐stressed bacteria, as previously shown in several independent transcriptome studies (Sobisch et al. 2019; Loi et al. 2018; Clauss‐Lendzian et al. 2018; Vaishampayan et al. 2018; Tawiah, Gaessler, et al. 2025).

AGXX has recently been shown to induce detectable DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs) in a ROS‐dependent manner when applied in sufficiently high yet still sublethal concentrations (Tawiah, Gaessler, et al. 2025). Although aminoglycosides specifically target protein translation, kanamycin has been reported to damage DNA bases, likely via indirect ROS‐mediated damage (Kang et al. 2012). Likewise, DSBs have been reported to occur in response to subinhibitory concentrations of tobramycin in radD‐defective cells (Negro et al. 2019). In our current study, we did not observe significant Gam‐GFP foci formation when cells were treated with sublethal tobramycin, likely due to the presence of radD. Strikingly, AGXX/Tob co‐treated cells exhibited markedly increased DNA damage, as indicated by the formation of Gam‐GFP foci (Figure 4). This suggests that the combinatorial treatment with AGXX/Tob significantly enhances AGXX's DNA‐damaging activity in UPEC.

We further demonstrate that ROS are not merely byproducts of, but drivers of, AGXX/aminoglycoside toxicity. We found that pretreatment with antioxidants such as catalase and thiourea or growth under strictly anaerobic conditions abolished AGXX/Tob‐induced ROS accumulation, restored bacterial survival to levels comparable to those of untreated bacteria, and suppressed IbpA protein foci formation and protein aggregation (Figure 5 and Figure S4). These data therefore establish oxidative imbalance as the initiating event linking AGXX exposure to enhanced aminoglycoside efficacy. This aligns with ROS‐dependent models of aminoglycoside killing (Kohanski et al. 2008, 2007; Donkor et al. 2023) and reconciles previous controversies (Liu and Imlay 2013; Herisse et al. 2017) by demonstrating that ROS becomes critical when a redox‐active compound amplifies aminoglycoside stress. Together, these ROS‐driven insults overwhelm bacterial macromolecules, providing a mechanistic explanation for the pronounced bactericidal synergy of AGXX with aminoglycosides.

Bacteria Produce Polyphosphate to Counter AGXX/Aminoglycoside Toxicity

Providing further evidence for the ROS‐driven bactericidal synergy of AGXX with aminoglycosides, we found that UPEC protect themselves from this toxicity through polyP production (Figure 6 and Figures S5 and S6). Gram‐negative bacteria have long been known to produce polyP as a stress defense strategy to counter host defense mechanisms (Dahl et al. 2015; Sultana et al. 2020; Rao et al. 2009; Bowlin and Gray 2021; Gray and Jakob 2015). In addition to being involved in various biological processes, including biofilm formation (Rashid et al. 2000; Fraley et al. 2007), motility (Rashid and Kornberg 2000), virulence factor production (Neville et al. 2021; Shah et al. 2024), and host colonization (Rashid et al. 2000; Neville et al. 2021; Dahl et al. 2017), polyP has also been identified as a potent chemical chaperone that protects bacteria from proteotoxic stress (Groitl et al. 2017; Gray et al. 2014; Yoo et al. 2018; Cremers et al. 2016). Here, we demonstrate a significant increase in polyP production when cells are exposed to AGXX and AGXX/Tob (Figure S6). This is consistent with a previous report that showed that ∆ppk cells were highly sensitive to silver nitrate and that the transcriptome of AGXX‐treated UPEC cells exhibited signs of silver exposure (Tawiah, Gaessler, et al. 2025). While the deletion of ppk1, the gene responsible for polyP production, has been associated with increased susceptibility of E. coli and P. aeruginosa towards aminoglycoside and carbapenem antibiotics (Chen et al. 2016; Ortiz‐Severín et al. 2015), we did not detect a significant induction in polyP production in Tobramycin‐treated cells (Figure S6). Whether the activation of polyP production is in response to AGXX/aminoglycoside‐generated ROS or potentially to silver ions that are released from the AGXX formulation remains unclear. Previous studies support a role for polyP in protecting bacteria from oxidative stress through metal ion regulation (Gray and Jakob 2015). For instance, PolyP binds Mn2+, which can either be converted into catalytic superoxide scavengers such as MnHPO4 via polyP degradation or directly detoxify superoxide through non‐catalytic oxidation of Mn2+, potentially reducing the need for superoxide dismutase. PolyP also limits damage from Fenton chemistry by chelating iron and stabilizing Fe3+, thereby reducing hydroxyl radical production (Richter and Fischer 2006). In addition, polyP promotes the detoxification and export of harmful metals, including Cu2+, Cd2+, and Hg2+, through conserved phosphate‐dependent transport systems, further enhancing resistance to oxidative and metal‐induced stress (Grillo‐Puertas et al. 2014). We conclude that PolyP, likely induced by ROS accumulation, acts as a chemical chaperone to protect from the proteotoxic effects of AGXX/Tob, and propose that both IbpA expression and polyP production are important for a stringent defense. This may explain why, compared to ∆ppk strains, IbpA‐msfGFP expression is substantially reduced and IbpA‐msfGFP foci are present in much fewer cells in the polyP‐producing WT strain. In the absence of polyP, cells depend primarily on IbpA‐mediated chaperone activity to counter AGXX/Tob‐mediated proteotoxic stress. However, this compensatory mechanism is insufficient to prevent widespread damage, and our data highlights the critical role of polyP in protecting bacterial cells from redox‐induced proteome disruptions.

Collectively, our study positions AGXX as a dual‐function antimicrobial that potentiates aminoglycoside effects on Gram‐negative pathogens by intensifying ROS‐mediated proteotoxic and genotoxic stress. By driving bacterial stress responses beyond recoverable thresholds, AGXX potentiates the efficacy of aminoglycoside exposure with detrimental effects on bacterial viability. More broadly, our work highlights controlled redox stress as a rational strategy for antibiotic potentiation and provides a mechanistic framework for the development of ROS‐based antimicrobial adjuvants.

Experimental Procedures

Bacterial Strains and Growth Conditions

All strains, plasmids, and oligonucleotides used in this study are listed in Table S2. Unless stated otherwise, overnight cultures were grown aerobically in Luria‐Bertani (LB) broth (Millipore Sigma) at 37°C with shaking at 300 rpm for 16–20 h. For subsequent experiments, overnight cultures were diluted into 3‐(N‐morpholino) propanesulfonic acid minimal medium supplemented with 0.2% glucose, 1.32 mM K2HPO4, and 10 μM thiamine (MOPSg) (Neidhardt et al. 1974), or in artificial urine media (AUM) (Brooks and Keevil 1997), and incubated at 37°C under shaking conditions.

Preparation of Artificial Urine Media (AUM)

AUM was prepared as previously described (Brooks and Keevil 1997). The final concentrations of the components were as follows: 78.5 mM sodium chloride, 9 mM sodium sulfate, 2.2 mM sodium citrate dihydrate, 0.1 mM sodium oxalate, 3.6 mM potassium dihydrogen phosphate, 21.5 mM potassium chloride, 3 g/L tryptic soy broth, 3 mM calcium chloride, 2 mM magnesium chloride, 15 mM ammonium chloride, 6 mM creatinine, and 200 mM urea (pH 6.3). The AUM was freshly prepared every 7 to 10 days.

Preparation of AGXX Formulations

All AGXX formulations used in this study were developed and supplied by Largentec GmbH (Berlin, Germany). AGXX394C and AGXX720C microparticles used in this study were made from silver powders on cellulose with a particle size between 1.5 and 2.5 μm (MaTeck, Germany). The silver‐ruthenium complex was realized by Largentec proprietary electrochemical deposition processes.

Time‐Killing Assay of Clinical Isolates

Overnight cultures were diluted ~20‐fold into MOPSg by normalizing cultures to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) = 0.1 in a six‐well sterile cell culture plate. Samples were then treated with sublethal concentrations of AGXX720C and Gm or Tob individually or in combination. Cell culture plates were incubated at 37°C for 4 h at 150 rpm under aerobic conditions. Cells were collected at the indicated time points, serially diluted in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), plated on LB agar, and incubated at 37°C for 20 h to quantify surviving colonies.

Bacterial Survival Assays

Overnight cultures of UPEC strains CFT073 or CFT073∆ppk were diluted 50‐fold into the indicated media to an initial OD600 ~ 0.08 and incubated until reaching an OD600 = 0.3. Cultures were then transferred to sterile 125 mL flasks and exposed to sublethal concentrations of AGXX, the indicated aminoglycosides, or both. If indicated, cultures were pretreated with either 500 U catalase or 70 mM thiourea for 60 min before AGXX and/or aminoglycoside exposure. After 1 h, aliquots were serially diluted in PBS, plated on LB agar, and incubated overnight at 37°C with shaking to determine colony‐forming unit (CFU) counts. Anaerobic growth was performed as follows: overnight cultures were diluted to OD600 = 0.1 in MOPSg containing 1% KNO3 and grown to OD600 ~ 0.3 in 14‐mL polystyrene round‐bottom Falcon tubes sealed with parafilm prior to the indicated treatments. Cells were then serially diluted, plated on LB containing 1% KNO3, and incubated anaerobically at 37°C.

Checkerboard Assay

Checkerboard assays were performed as previously described (Canchola et al. 2024), with slight modifications. 100× stock solutions of the indicated aminoglycoside antibiotics were serially diluted and added to the respective wells of 12‐well plates, while the concentration of AGXX720C was held constant across wells. Exponentially growing UPEC CFT073 cultures in MOPSg minimal media were adjusted to an initial OD600 of approximately 0.05 and added to each well. Plates were incubated at 37°C with shaking for 16–18 h. OD600 was subsequently measured to assess bacterial growth.

Intracellular ROS Quantification

Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using the redox‐sensitive dye 2′,7′‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Exponentially growing cultures of CFT073 and CFT073∆ppk were left untreated or treated with the indicated concentrations of AGXX394C, Tob, or their combination for 1 h. Duplicate samples were then collected, washed once with pre‐warmed PBS, normalized to an OD600 ~1.0 in prewarmed PBS containing 10 μM H2DCFDA, incubated in the dark at 37°C for 30 min, and rewashed with PBS before DCF fluorescence was measured at excitation/emission wavelengths of 485/535 nm in a Tecan Infinite 200 plate reader. If indicated, cells were incubated with 70 mM thiourea, a ROS quencher, prior to stress treatments.

Gene Expression Analysis by qRT‐PCR

Overnight cultures of CFT073 were diluted into MOPSg medium to an OD600 ~ 0.08 and grown to mid‐log phase (OD600 ~ 0.3). Cultures were either left untreated or treated with 30 μg/mL AGXX394C, 0.5 μg/mL Tob, or their combination. After 30 min of incubation, transcription was halted by adding ice‐cold methanol. Total RNA was extracted from at least three biological replicates using a commercial RNA extraction kit (Macherey‐Nagel), residual genomic DNA was removed with the TURBO DNA‐free kit (Thermo Scientific), and mRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript cDNA synthesis kit (Takara). qRT‐PCRs were performed using manufacturer protocols (Alkali Scientific) with gene‐specific primers (i.e., soxS, sulA, dnaK, ibpA, and rrsD). Gene expression was normalized to rrsD, and relative transcript levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Pfaffl 2001).

Construction of In‐Frame Gene Deletions in E. coli Cells Expressing IbpA‐msfGFP

An in‐frame ppk deletion mutant was constructed using the lambda red‐mediated site‐specific recombination (Datsenko and Wanner 2000). In brief, the ppk gene in E. coli MG1655‐ibpA‐msfGFP was replaced by a chloramphenicol resistance cassette, which was subsequently resolved using pCP20 to yield the nonpolar in‐frame deleted strain MG1655‐ibpA‐msfGFP∆ppk. The chromosomal mutation was verified by PCR.

IbpA‐msfGFP Expression by Flow Cytometry

Overnight cultures of strains MG1655::ibpA‐msfGFP and MG1655∆ppk::ibpA‐msfGFP were diluted into fresh MOPSg medium to an OD600 ~0.01 and grown to mid‐log phase (OD600 ~ 0.3). Prior to cultures reaching mid‐log phase, cells were left untreated or pretreated with 70 mM thiourea, after which the indicated concentrations of AGXX394C, Tob, or their combination were added for 1 h. Cells grown under anaerobic conditions were treated as described before. Cells exposed to 50°C for 60 min served as positive controls. For flow cytometry analysis of IbpA‐sfGFP expression, samples were normalized to an OD600 of 0.05 in PBS (pH 7.4) and analyzed on a BD FACS Melody cytometer via the FITC channel, recording at least 10,000 events per sample. Data were analyzed with FCSalyzer.

IbpA‐msfGFP Binding to Protein Aggregates In Vivo

Overnight cultures of strains MG1655::ibpA‐msfGFP and MG1655∆ppk::ibpA‐msfGFP were diluted into fresh MOPSg medium to an OD600 ~ 0.01 and grown to mid‐log phase (OD600 ~ 0.3). At mid‐log phase, cells were either left untreated or treated with the indicated concentrations of AGXX394C, Tob, or their combination for 1 h. Cells grown under anaerobic conditions were treated as described before. Cells exposed to 50°C for 60 min served as positive controls. For visualization of IbpA‐sfGFP‐bound protein aggregates, cells were washed twice in PBS, stained with 5 μg/mL DAPI and 5 μg/mL FM4‐64 in the dark for 15 min, rewashed, and mounted on 1% agarose pads. Fluorescence microscopy was performed using a Leica SP8 confocal system with a DMi8 CS inverted microscope. At least 500 cells across all replicates were blindly quantified.

Gam‐GFP Binding to DNA Double‐Strand Breaks

Strain MG1655::gam‐gfp was grown overnight in LB supplemented with 200 ng/mL doxycycline to induce Gam‐GFP expression. Cells were diluted into doxycycline‐containing MOPSg medium to an OD600 ~0.08 and grown to mid‐log phase (OD600 ~ 0.3) and treated with the indicated concentrations of AGXX394C, Tob, or their combination. Ciprofloxacin was added as a positive control for DNA damage. Following 3 h of treatment, cells were washed twice, resuspended in PBS, stained with 5 μg/mL DAPI and 5 μg/mL FM4‐64 in the dark for 15 min, rewashed, and resuspended in PBS. Samples were imaged on 1% agarose gel pads using a Leica SP8 confocal system, which is equipped with a DMi8 CS inverted microscope. At least 500 cells across all replicates were counted blindly.

PolyP Extraction and Quantification

Intracellular polyP was extracted with minor modifications as previously described (Shah et al. 2024; Dahl et al. 2018), and quantified using the novel polyP‐specific fluorescent dye JC‐D7 (Yang et al. 2024). Mid‐log‐phase cultures of the IbpA‐msfGFP‐expressing E. coli WT and Δppk1 strains were grown in MOPSg at 37°C under aerobic conditions and then exposed to the indicated concentrations of Tob and/or AGXX394C for 1 h. Following treatment, cultures were normalized to an OD600 of 0.3, and 1 mL aliquots were harvested by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Cell pellets were resuspended in 200 μL guanidinium isothiocyanate (GITC) lysis buffer and incubated at 95°C for 10 min to release intracellular polyP. A 50 μL aliquot of the crude lysate was reserved for total protein determination using the Bradford assay. The remaining lysate was mixed with 95% ethanol and applied to silica membrane EconoSpin columns (Epoch Life Science) for polyP purification. Columns were washed twice with 5 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), and polyP was eluted with 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0). Eluted polyP was mixed with 25 μM JC‐D7, incubated in the dark for 10 min, and fluorescence was measured at excitation/emission wavelengths of 405/535 nm, which detects the JC‐D7‐polyP complex. JC‐D7 mixed in the elution buffer was deducted as the buffer control. Further, the background was determined by treating the purified polyP samples with 1 mg ScPPX for an hour prior to staining with JC‐D7. PolyP concentrations were then extrapolated from a sodium polyP standard curve prepared in 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0.

Growth Curve‐Based Assays

Overnight cultures of the indicated strains were diluted ~50‐fold into MOPSg media, grown to early stationary phase, diluted again into fresh MOPSg to an OD600 = 0.02, and incubated in a Tecan Infinite 200 plate reader with or without the indicated concentrations of AGXX394C, Tob, or their combination. Absorbance at 600 nm was measured every 10 min for 16 h.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism version 8.0.
