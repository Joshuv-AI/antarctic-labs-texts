Comparative contributions of visceral adiposity and muscle-related parameters to postoperative complications after gastrectomy: a domain-based analysis
Authors: Zhang Lin, Li-Min Xia, Qian Wu, Xiaohan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBody composition is increasingly recognised as an important determinant of surgical outcomes, yet the relative contributions of its distinct physiological domains, particularly metabolic burden and structural or functional reserve, remain unclear. We aimed to comparatively evaluate the independent associations of visceral adiposity and muscle-related parameters with postoperative complications after gastrectomy within a unified analytical framework.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we analysed 362 patients undergoing curative gastrectomy for gastric cancer. Preoperative body composition was assessed using CT-derived skeletal muscle index, muscle density, and visceral fat area, together with functional measures including grip strength and walking speed. Associations with short-term and 30-day postoperative complications were examined using hierarchical multivariable logistic regression models. Dose–response relationships were evaluated using restricted cubic splines, and supplementary sensitivity and internal validation analyses were performed.ResultsAcross all adjusted models, visceral adiposity showed a consistent and independent association with postoperative complications. Each one-standard deviation increase in visceral fat area was associated with higher odds of short-term complications (OR 1.55, 95% CI 1.14–2.10) and 30-day complications (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.23–2.36). In contrast, skeletal muscle index, muscle density, grip strength, and walking speed were not independently associated with postoperative outcomes after adjustment. Dose–response analyses demonstrated a graded increase in risk across increasing levels of visceral fat area, supporting a continuous association with postoperative risk. The addition of visceral fat area to conventional clinical models provided modest incremental predictive information, particularly for 30-day postoperative complications.ConclusionsIn this comparative analysis, visceral adiposity emerged as a more consistent correlate of postoperative complications than muscle-related parameters. These findings support a domain-based interpretation of body composition and suggest that metabolic and inflammatory burden may play a more prominent role than structural or functional reserve in determining perioperative risk after gastrectomy. Introduction

Gastric cancer remains a major global health burden and continues to be a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, particularly in East Asia where both incidence and disease burden are highest [1]. Surgical resection offers the only curative treatment for most patients; however, postoperative complications remain common and continue to limit recovery and long-term outcomes. These complications not only prolong hospitalisation and increase healthcare costs, but may also delay or preclude adjuvant therapy and ultimately compromise survival [2, 3]. In this context, accurate preoperative risk stratification is essential for optimising perioperative management, particularly within enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways [4].

Current risk assessment strategies are largely based on demographic and clinical characteristics such as age, comorbidities, and tumour stage. Although these factors capture aspects of disease severity and general health status, they provide only a limited representation of an individual’s physiological reserve [5–7]. Increasing attention has therefore shifted toward body composition as a more direct reflection of the balance between metabolic resources and functional capacity. In particular, skeletal muscle mass and function have been widely studied as indicators of frailty, and sarcopenia has been associated with adverse outcomes across multiple cancer populations [8, 9]. However, findings in patients undergoing gastrectomy have been inconsistent, especially after adjustment for clinical and tumour-related factors, raising questions about whether muscle-related indices alone adequately capture perioperative risk [10]. Importantly, these inconsistencies suggest that muscle-related measures may not represent a uniform or independent physiological construct, but may instead reflect broader dimensions of patient vulnerability, including ageing, comorbidity burden, and nutritional status.

In parallel, a growing body of evidence has highlighted the role of adipose tissue, particularly visceral fat, as an active contributor to systemic metabolic and inflammatory states [11]. Unlike subcutaneous fat, visceral adiposity is closely linked to insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and chronic low-grade inflammation. It serves as a major source of pro-inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor-α, which are known to influence immune function, endothelial integrity, and tissue repair [12, 13]. These mechanisms are highly relevant in the perioperative setting, where excessive inflammatory responses and impaired immune regulation may increase susceptibility to postoperative complications [14]. From a technical perspective, excess visceral fat may also complicate surgical exposure and increase operative difficulty, thereby further amplifying perioperative risk [15]. Importantly, these effects are not adequately captured by body mass index, which does not reflect fat distribution [16]. Together, these observations suggest that visceral adiposity may represent a physiological domain related to metabolic and inflammatory burden that is complementary to, rather than fully captured by, muscle-based or conventional clinical measures.

Despite increasing recognition of both sarcopenia and obesity in surgical risk assessment, their relative contributions remain incompletely understood. Most studies have evaluated muscle-related parameters or adiposity in isolation, while those examining combined phenotypes often rely on dichotomised definitions (e.g., sarcopenic obesity), potentially obscuring clinically meaningful gradients of risk and limiting insight into their independent and joint effects [17]. As a result, existing evidence does not clearly distinguish whether these measures reflect overlapping aspects of the same underlying vulnerability or fundamentally different physiological processes. Moreover, few studies have simultaneously incorporated CT-derived skeletal muscle mass, muscle density, functional measures such as grip strength and walking speed, and visceral fat area within the same analytical framework that allows direct comparison of their independent effects [16]. Even fewer have evaluated these components as continuous variables using standardised approaches to characterise dose–response relationships. This lack of direct comparability limits the ability to determine which components of body composition are most relevant for postoperative risk, whether their effects operate along a continuum rather than through predefined categories, and whether different body composition measures provide overlapping or distinct information regarding perioperative vulnerability.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the relative contributions of skeletal muscle mass, muscle function, and visceral adiposity to postoperative complications in patients undergoing gastrectomy for gastric cancer. Rather than focusing on individual parameters in isolation, we examined these domains within a unified analytical framework. We specifically assessed whether visceral fat area showed more consistent associations with postoperative complications than muscle-related parameters after adjustment for demographic, clinical, tumour-related, and surgical factors. We further explored whether visceral fat demonstrated graded associations with postoperative risk across its continuous distribution and examined these components as reflecting distinct physiological domains rather than interchangeable predictors. By directly comparing metabolic and functional dimensions of body composition within the same framework, this study sought to characterise the relative contributions of metabolic–inflammatory burden and structural or functional reserve to postoperative risk after gastrectomy. In doing so, we aimed to provide a comparative and domain-oriented perspective on body composition assessment in surgical oncology rather than to evaluate any single parameter in isolation.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was a retrospective observational analysis based on prospectively collected data from a consecutive cohort of patients with gastric cancer who underwent gastrectomy at a tertiary referral centre between 2013 and 2019. The dataset analysed in the present study was derived from the same institutional cohort previously described in studies evaluating cancer-related malnutrition and cachexia, with standardized perioperative data collection protocols applied throughout the study period [18].

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older with pathologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma who underwent curative-intent gastrectomy. Curative-intent gastrectomy was defined as surgical resection performed with the aim of complete tumour removal in patients without evidence of distant metastasis. Patients were excluded if they had evidence of distant metastasis at diagnosis, a history of other malignancies within the preceding five years, severe comorbid conditions that precluded radical gastrectomy, or missing preoperative abdominal computed tomography data or other key variables required for the present analyses. In this study, severe comorbid conditions referred to severe cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal dysfunction that made patients unable to tolerate radical gastrectomy for gastric cancer. To ensure consistency across analyses, a complete-case cohort was constructed including participants with available data on exposures, outcomes, and covariates of interest. Data collection and clinical assessment

Clinical and demographic information was extracted from electronic medical records using standardized institutional protocols. Data collection was performed within 24 h of admission or during routine preoperative assessment. Variables included age, sex, body mass index, nutritional risk screening score (NRS 2002), comorbidity status, and laboratory indicators such as hemoglobin and serum albumin. Tumour-related characteristics, including tumour size, anatomical location, and TNM stage, were recorded based on pathological reports.

Perioperative variables included surgical approach, operative duration, type of gastrectomy, extent of lymph node dissection, and combined organ resection. All patients underwent curative-intent gastrectomy according to institutional surgical standards. Postoperative outcomes, including complications, length of hospital stay, and 30-day readmission, were systematically documented according to institutional follow-up protocols.

Assessment of body composition and muscle function

Preoperative abdominal computed tomography scans were used to quantify body composition. All patients underwent routine preoperative contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography as part of standard clinical assessment before gastrectomy. CT examinations were performed using a 64-slice spiral CT scanner (LightSpeed VCT; GE Healthcare, USA). Patients were scanned in the supine position from the diaphragmatic dome to the pelvic inlet. Images were acquired with a slice thickness of 5 mm and a pitch of 1.375:1. Abdominal window settings were used for image interpretation (window width 400 HU; window level 25 HU).

Cross-sectional images at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3) were analysed using dedicated imaging software by trained investigators who were blinded to clinical outcomes. Skeletal muscle area was identified using predefined Hounsfield unit thresholds ranging from − 29 to + 150 HU, and skeletal muscle index was calculated by normalising muscle area to height squared (cm²/m²). Muscle density, reflecting intramuscular fat infiltration, was defined as the mean attenuation value of skeletal muscle within the same region.

Visceral fat area was quantified from the same L3 slice using established attenuation thresholds for adipose tissue. Because all CT images were obtained using a standardized institutional preoperative imaging protocol, body composition measurements were derived under broadly consistent acquisition conditions across the study cohort. To facilitate comparability across variables in regression analyses, continuous body composition measures were standardised and analysed per one standard deviation increase.

Muscle function was assessed using handgrip strength and walking speed. Handgrip strength was measured using a calibrated dynamometer, with the maximum value from three attempts recorded. Walking speed was assessed over a standardised distance under usual pace conditions.

Definitions of body composition phenotypes

Low skeletal muscle mass was defined using sex-specific cut-offs for skeletal muscle index based on previously published studies in Asian populations, where thresholds of 49 cm²/m² for men and 31 cm²/m² for women have been widely used [19]. Low muscle strength and reduced physical performance were defined according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 consensus, using handgrip strength thresholds of < 28 kg for men and < 18 kg for women, and a usual walking speed of < 1.0 m/s, respectively [20].

Myosteatosis was defined using BMI-specific skeletal muscle radiation attenuation cut-offs originally proposed by Martin et al., with thresholds of < 41 HU for individuals with BMI < 25 kg/m² and < 33 HU for those with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² [21]. Visceral obesity was defined according to the classification provided in the original dataset, based on established thresholds for visceral fat area.

In addition to individual parameters, a combined phenotype of visceral obesity and low muscle strength was constructed to examine their joint effects on postoperative risk.

Definition of postoperative complications

Postoperative complications were defined as any complication of grade II or higher according to the Clavien–Dindo classification occurring within 30 days after surgery [22]. Complications were identified through retrospective review of medical records and institutional follow-up documentation. Two trained investigators independently reviewed and classified postoperative complications according to the Clavien–Dindo system. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a senior clinician. The primary outcome of interest was the occurrence of short-term postoperative complications. Secondary outcomes included 30-day postoperative complications and specific complication subtypes as recorded in the dataset.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were undertaken to characterise the relationship between preoperative body composition, muscle function, and postoperative outcomes following gastrectomy, with short-term postoperative complications designated as the primary endpoint.

Continuous variables were summarised as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), depending on distributional characteristics, and categorical variables were presented as number (percentage). Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

The primary analytical framework was based on multivariable logistic regression to evaluate associations between body composition parameters and postoperative complications. Key exposure variables included visceral fat area, skeletal muscle index, muscle density, grip strength, and walking speed. To facilitate direct comparison across heterogeneous measures, all continuous exposures were standardised prior to modelling, and effect estimates were expressed per one standard deviation increase.

A hierarchical modelling strategy was used to evaluate the robustness of associations between body composition, muscle function, and postoperative outcomes. Model 0 provided unadjusted estimates. Model 1 adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 further adjusted for preoperative clinical and tumour-related factors, including body mass index, Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 score, American Society of Anesthesiologists classification ≥ 3, Charlson comorbidity score, TNM stage, tumour location (cardia versus non-cardia), and maximum tumour size. Model 3 additionally incorporated key surgical factors, including surgical resection range, laparoscopic surgery, combined organ resection, and operative duration, to account for heterogeneity in surgical management. To minimise collinearity, variables used to define derived phenotypes were not simultaneously included in the same model when the corresponding composite exposure was analysed.

Complementary analyses were conducted using both continuous and clinically defined categorical representations of exposures, including low skeletal muscle index, myosteatosis, visceral obesity, low grip strength, and low walking speed, based on established thresholds. Potential non-linear relationships between visceral fat area and postoperative complications were evaluated using restricted cubic spline models, with model selection guided by information criteria and tests for nonlinearity.

Beyond association analyses, model discrimination and incremental predictive information were evaluated within a risk prediction framework. Discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves and the area under the curve (AUC), with comparisons between models performed using DeLong’s test. Changes in model fit were additionally evaluated using Akaike and Bayesian information criteria (AIC and BIC). Trend analyses across quantiles of visceral fat area were conducted to assess consistency of risk gradients.

To reduce optimism in apparent model performance, internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples. Apparent AUCs and optimism-corrected AUCs were calculated by comparing model performance in bootstrap samples with performance in the original complete-case cohort. These analyses were used to assess the stability and potential overfitting of discrimination estimates.

The incremental predictive contribution of adding visceral fat area to the baseline clinical model was evaluated using complementary approaches and was interpreted as evidence of incremental predictive information rather than as a definitive clinical prediction model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to examine net benefit across threshold probabilities ranging from 5% to 50%, with uncertainty estimated using bootstrap resampling. To further assess changes in risk stratification, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated, and bootstrap methods were used to derive confidence intervals. Given the retrospective single-cohort design and absence of external validation, NRI and IDI were interpreted as exploratory measures of incremental information.

Model calibration was evaluated using calibration plots based on deciles of predicted risk, calibration intercept and slope, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. These analyses were used to assess agreement between predicted and observed risks and to identify potential systematic miscalibration.

To explore combined physiological phenotypes, joint exposure models were constructed to examine the interaction between visceral obesity and low grip strength. Associations with specific types of postoperative complications were examined in secondary analyses and restricted to outcomes with at least 20 events to minimise instability of estimates. Time-to-event analyses for overall survival were performed using Cox proportional hazards models with the same hierarchical adjustment strategy and were considered exploratory.

All primary analyses were conducted in a unified complete-case cohort to ensure consistency of covariate adjustment across models. Additional analyses using available-case datasets were performed to assess robustness. To further address potential bias arising from missing data and selection into the complete-case cohort, inverse probability weighting (IPW) analyses were conducted. Stabilised weights were estimated from a logistic regression model predicting inclusion in the complete-case cohort and were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of findings under alternative skeletal muscle index definitions and exposure classifications. To assess potential temporal confounding arising from changes in surgical practice during the study period (2013–2019), supplementary analyses were performed with additional adjustment for calendar year and surgical period (2013–2015, 2016–2017, and 2018–2019).

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Patients who developed short-term postoperative complications were older than those without complications (68.75 ± 10.52 vs. 63.40 ± 10.29 years, P < 0.001), whereas sex distribution and BMI were similar between groups. Markers of general condition showed modest differences, with higher proportions of ASA ≥ 3 and higher NRS2002 scores among patients with complications, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. Patients with complications also had a higher prevalence of hypertension and kidney disease, as well as lower preoperative albumin and haemoglobin levels.

Body composition and muscle function differed between groups in a pattern that suggested greater physiological vulnerability among patients with complications. Muscle density was lower, visceral fat area was higher, grip strength was lower, and walking speed was slower among patients with short-term complications. In contrast, skeletal muscle index did not differ significantly between groups. Low grip strength and low walking speed were more frequent among patients with complications, whereas low skeletal muscle index and myosteatosis were not significantly different.

Tumour and surgical characteristics also varied by complication status. Patients with complications had larger tumours and were more likely to have cardia tumours. They also more frequently underwent total gastrectomy and had longer operative duration. Although TNM stage distribution was not statistically different, patients with complications tended to have a lower proportion of stage I disease and a higher proportion of stage III disease. Postoperative outcomes were substantially worse among patients with short-term complications, including higher rates of 30-day complications, readmission, death during follow-up, recurrence, longer postoperative hospital stay, higher hospitalisation costs, and shorter overall and disease-free survival.

Each 1-SD increase in visceral fat area was associated with higher odds of short-term postoperative complications (OR 1.55, 95% CI 1.14–2.10; P = 0.006) and 30-day postoperative complications (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.23–2.36; P = 0.001).

This association remained stable across sequential models. For short-term complications, the OR for visceral fat area was 1.38 in the crude model, 1.35 after adjustment for age and sex, 1.55 in Model 2, and 1.49 after further adjustment for surgical factors in Model 3. For 30-day complications, the corresponding ORs were 1.62, 1.57, 1.70, and 1.65, respectively. Visceral obesity as a categorical variable was significantly associated with 30-day postoperative complications in both Model 2 and Model 3, although its association with short-term complications was weaker.

In contrast, skeletal muscle index, low skeletal muscle index, muscle density, myosteatosis, grip strength, low grip strength, walking speed, and low walking speed were not independently associated with short-term or 30-day postoperative complications after multivariable adjustment. Several muscle-related variables showed significant associations in crude analyses, particularly muscle density, grip strength, low grip strength, and walking speed, but these associations were attenuated after adjustment for age, clinical status, tumour characteristics, and surgical factors. This pattern suggests that muscle-related measures may partly reflect age, tumour burden, or general clinical condition rather than independently driving postoperative risk.

Available-case analyses in Supplementary Table 3 yielded broadly similar findings. Visceral fat area remained associated with short-term postoperative complications in the clinical and surgical adjustment models, and muscle-related associations were generally attenuated after multivariable adjustment. To further assess the potential influence of complete-case selection, inverse probability weighting analyses were performed. Stabilised weights were tightly distributed around 1.0, with a mean of 0.9965, standard deviation of 0.0351, minimum of 0.9533, and maximum of 1.1312. The 1st and 99th percentiles were 0.9537 and 1.1278, respectively, indicating no evidence of extreme weights. The effective sample size was 361.6, which was nearly identical to the observed complete-case sample size of 362, suggesting minimal loss of precision after weighting (Supplementary Table 4).

To evaluate potential temporal confounding related to changes in surgical practice over the study period, additional sensitivity analyses were performed with further adjustment for calendar year and surgical period (Supplementary Table 5). The associations between visceral fat area and postoperative complications remained materially unchanged. For short-term postoperative complications, the odds ratio for visceral fat area was 1.49 (95% CI 1.09–2.04) in the original Model 3, 1.49 (95% CI 1.09–2.04) after adjustment for calendar year, and 1.40 (95% CI 1.02–1.92) after adjustment for surgical period. Similarly, for 30-day postoperative complications, the corresponding odds ratios were 1.65 (95% CI 1.18–2.31), 1.65 (95% CI 1.18–2.31), and 1.61 (95% CI 1.15–2.26), respectively.

Sensitivity analyses using alternative skeletal muscle index cut-offs and exclusion-based approaches in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7 further supported the robustness of the primary finding that visceral adiposity, rather than skeletal muscle mass, showed the most stable association with postoperative complications.

Associations with specific types of postoperative complications

Associations with specific postoperative complication subtypes are shown in Supplementary Table 8. To reduce sparse-event bias, only complication categories with at least 20 events were included in the regression analyses. Gastrointestinal, respiratory, and infectious complications met this threshold, with 26, 30, and 33 events, respectively.

Among these specific outcomes, visceral fat area showed the clearest association with gastrointestinal complications. Each 1-SD increase in visceral fat area was associated with nearly twofold higher odds of gastrointestinal complications (OR 1.95, 95% CI 1.19–3.19; P = 0.008). In contrast, the association between visceral fat area and respiratory complications was not evident. These exploratory subtype analyses suggest that gastrointestinal complications may partly drive the overall association between visceral adiposity and postoperative morbidity, although the results should be interpreted cautiously because of multiple comparisons and limited event numbers for specific complication categories.

Dose-response relationship between visceral fat area and postoperative complications

Restricted cubic spline model selection is presented in Supplementary Table 9. Models with 3, 4, and 5 knots were compared using AIC, BIC, overall association P values, and nonlinearity P values. Although the numerically optimal specification varied slightly by outcome and adjustment model, 4 knots were selected for graphical consistency unless model fit strongly favoured another specification. Across outcomes, the overall association between visceral fat area and postoperative complications was statistically significant, whereas the nonlinearity tests were not significant or only borderline, supporting an approximately linear risk gradient rather than a clear threshold effect.

Based on this specification, restricted cubic spline curves showed a graded increase in the odds of both short-term and 30-day postoperative complications with increasing visceral fat area (Fig. 3). The risk rose progressively across the observed distribution of visceral fat area, with wider confidence intervals at the extremes. Similar patterns were observed after additional adjustment for surgical factors in Model 3, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Together, these results support a continuous dose-response association between visceral adiposity and postoperative complication risk.

Trend, interaction, and incremental predictive value of visceral fat area

Trend and incremental predictive analyses are summarised in Supplementary Table 10. When visceral fat area was categorised into tertiles, complication rates were highest in the upper tertile. Short-term complication rates were 28.9%, 21.7%, and 41.3% across increasing tertiles of visceral fat area, and 30-day complication rates were 17.4%, 16.7%, and 36.4%, respectively. A similar pattern was observed in quartile-based analyses, with the highest event rates generally seen in the highest visceral fat area category.

The addition of visceral fat area to the clinical Model 2 improved model discrimination, particularly for 30-day postoperative complications (Supplementary Tables 11 and Fig. 4). For short-term complications, the AUC increased from 0.695 to 0.715 after adding visceral fat area, although this improvement did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.139). For 30-day complications, the AUC increased from 0.697 to 0.730, with a statistically significant improvement by DeLong’s test (P = 0.041). Consistent with these findings, the extended model showed lower AIC and BIC values, indicating improved model fit.

To assess potential optimism in model discrimination, bootstrap internal validation was performed using 1000 resamples in the unified complete-case cohort (Supplementary Table 12). For short-term postoperative complications, the optimism-corrected AUC was 0.659 for the clinical Model 2 and 0.677 after adding visceral fat area. For 30-day postoperative complications, the corresponding optimism-corrected AUCs were 0.654 and 0.687, respectively.

Decision curve analysis further demonstrated modest improvements in clinical utility after incorporating visceral fat area, with higher net benefit observed across selected intermediate threshold probabilities, particularly for 30-day postoperative complications (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 13). Calibration analyses showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed risks for both outcomes, with no evidence of poor calibration based on Hosmer–Lemeshow tests (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 14). Reclassification analyses showed small improvements in discrimination slope as reflected by positive IDI values, whereas categorical NRI estimates were modest and their confidence intervals crossed zero (Supplementary Table 15).

Overall, these findings indicate that visceral fat area provides modest but consistent incremental predictive information beyond conventional clinical and tumour-related risk factors, with the most robust improvements observed for 30-day postoperative complications.

Discussion

In this study of patients undergoing gastrectomy, we provide evidence that perioperative risk may be more strongly driven by metabolic–inflammatory burden than by structural or functional reserve. We found that visceral adiposity showed a consistent and independent association with postoperative complications across multiple analytical approaches, whereas muscle-related parameters did not retain independent effects after multivariable adjustment. This pattern was observed for both short-term and 30-day complications and was further supported by dose–response analyses demonstrating a graded increase in risk across levels of visceral fat. In contrast, skeletal muscle index, muscle density, grip strength, and walking speed were associated with outcomes in unadjusted analyses but were attenuated after accounting for clinical and tumour-related factors. Taken together, these findings indicate that different components of body composition contribute unequally to perioperative risk and likely reflect distinct physiological domains rather than interchangeable indicators.

Our findings help to reconcile the inconsistent role of sarcopenia reported in the literature. Previous studies evaluating skeletal muscle mass, muscle quality, and sarcopenia in patients undergoing gastrectomy have reported heterogeneous findings, with some studies identifying significant associations with postoperative morbidity whereas others have observed substantial attenuation after adjustment for demographic, nutritional, and clinical factors [23–25]. Previous studies have frequently identified associations between low muscle mass or function and adverse outcomes after cancer surgery, yet these associations are often attenuated after adjustment for age, comorbidities, nutritional status, and disease severity [23]. Importantly, such inconsistencies may also arise from methodological limitations, including reliance on dichotomised definitions of sarcopenia, failure to simultaneously incorporate multiple dimensions of body composition, and the lack of standardised approaches for comparing heterogeneous measures. This attenuation has not always been explicitly addressed. In our analysis, the loss of independent associations for both skeletal muscle index and functional measures after adjustment suggests that muscle-related parameters may primarily capture underlying frailty and general physiological reserve, rather than acting as direct determinants of short-term postoperative complications.

By analysing muscle mass, muscle quality, physical function, and adiposity concurrently using standardised continuous measures within a unified framework, our study provides a more direct comparison of their relative contributions. This interpretation does not contradict prior findings but instead provides a framework for understanding why sarcopenia appears inconsistently associated with early postoperative outcomes across studies, and supports the view that muscle-related measures may function predominantly as proxies of broader patient vulnerability rather than independent causal drivers of early complications [24, 25].

In contrast, visceral adiposity remained consistently associated with postoperative complications across multivariable models [26, 27]. This finding is broadly consistent with previous studies showing that excess visceral fat is associated with increased postoperative morbidity, infectious complications, anastomotic leakage, and prolonged recovery after gastrectomy [28–31]. However, most earlier studies evaluated visceral adiposity in isolation or compared it only with conventional anthropometric measures such as body mass index. Direct comparisons between visceral adiposity and multiple muscle-related domains, including skeletal muscle mass, muscle quality, and physical function, have been limited. By incorporating these components within the same analytical framework, our study demonstrates that visceral adiposity retains its association even after accounting for multiple dimensions of body composition and clinical status. This suggests that visceral adiposity captures a physiological domain that is not fully reflected by muscle-related measures and may represent a more stable correlate of short-term postoperative risk. Biologically, visceral adiposity is metabolically active and contributes to chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and immune dysregulation, which may amplify the inflammatory and metabolic stress responses to surgery and impair tissue repair [32, 33]. Notably, in our analysis, visceral adiposity showed a clearer association with gastrointestinal complications than with other complication types, which may reflect its specific role in intra-abdominal inflammation, anastomotic healing, and local tissue perfusion. In addition, increased intra-abdominal fat may increase operative complexity and technical difficulty by impairing surgical exposure, complicating lymphadenectomy, and prolonging operative time [15, 34]. Although these mechanisms were not directly measured, the consistency of associations across multiple models, sensitivity analyses, and dose–response analyses strengthens the biological plausibility of these pathways.

Taken together, these findings support a domain-based interpretation of body composition, in which perioperative risk is shaped by the interplay between metabolic and inflammatory burden and structural or functional reserve. Within this framework, visceral adiposity and muscle-related parameters should be viewed as complementary rather than competing indicators. This interpretation is reinforced by the combined phenotype analysis, in which the highest complication rates were observed among patients with both visceral obesity and reduced grip strength. The absence of independent effects for muscle-related measures after adjustment suggests that functional impairment may increase vulnerability in the presence of metabolic stress, rather than acting as a primary determinant on its own. The distinction between these domains also helps explain outcome-specific associations. In our study, visceral adiposity was associated with short-term postoperative complications but not overall survival after adjustment. This may reflect the fact that early postoperative outcomes are strongly influenced by perioperative stress, inflammation, and host recovery capacity, whereas long-term survival after radical gastrectomy is more strongly determined by tumour burden, pathological stage, radicality of resection, and receipt of oncological treatment [28, 35, 36]. Muscle-related parameters, although not independently associated with short-term complications in our analysis, may therefore remain relevant for longer-term recovery and survival.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that an exclusive focus on sarcopenia may provide an incomplete assessment of perioperative risk. At the same time, the role of visceral adiposity should be interpreted with caution. Although the inclusion of visceral fat area provided additional information beyond conventional clinical variables, improvements in model discrimination were modest. This indicates that visceral adiposity should not be considered a stand-alone predictive tool, but rather a marker that captures aspects of metabolic and inflammatory risk not reflected in traditional assessments [30, 37, 38]. Importantly, because abdominal CT imaging is routinely performed as part of preoperative staging, visceral adiposity can be readily derived without additional testing. Our findings therefore suggest that visceral adiposity may provide modest incremental risk stratification information beyond conventional clinical factors. However, further external validation is required before routine incorporation into prediction models or clinical decision-making. Future studies are needed to determine whether the identification of patients with high visceral adiposity can inform targeted perioperative optimisation strategies and ultimately improve postoperative outcomes [31, 39] within a unified analytical framework. By simultaneously incorporating CT-derived skeletal muscle mass, muscle quality, functional measures, and visceral adiposity, this study enables direct comparison of their independent associations with postoperative complications. This approach addresses a major limitation of prior studies that have typically examined these components in isolation or relied on dichotomised phenotypes. Furthermore, the use of standardised continuous variables allows direct comparison of effect sizes across heterogeneous measures, providing a more nuanced understanding of their relative contributions. In addition, the use of standardised continuous variables, hierarchical adjustment strategies, and complementary analyses including dose–response modelling and sensitivity analyses enhances the robustness and interpretability of the findings. The consistency of the observed associations across different modelling approaches further supports the internal validity of the results.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, the retrospective single-centre design limits causal inference and may affect generalisability. Second, although bootstrap-based internal validation was performed, the absence of external validation restricts the ability to assess the robustness of these findings across different populations and clinical settings. Third, although inverse probability weighting was applied to mitigate potential bias, the use of a complete-case cohort introduces the possibility of residual selection bias related to missing data. Fourth, body composition measurements were derived from routine preoperative CT examinations. Although all scans were obtained according to institutional imaging protocols, detailed acquisition parameters such as tube voltage and reconstruction kernel were not available retrospectively, and minor variations in imaging procedures over the study period may have influenced attenuation-based measurements. Fifth, information regarding neoadjuvant chemotherapy and adherence to Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols was unavailable and therefore could not be incorporated into the analyses. Although additional adjustment for calendar year and surgical period yielded materially unchanged results, residual confounding related to temporal changes in perioperative management cannot be completely excluded. Finally, mechanistic data, including inflammatory biomarkers and direct measures of operative complexity, were not available, limiting the ability to directly evaluate the biological and technical pathways underlying the observed associations. However, the robustness of the findings across multiple analytical approaches and the presence of a consistent dose–response relationship provide indirect support for the validity of the observed associations. In addition, body composition was assessed at a single preoperative time point, and longitudinal changes in muscle and adipose compartments were not captured.

Future research should build on these findings in several directions. First, prospective multicentre studies with external validation are needed to confirm the generalisability of the observed associations. Second, integrating mechanistic measurements, including inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers, may help elucidate the biological pathways linking visceral adiposity to postoperative complications. Third, the incorporation of detailed intraoperative metrics, such as operative difficulty scores or objective measures of surgical complexity, could clarify the technical contribution of visceral fat to perioperative risk. Fourth, longitudinal assessments of body composition may provide insight into dynamic changes before and after surgery and their relationship with outcomes. In addition, interventional studies are warranted to determine whether visceral adiposity represents a modifiable risk factor and whether targeted strategies, such as metabolic optimisation, weight management, or structured prehabilitation programmes, can translate into improved postoperative outcomes [40]. Furthermore, integrating body composition metrics into multivariable prediction models may enhance personalised perioperative risk stratification [41]. Finally, further studies are needed to determine whether visceral adiposity represents a modifiable risk factor and to evaluate whether targeted perioperative interventions, such as metabolic optimisation or prehabilitation strategies, can improve outcomes in patients with high visceral fat burden.

In summary, visceral adiposity showed a more consistent independent association with postoperative complications after gastrectomy than muscle-related parameters. These findings suggest that metabolic and inflammatory burden may represent an important component of perioperative vulnerability that is not fully captured by measures of structural or functional reserve. Together, the results support a domain-based perspective on body composition and indicate that visceral adiposity may provide modest incremental risk stratification information beyond conventional clinical factors.
