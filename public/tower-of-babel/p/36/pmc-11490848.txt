A rare case of giant mediastinal tumor originating from lung tissue characterized by high fever and arrhythmia
Authors: Xiujing Yao, Ruyue Li, Ying Li, Mengru Liu, Yintao Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArrhythmias in patients with cancer are predominantly attributed to heart disease, anti-tumor drugs, and primary cardiac tumors. Arrhythmias directly induced by noncardiac primary tumors are rare. To date, there have been no reported cases of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia or sinus bradycardia caused by a posterior mediastinal tumor originating from the lung tissue.Case descriptionWe present the case of a 65-year-old male diagnosed with a giant posterior mediastinal tumor originating from the lung tissue, A mediastinal lymph node biopsy was performed, and histopathological examination revealed a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma of pulmonary origin. Based on the PET-CT and cranial enhancend MRI results, the posterior mediastinal tumor has a diameter exceeding 7 cm and has metastasized to the right hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes, with no distant metastasis observed. On the basis of these findings, the patient was diagnosed with stage IIIB mediastinal lung adenocarcinoma (cT4N3M0). The tumor measured 12.8 cm × 8.1 cm and could not be completely resected. Prior to immunochemotherapy, the patient experienced paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, followed by sinus bradycardia. After the first cycle of immunochemotherapy, the arrhythmia was corrected, and the body temperature returned to normal. However, the massive tumor led to malnutrition and various complications prior to the second cycle of treatment. Despite undergoing chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy, the patient's condition worsened, ultimately resulting in death. The overall survival period was 3 months.ConclusionThis case report highlights the fact that a giant mediastinal lung adenocarcinoma can induce arrhythmias, which may manifest as supraventricular tachycardia or sinus bradycardia. Additionally, tumor fever also can induce elevated temperatures of 39 °C. Notably, giant posterior mediastinal tumors that are unsuitable for resection have a significantly poorer prognosis.Keywords: Giant mediastinal lung adenocarcinoma, Fever, Arrhythmia, Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, Sinus bradycardia

Introduction

Cardiac arrhythmia is characterized by an irregular heartbeat rhythm, which can be either tachycardia (>100 beats/min) or bradycardia (<60 beats/min). Arrhythmias in patients with cancer can be attributed to patient characteristics as well as the cancer and anticancer therapies. Patient-related factors such as cardiovascular history, aging, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and genetics are common causes of arrhythmias. Cancer can induce arrhythmias through primary cardiac tumors or, more commonly, through metastatic tumors that invade the heart directly or cause systemic abnormalities, such as autonomic nervous system disorders and inflammation. Additionally, cancer treatments, such as systemic anticancer therapy (chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy), chest radiation therapy, and supportive drugs, such as antiemetics, can lead to arrhythmias [1]. However, mediastinal lung cancers rarely cause arrhythmias directly. Only two cases of mediastinal masses causing atrial fibrillation to have been reported previously [2]. Tumor fever refers to fever that is directly related to the tumor itself, in addition to infectious factors. Most cases of tumor fever are caused by the absorption of necrotic substances from the tumor, and the general symptoms are usually long-lasting and are characterized by low-grade fever [3]. High fever, a manifestation of tumor fever, is relatively uncommon. Herein, we present a case of arrhythmia and high fever caused by a giant mediastinal tumor originating from the lung tissue.

Case report

A 65-year-old man was admitted to the hospital for the first time (Fig. 6), because of intermittent fever with a maximum body temperature of 39 °C (Fig. 1). The patient was admitted with symptoms of loss of appetite, weight loss, and diarrhea. The patient had an unremarkable medical history and was an active smoker with a 40-pack-year history. He had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) Performance Status (PS) of 1 and had smoked for over 30 years. There was no personal or family history of any tumor disorder.

Body temperature map

The red arrow indicates that the body temperature has returned to normal after treatment.

On June 8, 2023, positron emission tomography and computed tomography (PET-CT) revealed a soft tissue mass in the posterior mediastinum with uneven density, measuring 11.1 cm × 8.1 cm in the maximum cross-section. The mass showed increased radioactivity uptake with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 23.6. This caused compression and displacement of the adjacent esophagus, and the boundary between the lesion and the adjacent left atrium was unclear. Enlarged lymph nodes with diameters of approximately 3.0 cm were observed in the 8R area of the right hilum and mediastinum (Fig. 2). On June 26, a computed tomography (CT) scan showed heterogeneous soft tissue density in the right lower hilum and adjacent posterior mediastinum, with a cross-section measuring approximately 12.8 cm × 8.1 cm (Fig. 3). Brain and bone scans revealed no abnormalities.

Heterogeneous soft tissue shadows are observed in the right lower lung hilum and adjacent posterior mediastinum. The larger of these shadows measures approximately 12.8 cm × 8.1 cm in its cross-section. The presence of tumors in this area is indicated by red arrows. CT, computed tomograph.

A mediastinal lymph node biopsy was performed, and histopathological examination revealed a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma of pulmonary origin. Immunohistochemical analysis staining was positive for CKpan, CK8/18, CK7, ENA, and SMARCA4/Brgl; staining was negative for TTF1, P40, CD30, CD15, CD20, CD3, S-100, HMB45, CD5, CD117, ALKp80, and TDT. The PD-L1 (22C3) tumor proportion score was 40 % with a Ki-67 proliferation index of 70 %. Based on the imageological examination results, the posterior mediastinal tumor has a diameter exceeding 7 cm and has metastasized to the right hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes, with no distant metastasis observed. On the basis of these findings, the patient was diagnosed with stage IIIB mediastinal lung cancer (cT4N3M0).

Laboratory results revealed elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) (189.6 mg/mL) and procalcitonin (PCT) (0.12 ng/mL). Routine blood tests indicated mild anemia, with a hemoglobin level of 94 g/L and a white blood cell (WBC) count of 26.45 × 1012/L. Before admission, the patient's electrocardiography (ECG) showed an atrial sinus rhythm. However, during hospitalization, the patient experienced paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (Fig. 4A), which was successfully treated using the Valsalva maneuver to stimulate the vagus nerve and restore the sinus rhythm. Subsequently, the patient developed sinus bradycardia (Fig. 4B).

Electrocardiography findings. A: The pink arrow indicates the presence of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT), a rapid rhythm originating from above the heart ventricles. B: Red arrows indicate sinus bradycardia, which is a slower-than-normal heart rate originating from the sinus node, a natural pacemaker.

According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, the patient's tumor was unresectable due to its location in the posterior mediastinum and diameter of 12.8 cm. Therefore, the patient received immunochemotherapy with the following specific regimen: sintilimab 200 mg on day 1, pemetrexed 800 mg on day 1, and carboplatin 400 mg on day 2. At follow-up after completing the first cycle of treatment, the patient's body temperature had returned to normal, with no recurrence of arrhythmia.

During the second admission (Fig. 7), the patient presented with a mild fever, with a maximum body temperature of 38 °C. Bacterial cultures and NGS Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) microbial sequencing yielded negative results. Laboratory tests showed elevated levels of PCT (0.34 ng/mL) and CRP (192.7 mg/mL), as well as increased levels of serum neuron-specific enolase (NSE) (23.20 ng/mL) and squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCCA) (85.4 ng/mL). Routine blood tests indicated a white blood cell (WBC) count of 40.92 × 109/L, with a predominance of neutrophils (37.59 × 109/L), and moderate anemia, with a hemoglobin level of 70 g/L. The patient also exhibited hypokalemia with a potassium level of 2.22 mmol/L.

Case timeline of the second admission.

On July 17, 2023, CT revealed heterogeneous nodules with soft tissue density and mass shadows in the right lower lung hilum and adjacent posterior mediastinum, with a larger cross-sectional area measuring approximately 13.9 cm × 10.1 cm (Fig. 5). In the second treatment cycle, chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy was administered with a reduced dose of pemetrexed (800 mg) and sintilimab (200 mg). Following discharge, the patient was followed up, but unfortunately, he died of the disease after a total overall survival of 3 months.

Discussion

The clinical manifestations of mediastinal tumors are diverse, with a cough being the primary symptom. However, atypical manifestations can occur due to the compression of adjacent tissues and organs by the tumor. In a previous report, a teratoma located in the upper and middle mediastinum caused atrial fibrillation. Surgical resection of the tumor in that case resolved atrial fibrillation [2]. In the present case, the giant mediastinal lung tumor was in the posterior mediastinum, adjacent to the esophagus and left atrium, with a maximum cross-section of 11.1 cm × 8.1 cm. This caused paroxysmal tachycardia followed by sinus bradycardia.

Tumor fever rarely manifests as high fever; however, a few previous studies have reported high fever caused by tumors [4,5]. In this case, the patient developed a rare high fever due to the extensive necrotic area in a giant mediastinal tumor originating from the lung tissue. Infection was ruled out as the cause of fever based on the negative blood bacterial culture and second-generation microbial sequencing results. Recognizing the atypical symptoms of mediastinal lung cancer is important to avoid missed or misdiagnosis.

The patient in this case was treated according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines; however, there was no improvement in survival time, with an overall survival of only 3 months. Several factors may have contributed to this outcome. First, the patient experienced prolonged high fever, and even after controlling the fever, an intermittent low-grade fever persisted, significantly affecting the patient's physical condition and reducing survival time. Additionally, imaging before and after treatment revealed that anti-tumor treatment was ineffective in curbing tumor growth. Within a month and a half, the tumor had increased in size by 2.8 cm. The rapid growth of the tumor led to malnutrition, as daily energy consumption exceeded intake. Patients with tumors have a high metabolic rate with increased conversion cycles of sugar and protein, hyperlipidemia, and gluconeogenesis [6]. In the case of this giant mediastinal lung cancer, the metabolic rate was even higher owing to rapid tumor growth. In addition, prolonged fever contributed to gastrointestinal dysfunction, delayed gastric emptying, and inadequate nutritional intake, leading to malnutrition. Malnutrition can have adverse clinical outcomes in patients with malignant tumors receiving palliative treatment, resulting in poor prognosis, increased incidence of adverse reactions, prolonged hospital stays, and even death [[7], [8], [9]].Pathological analysis demonstrated a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma and a high Ki-67 proliferation index of 70 %, establishing a profoundly malignant tumor with rapid cellular proliferation and an unfavorable prognosis.

Finally, multiple complications caused by the tumor, including hypoproteinemia, severe anemia, and hypokalemia, were not effectively managed with supportive symptomatic treatments. This further contributed to adverse clinical outcomes.

In summary, the patient's tumor diameter exceeds 10 cm, and the substantial tumor burden from an unresectable tumor leads to a poor prognosis. Therefore, early detection, diagnosis, and treatment of tumors are critical. Primary care physician plays a vital role in this process, and it is worth considering how to employ simple, efficient, and cost-effective methods for tumor screening. When primary care physician observes a patient with high fever and cardiac arrhythmias, they should consider not only primary cardiac causes but also the possibility of tumor-induced symptoms. Moreover, in today's context of persistently high tumor incidence rates, public health experts need to develop corresponding public health strategies to reduce tumor incidence. Currently, screening methods primarily relying on tumor markers can yield false-positive results. However, screening using imaging techniques such as CT involves cumbersome processes of examining each body part individually, resulting in low screening rates. There is a lack of specific markers in clinical practice for the early detection of tumors. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is emerging as a promising tool. Recent studies have utilized ctDNA levels for monitoring post-surgical tumor recurrence, demonstrating extremely high sensitivity [10]. Elevated ctDNA levels can indicate tumor recurrence earlier than imaging techniques. Nonetheless, whether ctDNA can be used for monitoring in healthy populations requires further research.

Conclusion

This case highlights a detailed description of an unresectable stage IIIB giant mediastinal tumor originating from lung tissue. Giant mediastinal tumors can cause cardiac arrhythmias, including tachycardia and sinus bradycardia. Moreover, they can lead to high fever accompanied by a shortened overall survival. Primary care physician should be highly vigilant of the atypical clinical symptoms presented by tumors to avoid missed diagnoses or misdiagnoses. Therefore, it is necessary to research more sensitive and convenient technological methods, as well as public health strategies, to detect tumors early, reduce tumor incidence, and improve the survival time of cancer patients.

Limitation

Firstly, our study sample size is limited, with a small number of samples having tumors larger than 10 cm in diameter. The conclusions drawn from this case discussion lack sufficient clinical samples for validation.
