Immediate and deferred changes in social competences of physiotherapy students following the participation in interpersonal skills workshops and communication
Authors: Joanna Femiak, Monika Guszkowska, Marcin Czechowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe work of physiotherapists underscores the significance of social competencies that are indispensable for the effective implementation of therapy. The participation of healthcare professional students, including physiotherapists in social skills workshops has been demonstrated to result in a notable enhancement of these skills measured immediately after the class. This was confirmed by the research presented. The aim of the present study was to evaluate the immediate and deferred effects of workshop activities designed to develop the social competences and emotional intelligence of physiotherapy students.MethodsThe study was conducted using a quasi-experimental model with a pre-test and two post-tests in one experimental group with an intervention targeting the development of social competences. The experimental factor was interpersonal skills and communication workshop based on active methods (10.5 h). Scores on the Social Competence Questionnaire (KKS) and the Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire (INTE) with a Polish adaptation were used as indices of social skills. Observations from eighty-nine Józef Piłsudski University of Physical Education in Warsaw physiotherapy students including 50 women were analysed; age of participants (follow-up test): 19–32 years, x̄ =21.56, Mdn = 21.ResultA significant increase in the students’ indices of social competences and emotional intelligence was observed immediately after the workshop and a decrease to baseline values was found after a period of nine months. No gender effect was found. The strongest predictor of competence growth in the third measurement was found to be their high level in the pre-test. In contrast, a negative predictor of competence change was an increase in competences immediately after the workshop.ConclusionInterpersonal skills workshops are likely to contribute to the increase in social competences and emotional intelligence of physiotherapy students. Nevertheless, the impact of the workshops is short-term, with participants’ levels of social competences and emotional intelligence returning to their baseline over time. It seems necessary to incorporate additional courses in the university curriculum to sustain these skills or to integrate such effects into existing courses.

Background

Physiotherapists have two main roles: medical, in which they help the patient achieve optimal fitness and prevent relapse, and educational, which involves shaping the patient’s knowledge and skills to avoid relapse and to achieve optimal fitness. Both roles require social competence, including sophisticated client communication skills [1]. Here, we view social competences broadly, as complex skills determining the effectiveness of coping with different social situations, acquired by the individual during social training [2]. In the approach adopted in this study, they consist of competences determining the effectiveness of behaviour in intimate situations, social exposure and situations requiring assertiveness, emotional intelligence understood as a person’s ability to keep track of their own and other people’s feelings and emotions, to differentiate between them and use the information they provide in guiding thought and action, and communication skills in social situations.

The spontaneous development of social competences during the accumulation of clinical experience may be impeded if, at the beginning of this stage of education, the student lacks the requisite skills to establish contact with the patient and shape the therapeutic relationship. According to many authors, the development of social competences should be included in educational programs and should take place especially at the stage of preparation for clinical classes [3–7]. Subsequently, during clinical activities, a student equipped with social competences can focus primarily on the medical aspects of the relationship with the patient [3–7]. Also, the training of the competences that comprise emotional intelligence should, according to some authors, be scaffolded within the curricula of courses preparing students for healthcare professions [8, 9].

The activities in which social competences can be acquired differ from the conventional ones, as they take the form of workshops or training. This form results from the use of active teaching methods. Active methods primarily take into account learning rather than teaching processes, engage the student, and take into account their needs. While there is no single optimal method of education, the recent developments in didactics indicate a clear shift towards active methods, and a tendency to focus the educational process on the student rather than the teacher [10–12]. This turn is also observed in the context of teaching physiotherapists and other health professionals [13–15]. It is also related to the change in students’ expectations from teachers, who are nowadays required to teach critical thinking, creative attitudes towards knowledge, integration of knowledge with practice, responsibility, and an interdisciplinary approach to problems [15, 16].

Workshops or social skills training can take many forms of educationally structured activities. They can be conducted using both online and offline methods, but they are always aimed at developing communication skills [9, 17].

Research confirms that active methods foster the development of high levels of confidence, communicative competence, self-confidence, and creativity in school and university students [9, 11, 16, 18]. Furthermore, they develop their self-awareness, self-reflection, and self-observation skills [5, 19–21].

Previous studies have evaluated the effectiveness of social skills workshops integrated into students’ education mainly in the short term, measuring social competences immediately before and after their completion. This helps assess their direct impact [9, 19]. Deliberate, planned, and effective stimulation of the process of developing the social competences of future physiotherapists requires looking at the whole five-year educational pathway rather than at a specific course of study or even one year. The development of physiotherapists’ social skills requires not just one dedicated class, but a consistent educational pathway followed over the first three years, aimed to prepare them for clinical classes. Consistently delivered subjects can effectively prepare students to undertake therapeutic tasks and allow them to focus on the medical aspect of the relationships with patients during clinical sessions [5, 7, 9, 22]. Participation in the interpersonal skills workshops planned in the first year of study should initiate the process, both guided and spontaneous, of their development in subsequent years. In order to fill the gap, the aim of the study was to determine the short- and long-term effects of interpersonal skills and communication workshops conducted using active methods with second-year physiotherapy students and how these differed by gender. Previous studies have shown no gender differences in social competences between physiotherapy students and physiotherapists [23, 24]. However, in studies of younger youth, it was established that the impact of workshops improving social skills is greater for girls than for boys [25]. No such differences were found among resident physicians [26] or undergraduates [27]. For this reason, the gender was included as a potential factor differentiating the effects of workshops. The aim was also to determine whether the long-term effects of the workshops depended more on the initial level of emotional intelligence and social competences or on the extent to which they changed immediately after the workshops. The baseline level of emotional intelligence should be considered, as physiotherapy students in Poland demonstrate significantly higher scores compared to the population average [28]. Moreover, studies on educational interventions employing active learning methods confirm their effectiveness and sustainability in medical professions [29, 30].

Method

Design

The study was conducted using a quasi-experimental model with pre-tests and post-tests in one experimental group with an intervention targeting the development of social competences.

Intervention

The intervention took the form of interpersonal skills and communication workshops. The social competence workshop program was developed in consultation with Polish Psychological Association. The first implementation of the program took place in 2015 for physical education students [19, 31]. The program has been adapted for physiotherapy students by the authors. The program employs active learning through simulated patient encounters, reflecting typical physiotherapy practice. These simulations were based on real-life scenarios provided by students from their clinical internships. Key situations analyzed during the workshops included: patients requesting non-recommended interventions, violations of professional boundaries, lack of exercise motivation, and non-attendance. These active simulations were conducted over six sessions. In the initial session, students categorized their experiences into six workshop topics and planned their implementation. These personal examples encouraged active role-playing and the adoption of new communication tools.

The workshop was conducted by a licensed interpersonal skills trainer of the Polish Psychological Association as part of the second-year physiotherapy didactics course. It took place for seven consecutive weeks at a rate of 1.5 h per week. They covered the following topics:

Research tools

Scores on the Social Competence Questionnaire (KKS) Matczak (2007) and the Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire (INTE) by N.S. Schutte et al. with a Polish adaptation by Jaworowska and Matczak (2008) were used as indices of social competences. The KKS questionnaire is used to assess social competences, understood as acquired skills that determine a person’s effectiveness in various social situations. In addition to the general (summative) index of social competences (SC-GI), the questionnaire also provides three specific indices of the level of competences in situations: of social exposure meaning being the object of attention and potential evaluation from many people (SC-SE);of an intimate nature - close interpersonal contacts and those related to self-disclosure (SC-IS);requiring assertiveness i.e. achieving one’s own goals or satisfying one’s needs by exerting influence or resisting the influence of others (SC-A) [32].

The questionnaire consists of 60 diagnostic items describing behaviors that are indicators of social competence and 30 buffer items. The answers are scored on a scale from 1 (definitely bad) to 4 (definitely good). The result was determined by dividing the number of points obtained on a given scale by the number of items, so it can range from 1 to 4 points.

The INTE questionnaire is used to measure emotional intelligence, understood as the ability to recognize, understand, and also control one’s own and others’ emotions, as well as the ability to use emotions effectively in directing one’s own and others’ actions. It consists of 33 items, the answers are scored on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The result was determined by dividing the sum of the points by the number of items, so it can range from 1 to 5 points [33]. Both scales are characterized by satisfactory reliability and validity.

Study participants

The study included physiotherapy students of the Faculty of Rehabilitation at the Józef Piłsudski University of Physical Education in Warsaw. In the first measurement (March 2022), 134 students were surveyed, including 75 women. In the second measurement (April 2022) “after the workshop”, 131 students were surveyed, including 74 women. The post-clinic survey (16.01.2023) was conducted during a lecture and included 101 students including 57 women. Each time, the questionnaires were coded which allowed all observations of a particular student to be linked.

Only cases where fully completed and correctly coded questionnaires were collected across all surveys were eligible for analysis (89 people including 50 women). The mean age of respondents at the final stage was 21.56 years (men: 21.60; women: 21.54). The difference in the number of observations between the first and third surveys is due to students dropping out or being expelled from university due to failure to meet academic requirements and students being absent on the day of the survey - the percentage of incorrectly coded questionnaires in all 3 survey stages was 2.19% (8 cases out of a total of 366 observations).

The third study after the clinics checked the long-term effects of the workshops, because the reports on this topic are contradictory [34–36]. Statistical analysis

A two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (gender x measurement) was used to determine changes in social competences and emotional intelligence and how they differed by gender. Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests were conducted to determine the significance of changes between measurements and differences between men and women for each measurement. Input method of regression analyses was used to identify predictors of change in social competences and emotional intelligence. The pretest results and the differences between the pre-test and the post-test were considered as potential predictors of the level of dependent variables in the third measurement (follow-up) and change indicators (differences between measurements). A level of significance was set at p < 0.05. The effect size was assessed using the partial value of η2. The following classification was adopted: 0.01 - small effect size, 0.06 - medium effect size, 0.14 - large effect size.

With the score on the first measurement (pre-test) and the increase in competences after the workshop (post-test minus pre-test) introduced into the equation as factors, a high competence score on the third measurement is to be expected from students who had a high level of competences on the pre-test and a high increase in competences after the workshop (positive predictors). The result in the third measurement (follow-up) was more strongly dependent on the result in the first measurement (baseline) than on the change in competences after the workshop. The coefficient of determination R2 obtained the highest value for the aggregate social competence index and the lowest for emotional intelligence. The highest beta values for both pre-test and post-test minus pre-test were observed for assertiveness. The emotional intelligence pre-test showed the lowest beta coefficient. For the post-test minus pre-test difference, the lowest beta was recorded for intimacy.

Discussion

The permanent introduction of interpersonal skills workshops delivered using active methods into the physiotherapist training program requires prior evaluation of their effectiveness. A review of the literature by MacDonald-Wicks and Levett-Jones [37] suggests a paucity of high-quality research on interventions targeting the development of communication competences. We intended to fill this gap with the present study. It took three measurements of social competences and emotional intelligence - in the second year of study, immediately before the interpersonal skills workshop (at the beginning of the first classes), immediately after the workshop (at the end of the last classes, after seven weeks) and nine months later, at the end of the first term of taking clinical classes. A significant main effect of measurement was found for all indices of social competences and emotional intelligence. This means that the level of all the variables studied changed significantly over time.

A main effect of gender was also found. Male physiotherapy students showed significantly higher assertiveness and social competences in exposure situations. No significant differences were found in emotional intelligence and social competences in intimate situations. The results of population-based studies have demonstrated differences in social competences between men and women, also among Polish physiotherapists and physiotherapy students. Men revealed higher levels of competences in situations that required assertiveness and in situations of social exposure [23, 24], while women revealed higher levels of competences in intimate situations [38]. The results of the post-hoc tests suggest that the observed significant gender effect was due to the summation of weak non-significant effects in successive measurements rather than caused by differences in the results of men and women in a particular measurement. The lack of such a clear differentiation is probably due to the similarity of experiences in interpersonal relationships, including interactions with professional teachers, members of medical teams, and patients gained by both women and men during their studies in physiotherapy.

Closer information on the dynamics of changes in social competences and emotional intelligence was provided by an analysis of post-hoc tests conducted separately for each gender. Women were found to have significantly higher levels of all social competences and emotional intelligence in the second measurement taken immediately after the workshop compared to the measurement taken before the workshop. In the male group, there was a significant increase in the levels of emotional intelligence and the aggregate index of social competences. In the case of social competences in intimate situations and social exposure, the difference reached the trend level.

Due to the short time of seven weeks between the first and second measurements, it is difficult to explain the observed changes by the naturally occurring development of social competences. Therefore, the results obtained seem to confirm the expected short-term effects of the workshop classes in terms of an increase in the level of competences studied. They were more pronounced in women, especially in the case of social competences in intimate situations and situations of social exposure.

Results from previous studies have shown that even a one-off workshop activity involving second-year physiotherapy students interacting with simulated patients significantly reduced anxiety and increased confidence in clinical communication [39]. Similarly, a single 45-minute e-learning class had a positive impact on medical students’ communication skills [31/35]. A study of the effect of one session of communication skills training using PowerPoint, video clips, and role-playing found an increase in empathy levels in medical students [40].

Changes induced by participation in longer programs can be expected to be even more pronounced and sustainable. This is consistent, among other things, with the results of a systematic review of studies on the effects of interventions targeting the communication competences of healthcare professionals, including physiotherapists [41]. The most robust evidence comes from two studies that conducted a series of single-case experiments [42, 43]. Both studies evaluated interventions for qualified healthcare professionals in brain injury rehabilitation facilities, which included experience-based training and observations. The training programs were prepared based on previous scientific research on the importance of specific skills. Both studies showed the positive effects of training on participants’ practical behaviour. This allowed the authors to find “fairly strong evidence of the positive effects of interventions targeting specific skills and designed based on clear empirical research”. Similar to those found in our study, short-term effects expressing a significant increase in empathy levels were also observed in sixth-year medical students two weeks after completing training in communication skills in the case of serious diseases. The training lasted five teaching hours and took place over one week during a work placement in an internal medicine department [44].

However, the increase in social competences and emotional intelligence of physiotherapy students observed in our study was limited by time. When surveyed nine months after the workshop, the men were found to have similar levels of social competences as in the pre-test before the workshop. Only the index of emotional intelligence was significantly higher in the third measurement than in the first, suggesting that the changes were persistent. In women, all indices of social competences and emotional intelligence at follow-up were not significantly different from those before the workshop, indicating a return of competences to baseline levels.

The results of previous studies showing the sustainability of the effects of social skills workshops are not consistent. Empathy levels in medical students returned to baseline after just three weeks following the 45-minute workshop [40]. In contrast, the effects of the 5-hour empathy training were maintained in the follow-up three to six months after its completion [34].

Interesting information is also provided by the results of the regression analysis, which indicated that emotional intelligence and social competences at follow-up (after clinical classes) can be better predicted by their baseline level than by the changes after the workshop. However, both factors are significant predictors. This suggests that natural predispositions, dependent on personality factors and previous experiences, play a more important role in determining levels of social competences and emotional intelligence after initial clinical placements.

Only the changes in competences after the workshop allow for predicting the difference between the first and third measurements but they explain it to a much lesser extent. The same is true for the difference between the second and third measurements. It can be expected that if competences increase substantially after a workshop, they will decrease the most afterwards. This means that the effects of the workshops are limited in time and activities to enhance the competences are needed to prevent or at least limit their decline.

The decline in social competences and emotional intelligence observed after the first clinical experience, especially in the female group, suggests that starting clinical practice may have a negative effect on them. Findings to date on the impact of clinical practice on the development of interpersonal skills and the improvement of students’ emotional intelligence are not consistent. Previous studies have shown that physiotherapy students may become less empathetic during training [45], and also indicate a decline in emotional intelligence or assertiveness during clinical placements [7]. In contrast, other data show an increase in emotional attention, emotion regulation, optimism, and self-confidence in occupational therapy, speech therapy, and physiotherapy students [34, 46]). The results of a study by Pades Jimenez et al. [47], who compared the emotional intelligence and assertiveness of students over four consecutive years of a physiotherapy course, led to similar conclusions. The level of assertiveness in fourth-year students was found to be significantly higher than in all junior students and correlates with the number of clinical classes. Similar differences and correlations were not observed for emotional intelligence. However, it should be clearly emphasized that the study was cross-sectional and not longitudinal, so changes were only inferred from the results of intergroup comparisons.

A decrease in emotional intelligence over 16 months of full-time placement was also found by Gribble et al. [34] in occupational therapy students. The authors estimated that emotional expression, assertiveness, self-expression, and stress tolerance deteriorated in a worryingly high proportion of students. They attribute the reasons for the drop in results to the initially high levels of subjective confidence, assertiveness, and coping with stress in students, who, however, quickly realize that working in healthcare is a difficult place to be assertive. Another reason for the decline in emotional intelligence during clinical placements may be the poor quality of support from clinical teachers [35, 36] It was indicated that clinical supervisors who show poor communication skills, lack of commitment, and high levels of control can negatively affect student competences. For example, an intimidating or uninvolved supervisor can reduce a student’s assertiveness and self-expression [35, 36].

In publications, Gribble proposed the inclusion of communication classes in the curriculum of future physiotherapists, which may result in an increase in the emotional intelligence of students, prior to the start of their first full-time placements and, more importantly, when they begin their professional work after graduation [48–50]. Furthermore, Stoller et al. [6] suggested that the development of emotional intelligence should not be implemented as a stand-alone module in the education curriculum but should be integrated into various subjects at subsequent stages of education.

Our study does not support the hypothesis proposed by Gribble et al. [34, 35] that participation in dedicated classes would maintain increased levels of communication competences during clinical classes. We believe that removing the effect of workshops aimed at developing interpersonal skills in the third year of study after the first clinical classes may be related, firstly, to the lack of continuation of education on communication and, secondly, to the regression of educational results in the face of the new and major challenge of such classes.

Further longitudinal studies, conducted throughout the study period and the first stages of the professional career are needed to determine whether and how social competences and emotional intelligence of students are changing. This is because in the development of these competences, not only the special training and experiences acquired in workshops but also learning in the workplace, i.e. maintaining the acquired skills during clinical classes and later, in independent work as a physiotherapist, are important [8]. Work-based learning takes place when an employee or student does real work in a real workplace. Mechanisms that lead to workplace learning include modelling the roles by expert colleagues, mentoring, coaching, and teamwork. Work-based learning has been shown to develop a range of compound skills considered essential in the 21st century, especially problem-solving, creativity, and teamwork [8]. Workshop-type activities, peer coaching, and mentoring programs can be particularly useful in supporting the development of social competences and emotional intelligence of graduates [21, 51].

However, it should be emphasized that the development of interpersonal competences is not a natural consequence of medical students undertaking professional tasks. For example, Larin et al. [52] found no significant changes in the emotional intelligence of nursing and physiotherapy students after their first year-long clinical placements.

It should be emphasized again that clinical teachers have a special role to play here and should be aware of their influence on the communication competences of physiotherapy students. It is worth considering the possibility of preparing clinical teachers to provide ongoing feedback to students not only on their clinical but also communication skills, which are also important. Continued learning of communication competences, even in the form of professionally given feedback, may ultimately result in the maintenance of educational outcomes after the workshop, and thus overall better clinical placement outcomes and better preparation for entering the labour market [34].

Regardless of the form of the classes, the introduction of activating methods focused on learning processes entails changing forms of assessing medical students, including those studying physiotherapy [53]. The forms of assessment of physiotherapy students have been a topic of discussion undertaken by observers of the educational reality of these professionals. Assessment should reflect not only knowledge but also practical skills, including social competences and learning styles [24]. Active methods have been used in the education of future physiotherapists and people preparing for other health professions, despite considerable organizational effort [8, 54]. A review [55] shows that methods such as simulated contact with a standard patient, role-playing with a standard family, experiential learning, collaborative learning, virtual reality simulation, mutual evaluation method, video recording, and standardized and specific patient feedback are used to improve health students’ communication skills. The number of classes dedicated to teaching social competences, their organization within general education, and the methods used are areas for further research and analysis [8].

It should also be noted that further methodologically sound research is needed to determine what specific psychological and communication skills physiotherapy students particularly require [56]. It is not enough to assume that social skills should be developed in the training of future physiotherapists. It is important to identify which of them plays a particular role based on scientific evidence. In the program of Communication, Interaction & Teamwork (KIT), the content was selected by a dedicated group of specialists, whose members kept the program up to date and made any changes. Furthermore, students and instructors had the opportunity to evaluate the KIT at all times, and their evaluations were taken into account as the KIT was continuously updated [57]. The KIT courses were taught by instructors from various fields who had attended compulsory training courses in preparation for teaching KIT. The issue of adequate content preparation of teachers for social skills workshops should be addressed in further research.

In our view, the strength of the present study is its longitudinal nature. By performing three measurements of social competences and emotional intelligence, the short- and long-term effects of the social skills workshops were determined. The workshops lasted long enough to expect lasting changes in social competences and emotional intelligence. However, it should be noted that the recommended duration for this type of workshop is 40 h. An example of a long-term program focused on the development of communication skills is a program implemented at the Charité - Universitätsmedizin in Berlin, with 102 h of classes taught in seven semesters [57]. They are focused on the development of social and communication competences and are conducted in small groups. The course content is closely linked to the topics discussed in each semester and becomes increasingly complex over the course of study. In the fourth and ninth semesters, students took final examinations to test their communication competencies. Semester evaluations of students and instructors indicated that students participating in KIT improved their conversation skills. This communication training was well received by students and instructors and led to the improvement in general and specific communication skills. It can therefore be expected that in our students, more workshop classes delivered over a longer period of time would also result in competence changes more resistant to the negative impact of first clinical experience.

The biggest limitation of the study is the lack of a non-intervention control group. Such a group would allow for control changes in social competences that take place in the process of spontaneous social development, which, after all, also takes place during late adolescence and the first years of early adulthood, as well as the changes that result from students’ educational experiences other than those in workshops. Unfortunately, as the competence workshop was implemented as a compulsory subject, it was not possible to create a control group without intervention. I In the future, we will try to conduct research on people studying physiotherapy at another university, where the program does not include classes in the field of communication. Finding such a university will not be easy. The education of physiotherapists in Poland is carried out according to plans that usually include some form of classes devoted to this subject.

Based on the results obtained by other authors, we know that the change in social competences does not occur spontaneously [52]. The strength of our study is the conduct of three measurements, especially the follow-up, which allows us to determine the durability of the effects of the workshops.

The obligatory nature of the workshop may also be a limitation of the project. At least some students may have regarded them as just another curricular activity that has to be attended. On the other hand, voluntary classes could be a source of a different kind of distortion, as they would probably be attended by students who are interested in communication, with a higher level of social competences, and probably strongly motivated to increase their interpersonal skills [58]. It would be interesting to compare the effects of compulsory and voluntary activities outside the study program for students interested in personal development in this field.
