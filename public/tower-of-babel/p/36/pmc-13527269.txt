Multi‐Contrast Human Brain CEST MRI at 11.7 T: First In Vivo Demonstration
Authors: Camélia Ressam, Joseph Brégeat, Alfredo L. Lopez Kolkovsky, Alexis Amadon, Alexandre Vignaud, Aurélien Massire, Son Chu, Shajan Gunamony, Nicolas Boulant, Vincent Gras, Franck Mauconduit, Luisa Ciobanu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo implement and evaluate chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST) at ultra‐high field on a whole‐body 11.7 T scanner using parallel transmission (pTx).MethodsTailored pTx CEST saturation pulses were optimized to achieve a spatially uniform target B1 + while satisfying SAR and power constraints either defined by conservatively defined radio‐frequency (RF) power limits or by a virtual observation point (VOP)–based framework. The resulting pulse performance at 11.7 T was assessed through numerical simulations and compared against corresponding designs at 7 T. The proposed saturation pulses were integrated into a two‐dimensional multi‐slice EPI sequence. Experimental testing of the sequence was performed in vitro using metabolite‐doped phantoms and in vivo in four healthy volunteers.ResultsThe pTx pulse designs optimized under VOP‐based SAR constraints at 11.7 T achieved comparable performance to 7 T designs despite higher intrinsic B1 + inhomogeneity. In vitro experiments validated the sequence implementation and confirmed detection of the targeted metabolites. In vivo experiments demonstrated acceptable saturation homogeneity (nRMSE ≤ 12.1%) in a slab of thickness 1 cm, with only minimal additional improvement achieved through B1 + post‐processing correction. Across the cohort, robust gray matter–white matter contrast was consistently observed for multiple CEST contrasts.ConclusionVOP‐based SAR‐constrained pTx CEST saturation pulse design enabled robust and reproducible contrast at 11.7 T by effectively mitigating B1 + inhomogeneities while remaining within safety limits, facilitating translation to future in vivo clinical research applications.

Introduction

Chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST) [1] is a noninvasive MRI contrast mechanism that enables the indirect detection of low‐concentration metabolites and mobile proteins by exploiting proton exchange with bulk water. Selective radio‐frequency (RF) saturation of exchangeable solute protons at their resonance frequencies results in the transfer of saturation to bulk water protons, generating CEST contrast through changes in the water signal only. Prolonged saturation amplifies the resulting attenuation of the water signal, thereby enhancing sensitivity to molecular components that are difficult and time‐consuming to image using conventional MR spectroscopic imaging [2] while eliminating the need for exogenous tracers, unlike positron emission tomography. CEST MRI has been used to probe a variety of molecules, such as amide protons in brain tumors [3, 4, 5] or glutamate in the healthy brain at 7 T [6] and is increasingly explored in clinical research in stroke [7], epilepsy [8, 9], and other neurologic or oncologic diseases [10, 11, 12, 13]. The launch in 2024 of the investigational whole‐body 11.7 T Iseult MR scanner [14] in France (NeuroSpin, CEA) offers a unique opportunity to further explore the capabilities of CEST MRI at extreme magnetic fields.

At high magnetic field strengths, both the longitudinal relaxation time of water and the spectral separation between resonances increase [15], enhancing CEST sensitivity and potentially enabling the detection of a broader range of CEST effects. In this context, glutamate‐weighted CEST at 11.7 T is of interest, as its detectability at field strengths below 7 T remains difficult due to its high exchange rate [6, 16]. Hydroxyl‐weighted CEST, with resonances as close as 1 ppm to water, may benefit substantially from the increased spectral separation available at ultra‐high field [17], as previously demonstrated at 7 T [18].

While ultra‐high magnetic field strength provides clear benefits for CEST MRI, it also introduces significant technical challenges. In particular, spatial B0 and B1 + inhomogeneities intensify, resulting in enhanced susceptibility effects, pronounced spatial variation in RF excitation, and increased RF energy deposition. Addressing these limitations has therefore become a major focus of recent methodological developments in ultra‐high field CEST MRI.

The most efficient way to mitigate B1 + inhomogeneities is to use parallel transmission (pTx) [19], which employs multiple independently driven transmit channels. Dynamic RF shimming with pTx has been widely adopted in ultra‐high field MRI, notably for slice‐selective spoke excitations [20] and three‐dimensional excitation schemes [21] to reduce flip‐angle nonuniformity. More recently, pTx has been extended to CEST MRI to improve saturation homogeneity at ultra‐high field. The benefits of pTx for CEST imaging were demonstrated using the MIMOSA [22] approach, which employs two complementary alternating static B1 + shim configurations to mitigate B1 + inhomogeneities. Further improvements were achieved using PUSH [23] saturation pulses, which optimize saturation homogeneity based on the measured B1 + distribution. While subject‐specific pTx pulse optimization remains time‐consuming, universal pulse (UP) [24] implementations of CEST saturation pulses have also been proposed in two‐dimensional acquisitions at 7 T [18], paving the way for simpler, faster, and clinically feasible CEST protocols. The utility of such approaches was further demonstrated for whole‐brain 3D CEST imaging at 7 T [25, 26], using conservative vendor‐defined specific absorption rate (SAR) limits for the pulse design. Delebarre et al. [18] proposed the optimization of CEST saturation pulses using virtual observation points (VOPs) [27], enabling real‐time SAR supervision and more efficient use of pTx capabilities at 7 T, resulting in substantial gains in CEST saturation uniformity of up to 4.7 fold.

The present work reports the first implementation and assessment of CEST MRI on a human 11.7 T scanner, combining custom sequence development, numerical simulations, phantom experiments, and in vivo measurements. Tailored CEST pTx pulses and slice‐specific three‐spoke excitation pulses were designed to achieve optimal saturation under VOP‐based SAR constraints. The performance of the CEST pulses was evaluated through simulations and benchmarked against numerical results at 7 T. Several CEST contrasts were evaluated in realistic head phantoms doped with metabolites and in healthy volunteers. Finally, a B1 + post‐processing correction [28] was evaluated on the acquired in vivo data.

Methods

Experimental Set‐Up

All in vivo experiments were performed on the investigational whole‐body 11.7 T Iseult MR scanner using an in‐house head coil with 8 transmit (Tx) and 31 receive (Rx) channels [29]. In vitro experiments were conducted on the same system, as well as on an investigational whole‐body 7 T MR scanner equipped with a Nova head coil (Nova medical, Wilmington, MA, USA) featuring 8Tx‐32Rx channels.

For the in vitro experiments, a human head‐shaped phantom was fabricated using 3D printed resin [30]. The phantom was initially filled with a phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) solution containing 10 mM L‐glutamic acid, 10 mM creatine, and 20 mM D‐glucose. To approximate the electrical and relaxation properties of human brain tissue, 4 g/L sodium chloride and 0.064 mM gadoterate meglumine (Dotarem) were added. To prevent bacterial degradation, 4 mM sodium azide was also included. The pH was adjusted to 7, and the phantom was heated overnight at 37°C prior to the experiments.

Subsequently, the phantom was emptied and refilled with 3% porcine skin gelatin to enable APT‐weighted CEST contrast, while the quantities of all other components remained unchanged relative to the PBS‐based phantom.

For the in vivo experiments, four healthy volunteers (26 ± 5 years old, two males and two females) were scanned after providing written informed consent, in accordance with a French ethics committee.

CEST MRI Methodology

Tailored pTx Pulse Design and Implementation

Tailored pTx CEST saturation and excitation pulses were designed for each acquisition, for both in vitro and in vivo experiments.

To this end, a B0 field map was acquired using a three‐dimensional gradient‐recalled echo (GRE) sequence, followed by B1 + field maps acquired with an interferometric turbo‐FLASH sequence [31], with 2.5 mm isotropic resolution and 5 × 5 × 2.5 mm3 resolution with a 2.5 mm slice gap, respectively (matrix size 128 × 128 × 72 and 40 × 64 × 40, respectively). B0 shimming was performed at the beginning of each acquisition session using in‐house software based on quadratic programming [32] specifically designed for ultra‐high field acquisitions.

The CEST pulse optimization followed the methodology described by Delebarre et al. [18] and consisted in designing pTx Gaussian saturation pulses composed of two complementary modes (A and B) to achieve effective saturation over a 1 cm axial slab positioned at the scanner isocenter. The two modes were applied in an alternating fashion and repeated N times until the desired total saturation time (T sat) was reached. The optimization minimized the L2 norm between the effective root mean square transmit field, B1,rms +, and a predefined B1 + target, under constraints of peak and average RF power and SAR (local and global). Two configurations were evaluated: (i) protected mode (PM), in which conservative RF power limits are used to indirectly constrain SAR, and (ii) VOP mode, in which RF energy limits and explicit SAR limits are applied. In the VOP mode, the average RF power per channel was limited to 3 W, compared to 1.5 W in PM. The total power limit (sum across channels) in the VOP mode was set to 18 W. In the PM, this limit was 8 W. In the VOP mode only, local SAR constraints were set to 20 W/kg and global SAR was limited to 3.2 W/kg, corresponding to the “Level 1” SAR mode of IEC [33]. All optimizations were performed in MATLAB (MathWorks, version 24.1, R2024a).

For the imaging (excitation) module of the CEST sequence, dynamic RF shimming with three‐spoke excitation [20] was employed for each slice.

The optimized saturation and excitation pulses were subsequently integrated into a two‐dimensional multi‐slice EPI CEST pulse sequence [18], for which the CEST saturation module was played once per repetition time (TR) right before the EPI slice selection.

For comparison purposes, pTx pulses were also optimized for 7 T simulations using the same power limits. The local SAR constraint was, however, set to 10 W/kg while the global SAR constraint remained 3.2 W/kg (“Normal” mode of IEC [33]).

CEST Acquisition Parameters

For both in vitro and in vivo acquisitions, 5 slices (1.6 mm isotropic resolution, 0.3 mm slice gap) were acquired. The imaged field of view was 210 × 210 mm2, with a matrix size of 128 × 128 and GRAPPA [34] acceleration factor 2. The EPI bandwidth was set to 1698 Hz/Px. Two sets of CEST pulses (Gaussians of duration 50 ms) were designed to provide efficient saturation of the target protons while maintaining frequency selectivity: “low‐B1 +” pulses for amide, guanidinium, and hydroxyl protons, and “high‐B1 +” pulses for glutamate protons.

For the low‐B1 + acquisitions, 12 saturation pulses (comprising 6 A‐B pairs) were applied, resulting in an effective saturation time of 600 ms at a target B1,rms + of 1.5 μT. Other imaging parameters were: TR = 2500 ms, echo time (TE) = 16 ms, flip angle (FA) = 73°. The acquisition duration was 2:53 min.

For the high‐B1 + acquisitions, 6 saturation pulses (comprising 3 A‐B pairs) were applied, resulting in an effective saturation time of 300 ms at a target B1,rms + of 2.5 μT. Only for Participant 3 (P3), the B1,rms + target was 3.0 μT and due to exceeded RF coil power limits, the acquisition volume for this contrast was reduced to 4 slices. Due to the higher energy deposition of the high‐B1 + CEST saturation module, the TR was increased to 5000 ms, resulting in an acquisition duration of 4:05 min. Other imaging parameters were: TE = 16 ms, FA = 85°.

To correct for static field inhomogeneities, Water Saturation Shift Referencing (WASSR) [35] scans were acquired throughout the protocol using either a high‐B1 + or a low‐B1 + pulse design, but with the pulse amplitude scaled down to B1,rms + = 0.3 μT. Other imaging parameters were: TR = 1500 ms, TE = 16 ms, FA = 62°. The acquisition duration was 45 s.

To test the performance of the CEST saturation module, a 1.5 μT low‐B1 + pulse design was evaluated on the PBS‐based phantom, with the pulse amplitude reduced to achieve five B1,rms + targets: 1.5, 1.4, 1.2, 1.0, and 0.5 μT. The imaging parameters were the same as for the low‐B1 + acquisitions.

In vivo, the relayed nuclear Overhauser effect (rNOE), arising from aliphatic protons of mobile proteins and macromolecules, was imaged with the low‐B1 + pulse amplitude reduced by 33% to obtain B1,rms + = 1.0 μT. All other imaging parameters matched those for low‐B1 + acquisitions.

For two participants (P2 and P4), an additional low‐B1 + acquisition (B1,rms + = 1.5 μT) with 16 saturation pulses was added to study the effect of prolonged saturation (T sat = 800 ms) on the CEST contrast. Other imaging parameters were: TR = 4500 ms, TE = 16 ms, FA = 73°. The repetition time had to be increased when increasing the saturation time to comply with SAR and system limits. The acquisition duration was 5:11 min.

Data Processing

In vivo CEST acquisitions were motion corrected using FSL flirt [39]. The first volume of each acquisition was aligned across acquisitions, after which all volumes within each session were realigned to the corresponding session's first volume. Both stages employed rigid‐body transformation. From the two scans acquired with opposite phase encoding directions, a susceptibility‐induced off‐resonance field was estimated using FSL Topup [36, 37] and was applied to each CEST volume to correct for EPI distortions ranging up to ±2 voxels over our cohort.

For both in vitro and in vivo acquisitions, voxel‐wise frequency offset maps obtained from WASSR acquisitions were used to correct B0 inhomogeneities by interpolating each CEST Z‐spectrum to the corrected frequency offsets. Repeated in vivo CEST acquisitions were then averaged to improve the signal‐to‐noise ratio and cubic‐interpolated to 0.1 ppm spacing between −5 and 5 ppm.

To generate the different CEST contrast maps, the magnetization transfer ratio asymmetry [40] (MTRasym) at a given frequency offset Δω was computed on a voxel‐by‐voxel basis: (1) MTRasym(Δω)=S(−Δω)−S(Δω)Sref where S is the acquired CEST signal and S ref = S (−40 ppm) is the unsaturated reference signal used for normalization.

For both in vitro and in vivo acquisitions, hydroxyl‐ and glutamate‐weighted contrasts were generated by computing MTRasym at five frequency offsets (0.8:0.1:1.2 ppm for hydroxyl protons and 2.8:0.1:3.2 ppm for glutamate protons) and averaging the resulting MTRasym maps for each contrast. For each volunteer, APT‐weighted, guanidinium‐weighted, and rNOE‐enhanced maps were generated by computing MTRasym at discrete frequency offsets within defined ranges, 3.5 ± 0.1, 2.2 ± 0.2, and −3.5 ± 0.5 ppm, respectively, and averaging the resulting maps.

Additionally, for both APT‐weighted and guanidinium‐weighted contrasts, Lorentzian difference analysis (LDA) [41] was performed to remove magnetization transfer (MT) and direct saturation (DS) effects to better reflect the contribution of each pool. Specifically, DS and MT were fitted using Lorentzian functions, and the resulting spectrum (reference spectrum) was subtracted from the normalized Z‐spectrum. The residual signal was then integrated over the same frequency offsets as those used for MTRasym. The initial conditions and boundaries of the fits are listed in Table S1.

Gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) segmentations were performed on the T1‐weighted images using sMRIprep [42] (version 0.18.0) and co‐registered with the CEST images using custom Matlab scripts. For each subject, median MTRasym and LDA values were computed within GM and WM ROIs. Group results are reported as the mean across subjects.

Results

Simulations

In vitro normalized circularly polarized (CP) B1 + profiles obtained at 7 and 11.7 T are shown in Figures 1A and 1B, respectively. To evaluate the effectiveness of the pTx pulse design in mitigating B1 + inhomogeneities at 11.7 T and to compare it with that at 7 T, the normalized root mean square error (nRMSE) was computed for both the protected and the VOP modes as a function of B1,rms+DC, where DC=Tsat/TR is the duty cycle (Figure 1C). For further comparison, the performance of the CP design under the same energy management strategies is also shown in Figure 1D. The results obtained for 7 T are consistent with previously reported findings [18, 23]. Across all pulse design strategies, 7 T optimization consistently yielded lower nRMSE values than at 11.7 T. This difference can be attributed to the more homogeneous initial CP B1 + profile at 7 T (Figure 1A) compared to that at 11.7 T (Figure 1B), which facilitates convergence toward lower nRMSE values during optimization. At 11.7 T, the use of VOP‐based SAR constraints was essential to mitigate transmit field inhomogeneities, resulting in an average reduction of the pTx pulses nRMSE by 13% compared to the PM constraints across the total B1,rms+DC range tested. For B1,rms+DC values up to 1 μT, 11.7 T pTx pulses designed under VOP‐based SAR constraints achieved similar performance to 7 T pulses, yielding average nRMSE values of 6.3% (vs. 4.6% at 7 T). Given the superior performances of VOP‐SAR‐constrained pulses, in vitro and in vivo acquisitions were only conducted with pulses optimized under these constraints.

Experiments performed on the PBS‐based phantom demonstrated clear spectral separation of glutamate, guanidinium, and hydroxyl protons across five B1 + amplitudes (Figure 2). Saturation homogeneity was evaluated on the gelatin‐based phantom and showed moderate spatial variations, attributable to experimental factors inherent to ultra‐high field phantom imaging (additional details are given in the Supporting Information and Figure S1).

Discussion

This study reports the first in vivo human brain CEST images acquired at 11.7 T, demonstrating the feasibility of the method at this field strength. A tailored interleaved pTx CEST pulse design under VOP‐based SAR constraints was employed to mitigate transmit field inhomogeneities. Following initial testing on metabolite‐doped PBS and gelatin phantoms, multiple contrast weightings were acquired at different saturation amplitudes in four healthy young volunteers, yielding measurable and consistent CEST effects across the cohort despite the increased technical challenges associated with ultra‐high field MRI.

One of the main motivations for performing CEST MRI at high magnetic fields is the increased selectivity and detectability of metabolites [17], driven primarily by the larger chemical shift dispersion between exchanging proton pools (in Hz). This increased dispersion reduces spectral overlap and spillover effects, enabling a finer inspection of metabolite‐related features in the Z‐spectrum. In the present study, the low‐B1 + Z‐spectra show clearly resolved dips corresponding to amide and guanidinium resonances, which are typically barely discernible at 7 T [43]. No additional CEST resonances beyond those established at lower field strengths were identified over the investigated frequency range, though we note that the acquisition parameters were optimized for robust imaging of known metabolites rather than systematic spectral exploration.

The acquired CEST images exhibited clear gray‐white matter differentiation across multiple CEST contrasts corresponding to different saturation offsets. The highest GM–WM contrasts were observed in MTRasym‐based APT‐weighted and rNOE‐enhanced images, with values comparable to previous studies at 7 T [12, 18, 41, 44]. Higher rNOE‐enhanced contrast values have been reported using a relaxation‐compensated metric [5]. MTRasym‐based guanidinium‐weighted CEST imaging at 11.7 T revealed clear tissue differentiation across all participants, with GM–WM contrast exceeding that reported in previous studies [25, 43, 45]. Following LDA, both APT and guanidinium GM–WM contrasts were reduced compared to MTRasym analysis, as expected given the rNOE contamination inherent to asymmetry‐based analyses. Nonetheless, GM signal remained higher than WM signal across all subjects for both pools (APT: GM 1.3%–1.7% vs. WM 1.0%–1.5%; Guanidinium: GM 2.0%–2.3% vs. WM 1.6%–2.1%), consistent with known higher concentrations of cellular proteins and creatine in GM [46, 47, 48, 49, 50]. Glutamate‐weighted GM–WM contrast was moderate but remained satisfactory, particularly at B1,rms + = 3 μT, with values close to those recently reported at 7 T [51] with longer saturation times (800 ms). Human brain imaging of resonant metabolites around 1 ppm has been reported in only a few studies, and while hydroxyl‐weighted images showed the weakest contrast at 11.7 T, the observed values still surpassed those previously reported [18, 52].

Absolute CEST signal values were sometimes lower than those reported in prior studies, likely due to the shorter saturation times used in the present work. For example, APT‐weighted CEST studies typically employ saturation durations between 1 and 3 s, and report correspondingly larger signal amplitude values [12, 53, 54]. Guanidinium‐weighted CEST at 2 ppm has also been reported using a saturation time of 1500 ms, yielding higher CEST signal intensity values; however, these measurements were restricted to localized regions of interest and did not assess whole‐brain GM–WM contrast [44]. In the present study, increasing the saturation time from 600 to 800 ms yielded modest improvements with higher GM CEST signal intensity values in APT‐, hydroxyl‐ and guanidinium‐weighted CEST but no significant changes on the GM–WM contrast. For high‐B1 + acquisitions, as previously suggested in the literature [16], increasing the B1 + target to 3 μT improved CEST signals and GM–WM contrast but this was achievable only with a reduced slice coverage due to the coil protection limits being exceeded and also resulted in increased saturation inhomogeneity. These constraints on saturation duration and B1 + amplitude remain one of the main challenges for CEST imaging of the human brain at ultra‐high field.

Despite these limitations, the present acquisition conditions allowed clear detection of anatomical contrast at 11.7 T, highlighting the potential of ultra‐high field CEST for targeted studies of localized brain regions in neurological diseases. At the same time, quantitative interpretation across studies remains complicated by the strong dependence of the CEST contrast on both tissue properties (pH, temperature, conductivity…) and acquisition‐specific factors, including saturation scheme and field strength, which vary substantially across the literature [55, 56, 57].

In this work, the MTRasym metric was selected as a simple and low‐assumption approach for CEST contrast quantification. As previously discussed, this metric reflects overlapping contributions from multiple effects, including rNOE and magnetization transfer from macromolecules such as proteins and peptides. These confounding contributions are particularly important for guanidinium and APT contrasts; LDA [41] analysis was therefore performed for these two contrasts to improve specificity. More advanced quantification metrics based on voxel‐wise fittings such as multi‐pool Lorentzian fitting [58], polynomial and Lorentzian line‐shape fitting [59] have been proposed. While such methods may enable improved separation of individual CEST contributions, they are highly sensitive to the choice of initial fitting values and boundary conditions, which can lead to substantial variability in the resulting contrast and require careful interpretation [60]. Moreover, for high‐B1 + power, Lorentzian fitting approaches are not well‐suited as the assumption of no interaction between the pools breaks down [55]. The systematic evaluation of such additional post‐processing strategies was beyond the scope of the present study, which was primarily designed to establish the technical feasibility of CEST MRI on an 11.7 T system for humans and to acquire robust and reliable CEST images of the human brain. Nevertheless, quantitative CEST represents a particularly compelling avenue for future work at this field strength, where the increased chemical shift dispersion and sensitivity could be leveraged for more accurate estimation of metabolite concentrations and exchange rates [61, 62]. T1‐normalized metrics such as AREX [5] could additionally improve specificity by removing T1 contributions to the CEST contrast. In our healthy cohort, AREX‐derived CEST maps showed spatial patterns highly consistent with MTRasym‐derived maps on all participants, for all contrasts (see Figure S4 showing AREX maps for Participant 3). However, AREX may be particularly relevant in pathological conditions where T1 could vary substantially across tissue types.

As expected, the B1 + inhomogeneities were more pronounced at 11.7 T than at lower field strengths. Nonetheless, by allowing a higher local SAR (20 vs. 10 W/kg) the pTx‐VOP mode enabled transmit field homogenization comparable to previously reported 7 T CEST optimizations [18, 23], resulting in acceptable saturation homogeneity (typically smaller than 12.1% over a 1 cm slab). The retrospective B1 + correction provided minimal gains overall with the exception of very localized improvements observed in a single participant, consistent with previous observations by Völkze et al. [25]. These results suggest that, in the context of effective pTx optimization, retrospective B1 + correction may offer limited additional benefits. This finding is particularly important for future high‐field studies in patient cohorts, for which avoiding the need for additional datasets is critical to minimize total scan time.

In this study, saturation pulses were tailored on a per‐subject basis to account for inter‐individual B1 + variability; the development of universal pTx pulse designs applicable across subjects would substantially simplify clinical research deployment. Future improvements in pTx RF coil design may help achieve a more homogeneous B1 + field, which in turn may decrease the SAR and energy demand of CEST saturation and excitation pulses and allow more flexibility for the CEST saturation parameters.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates the feasibility of performing multi‐contrast CEST MRI in the human brain at 11.7 T with 1.6 mm isotropic resolution using pTx‐optimized CEST saturation and excitation pulses. The acquisition and optimization framework, based on pTx saturation pulse design under VOP‐based SAR constraints and previously established at 7 T [18], was successfully translated to 11.7 T. The primary technical adaptations were the use of higher local SAR limits within the IEC Level 1 operating mode and a dedicated B0 shimming protocol. By combining custom sequence implementation with tailored pTx saturation pulse design, we achieved robust and reproducible gray‐white matter CEST contrast across multiple contrast weightings in healthy volunteers despite the stringent RF and SAR constraints. Importantly, at 11.7 T, the combination of higher field strength and our optimized experimental conditions produced clear gray‐white matter contrast, which may provide a more informative readout than single‐compartmen CEST signals alone. CEST acquisitions at 11.7 T remain limited by B1 + inhomogeneity and coil protection constraints; however, the pTx‐VOP mode effectively mitigates these challenges, enabling practical in vivo imaging. Further advances in RF hardware, saturation pulse design and optimization will improve performance and spatial coverage, thereby fully realizing the potential of ultra‐high field CEST MRI.
