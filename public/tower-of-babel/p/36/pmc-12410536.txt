Protocol for the coculture of murine vaginal epithelial organoids and T cells to induce resident memory CD8 T cell differentiation
Authors: Julian C Ramprashad, Ying Lin, Lalit K Beura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryInducing robust resident memory T cell (TRM) establishment in mucosal tissues could enhance immunotherapy and vaccine efficacy. However, the factors influencing TRM formation are not fully understood. We present a protocol to coculture murine effector CD8 T cells with epithelial organoids, which serve as a reductionist model for investigating TRM differentiation in vitro. We describe steps for naive CD8 T cell activation, coculture of T cells with vaginal epithelial organoids (VEOs), and analysis of T cell phenotypes via flow cytometry.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Ulibarri et al.1Subject areas: Cell culture, Immunology, Organoids, Tissue Engineering

Before you begin

The protocol below describes the coculture of murine CD8 T cells with murine vaginal epithelial organoids (VEOs). The murine VEOs were grown as described before with minor modification.2 We performed this protocol successfully using T cells from several CD8 T cell transgenic mice strains including P14, gBT-I, and OT-I mice. Extending this protocol to other organoid systems will require modification of organoid-specific growth factors in the coculture medium. For example, TGF-β inhibitors SB431542 and A83-01, used for organoid maintenance,1,2 are absent in the coculture media as they influence proper epithelial CD8 TRM differentiation. If the changes necessary for coculture excessively impair organoid growth, coculture may need to be performed using preformed rather than dissociated organoids depending on the specific organoids used.

CRITICAL: Before you begin, coordinate the growth of your epithelial organoids with the timing of the coculture process. Coculture of CD8 T cells with epithelial organoids must occur 4–5 days after the “Isolation, enrichment, and activation of naive CD8 T cells” procedure. We generally use organoids that are 6–9 days old (post-passage) for coculture, which is also the age range in which we normally subculture them. However, the ideal age of use will vary if using different organoids.

Expected outcomes

We frequently assess the results of organoid-T cell cocultures at 7, 10, or 12 days post-coculture. After 10 days of coculture with murine VEOs pulsed with 10 μg/mL antigen at the point of coculture, we typically can isolate approximately 500-2500 T cells per well (96-well plate) via flow cytometry, 7-40% of which are CD69+CD103+ (Figure 4). This “double positive” population resembles the classical epithelial TRM cells in murine and human tissues and upregulate PD-1 but remain low for circulating CD8 T cell marker L-selectin/CD62L (Figure 3).

CD8 TRM differentiation

Representative flow cytometry plot showing upregulation of TRM-associated markers CD69, CD103, and PD-1 and downregulation of CD62L (bottom row) 10 days post-coculture. CD8 T cells maintained in the absence of organoids failed to acquire these changes (top row). Cells are gated on Live CD45.1+ LCMV-specific CD8 T cells.

Limitations

The phenotype of cocultured T cells, especially when looking at the percentage of CD69+/CD103+ “double positive” cells, can vary significantly between cocultures, which may depend on the quality of the T cell activation and the health of the organoid culture.

Furthermore, this coculture method relies on the ability of the epithelial organoids to grow in the absence of TGF-β inhibitors in which they are typically maintained (SB431542 or A83-01),1,2 as addition of the inhibitor can hinder CD8 TRM differentiation. We have noticed a modest attenuation in growth rate of the VEOs under this condition, but the organoids still supported robust TRM differentiation. If an organoid absolutely needs TGF-β inhibitor for growth, one can possibly use preformed organoids for coculture rather than digested organoids.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Basement membrane extract solidifies rapidly upon use, making resuspension of the coculture pellet and plating difficult.

Potential solution

•It is crucial to work swiftly with the basement membrane extract (BME) to avoid solidification during resuspension and plating. Be sure to position all materials in your tissue culture hood in an easy-to-access arrangement before beginning Step 29. As a rule of thumb, we only remove the aliquot of BME from 4°C/ice immediately before use and return it to 4°C/ice as soon as possible (before and after Step 29b). In our experience, the viscosity, optimal thawing time, and solidification rate of the BME can vary between batches. Adequate testing and using materials from the same batch where possible can be helpful.

Problem 2

Excessive bubble formation while resuspending and plating coculture droplets.

Potential solution

•Working swiftly with the BME suspension will help avoid solidification, which can cause bubbles. Avoid dispensing past the first stop of the pipette to avoid creating an air bubble. Making a stirring motion with the pipette tip while pipetting the BME suspension can also help avoid bubbles while aiding in even resuspension (Steps 29b&c).

Problem 3

BME droplets detach from the bottom of their wells during coculture.

Potential solution

Wells in which the extracellular matrix droplet has detached from the well-bottoms during coculture are still viable. However, special care must be taken not to aspirate the droplet during media changes (Step 32a). Droplet detachment can be minimized by reducing the number of bubbles present in the droplet at the point of coculture and by directing the pipette towards the well edge when aspirating and dispensing media for media changes (Step 29c).

Problem 4

Organoids will not grow after passage into coculture.

Potential solution

•Reduced organoid growth in coculture compared to typical maintenance culture is expected due to the removal of media components that interfere with T cell phenotypes, such as TGF-b inhibitors SB431542 or A83-01. Organoid growth during coculture may also depend on the type of organoid being used—the murine VEOs used in this protocol grow robustly.
