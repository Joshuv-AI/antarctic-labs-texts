Ischemic Stroke in Patients With Congenital Heart Disease and Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Arvid Holmgren, Kok Wai Giang, Maria Fedchenko, Peter Eriksson, Mikael Dellborg, Zacharias Mandalenakis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is a common arrhythmia in patients with congenital heart disease (CHD), but little is known about the risk for ischemic stroke among younger patients with CHD (aged <65 years) with AF.Methods and ResultsUsing data from the National Swedish Patient Register and Cause of Death Register, we identified all patients with both CHD and AF born in Sweden between 1970 and 2017. The Swedish total population register was used to identify age‐ and sex‐matched patients without CHD; those with AF were used as controls. Bottos hierarchical classification was used to define CHD as either complex or noncomplex. Controls were followed from the onset of AF until the index ischemic stroke, death, or end of study (December 31, 2017). We identified 951 patients with CHD with AF and 606 controls with AF. Among patients with both CHD and AF, 2.9% of patients (n=28) developed ischemic stroke, compared with 0.5% (n=3) in controls. When adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, and heart failure, a hazard ratio (HR) of 5.16 (95% CI, 1.52–17.46) was acquired. The HR in noncomplex CHD was 3.84 (95% CI, 1.07–13.84), and the HR in complex CHD was 8.34 (95% CI, 2.27–30.57). For patients born in 1970 to 1989, the HR in ischemic stroke was 7.35 (95% CI, 1.70–31.75). No adjusted HR for patients with CHD born in 1990 to 2017 could be acquired due to few events.ConclusionsThe risk for ischemic stroke may be 5 times higher in patients with both CHD and AF compared with patients without CHD with AF. However, larger studies may be needed to confirm/refute these results.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, congenital heart disease, ischemic strokeSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Atrial Fibrillation, Congenital Heart Disease

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia in clinical practice and an increasing comorbidity in patients with congenital heart disease (CHD). 1 , 2 The prevalence of atrial arrhythmias in the adult (aged ≥18 years) CHD population is estimated to be 15%, with projections suggesting that up to 50% of patients with CHD will experience atrial arrhythmias by the age of 65 years. 1 , 2 In the general population, AF and the risk of ischemic stroke (IS) have been subject to rigorous research, and evidence‐based assessment tools and guidelines such as the CHA2DS2‐VASc score now facilitate clinical decision making for initiation of anticoagulation treatment for stroke prevention in patients with AF. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 However, patients with CHD develop AF at a younger age, with 22 times higher risk for AF before the age of 42 years, compared with matched controls from the non‐CHD population. 8 Furthermore, studies indicate that patients with CHD with or without AF have a higher risk for stroke, with estimated incident rates up to ≈7% and a relative risk of up to 10.8 in younger patients compared with the non‐CHD (ie, patients without CHD) population. 9 , 10 , 11 A multicenter retrospective cohort study found that the CHA2DS2‐VASc score was a poor predictor of IS in atrial arrhythmias and CHD patients. 12 Moreover, guidelines for stroke prevention in this patient group are not entirely conclusive and encourage clinicians to tailor their management of patients on the basis of individual assessments. 13 , 14

Few studies have explored the correlation between AF and IS in the CHD population. Population‐based cohort studies have shown clear correlations between AF and IS in CHD, 2 , 10 , 11 while in contrast, another population‐based cohort study failed to identify AF as a significant risk factor for IS in CHD. 15

While both AF and IS are clearly far more common in young patients with CHD than in controls without CHD in the same age and sex group, more studies are needed to shed light on the risk that AF poses for young patients with CHD. The purpose of this nationwide, retrospective cohort study was to examine the risk for developing IS among patients with CHD with AF when compared with that among controls without CHD with AF.

METHODS

Data Sources

All necessary data underlying this article are available in the article and in its online supplementary material. All raw data from the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare are available provided that ethical permission can be obtained from the Swedish Ethical Review Authority. For more information, contact the corresponding author.

This is a nationwide study using data from the Swedish National Patient Register and the Swedish Cause of Death Register. All diagnoses are coded according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD). In Sweden, a 10‐digit identification number indicating date of birth and sex is assigned to each resident at birth or immigration, facilitating patient identification in the register. Administered by the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare in 1964, all hospitals, public as well as private, are mandated to report to the register. A complete nationwide coverage of the register was achieved in 1987.

The Swedish National Prescribed Drug Register, which includes data from drug prescriptions from 2005 forward, was linked to the Swedish National Inpatient Register (IPR). The data were used to identify all patients receiving anticoagulation therapy (ACT) among the patients with both CHD and AF and controls with AF included in the study.

Surgical and interventional procedures are reported to the IPR using codes from the Nordic Medico‐Statistical Committee Classification of Surgical Procedures. To investigate the potential impact on AF burden and risk for IS before and after ablation, Nordic Medico‐Statistical Committee Classification of Surgical Procedures codes for ablation therapy for AF were used to identify patients with CHD and controls who underwent ablation therapy for AF. The Nordic Medico‐Statistical Committee Classification of Surgical Procedures codes used to define ablation for AF are specified in Table S1.

Definitions

In the current study, ICD, Eighth Revision (ICD‐8), Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes were used to extract diagnoses of CHD from the IPR. CHD was categorized as suggested by Botto et al 16 and then modified by the method of Liu et al, 17 hierarchically dividing CHD into 6 lesion groups (Table S2). Because of the small number of controls, the lesion groups were divided into 2 larger groups: the complex group including lesion groups 1 and 2, and the noncomplex group including lesion groups 3 through 6.

ICD codes 427.92 in ICD‐8, 427D in ICD‐9, and I48 in ICD‐10 were used to identify a diagnosis of AF. IS was identified by ICD codes 433 and 434 in ICD‐8, 434 and 436 in ICD‐9, and I63 and I64 in ICD‐10. The known risk factors hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, and peripheral artery disease were considered present if they were discovered before diagnosis of IS. An IS event was defined as developing IS after the onset of AF. All ICD codes used to identify AF, IS, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and peripheral artery disease are specified in Table S3. ACT was defined as receiving any of the following: rivaroxaban, dabigatran, apixaban, or warfarin.

Study Population

We identified patients with CHD born in Sweden between 1970 and 2017 using the IPR and the Cause of Death Register. All patients with CHD were matched with ≈10 age‐ and sex‐matched controls without CHD using the Total Population Register. Each control could be matched only once for each patient. The study population included patients with CHD who developed AF between 1970 and 2017. Among the controls assigned to the patients with CHD, controls (without CHD) with AF were identified for comparison and constituted the control population. Patients and controls were followed from onset of AF until death, first IS, or the end of the study (December 31, 2017), whichever occurred first. All patients with a diagnosis of IS before diagnosis of AF were excluded from the study.

A total of 71 941 patients with CHD and 714 462 age‐ and sex‐matched controls born between 1970 and 2017 were identified. Of these, 1 patient with CHD and 6 controls were excluded because of nonspecific birth dates, and 43 patients with CHD and 6 controls were excluded because of IS before AF onset. A total of 951 of the remaining patients with CHD and 606 of the remaining matched controls developed AF and were included in the final study population.

We divided patients receiving ACT into 2 groups: those receiving direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and those receiving warfarin. The National Prescribed Drug Register was used to identify the dates when patients obtained their prescription drugs from their pharmacy. These dates were used to identify patients receiving ACT at any time before or after the onset of AF. To exclude patients who received temporary ACT before electrocardioversion of AF in a polyclinical setting, we excluded patients who received ACT after onset who obtained anticoagulants from their pharmacy at fewer than 3 separate dates. Patients receiving ACT were stratified into different groups on the basis of the timing of ACT initiation in relation to onset of AF. One group consisted solely of patients who received ACT before the onset of AF, the other group included patients who received ACT before as well as after the onset of AF. Additionally, another group was composed of patients who developed IS while receiving ACT. Patients with a history of receiving both a DOAC and warfarin were not excluded from the study, and they are presented separately within both the DOAC and warfarin groups.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 3.6.1 (R Software Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 18 For baseline characteristics comparison, the χ 2 test was used for groups and the t test for continuous variables.

All analyses were performed on patients with both CHD and AF, as well as subgroup analysis on the basis of birth period, time since AF onset in years, and complexity of CHD, using matched patients without CHD with AF as controls. In the subgroup analysis based on complexity of CHD, the noncomplex as well as the complex group were compared with all identified controls. Hence, patients with complex and noncomplex CHD were compared with all controls regardless of which patients (ie, complex or noncomplex CHD) these controls were initially assigned to. The rationale for this method of assigning controls was to create a balanced control population, given the lack of balance in how controls with AF were allocated to the complex and noncomplex groups. For the subgroup analysis based on birth period and time since AF onset, patients with CHD were compared with the control patients assigned to them during the initial matching process (Table S4).

Cumulative incidence with 95% CI was calculated using the Fine–Gray model with death from all other causes than IS as a competing event. The incidence rate (IR) was reported as events per 1000 person‐years, calculated as the number of events divided by the total number of years of follow‐up time with 95% CI (based on the Poisson method).

Calculations to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs for the development of IS after AF were performed with Cox proportional hazard models. To estimate the differences in risk of developing IS after AF, Cox proportional hazard models were performed. The identified patients without CHD with AF were used as the reference group for all Cox regression analyses. To compensate for differences between controls and patients with CHD, the Cox regression analysis was adjusted for sex, age at diagnosis of AF, birth period, hypertension, and heart failure. The rationale for this was to adjust for the risk factors described by the CHA2DS2‐VASc score. However, there were too few cases of diabetes, ischemic heart disease, and previous myocardial infarction for these to yield a meaningful modification of our analysis (Table 1). Both unadjusted and adjusted data are presented. The adjusted models were not adjusted for competing risks. To examine the risk of IS between different lesion groups, we stratified our Cox regression analysis with regards to lesion group. The proportional hazard assumption was examined using Schoenfeld residuals. In our final analysis, all Cox proportional hazards models met the requirements of proportionality.

Ethics

The regional ethics board of Gothenburg confirmed ethical approval of this study (Gbg 912–16, T619‐18). Because the study was based on previously collected anonymized data from administrative registers, the ethics board concluded that the study could be carried out without individual patient consent. The received data set consisted of data in which the personal identification data had been replaced by a code.

RESULTS

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the study population. A total of 951 patients with both CHD and AF and 606 controls with AF were identified. Of these, 626 (65.8%) patients with CHD were male patients, while the corresponding number in the control group was 467 (77.1%). Overall, 755 (79.4%) patients with both CHD and AF and 496 (81.8%) controls were born in 1970 to 1989. A total of 355 (37.3%) patients with CHD were classified has having complex disease; 158 (16.6%) had heart failure at onset of AF, compared with 17 (2.8%) in the control patients. The average age at onset of AF was 24.4±12.9 years among patients with CHD and 29.9±10.0 years for controls. The average age at onset of IS was 24.4±12.9 years among patients with CHD compared with 29.9±10.0 years in controls.

Table 2 shows the numbers of events and IR for patients with CHD and controls. Of 951 patients with CHD and AF, a total of 28 patients subsequently developed IS. The prevalence of IS was 2.9% in patients with CHD compared with 0.5% in controls. The IR of IS was 3.11 (95% CI, 2.06–4.49) per 1000 person‐years in the CHD population and 0.73 (95% CI, 0.15–2.13) per 1000 person‐years in the controls. The complex lesion group had an IR of 4.06 (95% CI, 2.32–6.60). The corresponding result in the noncomplex group was 2.36 (95% CI, 1.22–4.13). Moreover, Table 2 shows that 5 patients with CHD developed IS within 1 year of AF onset with an IR of 5.64 (CI 1.83–13.17) per 1000 person‐years, 9 patients with CHD developed IS between 1 and 5 years since AF onset, with an IR of 3.2 (95% CI, 1.46–6.08) per 1000 person‐years. A total of 14 patients with CHD developed IS after ≥5 years after AF onset, with an IR of 2.63 (95% CI, 1.44–4.42) per 1000 person‐years.

DISCUSSION

Our results show that 1 in 30 patients with both CHD and AF had a stroke before the age of 47 years. Moreover, the incidence of IS was higher than that of the control population, and our results suggest a relative risk of developing IS in patients with both CHD and AF that may be 5 times higher than that of patients with AF without CHD. The highest risk was seen in patients with complex CHD, with the risk of IS being 8‐fold that of the controls. However, a more modest increase in risk was seen among patients with noncomplex CHD (HR, 3.84 [95% CI, 1.07–13.84]).

The prevalence of AF was lower in this study when compared with other studies on the subject of patients with both CHD and AF. However, this study includes only young patients (aged up to 47 years) with CHD. Seeing that AF is more common in older patients, a lower prevalence is to be expected. 1 , 2

The design used in our study is unique in that it compares patients with AF, with the variable being the presence or absence of CHD. Another unique feature is the young study population. In the general population, the risk of IS is considered low in young patients with AF. 4 Effectively, our study implies that in young patients with both CHD and AF, the risk of developing IS is relatively high and cannot be equated with that of the risk for young patients in the general population. Because our model is adjusted for all risk factors in the CHA2DS2‐VASc score, the increase in risk should be seen in all patients with CHD regardless of CHA2DS2‐VASc score. Consequently, our results support the idea that patients with both CHD and AF have increased risk of IS, and that particular attention is warranted for these patients with regard to stroke prevention.

In a population‐based study by Pedersen et al, the risk of IS in adult patients with both CHD and AF compared with that in the control population was estimated to be an HR of 6.0 for patients aged <60 years. 10 Our study shows a more modest risk increase. However, this is not unexpected because Pedersen's study uses a different study design, comparing AF in patients with CHD with matched healthy controls (ie, controls not necessarily having a history of AF). Furthermore, a recent nationwide registry‐based cohort study by Lee et al similar to the study by Pedersen et al suggested an HR of 2.37 (2.21–2.54) when comparing patients with CHD to non‐CHD controls. 19 However, the study included patients of all ages with no subanalysis for younger patients.

In a registry‐based retrospective cohort study from Yang et al, AF was not identified as being significantly associated with IS in patients with CHD with atrial arrhythmias. 15 However, this study used a different study design. Yang's study was performed on only patients with CHD, comparing patients with and without atrial arrhythmias. Moreover, 2 retrospective cohort studies identified an increased risk for IS and other thromboembolic events among patients with CHD and atrial arrhythmias, 20 , 21 but these studies were smaller with different study designs, comparing patients with both CHD and AF with other patients with CHD rather than non‐CHD controls. A comparison of the available literature regarding risk for IS among patients with both CHD and AF can be found in Table S5.

Previous studies have concluded that the CHA2DS2‐VASc score is a poor predictor of IS in patients with both CHD and AF. 13 , 14 Instead, severity of lesion has been proposed as the main indicator of risk for IS in CHD. 13 , 14 Current guidelines suggest that the CHA2DS2‐VASc score can provide guidance in patients with simple CHD, while ACT can be considered in patients with moderate or severe CHD and AF regardless of CHA2DS2‐VASc score. 12 , 13 , 14 Our results support the idea that patients with complex CHD have an increased risk of IS compared with patients with noncomplex CHD. However, our findings also suggest an increased risk of IS in the noncomplex group. Therefore, the principle of risk assessment based solely on the complexity of CHD may underestimate the risk of IS among patients with noncomplex CHD and AF.

Our results indicate that patients with both CHD and AF are more likely to have received ACT before AF onset, which is most likely due to the presence of mechanical heart valves and a higher degree of comorbidity. 14 , 22 A 2020 registry‐based retrospective descriptive study showed that 10% of adult patients with CHD received ACT, with 43.9% receiving DOAC, illustrating the increase in DOAC prescription among adult patients with CHD. 23 Our results show similar tendencies regarding ACT, with 14.3% receiving ACT before AF onset, although only 18.4% of these received DOAC.

The percentage of patients with CHD receiving ACT after onset of AF was twice as high as in non‐CHD controls, the majority of whom received warfarin. This is interesting because the guidelines regarding ACT in patients with both CHD and AF are not entirely conclusive. The choice of ACT in patients with CHD is difficult because of the lack of large randomized clinical trials. Two prospective longitudinal cohort studies show DOACs to be safe and efficient in preventing thrombotic events, but a large retrospective cohort study indicated that warfarin is superior in safety as well as efficacy. 23 , 24 , 25 None of the patients with CHD who developed IS received a DOAC. Because of the small sample size with regard to ACT, no certain conclusions can be drawn regarding choice of ACT and risk for IS.

As AF is an increasing morbidity in patients with CHD, clinicians will require better guidance regarding use of anticoagulation for patients with both CHD and AF. A multicenter prospective cohort study concluded that implementation of current guidelines was lacking because of conflicting recommendations in different guideline documents, 26 and as mentioned, our results indicate that current guidelines underestimate the risk for IS in patients with both noncomplex CHD and AF. The risk assessment of ACT in patients with both CHD and AF is further complicated by the risk of hemorrhagic stroke; a retrospective cohort study has shown that patients with CHD have up to 8‐fold risk of developing hemorrhagic stroke compared with non‐CHD controls. 27 More studies are therefore needed to better understand the predictors of IS in patients with both CHD and AF, as well as safe and effective use of anticoagulation. To date, we know of 1 ongoing prospective nonintervention cohort study aiming to examine the safety and efficacy of apixaban in patients with CHD with atrial arrhythmias. 28 Our results highlight the need for further studies regarding the effectiveness of ACT in patients with CHD and AF.

Limitations

This study was based on data from the IPR. An external validation from 2010 concluded that the positive predictive value of cardiovascular diagnoses in the register was 85% to 95%. 29 , 30 In a validation study of patients with both CHD and myocardial infarction in the IPR, Fedchenko et al were able to verify 74.8% of all patients classified as having CHD in the register, with 4% of these being misclassified. 30 Because the present study is based on data from medical registries, it is not possible to verify the diagnosis of IS or AF, nor is it possible to assess the severity of IS in the affected subjects. More detailed information such as ejection fraction, burden of AF, and compliance were not available in the registers. Moreover, the IPR does not include records from primary health care and outpatient units. While it is highly unlikely that IS would have been diagnosed and treated in an outpatient setting, there is a risk of missing comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes because these diagnoses are commonly treated by primary health care physicians. The Swedish National Prescribed Drug Register includes data only since 2005. Thus, the ACT data in this study include only prescriptions from 2005 to 2017.

A total of 43 patients with CHD and 6 controls developed IS before AF onset. It would be of interest to examine how many of these patients was diagnosed with AF during poststroke evaluation, implicating AF‐induced IS. Since this study relied on register‐based data, such an analysis was not possible. As such, including these patients in the study would be problematic due to the risk of bias, as these patients might not have been diagnosed with AF unless they had an IS.

The study was carried out among a Swedish population, taking advantage of Sweden's well‐funded health care system, which provides advanced health care for patients with CHD across the whole population. Consequently, there may be difficulties in extrapolating the findings of this study in countries where universal health care is not available to all residents.

Few IS events were recorded in this study. Thus, the results should be interpreted carefully. However, this patient group is considerably younger than both the average patient with AF as well as the average patient with IS 31 . Therefore, this low number of events is expected. Nevertheless, the low number of events constitutes a major limitation of this study, as it results in a high level of uncertainty of results. In particular, the HR for patients born in 1990 to 2017 cannot be considered entirely reliable due to the low number of events. In the subgroup analysis of complex and non‐complex CHD, each subgroup of CHD was analyzed using the entire control population, regardless of which controls the patients were initially assigned to. This means that there may be limitations with regard to mismatch in age and sex among controls and patients with CHD, making the model less reliable. The Cox regression model was adjusted for age and sex, partially compensating for this limitation.

The adjusted HR models presented in this article adjust for several morbidities, with few events recorded. Therefore, the reader the needs to interpret these results carefully, as the models may be susceptible to overmodeling, and adjusted models must be interpreted alongside the unadjusted model for comparison. Moreover, seeing that heart failure was far more common in patients with CHD and is a major risk factor for IS, it is difficult to rule out the possibility that heart failure may constitute a significant confounding factor for the increased risk of IS. Our adjusted models attempt to adjust for this limitation. However, the diagnosis of heart failure in CHD is difficult to define, particularly in complex CHD, which is often associated with elevated natriuretic peptides as well as exercise intolerance without clinically manifest heart failure/congestion. 32 Thus, AF can be a more clinically useful marker for risk of stroke compared with heart failure, as it is easily identified on an ECG.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study demonstrates that the risk of developing IS may be 5 times higher in young patients with both CHD and AF compared with that in controls with AF. Notably, an elevated risk was also observed in patients with non‐complex CHD, although patients with complex CHD exhibited the highest risk. However, due to limitations in the matching process and possible confounding effect of heart failure, the results need to be interpreted with caution. Because current guidelines primarily consider the complexity of the underlying CHD as the main indicator of risk for IS, our results highlight the need for better stroke score prediction models as well as for new studies examining the safety and efficacy of oral ACT in patients with both CHD and AF.
