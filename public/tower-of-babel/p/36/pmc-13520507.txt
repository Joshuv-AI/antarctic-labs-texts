More pronounced effects of the extent of discectomy over the extent of laminectomy on postoperative biomechanics and clinical outcomes in unilateral biportal endoscopy for lumbar disc herniation
Authors: Fei Wang, Tengjie Wang, Guangxian Lu, Songou Zhang, Minghua Xie, Ping Zhou, Zhi Chen, Jianfeng Ping
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE) can be performed to treat lumbar disc herniation (LDH). Nevertheless, the postoperative clinical outcomes and spinal biomechanical implications following partial discectomy and laminectomy, which are inherent to UBE techniques for treating LDH, lack robust documentation in the literature.MethodsA total of 132 patients underwent UBE surgery for LDH at the L4–L5 segment. On the basis of intraoperative determinations, cases were classified as conservative discectomy (CD) or aggressive discectomy (AD) depending on the extent of discectomy. Postoperative three-dimensional computed tomography (CT) was used to categorize the extent of laminectomy into the following groups: one-third laminectomy (LC1/3) to half laminectomy (LC1/2) (LC1/3–LC1/2), LC1/2 to two-thirds laminectomy (LC2/3) (LC1/2–LC2/3), and greater than LC2/3. The visual analog scale (VAS) and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) were used for the clinical evaluation. A biomechanical finite element method analysis was performed to assess the maximum von Mises stresses in the intervertebral discs and articular cartilage for six different surgical approaches.ResultsStatistically significant differences in both VAS scores for back pain and ODI scores were observed between the CD + (LC1/3–LC1/2) and AD + (LC1/3–LC1/2) groups within 3 months after surgery. Moreover, statistically significant differences in both the VAS scores for back pain and the ODI scores were observed between the CD + (LC1/2–LC2/3) and AD + (LC1/2–LC2/3) groups at 3 months after surgery. Statistically significant differences in both the VAS scores for back pain and ODI scores were also observed between the CD + (LC1/3–LC1/2) and CD + (LC1/2–LC2/3) groups within 1 month after surgery. No statistically significant differences were observed in the VAS or ODI scores between the AD + (LC1/3–LC1/2) and AD + (LC1/2–LC2/3) groups throughout the postoperative follow-up period. The maximum von Mises stresses of the L4‒L5 intervertebral disc and articular cartilage were greater in the AD group than in the CD group when the same-sized vertebral plate was removed. Although variations in the laminectomy size led to differential stresses on the intervertebral discs in both the CD and AD groups, the observed differences were not statistically significant.ConclusionsEarly postoperative dissatisfaction after UBE may be correlated with excessive disc resection and excessive laminectomy. During UBE procedures, the degree of disc resection has a greater effect on both clinical outcomes and segmental biomechanics than the extent of laminectomy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06955-5.

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is a spinal disorder characterized by the displacement of the nucleus pulposus from the intervertebral disc into the spinal canal, frequently leading to nerve root compression and manifesting as lower back pain and radicular leg symptoms [1]. The management of LDH ranges from nonsurgical approaches, such as pain control and physical therapy, to surgical interventions when conservative treatments fail to alleviate symptoms [2, 3]. In recent years, minimally invasive surgical techniques have gained significant popularity because of their advantages, including shorter recovery times, lower amounts of blood loss, and lower complication rates [4].

Unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE), a minimally invasive intervention for LDH, has gained considerable traction in orthopedic practice in China in recent years. UBE uses two distinct portals—one for endoscopic visualization and the other for instrument manipulation—and enables precise spinal decompression while preserving the surrounding soft tissues. This method combines the benefits of endoscopic surgery (e.g., reduced muscle injury and blood loss) with the ergonomic flexibility of traditional open techniques, thus allowing surgeons to address complex pathologies such as central or foraminal herniations, spinal stenosis, and calcified discs [5].

Although UBE has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative to traditional spinal surgeries, its requirement for partial laminectomy and facet joint resection raises significant clinical concerns. This structural compromise, which is essential for surgical access, has multiple consequences: biomechanical instability, abnormal stress distribution, and chronic pain syndromes [6, 7]. Moreover, previous biomechanical studies have demonstrated that increased removal of the nucleus pulposus leads to higher stress on the facet joints and greater segmental motion [6]. While UBE for LDH involves both the resection of partial lamina/facet joints and the removal of nucleus pulposus tissue, research investigating its biomechanical effects on the intervertebral disc stress distribution and facet joint loading has not yet been reported. This study combines an evaluation of the clinical outcomes of UBE in LDH treatment with a biomechanical finite element analysis to systematically verify and interpret postoperative issues, thereby establishing an optimized, evidence-based framework for developing rational surgical strategies in UBE procedures.

Methods

Setting and patient population

This retrospective study included consecutive patients aged > 18 years who underwent single-level UBE surgery at our institute from January 2023 to December 2024. Surgical interventions were performed at the L4–L5 levels based on preoperative MR images and clinical findings. The inclusion criteria were as follows:1) persistent radiculopathy or motor deficits unresponsive to ≥ 12 weeks of conservative management and 2) no prior spinal surgery or instrumentation at the affected level. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) multilevel disc herniation or concomitant spinal stenosis; 2) spondylolisthesis or segmental instability; 3) active spinal infection, malignancy, or severe osteoporosis; and 4) systemic comorbidities that precluded general anesthesia. This study was approved by the clinical research ethics committee of our hospital.

Clinical assessment

Operative time, intraoperative hemorrhage, length of hospital stay, time to return to work, and complications such as dural tear, postoperative wound infection, nerve injury, and reoperation were recorded. The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and visual analog scale (VAS) scores were used to assess the clinical outcomes of patients preoperatively and at 3 days, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively.

Construction of the lumbar spine finite element model (FEM)

A 42-year-old male patient diagnosed with LDH was enrolled in this study. The lumbar spine was scanned using a spiral computed tomography (CT) scanner (Canon Aquilion ONE 320‑slice; 120 kV; tube current: 40–400 mA) with a slice thickness of 0.5 mm, yielding 469 images saved in DICOM format. The L4–L5 segment was selected as the target level because of its high frequency of involvement in LDH [8].

The DICOM images were imported into Mimics 21.0 (Materialize, Belgium) for 3D reconstruction through threshold segmentation, mask editing, and cavity filling. The reconstructed model was exported as an STL file and imported into Geomagic Wrap 2021 for refinement, including repair, smoothing, and surface generation. After processing through point‑cloud, polygon, and surface stages, the model was saved as a STEP file and assembled in SOLIDWORKS, where intervertebral discs, cartilage, and surgical resections (lamina and disc) were created. The final assembly was exported in X_T format to ANSYS Workbench 2021 R1 for the finite element analysis.

A default mesh with an element size of 0.5 mm was generated. The model comprised seven structural types: cortical bone, cancellous bone, endplate, cartilage, nucleus pulposus, posterior elements, and ligaments. Each component was assigned elastic material properties (Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio) based on published data (Table S1), with all the tissues modeled as linear elastic solids.

Establishment of UBE surgical models

In general, UBE surgery can be categorized into the following steps: 1. The observation and working portals were established, followed by endoscopic-guided radiofrequency ablation of soft tissue to create an operative field. 2. Soft tissue within the visual field was removed to expose the lamina and articular processes of the superior vertebral body, which were then partially resected using a high-speed burr. 3. A portion of the ligamentum flavum was resected with a Kerrison rongeur to expose the intraspinal nerve root and dura mater. The nerve root was gently retracted, and discectomy was performed using a pituitary rongeur. The extent of articular process and nucleus pulposus removal was determined based on clinical surgical experience.

On the basis of the degree of laminectomy, the models were categorized into three groups: one-third laminectomy (LC1/3), half laminectomy (LC1/2), and two-thirds laminectomy (LC2/3). In accordance with the amount of nucleus pulposus removed, the models were further divided into conservative discectomy (CD) and aggressive discectomy (AD) groups. Six distinct model groups were established. Boundary and loading conditions

In each model, torque and load were applied to the nodes coupled to the upper surface of the L4 vertebra, completely fixing and constraining the six degrees of freedom at the bottom of the L5 vertebra. A 500-N follow-up load was applied to the coupling point on the upper surface of the L4 vertebral body to simulate the compression load produced by the synergistic actions of the body mass of the upper body and the muscle tissue of the waist. A physiological torque of 10 Nm was applied at the coupling point to simulate six postures of the lumbar spine, namely, forward bending, backward extension, left bending, right bending, left rotation, and right rotation [9]. During each of these six loading phases, the maximum load for each model was recorded, along with the maximum von Mises stress of the intervertebral disc and the left and right cartilages under different motion conditions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software v. 21.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were compared between the CD and AD groups using independent sample t tests. Preoperative and postoperative ODI and VAS scores were compared using paired t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed with a chi-square test, and a P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The biomechanical evaluation was based on the maximum von Mises stress values.

Biomechanical finite element analysis

We divided the surgical techniques into six groups to simulate changes in stress in the L4–L5 segments using different surgical methods: CD + LC1/3, CD + LC1/2, CD + LC2/3, AD + LC1/3, AD + LC1/2, and AD + LC2/3 (Fig. 2).

Intervertebral disc stress (IDS)

We explored differences in the intervertebral disc stress profile to compare the effects of CD and AD on degenerative changes in surgical segments (Figs. 3, 4, 5). Figure S6 shows stress cloud diagrams of the L4–5 disc under six postures for each of the six groups. The maximum von Mises stress in the L4‒5 intervertebral disc was greater in the AD group than in the CD group when the same extent of the vertebral plate was removed (Table S6). The most pronounced change in stress between the CD and AD groups was observed in the left bending state. Stress on articular cartilage (SAC)

Discussion

Owing to its advantages, including user-friendly operation, visualization of a wide surgical field, a short learning curve, and independence from expensive equipment, UBE has gained increasing popularity in China for treating spinal disorders. However, with expanding clinical applications, various complications, including epidural hematoma, dural tears, spinal hypertension-like reactions, inadequate decompression, iatrogenic spinal instability, nerve injuries, infections, and recurrence, have emerged [10, 11]. During UBE procedures for LDH, surgical corridors are established through the paraspinal muscles with partial laminectomy to remove nerve root-compressing nucleus pulposus tissue for symptom relief. Nevertheless, in follow-up observations of patients who underwent simple nucleus pulposus removal via UBE, some cases of persistent or aggravated low back pain were identified. These findings suggest that the extent of soft tissue and osseous structure disruption during UBE procedures for LDH may significantly impact postoperative clinical outcomes.

While recurrence prevention remains a pivotal concern postoperatively, the current evidence fails to establish a quantitative correlation between the amount of excised nucleus pulposus tissue and recurrence rates. Although more extensive disc tissue resection may reduce recurrence rates, it comes at the cost of significantly worse low back pain outcomes and ODI scores during the first postoperative year [12]. Overly aggressive nucleus pulposus excision may destabilize the spinal motion segment, thereby disrupting the inherent biomechanical equilibrium [13]. A larger volume of removed tissue directly increases intradiscal stress concentrations in a resection-dependent manner [14]. Moreover, excessive disc tissue resection reduces the intervertebral height, thereby increasing stress loading on the posterior facet joints [14]. However, while existing findings primarily address PELD, the biomechanical outcomes and clinical efficacy of discectomy via UBE have not been reported in the literature. In our study, we found that in the early postoperative stage following simple nucleotomy via UBE, patients who underwent AD experienced more severe low back pain and lower satisfaction than those who underwent CD. One study also indicated that the limited discectomy group exhibited significantly greater improvements in VAS and ODI scores within the first month postoperatively than the aggressive discectomy group [15]. Subsequently, using a three-dimensional FEM to simulate discectomy, we found that AD induced greater stress in the remaining disc than CD. Aggressive discectomy may decrease the facet joint distance and increase spinal instability, which may contribute to facet joint degeneration and low back pain [16]. TELD with a high grade of nucleotomy results in obvious biomechanical deterioration [17]. Therefore, we conclude that excessive discectomy tends to induce biomechanical alterations at the surgical level, thereby adversely affecting postoperative outcomes. However, during UBE surgery, partial removal of the lamina and inferior articular process is necessary to access and remove the nerve-compressing nucleus pulposus tissue. Consequently, the extent of lamina and articular process resection inevitably impacts biomechanical behavior at the surgical level and subsequent clinical outcomes [6]. We found that the extent of laminectomy correlated with increased intervertebral disc stress only during right bending and left rotation; however, this increase was not statistically significant. Therefore, we suggest that partial laminectomy may not have as substantial an effect on intervertebral disc biomechanics as previously assumed during UBE surgery for LDH.

The finite element analysis of PTED demonstrated that increased facet joint resection directly increases stress levels in the surgical-level disc and endplates while concurrently inducing segmental instability [18]. More extensive facet joint resection may increase the risk of biomechanical compromise, thus potentially predisposing patients to postoperative low back pain [19]. In our study, we observed that patients who underwent CD with wider laminectomy exhibited a more significant increase in early-stage low back pain postoperatively. In contrast, those who underwent AD with wider laminectomy did not show a significant increase in postoperative low back pain. As a more extensive laminectomy may involve the partial destruction of the facet joints, it could alter the biomechanics at the surgical level. Our biomechanical finite element analysis revealed that AD results in greater stress on the articular cartilage than CD. However, partial resection of the lamina and facet joints resulted in significantly increased articular cartilage stress only during left lateral bending and right axial rotation. Numerous studies have shown that the greater the extent of posterior spinal column resection, the greater the likelihood of inducing biomechanical instability and changes in the stress distribution across the intervertebral discs and/or facet joints [20, 21]. However, one study reported that increased bone resection was associated with reduced facet joint stress across three laminectomy models [22]. Our findings reveal that the effects of partial laminectomy on surgical-level biomechanics are significantly less pronounced than those of discectomy, particularly aggressive discectomy. Therefore, we believe that during UBE surgery, the partial laminectomy required for adequate surgical exposure likely induces minimal changes in segmental biomechanics. Instead, the critical issue demanding greater attention is striking a balance between aggressive nucleus pulposus removal to prevent recurrence and conservative removal to maintain biomechanical stability.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective study with a small sample size and a short follow-up duration. Second, our biomechanical FEM did not analyze the spinal range of motion (ROM) or include a nonsurgical control group. Although the biomechanical effects of posterior lumbar column resection on spinal motion have been well documented in previous simulation studies, our study focused specifically on quantifying how varying the volumes of the resected lamina and inferior articular processes differentially impact spinal biomechanics. Third, the distinction between conservative and aggressive discectomy is based on an intraoperative assessment rather than objective volumetric quantification, which introduces potential surgeon-dependent bias. Finally, our model did not account for the mechanical effects of the paraspinal muscles, which may limit its clinical applicability. Thus, a study with a larger sample size and a longer follow-up is needed to further validate our clinical findings.

Conclusions

UBE discectomy is a safe and effective surgical procedure that can achieve satisfactory results in the treatment of LDH. Early postoperative dissatisfaction following UBE surgery may be associated with AD and excessive resection of the lamina and facet joints. The degree of discectomy exerts a more profound effect than the extent of laminectomy on both postoperative clinical outcomes and biomechanical alterations at the surgical segment. In UBE surgery for LDH, adequate discectomy to achieve sufficient neural decompression should receive greater priority than the disruption of the bony architecture. This strategy optimally preserves segmental biomechanics while maximizing clinical efficacy.
