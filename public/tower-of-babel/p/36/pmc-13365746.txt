International development of a lupus-specific instrument to assess cognitive symptoms in patients with SLE: Lupus Brain Fog Severity Scale (LBFSS) study
Authors: Laurent Arnaud, Matteo Piga, Guillermo J Pons-Estel, Yurilis J Fuentes-Silva, Hervé Devilliers, Raquel Faria, Ali Duarte-Garcia, Daniel Guimarães de Oliveira, Marina Scolnik, Sander van Leuven, Eve Smith, Simone Appenzeller, Ivan Padjen, Michelle A Petri, Alessandra Bortoluzzi, Ioannis Parodis, Meggan Mackay, Faisal Parlindungan, Odirlei A Monticielo, Antonis Fanouriakis, Catherine Kleitz, Andrea M Knight, Nathalie Philippi, Alberta Hoi, Zoe Karakikla-Mitsakou, Sofia Silva Ribeiro, Annemarie Sluijmers, Aldevina Sturiene, Zahi Touma

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCognitive symptoms such as ‘brain fog’ are frequent and disabling in patients with SLE. We aimed to develop and validate the Lupus Brain Fog Severity Scale (LBFSS), the first patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) for cognitive symptoms in SLE.MethodsThe LBFSS was developed using a multi-step international approach involving healthcare professionals and patients with SLE. Patients provided free-text descriptions of brain fog, generating candidate domains through thematic analysis (step 1). Domains were rated for relevance (step 2) and those retained converted into self-assessable items (step 3). The pilot LBFSS was iteratively refined (step 4). The finalised LBFSS was validated (step 5) in a large international SLE cohort. Psychometric evaluation included internal consistency, exploratory factor analysis and assessment of construct, convergent, known-groups and criterion-related validity.ResultsIn step 1, 147 SLE participants across 39 countries generated 21 domains for cognitive symptoms, of which 13 were retained in consensus (step 2), converted into self-assessable items (step 3) and iteratively refined using patient and physician feedback. In step 5, validation of the LBFSS instrument in 378 patients from 36 countries demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.95) and a unidimensional structure explaining 62.2% of variance. LBFSS scores were significantly higher in participants reporting brain fog (p<0.0001), confirming known-group validity. Construct validity was supported by significant correlations with functional impact and symptom severity (ρ=0.46–0.65, p<0.0001), strong convergent validity with the Perceived Deficits Questionnaire-20 (ρ=0.84, p<0.0001) while the discriminative performance for presence versus absence of self-reported brain fog was high (area under the curve=0.85).ConclusionThe LBFSS is a new, valid and feasible PROM to assess brain fog and cognitive symptoms in SLE.Keywords: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Autoimmune Diseases, Health-Related Quality Of Life, Patient Reported Outcome Measures

Introduction

SLE is a heterogeneous autoimmune disease1 with potential involvement of the central and peripheral nervous systems, collectively termed neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE). NPSLE encompasses a broad spectrum of neurological and psychiatric manifestations, among which objectively measurable cognitive dysfunction is one of the most frequently reported features.2 This dysfunction typically affects domains such as memory, attention, processing speed and executive function.3

In parallel, individuals with SLE frequently report subjective cognitive symptoms that impact daily functioning and quality of life.4 These patient-reported experiences are not restricted to those with NPSLE and do not consistently correlate with objective measures of cognitive dysfunction. Among these symptoms, ‘brain fog’ has emerged as a common and clinically relevant concern,5 6 often described as mental clouding, slowed thinking or reduced clarity of thought. Despite its apparent frequency, brain fog remains poorly characterised, with no universally accepted definition or standardised assessment approach.

Efforts to link subjective cognitive symptoms, including brain fog, with objective cognitive impairment or markers of central nervous system involvement7 have produced inconsistent results.8 This dissociation underscores the difficulty of capturing patient-perceived cognitive difficulties using conventional clinical or neuropsychological tools.

The Lupus Brain Fog Severity Scale (LBFSS) study was therefore designed to address this unmet need by developing a self-administered, patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) specifically aimed at quantifying the severity of subjective cognitive symptoms in individuals with SLE. Such a tool, if shown to be reliable and valid, could enhance clinical care by improving the recognition and longitudinal monitoring of cognitive issues, while also providing a standardised outcome measure for research, including clinical trials.

Methods

Study design

The LBFSS study was conducted over a 12-month period and followed a five-step mixed-methods approach to develop and validate a self-administered PROM targeting perceived cognitive symptoms in SLE. The process combined sequential input from patients and healthcare professionals (HCPs) and included domain generation, domain prioritisation, item development, cognitive debriefing and psychometric evaluation.

Steering committee and participants

The study was guided by an international steering committee comprising 21 physicians with expertise in SLE and neuropsychiatric involvement, alongside a neurologist with specialisation in cognitive disorders, a neuropsychologist, a clinical psychologist, a methodologist and three patient representatives affiliated with Lupus Europe and Agrupación Lupus Chile. Across the different phases, participants with a self-reported, medically-confirmed diagnosis of SLE were recruited through social media and patient organisations.

Development and validation process

The five-step methodology underpinning the LBFSS (figure 1) was largely based on the European Reference Network (ERN) ReCONNET ELICIT framework, developed within the European Reference Networks to address the issue of poorly defined constructs in rare and complex connective tissue diseases. This approach relies on broad stakeholder engagement, initial crowdsourcing of concepts, structured thematic analysis and iterative consensus-building. In the present study, the first two steps focused on identifying and prioritising domains reflecting the patient experience of ‘brain fog’ and cognitive symptoms, whereas subsequent steps addressed LBFSS questionnaire construction and validation.

Step 1: identification of domains (patient crowdsourcing)

Patients with SLE were recruited between April and June 2025 to participate in an online crowdsourcing exercise. Participants responded to an open-ended prompt inviting them to describe all aspects of their experience of ‘brain fog’ including symptom characteristics (frequency, duration, intensity) and impact on daily life. For all subsequent analyses, only responses from participants who reported a medically confirmed diagnosis of SLE were retained. Responses were submitted in English but participants could translate their contributions from their native language when necessary, using back-translation to ensure that the translation reflected the intended meaning. Individual submissions were reviewed, with duplicate or overlapping concepts removed. The remaining content was coded and organised into domains using thematic analysis by the first author (LA), according to the ERN ReCONNET ELICIT methodology.

Step 2: domain prioritisation

A multidisciplinary panel of HCPs and patient representatives evaluated the domains identified in Step 1. Each domain was rated for relevance on a 9-point scale (1=lowest relevance; 9=highest relevance). Domains with a median score ≥7 were retained for further development.

Step 3: item generation and refinement

Retained domains were translated into questionnaire items to create a preliminary version of the LBFSS. Initial wording was drafted in English and iteratively refined with input from patient representatives and HCPs a web-based review process. Participants could endorse or suggest revisions to each item, with the aim of improving clarity, interpretability and cross-cultural applicability.

Step 4: questionnaire debriefing and feasibility

The pilot instrument was tested in an independent sample of patients with SLE from Europe and Latin America. Participants completed the questionnaire and provided structured feedback on clarity, relevance, ease of completion, response options and overall burden. Completion time was recorded. Minor revisions were made based on this feedback before finalising the instrument.

Step 5: psychometric validation

The finalised LBFSS was administered to a large international online cohort of adults with self-reported, medically confirmed SLE. Participants reported demographic characteristics, indicated whether they had experienced brain fog within the preceding 10 days and completed the LBFSS. Specifically, brain fog was assessed over the preceding 10 days to align with the timeframe used in established SLE assessment instruments, including earlier versions of the SLE Disease Activity Index.9 In addition, we aimed to keep the recall period relatively short to minimise recall bias and to capture fluctuations in symptoms that are characteristic of brain fog while remaining practical for participants completing the questionnaire.

Convergent validity was assessed using the Perceived Deficits Questionnaire-20 (PDQ-20), a validated generic self-report measure of perceived cognitive symptoms in daily functioning.10 Participants also rated brain fog severity and its impact on daily life, relationships and work or study using 0–10 numerical rating scales. These measures were used to examine construct validity and criterion-related associations.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as median and IQR (25th–75th percentile) and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the χ² test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables.

Internal consistency of the LBFSS was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and standardised Cronbach’s alpha. The dimensionality of the instrument was evaluated using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with maximum likelihood extraction applied to all 13 items. The number of factors retained was determined based on: eigenvalues >1 and scree-plot inspection. Factor loadings ≥0.40 were considered acceptable. The proportion of variance explained by the retained factor(s) was reported.

The distribution of LBFSS scores was examined to identify floor or ceiling effects. Known-groups validity was evaluated by comparing LBFSS scores between participants reporting versus not reporting brain fog. Construct validity was assessed using Spearman correlation coefficients between LBFSS scores and patient-reported brain-fog severity and its impact on daily life, relationships and work/study capacity. Convergent validity was evaluated by examining correlations between LBFSS scores and PDQ-20 scores. Criterion-related validity was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) used to quantify the ability of the LBFSS to discriminate between participants with and without brain fog during the previous 10 days. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using JMP V.13.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, USA).

Results

Thematic analysis yielded a total of 21 potential cognitive symptom domains (table 1). In step 3, each of the 13 domains retained after step 2 was transformed into a self-assessable questionnaire items for patients. A total of 23 HCPs and seven other patients (or patient representatives) provided feedback on each question of the pilot LBFSS instrument, leading to several important changes in the wording of four questions (online supplemental table 3).

In step 4, the pilot LBFSS was presented to 12 patients with lupus (online supplemental table 4), and a cognitive debriefing was conducted to gather additional insights about the instrument. Patient feedback supported the feasibility and acceptability of the pilot LBFSS, as the instrument was considered clear in its instructions, items and response scale, culturally relevant, easy to complete and of appropriate length with a median completion time of 6.5 min (IQR: 3.75–9.25). However, one participant highlighted that items 7 (‘Q7. How much have you been feeling disoriented, for example unsure of where you are or what you are doing?’) and 8 (‘How much have you been feeling as if the world around you is not real or if you are living in a dream’) may trigger psychiatric stigma in Latin American patients. Following additional discussions with patient-representatives, these two items were subsequently adjusted to as ‘How much have you felt lost in familiar places or about what you are doing?’ and ‘How often have you felt like things around you were feeling unreal, as in a dream?’, respectively. Finally, the HCP panel unanimously confirmed the content validity of the final LBFSS (table 3), judging the instrument to be clear, easy to understand, culturally relevant and appropriate for assessing brain fog in patients with SLE, with items expected to be easily answerable by patients.

The LBFSS demonstrated excellent internal consistency reliability, with both Cronbach’s alpha and standardised Cronbach’s alpha equal to 0.95. Item response distributions across the five scale modalities for each of the 13 LBFSS items during the validation phase supported appropriate use of the full response range (online supplemental table 6). EFA indicated a single dominant factor, with an eigenvalue >1, supporting the unidimensionality of the 13-item LBFSS. All items loaded strongly (≥ 0.60) on this factor, which explained 62.2% of the total variance.

The distribution of LBFSS scores showed no evident floor or ceiling effects (figure 2). The median score was 21 (IQR: 12–30), with a mean (±SD) of 21.4 (±11.6), covering the entire theoretical LBFSS range from 0 to 52.

Distribution of LBFSS scores in total participants (n=378), as well as in those with (n=271) and without brain fog (n=107) during the previous 10 days. LBFSS, Lupus Brain Fog Severity Scale.

Known-groups validity was supported by significantly higher LBFSS scores among participants reporting brain fog (median: 25 (IQR: 17–33)), compared with those without brain fog (median: 10 (IQR: 3–17); p<0.0001). The limited overlap between the distributions further highlights the LBFSS ability to discriminate between participants with and without brain fog.

Construct validity was further supported by moderate to strong correlations (figure 3) between LBFSS scores and self-reported impact of brain fog on daily life (Spearman’s ρ=0.55, p<0.0001), personal relationships (ρ=0.53, p<0.0001) and ability to study or work (ρ=0.46, p<0.0001), as well as a strongly significant correlation with brain fog severity over the preceding 10 days (ρ=0.65, p<0.0001) as hypothesised.

Convergent validity was demonstrated by a very strong correlation between LBFSS scores and the PDQ-20 (ρ=0.84, p<0.0001), a widely validated generic measure of perceived cognitive symptoms. Criterion-related validity was further supported by comparable discriminative performance of the LBFSS and the PDQ-20 for identifying presence versus absence of brain fog, as self-reported by patients, with similar areas under the ROC curve (AUCLBFSS=0.85; 20 AUCPDQ-20=0.84).

Discussion

The LBFSS study was designed to clarify how patients with SLE describe ‘brain fog’ and related cognitive symptoms with the goal to develop a patient-centred, self-administered instrument capable of quantifying the severity of cognitive symptoms in SLE. Importantly and as such, the LBFSS is intended to assess subjective cognitive experience and to complement, rather than substitute for, objective neuropsychological assessment, which is expected to be largely uncorrelated.

The initial crowdsourcing phase yielded a wide range of patient descriptions, underscoring the heterogeneity of cognitive symptoms in SLE. Thematic analysis allowed these narratives to be organised into broader domains, highlighting the multidimensional nature of the construct. Subsequent prioritisation by both HCPs and patient representatives showed substantial agreement, supporting the relevance and clinical credibility of the selected domains.

Translation of these domains into questionnaire items was iterative and informed by both patient and clinician input. Revisions primarily aimed to improve clarity and interpretability. The choice of a 10-day recall period was guided by consistency with existing SLE outcome measures,9 while limiting recall bias in a population where memory and attention may be affected.

Cognitive debriefing confirmed that the instrument was understandable, acceptable and feasible across different cultural settings. Wording adjustments were introduced, particularly to address concerns related to psychiatric connotations in Latin American participants. These refinements, grounded in direct patient feedback, strengthened content and face validity prior to formal testing. Final review by the clinical panel further supported the relevance and clarity of the instrument.

Psychometric evaluation provided consistent evidence supporting the validity of the LBFSS. Internal consistency was high, and response distributions indicated appropriate use of the full scoring range, with negligible floor or ceiling effects. Factor analysis identified a single dominant dimension accounting for a substantial proportion of variance, supporting the use of a global summary score. Together, these findings suggest that the LBFSS captures a coherent underlying construct of perceived cognitive dysfunction.

Construct validity was supported by significant associations between LBFSS scores and patient-reported impact on daily functioning, interpersonal relationships and work or academic activities, as well as with overall brain fog severity. Known-groups validity was demonstrated by higher scores among participants reporting brain fog. Notably, score variability among those not explicitly reporting brain fog suggests that the instrument captures a broader spectrum of cognitive symptoms, a finding consistent with its unidimensional structure. Convergent validity was reinforced by a strong correlation with the PDQ-20, a widely used generic measure of perceived cognitive deficits.

The LBFSS also showed comparable discriminative performance to the PDQ-20 in identifying brain fog, despite comprising substantially fewer items.10 This shorter format may reduce respondent burden while preserving measurement performance, which is particularly relevant for clinical studies and routine care.

Beyond its measurement properties, the LBFSS is expected to have practical implications for both clinical care and research. In routine practice, cognitive issues in SLE are often under-recognised or insufficiently explored, despite their impact on daily functioning.5 6 The LBFSS is primarily intended to quantify the severity and diversity of cognitive symptoms, rather than to serve as a diagnostic tool for brain fog. In routine clinical practice, it could be used to systematically assess cognitive symptom burden in patients with SLE who report brain fog or cognitive difficulties. This structured approach may facilitate more focused discussions between patients and clinicians. This may help address a gap in care, where cognitive difficulties are frequently reported by patients but are not always fully taken into account or systematically assessed by clinicians.6 By providing a quantitative score, the LBFSS may serve as a first-line screening tool to identify patients who would benefit from targeted supportive measures, while the utility of systematic screening for cognitive symptoms in all patients with lupus remains to be further defined. Future research should explore how incorporating the LBFSS into routine care could inform clinical decision-making, identify patients who may benefit from comprehensive neurocognitive assessment and ultimately improve patient outcomes. In the research setting, the LBFSS offers a standardised approach to capturing patient-reported cognitive symptoms, an area that remains insufficiently addressed in SLE studies. Its relatively short format may facilitate inclusion in observational cohorts and clinical trials without substantially increasing participant burden. The availability of a disease-specific, patient-centred measure may also improve the assessment of treatment effects on cognitive outcomes, which are rarely included as primary or secondary endpoints. More broadly, its use could contribute to a better understanding of the determinants and trajectories of cognitive symptoms in SLE.

Several limitations should be considered. Psychological factors such as anxiety and depression may influence cognitive symptoms which may also overlap with those observed in comorbid conditions such as fibromyalgia. Importantly, the LBFSS is a PROM designed to capture cognitive symptoms in patients with SLE, irrespective of aetiology. Further work is ongoing to better understand the role of contributing factors, together with the association between subjective cognitive symptoms and cognitive dysfunction. Recruitment through online platforms and patient organisations may introduce selection bias, although only participants reporting medically-confirmed SLE were included in the study, and therefore no formal disease activity measure was available. Although participants to the validation step of the LBFSS represented a range of geographical regions, including Europe, Africa, Latin America, the Caribbean and Asia, additional validation in larger and more ethnically diverse populations would further strengthen the assessment of the scale’s cross-cultural applicability and generalisability. The instrument was developed in English, and while the study population was geographically diverse, formal cross-cultural adaptation will be required for broader implementation. Finally, test-retest reliability and responsiveness were not evaluated in this study and should be addressed in future studies, particularly in longitudinal and interventional settings.

In conclusion, the LBFSS is a PROM developed to assess brain fog and related cognitive symptoms in SLE. Its development followed a structured, multistep process integrating patient experience and clinical expertise. The instrument demonstrates good feasibility, strong internal consistency and multiple forms of validity. By capturing both brain fog and wider cognitive symptoms, it reflects the complexity of patient-reported cognitive symptoms in SLE. Further evaluation of reliability and sensitivity to change will help define its role in clinical practice and research.
