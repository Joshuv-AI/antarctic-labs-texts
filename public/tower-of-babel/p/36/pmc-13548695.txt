Cross-lagged analysis of physical exercise, personal sense of mastery, and involution-lying flat mentality among Chinese youth
Authors: Qifei Xia, Yanmao Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground“Involution” and “lying flat” are typical societal mindsets among Chinese youth under pressure and resource scarcity, corresponding to “excessive competition” and “passive withdrawal” respectively. However, longitudinal research on the temporal causality and underlying mechanisms between these mindsets and physical exercise (as an adaptive coping mechanism) is lacking.MethodsThis study adopted an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. Quantitatively, based on COR theory and SDT, a one-year, three-wave longitudinal survey was conducted with 492 youth (20–35 years old) from Shaanxi, Hubei, and Sichuan provinces, constructing a cross-lagged model (CLPM) to examine the interrelations between involution perceptions, lying flat behavior, physical exercise, and Personal Sense of Mastery. Qualitatively, semi-structured interviews with youth of different occupations revealed the generative logic of causal mechanisms.ResultsResults showed that physical exercise significantly buffered involution, activated "lying flat" groups, and had bidirectional negative predictive effects with both mindsets. Personal Sense of Mastery played a mediating role, with early physical exercise predicting later involution and lying flat through it. Qualitative findings corroborated this and indicated occupational differences in mechanisms.ConclusionsThis study highlights physical exercise’s long-term impact on youth mindsets, offering insights for context-specific interventions. Future research should explore causal boundary conditions to optimize targeted exercise interventions.Keywords: Physical exercise, Perception of "involution", "Lying flat" behavior, Personal sense of mastery, Social mentality

Introduction

The rapid development of the digital economy and the continuous intensification of social pressures have created a highly complex and uncertain living environment for residents. According to the China National Mental Health Development Report (2021–2022), individuals aged 18–34 experience higher levels of anxiety, with those in the early stages of their careers facing particularly prominent competitive pressures [1]. As a crucial force for the long-term stability and development of society, young people are particularly vulnerable to falling into passive, excessive engagement behaviors under the current competitive and resource-scarce environment. These behaviors include forced studying, staying up late for overtime work, and other unhealthy habits [2]. Such behaviors can trigger a range of mental health issues such as fear, anxiety, premature aging, and even lead to extreme psychological crises like suicidal tendencies [3, 4]. On the other hand, some individuals, overwhelmed by competition, resort to “lying flat” behavior, which involves avoiding the pressure by giving up goals, unwilling to put in effort, and lowering expectations as a form of withdrawal, which over time leads to a state of confusion, uncertainty, and weakened psychological drive [5]. Against this backdrop, the terms “involution” and “lying flat” have emerged as key indicators to describe the social mentality of young people, attracting attention and discussion from scholars across various disciplines.

In recent years, the rapid iteration and upgrading of online media technologies have given rise to a series of internet buzzwords reflecting social realities, such as “working people,” “cattle and horses,” and “96-hour workaholics" [6], which have quickly gained significant public influence and cultural dissemination power. “Involution” and “lying flat” have become two unique linguistic cultural symbols that have gradually expanded from the educational field to labor production, consumer behavior, and various aspects of daily life, deeply revealing the dual sides of the survival dilemmas faced by young people in contemporary society. From a sociocultural perspective, “involution” manifests as the blind pursuit of success standards and irrational competition in the context of societal structural constraints, leading to a loss of self-value [7]. “Lying flat” presents as an individual’s self-deprecating attitude, contentment with the status quo, and abandonment of future expectations, reflecting a state of psychological stagnation [8]. Both terms point to an individual’s self-diminishing survival mentality in the real-world context. From a cultural psychology perspective, excessive involution is prone to triggering individuals to adopt upward comparison strategies, thereby intensifying competitive conflicts and consuming significant psychological resources, leading to high pressure, anxiety, and other negative emotions [9]. On the other hand, the behavioral regression brought about by blind “lying flat” may avoid the loss of self-value caused by failure, but it further weakens an individual’s social adaptability and developmental motivation [10]. Currently, young people are trapped in a vicious cycle between the mental exhaustion caused by “involution” and the behavioral stagnation solidified by “lying flat.” The contradictory psychological structure presented by this cycle severely affects mental health and the ecological development of talent cultivation systems. This situation urgently requires the integration of interdisciplinary perspectives, such as positive psychology and health behavior science, to develop effective intervention pathways. Physical exercise, as a proactive, embodied daily practice, serves multiple functions, including physical and mental adjustment and social adaptation. Existing cross-sectional studies have shown that regular exercise can alleviate anxiety and depressive emotions under high pressure by releasing endorphins and improving neurotransmitter balance [11]. It can also enhance self-efficacy and improve social adaptability through psychological mechanisms that promote positive behavioral tendencies [12, 13]. However, there is a lack of research evidence on whether physical exercise can serve as an effective balancing lever between young people’s perception of “involution” and their “lying flat” behavior. The existing studies present notable limitations: first, many studies rely on cross-sectional survey designs, which can only reveal correlational relationships between variables, but cannot clarify the dynamic causal sequence between physical exercise, perception of “involution,” and “lying flat” behavior; second, they neglect the potential bidirectional relationship and phase fluctuations between physical exercise and “anti-involution” or “refusal to lie flat;” and third, they lack a systematic theoretical framework to deeply explore the internal pathways of action, as the existing studies only stay at the statistical correlation level and do not fully investigate the career-contextualized differences in influencing pathways.

Based on this, this study attempts to adopt an explanatory sequential mixed design. By constructing a cross-lagged model and complementing it with semi-structured tracking interviews, it focuses on examining the bidirectional predictive relationship between physical exercise and involution perception, as well as the lying flat behavior, analyzing the temporal causal relationship, clarifying the core mechanism and contextual boundaries, and providing theoretical support and practical references for resolving the youth’s dilemma of internal competition and lying-back.

Literature review and research hypotheses

The conceptual connotations of “involution” and “lying flat”

The term “involution” has rich theoretical connotations in fields such as social science, social psychology, and cultural economics. Its etymology traces back to the Latin “Involutum,” meaning “to roll or wind up,” symbolizing a state of entanglement, complexity, and chaos. Anthropologist Geertz introduced the concept into agricultural economics, where, within traditional farming systems, increasing inputs and expanding production scale led to refined cultivation but also to diminishing marginal returns, forming the “involution trap" [14]. As societal structures evolved, the concept of involution extended beyond economic history and became a generalized term for describing social competition. From a theoretical standpoint, COR theory posits that individual psychological adaptation is a dynamic balance between resource acquisition and consumption [15]. The core of involution lies in the irrational competition where individuals and organizations continuously consume time and resources within a limited domain. This is characterized by three main features: first, the homogenization of competitive goals, where individuals shift focus to internal competition to surpass others with shared objectives, such as outperforming peers in performance competitions. Second, an imbalance between resource consumption and returns, seen in the continuous and meaningless expenditure of time, energy, and money, such as the overtime culture in the “996” system. Third, a group-driven environment, where the emotions, attitudes, and behaviors of peer groups influence individual psychological states, leading to passive involvement in involutionary behavior, as seen in the collective overtime culture within companies.

The term “lying flat” has evolved from a viral internet slang into a significant social-cultural phenomenon, characterized by its unique symbolic features and underlying philosophical meanings [16]. It typically refers to the psychological state experienced by adolescents when the psychological pressure they endure exceeds a threshold, leading them to forgo effort, resist progress, and feel powerless against reality. Originating from expressions like “emptying the body” and “complete rest” in online platforms, this cultural form reflects the collective mental fatigue in the fast-paced societal structure. As a prominent manifestation of youth subculture, “lying flat” is closely associated with both Buddhist culture and funeral culture [17], promoting an attitude of “no desires, no demands” and “non-competition”, thus highlighting a culture and set of values that are passive and escapist. It is crucial to distinguish “lying flat” from Western notions like “slow living” and “cynical culture,” as it reflects a rational withdrawal from social competition among youth, adopting retreat, concealment, and self-reconciliation as responses to future life [18]. This form of escapist resistance reveals the hedonism and defiance of contemporary youth [8]. From a theoretical perspective, self-determination theory posits that the social environment maintains psychological balance through the fulfillment of three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness [19]. “Lying flat” thus represents an adaptive adjustment made when these needs are blocked, with a strategic element of resistance. In this context, the “involution” prevalent in Chinese society weakens the achievement motivation of youth, manifesting in passivity, confusion, and contentment with the status quo. Addressing this social dilemma requires effective intervention mechanisms at both the psychological and institutional levels.

Physical exercise, involution perception, and the lying flat state

In the current socio-cultural context of China, “involution” is defined as an irrational competitive state where individuals or groups exert continuous effort but achieve no meaningful outcomes due to overly homogeneous values, goals, and behavioral patterns [20]. In the workplace, when individuals perceive high scarcity of resources, consistent competitive behavior, strong organizational norms, and substantial psychological pressure, they develop a heightened perception of systemic “involution" [21]. From the perspective of resource conservation theory, this can be understood as an over-investment in competition under high pressure—a “resource trap” where the marginal returns on time and energy continuously decrease. This results in the depletion of key resources and negative psychological states such as a sense of unfairness, anxiety, and stress [22, 23]. Physical exercise, as an effective means of supplementing physical and mental health resources and shaping core competitive advantages, may play a crucial role in buffering involution perception. On one hand, exercise triggers neurochemical and physiological changes in the brain, alleviating anxiety and depression caused by resource depletion, while providing a sense of achievement and self-efficacy through goal attainment, thus preventing individuals from falling into the cycle of involution [24, 25]. On the other hand, exercise is viewed as a resource investment behavior to cope with adversity, actively reinforcing self-discipline and goal orientation to eliminate ineffective competition [26], thus reducing the perception of resource depletion caused by involution. For instance, participating in leisure activities such as watching basketball games, completing rock climbing or hiking can help one gain a deeper understanding of the overall meaning of life, pursue goals with positive values, and thereby reduce the constraints imposed by the busy work [27].

Correspondingly, “lying flat” has gradually become a prominent lifestyle choice for some youth to escape societal pressure, manifesting as a disengaged, complacent, non-ambitious, and low-desire mental state in the workplace or other competitive environments [16]. As the pace of life accelerates, social competition intensifies, and the pressure from information overload increases, prolonged lying flat can lead to the loss of value-driven ideals [28], weakened physical function [8], reduced work efficiency [29], and ultimately a sense of life’s meaninglessness [30]. Physical exercise may serve as a preventive mechanism to reduce or substitute lying flat behaviors. Under the framework of self-determination theory, regular participation in physical exercise is a primary means of fulfilling basic needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness: First, various types of sports, their settings, intensity, and content can satisfy the need for autonomy [31], thus reducing the likelihood of lying flat behavior; second, the competitive interaction between exercise partners or improvement in athletic skills continuously strengthens achievement motivation, counteracting the tendency towards lying flat caused by a loss of value [32, 33]; third, sports community activities provide a sense of belonging and recognition, promoting sustained positive social participation [34]. Based on these theoretical insights and literature, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1a: Physical exercise negatively predicts youth’s perception of involution.

H1a: Physical exercise negatively predicts youth’s perception of involution.

H1b: Physical exercise negatively predicts youth’s lying flat behavior.

Mediating role of personal sense of mastery

Personal Sense of Mastery refers to an individual’s perception and confidence in their life, work, and decision-making, encompassing personal ability, autonomy, and self-determination [35]. It is a crucial psychological resource for coping with negative situations. As a core psychological mechanism linking positive behavior and psychological adaptation, it may play an important mediating role in perceptions of involution. Youth actively engaged in physical exercise tend to manage time and resources more effectively, receiving continuous positive feedback [36], while individuals with a high sense of control are better able to reduce the mental exhaustion and burnout induced by competitive environments through effort and strategic adjustment, even in challenging situations such as the COVID-19 pandemic [37, 38]. In contrast, the self-control model highlights that the capacity for self-regulation is limited and requires time to recover [39]. Individuals with lower control may be more prone to learned helplessness, excessively depleting their self-control resources in involutionary environments, leading to higher psychological stress and self-doubt [40]. Regarding lying flat behaviors, “lying flat” is often viewed as a negative form of resistance when individuals fail to adapt to social competition, accompanied by feelings of frustration and powerlessness after goal obstruction [41]. According to self-determination theory, when basic psychological needs are unmet, motivation internalization and psychological health issues emerge [19]. As a key reflection of competence and autonomy, Personal Sense of Mastery may significantly inhibit lying flat behavior. Existing studies have preliminarily confirmed a bidirectional negative predictive relationship between basic psychological needs and lying flat behavior, demonstrating the negative impact of lying flat on mental health [42]. A survey of Chinese university teachers suggests that those with a higher sense of control are more satisfied with their life and relationships and are better at maintaining positive motivation and emotional states through self-regulation, thus enhancing overall psychological well-being [43]. Based on this, the theoretical framework of the study is constructed (see Fig. 1), and the following hypotheses are proposed:

H2a: Personal Sense of Mastery mediates the relationship between physical exercise and involution perception.

H2a: Personal Sense of Mastery mediates the relationship between physical exercise and involution perception.

H2b: Personal Sense of Mastery mediates the relationship between physical exercise and lying flat behavior.

H2b: Personal Sense of Mastery mediates the relationship between physical exercise and lying flat behavior.

Research Design Framework

Research design

This study employed an interpretive sequential mixed-methods design [44, 45], which involved conducting a quantitative phase first and then a qualitative phase, using qualitative data to explain and deepen the quantitative findings. Specifically, the quantitative phase constructed a cross-lagged model (CLPM) using three waves of longitudinal data to examine the temporal causal relationships and mediating effects between physical exercise, personal sense of control, involution perception, and lying flat behavior. The qualitative part had three purposes: first, to provide a mechanistic explanation by using the participants’ own words to illustrate how physical exercise reshapes their sense of control and reduces internalization pressure or laid-back tendencies; second, to capture the contextual differences in different occupations, life stages, and work environments, which are difficult to quantify in large-scale surveys; third, to triangulate the quantitative results and verify whether the directional paths identified in the CLPM are consistent with the actual life experiences of the participants. The qualitative sample was selected using extreme case sampling and quota sampling, with intentional extraction from the same longitudinal cohort to ensure that the interview data were directly related to the quantitative model. This mixed-method design not only enhanced the credibility of causal inferences but also provided operational insights for formulating intervention plans for different occupational groups.

Research methods 1: quantitative research phase

Measurement objects

Convenience sampling was used to select youth populations from three provinces in China for a longitudinal survey. To minimize selection bias, seed samples with maximal differences were selected based on the principle of maximizing heterogeneity, ensuring broad variations in key dimensions such as geographic distribution, gender, and occupation. The recruitment channels specifically include: posting recruitment posters in several communities or streets in Shaanxi Province, Hubei Province and Sichuan Province, releasing survey information through the WeChat public platform and local community forums, and at the same time using the snowball sampling method, where the participating young people recommend their peers to expand the sample coverage. The study focused on youth aged 20 to 35, conducting three consecutive rounds of surveys at one-year intervals. On one hand, the Chinese State Council’s “Medium and Long-term Youth Development Plan (2016–2035)” defines the youth age range as 14–35 years [46]. On the other hand, the 20–35 age group represents the core period of career development and family formation, characterized by similar societal backgrounds, education levels, and interests, aligning with the study’s defined scope. The first survey (T1) was conducted in August 2024, with 511 valid respondents. The second survey (T2) took place in February 2025, with 502 valid respondents due to some individuals being excluded for reasons such as illness, job changes, or refusal to participate. The sample size remained balanced. The third survey (T3) was conducted in July 2025, with 10 respondents lost, resulting in 492 valid respondents completing all three rounds (a 3.72% attrition rate). It should be noted that, as this study adopted convenience sampling and mainly relied on the research conducted by the research group in local universities in three provinces, we did not conduct stratified statistics on the sample size of each province, the distribution of cities, or the urban-rural household registration. We also did not record the exact number of people in each specific occupation type (such as university teachers, enterprise employees, freelancers, etc.). Due to the small sample size of some occupation types, no separate proportion statistics were made. During the survey, strict procedures were followed to ensure confidentiality. Participants were asked for their consent before the survey, and those who agreed signed an informed consent form. After each survey round, participants received small gifts (e.g., figurines or daily necessities) to ensure the research’s rationality and standardization. Of the participants, 231 were male (53%) and 261 were female (47%), with an average age of 25.77 (SD = 3.84). Of the participants, 442 (89.8%) were employed, and 50 (10.2%) were not. Chi-square tests showed no significant differences between the lost participants and those who completed all three surveys in terms of gender and employment status (p > 0.05) see Fig. 2.

Survey Time Periods

Physical activity level scale

This study uses the Physical Activity Level Scale revised by Liang Deqing and colleagues from Wuhan Sports University to measure the level of physical activity participation in the youth population [47]. The scale assesses an individual’s physical activity based on three dimensions: exercise intensity, duration, and frequency. The scale focuses on the objective assessment of exercise volume and does not include specific aspects of physical activity, such as the type of activity (e.g., team sports or individual exercise), regularity of the activity, or social interactions involved. Therefore, the “physical exercise” measured by the scale primarily reflects the cumulative effects of exercise intensity, frequency, and duration, rather than the influence of specific forms of exercise. The total score for physical exercise volume is calculated using the formula: “exercise intensity × (exercise duration − 1) × exercise frequency,” with a maximum score of 100 and a minimum of 0. A score below 19 is considered low physical activity, a score between 20 and 42 is considered moderate physical activity, and a score above 43 is considered high physical activity.

Personal sense of mastery scale

This scale, developed by Pearlin et al. [48], primarily assesses an individual’s perception of control over life events. The questionnaire consists of a single dimension with 7 items, scored using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly disagree” to “Strongly agree,” with scores from 1 to 5. Some of the items in the questionnaire include “I am unable to solve problems I encounter in life” and “My future will depend largely on myself.” Five of the items are reverse scored. The total score ranges from 7 to 35, with higher scores indicating a stronger sense of control over life events.

Involution perception scale

This scale, developed by Chinese scholars Zhang Wen et al. [20], comprehensively reflects a multidimensional psychological construct that integrates individual psychology, group behavior, culture, and social ecology, and effectively captures the characteristic dimensions of involution in Chinese society. The questionnaire consists of 18 items, covering four dimensions: resource scarcity (e.g., “There are too few resources in my environment for me to get the rewards I deserve”), social norms (e.g., “Most people around me continue to do more work even after meeting the minimum task requirements”), psychological pressure (e.g., “I feel frustrated in learning/work”), and competitive behavior (e.g., “People around me gain recognition through competition”). It uses a 5-point scale, ranging from “Strongly disagree” to “Strongly agree,” with higher scores indicating a stronger perception of involution.

Lying flat scale

The “lying flat” scale, developed by Chinese scholar Li Shuai and colleagues [49], was designed based on the characteristics of the youth population, incorporating interview methods and content analysis to measure “lying flat” behaviors. The scale consists of 12 items, divided into two sub-dimensions: “awakened lying flat” and “escape lying flat.” Sample items include: “My family can support me, so I choose not to work” and “I can’t afford a house, so I won’t buy one; renting is fine for a lifetime.” Responses are rated using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree.” The overall score ranges from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating a more pronounced degree of “lying flat.”

Data analysis

This study was approved by the Sports School of Ankang University. Data collection was conducted with the consent of participants, and the surveys were administered by trained experimenters. Prior to the survey, all participants signed informed consent forms. A standardized procedure was followed, with surveys distributed, completed, and collected on-site. Each session lasted between 15 and 20 min, and the procedure remained consistent across all three testing sessions. After completion, each respondent was assigned a unique identification number, and confidentiality was assured to protect anonymity, with the data being used exclusively for academic research to alleviate participant concerns.Data were organized, descriptively analyzed, and correlated using SPSS 27.0 and Python 3.11. Additionally, a cross-lagged model was constructed using AMOS 24.0 to examine the interactions between physical exercise, Personal Sense of Mastery, and perceptions of “involution”/passive resignation at three time points, as well as to test the longitudinal mediating role of Personal Sense of Mastery. The model evaluated autoregressive paths, cross-lagged paths, correlations between latent variables at the same time points, and measurement errors of the same observed variables across different time points.

Common method bias test and descriptive statistics

Due to objective constraints, the data in this study were primarily collected through self-reports, which could potentially introduce common method bias. To address this, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to examine common method bias across the data from three time points [50]. The results revealed that in all three measurements, the number of factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 was three. The variance explained by the first factor was 39.254%, 39.927%, and 39.241%, all below the 40% threshold, and the maximum variance explained by each factor was 54.265%, 55.008%, and 53.917%, respectively. These results suggest that there is no significant common method bias in the data from the three measurements. Correlation Analysis of Variables

Longitudinal measurement invariance test

A multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) within the structural equation modeling (SEM) framework was employed to test the measurement invariance across time points, ensuring that the variables had the same meaning at different time intervals. This study sequentially tested and compared three nested invariance models: (1) configural invariance, where parameters were freely estimated; (2) metric invariance, where factor loadings were constrained to be equal across time points; and (3) scalar invariance, where both factor loadings and intercepts were constrained to be equal. In psychometric research, strong invariance is required when the objective is to compare group differences in variable scores, whereas weak invariance is sufficient when only the relationships between variables are explored. The results in Table 2 show that the fit indices for all models met the requirements for weak invariance, with changes in the CFI being less than 0.01 and changes in RMSEA being less than 0.015, thus satisfying the necessary conditions for constructing the cross-lagged model.

Cross-lagged analysis of physical exercise and perceptions of ‘involution’/lying flat behavior

To investigate the directionality of the relationship between different types of physical exercise and perceptions of “involution,” a cross-lagged model was constructed based on the results of the correlation analysis. As shown in Fig. 4, the overall fit indices of the model were satisfactory: χ²/df = 3.905, NFI = 0.929, IFI = 0.947, CFI = 0.942, SRMR = 0.033, and RMSEA = 0.077. The autoregressive effects revealed that physical exercise at time Tn significantly predicted physical exercise at Tn + 1, and perceptions of “involution” at Tn significantly predicted perceptions of “involution” at Tn + 1, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Table 3 reports the cross-lagged model results for the relationship between physical exercise and perceptions of “involution.” Specifically, physical exercise at T1 significantly positively predicted physical exercise at T2 (β = 0.13, p < 0.01), and physical exercise at T2 significantly positively predicted physical exercise at T3 (β = 0.15, p < 0.001). Similarly, perceptions of “involution” at T1 significantly positively predicted perceptions of “involution” at T2 (β = 0.21, p < 0.001), and perceptions of “involution” at T2 significantly positively predicted perceptions of “involution” at T3 (β = 0.16, p < 0.001). The cross-lagged effects showed that physical exercise at T1 significantly negatively predicted perceptions of “involution” at T2 (β = -0.18, p < 0.001), and physical exercise at T2 significantly negatively predicted perceptions of “involution” at T3 (β = -0.12, p < 0.01). Conversely, perceptions of “involution” at T1 significantly negatively predicted physical exercise at T2 (β = -0.13, p < 0.01), and perceptions of “involution” at T2 significantly negatively predicted physical exercise at T3 (β = -0.13, p < 0.01).

Research design 2: qualitative research phase

Data collection

While quantitative analysis can effectively reveal causal relationships between constructs, it often fails to delve into the underlying substantive causes driving these mechanisms. To address this gap, the study recruited young adults from different occupational backgrounds for follow-up interviews after completing a questionnaire survey (see Table 7). The selected sample represented a range of professions with varying levels of intensity and work patterns. The specific sampling strategy is as follows: We adopted the extreme case sampling method in purposive sampling, based on the results of the three waves of quantitative research. From the 492 valid samples, we selected three categories: high physical exercise - low internal competition - low laid-back (4 people), low physical exercise - high internal competition - high laid-back (4 people), and medium level with representative occupational types (4 people). At the same time, in order to make the occupational structure of 12 people roughly correspond to the distribution of occupational categories of the employed individuals in the quantitative sample (based on the rough statistics at the time of recruitment, the quantitative sample mainly consisted of 40% from universities/enterprises, 35% from private enterprises, and 25% from freelancing/industries), we selected 4 university/enterprise personnel, 4 employees from private enterprises (including technical research, new media operation, internet programer), and 4 freelancers/industries personnel (including delivery riders, restaurant operators, short video producers, etc.) in proportion. The interview process was divided into three main steps: First, after obtaining informed consent from the participants, an interview guide was designed based on basic demographic information and job roles to guide the conversation. Second, using prompts, participants were asked about the frequency, type, and intensity of physical exercise they had engaged in over the past year, as well as their perceptions of involution and lying flat behavior before and after exercise. Third, the study introduced a life event assessment to evaluate participants’ overall sense of control over their lives during physical activity, analyzing the behavioral justifications for ‘involution’ and ‘perception’ to confirm and deepen the causal relationships between constructs.The interview data were collected in January 2025, three months after Study 1, and included 12 individual interviews (P1-P12), each lasting approximately 30 minutes. The interviews were conducted both online and offline, with audio recording permitted where applicable. At the end of each interview, participants were given a small token of appreciation for their support and contribution to the study. To ensure consistency between online and offline interviews, the following measures were taken in this study: First, the same semi-structured interview outline was used both online and offline, with the question sequence and prompts remaining consistent, and the questioning style and emotional response strategies did not change due to different media; Second, all interviews were conducted by the same researcher who had received unified training. This researcher maintained the same speaking speed, questioning style and emotional response strategy both online and offline; Third, offline interviews were held in quiet meeting rooms, and online interviews were conducted through online video communication to ensure focus and interaction quality; Fourth, all interviews were recorded throughout, with professional audio recorders used offline and the built-in recording function of the software used online. After completion, the text was transcribed according to the same format and annotation rules; Fifth, during the initial data analysis stage, we compared the response distributions of online (n = 5) and offline (n = 7) respondents on key themes (such as exercise frequency, changes in the feeling of internal competition, description of control sense), and did not find any systematic differences due to the interview method, indicating that the online and offline data are comparable and integratable.

Interview data analysis

This study employed the thematic summarization and counting method to analyze the interview data. This method is applicable to small sample size, qualitative research aimed at verifying and supplementing quantitative findings [51]. Since the core task of the qualitative part is not to construct new theories but to provide contextual evidence for the identified causal paths in the quantitative cross-lagged model, we adopted a concise and transparent analytical strategy: only numbering the respondents (P1-P12), directly classifying the transcripts by themes, and counting the number of respondents who mentioned each theme. The specific analysis steps are as follows: The first step is extracting the transcripts and conducting preliminary classification. The first researcher (with a background in psychology) reads through all 12 interview transcriptions, marking the original sentences related to the core constructs. At the same time, according to the path relationships in the quantitative model, the marked sentences are respectively classified into corresponding path categories. For sentences that involve multiple constructs simultaneously, they are classified into the most relevant path based on their main meaning. The second step is counting the number of respondents who explicitly support the relationship on each path (based on a 12-person base). For example, when a respondent explicitly states “After exercising, inner turmoil anxiety decreased” or a similar meaning, it is counted as a supporter of the “Physical exercise → Inner turmoil perception” path. Some respondents may provide multiple sentences on one path, but each person is counted only once per path to avoid double counting. The third step is independent reclassification and consistency check. To test the consistency of classification, the second researcher (also with a background in psychology) starts from a state where they have not read the interview transcripts, independently completing the above two steps (extracting, classifying, counting). The two researchers then compare their results, with the main discrepancies mainly concentrated on whether individual sentences are simultaneously attributed to two adjacent paths. After discussion, consensus is reached based on the core meaning of the sentences. The final version of all classifications is jointly confirmed by the two researchers. The fourth step is integration and presentation. Based on the classification and statistical results, Table 8 (representative interview transcripts) clearly lists examples of each key cross-lagged path and the number of respondents who support that path. It should be noted that this study did not conduct traditional open coding, axial coding, or selective coding, nor did it use software such as NVivo for multi-level abstract coding, as the purpose of this stage was not theoretical construction but to provide traceable contextual explanations for quantitative results.

Research results

The findings from the study and the interview data (see Table 8) indicate that, on one hand, the qualitative analysis corroborates the conclusions from the quantitative research, providing more comprehensive contextual evidence. The study confirmed the logical mechanism of physical exercise → Personal Sense of Mastery → perceptions of “involution”/lying flat behavior. The transcript analysis highlighted that youth across various occupational types enhance their sense of control over life through participation in physical exercise. This sense of control plays a balancing role between perceptions of “involution” and lying flat behavior. On the other hand, qualitative research further explored the cross-lagged mechanisms identified in the quantitative study, providing deeper insights into the underlying causes. Respondents generally believed that prolonged engagement in “involution” or lying flat behavior weakened their sense of control over life, which in turn affected their participation in physical exercise. In summary, based on the feedback from 12 interviewees, the results of the qualitative research provided a preliminary contextual explanation for the quantitative research conclusions, and suggested that there might be structural differences in the mechanisms through which different career types of young people exercise, but it should be noted that this difference is only based on a small sample observation and requires larger-scale qualitative research for verification. the qualitative results offer essential occupational context analysis, enriching the quantitative findings and providing a more comprehensive view of the complex mechanism through which physical exercise acts as a long-term strategy between perceptions of “involution” and lying flat behavior.

Discussion

Advancements in digital technologies and industrial upgrades are accelerating profound changes in social production methods. In this resource-constrained and highly competitive environment, youth exhibit two typical social attitudes: excessive internalized stress and passive resignation, both of which hinder societal progress. Effective intervention strategies are crucial for addressing these challenges. This study integrates three waves of longitudinal data with semi-structured interviews in a mixed-methods design, constructing a cross-lagged model to examine the dynamic relationships between physical exercise, perceptions of “involution,” lying flat behavior, and Personal Sense of Mastery. It offers preliminary insights into causal relationships among these variables and analyzes career structure differences, providing empirical support and policy recommendations for government and labor market agencies.

The core finding of this study indicates that physical exercise has a bidirectional negative predictive effect on perceptions of “involution” and lying flat behavior across time periods, which has significant theoretical implications. Specifically, physical exercise, as a central intervention mechanism, not only effectively reduces youth participation in meaningless competition but also prevents the decline in expectations and loss of value associated with prolonged passive resignation. More importantly, this study is the first to clarify the bidirectional causal relationships between these variables, challenging traditional views on how positive behaviors influence social mindsets and establishing a dynamic model of behavior-mindset-behavior. Previous research on “involution” and passive resignation often used cross-sectional designs or single quantitative/qualitative methods, which are limited in revealing temporal causality and underlying mechanisms [7, 8]. This study, through a mixed-methods design, overcomes the core limitation of cross-sectional research, which fails to distinguish causality. From a quantitative perspective, the cross-lagged model confirms the bidirectional causal relationships in key pathways, aligning with the core logic of COR and SDT theories.In the specific socio-cultural context of China, young professionals face significant life burdens, including career planning, marriage, and elder care, which inevitably drive them to compete for scarce resources such as quality education, promotion opportunities, and social recognition, thus falling into irrational competition. This results in disregard for physical costs, blind effort, and emotional outbursts. In this regard, the resource gain spiral shows that individuals with sufficient initial resources are more likely to efficiently acquire and integrate new resources, creating positive cumulative effects. Conversely, the resource depletion spiral model demonstrates that accelerated resource loss negatively impacts psychological stress [52]. Physical exercise provides a release for stress under “involution” pressures, acting as both a replenisher and builder of psychological resources. On one hand, regular physical activity enhances resilience to stress, improving emotional and cognitive abilities [53]. For young professionals, exercising three or more times a week at moderate to high intensity helps mitigate work-related overload, detaching from workplace pressures through skill development, fitness achievements, and team collaborations. This reduces hypersensitivity to resource demands and allows for a more rational view of competitive mechanisms. On the other hand, whether in competition or training, physical environments promote the enhancement of both physical and psychological resources through physical movement [54]. This spatial shift helps individuals temporarily escape performance-pressure-filled environments like offices and workplaces, gaining intrinsic experiences and physical sensations that reduce chronic “involution” anxiety. A technical researcher mentioned in a qualitative interview that “participating in physical exercise enhances cognitive agility,” further confirming that exercise helps individuals rationally face challenges in work and life, correctly understanding the “effort-reward” alignment. However, it must be acknowledged that “involution” is not exclusive to China. In some Western and Asian regions, youth face similar manifestations and social roots of “involution.” For instance, increased social stratification exacerbates educational disparities among youth [55], rising youth unemployment rates increase the proportion of gig economy workers [56], and corporate cultures of long working hours lead to high input-low reward situations [57]. These issues exhibit both global commonalities and regional differences. Our study emphasizes encouraging youth to adopt physical exercise as an alternative lifestyle, which can alter their physical and mental state, time perception, and sense of community, rather than becoming cogs in the corporate machine. This has policy implications not only for China but also for other regions globally.

The inhibitory effect of physical exercise on passive resignation behaviors warrants deeper investigation. According to 2024 OECD statistics, the proportion of “NEET” (Not in Education, Employment, or Training) youth exceeds 10% in most OECD member countries [58]. In Japan, some scholars describe the “Waku-zoku generation” as young people who are less interested in traditional upward mobility, instead opting for a lifestyle marked by low desires and minimalism [59]. An online survey of 240,000 individuals found that over 60% considered passive resignation (“lying flat”) the most ideal lifestyle [60]. These phenomena suggest that, in the context of demographic changes, increasing economic pressures, and growing social stratification, a significant proportion of global youth are adopting “lying flat” as a symbolic resistance strategy to gain freedom. Longitudinal evidence has shown that after one month of passive resignation, life satisfaction significantly decreases [30]. From an SDT (Self-Determination Theory) perspective, passive resignation may stem from the failure to satisfy basic psychological needs like autonomy and competence, and physical exercise provides the necessary conditions to bridge this gap. Specifically, sustained physical activity enables individuals to experience success or receive positive feedback in challenging tasks [61]. For example, one respondent (P3) mentioned that “running three times a week helps me better organize work tasks,” directly fulfilling the need for autonomy. The effects of exercise on the body depend on personal motivation and interests; participating in team sports like basketball or frisbee can help rebuild social connections based on shared interests and collective honor [62, 63]. However, passive resignation is not entirely harmful. Research indicates that “lying flat” is a new cultural expression among youth, where maintaining low desires and a clear awareness of social status and personal fate may help them better cope with future life challenges [64]. Nevertheless, our study found that physical exercise and passive resignation are bidirectionally negatively predictive. That is, youth who excessively choose to “lie flat” may compromise themselves, fall into idealistic fantasies, and weaken their motivation and willingness to engage in physical exercise. This suggests that future studies should further examine the forms and temporal cycles of lying flat behavior.

Another significant finding of this study is that Personal Sense of Mastery mediates the relationship between physical exercise and perceptions of “involution”/passive resignation across time periods. From a temporal dynamic perspective, physical exercise and Personal Sense of Mastery exhibit progressive cumulative characteristics. Evidence shows that physical exercise and mastery have bidirectional predictive effects [65], consistent with our findings. Specifically, with regular participation in physical exercise, youth experience success through skill acquisition, goal achievement, and victory in competitions, which fosters a stable belief in their ability to control their lives, behaviors, and surrounding environments. Qualitative research also supports this, suggesting that the physiological benefits derived from exercise translate into psychological resource advantages, enabling more effective management and control over work tasks and life environments.Furthermore, Personal Sense of Mastery significantly negatively predicts perceptions of “involution.” While some studies have found that high levels of mastery improve psychological health [66], few have systematically examined “involution” contexts. Our study shows that individuals with accumulated mastery through exercise are more adept at making strategic choices, focusing limited time and energy on solving problems, and reducing cognitive distortions related to ineffective efforts. From a professional perspective, whether in white-collar jobs or service roles, individuals with higher mastery are better able to clearly define the boundaries between work and rest, highlighting the contextual adaptability of the exercise-mastery-“involution” relationship.Another longitudinal mediation pathway reveals the core role of Personal Sense of Mastery in the relationship between physical exercise and passive resignation behaviors. Mechanistically, no prior study has proven the influence of Personal Sense of Mastery on passive resignation behaviors. Using cross-lagged analysis, we found that individuals with higher life mastery are more likely to adopt proactive coping strategies, avoiding the apathy and lack of passion associated with passive resignation. In contrast, excessive passive resignation leads to lowered expectations and behavioral investment, resulting in a loss of active control over life [67], a conclusion supported by qualitative interviews. The cross-lagged model constructed from three waves of longitudinal data validated Personal Sense of Mastery as a critical bridge between Conservation of Resources (COR) theory and Self-Determination Theory (SDT), breaking through the traditional limitations of cross-sectional research in identifying mediation effects. Moreover, the discovery of occupational heterogeneity not only triangulates with quantitative findings but also enriches the understanding of the mediation mechanism, providing a scientific basis for long-term physical intervention across different occupational characteristics.

Although this study has made some valuable contributions, there are still some limitations that need to be taken seriously. Firstly, there is a significant deficiency in the representativeness of the sample. We used convenience sampling, and the participants mainly came from Shaanxi Province, Hubei Province, and Sichuan Province. The sample was solely drawn from three central and western provinces, namely Shaanxi Province, Hubei Province and Sichuan Province. It did not include the eastern coastal areas, the northeastern regions, or the remote western provinces. Therefore, the external validity of the research conclusions is mainly confined to the three central and western provinces covered by the survey and should not be directly generalized to all Chinese youth. Due to the initial design focusing on the feasibility of tracking, we did not conduct detailed statistics on the sample size, city distribution, and urban-rural household composition in each of the three provinces, nor did we provide the precise proportion of each occupational category. This limited the external validity of the research conclusions for different regions, urban-rural areas, and occupational groups. Especially for the 442 individuals who were “already employed”, their specific occupational types were not further subdivided, which made the discussion about occupational differences in the text lack quantitative data support. We only provided preliminary clues based on 12 respondents in the qualitative interviews. Secondly, the study has not considered the generalizability of the research conclusions in different regions, groups, and household registrations. Future research should adopt more rigorous random sampling strategies, clearly define the quota ratios for urban-rural areas, provinces, and occupational types, and comprehensively compare the group heterogeneity of the model results from different countries and regions. Thirdly, the measurement method is still mainly based on the self-report of respondents, which may have problems such as subjective recall bias and measurement tracking failure. The self-assessment of “overwork” and “passivity” may be influenced by family, friends, and social opinions. Fourthly, the study has not distinguished the effect paths of different types of exercise. In the future, it is necessary to precisely subdivide the exercise categories and adopt random control, multi-source reporting, etc. methods to improve the accuracy of the conclusions.

Conclusion

This study, employing a mixed-methods approach with three waves of longitudinal surveys and semi-structured follow-up interviews, systematically investigates the dynamic causal relationships between physical exercise and perceptions of “involution” and “lying flat” among Chinese youth, and highlights the important mediating role of Personal Sense of Mastery. The core finding of this research establishes the positive regulatory role of physical exercise in shaping the social mindset of Chinese youth. Cross-lagged model analysis shows that physical exercise not only buffers youth perceptions of “involution” over time but also effectively suppresses passive resignation behaviors. Furthermore, the study reveals a bidirectional negative predictive relationship between these variables: as time progresses, both high perceptions of “involution” and passive resignation lead to reduced physical exercise. This inverse relationship underscores the spiraling dynamic between social mindset and positive behaviors, offering practical insights for formulating effective strategies.Additionally, Personal Sense of Mastery plays a crucial central role in this mechanism, indicating that physical exercise helps youth develop stable beliefs in their ability to control their lives, which, in turn, reduces both “involution” and passive resignation behaviors. This finding contributes to the advancement of COR and SDT theories. Finally, qualitative data corroborate the quantitative findings, providing deeper analysis of the occupational context differences in the mechanism pathways. These findings offer important scientific evidence for government and labor market authorities to design targeted exercise programs and guide the cultivation of a positive social mindset.
