A structured and embodied framework (SAEF) for pain communication and externalization☆☆☆
Authors: Asmath Asina M, RK Jaishree Karthiga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPain with physiological or psychological distress remains unexpressed because of emotional barriers, cognitive stigma and sensory strain. Due to an insufficient psychosocial or supportive care resources, people find it difficult to express their pain experiences in words, resulting in miscommunication and mental turmoil (Scarry, 1985). Existing models of pain articulation therapies rely more on verbal and narrative modes of expression than on innovative non-verbal approaches. This study presents a Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) for pain communication and externalization grounded in Embodied Cognition Theory, Interoception Theory, and Cognitive Behavioral Theory. The framework externalizes and communicates pain through a structured theatrical model involving character embodiment, gesture-based expression, emotional improvisation, and metaphorical performances for pain expression and emotional regulation, without disclosing the cause.•Theconceptual framework helps to communicate and express pain in people, moving them from the patient to participant phase.•The framework is an interdisciplinary exploratory tool that combines clinical psychology and applied theatre practice.•This framework is useful for therapists, theatre practitioners, clinicians, caregivers, and people in art-based avenues.Keywords: Pain communication, Psychosocial pain, Theatrical therapy, Embodied expression, Emotional release

Background

Pain in adults remains unrepresented or miscommunicated due to emotional inhibitions, cultural stereotypes and difficulty in translating physical and psychological suffering into verbal language. Global reports indicate that many individuals with chronic pain remain underserved in accessing appropriate care according to World Health Organization [1]. Communication difficulties, lack of validation, and challenges in expressing pain experiences can further limit engagement with psychosocial support [2]. Clinical and communication models struggle to address the embodied, emotional, and cultural dimensions of pain experiences in adults [3]. Individuals often express their pain through metaphors, gestures, and fragmented descriptions, making it difficult for caregivers and even the patients themselves to interpret them accurately [4]. Patients in pain struggle to share the internal state that lacks the visible clinical markers, and this results in underrecognition, emotional isolation and delayed care and support [5]. Existing models of pain communication include interviews, pain scales, and narrative medicine, which rely on self-disclosure and narrative language [6].

Applied theatre and Drama therapy help promote psychological release, emotional expression, and the embodiment of inner states through movement, symbols, metaphors, and role-play [7]. Drama-based techniques help patients to express unexplained experiences, reduce emotional discomfort and heal trauma or depression [8]. Applied theatre supports self-awareness, enhances empathy, and facilitates complex communication and emotional states in healthcare contexts. These findings suggest the necessity of an embodied, non-verbal theatrical framework that allows patients to externalize pain safely, which supports non-verbal expression and engagement with pain experiences. Recent research in psychological and non-pharmacological approaches to pain management highlights the growing importance of cognitive, behavioral, and expressive interventions in addressing pain beyond pharmacological treatment. A recent scoping review of psychological therapies for acute pain relief emphasizes the role of diverse modalities, including cognitive-behavioral strategies, emotional processing and patient-centred communication methods in improving pain-related outcomes. These approaches focus not only on symptom reduction but also on enhancing the individual’s ability to understand, express, and regulate pain experiences [9]. Within this broader context, the Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) is a structured, embodied, and non-verbal approach for pain communication and externalization.

The framework is directly involved working with human pain. It is necessary to formulate a framework on psychological principles of Embodied Cognition Theory, Interoception Theory, and Cognitive Behavioral Theory to protect the individual from emotional overload and to externalize subjective pain experiences in a psychologically safe environment. This approach contributes to the emerging field of interdisciplinary studies, which involves psychology, art therapy, pain communication, and embodied cognition.

Method details

Outline

The proposed method is a structural framework for pain communication, externalizing subjective pain experiences through embodied, symbolic and non-verbal practices. The framework is most appropriate for individuals experiencing psychosocial or non-clinical pain contexts, including emotional distress or chronic pain narratives. It is not intended for individuals with acute medical conditions, severe psychological distress, or active psychiatric symptoms without appropriate professional supervision. The method consists of multiple steps that help participants from bodily regulation to expressive enactment of pain without disclosing the cause. Through this framework, pain can be expressed, observed, transformed and regulated through action rather than considering it as a medical condition. This framework ensures transparency, adaptability and reproducibility in an exploratory research practice context using theatrical techniques.

A Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) integrates Embodied Cognition Theory, Interoception Theory, and Cognitive Behavioral Theory (CBT) from psychology. According to these theories, pain is conceptualized as a multidimensional experience shaped by bodily action, cognitive-behavioral patterns and internal sensory awareness. Traditional medical treatment addresses pain as a physical symptom, disregarding the subjective, expressive, and relational dimensions [10]. Recent psychological approaches treat pain as an embodied experience that can be regulated through the engagement of body and mind [11].

Embodied Cognition Theory explains that emotion and behavior are deeply connected to bodily movement, posture and the interaction of sensory-motor with the environment [12,13]. Similarly, pain associated with emotion is not perceived internally but is also interconnected with the bodily movements and the environmental situations in expression. The framework operationalizes this principle into theatrical gestures, posture, spatial orientation and the rhythm of translating internal pain into observable actions. This way of movement-based externalization helps patients to externalize pain without verbal expression of the cause, which is cognitively demanding and emotionally restrictive [14].

Interoception Theory supports the SAEF by prioritizing body’s internal sensation, tension, discomfort, heaviness and pressure [15]. Research shows that focusing on the location and intensity of bodily pain rather than its cause can improve emotional well-being and affective overload [16]. The method employs this interoceptive attention to identify and externalize pain through body mapping and sensation-focused movement. The narrative interpretation of pain is shifted to perceptual quality through this sensory-based approach.

Cognitive Behavioral Theory provides a regulatory structure for the method by establishing behavioral modulation, cognitive distancing and graded exposure to distressing experiences [17]. Principles of Cognitive behavioral Theory are applied through the theatrical techniques via symbolic enactment, role reversal, and controlled repetition instead of giving importance to the narration of the cause. This technique considers pain as a phenomenon that can be externalized rather than an undifferentiated internal state, leading to cognitive reappraisal without verbal cause analysis [18]. The steps and activities involved in the framework reflect the safety principles of Cognitive Behavioral Theory.

These theoretical perspectives function in an integrated manner rather than as isolated constructs. Interoception Theory supports the initial awareness of internal bodily sensations associated with pain. Embodied Cognition Theory enables the translation of these sensations into physical movement, gesture, and symbolic enactment. Cognitive Behavioral Theory contributes to the modulation and restructuring of these experiences through controlled repetition, distancing, and behavioral engagement. This integrated process creates a progression from internal awareness to external expression and regulated engagement with pain.

Theatre becomes a methodological tool rather than a performative process that externalizes pain through direct and non-intrusive engagement. In this framework, supported by ethical and psychological engagement, participants are active contributors of this structured process rather than being treated as patients or subjects.

Significance of the method

A Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) for pain communication and externalization is a systematic, theory-driven approach to express pain that brings psychological theories into theatre practice. It bridges the gap between psychological models of pain and performance methodologies in theatre that are employed more intuitively than procedurally [19]. Biopsychosocial and cognitive behavioral models are widely used in clinical psychology, whereas embodied practices are common in theatre and expressive arts. Their combined application as a methodological framework remains limited [20]. This method provides a clear sequence through which bodily sensation, perception, and symbolic action are systematically engaged for pain externalization.

Research shows that pain cannot be verbalized effectively when it is chronic, emotionally complex and socially mediated [4]. The framework for pain communication and externalization prioritizes a non-verbal, non-narrative form of communication in the pain expression. Application of gestures, movements and symbols in the procedure reduces the cognitive load and emotional defensiveness in pain expression.

Pain-related enactments can lead to vulnerability, so this framework is built on psychological principles that establish predictability, boundary setting and gradual engagement [21]. In addition, this method is designed around containment, safety and ethical engagement. The use of symbolic distancing and structured closure reduces the risk of emotional flooding and helps participants in emotional regulation.

This method can also be applied in various areas of theatre research, performance pedagogy, and exploratory expressive practices. The step-wise model helps researchers and practitioners modify stages according to their preferences while retaining theoretical coherence, promoting interdisciplinary application, and methodological openness.

Psychologically informed theatre-based framework for pain externalization

The Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) begins with a preparatory stage with safety containment, implementing psychological theories and embodied regulation principles. The process begins with interoceptive awareness, where participants identify and locate pain within the body. This is followed by embodied expression, where internal sensations are translated into gestures, movement, and symbolic forms. Cognitive-behavioral processes enable participants to modulate, distance, and reorganize their engagement with pain through structured activities. This progression from awareness to expression and regulation, supports the externalization of pain and facilitates controlled emotional processing within a non-verbal framework. While engaging with embodied pain expression, the psychological application supports the nervous system regulation by reducing threat and uncertainty. The preparation stage acts as a cognitive and emotional support that prevents participants from experiencing a state of unregulated affect. The theatrical framework contains 11 steps with various theatrical activities that facilitate pain externalization. Each step has an objective to externalize pain safely that may support emotional processing and structured engagement with distress. The process begins with physical grounding to stabilize the body and channel the focus towards emotional regulation.

Operational parameters and guidelines

The Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) should be implemented within defined operational parameters. A session including all the theatrical activities should be conducted over a duration of 90 – 120 min, depending on the participants' response and engagement. This methodological framework is recommended for a small group of 6 – 12 participants. Adequate space for movement, observation and facilitators' attention is essential for the execution of this method. The facilitator should be a trained professional in applied theatre practices and should have foundational knowledge of psychological principles in relation to embodied awareness, emotional regulation, and trauma-informed approaches. The session should be conducted in a safe, private and quiet space where participants can move comfortably and engage in embodied activities. Simple objects like cloth, masks, and symbolic items mentioned in the framework are required. These are the operational guidelines to be followed during the session to support the consistent implementation while maintaining flexibility.

Safety protocol

Steps involved in the Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) may evoke strong emotional responses, so it is necessary to follow the psychological safety measures. Participation in the activities is voluntary, and the individuals can pause or withdraw from the session at any stage using a pre-defined stop signal. The framework is not recommended for participants experiencing acute psychological distress, severe trauma-related conditions, or active psychiatric symptoms without professional supervision. It is the responsibility of the facilitator to monitor the signs of distress and pause activities when necessary. Support service from the mental health professionals should be provided if required. These measures ensure that the framework is implemented with attention to participant safety and ethical responsibility.

Step 1: Physical Grounding

The initial step of the Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) is physical grounding that focuses on interoceptive awareness and embodied stabilization. This preparatory step is designed to stabilize the body and provide a sense of safety before getting into the process of pain enactment. Participants are instructed to stand in a circle with enough space in between to move freely. The participants are instructed to place both feet firmly on the ground. Participants are guided towards slow inhaling and exhaling three to four times, with a stop signal at the end of each time, as shown in Fig. 1. Repetition of this pattern of breathing helps in progressive muscle relaxation and attention to bodily contact with the floor, which reduces physiological arousal. The bodily grounded posture and breathing directly influence the emotional and cognitive state, which is a perspective of Embodied Cognition Theory. This grounding is essential for preparing the body to engage meaningfully in subsequent expressive actions [12,22]. Grounding enhances interoceptive awareness among the participants, which prevents them from becoming overwhelmed and allows them to perceive the internal sensations [15]. In research, trauma-informed and body-based psychological approaches emphasize that grounding supports nervous system regulation and reduces the risk of dissociation or emotional flooding when individuals engage with distressing internal experiences [21]. In this framework, physical grounding acts as a containment mechanism that helps participants to remain embodied, attentive, and regulated. This creates the necessary conditions for safe pain externalization in later stages of the framework.

Physical grounding.

Step 2: Body Mapping (Somatic Awareness)

Body mapping is designed to help participants locate and recognize pain as a bodily sensation rather than a narrative problem. This step strengthens interoceptive awareness of pain as a bodily experience. Participants remain in the circle or move to individual spaces if they want. Participants are instructed to notice pain or discomfort felt in the body. Participants can point out the location of pain by placing a mark on the body. They can also draw the body and mark the location of pain with three different colors as shown in Fig. 2 (Red, Orange, and Yellow), according to the intensity of the pain. Emotional sufferings can be attributed to a location either in the mind or the heart. This step allows pain to be recognized physically without verbal disclosure. Participants attend to where pain is experienced and how it manifests (e.g., tightness, heat, pressure), without engaging in causal explanation. This activity strengthens awareness of internal body signals, which form the centre in subjective pain experiences [15,23]. Research in somatic psychology shows that attending to bodily sensation without interpretation reduces emotional defensiveness and cognitive overload in individuals with chronic or unexplained pain [24]. In the framework, body mapping serves as a bridge between internal sensation and external expression, enabling pain to be acknowledged safely without verbal disclosure.

Body mapping.

Step 3: Gesture-Based Externalization

This step reflects embodied cognition by translating sensation into movements and supports behavioral modulation. Participants are instructed to do a small bodily gesture on the pain-located areas that reflects sensation. The action is repeated slowly, and the rigor of the movement is increased and later reduced, demonstrating control. A pause is introduced between repetitions. This activity enables participants to realize that pain-related movements can be expressed, shaped and stopped at will. Pain is embodied through controlled gestures that diffuse internal experience into observable actions. Embodied Cognition Theory states that meaning-making occurs not only through language, but gestures can also be a primary mode of expression [12]. This activity aligns with cognitive-behavioral principles in which repetitive gestures help participants to modulate intensity and gain behavioral control over painful sensations [17]. Movement-based pain research indicates that symbolic movement enables somatic discharge without verbal traumatization [25]. In this framework, gesture-based externalization provides a non-verbal vocabulary for pain, allowing expression while preserving psychological distance and self-regulation. Step 4: Mask / Object Personification

Mask or object personification creates a symbolic distance by transferring pain to an external object. Participants are requested to choose a simple object (cloth, stone, ribbon) or a basic mask. They are asked to hold the object or wear the mask and imagine that the object or mask represent their pain. Participants move slowly with an object or a mask while focusing on the object rather than inflicting pain on themselves. The facilitators should avoid interpretations and encourage exploration, which creates an emotional distance between body and pain. Participants project sensation, emotion, or intensity symbolically on a physical object, allowing pain to be observed rather than inhabited, as shown in Fig. 3. This step incorporates cognitive-behavioral distancing through symbolic externalization. From the perspective of Cognitive Behavioral Theory, this externalization helps in cognitive diffusion by separating the self from distressing experience [18]. Theatre and drama therapy literature explains that object intervention can reduce shame and self-identification with suffering [7]. Embodied engagement with symbolic objects allows participants to explore pain through posture and movement while maintaining containment. This step transforms pain into a tangible, manageable entity, reinforcing agency and emotional safety.

Mask or object personification.

Step 5: Role Reversal (Becoming the Pain)

This step supports cognitive and perceptual distancing through role-based embodiment. Participants are instructed to become the pain for a short time as they move, stand or while doing a gesture as stated in Fig. 4. Participants behave according to the intensity or the nature of their pain and get back to the neutral posture after some time during the enactment. This enactment is observed by other participants from outside, which supports a perceptive shift and reduces identification of their own pain. Drama-based research shows that role reversal enhances insight by allowing individuals to observe their experience from a new perspective [31]. Switching roles reorganizes sensorimotor patterns associated with pain and reduces experiential rigidity [12]. In this framework, role reversal enables participants to engage pain as an “other,” reflecting distance and reducing fear-based identification.

Role reversal.

Step 6: Emotional Improvisation / Playback

The emotional improvisation stage helps participants externalize pain through small enactments on a neutral prompt or a single word/image like pressure, heavy, heat, tight, storm, ease, balance, light, etc. This step integrates embodied expression with emotional processing through shared enactment. Participants develop these words into movements or actions for 1 or 2 min. Other participants are requested to observe pain quietly. The performed movement is mirrored by the facilitator and a few participants in this step. The facilitator should not give any verbal feedback. The mirrored action by others gives a sense of acknowledgement to the performing participant, which supports emotional expression through shared embodied witnessing. Mirroring action is supported by the neuropsychological principles, which state that observing and reproducing movements can activate shared neural representations combined with recognition and validation [32]. Movement-based expression shows emotional regulation and reduces emotional distress by facilitating bottom-up processing rather than cognitive appraisal [14].

Playback in drama therapy serves as a non-verbal expression, in which participants experience psychological safety without interpretation or judgment [19,33]. Although direct clinical evidence connecting theatrical playback in pain reduction is limited, research in dance/movement therapy and somatic practices shows decreases in emotional distress and perceived bodily tension following embodied expression and social attunement [14,34]. The outcome of emotional improvisation or playback is not just catharsis or symptom resolution, but a structured expression and validation where pain externalization happens with safety, containment and group attunement.

Step 7: Vocal Release (Controlled)

Controlled vocal release supports in reducing the tension stored in breath and voice, which are associated to autonomic regulation. In this step, participants are instructed to make non-verbal sounds such as humming, sighing or extended exhalation. The volume of the sound is said to be kept low at the beginning and increased comfortably. The facilitator can give pauses in between for the participants to breathe as instructed in Fig. 5. This may help reduce perceived tension held in the voice and breath. According to the polyvagal theory, the role of vocal expression can regulate emotional states and restore physiological balance [35]. Vocalization integrates breath, posture, and sensation into expressive release [25]. This step emphasizes on modulation rather than intensity of verbal sounds, ensuring safety while enabling somatic discharge. Vocal release functions as a transitional mechanism between expression and integration. This step facilitates physiological and emotional regulation through controlled vocal expression.

Vocal release.

Step 8: Scene Reconstruction (Symbolic)

Scene reconstruction invites participants to enact pain symbolically and then to improvise the enactment through change and intervention. This step aligns with principles of cognitive restructuring, as participants actively modify behavioral responses to distress [17]. Theatre-based research suggests that symbolic rehearsal enhances perceived control and agency [36]. In the scene reconstruction step, participants are instructed to enact a scene with issues like a storm, hunger, an accident, etc., with pain as shown in Fig. 6. Participants are guided to find a solution to the problem that can lessen the pain of the character caused by the issues. Participants rehearse the same scenes performed with the positive transformed action created from the solution identified with changing movements, setting a boundary or shifting positions. In this phase, participants should not bring in their personal narratives. This step aligns with cognitive behavior therapy of restructuring, where participants change behavioral responses according to the distress [17]. Theatre-based research suggests that symbolic rehearsal can enhance perceived control and agency [36]. Embodied engagement involving simulated scenarios allows participants to rehearse alternative outcomes without revisiting personal trauma. Within the framework, scene reconstruction supports meaning-making and restores a sense of mastery.

Scene reconstruction.

Step 9. Ritualized Release (Closure Act):

The ritualized release stage provides a structured, symbolic closure that marks the transition from pain expression to emotional integration. This step functions as a meaning-making and containment mechanism. It allows participants to externalize pain into a symbolic action rather than aiming the literal symptom resolution. Ritualized acts have long been recognized in psychology and anthropology as tools for emotional regulation, transition, and cognitive reorganization when internal experiences are difficult to verbalize [37,38].

Each participant is made to perform a simple symbolic action representing release, such as placing an object in a box, folding a cloth differently, or setting an object down and stepping away. A single word may be spoken if desired. The ritual is brief and contained. This step marks a psychological transition and closure.

Symbolic rituals support psychological distancing, which helps individuals relate distress as an external entity rather than an internal identity in therapeutic contexts [39]. Closure rituals are used to prevent emotional residue and restore role boundaries after intense enactment in drama therapy and expressive arts [19,33]. Though the direct experimental evidence associating ritual acts with pain reduction is limited, studies in expressive and somatic therapies report improved emotional regulation and perceived relief while following structured symbolic closure [14,34].

The act of naming a single word during the ritual further integrates cognitive awareness without reopening causal narratives, supporting consolidation of the embodied experience. Step 10. Integration and Sharing

The Integration and Sharing stage consolidates the embodied and emotional experiences generated during theatrical pain externalization and translates them into practical, self-regulatory strategies that can be applied beyond the session. This step consolidates regulation and meaning-making through reflective integration.

This stage begins with a slow cool-down process involving guided breathing and gentle stretching to stabilize physiological arousal. Participants then engage in brief and structured reflection, responding to prompts such as “What shifted?” and “One practical step I can take this week…”. Verbal sharing is optional and concise, and it can avoid an analytical discussion of the cause of the pain. Participants can share personal calming rituals or safety plans, such as a grounding gesture or breath practice to use if pain resurfaces.

Integration prevents abrupt emotional termination after expressive work of various steps and supports meaning-making without reactivation. Translating embodied insight into small, concrete actions strengthens continuity between the structured session environment and daily life. This aligns with trauma-informed practice, which emphasizes titration, grounding, and post-activation regulation [41].

Somatic and movement-based therapies consistently highlight the importance of post-expression integration for nervous system regulation and learning consolidation [14,34]. Reflective meaning-making following embodied activity is also supported in experiential learning theory, where integration phases enable transfer of learning to real-world contexts [42].

Step 11. Debrief & Follow-up

The Debrief & Follow-up stage ensures emotional safety, ethical responsibility, and continuity of care following potentially intense theatrical or embodied work.

This step involves private check-ins with participants to assess residual distress, emotional stability, and readiness to leave the session. Facilitators can provide appropriate resources, such as therapist referrals or support contacts. The practitioner may also suggest low-intensity follow-up practices, including micro-gestures, grounding routines and one-line ritual. If significant distress is identified, escalation protocols are followed in line with ethical and safeguarding guidelines.

Debriefing functions as a containment and closure mechanism, preventing unprocessed material from carrying into daily life. Follow-up reinforces ethical accountability in methods that engage with pain without direct clinical intervention. This step is essential when theatrical practices intersect with emotional vulnerability.

Debriefing and follow-up are standard requirements in trauma-informed, expressive arts, and qualitative research methodologies to mitigate risk and ensure participant well-being [19,33]. Post-session check-ins can reduce adverse emotional after effects and support participant safety, even when interventions are non-clinical [43]. While empirical studies on theatrical pain communication and externalization are limited, ethical frameworks support structured debriefing as a significant practice. Procedural Protocol Summary of the SAEF.

The eleven steps constitute a structured theatrical framework that guides participants from an internalized experience of pain toward regulated externalization and embodied integration. Each stage is built upon the previous one by carefully modulating bodily awareness, emotional distance, and expressive intensity, ensuring that pain is engaged without narrative exposure or emotional overload. By embedding principles from Embodied Cognition Theory, Interoception Theory, and Cognitive Behavioral Theory throughout the sequence, the framework maintains both expressive depth and psychological safety. SAEF follows a structured progression in which interoceptive awareness enables recognition of pain, embodied cognition facilitates its expression, and cognitive-behavioral processes support its modulation and integration. This integrated mechanism underpins the framework’s approach to pain externalization and emotional regulation. The progression from grounding and somatic awareness to symbolic enactment, ritualized release, and integration enables pain to be transformed into observable, manageable, and meaningfully contained action. The framework is designed as a process-oriented method rather than a diagnostic or outcome-driven intervention, which can be adapted across research, performative, and exploratory practice contexts.

This Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) presents a replicable, theory-informed theatrical framework for pain communication and externalization that prioritizes embodied expression over verbal explanation. The framework offers researchers and practitioners a structured approach for studying and facilitating pain externalization. The framework maintains ethical and emotional safeguards by systematizing theatre-based practices within established psychological models. The stepwise design, supported by diagrams, flowcharts, and procedural tables, allows for transparent application, methodological adaptation, and future empirical validation. As a methodological contribution, this framework extends the scope of performance-based research by demonstrating how theatre practices can be organized to examine subjective pain experiences within interdisciplinary research settings.

Theoretical rationale and conceptual justification

The proposed Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) for pain communication and externalization is a theory-driven methodological model rather than an empirically tested intervention. Theoretical grounding, procedural coherence, and alignment with interdisciplinary practices help conceptualise the methodology.

Embodied Cognition Theory, Interoception Theory and Cognitive Behavioral Theory support all the steps in the procedure. All these theories are grounded in the area of psychology and pain research. The theoretical application provides a consistent and conceptual acclaim for the method. The procedure involved in pain communication includes independent components such as grounding, somatic awareness, gesture-based expression, symbolic externalization, role enactment and ritual closure that are previously accepted in drama-based and embodied methods while engaging with pain experiences. Each component is not made into a complete sequence in the procedure and implemented as a single protocol.

Conceptual Justification of this method is also supported by procedural transparency. The method is defined with aims and processes supported by diagrams, flowcharts and tables with relevant evidence from early research in different disciplines. Containment, gradual progression, and structured closure, aligning with trauma-informed research practices, combine ethical and safety considerations throughout the framework.

While the present framework is conceptually and theoretically grounded, future empirical applications of the SAEF would benefit from the use of psychometrically grounded measurement instruments to assess outcomes related to pain perception, emotional regulation, and pain communication and externalization. In pain research, psychologically grounded tools play a critical role in ensuring reliability and comparability of findings across contexts. For example, recent work on the development and validation of structured pain assessment instruments, such as the Low Back Pain Questionnaire for Nurses (LBP-NS), highlights the importance of rigorous measurement approaches in capturing subjective pain experiences [44]. Incorporating such tools in future studies would strengthen the empirical evaluation and applicability of the SAEF framework.

The framework is a methodological contribution appropriate for exploratory research and future empirical testing. Its structured design allows future studies to evaluate feasibility, effectiveness, and contextual application without misapplying the method with clinical outcome claims.

Future empirical evaluation of the framework

The Structured And Embodied Framework (SAEF) can be empirically evaluated through staged research designs that align with its embodied and non-verbal structure. Initial feasibility studies will examine participant engagement, safety, and acceptability of specific components of the framework, such as physical grounding, body mapping, gesture-based externalization, and symbolic enactment. These studies may document participant responsiveness to non-verbal pain expression and the feasibility of conducting structured theatrical sessions in small-group settings.

Subsequent pilot studies will explore how participants engage with the stages of the framework, particularly the transition from interoceptive awareness (body mapping) to embodied expression (gesture and movement) and symbolic externalization (object personification, role reversal, and improvisation). Observational and qualitative data of the participant reflections during the integration stage will provide insight into changes in pain awareness, expression, and perceived regulation.

Mixed-methods research designs will be suitable for evaluating SAEF, including qualitative accounts of participants’ experiences and quantitative measures of pain communication and externalization, emotional expression, and perceived distress. Specific attention should be given to how participants externalize pain without verbal disclosure and how structured activities such as vocal modulation, scene reconstruction, and ritualized closure contribute to emotional containment and regulation.

Future studies can also examine the feasibility of all the activities as a structured intervention model by comparing pre- and post-session responses related to pain perception, emotional state, and engagement. The use of empirically tested assessment instruments can further support systematic evaluation of these outcomes. Such empirical investigations would contribute to establishing the applicability, safety, and potential value of SAEF as a structured methodological framework for pain-related research.

Ethics statements

The study does not involve any human participants, as the research is a theory-based methodology proposed for future application in clinical psychology and theatre therapy. No approval or consent is required from the Ethical Committee, as the study uses psychological theories of pain, cognition and emotional processing in framing the method. Any future empirical application of the SAEF involving human participants will require prior ethical approval, informed consent, and appropriate safeguards to ensure participant safety during emotionally engaging activities.
