Deciphering mismatch repair protein expression variability in gastric cancer: clinical and prognostic implications
Authors: Fengmei Zhong, Mengjia Zhang, Like Xu, Jinlong Cui, Zhen Chen, Qiqi Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) exhibits diverse molecular characteristics, among which microsatellite instability (MSI) plays a pivotal role in determining treatment responses, particularly to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Mismatch repair (MMR) protein immunohistochemical (IHC) staining is a primary screening method for MSI; however, heterogeneity in staining patterns can complicate interpretation.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the clinicopathological features of GCs with heterogeneous mismatch repair(hMMR) protein expression, specifically (sub)clonal pattern (the coexistence of retained/lost staining in the tumor area), and to assess its association with MSI status.MethodsWe analyzed 1049 cases of gastric adenocarcinoma from the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine between 2018 and 2024. Heterogeneous staining for MMR proteins was observed in seven cases, characterized by areas of sudden staining loss alongside retained stain. These cases were subjected to tumor microdissection to ascertain MSI status accurately. Also, the association between MSI status and clinicopathological features was investigated in a cohort of 107 selected patients.ResultsOur findings indicated that GCs with hMMR, classified as proficient MMR (pMMR) by IHC, paradoxically exhibited MSI-H status upon microdissection. This discovery underscores the potential for misdiagnosis if intratumoral heterogeneity is overlooked. Furthermore, patients with MSI status displayed distinct clinicopathological features, including older age, distal stomach location, intestinal adenocarcinoma, lower lymphatic metastasis, lower perineural invasion and lower grade of clinical stage. No statistically significant difference in 45-month disease-free survival (DFS) was observed between MSS and MSI cohorts (p = 0.122). The age (p = 0.023, HR:6.017, 95%CI:1.281–28.265) and pTNM stage (p = 0.015, HR:6.632, 95%CI:1.451–30.316) are identified as significant prognostic factors for PFS in patients with GC.ConclusionAccurate diagnosis of MSI status in GC is critical for optimizing therapeutic strategies. We advocate for detailed reporting of MMR protein heterogeneity patterns and quantification of IHC staining in clinical practice to enhance diagnosis precision and enable personalized treatment approaches for GCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14857-8.Keywords: Heterogeneity, Mismatch repair protein, Gastric cancer, Microsatellite instability

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC), ranking as the fifth leading cause of global cancer mortality and among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, demonstrates molecular heterogeneity critical for therapeutic stratification [1, 2]. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) molecular taxonomy categorizes GC into four distinct subtypes: microsatellite instability (MSI), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-positive, genomically stable (GS), and chromosomal instability (CIN) tumors [3]. MSI-high/deficient mismatch repair (MSI-H/dMMR) GC represents a relatively rare molecular subtype characterized by unique genomic features. It includes hypermutated phenotypes, elevated tumor mutational burden (TMB), and pervasive genomic instability [4]. This distinct molecular profile confers heightened sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), contrasting sharply with the therapeutic resistance typically observed in microsatellite-stable/proficient MMR (MSS/pMMR) tumors [4–7]. Furthermore, the immunogenic microenvironment of MSI-H/dMMR tumors is characterized by prominent tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) infiltration and upregulated programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression. These features collectively establish MSI-H/dMMR as a validated predictive biomarker for immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) efficacy [4, 6, 8].

MSI arises from defects in DNA MMR pathways, driven either by epigenetic silencing (commonly sporadic and associated with BRAF V600E mutations) or germline mutations in MMR genes (typically linked to Lynch syndrome, LS) [9]. Clinical implementation of MSI/dMMR testing enables dual applications: identifying LS carriers in cancer cohorts and informing prognostic/therapeutic strategies for sporadic malignancies [10].

IHC assessment of four core MMR proteins—MutL homolog 1 (MLH1), PMS1 homolog 2 (PMS2), MutS homolog 2 (MSH2), and MutS homolog 6 (MSH6)—serves as the major method for dMMR screening [11, 12]. Tumor cells typically exhibit either complete MMR protein retention or uniform loss [8, 13]. However, heterogeneous staining patterns observed in some cases complicate interpretation. MMR heterogeneity in tumors manifests through distinct spatial patterns:1)(Sub)clonal heterogeneity may comprise two distinct and spatially segregated populations, characterized by proficient and deficient MMR areas, respectively [14]; 2) Intraglandular heterogeneity manifests as a mosaic architecture, with MMR-proficient and MMR-deficient regions intricately interdigitated within tumor masses [11, 13].

Current interpretation criteria for MMR protein IHC staining: Positive expression is defined as any definitive nuclear staining observed in tumor cells, while negative expression is confirmed only when tumor cell nuclei exhibit complete absence of staining [13]. These often classify such cases as pMMR, potentially misclassifying biologically relevant dMMR subclones. This interpretive ambiguity poses significant challenges for pathologists, particularly given the direct implications for therapeutic decisions. Misclassification may exclude eligible patients from ICIs or delay LS screening [10, 13]. Emerging evidence suggests that even focal dMMR regions may confer clinical vulnerability to immunotherapy, necessitating refined diagnostic criteria for heterogeneous cases [14].

By systematically mapping clonal heterogeneity in MMR protein expression and analyzing associations between MSI status and clinicopathological characteristics, we establish a mechanistic framework that refines the interpretation of MMR IHC profiles and enhances prognostic stratification in GC.

Materials and methods

Case selection

This retrospective cohort study enrolled 1049 patients with gastric carcinoma who underwent radical gastrectomy or endoscopic submucosal dissection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (2018–2024). Inclusion criteria comprised:1) histologically confirmed primary gastric adenocarcinoma;2) patients with histologically verified R0 resection (microscopically negative margins);3) patients did not receive neoadjuvant therapy or radiotherapy prior to surgery; 4) complete clinicopathological data retrievable via electronic health records;5) availability of MMR protein immunohistochemistry or PCR-based MSI testing. Tumor staging followed the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) guidelines. All histopathological diagnoses, IHC results, and molecular findings were independently reviewed and confirmed by two senior pathologists. All discrepant cases underwent blinded re-evaluation by an independent senior pathologist to establish a consensus diagnosis. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Number: 20240892).

Immunohistochemistry

Three-micrometer-thick sections of paraffin-embedded tissue were processed using an automated IHC staining system (Bond-max, Leica, Germany). The following primary antibodies were employed: MLH1 (mouse monoclonal, clone OTI4H4, 1:600 dilution), PMS2 (rabbit monoclonal, clone EP51, 1:1000 dilution), MSH2 (rabbit monoclonal, clone RED2, 1:80 dilution), and MSH6 (rabbit monoclonal, clone EP49, 1:300 dilution). All four antibodies exhibited nuclear localization patterns. Positive staining was defined by detectable nuclear staining in ≥ 1 tumor cell, while absence of nuclear staining was interpreted as negative. Cases showing loss of nuclear staining in ≥ 1 antibody were classified as dMMR, whereas intact nuclear expression of all four antibodies defined pMMR [13]. Appropriate internal controls (normal gastric mucosa and stromal cells) and external controls demonstrated expected positive staining in all cases.

HMMR((hMMR) deficiency was defined as a focal loss of nuclear staining in the tumor area, with adjacent tumor regions retaining normal expression. In our study, tissue blocks from heterogeneous cases underwent microdissection when focal deletions encompassed ≥ 10% of the total area.

MSI analysis

Following the manufacturer’s protocol for the human MSI detection kit (multiplex fluorescent PCR-capillary electrophoresis), genomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples using the AmoyDx FFPE DNA Kit (AmoyDx, Xiamen, China). To assess MSI status, laser capture microdissection (LCM) was performed to isolate tumor regions with retained MMR protein expression and those exhibiting complete loss of staining. The following microsatellite markers were analyzed: NR-21, BAT-25, NR-24, NR-27, MONO-27, BAT-26. MSI classification was defined as follows: 1) MSI-high (MSI-H): ≥2 unstable loci;2) MSI-low (MSI-L): 1 unstable locus;3) Microsatellite stable (MSS): No unstable loci.

Statistical analyses

Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the duration from initial diagnosis to disease progression or death from any cause. Follow-up data were obtained through electronic health record review or follow-up cases were contacted by telephone. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with between-group comparisons assessed by Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to evaluate prognostic factors associated with DFS. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (v.29; IBM Corp.).

Results

Screening study and clinicopathological characteristics

Seven cases exhibiting hMMR deficiency were identified and enrolled in this study. The cohort comprised four male and three female patients, with a median age at diagnosis of 68 years (range: 54–82 years). Tumor localization analysis revealed four cases in the distal stomach (gastric angle and antrum) and two cases in the proximal stomach (gastric body). Notably, Case 7 presented at the gastrointestinal anastomosis site, with a documented history of gastric ulcer resection 50 years prior.

Histopathological evaluation classified two cases (Cases 1 and 2) as early gastric cancer (EGC), both demonstrating well-differentiated intestinal-type adenocarcinoma without evidence of lymphatic metastasis, lymphovascular invasion, or perineural invasion. The remaining five cases were advanced gastric cancers (AGC) exhibiting moderately-to-poorly differentiated intestinal-type adenocarcinoma. These AGC cases showed higher rates of metastatic involvement, including: lymph node metastasis (3 cases), lymphovascular invasion (4 cases) and perineural invasion (3 cases).

Two AGC patients received adjuvant chemotherapy postoperatively. Case 3 developed peritoneal metastases four months post-surgery and currently remains in palliative care. Case 4 received adjuvant chemotherapy but succumbed to disease progression at 10 months postoperatively. Case 5 succumbed to disease progression 18 months after surgery (treatment details unavailable). The remaining patients maintain disease-free survival at last follow-up (Table 1).

Histopathological assessment according to Lauren classification identified intestinal-type adenocarcinoma as the most prevalent subtype (39.3%, n = 46), followed by mixed-type (33.3%, n = 39) and diffuse-type (27.4%, n = 32). Tumor differentiation analysis revealed predominantly poor differentiation (53%, n = 62), with moderate and well-differentiated phenotypes representing 23.1% (n = 27) and 23.9% (n = 28) of cases, respectively. The cohort comprised 79 MSS cases and 38 microsatellite MSI-H cases. MMR deficiency status demonstrated significant associations with several clinicopathological parameters (P < 0.05), including: advanced age at diagnosis (P = 0.036), predilection for distal gastric localization (P = 0.006), intestinal-type histology by Lauren classification (P = 0.01), reduced frequency of lymphatic metastasis (P < 0.001), lower incidence of perineural invasion (P < 0.001), earlier clinical stage (P = 0.002).No statistically significant correlations were observed between MMR status and gender, gross morphological type, histological differentiation grade, HER2 status, or lymphovascular invasion (all P > 0.05).

Patients treatment and prognosis

In the MSS cohort, 50 patients received postoperative chemotherapy, whereas 10 patients were treated with a combination of postoperative chemotherapy plus immunotherapy or targeted therapy. Nineteen patients did not undergo any adjuvant therapy. In the MSI cohort, 15 patients received adjuvant chemotherapy, 3 patients were treated with combined chemotherapy and immunotherapy, and 20 patients received no postoperative treatment.

Patient outcomes were prospectively monitored for 45 months. In the MSS cohort, disease progression occurred in 21 cases, including 12 deaths during follow-up. The remaining 58 patients showed no evidence of disease recurrence. In the MSI cohort, disease recurrence occurred in 9 patients, 8 of whom died, while the remaining 29 patients maintained DFS.

We further evaluated the association between MMR status and patient DFS outcomes. There was no significant difference in 45-month DFS between the MSS and MSI cohorts (p = 0.122; Fig. 3).

Discussion

Histological and molecular heterogeneity represents a defining characteristic of tumor biology. In our study, we demonstrate a (sub)clonal distribution of hMMR deficiency in GCs. The four MMR proteins exhibited heterogeneous expression patterns, either as paired heterodimers (MLH1/PMS2) or as isolated single proteins with partial loss of one or two components.

Notably, a subset of GCs classified as pMMR by conventional assessment paradoxically displayed MSI-H status upon microdissection-based analysis. This (sub)clonal hMMR pattern aligns with previous reports [13, 15–18] and was further corroborated by immunohistochemistry. It revealed two distinct clonal subpopulations within the same tumor mass: one subpopulation retained intact MMR protein expression and maintained MSS, while the other exhibited MMR protein loss and displayed MSI. In clinical practice, pathologists should account for tumor heterogeneity when interpreting MMR staining results. If necessary, microdissection should be performed to avoid misclassifying dMMR cases as pMMR, which could lead to missed opportunities for immunotherapy. Based on these considerations, we propose the following four recommendations: 1) systematic documentation of MMR protein heterogeneity patterns; 2) quantitative IHC assessment; 3) For heterogeneous cases, microdissection should be performed when necessary to specifically analyze areas with loss of tumor staining for MSI status determination.; 4) Integration of molecular and histopathological data.

Our study identified distinct molecular profiles among MMR-deficient subgroups. The MLH1/PMS2 double-heterogeneity subgroup (n = 4) demonstrated MSI-H status in all cases except case 5, which maintained MSS. Similarly, both cases in the MSH6 single-heterogeneity subgroup (with concomitant loss of MLH1 or PMS2 expression) showed MSI-H characteristics.

While our study did not comprehensively characterize the genomic landscape across different tumor regions, existing literature provides relevant insights. Wang et al. established that tumors with complete MLH1/PMS2 loss consistently displayed MLH1 promoter hypermethylation without MMR germline mutations [15]. In contrast, tumors with isolated MMR protein loss frequently harbored somatic MMR mutations and POLE exonuclease domain mutations, correlating with ultra-high tumor mutational burden.

Notably, hMMR double-loss cancers exhibited significantly elevated tumor mutational burden and distinct tumor microenvironment characteristics, including increased tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) infiltration [11]. Riedinger et al. reported that in endometrial cancers with (sub) clonal/mosaic MMR patterns, the underlying mechanisms were distributed as: 66.7% epigenetic silencing, 16.7% germline mutations, and 16.7% somatic mutations [16].Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that (sub)clonal MMR patterns may still be associated with Lynch syndrome germline mutations [14, 19].

MSS/pMMR and MSI-H/dMMR GCs represent two distinct molecular subtypes with divergent clinicopathological characteristics, genomic profiles, and tumor immune microenvironments. The prevalence of resectable MSI-H/dMMR GCs demonstrates considerable geographical variation, with higher reported incidence in Western populations compared to Asian cohorts [1]. This molecular phenotype shows strong stage-dependent distribution, occurring more frequently in early-stage (I-II) disease [20, 21].

Clinically, MSI-H/dMMR GCs typically present with distinctive histopathological features, including mucinous differentiation, intestinal-type histology, and high-grade nuclear atypia [4]. These tumors often display poorly differentiated growth patterns with solid or nested architecture, accompanied by prominent tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) within the stromal compartment [4, 5, 22]. Additional characteristic findings include: 1) Predilection for elderly patients and female gender; 2) Predominant distal gastric localization (particularly antrum and angular region); 3) Lower rates of lymphatic metastasis and perineural invasion; 4) Earlier TNM stage at presentation [21, 23].

Our cohort of 117 patients corroborated these associations, demonstrating significant correlations between MSI-H status and: advanced age (p < 0.01), distal tumor location (p = 0.003), intestinal-type histology (p = 0.02), reduced lymphatic metastasis (p = 0.015), lower clinical stage (I-II; p = 0.008).

The favorable prognostic significance of MSI-H status is well-established, rendering it a robust biomarker for clinical decision-making. The key favorable prognostic implications encompass the following four aspects: 1) Superior surgical outcomes in MSI-H/dMMR GCs without adjuvant chemotherapy [24]; 2) Enhanced immune cell infiltration and response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [5, 25–27]; 3) Demonstrated survival benefits in multiple Phase III trials [25, 28–30]; 4) Significant improvements in disease-free survival (DFS) and 5-year overall survival (OS) [31–33].

Notably, conventional chemotherapy demonstrates limited efficacy in MSI-H/dMMR GCs, with prognosis remaining independent of T/N stage. Current guidelines consequently recommend against adjuvant chemotherapy for stage II/III MSI-H/dMMR GCs [32]. While our study failed to demonstrate superior 45-months DFS in MSI-H/dMMR cases - potentially attributable to limited sample size and shorter follow-up duration. But multivariate analysis confirmed age and clinical stage as independent prognostic factors.

Our study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, the low incidence of dMMR cases resulted in a small sample size from a single institution, which may limit the statistical power of the analyses. Second, as most cases were diagnosed within the past three years, the follow-up duration remains relatively short. The survival outcomes observed in this study may lack sufficient power to detect potential differences in GC survival and might not fully reflect clinical reality. Future multicenter collaborative studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up periods (e.g., 5 or 10 years) will be required to validate our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that histological microdissection can identify paradoxical MSI-H status in tumors exhibiting hMMR deficiency. We further characterize the distinct clinicopathological profile of MSI-H/dMMR GCs, including: 1) Predilection for elderly patients; 2) Predominant distal gastric localization; 3) Intestinal-type histology; 4) Reduced lymphatic metastasis and perineural invasion; 5) Earlier clinical stage presentation.

Although our study did not demonstrate statistically significant improvement in 45-month DFS for MSI-H/dMMR patients. But these findings underscore the clinical importance of comprehensive MMR status evaluation. Personalized therapeutic strategies for GC patients should be established through the integration of precise histopathological diagnosis, accurate interpretation of immunohistochemical findings, and comprehensive molecular profiling.
