Study on the Effectiveness of NH4H2PO4 Powder in Inhibiting the Pyrolysis Gas Flame of Coniferous Woods and Its Suppression Mechanism
Authors: Jian Gao, Kai Zhang, Guangxin Li, Kaikai Liu, Yiqing Chen, Jian Lu, Jianhao Wang, Haoyang Li, Bing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Forest fires are one of the key challenges and focus areas in the field of fire science research today. Due to the complex multiphase coupling and rapid spread characteristics of forest fuels, such fires are often difficult to extinguish directly. As a typical solid fuel, the flame spread of wood is essentially the result of the coupling between solid-phase pyrolysis and gas-phase combustion chemical reactions. Therefore, by inhibiting either solid-phase pyrolysis or gas-phase combustion, the spread of flames in wood can be effectively restrained, providing important support for subsequent fire suppression. Previous studies on forest fire suppression have largely focused on the macroscopic inhibitory effects of various extinguishing agents on overall fire behavior. Although fire suppression effectiveness parameters can be obtained through repeated experiments, they often lack in-depth analysis of the microscopic interactions between extinguishing agents and flames. To address this, this study begins with the key aspect of gas-phase combustion suppression, attempting to construct a scaled-down experimental model to focus on the chemical suppression effects of extinguishing agents in the gas phase. The study selects typical coniferous species and uses pyrolysis analysis to obtain their gas-phase combustion components. Based on this, a cup-type burner experimental system is developed to extinguish the pyrolyzed gas flame of coniferous wood with ammonium dihydrogen phosphate powder. Although this system simplifies the solid fuel and multiphase combustion interface found in real forest fires, it helps explore, under controlled conditions, the dynamic evolution of flame suppression by powders, revealing their chemical suppression characteristics and mechanisms. This, in turn, provides theoretical and experimental support for understanding and further improving the effectiveness of powder extinguishing agents in complex fire scenarios.

Introduction

In recent years, the importance of forest fire prevention and control has increasingly come to the forefront as public safety awareness has heightened. Forest fires exhibit characteristics such as intense combustion, significant energy release, and rapid propagation, making them difficult to extinguish directly. Therefore, effectively suppressing flame propagation has become one of the critical steps in extinguishing forest fires. As a typical solid fuel, wood initially undergoes pyrolysis when heated, releasing combustible gases; these pyrolysis gases then diffuse outward and mix with oxygen in the air, resulting in flame formation. The propagation of wood flames is essentially a process of coupling solid-phase pyrolysis with gas-phase combustion. Thus, either suppressing solid-phase pyrolysis or obstructing gas-phase combustion can effectively hinder the spread of forest fires.

Previous research on suppressing wood combustion has primarily focused on two aspects: on one hand, various substances have been utilized to prepare flame retardants to enhance the intrinsic fire resistance of wood. For example, studies by Yao Chunhua et al. demonstrate that treatments with magnesium hydroxide, magnesium chloride, and basic magnesium carbonate can significantly reduce the smoke emissions from wood chips and lower the heat release rate. Chen Desheng et al. analyzed the advantages and preparation processes of rare earth suppressants. Taghiyari − investigated the fire resistance of metallic silver, while Stevens et al. confirmed that phosphorus-containing compounds (such as alkyl and phenyl phosphates) can enhance the flame retardancy of wood. On the other hand, research has examined the suppression efficacy of various extinguishing agents on forest fires. Liodakis et al. investigated the effects of airflow, sample size, and additives (such as (NH4)2HPO4 (DAP) and (NH4)2SO4 (AS)) on the ignition characteristics of pine needles, finding that airflow significantly affects ignition delay while sample size primarily influences combustion duration. VOLKOV et al. studied the effectiveness of different water-based extinguishing methods (such as single large water droplets, water mist, and surface water film) in suppressing wood flames, with water mist showing the shortest extinguishing time.

For forest fires in open spaces, liquid and solid extinguishing agents are more suitable than inert gases or aerosol extinguishing agents. − Ammonium phosphate salts have become one of the main suppressants due to their good efficacy in extinguishing solid combustible fires (including forest fires), low cost, and ease of storage. Therefore, this study selects NH4H2PO4 powder as the extinguishing agent to investigate its suppression of wood pyrolysis gas flames. To facilitate research, this study adopts a gas fire research approach to establish a small-scale simplified flame model. Commonly used small-scale gas phase flame research devices include premixed flame burners, − opposing diffusion flame burners, and coaxial diffusion flame burners (Cup-burners). Since the flow fields of premixed and opposing flames can be approximated as one-dimensional, they are often evaluated based on laminar combustion rates or extinguishing stretch rates. However, actual fires are influenced by buoyancy plumes and large-scale vortices, resulting in dynamically complex flame structures that differ significantly from laboratory stable flames, making extinguishing efficacy assessments based on the latter potentially unsuitable for real-world scenarios. In order to better assess extinguishing efficacy in real fire situations, ICI’s Hirst and Booth developed the Cup-burner. The nonpremixed entrained diffusion flames produced by this device exhibit instability due to the development of annular vortices and air convection. Its characteristics (such as the separation method of fuel from oxidant, flame flickering frequency, bottom stability, etc.) are highly similar to buoyant diffusion flames in free-burning conditions. From a fire safety perspective, such low stretch rate flames are significantly hazardous, and their stability exceeds that of actual flames, leading to experimental measurements of the minimum extinguishing concentration (MMC) of extinguishing agents including safety margins, typically higher than what is actually needed. For this reason, results from Cup-burner experiments are widely utilized in the field of fire protection engineering as an important standard for evaluating extinguishing agent performance and establishing safety regulations. However, the standard Cup-burner is only suitable for gas-phase extinguishing agent inputs and cannot be directly applied to the study of condensed-phase extinguishing agents such as NH4H2PO4 powder. Therefore, this chapter will make targeted modifications to the classic Cup-burner based on improvements to the Bunsen burner flame. The improved device will enable a more effective investigation of the process by which NH4H2PO4 powder suppresses wood pyrolysis gas flames.

In summary, this research will explore the suppression effects of NH4H2PO4 powder on the pyrolysis gas flames of coniferous trees based on the improved experimental apparatus. Specifically, it will include: determining the minimum extinguishing concentration (MEC) of NH4H2PO4 powder for extinguishing coniferous pyrolysis gas flames; systematically investigating the influence of different particle sizes of the powder on the MEC; and analyzing the elementary reaction mechanisms of the interactions between the extinguishing agent and the flames using reaction kinetics analysis software. The research findings will provide important experimental data and theoretical foundations for the development of efficient extinguishing agents suitable for forest fires.

Experimental Setup and Methods

Composition Analysis of Conifer Pyrolysis Gas

Coniferous trees have been selected as the subject of this study due to their widespread global distribution, abundant resources, and close ties to human life. After being crushed and sieved (with a particle size of 100 mesh, approximately 0.15 mm), the samples were subjected to oven drying. The combustion process of wood follows a pyrolysis pathway that proceeds from the surface to the interior: the outer layer participates directly in combustion, while the inner core undergoes pyrolysis under high-temperature and oxygen-deficient conditions, subsequently releasing its volatile products to participate in the reaction. In light of this characteristic, this study chose to conduct pyrolysis experiments of coniferous trees under an inert gas (helium) atmosphere. Approximately 3.5 mg of the sample was weighed and placed in an Al2O3 crucible, then heated to 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a helium flow rate of 70 mL/min. High-purity helium continuously transported the pyrolytic volatile products away from the sample area to minimize possible secondary reactions. The composition analysis of the pyrolysis gases was performed using a Thermo Scientific ISQ series gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS, ITQ1100 mass spectrometer).

Construction of Cup-Burner Experiment System

This experiment uses the modified Cup-burner system shown in Figure . The system consists of five main components: a gas control system, the Cup-burner combustion chamber, a powder delivery device, a high-speed imaging system, and a powder collection system. During the experiment, pyrolysis gases are mixed with air and introduced into the Cup-burner, where they are ignited at the cup mouth to form a diffusion flame. NH4H2PO4 powder is mechanically pushed into the powder outlet by a mechanical paddle and is carried by the airflow to the cup mouth, where it interacts with the flame. To ensure stable airflow while accommodating powder particle passage, a three-layer 50 mesh/cm metal screen is installed at the burner inlet. Given that some powder may adhere to the delivery pipe, the combustion cup wall, and the inner wall of the glass cover tube, a powder collection device is installed at the outlet of the quartz glass cover tube to measure the actual amount of powder reaching the combustion cup mouth.

Cup-burner experimental system.

The experimental procedure for determining the Minimum Extinguishing Concentration (MEC) of NH4H2PO4 powder to extinguish the flame is as follows: First, set the air volume flow rate to 40 L/min and introduce the pyrolysis gas from coniferous wood, adjusting its flow rate to stabilize the flame height at 8 cm. After the flame burns stably for 60 s, the powder delivery is initiated, and the powder concentration is increased by one step every 10 s. To avoid overestimating the MEC due to excessively long extinguishing times, the target extinguishing time is controlled between 50 and 60 s. Finally, the experiment is repeated 10 times, and the average MEC value is taken as the result. The Cup-burner experimental system developed in this study has already been validated in the paper “Research on the Combustion Suppression Mechanism of Red Pine Pyrolysis Gas and Its Application”, and thus will not be elaborated further here.

Therefore, this study will select the pyrolysis gas components at 350 °C, 450 °C, and 550 °C as the research subjects, with 450 °C chosen as an intermediate value to reflect the changes in gas composition between 350 and 550 °C to some extent.

The study revealed that regardless of particle size, as the amount of extinguishing agent increased, the flame color transitioned from bright yellow to dark yellow, and the base of the flame gradually became unstable until extinguishment occurred. However, compared to larger particle size extinguishing agents, smaller particle size extinguishing agents caused a more gradual process of flame instability, with characteristic changes being clearly observable: as shown in Figure c, in stage (2), the base of the flame began to detach from the cup opening; in stage (3), the detachment progressed further, accompanied by slight fluctuations; and in stage (4), a noticeable instability of the flame base was evident, demonstrating a more gradual dynamic of instability.

Analysis of Minimum Fire Extinguishing Concentration

This study measured the minimum extinguishing concentration (MEC) of different particle sizes of NH4H2PO4 powder for extinguishing the pyrolysis gas flames at 450 and 550 °C. For different particle sizes, it is necessary to precisely control the amount of powder fed through the powder delivery device, as both excessive and insufficient amounts can lead to significant measurement errors in the MEC.

Figure illustrates the input concentration (i.e., the concentration of powder flowing from the powder storage carousel to the outlet) and output concentration (i.e., the concentration of powder being expelled from the Cup-burner cup under airflow) for various particle sizes at different speed levels. For convenience in expression, nonuniformly increasing speed levels were used for classification.

Powder concentrations on the lower and upper sides of powders with different particle sizes at each speed level.

In the Figure , (a–c) correspond to powders with particle sizes of 30 μm–40 μm, 20 μm–30 μm, and 10 μm–20 μm, respectively, showing the input and output concentrations at speed levels 1–10. The analysis indicates that the input concentrations for all particle sizes are generally higher than the output concentrations. This difference arises from some particles adhering to the walls of the pipes or devices during the airflow transport process, as well as sedimentation effects caused by particle collisions.

Further observation of the three graphs in Figure reveals that both the input and output amounts increase linearly with the rise in speed levels. However, at lower speed levels, the difference between input and output amounts is relatively small, while this difference gradually increases as the speed levels rise. This is because, at low speeds, the powder concentration is lower, resulting in fewer particle collisions and minimal sedimentation losses. Conversely, at high speeds, the powder concentration increases, leading to more frequent particle collisions and enhanced sedimentation effects, which consequently increases the relative loss proportion of the output compared to the input.

To ensure the statistical reliability of the experimental results, this study conducted 10 repeated tests for each set of experimental conditions. The speed level at which the flame extinguished and its corresponding extinguishing concentration were recorded for each test, and the arithmetic average of the 10 concentrations was taken as the final minimum extinguishing concentration (MEC) value, as shown in Table .

The data in Table indicates that the Minimum Extinguishing Concentration (MEC) is not only influenced by the components of the pyrolysis gas but also closely related to the particle size of NH4H2PO4 powder. At the same particle size, the MEC value for extinguishing the 550 °C pyrolysis gas flame is significantly higher than that for the 450 °C pyrolysis gas flame. This is attributed to the reduction of CO2 content in the 550 °C pyrolysis gas, with a significant increase in the content of H2 and CH4. Under the same temperature conditions, as the particle size of the powder decreases (from 30 μm–40 to 10 μm–20 μm), the MEC value shows an upward trend: the powder with a dominant particle size of 30 μm–40 μm has the lowest MEC value, which is significantly lower than that of the other two particle sizes, while the MEC values of powders with dominant particle sizes of 20 μm–30 and 10 μm–20 μm show relatively small differences. It is noteworthy that, although it is generally believed in the field of fire engineering that reducing the particle size of extinguishing agents improves their extinguishing efficiency, the Cup-burner MEC experimental results in this study show that the MEC value of 30 μm–40 μm powder is actually lower than that of 10 μm–20 μm powder. This phenomenon indicates that the effect of the particle size of extinguishing agents on fire suppression efficiency is closely influenced by the characteristics of the experimental system (fire model). This study suggests that the reason for this phenomenon is that the smaller particle size powders, after entering the flame root with the airflow, are carried away by the airflow before fully reacting due to their small particle size, preventing them from fully exerting their extinguishing effect. This leads to the observed MEC values not showing a trend of smaller particle size corresponding to better extinguishing efficiency. To validate this hypothesis, powders with a particle size of 40–50 μm were selected for further experimentation. The experimental results show that the MEC value for extinguishing the 450 °C pyrolysis gas is 36.136g/m3, and for the 550 °C pyrolysis gas, it is 44.652g/m3. It can be seen that, as the particle size increases, the MEC value further decreases, but the change is not significant and even weak. When the particle size was further increased, powders with a particle size of 50–60 μm were used, and during the powder delivery process, noticeable powder residue was found in the pipeline. This indicates that the particle size was too large, and the airflow was insufficient to carry all the powder to the flame root for reaction, making these results unreferable. From the above, it can be concluded that the reasoning behind the earlier observed phenomena is reliable.

Motif Reaction Sensitivity Analysis

Based on the characteristics of the flame in the Cup-burner, the following assumptions are made in this study: When various gases are mixed and flow into the Cup-burner, their overall motion characteristics are the same, and the extinguishing agents do not affect the flame morphology. Therefore, the PSR model is selected as the simplified model for the Cup-burner experiment, ignoring transport and mixing effects. To reveal the mechanism by which NH4H2PO4 suppresses the pyrolysis gas flame, a detailed gas-phase suppression model is established under the assumption of neglecting solid/gas phase conversion suppression. The physical heat absorption effect of ammonium dihydrogen phosphate powder in the flame suppression process is relatively low compared to its chemical extinguishing effect, and since this study mainly focuses on the mechanism of its chemical extinguishing effect, it is acceptable to neglect some of the physical heat absorption effects.

This mechanistic model consists of three submechanisms: the USC-Mech simulates the thermal runaway gas/air reactions, the mechanism developed by Okafor et al. describes the reactions of NH3, and the mechanism by Korobeinichev et al. analyzes the reactions of phosphorus compounds, ultimately forming a complete system consisting of 197 species and 1685 reactions. To validate the effectiveness of the kinetic mechanism model, the MEC value was predicted using the PSR model based on this mechanism and compared with the experimental values. The initial temperature of the reactor was set to 2500K, the reactor volume to 50 cm3, and the pressure to 1 atm. The inlet gas temperature was set to 300 K, and the equivalence ratio was set to 1. The pyrolysis gases at 450 and 550 °C, along with nitrogen as the fuel and extinguishing agent, were selected. The reactor reached equilibrium after a sufficient residence time. When the flame extinction time is longer than the residence time, the temperature inside the reactor was used to characterize the chemical reaction. The temperature variation of the two pyrolysis gases in the reactor with residence time in the presence of nitrogen was obtained. By substituting the calculated residence time, a relationship diagram of reactor temperature as a function of NH4H2PO4 concentration for the two pyrolysis gases was produced, as shown in Figure .

Shows the relationship between the temperature of the two pyrolysis gas reactors, residence time, and NH4H2PO4 concentration.

As shown in Figure a, with the increase in time, the reactor temperature suddenly rises from the initial 300 K to above 1500K at a certain moment, and then stabilizes. This time corresponds to the flame extinction time of nitrogen at that concentration. According to experimental results averaged over ten trials, the minimum extinguishing concentrations of nitrogen for extinguishing the pyrolysis gas flames at 450 and 550 °C were 15.8 L/min and 20.64 L/min, respectively (this experimental process is quite common and similar to previous ones, so it is not elaborated further). The corresponding flame extinction times were 0.414 ms and 0.515 ms.

After determining the flame extinction times of nitrogen for the 450 and 550 °C pyrolysis gas flames, the residence times were set to 0.414 ms and 0.515 ms, respectively. The extinguishing agent was then changed to NH4H2PO4. This allowed the determination of the relationship between reactor temperature and NH4H2PO4 concentration, as shown in Figure b. From the graph, it can be observed that when the NH4H2PO4 concentration was 5.3% and 6.6%, the reactor temperature dropped instantly until it reached 300 K. Therefore, it can be concluded that the minimum extinguishing concentrations of NH4H2PO4 for extinguishing the 450 and 550 °C pyrolysis gas flames are 5.3% and 6.6%, respectively.

From the earlier experimental results, it was found that due to the different particle sizes of NH4H2PO4 powder, the minimum extinguishing concentration experimental values ranged from 3.5% to 5.4% for 450 °C and from 4.1% to 6.3% for 550 °C. The PSR model did not consider the effect of particle size, and the suppression effect of solid-to-gas conversion was neglected in the mechanism model. Therefore, there is a certain error between the calculated values from the PSR model and the experimental values. However, the calculated values still fall within the range of experimental values, indicating that the kinetic mechanism is highly reliable.

The analysis revealed that R1098 (HOPO2 + H = PO2 + H2O), R1116 (PO2 + H + M = HOPO + M), and R1120 (HOPO + OH = PO2 + H2O) are the three key phosphorus reactions directly related to flame suppression. Meanwhile, R1092 (PO2 + OH + M = HOPO2 + M) is the key phosphorus reaction indirectly related to flame suppression, as it is involved in the consumption and recombination for the HOPO2 ↔ PO2 reaction. The remaining reactions are core reactions that regulate combustion, with R1 (H + O2 = O + OH) exhibiting the highest positive sensitivity coefficient, which continues to increase with the addition of the extinguishing agent, thereby playing a dominant role in the overall reaction rate.

Phosphorus-containing species (HOPO2/HOPO/PO2) exert their main suppression effect by reducing the reaction activity. The sensitivity coefficients of the representative reactions R1092, R1098, R1116, and R1120 show a nonmonotonic characteristic, initially increasing with the concentration of the extinguishing agent before subsequently decreasing.

Element Reaction Yield Analysis

Based on the sensitivity analysis of the pyrolysis gas/air/H3PO4/NH3 combustion system, it has been confirmed that phosphorus-containing species (HOPO2, HOPO, PO2) and their related reactions R1092 (PO2 + OH + M = HOPO2 + M), R1098 (HOPO2 + H = PO2 + H2O), R1116 (PO2 + H + M = HOPO + M), and R1120 (HOPO + OH = PO2 + H2O) have a significant inhibitory effect on the combustion reaction rate.

Through reaction pathway (ROP) analysis, the quantitative contributions of the elementary reactions to the net production of substances are evaluated. Positive ROP values indicate the generation of substances, while negative values correspond to their consumption. This analysis allows for a deeper understanding of how specific reactions affect the overall combustion dynamics and the mechanisms by which phosphorus components contribute to flame suppression.

Figure illustrates the dynamics of PO2 generation and consumption when H3PO4/NH3 is added to the pyrolysis gas/air flames at 450 and 550 °C. The two temperature systems display similar patterns: R1120 (HOPO + OH → PO2 + H2O) serves as the core generation pathway for PO2, while R1116 (PO2 + H + M → HOPO + M) represents the primary consumption pathway. These reactions form a suppression cycle between HOPO and PO2, which reduces the overall reaction rate through the catalytic recombination of free radicals (H + OH → H2O).

Productivity of PO2 from pyrolysis gas/air/H3PO4/NH3 diffusion flame at 450 and 550 °C.

Additionally, R1092 and R1098 establish a secondary suppression cycle between HOPO2 and PO2, which cooperatively catalyzes the production of H2O. Notably, the peak rates of PO2 generation and consumption exhibit a nonmonotonic characteristic, initially increasing and then decreasing as the molar fraction of H3PO4/NH3 in the gas phase increases. This suggests that there is an optimal concentration of these extinguishing agents for effective flame suppression, after which the effectiveness may decline.

Conclusion

This study systematically reveals the combustion characteristics of pyrolysis gas from coniferous wood and the suppression mechanisms of NH4H2PO4 powder. The experimental results indicate that stable combustion conditions exist for pyrolysis gas at 350 °C, while the pyrolysis reactions are nearly complete at 550 °C (with component differences <5% compared to 600 °C). Thus, 350 °C, 450 °C, and 550 °C pyrolysis gases were chosen as the subjects of study (see Table ). Through improvements to the Cup-burner system, the following observations were made:1.As the amount of extinguishing agent added increases, the flame transitions through a sequence of changes from bright yellow to dark yellow, then to instability at the root, and finally extinguishment; however, small particle sizes (10–20 μm) result in significantly stronger instability compared to larger particle sizes (see Figure ).2.There is a notable settling loss during the powder delivery process (where input concentration > output concentration), and under high rotational speeds, the loss rate increases due to intensified particle collisions (see Figure ).3.The determination of the minimum extinguishing concentration (MEC) indicates that the MEC value for the 550 °C pyrolysis gas is significantly higher than that of the 450 °C system due to increased concentrations of H2/CH4 (see Table ). Notably, the 30–40 μm particle size powder exhibits the lowest MEC value (approximately 30% lower than that of the 10–20 μm size), contradicting the traditional understanding that reducing particle size enhances extinguishing efficiency. This finding confirms that the performance of extinguishing agents is highly dependent on the characteristics of the fire model.
