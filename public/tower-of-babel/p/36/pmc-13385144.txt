Cross-training of nurses during a global pandemic: a two-stage stochastic programming approach
Authors: Hendrik Winzer, Jens Bengtsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring a global pandemic, hospitals face challenges of uncertain patient influx and increased risk of absenteeism among medical personnel, both of which adversely impact patient safety. To address these challenges, we propose a two-stage stochastic program for nurse staffing that incorporates uncertainties in patient demand and absenteeism. Our model supports two critical staffing decisions to optimize nurse allocation. The first is a tactical decision regarding the number of nurses to be cross-trained, and the second is an operational decision concerning the number of temporary nurses that need to be hired. Applied to data from a Norwegian tertiary public hospital during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, our model identifies a bottleneck in intensive care unit nurse availability. Sensitivity analyses reveal that the effect of increasing the penalty for untreated patients is much larger than the effect of changes in cross-training parameters, such as the number of trainees per mentoring nurse or cross-training cost. Moreover, we highlight that cross-training helps to reduce bottlenecks and improves future service levels. Thus, cross-training remains advantageous overall, despite temporarily reducing nurse availability during the cross-training period. Despite the study’s limited scope on a single patient pathway and its focus solely on nurses, it provides valuable insights into nurse staffing strategies for practitioners. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to model cross-training as a tactical staffing decision and its implications for workforce availability during the cross-training period, while also accounting for an increased risk of absenteeism.Keywords: Two-stage stochastic programming, Nurse staffing, Cross-training, Absenteeism, Hospital, Tactical staffing decisions

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic showed how infectious disease outbreaks can disrupt hospital operations by increasing uncertainty in patient influx and raising the risk of absenteeism. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), workforce-related issues, such as high absenteeism and skill shortages, were the most common causes of disruption during the COVID-19 pandemic [1]. These issues can compromise patient safety and, in extreme cases, may pose a risk to human life. Consequently, hospitals must identify mitigation strategies to overcome these challenges, especially for nurses, the largest employee group in a hospital. Temporary hiring of nurses was one option, but it was limited during the COVID-19 pandemic, and its long-term viability is constrained by projections from the WHO indicating a shortage of 1.4 million healthcare workers in Europe by 2030, with nurses comprising a substantial share [2]. Greater flexibility in nurse staffing is therefore needed to expand workforce capacity. Cross-training enables nurses to perform roles outside their usual responsibilities, thereby allowing flexible assignments. However, cross-training comes at a cost: e-learning and training infrastructure must be established, and nurses (trainees and mentors) are unavailable for patient treatment during cross-training, both in classroom teaching and supervised practice [2, 3]. As the cross-training decision is both irreversible and resource-intensive, it must be carefully balanced with the potential gains in workforce capacity.

Nurse staffing has attracted scholars’ interest since the early twentieth century, reflecting the critical role nurses play as intermediaries between doctors and patients [4]. With the rise of multidisciplinary patient pathways and increasing departmental specialization, integrating skill requirements into staffing decisions has become more important [5]. Consequently, specialized treatment can only be performed by nurses with specific skill sets, which staffing decisions must incorporate to be effective [6]. In this study, we define skills as the competency to perform an assigned task without errors while ensuring adequate patient safety. Operations research methods, particularly stochastic programming, have been applied to model uncertainty in nurse staffing [7–9]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no prior study has jointly considered cross-training and temporary hiring under uncertain patient demand and absenteeism within a two-stage stochastic program. Therefore, we extend the model in Maas et al. [8] by explicitly accounting for nurse unavailability during the cross-training period. This reflects the requirement for mentoring nurses during supervised practice and ensures that a minimum patient-to-nurse ratio is maintained throughout.

We apply our two-stage stochastic program to data from a Norwegian tertiary public hospital during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, we examine how non-treatment costs, cross-training costs, and the number of trainees per mentoring nurse affect the total cost, service levels, cross-training decisions, and temporary hiring decisions.

Literature review

Nurse staffing

Lewinski-Corwin [4] was the first to address the problem of determining adequate nurse staffing levels to meet patient demand. Decisions regarding nurse staffing are critical for ensuring patient safety, yet they are complicated by factors such as the diversity of patient diseases and varying acuity levels [9]. Despite these complexities, many operations managers continue to rely on static patient-to-nurse ratios for capacity planning [10]. An alternative strategy involves allowing patients to choose their hospital by making nurse staffing levels transparent, although this primarily applies to elective appointments [11]. Patient-to-nurse ratios often do not accurately reflect the actual workload that nurses experience, as these ratios differ across regions and nurse competencies [12, 13]. Nonetheless, there is a consensus that lower patient-to-nurse ratios improve patient outcomes [10, 14]. Another downside of static patient-to-nurse ratios for capacity planning is their inability to accommodate all possible patient demand scenarios, necessitating in extreme cases mitigation strategies such as overtime, temporary hiring, or reallocating nurses within the hospital [15]. However, these mitigation strategies are unsustainable long-term due to financial limitations and potential staff overload, which may result in higher employee turnover. Therefore, nurse staffing research has sought to balance adequate staffing, patient outcomes and financial expenses.

There is a higher risk of understaffing when nurse staffing decisions ignore variability and uncertainty in patient demand [16]. To address this problem, academics developed decision support tools, such as the RAFAELA workforce planning system or the “Safer Nursing Care Tool”, that offer data-driven approaches to determine optimal nurse staffing levels [17, 18]. As these tools are based on historical data, they require accurate baseline data to avoid understaffing [19]. Furthermore, these tools often overlook variations in patient demand and changes in nurse skill mix or availability beyond seasonal fluctuations.

Nurse staffing strategies addressing skills and absenteeism

Effective nurse staffing decisions necessitate consideration of the skills required by different diseases and patient pathways [8, 9, 20, 21]. The workforce planning literature distinguishes between hierarchical and categorical skills [6]. Hierarchical skills are universal and allow higher-skilled nurses to perform more complex tasks within their roles and, when needed, substitute for lower-skilled nurses at higher costs, but this flexibility is limited by role constraints and higher costs [6]. Categorical skills acquired through cross-training are more context-specific and require additional training when tasks, settings, or patient requirements change.

Cross-trained or pooled nurses can be allocated across departments, thereby increasing flexibility, allowing for better adaptation to changes in demand, and reducing costs [22–25]. However, cross-training incurs additional costs, and cross-trained nurses are less efficient than their specialized counterparts [24, 26]. Consequently, there is a trade-off between the benefits of increased flexibility and the drawbacks of reduced efficiency. Centrally coordinated cross-training achieves greater flexibility than decentralized approaches [27]. Moreover, coordinated cross-training within a primary healthcare network can diversify services and reduce costs [28]. However, in complex environments, such as intensive care units (ICUs), hiring temporary nurses is often more cost-effective [10].

Absenteeism is another key factor for nurse staffing that can arise from endogenous factors, like workload-related stress, as well as exogenous factors, such as caring for a sick relative or child [29–32]. While overtime can provide a short-term solution, assuming legal compliance, temporary hiring is a more practical alternative [30]. To address this challenge, Ryu and Jiang [33] applied a distributionally robust optimization approach to design layouts for cross-trained nurse pools, considering demand variability and absenteeism, and found centralized workforce planning most beneficial in departments with high absenteeism. Maas et al. [8] proposed a two-stage stochastic program with strategic decisions on organizational layout and operational decisions on hiring additional nurses to manage absenteeism. Their study highlighted the impact of additional nurses on optimal staffing levels but did not account for cross-training and the unavailability of nurses during the cross-training period.

Nurse staffing during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic

As a case study, we selected Akershus University Hospital, a Norwegian tertiary public hospital in southeastern Norway. The hospital serves about 560 thousand inhabitants. During the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic in March and April 2020, the hospital faced two primary challenges.

First, the average length of stay for COVID-19 patients was longer than that for patients with similar respiratory diseases [34–36]. Patients with severe COVID-19 required admission to the ICU for respiratory support and close monitoring. Patient care was categorized into three categories: general wards, the Intermediate Care Unit (IMCU) for ICU patients not requiring respiratory support, and the ICU for those requiring respiratory support and close monitoring [37]. Early estimates indicated that 4–20% of all individuals who tested positive for COVID-19 required hospitalization, with 25% admitted to the ICU [38]. However, these ratios depended on the testing capacity and the evolving epidemiology. To expand capacity, the hospital procured additional medical equipment (e.g., respirators), freed up physical space, and cross-trained nurses. At the peak of the first wave, 62 COVID-19 patients were hospitalized at Akershus University Hospital: 38 in general wards and 24 in the IMCU/ICU [37]. With elective appointments canceled or postponed, additional nurses became available for COVID-19 patient care after completing cross-training, which included classroom teaching or online learning and supervised practice.

Second, absenteeism increased by 45% in March 2020 compared to March 2019. This increase was attributed by isolation, quarantine, childcare necessitated by school closures, and travel bans affecting cross-border staff from Sweden [39, 40]. Regulations varied by region, based on local incidence rates.

In conclusion, these challenges forced the hospital to maintain patient safety with limited staffing. Therefore, our study aims to develop a decision support tool for nurse cross-training and temporary hiring under uncertain patient demand and increased risk of absenteeism.

Methodology

Two-stage stochastic programming

We chose a stochastic programming approach since it is well suited for our underlying decision-making problem that involves uncertainty. This method yields robust solutions by accounting for parameter distributions and allowing for recourse actions to refine initial decisions [41]. Furthermore, it offers decision support across a range of scenarios, rather than merely focusing on average scenarios [42]. In contrast, a deterministic approach that replaces parameters, such as patient demand and absenteeism, with their expected values may provide an inaccurate representation of reality and lead to severe consequences for patients. For example, underestimating absenteeism could cause staff shortages; while overestimating it could lead to inefficient allocation of financial resources that might be better used elsewhere, such as in the procurement of personal protective equipment.

The sequential decision structure of our two-stage stochastic program is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Sequential decision structure and information structure, including related outcomes

At the start of stage 1, the model selects exactly one cross-training option for each origin-target category combination, based on predicted patient demand for the upcoming week (7 days), which corresponds to the cross-training period. Each cross-training option specifies the nurses' initial category (general ward, IMCU, ICU), the category they are cross-trained into (general ward, IMCU, ICU), and the number of nurses. After the cross-training options are selected, patient demand in stage 1 is revealed, and the model determines the number of treated and untreated patients in each category.

At the start of stage 2, the cross-trained nurses become available. The model then chooses exactly one temporary hiring option per category (general ward, IMCU, ICU) based on the predicted absenteeism and patient demand. We assume that temporarily hired nurses become immediately available, allowing the model to determine the number of treated and untreated patients in each category during stage 2.

The cross-training options are predefined and may depend on the availability of trainers or the capacity of training infrastructure. Similarly, the temporary hiring options are predefined and reflect the limitations of external nurse availability.

The penalty function is modeled as a soft constraint, which allows for patient redirection to nearby hospitals when demand exceeds capacity. This approach is consistent with previous research, which shows that regional cooperation can prevent congestion and reduce mortality [43, 44]. By contrast, hard constraints would result in overly conservative, worst-case solutions.

Data collection

The initial parameters for our two-stage stochastic program are publicly accessible from three sources: 1) OECD, 2) Norwegian Directorate for Higher Education and Skills, and 3) Akershus University Hospital. First, we obtained data on the average patient-to-nurse and nurse-to-doctor ratios in Norway from OECD [13]. Second, we collected Norwegian data on nurse specialization in the ICU and other specializations, as well as related salary information [45]. Third, we extracted case-specific information from reports published by Akershus University Hospital [46, 47]. These reports include the number of COVID-19 patients in each category between March 10 and April 14, 2020. Additionally, they provide data on personnel absenteeism, the average cost of temporarily hired staff, and the syllabus for cross-training. We triangulated the total number of hospitalized COVID-19 patients at Akershus University Hospital using COVID-19 information from the Norwegian Directorate of Health [48].

Parameter estimation

We derived the weekly treatment costs per patient from the literature [49] (see Appendix A): 417,524 NOK for ICU, 222,699 NOK for IMCU, and 49,982 NOK for general ward. The cost for untreated patients was set to twice the weekly treatment cost per patient in each category, reflecting opportunity costs from lost revenue and additional health expenditures when patients are transferred to other hospitals in the region. Non-treatment costs are assumed to be linear, representing consistent administrative and revenue losses regardless of patient volume.

We adopted minimum patient-to-nurse ratios from the literature: 2:1 for ICU, 10:1 for IMCU, and 20:1 for general ward. These ratios were used to define the minimum number of nurses in each category [50]. Cross-training costs and temporary hiring costs include both fixed and variable components (see Appendix B). Temporary hiring costs follow a progressive structure that reflects the increased scarcity of nurses during a global pandemic. Each mentoring nurse can train five general ward nurses, three IMCU nurses, or three ICU nurses. Based on the calculated total number of nurses at the case hospital, we defined the initial capacity of qualified nurses for COVID-19 treatment in each category, which is linked to the proportion of cancelled and postponed appointments.

Using data from Akershus University Hospital, we generated scenarios separately for patient demand and absenteeism as these represent distinct sources of uncertainty in the model (see Appendix B1, B2). For each, we calculated the weekly changes and fitted a probability distribution to the resulting data. Among the candidate distributions considered – exponential, lognormal, normal, and uniform – a normal distribution provided the best fit for both patient demand and absenteeism, as determined by minimizing the sum of squared errors. Furthermore, the Shapiro–Wilk normality test confirmed that the weekly changes in patient demand for the IMCU and ICU followed a normal distribution. We then estimated the mean and standard deviation of the fitted normal distributions and discretized them into five scenarios per category and stage. In stage 2, each scenario represents a combined realization of patient demand and absenteeism, capturing any dependency between these two uncertain parameters. Five scenarios were selected to capture the range of uncertainty while keeping the model computationally manageable. Moreover, increasing the number of scenarios could raise estimation error without necessarily improving solution quality.

Sensitivity analyses

Our research followed an experimental design comprising two phases. First, we applied our two-stage stochastic program to the data from Akershus University Hospital, retrospectively optimizing the total cost. Second, we conducted three sensitivity analyses, each altering one parameter: 1) non-treatment cost, 2) cross-training cost, and 3) number of trainees per mentoring nurse. These analyses provide insights into the relationships between the decision variables (e.g., cross-training) and total cost. We implemented the model in Python (Spyder 5.5.1) with Gurobi 9.1.1, using 15 absenteeism and 15 demand states as inputs. It included 468 integer variables (288 binary), 813 constraints, and ran in 0.05 s on an Intel(R) Core (TM) Ultra 7 155U CPU, with a gap of 0.01% for the base case.

Analysis

Case study

As a case study, we applied our two-stage stochastic program to data from Akershus University Hospital. The results highlight nurse availability in the ICU as a bottleneck because it is the only category with unmet patient demand. Therefore, both cross-training and temporary hiring focus exclusively on the ICU, and these measures reduce the number of untreated patients from 95 in stage 1 to eight in stage 2. As a result, the service level increases from 83% in stage 1 to 99% in stage 2. However, cross-training nurses temporarily reduces ICU capacity during stage 1, which increases the number of untreated patients in stage 1. Despite this impact, cross-training significantly enhances ICU capacity in stage 2.

Sensitivity analyses

We now present the results from three sensitivity analyses in which we altered exactly one of the following input parameters from 10 to 200% of the base value: 1) the non-treatment cost, 2) the cross-training cost, and 3) the number of trainees per mentoring nurse. For each analysis, we show how changes in the parameter affect three outcomes: the expected total cost (and its breakdown into five cost components), the expected overall service level for each stage, and the expected number of cross-trained or temporarily hired nurses. Detailed results for each sensitivity analysis are provided in Appendices C, D, and E.

Sensitivity to non-treatment cost

The cost per untreated patient reflects opportunity costs from lost revenue and additional community health costs, such as when patients are transferred to other hospitals due to local capacity limitations.

The expected total cost increases steadily from 67.5 million NOK to 366.2 million NOK as the non-treatment cost per patient grows from 10 to 200% of the base value, with non-treatment and treatment costs being the primary cost drivers (see Fig. 2a). When the non-treatment cost for each patient reaches 50% or more of the base value, the model begins treating patients in all categories during both stages. This behavior is expected, given the assumption that the base non-treatment cost is twice the treatment cost. At the threshold of 50%, the model initiates cross-training starting with 170 nurses for the ICU, and from 70% onward, it also begins temporary hiring of nurses for the ICU. Notably, the model initially chooses to cross-train 220 ICU nurses, accepting a lower capacity in stage 1 to minimize hiring costs in stage 2. As the non-treatment cost for each patient increases, hiring becomes more advantageous, and the number of cross-trained ICU nurses decreases to 180. Supervised practice temporarily reduces ICU nurse availability, resulting in a service level of 83.0% in stage 1 and 95–99% in stage 2. When the non-treatment cost exceeds 130% of the base value, the model reaches a steady state, with no additional ICU nurses temporarily hired or cross-trained.

Sensitivity to cross-training cost

A varying cross-training cost simulates potential changes in the cross-training scope, such as curriculum adjustments or the adoption of e-learning platforms, which could reduce the need for classroom teaching.

When the cross-training cost is varied from 10 to 200% of the base value, the total cost increases steadily, but only by 1.9% (+ 5.3 million NOK) (see Fig. 3a). Again, most of the total cost is associated with treated and untreated patients. The service level in stage 1 is 15% lower than in stage 2 because cross-training temporarily reduces the number of nurses available for treatment (see Fig. 3b). When the cross-training cost exceeds 150% of the base value, the model reduces the number of cross-trained ICU nurses from 180 to 140. This increases the service level by 0.7% in stage 1, since fewer nurses are temporarily removed from treatment capacity. However, with fewer cross-trained ICU nurses, more temporary hires are needed. As a result, the expected number of temporarily hired ICU nurses increases from 66 to 94 (see Fig. 3c).

Sensitivity to number of trainees per mentoring nurse

Finally, decreasing in the number of trainees per mentoring nurse during supervised practice helps the hospital balance nurse availability and manage demand fluctuations. However, increasing the number of trainees per mentor may compromise training quality.

Increasing the number of trainees per mentoring nurse from 10 to 200% of the base value reduces the expected total cost by up to 11.7 million NOK (− 4.0%) (see Fig. 4a). At 50% of the base value, 90 nurses are cross-trained for the ICU, reducing the stage 1 service level by 3.6%. When these ICU nurses become available in stage 2, the service level increases by 4.8% (see Fig. 4b). As the number of trainees per mentoring nurse further increases, the number of cross-trained ICU nurses rises to 220 when mentoring capacity is doubled (see Fig. 4c). Hence, the stage 2 service level becomes 15% higher than the stage 1 level, compared with a difference of only 5.1% without cross-training.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates how cross-training can function as a tactical capacity planning strategy to adjust the nurse skill mix for uncertain patient influx during the initial phase of a global pandemic. We extended the two-stage stochastic program by Maas et al. [8] by introducing a tactical cross-training decision and explicitly accounting for its impact on nurse availability during cross-training. Our model found an expected optimal total cost of 282.8 million NOK for Akershus University Hospital and an overall service level of 90.8%. 180 nurses are cross-trained for the ICU and 66 ICU nurses hired temporarily, highlighting ICU capacity as the primary constraint. Sensitivity analyses showed that cross-training is preferred when non-treatment costs increase, despite initially lower service levels. While temporary hiring is more expensive and short-term oriented, cross-training improves future service levels. Furthermore, our results indicate that changes in cross-training costs or the number of trainees per mentoring nurse have a significantly smaller impact on the total cost than non-treatment costs. Unlike Maas et al. [8], who predefine cross-trained nurse pools and allow only temporary hiring, our model dynamically optimizes cross-training and permits inter-hospital cooperation to handle demand surges, thereby reducing equipment needs and avoiding infeasibility due to excessive demand [44, 51, 52].

However, our model does not come without limitations. First, our model relies on census data that does not capture intra-day demand variability or individual patient pathways. Similarly, fixed average patient-to-nurse ratios do not reflect variation in patient acuity or care complexity, and more granular data on nurse time available for direct patient care would improve input accuracy. Second, the model does not account for individual nurse learning curves or prior experience, which can affect post-training efficiency and nurse-to-patient ratios [53]. Third, the analysis focuses solely on nurses, and the model addresses only one disease at a time, and without accounting for interdepartmental dependencies.

Regarding generalizability, we argue that our two-stage stochastic program can be applied in other hospitals facing similar situations where an infectious disease outbreak requires nurses to acquire additional skills. However, we acknowledge that not all types of hospitals have the same capacity to transfer patients in cases of insufficient treatment capacity. For instance, tertiary hospitals may have limited transfer options due to lack of cooperative facilities at the same treatment level in the region.

The model may also be applicable to situations not caused by infectious diseases, such as heat waves. Heat waves can increase overall patient influx and acuity while simultaneously raising absenteeism risk due to heat-related illness among staff [54]. Beyond heat waves, high workload more generally is a known driver of absenteeism, which the model is also equipped to handle [33, 55]. We also propose that our model can be applied to other service industries employing specialized workers who face sudden changes in demand, especially where regulations define the required skill set for varying demand-mix scenarios. Insurance companies are a relevant example, as natural disasters or pandemics can influence the mix of incoming claims that need to be processed and require specific expertise.

Future research is needed to refine optimal nurse cross-training strategies under conditions of absenteeism, explore multistage cross-training strategies, optimize nurse pool configurations, and treat absenteeism as both exogenous and endogenous factors. Further studies should examine how other parameters affect skill levels and performance by modeling cross-training as a continuous rather than a binary variable. Factors such as motivation and prior experience influence post-training efficiency and team composition, raising important questions about the number of nurses to cross-train per department. Finally, validating our results with practitioners would enhance the practical relevance of this study.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a two-stage stochastic program as a decision support tool for tactical nurse staffing that incorporates cross-training, temporary hiring, and absenteeism. We investigated the effects of variable non-treatment cost, cross-training cost, and the number of trainees per mentoring nurse on the total cost and its cost components, as well as on cross-training and temporary hiring decisions, and service levels for each stage. Sensitivity analyses revealed that non-treatment cost has a much greater impact on total cost than either the cross-training cost or the number of trainees per mentoring nurse. Although cross-training temporarily reduces the service level in stage 1, it increases nurse availability in stage 2 and improves the overall service level, making it advantageous in the long run. When cross-training costs or the number of trainees per mentor are adjusted, cross-training and temporary hiring decisions are inversely related.

However, our study is limited by its focus on a single disease and by the granularity constraint of the underlying patient census data. As a result, the model does not capture interactions across multiple patient pathways or provide a hospital-wide perspective. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into tactical nurse staffing decisions and demonstrates how cross-training can be used as a mitigation strategy.

Our model can act as a decision support tool for operations managers in hospitals, helping them to make more informed decisions and improve patient safety while minimizing the total cost. In addition, the study improves understanding of how cross-training can help hospitals use existing nursing staff more efficiently and prepare for future challenges, such as infectious disease outbreaks and population again.
