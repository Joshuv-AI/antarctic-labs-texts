Unwilling to Disclose: Privacy and Pregnancy in Post-Roe America
Authors: Cindy D Kam, Colette Marcellin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOn June 24, 2022, the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade and ended the constitutional right to abortion across the United States. The Dobbs decision created a disrupted and fragmented policy environment, with significant losses of reproductive rights in some US states and high levels of uncertainty in others. We analyze data from the nation’s premier health survey to examine whether Dobbs influenced women’s willingness to disclose whether they are pregnant. We find that women became more likely to refuse to disclose their pregnancy status in the wake of the Dobbs decision. This effect emerges most strongly among younger women and among those living in states where abortion rights were not actively threatened. Our results suggest that Dobbs may have reduced women’s willingness to share their pregnancy status through presenting policy change threat, with concerning implications for survey research, health policy, and women’s health.

On June 24, 2022, the Supreme Court of the United States overturned Roe v. Wade in Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization and, with it, ended the constitutional right to abortion in the United States. The Dobbs decision dissolved federal protections for abortion rights; as a result, more than a dozen states banned abortion with very limited exceptions (Nash and Ephross 2022). The Dobbs decision represents the “first time in history that the Supreme Court has taken away a fundamental right” (Center for Reproductive Rights 2024b).

The fall of Roe created a disrupted and fragmented policy environment, with significant losses of reproductive rights in some states and high levels of uncertainty in others. In states with bans and restrictions, the Dobbs decision has dramatically limited access to abortion; some women1 have had to travel to other states to access care, and others have had to carry to term (Rader et al. 2022; Dench et al. 2024; Estep 2024; Myers et al. 2025). The decision has also had a range of negative consequences beyond its direct effects on access to abortion (Human Rights Watch 2023), including substandard care for pregnancy-related complications and poor-quality medical care more broadly (Grossman et al. 2023; Manian 2023). Anecdotal evidence suggests that medical professionals have become hesitant to provide care, for fear of facing prosecution (Kulczycki 2022).

This uncertain and restrictive policy environment has created the conditions for a chilling effect on women’s willingness to disclose whether they are pregnant. In the wake of the Dobbs decision, patients and doctors have been prosecuted for accessing and providing access to abortion, and law enforcement has used patients’ personal data against them (Human Rights Watch 2023). Following Dobbs, women were advised to be more careful about what they share about pregnancy and to reduce their digital footprint, from online searches to period tracking apps to personal text messages (Faife et al. 2022). Social media users encouraged women to delete their period tracking apps, yet downloads also doubled as women aimed to keep better track of their menstrual cycles (Hill 2022; Krumbholz et al. 2024). In this post-Dobbs context of criminalized abortion and legalized data surveillance (Nasrolahi 2023; Dellinger and Pell 2024), women have reason to fear that information they share may be used against them.

We argue that this policy environment may have changed how women engage with their health, not only in how they track and communicate about their health using technology, but also in what they disclose on surveys and to their doctors. In particular, women may have become wary of sharing their pregnancy status on health surveys, for fear of facing criminal penalties if they are pregnant and seek an abortion—or even if they have a miscarriage (e.g., Smyth 2023).

Our core research questions are: Did women’s willingness to disclose their pregnancy status change after Dobbs? And, if so, did changes in women’s willingness to disclose their pregnancy status differ across subgroups of women and across state-level policy environments? To answer our research questions, we analyze data from a longstanding health survey that was in the field pre- and post-Dobbs: the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS). Overall, we find that women became significantly more likely to refuse to answer a question about pregnancy status in the wake of the Dobbs decision. We find that this increase in item refusal primarily emerges among younger women. Interestingly, younger women living in states where abortion rights were not actively threatened manifested the strongest reactions to Dobbs.

Our paper contributes important insights to multiple literatures. Building on research documenting the direct effects of Dobbs on women’s health (e.g., Rader et al. 2022; Dench et al. 2024) and changes in public opinion regarding abortion (e.g., Pew Research Center 2022; Norrander and Wilcox 2023), we examine the effects of the decision on women’s health disclosures on surveys. Our paper contributes to the multidisciplinary literature on abortion, which has been understudied in disciplinary journals and “siloed” within social science disciplines (Kimport and Kreitzer 2023, 463). The chilling effect that we find on women’s willingness to disclose their pregnancy status has a range of implications for health policy and health research, which rely on survey-based disclosures of pregnancy status. Our paper also underscores the difficulties of collecting complete survey responses in the context of rights restrictions and surveillance concerns. Our findings suggest that when rights are restricted, the quality of survey data suffers—even for plausibly apolitical questions about health.

Policy Change and Policy Threat in the Wake of the Dobbs Decision

The Dobbs decision created a highly uncertain policy environment in which abortion rights were significantly threatened across the country and in many states. And Americans noticed: before the decision, less than 1 percent of respondents to the Gallup Poll mentioned abortion as the most important problem facing the country. After the decision, the percentage of respondents increased to 8 percent—the highest percentage to have ever mentioned abortion as one of the “most important problems” (Newport 2022). Analyzing the July 2022 Gallup survey, we find that women were six times more likely to mention abortion as one of the most important problems facing the country compared to men (12.4 percent versus 2.2 percent). Democrats (13.0 percent) were about three times more likely to mention it than Republicans (3.6 percent). Younger people, especially those aged 18–29, were significantly more likely to mention it than older people. Compounding these categories: one in three female Democrats aged 18–29 mentioned abortion as one of the most important issues facing the country (32.5 percent) in the July 2022 Gallup survey (Gallup 2022).

Data from a May 2022 Public Religion Research Institute (PRRI) survey showcase this heterogeneity as well. Though the majority (64 percent) of the American public thinks abortion should be legal in all or most situations, there are searing partisan divides: 86 percent of Democrats support legality in all or most situations, whereas only 36 percent of Republicans share that view. This gap remains across women, with 86 percent of Democratic women supporting legality and only 39 percent of Republican women sharing that view. And this partisan divide remains even among those with the highest childbearing potential: women aged 18–29, with 92 percent of Democratic women in this age group supporting legality and only 36 percent of Republican women supporting it. Further, the majority support for abortion legality masks considerable variation across states. The PRRI survey reveals that support for abortion varies by political geography, as people who are least supportive of abortion live in states with the most restrictive abortion policy environments, and vice versa (PRRI 2023).

This wide variation in public opinion about abortion underscores the differential ways in which gender, partisanship, age, and political geography may have shaped reactions to the Dobbs decision. We argue that, among American women who support abortion rights, Dobbs likely induced policy dissatisfaction with federal and state-level policy changes, and policy change threat at the prospect of potential federal and state-level policy changes.

Policy dissatisfaction refers to negative reactions to policy changes that have occurred and/or are presently occurring (Hansen 1985; Miller et al. 2016).2 Policy change threat refers to the “future-oriented perception of possibility” of an unwanted change in policy (Miller et al. 2016, 175; see also Holbrook et al. 2016). Policy changes (whether enacted or potential) may be undesirable because they impinge on an individual’s needs, rights, or self-interest and/or because they undermine an individual’s policy preferences, identities, or worldviews (e.g., Campbell 2003; Mettler et al. 2023). In the face of policy changes, threatening conditions can prompt people to modify their behaviors because they fear repercussions. For example, policy change threat is said to have decreased immigrants’ willingness to use public assistance programs because of fear and confusion about immigrants’ access to public programs (Haley et al. 2020).

The Dobbs decision initiated a cascade of policy changes and policy change threats. We argue that these policy changes, whether already realized or anticipated for the future, affected women in many ways, including those who might foresee a personal loss of access to reproductive services, fear surveillance and culpability in the event of an abortion or a miscarriage, or view the policy changes as a symbolic threat to their values and worldviews. Importantly, information-seeking is an observable implication of the anxiety induced by policy threat (e.g., Brader et al. 2008; Valentino et al. 2008), and our analysis of Google Trends and recent studies find increases in abortion-related online searches around the time of the Dobbs decision and leak (Gupta et al. 2023; Lemieux et al. 2024).3

We argue that this policy environment may have increased women’s perceptions of the sensitivity of questions about pregnancy and, in turn, may have increased their refusal to answer questions about pregnancy status. Fear and social desirability concerns can exacerbate the difficulties of collecting accurate survey responses, especially in contexts of rights restrictions, repression, and authoritarianism (Arnold and Feldman 1981; Kuran 1997; Jiang and Yang 2016; Truex and Tavana 2019). Even in non-authoritarian contexts, when individual rights are restricted, “fear of political repercussions can lead to self-censorship” on surveys (Truex and Tavana 2019, 1017). This suggests that when rights are restricted, individuals may self-censor by refusing to disclose sensitive information.4

Self-censorship may take various forms, including a “Don’t Know” or a refusal to answer the question. We expect the sensitivity of questions about pregnancy in the wake of Dobbs to trigger a refusal to answer the question, rather than a “Don’t Know” response, because “Don’t Know” responses tend to be prompted by questions that require cognitive effort or are complex rather than sensitive (Shoemaker et al. 2002; Berinsky 2004). According to Berinsky (1999), “If a person constructs a private judgment that they do not feel comfortable expressing, they might choose to abstain from the question” (1211–12). Refusals are more commonly associated with sensitive questions about “potential illegal behavior or social stigma” than “Don’t Know” responses, which more commonly reflect challenges in remembering (Mignogna et al. 2023, 1373).

If women perceived questions about pregnancy to be more sensitive because of the threat of disclosure—or the “consequences of giving a truthful answer should the information become known to a third party” (Tourangeau and Yan 2007, 860)—responding “Don’t Know” could suggest that a respondent might be pregnant, whereas refusing to answer the question would allow women to “plead the fifth” and have plausible deniability if they may be pregnant and may seek an abortion or experience a miscarriage. Women encounter this question most frequently in a medical setting, where women are regularly asked, “Is there a chance that you may be pregnant?” A woman can say no, there is no chance; yes, I know that I am pregnant; or I don’t know/there is a chance. Medical studies find that saying “there is a chance” is positively correlated with pregnancy (Bastian and Piscitelli 1997). Given how often women are asked about their pregnancy status in a medical setting, and given that any yes or possible yes responses can trigger a pregnancy test, we expect that women will bring similar considerations and norms to the survey setting. Women may think a “Don’t Know” response may suggest “possibly yes” and be treated as a yes. Thus, rather than responding “Don’t Know,” women may refuse to answer the question if they are concerned about the potential consequences of even suggesting “possibly yes.”

We also expect that the Dobbs decision will have heterogeneous effects on pregnancy disclosure. Among women, those of childbearing age—and among them, younger women—experienced the greatest personal threat, given that women’s fertility is inversely related to their age (e.g., ACOG 2014). In addition, younger women expressed greater disapproval of Dobbs and viewed abortion as a more important issue than other age and gender groups (KFF 2022).

Further, political geography may contribute to actualized policy dissatisfaction and policy change threat. Though the majority of Americans disapproved of the Dobbs decision, 41 percent of Americans supported it (Pew Research Center 2022). Rather than reacting with dissatisfaction, these Americans may have welcomed the post-Dobbs policy changes. As discussed above, people living in states with more restrictive policies also have more restrictive views on abortion (PRRI 2023). Thus, people living in states with abortion restrictions, if they support such restrictions, may not show the effects that we are hypothesizing.

Before the Dobbs decision, more than a dozen states enacted anticipatory measures (called “trigger bans”) that almost immediately eliminated abortion rights once the decision became official (e.g., Dench et al. 2024). Several states that did not enact total bans still severely restricted abortion pre-viability (Roth and Lee 2023), and six states voted on ballot measures about abortion (Ballotpedia 2022). Women living in states where abortion rights were eliminated, reduced, or threatened faced a greater realistic threat than women in states like California or Vermont, where state governments affirmed protections for abortion. However, it is worth noting that though Roe provided nationwide constitutional protections for abortion, it did not guarantee nationwide access to abortion. Even in the Roe era, women faced severe state-level restrictions on both patients and providers. Women living in states with pre-Dobbs barriers may not have perceived the post-Dobbs changes to be a dramatic departure from the preexisting and already restrictive regulatory environment they faced.

Among women living in states that affirmed residents’ abortion rights, uncertainty and the threat of a federal ban still loomed large (e.g., Korte et al. 2022). Further, people living in states with less restrictive policies expressed stronger support for abortion rights (PRRI 2023). Thus, it is possible that women in these states may have perceived policy change threat, even if their state policies protected abortion access.

We posit that states’ many attempts to restrict or eliminate abortion rights in response to the Dobbs decision—whether successful or not—created a significantly more threatening environment than existed before the decision. Though we might expect women living in more threatening state policy environments to demonstrate increased reluctance to disclose their pregnancy status, it is also possible that they will not adjust their behavior significantly because of their policy preferences and because of their experiences having lived in states with preexisting restrictions. Women living in states that assured their rights also faced a threatening and uncertain federal policy environment. Our analyses will examine the degree to which women living in assured versus contested state policy environments increase their refusal to answer a question about pregnancy post-Dobbs.

Disclosure Hesitance in the Nation’s Premier Health Survey

To test for the impact of the Dobbs decision on disclosure of pregnancy status, we analyze data from the BRFSS, a longstanding system of surveys managed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). According to the National Center for Chronic Disease Prevention and Health Promotion (NCCDPHP) (2018), the BRFSS is “the largest continuously conducted health survey system in the world.” The BRFSS consists of annual cross-sectional survey interviews conducted continuously throughout each year by the CDC and state health departments. The BRFSS conducts more than 400,000 interviews each year and uses Random Digit Dialing techniques to select landline and cell phone numbers for interviews, with each state health department designing a probability sample of all households with telephones in the state. The sample is designed to represent the noninstitutionalized adult population residing in each state, and all adults who are 18 years or older are eligible to participate (NCCDPHP 2018; BRFSS 2022).5

The BRFSS data are used for a range of important purposes. According to the NCCDPHP (2018), every US state uses the BRFSS data to “establish and track state and local health objectives, plan health programs, implement disease prevention and health promotion activities, and monitor trends.” A number of policymakers, scientists, and government agencies and municipalities rely on the BRFSS (Rolle-Lake and Robbins 2023). Overall, the BRFSS is a “powerful tool for targeting and building health promotion activities” (NCCDPHP 2014).

The BRFSS is well suited for our inquiry for several reasons. Because it is continuously fielded throughout the year, we are able to examine the temporal discontinuity presented by the Dobbs decision. Further, it includes a key question for our inquiry: pregnancy status. The question reads, “To your knowledge, are you now pregnant?,” with the response options of Yes, No, Don’t Know/Not Sure, or Refused. This question is asked only to women who are younger than 50 years old, resulting in about 80,000 respondents who were administered the question in 2022.6

We begin with a descriptive analysis of item refusal rates in 2022, by month, in table 1. Though more than 80 percent of women aged 40–44 have given birth in their lifetime (Livingston 2018), at a specific moment in time, pregnancy is a rare event. In a given month, the percentage of respondents who say they are pregnant ranges from 2.6 to 4.6 percent of the sample. More than 94 percent of women in any given month say they are not pregnant. Small percentages of women (0.1–0.7 percent) respond “Don’t Know,” and slightly higher percentages (0.2–0.9 percent) refuse to answer the question altogether. Notably, the refusal rate reaches a high point in the month immediately after the decision (June 24–July 31, noted as July+) and remains relatively high through December. Table 1 provides initial evidence that women who were interviewed after the Dobbs decision was released were more likely to refuse to answer the pregnancy question than women who were interviewed before.

Heterogeneity: Demographic Characteristics

Next, we investigate potential sources of heterogeneity among women in their reactions to the Dobbs decision. We begin with analyses that explore heterogeneity by individual characteristics and then proceed to analyses by state policy environment. To simplify our analyses, we focus on respondents who were interviewed in 2022.

Our individual-level analyses are exploratory and probe the degree to which reactions to Dobbs may be traceable to policy change threat and policy dissatisfaction. Given that we start with effects that are small in magnitude and engage in subgroup analyses,11 we may be underpowered to detect significant differences between groups.

We begin with age. Younger women are especially likely to be affected by the Dobbs decision, in terms of both policy change threat and policy dissatisfaction, as they have the highest chance of becoming pregnant (ACOG 2014) and express the greatest disapproval of the Dobbs decision and prioritization of abortion as an issue (KFF 2022). Thus, we examine whether differences emerge between younger and older women. We analyze equation [1] separately for subgroups of women aged 18–29, 30–39, and 40–49. Marginalization and lack of resources are also potential sources of heterogeneity in reactions to Dobbs. According to Human Rights Watch (2023), the effects of Dobbs “fall disproportionately on marginalized populations including Black, indigenous, and people of color; people with disabilities; immigrants; and those living in poverty.” These groups may lack the information and economic resources to access care (i.e., to travel out of state), and even pre-Dobbs, marginalized groups faced barriers to abortion access that were correlated with worse birth outcomes for Black women and less educated women (Redd et al. 2021).

Thus, we explore differential reactions based on several potential indicators of marginalization and lack of resources, beginning with race/ethnicity. We also might expect differences by race/ethnicity based on attitudes toward abortion; Black respondents report greater support for abortion legality than White and Hispanic respondents (Pew Research Center 2024) and thus might experience higher levels of policy dissatisfaction in response to Dobbs. Further, as discussed above, Dobbs also posed a greater policy change threat to minoritized groups. The increase in restrictiveness post-Dobbs might have further compounded inequities faced by Black women and other women of color (Kim et al. 2023; Cain Miller and Sanger-Katz 2025). The results in the top right panel of figure 2 suggest that White and Black women showed significant increases in item refusals, to a similar extent. Among Hispanic women, there is no significant effect of being interviewed post-Dobbs.12 Even so, the overlap in the confidence intervals suggests that racial/ethnic group membership does not clearly moderate reactions in the post-Dobbs period.

Low socioeconomic status (SES) may also impede access to abortion (Kim et al. 2023). Thus, we might expect women of lower SES to experience greater levels of policy change threat as a result of Dobbs. On the other hand, education and income may facilitate information acquisition about the abortion policy environment and awareness of concerns about criminalization and surveillance (e.g., Dahlhamer et al. 2006). These arguments suggest potentially countervailing patterns. The bottom left panel of figure 2 shows a fairly flat gradient across levels of income.13 The bottom right panel indicates a positive effect across levels of educational attainment, with potentially stronger effects among less educated women (among women with BAs, the positive effect has a p-value of 0.133).14 The high degree of overlap in the confidence intervals for income and education suggests that there is no clear evidence that SES moderates disclosure hesitance in the post-Dobbs period.

Overall, our subgroup analyses point to age as an important differentiator in women’s reactions to Dobbs. Our analyses also suggest that race/ethnicity and SES provide less clarity in distinguishing changes in women’s willingness to disclose their pregnancy status.15

Heterogeneity: State-Level Policy Environment

Now, we analyze the potential role of the state-level policy environment in triggering policy dissatisfaction and policy change threat. We compiled a state-level dataset that measures the regulatory environment regarding abortion in each state in 2022. To assess state policy environments post-Dobbs, we reviewed Dench and coauthors’ (2024) state abortion ban classifications. We also relied on data from the Center for Reproductive Rights (2024a) and state-level information from the Guttmacher Institute (2024), and we reviewed national and state news sources as needed.

As discussed above, the Dobbs decision created a fragmented and uncertain state policy environment. Some states affirmed their protections for abortion rights, whereas trigger bans quickly took effect in others, and some of these bans were challenged in court in 2022. Legislative debates occurred in many states, and laws to curtail abortion rights were passed in several. State-level ballot measures were raised, sometimes to enshrine abortion rights and sometimes to restrict abortion. Significant policy activity and debate took place in response to Dobbs, across states and within states over time.

There are many possible ways of characterizing state abortion policy environments post-Dobbs. Given that our aggregate effect is small, to preserve statistical power, we code states as Assured (abortion rights remained assured throughout 2022) or as Contested/Restricted (rights were contested, reduced, or eliminated at any point post-Dobbs in 2022).16 We acknowledge that this coding oversimplifies the complex and evolving state policy environment during this time period.17

We again confine our estimates to 2022, and we analyze equation [1] separately for women living in each subgroup of states: Assured and Contested/Restricted. This increase in item refusals among women in assured states may be surprising from a strictly local “realistic threat” perspective. Women living in states with an abortion ban certainly had less access to reproductive services post-Dobbs, yet women in these states were already facing highly restrictive policy environments pre-Dobbs. Thus, the chilling effect of Dobbs may have been larger among women living in assured states, since these women had not experienced as restrictive environments pre-Dobbs and, despite state-level assurances, may have feared the specter of a national abortion ban.

The BRFSS does not contain attitudinal questions on abortion; however, we can leverage secondary data to shed light on women’s reactions to Dobbs. We analyze the American National Election Studies 2022 Pilot Study (from December 2022) and we find, consistent with PRRI (2023), that women living in Contested/Restricted states reported more conservative abortion attitudes than women living in Assured states. On the flip side, women living in Assured states were far more supportive of unqualified access to abortion, were much more likely to oppose the overturning of Roe, and expressed significantly more anxiety and outrage in response to the decision.18

Conclusions

Our analyses demonstrate that women became more likely to refuse to answer a question about pregnancy status after Dobbs relative to before the decision in 2022 and relative to previous years. Further, this increase in item refusal is primarily driven by younger women living in states where abortion rights were not threatened. Together, these results suggest that policy dissatisfaction and policy change threat from Dobbs may have reduced women’s willingness to disclose their pregnancy status.

Our paper has critical implications for the growing literature on the consequences of the Dobbs decision. It identifies an important way that Dobbs has affected women’s health beyond its direct effects on access to abortion and women’s health care. Our finding that women have become less willing to respond to a question about pregnancy status has concerning implications for other aspects of women’s health, including women’s doctor-patient relationships.

As the nation’s premier health survey, it is important that the information provided by the BRFSS be accurate, and in our case in particular, be accurate in measuring state residents’ pregnancy status. States including Florida, Indiana, New Jersey, and Utah use the BRFSS to inform state programs related to pregnancy. Indiana uses the survey to compare pregnant women to other women of childbearing age for research and programmatic purposes (NCCDPHP 2016). Thus, the increase in missing data that we find could have significant implications for state policies and programs. If states follow “the norm in social science analysis” of analyzing data without accounting for item non-response, their findings may have “serious biases” (Berinsky 2011, 342). Though the item refusal rate remains small, the increase is significant and substantively important. This significant increase in item non-response can bias a range of statistical tests and is especially problematic when surveys (like the BRFSS) use weighting (Halbesleben and Whitman 2013). Further, the chilling effect that we find on women’s willingness to disclose their pregnancy status in the BRFSS has important implications for health policy and health research that rely on survey-based disclosures of pregnancy status. In terms of substantive effects, the vast majority of American women become pregnant at some point in their lives (e.g., Livingston 2018), and more than 75 million American women are of childbearing age (Blakeslee et al. 2023). At the lowest item refusal rate in 2022 (in April), extrapolating from this paper would mean missing pregnancy data from more than 150,000 women.

Our paper also contributes to research on survey measurement in political contexts of rights restrictions and surveillance concerns. Our results suggest that rights restrictions and policy uncertainty can have significant effects on question refusals and thus on the accuracy and precision of measurements gathered through survey self-reports. Overall, our paper demonstrates the difficulties of collecting complete survey responses in contexts of restrictions and surveillance.

The Dobbs decision has had a range of harmful consequences for women’s health. Our paper identifies an important spillover consequence: a reduction in women’s willingness to disclose their pregnancy status on a health survey that may suggest a more generalized hesitance among women to share information about their health. Our findings thus broaden the potential scope of negative repercussions associated with the Dobbs decision and highlight the need for interventions that protect privacy, promote trust, and encourage disclosure, to mitigate against what could pose an even wider array of negative health outcomes for women.
