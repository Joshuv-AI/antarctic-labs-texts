Mechanical Thrombectomy in Cases of Posterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery Occlusion (with or without Vertebral Artery Occlusion): A Case Series
Authors: Cetin K Akpinar, Hasan Dogan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) occlusion (with or without vertebral artery occlusion) may result in severe disability and even mortality. Patients who underwent mechanical thrombectomy (MT) for PICA occlusion (with or without vertebral artery occlusion) between 2021 and 2023 were included in the study. All five patients who underwent MT were male, aged between 45 and 68 years, with National Institute of Health Stroke Scale scores ranging from 3 to 10. The successful recanalization rate in our cases with PICA occlusion was 60%. As the MT technique, an isolated stent retriever was used in two cases, and a combined technique was used in three cases. Retrograde approach (via asymptomatic vertebral artery) was used in two cases, and an anterograde approach was used in three cases. Two cases had isolated PICA occlusion, and three cases had PICA occlusion with vertebral artery involvement. MT is a treatment option that should be considered in PICA occlusions causing severe disability.

Introduction

Occlusions of the anterior inferior cerebellar artery, superior cerebellar artery, and posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) account for approximately 2% of all strokes and may result in severe disability.[12] Despite the fact that intravenous thrombolytic therapy is the recommended treatment for these types of occlusions in the literature, intra-arterial thrombolytic therapy and mechanical thrombectomy (MT) have also been performed in specific cases, and successful results have been reported.[345] In PICA occlusion (with or without vertebral artery occlusion), severe cerebral edema, obstructive hydrocephalus, large cerebellar infarction, and even death may be observed.[6] Consequently, MT may be administered to a limited number of patients who experience severe disability as a result of PICA occlusion or a large cerebellar infarctions. In our clinic, five cases of PICA occlusion were treated with MT.

Case Report

This study examined five patients who were admitted to a single comprehensive stroke center between 2021 and 2023 with a diagnosis of PICA occlusion (with or without vertebral artery occlusion) and who underwent MT. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient data were evaluated retrospectively. An interventional neurologist with 7 years of experience performed the MT.

MT was performed through the right femoral artery. The occlusion site was reached using the MT procedure through an anterograde approach (access to the occluded PICA and/or vertebral artery via the ipsilateral vertebral artery) or a retrograde approach (access to the occluded PICA and/or vertebral artery via the contralateral vertebral artery).

Five patients who were diagnosed with PICA occlusion and underwent MT had a mean age of 58 years. All five patients were male. The median baseline Posterior circulation Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (pcASPECTS) was 10, and the median baseline National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) was 5 (Interquartile Range [IQR] 3–14).

Successful reperfusion of the whole posterior circulation at the end of the procedure was achieved in 3/5 (60%) patients. No hemorrhage was observed on the 24-h follow-up computed tomography (CT) scan of the patients. Three patients with concomitant vertebral artery and PICA occlusion also had basilar artery filling via the other vertebral artery.

Good patient outcome (modified rankin score [mRS] ≤2) was achieved in 2/5 (40%) patients at discharge and in 3/5 (60%) at the 90-day follow-up. MT was performed in two patients due to clinical progression and in three patients due to severe disability. Discussion

The PICA provides blood supply to the medulla, choroid plexus, and tela choroidea of the fourth ventricle, as well as the tonsils, inferior vermis, and inferior margins of the cerebellar hemispheres.[67] The cerebellar branches of the PICA form anastomoses with the anterior inferior cerebellar artery and superior cerebellar artery, whereas the medullary branches lack anastomosis. Therefore, the most common site of PICA-related infarction is the dorsolateral medulla supplied by the medullary branches.[567] Clinical findings of Wallenberg syndrome, which develops following PICA occlusion, include vertigo, nausea, vomiting, ipsilateral facial pain and heat, decreased pain and temperature sensation in the contralateral body half, dysmetria, ipsilateral Horner syndrome, dysarthria, dysphagia, ipsilateral ataxia, dysphonia, ipsilateral loss of gag reflex, hiccups, and hemiparesis/hemiplegia.[8]

PICA occlusion; the most common etiological cause is cardioembolism. Rarely, it may be observed due to atherosclerosis or dissection.[6] Styczen et al.[3] performed successful MT (modified treatment in cerebral infarction [mTICI] 2b) in a patient with PICA occlusion and reported an mRS of 2 on the 90th day. An isolated stent retriever was preferred as the MT technique. Zhang et al.[5] performed successful MT with the ADAPT technique (mTICI 3) in a patient with PICA occlusion and reported an mRS of 0 on the 90th day. In the same study, they calculated the PICA diameter as approximately 1.5 mm, and a 3 MAX device was used as the aspiration catheter.[5] MT has been performed in a very small number of PICA occlusions as case reports in the literature, and successful results have been obtained in terms of both recanalization and good clinical outcome. The cases reported in the literature were observed to consist of isolated PICA occlusion. In our study, PICA occlusion was associated with vertebral artery (V4) in three cases. The endovascular procedure for these cases was easier than for isolated PICA occlusion, because the diameter of the PICA is approximately 1.5 mm, while the diameter of the vertebral artery is approximately 3 mm.

In an occlusion that develops due to dissection, it may be difficult to cross the dissected segment. Similarly, in cases with severe subclavian or vertebral artery tortuosity, it may be difficult to access or cross the occluded segment. In these cases, we may need to use a retrograde approach (using the other vertebral artery) to reach the occluded PICA region.

We usually observe occlusion of the PICA together with vertebral artery occlusion. This facilitates MT and reduces the risk of complications. In isolated PICA occlusions, intervention is more risky because the PICA has a very tortuous anatomy. Both placing and pulling back the stent retriever are very risky. In isolated PICA occlusions, small-diameter (baby) stent retrievers (2–2.5 mm) should be preferred. Because the PICA caliber is very thin, the ADAPT technique is also risky, as it may cause dissection of the vessel. The Solumbra technique can be applied in isolated PICA occlusions or PICA occlusions with vertebral artery. When performing this technique, the aspiration catheter must be carefully advanced to the origin of the PICA. Avoiding force if advancement is not possible minimizes the risk of dissection.

For isolated PICA occlusions, smaller aspiration catheters, microcatheters, and baby stent retrievers should be preferred. If there is vertebral artery and PICA occlusion, a 5F aspiration catheter, 0.021-inch microcatheter, and 3.5- or 4-mm stent retrievers may be preferred. But, if there is isolated PICA occlusion, we should prefer a 3F or 4F aspiration catheter, 0.017-inch microcatheter, and 2–2.5 mm stent retrievers.

Conclusions

Because the diameter of the PICA is very small, one should be more careful when deciding on MT for isolated PICA occlusions. It is a technically difficult operation with a high risk of complications. However, in patients with vertebral artery occlusion, the technique is easier, and the risk of complications is reduced. MT should be considered in cases of PICA occlusion with clinically significant deficits or severe disability.
