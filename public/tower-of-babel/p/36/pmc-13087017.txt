Multi-objective optimization of a regional biogas supply chain using organic waste
Authors: Ivan P Malashin, Dmitry Martysyuk, Vladimir Nelyub, Aleksei Borodulin, Andrei Gantimurov, Vadim Tynchenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing volume of agricultural and municipal organic residues presents both a waste-management challenge and an opportunity for low-carbon energy production. This study develops a multi-objective optimization model to support the strategic design of regional biogas supply chains, explicitly accounting for economic, environmental, and system-resilience criteria. The framework is demonstrated using data from a representative biogas plant case study to explore alternative system configurations and trade-offs among total cost, greenhouse gas emissions, energy revenue, resource consumption, and supply reliability. Results indicate that moderate investment levels allow substantial emission reductions, whereas beyond a certain threshold additional expenditure yields progressively smaller environmental benefits. Likewise, increasing plant throughput raises energy revenue, but the incremental financial return declines as capacity limits are approached. Sensitivity analysis shows that feedstock price and biogas yield have the strongest influence on overall system performance, while discount rate and transport-related emissions exert secondary effects. More balanced sourcing from multiple suppliers improves resilience and can enable further emission reductions without increasing capital cost. The proposed framework provides a transparent decision-support tool for designing economically viable and environmentally balanced biogas systems.Keywords: Multi-objective optimization, Bioenergy supply chain, Organic waste utilization, Greenhouse gas mitigation, Sensitivity analysisSubject terms: Environmental impact, Renewable energy

Introduction

The global transition from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources has stimulated considerable interest in the development of sustainable bioenergy systems. Among these, biogas production via anaerobic digestion of organic wastes offers a promising pathway to simultaneously address waste management, greenhouse gas mitigation, and decentralized energy generation1,2. However, the design and operation of regional biogas supply chains involve complex trade-offs between economic viability, environmental performance, resource consumption, and social acceptance.

Multi-objective optimization (MOO) methods are applied to describe the interacting economic, environmental, and technical criteria that govern bioenergy system design3–5. Typical objectives include the minimization of total cost, life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions, and resource footprints, together with the maximization of energy recovery and co-product valorization6,7. In regional biofuel supply chains, feedstock allocation, facility siting, and transport logistics are linked through non-linear production functions, capacity constraints, and spatially variable transport emissions8. These interactions generate non-convex Pareto sets9 where trade-offs between economic feasibility, emission abatement, and supply reliability cannot be represented by a single objective. MOO allows a formal exploration of these trade-offs and the identification of Pareto-efficient configurations reflecting alternative balances between centralized conversion efficiency and distributed collection flexibility.

Uncertainty in biomass yield10, moisture content11, and emission factors12 has been represented through fuzzy and robust formulations, while Geographic Information Systems (GIS) are used to parameterize transport distances, feedstock densities, and regional demand zones13,14. Existing studies rarely combine a high-dimensional, normalized MOO framework with explicit spatial representation. Such integration allows consistent comparison of heterogeneous indicators–economic, environmental, and social–on a common scale15, providing a unified basis for regional bioenergy planning. The Republic of Tatarstan is examined as a representative agro-industrial region with intensive agriculture, diverse organic residues, and developing biogas infrastructure typical of transitional economies in Eastern Europe and Central Asia. These conditions make it suitable for testing the applicability of a generalized framework that can also be transferred to other regions with similar feedstock diversity and infrastructural constraints.

This study addresses that gap by presenting an integrated MOO model for the strategic planning of a biogas supply chain. The model extends previous work on the Aktyuba bio-natural gas (BNG) plant in the Republic of Tatarstan, which demonstrated the value of mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) and life-cycle assessment (LCA) for multi-criteria decision support16. Here, eight performance criteria–covering cost, emissions, water and energy use, revenue, nutrient recovery, sanitation impact, and supply resilience–are formulated in a dimensionless framework to allow direct comparison and Pareto-front exploration.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section Related Work reviews relevant literature on bioenergy supply chain optimization and GIS integration. Section Materials and Methods presents the MOO model formulation, including decision variables, objective functions, and constraints. Section Results reports numerical results and Pareto-front analysis, and Section Discussion discusses policy implications and limitations. Finally, Section Conclusion summarizes key findings and outlines directions for future research.

Related work

Multi-criteria optimization models for biofuel supply chains

Designing an efficient biofuel supply chain (BSC) involves more than mathematical optimization; it requires balancing environmental objectives, economic feasibility, and technological uncertainty. Practitioners must continuously manage trade-offs, such as minimizing life cycle energy consumption without increasing greenhouse gas emissions or distribution costs. As a result, the BSC problem is inherently multi-objective, where priorities shift depending on context and policy goals. Jana et al.17 address this complexity using a bi-criteria model based on type-2 fuzzy sets to represent uncertainty in parameters such as biomass availability, demand variation, and transportation constraints. This approach captures the variability typical of biofuel systems, where agricultural yields, logistics, and policy incentives fluctuate. Their model jointly minimizes life cycle energy consumption and carbon emissions through a deterministic reformulation of fuzzy parameters, later solved via Goal Programming and the Weighted Sum Method. In a bioethanol case study, this approach reduced logistics costs and improved facility siting, though the authors note that translating model results into practice depends strongly on local economic and regulatory conditions.

Uncertainty remains a persistent challenge in BSC design, affecting biomass supply, feedstock quality, and market prices. Aboytes et al.16 developed a two-stage stochastic optimization framework that explicitly incorporates risk. The first stage determines the optimal locations of biorefineries, while the second optimizes biomass flows under uncertain parameters. To prevent overly optimistic planning, the authors employ Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) and extend it with Probability Value-at-Risk (PVaR) to better capture supplier-related disruptions. A hybrid of Simulated Annealing (SA)18–20 and the augmented -constraint method is used to generate robust Pareto solutions. SA applies probabilistic acceptance of sub-optimal moves to escape local minima, enabling a broader exploration of the solution space. Combined with the -constraint approach, it ensures a well-distributed Pareto front under uncertainty, showing that risk in biofuel supply systems can be mitigated through adaptive, stochastic search rather than eliminated deterministically. The challenge, however, lies in translating probabilistic outcomes into clear, actionable guidance for investors and policymakers.

A complementary stream of research incorporates robustness directly into the optimization framework. Savoji et al.21 introduced a bi-objective mixed-integer model using robust possibilistic programming to minimize cost and emissions under uncertain demand and emission factors. This approach ensures that even under worst-case conditions, the system remains feasible and economically viable, offering a safeguard against extreme fluctuations in biomass markets or sudden policy changes. In contrast, Garai et al.22 proposed a closed-loop supply chain (CLSC) model integrating herb cultivation, pharmaceutical production, and biofuel recovery from herbal residues. Their formulation explicitly accounts for government subsidies, highlighting how fiscal incentives can make circular biofuel systems economically sustainable.

Across these studies, common patterns emerge. Multi-objective frameworks help visualize trade-offs, but they cannot prescribe a “correct” solution. Should decision-makers prioritize emissions reduction or cost savings? Is social benefit as measurable as transport efficiency? Moreover, most models assume stable policies and rational behavior — conditions that rarely hold in dynamic agricultural and energy systems.

Nevertheless, the field is moving forward. By integrating fuzzy logic, stochastic optimization, and robustness, researchers are developing models that can handle ambiguity rather than avoid it. As data availability improves and computational tools advance, such models may increasingly bridge the gap between theoretical optimization and real-world biofuel policy design.

Optimization of biogas supply systems and co-production

The optimization of biogas supply systems and co-production processes relies on multi-criteria optimization methods to address the complex interactions between economic, environmental, and technical objectives. These approaches support the strategic design of supply chains–covering feedstock selection, transportation logistics, facility siting, and conversion technologies–with the goal of maximizing energy recovery and resource efficiency while minimizing costs and emissions. Considering multiple performance indicators simultaneously, such as greenhouse gas mitigation, operational reliability, and financial viability, enables a more comprehensive assessment of system sustainability and resilience.

Bio-natural gas (BNG) is recognized as a renewable substitute for fossil-based natural gas. Zhou et al.6 present an integrated framework combining life-cycle assessment (LCA), mixed-integer linear programming (MILP), and fuzzy optimization to capture trade-offs in BNG production. Their study evaluates anaerobic digestion, gasification, and syngas methanation, reporting unit production costs of $0.67, $0.40, and $0.36 per cubic meter, respectively. Life-cycle greenhouse gas reductions of 5–10 kg -eq per m³ are calculated relative to conventional natural gas, including production, transport, and end-use. The multi-objective formulation balances energy use and emissions, showing that regional decarbonization is feasible under proper infrastructure and regulatory conditions.

The ongoing transition from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources has also intensified interest in second- and third-generation biomass, to mitigate competition with food production. Second-generation biomass refers to lignocellulosic residues and non-food energy crops, such as agricultural residues, grasses, and woody biomass, while third-generation biomass primarily includes microalgae and other fast-growing, high-yield feedstocks that do not rely on arable land. Mahjoub et al.1 propose a multi-objective MILP model for a biomass-to-bioenergy supply chain that integrates anaerobic digestion and transesterification processes. Using feedstocks such as agricultural residues, livestock manure, microalgae, and Jatropha cultivated under various climatic conditions in Iran, their formulation seeks to minimize total cost while maximizing energy output. The results indicate that microalgae and Jatropha are more cost-effective and energy-efficient than crop residues, though yield variability and uncertain investment costs remain significant sources of model sensitivity.

A localized perspective is provided by Durmaz et al.2, who examine the utilization of poultry waste for biogas production. Their multi-criteria MILP model integrates Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) to determine optimal plant locations that balance profitability and transport distances. In their case study for İzmir, Turkey, the region’s poultry waste potential amounts to roughly 178 000 tons per year, capable of producing over 65 GWh of electricity annually. Operating costs are estimated at about $5 million per year, with an annual profit of $7.3 million and a payback period of 4.6 years. A sensitivity analysis demonstrates that profitability is strongly affected by fluctuations in biomass prices, underlining the importance of stable feedstock markets for investment planning.

At the process level, Ramos et al.7 analyze the conversion of biogas to synthesis gas (syngas) for subsequent Fischer-Tropsch fuel synthesis. Using Aspen Plus simulations within a multi-objective optimization framework, they explore the balance between greenhouse gas emissions and profit. Optimal operating conditions achieve profits between $5200 and $6500 per hour, while maintaining net negative emissions in the range of approximately −11 900 to −11 550 kg h. These findings show that, under suitable process control, emission mitigation and profitability can coexist.

The exploration of optimal thermochemical conversion routes continues with Kourdi et al.23, who investigate the slow pyrolysis of argan oil residues to co-produce bio-oil, biochar, and gas. Using response surface methodology, they develop a quadratic regression model capable of predicting product yields with about 10 % error. The identified optimum occurs at temperatures between 470 °C and 490 °C with minimal residence time. Depending on the residue type, bio-oil yields range from 19 % to 25 %, biochar from 36 % to 39 %, and gas from 37 % to 42 %, confirming that waste by-products such as argan shells and pulps can contribute meaningfully to decentralized energy systems.

Extending the analysis to power generation, Nandimandalam et al.13 develop a two-layer biomass supply chain network for replacing fossil fuels with rural wood waste. Their model minimizes total cost subject to generation and capacity constraints. Applied to Grenada County, Mississippi, a 25 MW biomass facility is shown to meet roughly 32 % of local electricity demand, whereas a smaller 10 MW plant covers only 10 % under stricter emission limits but at higher per-unit costs. The comparison illustrates how emission caps directly influence economic feasibility and optimal plant scale.

At a national level, Tesfamichael et al.14 design a biomass-to-biofuel supply chain for Ethiopia, focusing on long-term investment and profitability over a 20-year horizon. The model incorporates spatial data on candidate facility locations and feedstock availability, offering practical insights for energy planning in developing economies.

Finally, Esteves et al.24 investigate integrated crop–livestock systems (ICLS) on a representative Brazilian farm, linking biofuel production to land-use and life-cycle assessment metrics. Their multi-objective analysis minimizes greenhouse gas emissions while maximizing energy efficiency, showing that soybean-based biodiesel achieves high efficiency with relatively low emissions. However, the intensity of feedlot operations remains a key driver of both economic and environmental performance.

Taken together, these studies reveal a shared challenge: optimizing bioenergy systems means managing not just economics and emissions, but also risk, resource variability, and social feasibility. Across technologies and regions, the key lies in integrating spatial data, uncertainty modeling, and policy instruments to design supply systems that are both efficient and resilient.

Models for sustainable supply chains and emission minimization

The development of sustainable supply chain models aimed at emission reduction and co-production typically relies on multi-criteria optimization to balance environmental, economic, and operational objectives. Such models support systematic planning by accounting for resource availability, transportation impacts, conversion efficiency, and emission constraints. Integrating emission reduction targets with co-production strategies, including energy recovery and by-product generation, enables supply chain configurations that seek to improve both economic and environmental performance.

Figure 2 presents a conceptual representation of an integrated emission monitoring and utilization system. The figure is illustrative and serves to contextualize how emission management and resource recovery can be linked within broader sustainability-oriented supply chain frameworks. The depicted components–such as capture units, microalgae cultivation systems, and photobioreactors–highlight the potential transformation of industrial emissions into value-added outputs including biofuels and related co-products.

Conceptual diagram of an integrated emission monitoring and utilization system linking capture with microalgae-based biofuel and by-product generation.

Recent studies incorporate environmental, economic, and social dimensions into biofuel supply chain modeling. Rather than focusing solely on cost minimization, many frameworks integrate indicators such as water use, land allocation, food security, and carbon emissions, often drawing on the Water–Energy–Food–Land (WEFL) Nexus perspective. For example, Machado et al.25 compare conventional ethanol supply chains with WEFL-based formulations that embed environmental and social metrics alongside economic criteria. Their findings suggest that incorporating resource interdependencies can improve second-generation ethanol output while maintaining cost competitiveness. Similarly, Ge et al.26 demonstrate that flexible, multi-pathway conversion networks can moderately reduce production costs relative to single-path systems, with capital expenditure and plant throughput identified as key drivers.

Other contributions emphasize integrated planning across decision levels. Mamo et al.27 link strategic facility decisions with tactical routing under an eco-cost framework, enabling joint economic and emission assessment. Gutierrez et al.28 and Lin et al.29 incorporate employment and regional development into multi-objective formulations, illustrating trade-offs between profitability, emissions, and social equity. In a related direction, Co et al.30 apply robust optimization to a microalgal biorefinery, explicitly exploring the balance between profitability and demand uncertainty. Ransikarbum et al.31 combine GIS data with fuzzy multi-objective programming to address spatial and stakeholder uncertainty in biomass supply planning. Becker et al.32 extend sustainability modeling to renewable liquid fuels, integrating social acceptance metrics into optimization-based facility design.

Many existing studies concentrate on specific conversion pathways or limited objective sets, often emphasizing either cost or emissions without fully integrating broader regional resilience considerations. While stochastic and fuzzy approaches address uncertainty, fewer frameworks explicitly map compromise regions within multi-dimensional performance spaces or connect economic trade-offs with environmental outcomes in a transparent manner.

The present study proposes a multi-objective optimization framework for the strategic assessment of regional biogas supply chains utilizing organic waste. The model evaluates eight normalized criteria–total cost, greenhouse gas emissions, energy revenue, water footprint, nutrient value, energy consumption, sanitation impact, and regional resilience–and identifies alternative trade-off configurations using the NSGA-II algorithm. The framework enables structured exploration of performance interactions and supports balanced evaluation of economic, environmental, and regional objectives under varying system conditions. Materials and methods

In order to capture the complex trade-offs inherent in regional bioenergy planning, MOO model has been developed. The choice of decision variables and objectives based on literature review and in practical experience from a case study of the Aktyuba BNG plant in the Republic of Tatarstan. That plant processes a mixture of agricultural residues, livestock manure, and food-industry by-products to produce purified methane for heat, power, and vehicle fuel.

Spatial heterogeneity in feedstock supply, transport infrastructure, and environmentally sensitive areas was explicitly represented using ArcGIS Pro. Farm locations, road networks, and exclusion-zone buffers were overlaid to compute haul distances and identify areas unsuitable for siting or land application. Stakeholder priorities–encompassing economic, environmental, and social criteria–were encoded as weighting coefficients via the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), ensuring that the decision-making framework reflects regional policy objectives and investor preferences. Robustness of the resulting Pareto-optimal solutions was assessed through elasticity-based sensitivity analysis.

Study area and spatial data

The Republic of Tatarstan was selected as a representative region combining intensive agricultural activity, diverse organic residues, and an emerging biogas infrastructure typical of many parts of the Russian Federation. The facility has a nominal installed electrical capacity of approximately 0.5 MW and operates for about 8,000 hours per year. Its feedstock mix comprises cattle manure (65%), crop residues (25%), and food waste (10%), with an average biogas yield of 0.58 m kg VS, consistent with regional experimental and operational data. All process and logistical parameters were compiled from the company TransUT LLC33 and associated local project documentation.

Figure 3 illustrates the spatial configuration of the case study. Feedstock sources include major dairy and poultry farms located within a 20 km radius of the proposed anaerobic digestion (AD) site. Geospatial layers—farm locations, the road network, and environmentally sensitive zones—were imported into ArcGIS Pro to calculate transportation distances (), delineate exclusion areas, and evaluate potential land-use interactions.

The eight selected criteria provide an integrated sustainability perspective beyond conventional techno-economic assessments. Four conventional indicators–total cost (TC), greenhouse gas emissions (GHG), water footprint (WF), and energy consumption (EC)–capture primary economic and environmental trade-offs in plant design and operation, with parameter values and scaling exponents derived from prior literature1,6,7. Non-linear terms, including capital cost scaling () and transport distance exponent (1.2), reflect observed economies of scale and marginal emission increases with distance34,35. Where literature values were unavailable, coefficients were calibrated using analogous plant measurements or standard engineering heuristics.

The remaining four criteria–energy revenue (ER), nutrient value of digestate (NV), sanitation index (SI), and regional resilience index (RE)–capture revenue, circular-economy, public health, and robustness aspects that are rarely integrated within a single framework36–39. Energy revenue reflects substrate composition, process efficiency, and capacity effects, modeled with non-linear saturation and oscillatory functions. Nutrient value employs a quadratic saturation law to represent diminishing fertilizer efficiency at high digestate application rates40. The sanitation index incorporates a beta-function to approximate sigmoidal pathogen reduction observed in laboratory and full-scale AD operations41. Regional resilience combines minimum utilization and variance penalties to assess robustness under variable feedstock availability42,43.

The novelty of the formulation lies in integrating all eight criteria within a normalized, high-dimensional Pareto framework. This enables simultaneous evaluation of economic, environmental, and social objectives under common numerical bounds, revealing multi-domain trade-offs–such as cost–emission–resilience or revenue–sanitation–resource efficiency–that are not captured in conventional two- or three-objective models.

Optimization algorithm implementation

The optimization problem defined in Table 1 was solved using the Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II)59, implemented in Python via the pymoo framework. The algorithm was used to approximate the Pareto-optimal trade-off surface across the eight normalized performance indicators described in the model formulation.

Problem definition A custom class BiogasMOOProblem was developed, where the decision vector combines the feedstock allocation variables and the binary operation indicator y. Each solution candidate is evaluated by computing the eight objective functions listed in Table 1, followed by normalization to the range [0, 1] using precomputed bounds. Indicators associated with benefits (e.g., revenue or social indices) are inverted so that all objectives are expressed as minimization tasks, consistent with the NSGA-II framework.

Three inequality constraints were enforced to ensure operational feasibility:

Constraint ensures that the total feedstock input does not exceed the nominal design capacity of the digestion unit, maintaining realistic loading rates and preventing hydraulic or thermal overloading60. Constraint limits the cumulative feedstock extraction according to the available regional biomass potential , representing land-use and collection boundaries within the catchment area61. Constraint enforces a non-negative operational status, where corresponds to inactive operation and to full utilization62. Together, these conditions maintain physical feasibility of the optimization results and reflect the practical limits of reactor design, feedstock availability, and operational control.

Algorithm configuration The NSGA-II configuration was designed to achieve a balance between exploration and convergence efficiency. A population size of 100 individuals was adopted, ensuring adequate diversity without excessive computational cost. Initial solutions were generated via uniform random sampling within the feasible range of each variable. Simulated Binary Crossover (SBX) with a probability of 0.9 and a distribution index was employed to promote meaningful recombination of parental traits, while Polynomial Mutation () introduced controlled stochastic variation to maintain diversity. Duplicate elimination was activated to prevent redundant solutions. The termination criterion was set at 200 generations, corresponding to the empirical convergence of the Pareto front across multiple test runs.

Post-processing and analysis The algorithm outputs two primary result sets: the decision matrix res.X containing non-dominated solutions and the corresponding objective matrix res.F. These represent an approximation of the eight-dimensional Pareto frontier. For interpretation, solutions were projected onto two- and three-dimensional subspaces (e.g., cost vs. emissions vs. energy yield) to visualize trade-offs among competing objectives. Subsequent decision analysis, such as AHP or preference ranking, can be applied to identify an operational configuration that best aligns with stakeholder priorities.

A solution is defined as Pareto-optimal if there exists no other feasible solution such that for all objectives i, and for at least one objective j. The set of all Pareto-optimal solutions constitutes the Pareto frontier (or Pareto set), which represents the trade-off surface where improvement in one criterion cannot be achieved without deteriorating at least one other.

Discussion

The field of biofuel supply chain optimization63 faces challenges arising from the complex interactions among economic, environmental, and social objectives. This study addresses these challenges by optimizing biogas production in the Tatarstan region through the integration of eight normalized objectives within a unified NSGA-II framework, yielding approximately Pareto-optimal solutions. This approach extends prior work in biofuel supply chain design63 by explicitly modeling nonlinear cost–revenue interactions, mixture entropy effects, and capacity-dependent penalties.

The current model is designed and optimized based on present conditions and available data. However, it does not incorporate possible future developments such as policy adjustments, economic incentives (e.g., carbon taxes or subsidies64), or technological progress. As a result, the analysis reflects the existing context rather than potential long-term dynamics. Future changes in regulations, market structures, or technology performance could influence the model’s outcomes and the relative attractiveness of different biofuel options. Incorporating scenario-based or dynamic modeling approaches65 in subsequent studies could provide a broader understanding of how such factors might affect system performance over time.

Uncertainty in feedstock availability, energy yield and transport emissions has been addressed through elasticity-based sensitivity analysis66–68. A 20% variation in feedstock price () shifts the Pareto knee by €, while ±20% changes in the transport emission factor produce ±15% swings in maximum GHG reduction. These findings complement stochastic and fuzzy-CVaR methods69,70 by quantifying parameter impacts without prohibitive computational cost.

Despite the comprehensive formulation, the static nature of assumed bounds () and historical data may not capture temporal fluctuations in feedstock supply or market dynamics71. Incorporation of real-time sensor data and adaptive learning algorithms (e.g. reinforcement learning) could enhance parameter estimation and solution adaptability72,73.

Social dimensions such as employment and community acceptance remain indirectly represented via the SI index; direct inclusion of job creation metrics74, public perception factors75 and equity considerations76 would improve the model’s policy relevance. Furthermore, expanding sustainability metrics to encompass land-use impacts and circular-economy indicators77,78 would provide a more holistic assessment.

Meanwhile, broader social dimensions remain only partially captured. Factors such as employment generation79, community acceptance80, odor perception81, and noise disturbance82, known to strongly influence the local acceptance of biogas installations–were not explicitly modeled due to data unavailability at the regional level. These aspects represent non-trivial determinants of project feasibility, especially in rural and peri-urban settings where stakeholder perception and land-use compatibility often shape permitting outcomes.

Future extensions of the framework may integrate spatial and survey-based social indicators, such as local employment multipliers, public acceptance indices, and buffer-distance restrictions around residential areas. Coupling these factors with geospatial optimization could yield a more comprehensive assessment of sustainability that combines techno-economic, environmental, and socio-spatial criteria within a unified decision-support structure.

The dense cluster of Pareto-optimal solutions identified in the 3D TC–GHG–ER space suggests a robust “compromise zone” that policymakers and investors could target. Subsidies for co-digestion and diversified procurement are likely to shift this cluster inward, achieving over 30% additional GHG reduction without increased CAPEX. Interdisciplinary collaboration among engineers, economists and social scientists is recommended to refine incentive mechanisms and ensure socio-economic feasibility83,84.

Parallelized evolutionary algorithms on high-performance computing architectures (e.g. MPI-based NSGA-II implementations and GPU-accelerated fitness evaluations), together with surrogate-assisted heuristics (e.g. Kriging or radial-basis function metamodels), permit extension of the biogas optimization framework to regional or national scales under a wide range of scenarios20. Scaling in this way supports strategic planning that accounts for feedstock variability, market fluctuations, and policy changes, and informs the design of biogas systems aligned with energy and environmental targets.

The development of sustainable biofuel supply chains requires interdisciplinary collaboration and the integration of advanced modeling techniques. By addressing existing challenges and refining optimization approaches, future studies can contribute to more efficient and environmentally responsible biofuel production.
