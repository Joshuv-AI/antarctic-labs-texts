Temporal landscapes of the gut microbiota–host axis reveal mechanisms of age-related eggshell quality decline in laying hens
Authors: Dong Dai, Pengzu Wang, Haijun Zhang, Guanghai Qi, Jing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Age-related shifts in the gut microbiota of laying hens significantly affect eggshell quality. However, the temporal interactions of the gut microbiota during the eggshell mineralization cycle remain unclear. Existing research often overlooks the rhythmic synchronization required for mineralization, as well as the specific cellular landscape of the aging intestine that impairs effective host–microbe crosstalk. We integrated 16S rRNA sequencing, metagenomics, untargeted metabolomics, and single-cell RNA sequencing to compare young and aged hens during the initial (7 h post-oviposition) and rapid growth (17 h post-oviposition) phases of eggshell mineralization. Aged hens exhibited significantly lower eggshell strength, thickness, and Ca/P concentrations (P < 0.05), which were associated with mitochondrial cristae disruption and necrocytosis in ileal tissues. 16S and metagenomic analyses revealed that young hens maintain stochastic microbial assembly, whereas aged hens shift toward deterministic processes driven by environmental stress. Rhythmic shifts in Lactobacillus and Ligilactobacillus were observed in young hens, supporting energy metabolism and mineral absorption pathways. In contrast, the aged hen microbiome remained focused on basal survival and oxidative stress responses. scRNA-seq identified nine cell populations, highlighting T cell exhaustion and HIF-1-driven metabolic reprogramming in epithelial cells of aged hens. Mediation analysis identified Ligilactobacillus salivarius as a keystone species that enhances eggshell breaking strength and thickness by increasing rhamnose and tyrosol levels and modulating host CALB1 and BLB2 expression. These findings indicate that aging disrupts proactive host–microbe synergy required for eggshell formation and identify L. salivarius-derived metabolites as promising candidates for restoring mineralization function in aged hens.

Introduction

Global demand for affordable protein has led to extended laying cycles in hens to maximize egg output1, which poses a significant challenge: the progressive decline in eggshell quality as laying hens age2. During the late production period, the incidence of broken eggs increases markedly, reducing marketable yield and posing food safety risks due to compromised physical barriers against pathogens3,4. Eggshell formation is one of the most rapid and intensive biomineralization processes in nature, requiring precise mobilization and transport of large quantities of calcium within a 24-h circadian cycle5. Consequently, maintaining robust physiological systems for energy and mineral absorption is essential for sustaining high-quality production in aged flocks.

The intestine serves as the primary site for the absorption of energy and mineral precursors required for eggshell calcification6. Recent evidence establishes the gut microbiota as a critical mediator of host metabolic health, particularly in nutrient utilization and intestinal barrier integrity7,8. In laying hens, microbial community composition shifts with age and dietary interventions, influencing production performance and egg quality9,10. Furthermore, the mineralization process is highly rhythmic, involving the formation of the mammillary and vertical crystal layers during the initial and rapid growth phases, which impose fluctuating metabolic demands on the host11. Preliminary studies suggest that the gut microbiome may exhibit similar temporal shifts, potentially synchronizing with host physiological demands to optimize energy and mineral availability12–14.

However, the regulatory role of the gut microbiota in egg quality remains poorly understood, partly because most studies have not aligned microbial analyses with the distinct stages of eggshell formation. Elucidating the temporal dynamics of gut microbiota–host intestinal interactions across the mineralization cycle, therefore, represents a critical knowledge gap. Specifically, it remains unclear how microbial functions shift across successive phases of eggshell formation and how these rhythmic patterns are disrupted by aging15. Although aging is known to cause intestinal morphological decline and dysbiosis in hens, the high-resolution cellular landscape of the aged intestine and its impaired interaction with the microbiota during critical mineralization windows remain poorly characterized16,17. Addressing this gap is essential for developing precision nutritional or microbial interventions aimed at mitigating age-related declines in eggshell quality.

In this study, we hypothesized that the gut microbiota in young hens acts as a rhythmic, proactive collaborator, synchronizing its metabolic output with eggshell mineralization stages, whereas aging induces a loss of this temporal synergy due to cellular exhaustion and deterministic microbial assembly. To test this hypothesis, we employed a multi-omics approach to map temporal single-cell transcriptomic and microbial shifts across the mineralization cycle in young and aged hens, thereby elucidating the cellular mechanisms of age-induced intestinal dysfunction. Furthermore, keystone microbial taxa and metabolites involved in nutrient absorption were identified. Our findings provide a high-resolution framework for understanding host–microbe interactions in poultry and identify specific microbial targets, such as Ligilactobacillus salivarius, for improving eggshell quality in aged hens.

Methods

Animal experiments

All protocols involving animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Feed Research of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (approval No. AEC-CAAS-20230502).

A total of 180 healthy Hy-Line Brown laying hens were enrolled in this study to investigate age-related physiological changes in the intestine. The subjects were selected from a commercial flock of 2000 birds and categorized into two groups representing distinct production stages: a young group at the peak of egg laying (38 weeks of age) and an aged group in the late production phase (78 weeks of age). To maintain high experimental representativeness, individual hens were screened and selected based on flock-average egg production rates and consistent eggshell quality parameters. Each experimental group comprised ten biological replicates.

Animal management and sample collection

All birds were fed a consistent basal diet formulated to meet or exceed the nutritional requirements established by the National Research Council (Table S1)18. Prior to the formal trial, all animals underwent a 1-week acclimatization period with ad libitum access to both feed and water. The experimental birds were housed in a controlled environment with the ambient temperature maintained at approximately 24 °C. A standardized lighting program was implemented to provide a 16 h photoperiod and an 8 h dark cycle, with the lights programmed to switch on daily at 05:00. These conditions were strictly monitored to ensure environmental stability across both age groups throughout the duration of the study.

According to the oviposition timing recorded over 7 consecutive days, we accurately determined the eggshell mineralization timeline in laying hens. Samples were collected at 7 and 17 h post-oviposition, corresponding to the initial and rapid growth phases of eggshell mineralization, respectively. One hen was randomly selected from each of the ten replicates per group for tissue analysis. Euthanasia was performed via cervical dislocation in accordance with established animal welfare guidelines. This method was chosen because it does not chemically contaminate tissues, thereby preserving the integrity of the microbiota and host samples for downstream analyses, and is fully compliant with the American Veterinary Medical Association Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Ileal tissues and microbial samples were immediately collected following the procedure for subsequent molecular and histological evaluations. Luminal contents were harvested for 16S rRNA, metagenomic, and metabolomic analyses, while mucosal scrapings were exclusively utilized for single-cell transcriptomics. All animal handling and surgical procedures were conducted in strict compliance with the ethical standards for avian research. Eggshell quality assessment and ultrastructural characterization

Eggshell strength was determined using an Egg Force Reader (Orka Food Technology, Israel). Eggshell thickness was measured with a thickness gauge (Orka Food Technology, Israel). For ultrastructural analysis, eggshell fragments were excised from the equatorial region, and adhering membranes were carefully removed. Samples were air-dried, sputter-coated with gold, and examined using scanning electron microscopy (SU8020, Hitachi, Japan). Cross-sectional images were acquired at 180× magnification. The effective layer thickness was defined as the distance from the cuticle surface to the base of the palisade layer, while the mammillary layer thickness extended from the base of the palisade layer to the top of the shell membrane. Total calcified layer thickness was calculated as the sum of these two layers, and the relative proportion of each layer was expressed as a percentage of the total.

Determination of calcium and phosphorus contents in plasma and eggshells

At the exact designated temporal checkpoints (0, 7, and 17 h post-oviposition), approximately 3 mL of whole blood was drawn from the brachial wing vein of each subject using sterilized, heparinized syringes. Samples were immediately centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min at a constant 4 °C to isolate the plasma fraction. The plasma supernatant was carefully collected, aliquoted, and immediately stored at −80 °C to prevent degradation. Circulating calcium and phosphorus levels were subsequently quantified using a calcium assay kit (C004-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) and a phosphorus assay kit (C006-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). All procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Eggshell samples were rinsed with warm distilled water to remove surface contaminants, followed by drying and pulverization. Approximately 1 g of powdered eggshell was digested in 4 mL of nitric acid at 110 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, 3 mL of hydrogen peroxide was added, and the mixture was further digested at 160 °C until a clear solution was obtained. The digested solution was then diluted to a final volume of 100 mL with distilled water. Calcium and phosphorus concentrations were quantified using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; Agilent 7700, USA).

Ultrastructural analysis via transmission electron microscopy

Ileal segments were meticulously excised and immediately immersed in a 3% glutaraldehyde solution for initial processing. The tissue was further divided into small cubes of approximately 1 mm³ and fixed in the same glutaraldehyde buffer for a duration of 24 h. Following this primary fixation, the specimens were rinsed multiple times with phosphate-buffered saline to remove excess fixative. Dehydration was performed through a sequential series of ascending ethanol concentrations. Subsequently, the dehydrated samples were infiltrated and embedded in epoxy resin to provide structural support for sectioning. Ultrathin sections with a thickness ranging from 50 to 80 nm were prepared using an ultramicrotome and mounted onto copper grids. The sections underwent double staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate to enhance contrast. Finally, the morphological and ultrastructural characteristics of the ileal tissues were visualized and captured using a transmission electron microscope.

16S rRNA gene sequencing of intestinal microbiota

Luminal contents were collected from the mid-ileum and immediately transferred into sterile cryovials. Total DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A. Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality and concentration were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis (1%) and spectrophotometric analysis (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The V3-V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR amplification was performed on a GeneAmp 9700 thermocycler (Applied Biosystems) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 27 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 30 s), annealing (55 °C, 30 s), and extension (72 °C, 45 s), followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Amplicons were purified and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform. Raw sequencing reads were processed using QIIME 2, including quality filtering and denoising. Amplicon sequence variants were inferred using the DADA2 algorithm. Taxonomic assignment was performed against the SILVA database (version 138). Beta diversity was evaluated based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Statistical differences between groups were assessed using analysis of similarities (ANOSIM). Non-parametric tests, including the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Kruskal–Wallis test, were used to compare group differences. Differentially abundant taxa were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe).

Keystone species identification based on deep learning

Keystone taxa were identified using a deep learning–based perturbation framework to evaluate their ecological importance within microbial communities. Microbial abundance data were used to train a neural network model capable of capturing complex and non-linear interspecific associations. The contribution of each taxon was then assessed through in silico perturbation, in which individual taxa were systematically altered or removed, and the resulting shifts in predicted community structure were quantified. A keystoneness (Ks) was calculated to represent the magnitude of influence of each taxon, defined as the average change in community composition following perturbation relative to the unperturbed state. Higher Ks values indicate a stronger regulatory effect on community organization and stability, suggesting a greater likelihood of being a keystone species. Taxa with the highest Ks values were therefore considered to play disproportionately important roles in maintaining community structure and function.

Microbial co-occurrence network construction

Microbial association networks were constructed using the CoNet plugin implemented in Cytoscape (v3.8.2) to identify non-random co-occurrence patterns among taxa19. Separate networks were generated for microbiota derived from young and aged groups. Pairwise correlations were calculated using Spearman’s method, with a stringent threshold (correlation filter = 0.001). Statistical significance of associations was determined at P < 0.05 after optimization procedures.

Neutral community model fitting

The neutral community model (NCM) was applied to evaluate the contribution of stochastic processes to microbial community assembly20. Model fitting was performed using the getopt package (v1.20.3). The Nm parameter, representing the product of metacommunity size and migration rate, was used to describe dispersal dynamics. Model performance was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R²), where higher values indicate stronger agreement with neutral expectations and greater stochastic influence21. Confidence intervals (95%) were estimated using bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations).

Normalized stochasticity ratio analysis

The relative importance of stochastic versus deterministic processes in community assembly was quantified using the normalized stochasticity ratio (NST) within a null-model framework. An NST threshold of 50% was used to distinguish stochastic (>50%) from deterministic (<50%) assembly processes. NST values were calculated in R using the “NST” package (v3.1.10)22.

The β-nearest taxon index (βNTI) analysis

To account for the potential influence of unmeasured variables on community assembly, a phylogenetic null model approach was applied to infer underlying ecological processes23. The βNTI was calculated in R (version 3.3.1) using the iCAMP package (version 1.5.12). βNTI is derived from the β-mean nearest taxon distance and quantifies the standardized effect size of phylogenetic turnover between communities. Thresholds of βNTI were used to distinguish assembly mechanisms: |βNTI| ≥ 2 indicates that phylogenetic turnover significantly deviates from null expectations, reflecting deterministic processes, whereas |βNTI| < 2 suggests that community turnover does not differ significantly from the null model, indicating stochastic processes.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions

Ileal tissues specimens were harvested from eight laying hens and immediately transferred into sterile dishes containing 10 mL of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline to remove any residual contaminants. The tissues were mechanically minced and enzymatically dissociated using a solution of 0.25% trypsin and 10 µg/mL DNase I in PBS supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum. This digestion process was conducted at 37 °C with constant agitation at 50 rpm for a total duration of 40 min. To ensure optimal cell yield and viability, dissociated cells were collected every 20 min. The resulting cell suspensions were passed through a 40 µm nylon mesh filter, after which erythrocytes were eliminated using a specialized red blood cell lysis solution. Following a wash in PBS containing 2% FBS, cell viability and concentration were determined using 0.4% Trypan blue staining and a Countess II Automated Cell Counter.

10x Genomics library construction and sequencing

Single-cell libraries were generated using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell v3.1 system. Individual cells were encapsulated into Gel Beads in Emulsion to facilitate the attachment of unique molecular identifiers and cell-specific barcodes. Following cell lysis, polyadenylated mRNA was hybridized to the beads for reverse transcription, effectively tagging each cDNA molecule at the 5′ end. The subsequent steps, including second-strand cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation, and amplification, were performed according to the standard 10x Genomics protocol. Sequencing libraries were constructed by enriching for 3′ transcript ends through whole transcriptome amplification. The quality and quantity of the libraries were assessed using an Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Chip and a Qubit High Sensitivity DNA Assay. High-throughput sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq Xplus platform in PE150 mode by Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. in Shanghai.

Processing of single-cell sequencing data

The raw sequencing reads were processed with Cell Ranger v7.1.0, which involved aligning Illumina FASTQ files to the chicken reference genome GRCg6a using the STAR algorithm24. Gene barcode matrices were generated by quantifying unique molecular identifiers and filtering out barcodes not associated with cells. The resulting expression matrix was imported into Seurat v4.1.1 for downstream quality control and transcriptomic analysis25. Low-quality cells were excluded based on parameters such as the number of detected genes, total UMI counts, and the percentage of mitochondrial reads using the Percentage Feature Set function25.

The data were normalized, and highly variable genes were identified using the default Seurat functions. To integrate data across multiple samples, we utilized the Find Integration Anchors and Integrate Data functions25,26. Principal component analysis was employed to reduce the dimensionality of the data to the top 30 components after scaling. For visualization, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection was used to project the data into a two-dimensional space. Cell clustering was performed using the Louvain method based on a shared nearest neighbor graph25. Differential expression across clusters was determined using the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test. Cell types were identified using the SingleR package in combination with established canonical markers27. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change greater than 0.25 and an adjusted p value of 0.05 or less were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Functional enrichment and pseudotime trajectory analysis

To characterize the biological functions of the DEGs, enrichment analyses were conducted using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes and Gene Ontology databases. Significant enrichment was defined by a Bonferroni-corrected p value of 0.05 or less relative to the transcriptomic background.

The chronological progression of cellular changes was modeled through pseudotime trajectory analysis. The DDRTree algorithm within the Monocle2 software package was employed to reconstruct the sequence of gene expression changes and determine the relative position of each cell along the trajectory28. After reducing the dimensionality of the expression data, a minimum spanning tree was constructed to fit the optimal cellular trajectory. Additionally, branch expression analysis modeling was utilized to identify specific gene expression patterns that were dependent on distinct lineage branches.

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

Microbial DNA was extracted as described above. Genomic DNA was fragmented to an average size of ~400 bp using a Covaris M220 ultrasonicator. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Raw reads were filtered to remove low-quality sequences (length <50 bp, quality score <20, or containing ambiguous bases) using FLASH (v1.2.7). Clean reads were assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT based on a de Bruijn graph algorithm29. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using MetaGene30, and ORFs ≥ 100 bp were retained and translated into amino acid sequences. A non-redundant gene catalog was constructed through sequence clustering at high identity31. Gene abundance was estimated by mapping high-quality reads to the catalog (95% identity) using the Short Oligonucleotide Analysis Package32. Taxonomic and functional annotations were performed against the NCBI NR and KEGG databases using DIAMOND with an e-value cutoff of 1e-533.

Metabolomic profiling of microbial metabolites

Metabolite extraction of the ileal contents was performed by mixing 100 μL of sample supernatant with 400 μL of pre-chilled methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). After vortexing, samples were sonicated at 5 °C for 30 min and incubated at −20 °C for 30 min to precipitate proteins. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was collected for analysis. Metabolomic profiling was conducted using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters HSS T3 column under both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. Data processing included peak alignment, normalization using the total ion signal, and removal of low-abundance features. Metabolite identification was performed by matching mass spectra to the KEGG and HMDB databases. All analyses were carried out using the Majorbio Cloud Platform.

Statistical mediation analysis

To investigate the potential mechanistic pathways linking L. salivarius to host gene expression and metabolite profiles, mediation analysis was performed. This approach estimates the extent to which the association between an independent variable and an outcome is transmitted through an intermediate mediator34. Analyses were implemented in R using the mediation package (version 4.5.0). Briefly, regression models were first constructed to quantify the total effect of L. salivarius on target genes and metabolites, as well as the relationships between L. salivarius, candidate mediators, and eggshell quality. Direct and mediated effects were then estimated based on these fitted models. The statistical significance of mediation effects was subsequently evaluated to determine the contribution of intermediate variables to the observed associations.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Analysis Software (SAS, version 9.2). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to any parametric evaluation. Data meeting the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were analyzed using parametric tests, whereas non-parametric alternatives were employed when these assumptions were violated. Differences between the two groups were evaluated using two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance was applied, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and a P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Age-related decline in eggshell quality correlates with intestinal dysfunction

Significant physiological differences exist between young and aged hens regarding eggshell quality. Compared to young hens, aged hens exhibited significant reductions in eggshell strength, thickness, and hardness (P < 0.05; Fig. 1B). Furthermore, calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) concentrations in the shells of aged hens were significantly lower (P < 0.05; Fig. 1C), which subsequently impaired the eggshell ultrastructure. Specifically, the thickness of the calcified layer in aged hens decreased significantly (P < 0.05; Fig. 1D, E), accompanied by a reduced proportion of the effective layer and a significantly higher proportion of the mammillary layer (P < 0.05; Fig. S1). Given that the intestine is the primary organ for absorbing precursors required for mineralization, ileal morphology was examined using transmission electron microscopy. The results showed significant membrane damage and necrocytosis in the ileal tissues of aged hens (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, mitochondria exhibited cristae disruption and membrane dissolution (Fig. 1F), indicating a decline in ileal homeostasis. Notably, periodic fluctuations in blood Ca and P levels were significantly altered in aged hens compared to young hens (P < 0.05; Fig. 1G, H). Consequently, the decline in eggshell quality during late production is linked to mineral absorption disorders driven by the ileal dysfunction.

Rhythmic shifts in intestinal microbial composition synchronize with the mineralization cycle

The composition of the ileal microbiota in laying hens underwent a significant transition with age. Microbial richness in aged hens was significantly higher than in young hens (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A). PCoA demonstrated a clear separation of samples among groups, indicating distinct differences in microbial community structure between young and aged hens (P < 0.05; Fig. 2B). The ileal microbiota was primarily composed of Lactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, Romboutsia, Limosilactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Streptococcus (Fig. 2C). In aged hens, the relative abundances of Ligilactobacillus significantly decreased (P < 0.05; Fig. 2D), while Liquorilactobacillus, Paracoccus, and Gallicola were significantly enriched (linear discriminant analysis score >3.5; Fig. 2E). Furthermore, dynamic changes in the ileal microbiota were identified during the eggshell mineralization process. PCoA confirmed that the microbial communities in both young and aged hens shifted significantly throughout mineralization (P < 0.05; Fig. 2F). The most pronounced microbial variations occurred during the rapid growth phase of the mineralization cycle. The relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Ligilactobacillus increased significantly during the rapid growth phase (P < 0.05). Characterization of the specific microbial taxa responding to the mineralization cycle was subsequently performed. Species exhibiting a high median keystone index, including Limosilactobacillus, Enterococcus, Romboutsia, and Ligilactobacillus (Fig. 2H). They displayed substantial Ks fluctuations across different samples, suggesting pronounced community specificity. To explore the community specificity of these significant species, we analyzed the relationship between the median keystone index and the median absolute deviation of the keystone index (Fig. 2I). A strong correlation was observed (R2 = 0.71, P < 0.001) between them, which demonstrated that Limosilactobacillus, Enterococcus, Romboutsia, and Ligilactobacillus were identified as the primary keystone species driving the ileal microbial response to eggshell mineralization.

Functional shift of the intestinal microbiome from mineralization-specific synergy in young hens to basal maintenance in aged hens

A total of 483.30 Gb of raw metagenomic data was generated for this study. Following stringent quality control, including the removal of low-quality bases and host contamination, we obtained 120.42 Gb of high-quality metagenomic data, with an average of 3.17 Gb per sample. These metagenomic results corroborated 16S rRNA sequencing findings and further elucidated the temporal dynamics of microbial species in young and aged hens (Fig. 3A). Specifically, the relative abundances of Ligilactobacillus salivarius, Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus johnsonii, Limosilactobacillus faecipullorum, Lactobacillus gallinarum, and Limosilactobacillus reuteri significantly increased during the rapid growth phase of mineralization, while Enterococcus cecorum and Romboutsia ilealis levels decreased (P < 0.05; Fig. 3B). By querying the Carbohydrate-Active Enzymes (CAZy) database, a total of 9,275 genes involved in carbohydrate digestion were identified in the ileal of hens (Fig. 3C). In both groups, the expression of genes encoding Carbohydrate Esterases and Auxiliary Activities significantly increased during the rapid mineralization phase, while Glycosyl Transferases were significantly downregulated (P < 0.05; Fig. 3D). The functional transitions of the microbiota during mineralization further highlighted the adaptive relationship between the microbiome and the host. In young hens, the enrichment of energy metabolism was characterized in the initial phase of mineralization, which shifted toward functions associated with host physiological homeostasis during the rapid growth phase (Fig. 3E). In contrast, the microbiome of aged hens was enriched in oxidative stress pathways in the initial phase of mineralization, indicating a decline in microenvironmental stability (Fig. 3F). During the rapid growth phase of mineralization, the aged hen microbiome focused on pathways related to proliferation and repair to mitigate stress-induced damage (Fig. 3F). Calcium signaling (ko04020), mineral absorption (ko04978), Toll-like receptor signaling (ko04620), and T cell receptor signaling (ko04660) were significantly upregulated in young hens during the rapid growth phase of mineralization (Fig. 3G), accompanied by a downregulation of Lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis (ko00540). However, these functional adaptations were entirely absent in the microbiome of aged hens.

In the initial phase of mineralization, the ileal microbiome of young hens enriched pathways for structural synthesis and energy activation to facilitate active colonization and mineralization support (Fig. 3H). In contrast, the signaling pathways enriched in aged hens reflected a state of injury and stress (Fig. 3H). In the rapid growth phase, the microbiome of young hens significantly enriched molecular functions required for interaction with host epithelial cells, including cytoskeleton remodeling, focal adhesion activation, and immune regulation (Fig. 3I). The microbiome of aged hens remained focused on basal survival and metabolic maintenance (Fig. 3I). The contribution analysis of species confirmed that L. salivarius, L. crispatus, R. ilealis, and L. johnsonii play pivotal roles in these temporal functional shifts (Fig. S2). Therefore, during the critical physiological window of eggshell mineralization, the ileal microbiome of young hens acts as a proactive collaborator, storing energy and precursors in the initial phase to support strong interactions with host physiology during the rapid growth phase. However, the microbiome of aged hens is largely restricted to basal metabolism and survival, failing to effectively support the host’s energy and mineral requirements.

Aging-induced transition from stochastic to deterministic processes in intestinal microbial assembly

The underlying factors driving shifts in the ileal microbiota of aged laying hens remain poorly understood. This study investigated the impact of age on the mechanisms governing microbial community assembly. The microbial co-occurrence network in young hens comprised 32 nodes and 69 edges (|R| > 0.70, P < 0.05; Fig. 4A). The network in aged hens was substantially more complex, containing 46 nodes and 279 edges (|R| > 0.70, P < 0.05; Fig. 4B). This increased connectivity suggests that microbial interactions become more intricate in the aged ileal environment. To distinguish the contribution of stochastic processes to community assembly, we employed the NCM, which evaluates the relationship between the occurrence frequency of taxa and their relative abundance (Fig. 4C, D). Single-cell atlas of the intestinal microenvironment reveals cellular exhaustion and metabolic reprogramming in aged hens

To characterize the shifts and underlying mechanisms in the ileal microenvironment of aging hens, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform. A total of 72,351 cells were captured, with a median gene count ranging from 602 to 1261 per cell and a valid barcode rate between 94.4 and 96.9%. Following non-linear dimensionality reduction, the 822.06 Gb of raw data were partitioned into 36 clusters (Figs. S3 and S4). Based on cell-type-specific marker genes, nine distinct cell populations were identified, including epithelial cells, T cells, macrophages, B cells, plasma cells, erythrocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and glial cells (Fig. 5B). The relative proportions of these populations differed significantly between age groups. Notably, aged hens exhibited a substantial increase in epithelial cell numbers and a significant depletion of the T cell population (Fig. 5C). Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed that aged epithelial cells were characterized by the activation of inflammatory and apoptotic pathways, leading to a loss of barrier integrity and homeostatic imbalance (Fig. 5D). This was accompanied by HIF-1-driven metabolic reprogramming, which further suppressed mineral absorption (Fig. 5D). In the T cell compartment of aged hens, the significant activation of TCR signaling and Th1/Th2/Th17 differentiation were observed (Fig. 5E), indicating a persistent state of chronic inflammation (upregulation of TLR/NLR, IL-17, and chemokine pathways). Notably, T cells in aged hens showed hallmarks of senescence and exhaustion, evidenced by the upregulation of PD-1/PD-L1 and apoptotic pathways (Fig. 5E), resulting in impaired proliferative capacity and compromised effector functions. This cellular exhaustion aligns with the observed decline in T cell abundance. Furthermore, scoring systems for inflammatory response and T cell activation were significantly higher in aged hens compared to young hens (Fig. 5F). Correlation analysis revealed the differences in host–microbe interactions between young and aged hens. In young hens, microbiota was positively correlated with genes involved in protein digestion, mineral absorption, calcium transport, and bile acid secretion in the initial phase of mineralization, while microbiota showed a negative correlation with inflammatory genes in the rapid growth phase (Fig. 6E). L. salivarius-derived metabolites regulate intestinal homeostasis during eggshell mineralization

To investigate the mechanisms by which L. salivarius regulates ileal homeostasis during the mineralization process, a comprehensive metabolomic analysis of the hen ileum was performed, identifying a total of 3,177 metabolites (Fig. S6). PCA revealed distinct metabolic profiles between young and aged hens throughout the mineralization process (P < 0.05; Fig. 7A). There were 476 differential metabolites among groups (P < 0.05, VIP > 1, FC > 1.5; Fig. 7B). KEGG pathway enrichment highlighted key regulatory pathways involved in energy metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, antioxidant defense, and bile acid regulation, which were congruent with the temporal transcriptomic shifts observed in the ileal epithelium (Fig. 7C). Subsequent variable importance in projection analysis identified rhamnose as a critical temporal marker, with its concentration significantly higher during the rapid growth phase of mineralization compared to the initial phase (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, the ileum of young hens was significantly enriched in tyrosol and niacinamide, metabolites known to support host tissue homeostasis and energy metabolism (Fig. 7D). Conversely, aged hens exhibited a marked accumulation of taurocholic acid, indicating a profound disruption in bile acid metabolism (Fig. 7E). These findings suggest that the microbiota in young hens provides robust metabolic support for host homeostasis, whereas the aged microbiota is characterized by metabolic dysregulation. Integrated metagenomic and metabolomic analyses indicated strong correlations between specific taxa and metabolites (Fig. 7F). Notably, the abundance of L. salivarius was significantly associated with rhamnose and tyrosol levels (Fig. S6), suggesting a potential link between this species and these compounds, consistent with previous reports on its metabolic versatility36–38. The content variations of three microbial metabolites with greater effects on ileal temporal function were further validated during the mineralization stage in young and aged laying hens (Fig. 7G–I). To further explore the potential causality between the microbiota, metabolites, host genes, and eggshell phenotypes, mediation analysis was employed to construct a functional interaction model (Fig. 7J). The results established a potential mechanistic association: L. salivarius exerts a positive effect on eggshell quality through a specific microbial-metabolic axis. Discussion

The decline in eggshell quality during the late production period represents a multifactorial physiological dysfunction. The results of this study suggest that this phenomenon is not solely a consequence of organ senescence. Instead, it arises from disrupted temporal coordination between the gut microbiota and host intestinal homeostasis. Young hens maintain a state of proactive host–microbiota collaboration, whereas aging induces a shift toward deterministic microbial assembly and host cellular exhaustion. This loss of rhythmic synchronization provides a high-resolution mechanistic explanation for intestinal absorption disorders in aged flocks.

A key finding of this study is a shift in microbial assembly mechanisms with aging. In young hens, the gut microbiota is primarily shaped by stochastic processes. This stochasticity enables a flexible microbial community that can adjust its composition and functional output in response to host physiological signals during the mineralization cycle39,40. However, the aged gut shifts toward deterministic assembly. This shift is likely driven by environmental filtering, whereby harsh conditions in the aging intestine, including chronic inflammation and tissue damage, impose strong selective pressures8,41. Consequently, the aged microbiome loses functional plasticity and prioritizes stress survival over host support. The proactive collaboration observed in young hens reflects a coordinated metabolic preparation phase. During the initial stage of mineralization, the young microbiome enriches pathways related to energy activation and structural synthesis42. This serves as a metabolic reservoir to support the high demand for calcium and phosphorus transport during the rapid growth phase43,44. In aged hens, this anticipatory functional shift is absent. The aged microbiome remains in a static state of stress response, failing to provide the rhythmic support necessary for efficient biomineralization45. In addition, literature profiling the aged avian gut has documented a generalized state of dysbiosis, typically characterized by an expansion of Proteobacteria, a concomitant loss of Firmicutes diversity, and a systemic decline in highly beneficial short-chain fatty acid producers13. However, these foundational longitudinal studies have rarely accounted for the extreme circadian fluctuations inherent to the host’s daily mineralization cycle. Consequently, if previous studies conducted sampling at arbitrary or disparate time points relative to oviposition, the widely reported age-related microbial shifts may actually represent heavily confounded datasets, inextricably mixing the signatures of permanent biological senescence with highly transient rhythmic adaptations.

Single-cell transcriptomics provided deep insights into why the aged intestine no longer interacts effectively with the microbiota. The observed HIF-1-driven metabolic reprogramming indicates a transition toward severe physiologic hypoxia in the aged gut46,47. Hypoxia-induced inflammatory cascades and reactive oxygen species negatively regulate epithelial calcium channels through intermediate regulatory hormones. Consequently, the observed decline in CALB1 expression is highly likely a downstream consequence of global epithelial structural distress and compromised transcellular calcium influx48. Furthermore, the depletion of T cells and the upregulation of exhaustion markers like PD-1 characterize a state of immunosenescence in the gut49,50. This chronic inflammatory environment impairs normal host–microbe communication, preventing intestinal cells from responding to beneficial microbial signals that would otherwise promote homeostasis51,52. Previous work in laying hens has shown that altering light cycles to re-entrain microbial diurnal rhythms improved calcium absorption and shell strength45. Our results are consistent with this observation: a microbiome synchronized with the oviposition schedule may ensure the timely provision of metabolites (e.g., SCFAs and amino acids) and mineral chelators when eggshell gland demand peaks. Microbial metabolites such as gluconic and threonic acids were identified as Ca²⁺ chelators that accumulate during shell formation, potentially derived from microbes such as Weissella45. These findings are consistent with ours and indicate that gut microbes contribute specific metabolites that facilitate calcium handling. In summary, temporal coordination between gut bacteria and mineralization supports the hypothesis that a healthy, young microbiome actively promotes eggshell deposition.

A previous review proposed pathways by which the gut microbiota in laying hens regulate egg quality through metabolites such as SCFAs, bile acids, and amino acid derivatives13. Our study supports this view by showing that microbial metabolites and host–microbe signaling vary with age and calcification stage. Here, we provide stage-specific insights: the intestinal microbiome of young hens operates in a mineralization mode, enriching CAZymes involved in fiber degradation and vitamin/cofactor synthesis, whereas aged microbiomes shift toward maintenance metabolism. The identification of L. salivarius as a keystone beneficial species in our study extends recent probiotic research. Dietary supplementation with L. salivarius improves eggshell strength, villus height, and gut barrier gene expression in hens53. This is consistent with our finding that L. salivarius may positively influence eggshell strength by modulating intestinal homeostasis via rhamnose and tyrosol38,54–56. In contrast, previous studies have primarily focused on bone or uterine factors (e.g., hormonal or photoperiodic regulation), with less attention to gut dynamics. Our work thus proposes that microbiota–gut crosstalk represents an additional regulatory layer. The correlation between L. salivarius and host genes such as CALB1 (calcium transport) and BLB2 (immune regulation) highlights a potential axis for intervention. In contrast, the accumulation of taurocholic acid in older hens suggests a broader disruption in bile acid metabolism, which may further impair the absorption of mineral precursors57–59. By integrating metagenomics and scRNA-seq, we delineate both microbial and host cellular signatures of aging, which have not been previously characterized in detail in poultry.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the correlative nature of our findings precludes causal inference; definitive validation will require interventional models, such as microbiota transplantation or gnotobiotic systems. Second, while the single-cell RNA sequencing effectively mapped high-resolution cellular landscapes with robust statistical power, the inclusion of eight laying hens represents a limited biological replicate size for capturing inter-individual host variability. Third, while the experimental design systematically compared young and aged hens housed under standardized conditions, it is intrinsically challenging to completely isolate the core physiological effects of cellular aging from the cumulative impacts of prolonged environmental and management exposures. Further experimental studies considering the age factor need to be performed. Addressing these limitations will strengthen the mechanistic understanding of gut microbiota–host interactions in eggshell formation. Based on our results, future in vivo studies should focus on: (i) causal assessment of eggshell quality using germ-free or antibiotic-treated hen models colonized with young versus aged gut microbiota; (ii) targeted probiotic or postbiotic interventions, particularly those involving L. salivarius; and (iii) management strategies that reinforce microbial circadian rhythms, such as optimized light-dark cycles or timed feeding, to improve eggshell quality.

In summary, this study demonstrates that a youth-associated microbiome provides synchronized metabolic support for eggshell calcification, whereas aging disrupts this gut–microbiota synergy, leading to reduced eggshell quality. Young hens exhibit a rhythmic host–microbiota axis in which taxa such as L. salivarius proactively support the metabolic and mineral demands of the eggshell mineralization cycle. Conversely, aging shifts the intestinal microenvironment toward chronic inflammation and deterministic microbial assembly, thereby constraining the microbiome to a passive role focused on basal survival rather than host support (Fig. 8). These findings establish the gut microbiota as a key regulatory axis in eggshell mineralization.
