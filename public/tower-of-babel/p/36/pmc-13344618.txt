Inhibitory HLA‐C/KIR2DL2 Interactions and KIR Allelic Polymorphisms Are Associated With Susceptibility to Philadelphia Chromosome‐Negative Myeloproliferative Neoplasms
Authors: Velizar Shivarov, Gergana Tsvetkova, Evgueniy Hadjiev, Milena Ivanova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundImmune surveillance is increasingly recognized as a determinant of clonal selection in Philadelphia chromosome‐negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN). While HLA‐restricted adaptive immune effects have been described, the contribution of inherited variation in natural killer (NK)‐cell regulatory pathways remains less well defined. We investigated whether killer immunoglobulin‐like receptor (KIR) allelic polymorphisms and HLA–KIR receptor–ligand interactions are associated with susceptibility to MPN.MethodsWe performed high‐resolution next‐generation sequencing‐based KIR genotyping in 167 Bulgarian patients with Philadelphia chromosome‐negative MPN, including 137 with JAK2 V617F‐positive disease and 30 with CALR‐mutated disease, and in 97 healthy controls. All subjects had prior high‐resolution typing for HLA‐A, ‐B, and ‐C. Associations between KIR alleles and disease status were evaluated using additive generalized linear models adjusted for age and sex. HLA–KIR receptor–ligand interactions were analyzed using biologically defined interaction models.ResultsSeveral KIR allelic associations with MPN susceptibility were identified, although some involved low‐frequency variants. Across analyses, the most consistent finding was that inhibitory KIR2DL2, together with HLA‐C2 ligands, was associated with increased susceptibility to MPN overall and within mutation‐defined subgroups, with the strongest signal observed in CALR‐mutated disease. In contrast, the absence of KIR2DL2 was associated with a lower risk in both JAK2 V617F‐positive and CALR‐mutated MPN. These findings are consistent with a model in which inherited inhibitory NK‐cell signaling may influence early immune escape of emerging mutant clones.ConclusionInherited variation in KIR genes and HLA–KIR inhibitory interactions may contribute to susceptibility to Philadelphia chromosome‐negative MPN. The findings support a possible role for NK‐cell regulatory pathways in early MPN pathogenesis, but should be considered hypothesis‐generating and require validation in larger independent cohorts.Trial RegistrationThe author has confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.

Philadelphia chromosome‐negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) are clonal hematopoietic stem cell disorders driven predominantly by mutually exclusive somatic mutations in JAK2, CALR, or MPL [1]. Recent studies support a role for immune surveillance in early MPN pathogenesis, including mutation‐specific adaptive immune editing mediated by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) Class I presentation of neoantigens derived from JAK2 V617F and mutant calreticulin [2, 3, 4]. We hypothesized that inherited variation in natural killer (NK)‐cell regulatory pathways may provide an additional layer of immune selection. To test this, we performed high‐resolution next‐generation sequencing‐based killer immunoglobulin‐like receptor (KIR) genotyping in Bulgarian patients with Philadelphia chromosome‐negative MPN and analyzed both KIR allelic associations and HLA–KIR receptor–ligand combinations in relation to disease susceptibility.

We studied 167 patients with Philadelphia chromosome‐negative MPN, including 137 with JAK2 V617F‐mutated disease and 30 with CALR‐mutated disease, together with 97 unrelated healthy controls. All subjects had previously undergone high‐resolution typing for HLA‐A, ‐B, and ‐C [3, 4]. KIR genotyping was performed using the NGSgo‐AmpX KIR platform, followed by Illumina MiniSeq sequencing and allele assignment with NGSengine [5]. Associations between KIR alleles and disease status were tested using additive generalized linear models adjusted for age and sex, while HLA–KIR receptor–ligand interactions were analyzed using the midasHLA package [6], as in our previous work [4]. Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table S1. Exact carrier frequencies of the KIR alleles discussed in the manuscript are shown in Table S2, while exact frequencies of KIR2DL2 presence/absence, HLA‐C2 carriage, and the KIR2DL2/HLA‐C2 combination are shown in Table S3.

We first assessed associations between individual KIR alleles and overall susceptibility to MPNs. Several statistically significant signals were identified. The allele KIR2DL4*00801 (OR: 2.56; 95% CI: 1.31–4.99; p = 0.006) was significantly enriched in patients compared with healthy controls, suggesting a potential role in disease predisposition (Figure 1A). In contrast, KIR3DL1*00501 (OR: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.35–0.96; p = 0.034) and KIR3DL3*104 (OR: 0.17; 95% CI: 0.04–0.69; p = 0.013) were significantly depleted, indicating protective effects (Figure 1A). Absence of KIR2DL2 was also associated with lower risk for MPN (OR: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.38–0.89; p = 0.012) (Figure 1A). These findings support the hypothesis that inherited variation in NK receptor signaling influences early immunosurveillance of malignant hematopoiesis.

Mutation‐specific analyses revealed distinct immunogenetic landscapes. In JAK2 V617F+ patients, enrichment of KIR3DL3*00801 (OR: 13.36; 95% CI: 1.29–138.25; p = 0.03), KIR2DL4*00801 (OR: 3.08; 95% CI: 1.48–6.41; p = 0.003), and KIR3DL1*00101 (OR: 1.79; 95% CI: 1.04–3.06; p = 0.034) was observed, accompanied by reduced frequencies of KIR3DL3*104 (OR: 0.15; 95% CI: 0.03–0.76; p = 0.022) and KIR3DL1*00501 (OR: 0.54; 95% CI: 0.32–0.93; p = 0.026) (Figure 1B). Absence of the KIR2DL2 gene was also associated with a lower risk for JAK2 V617F+ MPN (OR: 0.61; 95% CI: 0.39–0.95; p = 0.03) (Figure 1B).

In contrast, CALRmut+ disease was characterized by enrichment of KIR3DL3*00701 (OR: 7.21; 95% CI: 1.06–48.85; p = 0.043) and KIR2DL2*00301 (OR: 2.82; 95% CI: 1.11–7.18; p = 0.03), while KIR2DL4*01101 (OR: 0.26; 95% CI: 0.07–0.91; p = 0.034) was depleted (Figure 1C). Absence of the KIR2DL2 gene was associated with a lower risk for the development of MPN with CALR mutation (OR: 0.47; 95% CI: 0.27–0.84; p = 0.01) (Figure 1C).

Direct comparison between mutation groups demonstrated divergent KIR allele distributions, consistent with mutation‐specific innate immune selection. KIR3DL1*00101 (OR: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.15–0.87; p = 0.023) was less frequent in CALR‐mutated cases, while KIR3DL3*00207 (OR: 4.26; 95% CI: 1.05–17.21; p = 0.042), KIR3DL3*00101 (OR: 2.31; 95% CI: 1.20–4.44; p = 0.012), and KIR2DL1*03701 (OR: 7.35; 95% CI: 1.14–47.49; p = 0.036) were less frequent in JAK2 V617F+ cases (Figure 1D). The corresponding group‐level KIR gene‐content frequencies, including KIR2DL2 presence and absence, are provided in Table S2.

Because individual KIR associations are difficult to interpret in isolation, we next examined biologically defined HLA–KIR receptor–ligand combinations. The most consistent finding was that the presence of inhibitory KIR2DL2 together with HLA‐C2 ligands was associated with increased susceptibility to MPN. This interaction was observed in the overall MPN cohort (OR 3.68, 95% CI: 1.41–10.57; p = 0.01), in JAK2 V617F‐positive disease (OR 2.30, 95% CI: 0.88–6.42; p = 0.048), and most strongly in CALR‐mutated disease (OR 7.91, 95% CI: 2.32–29.66; p = 0.001) (Figure 2A–D). The corresponding HLA‐C2 carrier frequencies are shown in Table S3. These results suggest that inherited inhibitory NK‐cell signaling may raise the threshold for elimination of emerging mutant clones and thereby facilitate early immune escape. This interpretation is consistent with our previous observations that HLA‐C*17:01 and HLA‐C*15:02 alleles, both belonging to the HLA‐C2 group, are associated with susceptibility to JAK2 V617F‐positive and CALR‐mutated MPN, respectively [2, 3, 4].

Somatic driver mutations arising in hematopoietic stem cells generate mutant clones that may be recognized through at least two complementary immunogenetic pathways: HLA Class I‐mediated presentation of mutation‐derived neoantigens to CD8+ T cells, and HLA‐dependent regulation of NK‐cell responsiveness through inhibitory KIR receptors. Germline variation across these systems may therefore influence mutation‐specific clonal selection and susceptibility to MPN.

Taken together, these data extend prior evidence for adaptive immune restriction in MPN and support a model in which inherited variation in both HLA‐mediated antigen presentation and NK‐cell inhibitory signaling contributes to mutation‐specific immune editing (Figure 2E). In this framework, mutant clones may be selected not only according to their capacity to evade cytotoxic T‐cell recognition, but also according to the inhibitory tone imposed by host HLA–KIR combinations. The recurrent protective association of the absence of KIR2DL2 in both mutation groups is compatible with this concept and suggests that reduced inhibitory input may favor more efficient NK‐cell‐mediated clearance of early malignant progenitors.

From a translational perspective, these findings have direct implications for the development of personalized immunotherapeutic strategies. Mutation‐specific neoantigens derived from JAK2 V617F and mutant calreticulin represent promising targets for therapeutic vaccination, and early‐phase clinical trials are currently exploring the feasibility of neoantigen‐based approaches in MPNs [5, 6, 7]. However, variability in clinical responses remains a major challenge. Our results suggest that this heterogeneity may be partly explained by host immunogenetic factors, including both HLA‐mediated antigen presentation and NK cell‐mediated immune surveillance.

Importantly, NK cells are increasingly recognized as critical regulators of vaccine efficacy. Beyond their direct cytotoxic activity, NK cells influence dendritic cell maturation, antigen presentation, and the magnitude and durability of adaptive immune responses [8]. Therefore, inhibitory KIR–HLA interactions may affect not only tumor immune escape but also the effectiveness of neoantigen vaccination. Individuals harboring immunogenetic profiles associated with stronger NK inhibition may demonstrate reduced immunogenicity following vaccination, whereas those with more permissive NK signaling may exhibit enhanced vaccine‐induced immune responses [8, 9].

This study has several limitations. The CALR‐mutated subgroup was modest in size; several associations involved rare alleles and wide confidence intervals; and the findings require replication in independent and ethnically diverse populations. The analyses should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, particularly for low‐frequency alleles and subgroup comparisons. Functional studies will also be needed to determine how the identified HLA–KIR combinations influence NK‐cell education, activation, and cytotoxicity in the context of MPN‐associated driver mutations and may provide insights in the precise mechanisms of evasion of the NK‐mediated immunosurveillance [9, 10].

Despite these limitations, high‐resolution KIR genotyping identified novel immunogenetic associations in Philadelphia chromosome‐negative MPN and highlighted inhibitory HLA‐C/KIR2DL2 interactions as candidate modifiers of disease susceptibility. These findings support a role for NK‐cell regulatory variation in early MPN pathogenesis and provide an innate immune complement to previously described HLA‐restricted adaptive immune editing.
