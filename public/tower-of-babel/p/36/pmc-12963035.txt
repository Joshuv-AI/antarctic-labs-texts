Commercial and Corporate Provision of Healthcare, Capitalism and the State
Authors: Nick J Fox
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis paper explores the role of the capitalist state in promoting the commercial and corporate provision of healthcare (CCPH). First, it reviews the breadth of CCPH in contemporary liberal democracies and identifies its strengths and weaknesses for the delivery of public health. It then explores the part that the capitalist state plays in supporting and extending the private provision of healthcare in the era of globalising capitalism. This is achieved through a secondary analysis of data from a recent study of the United Kingdom state's efforts to enhance overseas trade in private healthcare. This paper concludes with a discussion of the privatisation and commodification of healthcare, the role of the liberal democratic state in promoting both public and private healthcare and what this means for health inequalities and social justice, both within developed countries and globally.Keywords: capitalism, commercial provision of healthcare, neoliberalism, private healthcare, state

Introduction

Healthcare is increasingly embedded within market‐driven systems, in which private providers, pharmaceutical and medical device companies and insurance firms play a pivotal role (Ferreira and Mendes 2018; Hermann 2010, 125). An earlier paper (Fox 2024) considered how the capitalist mode of production and exchange commercialises a range of commodities linked to negative health outcomes, including tobacco, alcohol and ultra‐processed foods. This paper considers the obverse side of commerce and capitalism. It uses the term ‘commercial and corporate provision of healthcare’ (CCPH) to encompass the contribution of private enterprises to individual and population health in developed nation liberal democracies and their export to jurisdictions in the Global South. 1 , 2

As an economic mode, capitalism is founded on principles of efficiency and growth within a free market and the accumulation of capital (Freeman 1998; Smith 2010). However, healthcare does not always sit comfortably within such a framework when unpredictable demand, the complex delivery of healthcare and the values of equity, solidarity and universality in healthcare conflict with the profit‐driven logic of capitalism and commerce (Kennedy 2015; Mackintosh and Koivusalo 2005). Key to this is the part (if any) that the capitalist state performs to encourage a flourishing commercial healthcare sector—both at home and in developing nation markets (Dickinson et al. 2023; Hees and da Rocha Paranhos 2018). To address this, the paper asks the following question: What can a capitalist state do … about private healthcare? It also asks the empirical question: What is the evidence that a state assists commercial providers of healthcare to grow their businesses?

To answer these questions, this paper requires a theoretical framing capable of detailed analysis of the dynamic between capitalist enterprise and states. To that end—after a brief overview of the extent of CCPH—an early section reviews a range of theoretical perspectives on capitalism and the state. It settles on a relational ontology that focuses on what a capitalist state can do, rather than what it is. This paper then sets out a methodology cognate with that theory before secondary analysis of a qualitative dataset reporting how agencies and organisations within the United Kingdom state facilitated the export of private health services to India and China between 2010 and 2023 (Hunter et al. 2023). 3 After the data analysis, the concluding section considers what this study can add to sociological knowledge of the relationship between capitalism and the state in a contemporary liberal democracy; what this means for understanding how capitalism is shaping healthcare globally; and the implications for individual and population health in both the Global North and the Global South.

Overview of CCPH and Its Benefits/Shortcomings

In most developed nations, some kind of taxation‐ or health insurance‐funded public health service provides essential and emergency health services and, in much of Europe, comprehensive healthcare (Montagu 2021; Reibling et al. 2019). The United States remains the outlier among developed jurisdictions in lacking a comprehensive public healthcare system (Murray 2013; Navarro 1989).

Alongside public healthcare, many healthcare products and services are provided commercially, either directly to private consumers or as resources for public healthcare providers, such as medicines and equipment (Torchia et al. 2015). CCPH is an umbrella term for this skein of capitalist enterprises, including the industrial production of pharmaceuticals and medical devices; rents for public sector use of hospitals; clinics constructed under public–private partnerships; private clinical contractors and complementary therapists.

These activities are predicated on three features of capitalist enterprise: competition in an open market, the pursuit of profit and the accumulation of capital (Fox 2023b; Christophers 2021). McKee and Stuckler (2012: 238) described this aspect of the contemporary entrepreneurial and neoliberal landscape as the ‘medical‐industrial complex’. Globally, the value of CCPH in 2023 was estimated at $6.9 trillion (Research and Markets 2025). The following subsections summarise the diverse elements that comprise CCPH.

Pharmaceutical and Medical Device Industries

By far the largest CCPH player is the pharmaceutical sector, with an estimated global turnover in 2023 of US$1.6 trillion (Mikulic 2024). There is also a growing market in vitamins, minerals, herbal products and other dietary supplements marketed directly to consumers in high‐street outlets and online (Djaoudene et al. 2023). The medical device sector supplies a vast range of equipment, ranging from computerised axial tomography (CAT) scanners to hospital beds, from wheelchairs to surgical instruments, and from joint prostheses to hearing aids (Fox 2017), with a global market valued at US$518 billion in 2023 (Fortune Business Insights 2024). Near‐body and digital health devices include implantable body parameter monitors and heart pacemakers, as well as a growing market in self‐tracking devices (Fox 2017; Lupton 2016; Till 2014).

Private Healthcare Facilities

Private hospitals and clinics offer alternative care to public facilities, including elective treatments such as cosmetic surgery, fertility treatments and private maternity care. Diagnostic services such as MRI and CT scans, blood tests and genetic testing are offered by private laboratories and companies, whereas private nursing homes, assisted living facilities and home care services are available to elderly individuals or those with chronic conditions. Private providers offer opportunities for faster access and a higher‐quality care experience than in public hospitals (Allan et al. 2021; Mackintosh et al. 2016; Montagu 2021).

Private Health Professions and Contractors

Physicians, surgeons and a range of paramedical health professionals offer private consultations and treatments to inpatients and outpatients, either in their own clinics, in rented consultation rooms in private hospitals or clinics, or online. Private dental and optical services are available from individual or group practices and from high‐street businesses, while private general practitioners supply a means to access primary care (Salisbury et al. 2020; Waring and Bishop 2011; Westgarth 2022, 15). Other private healthcare contractors include alternative and complementary health providers, fitness centres and personal trainers (World Health Organization 2019).

Health Insurance

In many jurisdictions, individuals or employers purchase private health insurance plans to cover the costs of private consultations and treatment as outpatients or inpatients (Thomson et al. 2020) and to cover the costs of private dental and optical services and physiotherapy (Exley et al. 2012; Paccagnella et al. 2013). The total market value for the United Kingdom health cover market in 2024 was estimated at £7.59 billion (Read 2025).

Benefits and Shortcomings of CCPH

Studies suggest that private providers can be more efficient due to competition, leading to better resource allocation and innovation. Private providers can be more agile, especially in the adoption of new technologies and in responding to consumer need/demand, and thereby reducing waiting times (Acuna et al. 2022; Colombo and Tapay 2004).

A significant body of research has addressed inequities and inequalities linked to CCPH (Balarajan et al. 2011; Dickman et al. 2017; Mackintosh et al. 2016, 600–601; Morgan et al. 2016, 607). Market‐driven healthcare systems tend to exacerbate social inequalities by creating two‐tiered systems, where those who can afford private care receive faster, higher‐quality treatment, whereas the less affluent must rely on underfunded or overstretched public services. Although access to private care may be quicker via a private healthcare route, the costs may deter many individuals from seeking treatment, leading to delayed interventions in cases of severe mental illness (Punton et al. 2022).

Studies suggest that public systems are better suited for promoting population‐level interventions such as vaccination campaigns, smoking cessation and healthy lifestyle promotion (Basu et al. 2012, 2; Colombo and Tapay 2004, 33; Ozer et al. 2012). By contrast, for‐profit private providers often specialise in elective procedures such as cosmetic surgery and fertility treatment (Colombo and Tapay 2004, 15), leaving complex, costly treatments for chronic diseases and emergency care to public systems, posing questions of equity.

The CCPH industry has been criticised for prioritising profit over patient well‐being, particularly in systems where healthcare is considered by capitalist states as a commodity rather than a public good (Christiansen 2017; Hodgson et al. 2022; Kennedy 2015, 215; McKee and Stuckler 2012). Research has highlighted the need for effective regulation to protect patients' interests and ensure the quality of care (Basu et al. 2012; Sekhri and Savedoff 2006), which can conflict with the underpinning profit motive of commercial health enterprises and the need to provide a return for their shareholders (Baru 2013; Hjelmar et al. 2018). There may be less transparency in reporting outcomes in CCPH compared with public services that are often required to report key performance indicators (KPIs), undergo public audits and assure value for money (Allan et al. 2021, 140; Anderson et al. 2020).

This short review has documented research on the breadth of CCPH and its benefits and shortcomings. One area that has been relatively underexplored, however, has been the role of the state in encouraging private healthcare products and services.

Theorising the Capitalist State: From Essential Attributes to Relational Capacities

Sociological theorists including Durkheim, Weber, Marx, Parsons, Elias and Bourdieu all addressed ‘the state’ from their differing perspectives, and it has remained a core topic within contemporary political sociology (Drake 2010; Nash 2010). This brief overview contrasts essentialist perspectives (what a state is) that focus on attributes that are considered ontologically inherent and fixed, with relational approaches that explore a state's capacities (what it can do). Capacities are necessarily relational, dependent upon the other human or nonhuman entities with which a state is engaging.

Essentialist Approaches

Approaches that aim to describe what a state is may be categorised into pluralist, élite and class perspectives (Faulks 1999, 33–47). In the former, the state is seen as an expansive entity within civil society, tasked with a multitude of roles including tax collection, the maintenance of legal and bureaucratic systems, information processing, the regulation of economic and social activities, the assurance of national security and the provision of welfare services (Dunleavy 2014, 12–14).

Elite theories assert variously that state power and authority are concentrated in the hands of a socially exclusive minority, such as graduates from prestigious educational institutions, entrenched political parties or even dynastic families, thereby marginalising the remainder of society (Pakulski 2012; Rutland 2018, 284–285). In Bourdieu’s (2018, 3–4) analysis, different groups within society—from political parties to economic interest groups—vie for influence over state policies, seeking to secure control and achieve social stability.

Class theories, by contrast, regard the state as a foundationally biased social institution. Marx and Engels (2017) described the capitalist state as ‘a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie’. In this Marxian view, the capitalist state is ‘essentially coercive’ and an ‘instrument of class oppression’ (Sanderson 1963, 947) that uses its legal and economic structures to ensure production continuity and restrict workers' power. Elsewhere, Marx and Engels suggested that the state was relatively independent of society (Sanderson 1963, 951), an argument later echoed in the claim by Poulantzas (1978 [1968], 346) that the state sustained an arm's‐length relation to the entrepreneurial class, allowing it to mediate more effectively in its oversight of the capitalist economy. This suggestion of independence was challenged by Miliband (1969), who noted that the judiciary, military officer class and civil service are largely populated by ruling‐class élites. More recently, the neo‐Marxist Alami (2021, 163) identified a shift towards more interventionist state policies in Global North nations, with increased involvement in global networks of production, trade, finance, infrastructure and corporate ownership—a tendency he described as ‘state capitalism’.

Relational Approaches

Side‐stepping the problem of defining what a state is, relational analyses focus instead on a state's capacities: an exercise open to empirical inquiry and testing. This subsection explores three relational approaches to the state's capacities.

Foucault's understanding of the state was based in a perspective on power and resistance as productive of bodies and bottom‐up, rather than coercive and top‐down (Foucault 1981, 92–93). This perspective emphasised networks of power that operate at a micro level within institutions such as prisons, schools, hospitals and religious institutions (Rose and Miller 2010). The state, in this formulation, was a relational crystallisation of power relations rather than their essentialist origin (Jessop 2007, 36; Sawyer 2015, 136). For Foucault, the study of power at the level of population shifted from a focus on the state‐as‐institution to biopolitics and governmentality: that is, the rationalities and techniques that shape individual bodily capacities and conduct (Rose and Miller 2010).

Agamben drew upon Foucault's conception of biopower (Agamben 2015, 4) but introduced a further understanding: that the power of the contemporary biopolitical state rests on its sovereignty—its capacity to suspend the law in the name of preserving it. Examples include emergency decrees, counterterrorism measures and, most notably, detention camps (Erlenbusch 2013, 48). As in Foucault, the target of state action and oppression is the body, but for Agamben, the primary aim of the contemporary state is societal security (Agamben 2015, 5). Arguably, however, Agamben's ontology of the state struggles to explain the diverse forms in which contemporary states manifest (Drake 2010, 71; Erlenbusch 2013, 50–51).

A resolution to this latter challenge was offered in the relational ontology of the capitalist state developed in the work of Deleuze and Guattari (1984, 1988). Their analysis began from an entirely different place: the changing relation between the state and human and nonhuman matter in different economic formations, and how this shift established the conditions for the emergence of the capitalist mode of production (Deleuze and Guattari 1988, 455). They reconceptualised capitalism as an unintended by‐product of millennia of conflict and conquest. Waves of war, invasion, colonisation and enslavement incrementally re‐coded indigenous notions of ‘territory’ into commodifiable ‘land’ and human ‘activity’ into measurable ‘work’, while replacing barter with money, thereby facilitating abstract exchange (Adkins 2015, 221) and consequently industrial entrepreneurialism. These newly freed markets in commodities, labour and capital are progressively infiltrating every corner of the globe and most aspects of human social life (Guattari and Negri 2010 [1985]; Massumi 1992). 4

Deleuze and Guattari described contemporary capitalism as ‘an international ecumenical’ economic formation (Deleuze and Guattari 1988, 435). Unlike previous social and economic formations that required an oppressive state apparatus that depended on coercion and social control, they (1988, 453) argued that the capitalist state has now become little more than an appendage of the market. Its principal responsibility is to assure and enable the free flow of commodities, labour and money that underpins capital accumulation. This analysis also allows for the diverse formations of capitalism in the contemporary world, ranging from the ‘pure’ free market advocated by classical and neoliberal economists such as Friedman and Hayek through Keynesian interventionism to the planned economy of China and indeed the engaged welfare capitalisms that Alami (2021) describes as ‘state capitalisms’.

Recent research by the current Fox (2023a, 2023b) has built on this relational ontology of capitalism and the state to identify more precisely the relational dynamic by which free flows of capital, commodities and labour enable the accumulation of capital. In addition to the interactions between capital, commodities and labour in production and exchange described by Marx (1906 [1906]) in Capital, this new analysis identified a further interaction between the capacities of a commodity or service and the need for such capacities by a potential customer. It is this interaction—described in classical economics as ‘supply and demand’ (Moore 1925)—that is the mainspring that enables the accumulation of capital. Significantly, producers have no control over demand for a commodity's capacities, whereas customers have no control over which products are offered in a market and at what price (Fox 2023b; see also Roffé 2015, 150). Consequently, capitalism depends on this unconstrained interaction between the supply of capacities and the demand for them. With this added understanding, it follows that the primary focus of the capitalist state apparatus is to ensure that markets operate according to this supply and demand dynamic (Fox 2023a, 141–142), without ‘distortions’ such as subsidies, tariffs or restrictive labour practices (Lang 2019, 694–695).

However, this primary aim does not preclude the capitalist state from also performing other civil society duties. These include the provision or subsidy of health and social care, education, environmental protection, arts and culture, as well as regulating markets and providing consumer protection. Arguably, many of these ‘additional’ duties also serve indirectly the core purpose of assuring and enhancing the free flows of commodities, labour and capital that underpin the untrammelled dynamic of supply and demand (Fox 2023b). For example, one component of this core task entails ensuring the supply of a workforce that is healthy and consequently efficient, innovative and resilient, thereby contributing to the overall growth and prosperity of businesses. To this end, a capitalist state can play an active role in supporting and improving workers' health via public health services. Measures include maintaining public health infrastructure such as access to clean water and a sewage system; promoting vaccination programmes and disease prevention campaigns; enforcing standards for health and safety at work and in the home; assuring safety standards for products such as food, medicines and household appliances; and facilitating affordable healthcare. Alongside these public services, as the following case study will demonstrate, a capitalist state may also seek to extend the commercial and corporate provision of healthcare, with the added benefit (from a capitalist perspective) of enabling the generation of profit and capital accumulation.

The Study: What Can a Capitalist State Do … About Healthcare?

To explore further how a contemporary capitalist state develops and consolidates the commercial and corporate provision of healthcare, this research draws on data from a recent ESRC‐funded study conducted by a team at King's College London and Jawaharlal Nehru University, India (see Note 3 for full information on the original study and investigators). This study examined efforts by public and private organisations to position the United Kingdom within the global commercial healthcare economy, focusing on England and two emerging markets, India and China. The context for this study was the growth of international healthcare markets, the departure of the United Kingdom from the European Union (EU), a right‐of‐centre reforming government and private healthcare expansion and fee‐based services in potential overseas markets such as China and India.

For this paper, those data pertaining to the part played by nation‐states in enabling the commercialisation of United Kingdom healthcare have been re‐analysed by the current author using the analytic methodology outlined below to answer the research questions set out in the introduction: What can a capitalist state do about private healthcare? What is the evidence that a state assists commercial providers of healthcare to grow their businesses?

Data Collection

Interviews were conducted with respondents from public and private organisations in the United Kingdom, India and China by one or two members of the original research team. These organisations included NHS trusts, Royal Colleges and arm's‐length bodies in the United Kingdom, as well as senior managers and clinicians from public and private hospitals in India and China. Additionally, input was gathered from government officials, industry associations, journalists and cross‐border recruitment and training agencies.

Respondents were informed about their rights to anonymity and confidentiality and gave oral consent for participation. Because of commercial sensitivity, interviews were not audio‐recorded but instead written up from detailed notes taken during and immediately after the conversations. These interviews were summarised in 19 reports of conversations describing the activities of specific organisations and 144 reports of respondents' experiences and views on relevant activities and issues. Fully anonymised versions of all these documents were subsequently lodged with the UK Data Service (https://reshare.ukdataservice.ac.uk/856673/) for noncommercial research use, from where they were accessed for the secondary data analysis reported here.

Secondary Data Analysis Methodology and Methods

From the original dataset, a total of 23 transcripts based on interviews with present or past staff in UK government departments, consulates, arm's‐length and nongovernmental organisations and overseas healthcare organisations active in collaborations with UK providers were included in the analysis.

Quotations offered in the following ‘findings’ section are from summaries made by the original researchers and are not verbatim extracts from interviews. As a consequence, and contrary to the premise in humanist research that interview data have epistemological primacy and privilege, in this analysis, respondents have been treated as ‘key informants’ (KIs) (Fox and Alldred 2022), providing factual information on events. This information has been treated as data to be interpreted by the current author via the theoretical framing provided in the previous section. Wherever possible, summary data have been triangulated with other documentary data on those events. Respondent identifiers are those used by the original researchers but prefixed with ‘KI’.

All documents were entered into the qualitative data analysis package NVivo 14 for data extraction. To complement the earlier discussion of the Deleuzo–Guattarian perspective on the capacities of the capitalist state, the data were analysed using a coding frame based on Deleuze's (1988, 123–126) ‘ethological’ framing. 4 Ethology examines the affects (capacities for affecting and being affected) that emerge relationally between human and nonhuman entities (Deleuze 1988, 124). Assemblages are dynamic configurations of bodies and things shaped by flows of affect (Deleuze and Guattari 1988, 88), emerging unpredictably around actions and events and producing new capacities and constraints. For example, the healthcare marketplace can be analysed as an assemblage of corporate actors, medical devices, marketing practices and consumer behaviours, each influencing the capacities of others. Micropolitics, as described by Deleuze and Guattari (1988, 213), examines the flows of power and resistance within these assemblages. This approach does not preclude the analysis of large‐scale phenomena such as capitalism or neoliberalism but reframes them as emergent dynamics of the complex, interconnected interactions and affects that constitute these phenomena. In this way, the commercial provision of healthcare can be explored as an emergent outcome of affective interactions between corporate strategies, state policies and consumer practices (most notably affects associated with supply and demand).

Findings

This section first maps the human and nonhuman materialities in the ‘healthcare commercialisation assemblage’ and then goes on to identify the affects that assemble these materialities. It reports the enabling and constraining capacities that these affects produce and the consequent micropolitics of this commercialisation assemblage.

Affects in the Commercialisation Assemblage

The primary affect in this assemblage links the supply of health‐related commodities (knowledge, technologies, skills) with the demand for the capacities of these commodities to deliver desired outcomes. This reciprocity was revealed in the summary of an interview with a UK government official working in India.

The healthcare and life sciences team organises marketing activities for UK companies in India, for example, trade missions. Twice a year they organise trade visits for UK companies to India … where these companies pitch to big Indian companies. The UK‐based team recruits the supply side, while the India team recruits the demand side and manages arrangements once the UK delegation arrives in India. … at the moment there is much interest in buzzwords like ‘smart technologies’ and ‘[artificial] intelligence’.

(KI201)

An informant from the China‐Britain Business Council (a UK business network promoting trade and investment with China) described the demand for UK health technologies, as this summary discloses.

Pharmaceuticals and medical technologies are areas where the UK is prominent globally and where there is a lot of demand from Chinese companies to licence those products for use in China. Although China has moved up the value chain, they are not producing the most advanced technologies in some sectors and so they want to work with international companies to develop these.

(China‐Britain Business Council staff member)

Interview summaries also revealed the central role in the execution of this affect played by the United Kingdom government initiative Healthcare UK, established in 2013 by NHS England and the government Departments of Health and Social Care (DHSC) and Business and Trade (DBT). Healthcare UK's overarching goal is to increase the United Kingdom's share of exports in healthcare services (Healthcare UK website). This is achieved by assisting UK healthcare providers to expand internationally by connecting them with overseas organisations, helping them secure contracts and encouraging collaboration for major projects.

Healthcare UK was typified by informants in interview summaries as a ‘matchmaker’ (KI007) or ‘dating agency’ (KI006) that attempts to source new UK suppliers to export through a network of contacts whom they meet at conferences and exhibitions, matching these with potential customers through staff in embassies and government commissions and other contacts in over 100 overseas countries (Healthcare UK website). One interview summary gave a sense of the work entailed in this matchmaking.

The process for organising a trade mission begins with an analysis of commercial value—are there enough potential customers to do deals with? It is interesting to meet people from government, thinktanks and universities, but they usually do not lead to deals. … Healthcare UK has organised two oncology trade missions to China in the past. Once they have identified sufficient potential customers they test the supply market to see if there are enough potential suppliers from the UK to go on the trade mission, and then they put out a call for people to sign up. They advertise via flyers and websites and ‘usually get good sign‐up’. Then they organise logistics, including venues, speakers and audio‐visual equipment for participants to use.

(KI007)

Underpinning this supply and demand affect is a clear drive to increase trade and thereby revenue to the UK government. In 2019, Healthcare UK established its Export Collaborative, with the explicit aim to ‘markedly increase the benefit the NHS receives from overseas commercial work by bringing together multiple UK organisations, validating overseas opportunities, brokering deals and potentially acting as a prime contracting body where needed’ (Healthcare UK Annual Review 2019/2020). Meanwhile, Healthcare UK's Export Catalyst offered training, mentoring and advice to NHS organisations and private companies to ‘increase their international offer’ (KI007). The summary of one informant's responses suggested how the tools of diplomacy were also employed to further the objective of income generation.

Although there is a sniffiness from people in the FCDO [Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office] when it comes to trade, diplomats know that their career will be enhanced if they promote trade and it is therefore one of their top priorities. Ambassadors open doors and pull levers to get people from Healthcare UK into events and discussions that would not be possible for lone NHS representatives or businesspeople. This is enhanced further by bringing a government minister on a trade visit as it affords higher profile to the visit. In the case of China, a visit by George Osborne [former UK Chancellor of the Exchequer] was important for promoting the UK as a partner for Chinese contracts.

(KI006)

An informant (KI201) working for the UK High Commission in India described their work as the ‘commercial arm’ of the British consular team, saying (quoted verbatim) that ‘our job is to facilitate introductions’. This informant's primary role was to promote UK exports in India, particularly in healthcare and the life sciences, by facilitating ‘good conversations’ between companies in the United Kingdom and India, with a focus on potential projects that will bring good export value for the United Kingdom. The task of the commercial team, according to this interview summary, was to

… maintain close relationships with the biggest companies in India—the biggest spenders, the cream of the industry who are the team’s top accounts—through strategic relationship management with networks of high‐level contacts in these companies. This entails regular meetings with people and keeping them updated with the latest marketing activities and opportunities for business networking at the High Commission. They love to be associated with the High Commission.

(KI201)

In addition to these complementary affects matching suppliers with overseas customers, the data revealed a competition affect in the commercialisation assemblage that pitted the United Kingdom against other Global North countries similarly seeking trade opportunities with developing nations, according to the interview summary with an informant from the UK government.

The USA is the main international competitor, and France also seems to be active, but it varies from context to context. As an example, in one country in Central Asia, the main competitors have been the USA, Japan and the Asian Development Bank. Some high‐income countries use their aid or development [activities] to give them in‐country presence and to fund projects that lay the tracks for commercial activities down the line.

(KI012)

To counter this competitive edge, interviews with informants working in UK government departments at home and in China suggested that the UK's National Health Service's reputation for quality, efficiency, accessibility and innovation was exploited as a ‘fantastic asset’ (KI007) and ‘second‐to‐none’ in terms of cost‐effectiveness and fairness (KI126) when promoting UK healthcare providers to potential overseas customers. NHS initiatives around sustainability and comprehensive healthcare were specific strengths mentioned by informants.

Enabling and Constraining Capacities

Interviews with informants described ways in which the affects in the commercialisation assemblage supplied capacities that either enabled or constrained the ability of the UK state to deliver on its objectives of trade and the accumulation of capital. One interview summary reported how the worldwide network of UK consular and embassy staff enabled the development of new international trade opportunities.

Embassies in other countries perform an important role for market research on behalf of UK companies. Staff from the trade departments in UK embassies talk with people and have connections through British Chambers of Commerce and with domestic agencies in those countries that aim to promote trade and investment. For example, reporting to Healthcare UK if they hear that a company is thinking of building a new hospital in the country. These leads range from something one minister says to another during a trade delegation, to tenders put in a newspaper, to tittle‐tattle from a cocktail party in Shanghai.

(KI006)

Sometimes, development aid engagements with overseas countries (such as the now defunct Prosperity Fund that sought to promote systemic changes in partner countries) could be a starting point for potential future commercial trade links to be forged, as summaries of interviews with informants from the UK government and an arm's‐length body indicated.

Overseas Development Aid [ODA] is a real opportunity for the UK. This has two main benefits for encouraging exporting. First, it gives NHS organisations experience working in these markets and helps to develop relationships with providers and governments which may turn into commercial relationships. It also creates an environment which is more favourable for British companies, as UK sourced devices, systems and regulations favour UK companies with experience using them.

(KI007)

The summary of an interview with an informant working for the UK government in China described how new trade capacities had been facilitated by embedding branches of the UK FCDO and DBT within British embassies in China.

The DBT China Office … maintains offices in the British consulates in Beijing, Shanghai, Wuhan, Guangzhou and Chongqing. … The Office provides consulting services to British companies and institutions to help them enter the Chinese market and seek profitable development opportunities. At the same time, it helps Chinese investors assess investment opportunities in the UK and identify suitable business development channels. The goal is to connect businesses and institutions in China and the UK to effectively drive business development.

(KI121)

Other interview summaries suggested constraints that the commercialisation assemblage generated. A perceived lack of enthusiasm for overseas commercial collaborations by management and clinical staff within NHS trusts limited the extent of investment.

One of the barriers for working internationally on a commercial basis is that NHS organisations are set up for domestic service provision, not as commercial operators. … NHS organisations are not set up constitutionally and legally, and have tight controls around due diligence which make it impossible to work with organisations in some countries. … Some Trusts have found ways to work internationally, for example Northumbria [the Northumbria International Alliance] has worked through the county council rather than the [NHS] Trust because the former has more administrative flexibility.

(KI033)

Sometimes, despite their best efforts at facilitating the supply side of the assemblage, political or cultural problems on the demand side could scupper deals.

Not all potential projects succeed. An NHS Trust in London visited several hospitals in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou. But the result was not satisfactory because the Trust found the recognition of the British healthcare system and NHS amongst China's public hospital was very low. These famous public hospitals in China know little about NHS and the Trust and have little interest in cooperation. … Hospital leaders tend to recommend overseas hospitals they are familiar with for cooperation and negotiation, and they tend to reject unfamiliar hospitals, which increases the difficulty of cooperation.

(KI121)

Finally, economic and cultural mismatches or misunderstandings between potential suppliers and potential customers could limit trade opportunities. Interview summaries suggested that interest from UK organisations to work in China was constrained by a belief that it was still a communist nation (KI012) and that its healthcare system ‘is closed’ (KI121). KI203 (a respondent working on the India desk of the DBT in the British embassy) also described how Indian companies or collaborators were unenthusiastic about working with UK companies seeking business in India who were not physically present, even if only in a small local office.

Micropolitics of the Commercialisation Assemblage

These enabling and constraining capacities of the capitalist state shaped the flows of money, commodities and labour in this commercialisation assemblage. The processes and bodies established and adapted by the UK state to increase international trade in health achieved several significant collaborations between NHS organisations and overseas governments and private companies, according to interview summaries. In China, these included the Rongqiao International Hospital, a relatively large cooperation project jointly built by the Northumbria International Alliance (a healthcare consultancy selling expertise to international clients). In India, there had been some major investments in healthcare.

Healthcare UK invested a lot of time into promoting the Indo‐Uk Institutes of Health [IUIH] concept in India. It facilitated a meeting in 2015 between Prime Ministers David Cameron and Narendra Modi where they signed an agreement for government‐to‐government support and agreed to develop eleven sites. Nagpur is the first under development: 150 acres with a 1,000‐bed hospital and medical and nursing school, pharmaceutical manufacturing, and hotel and retail facilities. They also have sites in Hyderabad and Amravati, all purchased with £16 million investment from medical equipment firms Stryker and Zimmer.

(KI006)

From the data reported here, these success stories can be attributed to the micropolitical capacities of the state apparatus of policymakers and civil service officers to set and prioritise overarching trade development policy aims and then deploy its financial and human resources and processes to deliver objectives that can achieve these aims. Specific capacities included using the state's worldwide networks to gather and process information; bringing together suppliers and customers; training and mentoring health service and private company staff; cutting across organisational divides (for instance, between diplomats, health department officers and trade specialists); and drawing as required upon the services of high‐profile figures such as senior government ministers or health service leaders to support its efforts towards the overarching policy aim.

However, data analysis also indicated complex flows of power and resistance in the healthcare commercialisation assemblage. During the period covered by the case study, the UK's Department for Business and Trade attempted to establish ‘Education UK’ to perform a similar function to Healthcare UK. This foundered on resistance from the Department for Education and the British Council (a cultural and educational organisation linked to the UK FCDO), who objected to the planned emphasis on trade and overseas investment (KI006). Meanwhile, a policy paper circulated in government circles in 2015 entitled Aid in the National Interest reportedly horrified FCDO staff (according to the summary of an interview with KI028) with its suggestion that the primary objectives of foreign aid should be opening up markets, soft power and global health diplomacy rather than poverty alleviation.

Discussion

The private provision of healthcare and related commodities is set to grow globally to over $11 trillion by 2030, driven by advances in digital health technologies, personalised medicine, precision treatments, convenience, AI and robotics (Research and Markets 2025). The main focus of the paper has been on how this growth in CCPH has been supported and encouraged by capitalist states. Within the Deleuzo–Guattarian perspective on the dynamics of capitalism outlined earlier, free flows of money, commodities and labour are essential for the operation of supply and demand in capitalist markets and the consequent accumulation of capital (Deleuze and Guattari 1988, 456), while the primary objective of a capitalist state is to sustain and enhance these flows (Deleuze 1992, 5; Guattari and Negri 2010, 49). The paper set out to test this hypothesis by exploring how the capacities of the UK state apparatus have adapted in recent years to encourage and enhance healthcare trade and overseas investment.

Analysis of these data revealed that, during the period studied, the UK state was highly proactive towards this objective. Significant governmental reorganisation at the department level and in overseas embassies facilitated efforts to increase trade and private healthcare business, most notably via the establishment of Healthcare UK to coordinate this work. UK government officials, trade bodies and healthcare organisations established strategies to find overseas partners, with government ministers and prime ministers flown out when needed to bolster these efforts. On occasions, this focus subverted other objectives, such as providing aid to developing nations to reduce poverty and build infrastructure: To this end, the Foreign and Commonwealth Office was merged with the Department for International Development in 2020.

As noted earlier, the Deleuzo–Guattarian understanding of capitalism as an ‘ecumenical international’ formation (Deleuze and Guattari 1988, 453) acknowledges that capitalist states take a variety of forms, from a ‘pure’ neoliberal free market to neo‐Keynesian interventionism to state capitalism. In these diverse forms of capitalism, we may expect differing state emphases on private enterprise. The rudimentary provision of public healthcare in the United States reflects the most extreme case among liberal democracies of reliance on the market to deliver healthcare, whereas the data in the study reported in this paper document the efforts by ‘state capitalist’ China to develop private healthcare infrastructure alongside its public systems.

The focus on international enterprise in the field of healthcare reported in this paper came during a period when a right‐of‐centre UK government was in power, with a manifesto commitment to growing the economy, reducing public spending, rolling back business regulation and increasing international trade in the wake of the departure from the European Union (Conservative and Unionist Party 2019, 32–34, 51). However, it is worth noting that the centrist ‘New Labour’ government of Tony Blair and Gordon Brown (1997–2010) was also enthusiastic about corporate involvement in the nation's health, establishing large health infrastructure projects funded via public–private partnerships (PPPs) alongside substantial increases in funding to the NHS. At the time of writing, the UK Labour government of Keir Starmer seems as intent on economic growth and enterprise as previous right‐of‐centre administrations, suggesting there will be no scaling back of efforts to boost trade in healthcare services and products with countries such as China and India.

So, this paper adds weight to the proposition that in liberal democracies, state apparatuses of capitalist countries will support and encourage the commercial and corporate delivery of health services, even while also being responsible for a public health system. Indeed, it explains what may seem a contradiction. Although most developed countries possess a variant on a comprehensive public health system, these jurisdictions are capitalist economies and depend on economic growth (and the accumulation of capital) to generate wealth, provide employment and supply tax income for public spending. This finding refines understanding of what a capitalist state can do in relation to healthcare and suggests that CCPH will continue to grow and be a source of income for both private enterprises and capitalist economies into the future. This analysis of the capitalist state goes some way towards re‐politicising discussions in mainstream economics of markets and welfare service provision (Roffé 2015, 3–4; Swanson 2008, 63–65).

More generally, this paper has supplied a refined theoretical understanding of the state by focusing on capacities: what it can do. It recognises that capitalist economies depend upon free flows of capital, commodities and labour and that the fiscal, legal and regulatory capacities of a state will always be required in such economies to assure and enhance both these material flows and the supply and demand affects that enable the capitalist circuits of production and exchange (Fox 2023b).

At the same time, these states are staffed by human actors (politicians and administrators) with their own agendas and—in a liberal democracy—subject to the whims of an electorate. In such circumstances, capitalist states might complement or even partially compromise their primary objective of capital accumulation to ameliorate some of the unpopular negative consequences of capitalism, including health and wealth inequalities, wastefulness, environmental degradation and the job insecurity that a market economy generates (Fox 2023b). The breadth of contemporary liberal democratic state activities—ranging from national and cyber security to welfare services (Dunleavy 2014, 12–14)—reflects the extent to which capitalist state capacities diversify in developed nations. The volume of these additional capacities will vary according to the cultural, political and ideological commitments of elected governments (for instance, the high‐tax welfare capitalist economies of Nordic states or the libertarian ideology underpinning free‐market capitalism in the USA).

This analysis explains why many liberal democratic states sustain parallel private and public healthcare provision and will do so into the future, with continuing or even growing inequalities contingent upon a divide between those able and those unable to afford access to private health insurance and services. For scholars, these conclusions set a theoretical agenda for research to further explore how private providers of healthcare and governments generate capital and wealth from health and the part capitalist states play in encouraging and facilitating CCPH; how the commercial viability of CCPH is contributing to the privatisation and commodification of healthcare; and how to mitigate the growth of social and health inequities and inequalities consequent upon this trend. For policymakers, healthcare practitioners and patients, it offers an invitation to fully acknowledge the profit motive underpinning private healthcare provision and the ongoing challenges that a market economy and a capitalist state pose for the development and continuity of comprehensive and equitable health services and the public health in both the Global North and the Global South.
