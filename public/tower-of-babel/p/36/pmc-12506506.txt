Exploring the impact of missing data on maternal tobacco program results: an analysis of self-efficacy on smoking cessation
Authors: Mengting Zhao, Benjamin Langworthy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTobacco use is a major contributor to chronic illnesses worldwide, leading to significant morbidity and mortality. Effective tobacco cessation programs are crucial in reducing the health risks associated with smoking. This study aims to investigate the relationship between confidence scores, indicating individuals’ confidence in quitting smoking on a scale of 0 to 10, and carbon monoxide (CO) levels over time among participants in the North Dakota State Department of Health Maternal Tobacco Program.MethodsWe analyzed data from 226 participants, who enrolled in the program between August 2020 and February 2023, using linear mixed-effects models and accounted for missing data using complete case analysis, multiple imputation under a missing at random (MAR) assumption, and multiple imputation under a missing not at random (MNAR) assumption with various delta-adjustment values. Background

Tobacco use is a prevalent practice worldwide, and it is a significant risk factor for chronic illnesses, leading to substantial morbidity and mortality. The World Health Organization reports that tobacco use is the leading preventable cause of death, resulting in over 8 million deaths annually [1]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) also reports that smoking can make it harder for women to conceive and increase their chances of never being able to conceive. Additionally, smoking during pregnancy can result in tissue damage to the fetus, particularly in the brain and lungs. Studies have also suggested a possible connection between maternal smoking and cleft lip [2]. Given the negative health effects associated with tobacco use, tobacco cessation programs are of great importance. Studies have shown that quitting tobacco use can significantly reduce the risk of developing chronic diseases, improve lung function, and enhance overall quality of life [3].

Patient retention in tobacco cessation programs is a pressing concern, as the duration of a patient’s engagement with the program is believed to significantly impact their chances of successfully quitting smoking. Studies have shown that self-efficacy can be a powerful predictor of smoking cessation success. Smokers with higher self-efficacy are often more determined and persistent in their efforts to quit and thus have a higher probability of remaining in the program [4]. In the context of smoking cessation, self-efficacy is defined as smokers’ confidence in their ability to quit smoking after going through all the smoking quit sessions [5]. Recognizing the importance of self-efficacy, many tobacco cessation programs now incorporate self-efficacy assessment questions at the time of registration. These questions help gauge a patient’s level of confidence in their capacity to overcome the challenges of quitting smoking. Consequently, researchers may utilize self-efficacy data to predict the likelihood of smoking cessation. In studies where biochemical verification is used to assess recent smoking behavior, exhaled carbon monoxide (CO) is commonly employed as a non-invasive biomarker of recent tobacco exposure. CO levels of 4 parts per million (ppm) or higher are typically associated with active smoking [6]. While CO is not a direct measure of quitting, it serves as an objective indicator of recent smoking.

By understanding a patient’s self-efficacy levels, healthcare providers can adapt their interventions to provide the necessary support and strategies to bolster the patient’s confidence and increase their chances of a smoke-free life. Therefore, it is imperative to assess the impact of self-efficacy on the smoking cessation outcome to optimize treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes.

Missing data are a common occurrence in tobacco cessation studies, and they can pose a significant challenge for research, resulting in biased estimates, reduced statistical power, and loss of information. In the specific context of this study, where participants engage in multiple sessions over time, it’s common for some individuals to not return for subsequent sessions. As a result, there exists incomplete data for those patients who discontinue their participation in the program. Therefore, addressing missing data is crucial in research.

Numerous approaches have been suggested in the literature to handle missing data, but selecting an appropriate mechanism for handling missing data remains a subject of ongoing debate. To assess the potential impact of missing data on registry results, it is crucial to examine the underlying reasons for data gaps. Missing data are commonly split into three different categories: Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), when the probability of missing data is unrelated to both the observed and unobserved data; Missing at Random (MAR), when the probability of missing data is systematically related to the observed data but not the unobserved data; and Missing Not at Random (MNAR), when the probability of missing data is related to both the observed and unobserved data [7]. One common approach for handling missing data is complete case analysis, which involves deleting cases with missing data and analyzing the remaining data. This approach is often used as the default option in many statistical software packages. However, complete case analysis can only produce unbiased estimates and valid results if the assumption of missing completely at random (MCAR) is satisfied. If the MCAR assumption is not valid, the estimates of the parameters may be biased [8]. To address this issue, alternative strategies can be used. One popular strategy is multiple imputation, which is particularly useful when the MCAR assumption is not valid. With multiple imputation, the missing values are replaced with a set of plausible values, and the resulting datasets are analyzed using standard statistical methods. The results from each dataset are then combined using Rubin’s combination rule to obtain the final inference [9].

Incomplete data are often analyzed under the MAR assumption, even though data may be actually MNAR. In the context of a tobacco cessation program, addressing MNAR is particularly important due to the likelihood of individuals who return to smoking not coming back for follow-up visits. Instead of relying solely on multiple imputation techniques assuming MAR, a sensitivity analysis can be employed to account for MNAR and assess its impact on the study results. One approach is using multiple imputation with delta adjustment, which has been utilized in similar studies. This sensitivity analysis extends multiple imputation under the MAR assumption by varying the delta parameter across a range of values. The delta adjustment parameter represents the assumed difference in the mean of the imputed values for the missing data compared to the observed data. By shifting the imputed values up or down, researchers can explore how conclusions might change under different MNAR scenarios. Ideally, these values are elicited from a subject-matter expert, allowing for an analysis of sensitivity to departures from the MAR assumption [10]. Incorporating such techniques provides a more comprehensive and accurate understanding of the data, mitigating potential bias and enhancing the reliability of the study findings.

This article is structured as follows: Methods section provides an overview of the Maternal Tobacco Program study design and data collection, as well as the statistical model used for the dataset. We also discuss various mechanisms employed to address missing data under different assumptions. Results section presents the analysis results of the Maternal Tobacco Program study using different missing imputation. Finally, concluding remarks and discussions can be found in Discussion section.

Methods

Study design and data collection

The dataset was provided by the North Dakota State Department of Health Maternal Tobacco Program [11]. The primary objective of this program is to decrease the number of health problems caused by tobacco use among pregnant women by reducing smoking during and after pregnancy. To be eligible for the program, pregnant women must currently use tobacco products or have used them at the time of conception. The Maternal Tobacco Program provides one-on-one sessions with a tobacco treatment specialist to help participants quit smoking. Each participant is scheduled to attend four prenatal sessions and six postpartum sessions, each lasting approximately 20–30 minutes. During each session, they are asked to complete a carbon monoxide (CO) breath test, measured in parts per million (ppm), or a saliva test to monitor their tobacco use. Participants who attend counseling cessation sessions receive education and support for quitting tobacco and staying quit. All sessions are conducted in person with a certified facilitator. Participants were asked a series of demographic and tobacco use–related questions during program registration, including insurance type, education level, race, whether smoking occurred in the home, smoking status in the past 30 days, number of previous quit attempts, time to first cigarette after waking, and the agency administering the program. Baseline characteristics

The final dataset used in this study includes a total of 226 participants who enrolled in the program between August 2020 and February 2023, each of whom should have their CO levels measured at each visit if they attend the session. To facilitate the analysis, the dataset was divided into two subsets: Prenatal and Postpartum. The Prenatal data includes observations from the visits before giving birth for each participant, resulting in a total of 904 observations, including 521 that didn’t have CO level measurements and 383 that had CO level measurements. See Table 2 for more detailed information. All prenatal visits occur during the participant’s pregnancy, and there is a minimum interval of one week between each visit. The Postpartum data includes observations from all visits after giving birth for each participant, resulting in a total of 1356 observations, including 1219 that didn’t have CO level measurements and 137 that had CO level measurements (see Table 2 for more information). For postpartum visits, if all follow-up data—including CO measures—are missing, it typically indicates that the participant either did not schedule or did not attend the visit. For prenatal visits, there are some cases where a visit date is recorded but the CO measure is missing. In these instances, it is unclear whether the participant declined to provide a CO sample, the provider omitted the measurement (e.g., due to oversight or deeming it unnecessary if the participant had reported quitting), or if the participant left before the CO could be collected. Unfortunately, the dataset does not contain documentation specifying the reason for each missing CO value.

In addition to CO level measurements, each participant was asked at baseline how confident they were that they would be able to quit using tobacco products on a scale of 0 to 10. Other demographic information, such as race and education level was also collected at baseline. These variables provide important contextual information that may impact a participant’s ability to quit smoking. Our primary question of interest is whether the confidence score at baseline predicts how likely a participant is to continue smoking, with their CO levels at follow-up visits used to measure if they are still smoking.

Multiple imputation

Complete case analysis utilizes only the cases in a dataset for which there are no missing values on any of the variables used in analysis [8]. It assumes that missing data is MCAR or MAR, conditional on the covariates in the analysis model [12]. One of the limitations of the complete case analysis method is that it may result in a smaller sample size and reduced statistical power if a large proportion of participants have missing data.

In addition to complete case analysis, we also consider multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). MICE is a popular multiple imputation technique that operates under the assumption that missing data are MAR [9]. Suppose we have observed data , for each subject and visit . is a vector of non-missingness indicators for subject i at visit j, where indicates the value is not missing for ith subject at jth visit and is observed data. MICE involves the following three overarching steps:

Step 1: For each individual i at jth visit where , we impute the missing values M separate times, creating M different imputed “full” datasets.

Step 2: The full data analysis is carried out separately on each of these M different imputed datasets.

Step 3: The results of each of the M analyses are combined to yield a single analysis. In MICE, the estimates and standard errors from each imputed dataset are combined using Rubin’s rules [13].

MAR assumption

In our study, we developed two separate imputation models: one for the prenatal dataset and one for the postpartum dataset. Both models were constructed under the MAR assumption and were based on a linear mixed-effect model. The model included all demographic variables as predictors along with the confidence score, number of days from the first prenatal visit, and CO level measurements as the response variable. We used the mice package fin R to create 5 imputed datasets for each imputation model. In addition, we investigated the effect of confidence scores on CO levels by including interaction terms of time of visit and confidence score in the outcome model for each imputed dataset. Results

Exploratory analysis

Table 1 presents an overview of the demographic and baseline characteristics of the study population, including insurance type, education level, race, smoking in the home, smoking in the past 30 days, number of quit attempts, time to first cigarette after waking, and the agency administering the program. All of these demographic variables were included in the linear mixed effects mean model as well as the imputation model for both the MAR and MNAR delta adjustment methods. Among the 226 participants included in the study, a small proportion (8.8%) reported being uninsured, while the majority (80%) had at least a high school education. Most participants self-identified as white. Of the total participants, 27% reported that smoking or vaping inside their homes is allowed. Over two-thirds of participants (70%) reported using some form of tobacco product in the past 30 days, and 16% reported using tobacco within five minutes of waking up. Additionally, the majority of participants (66%) attended sessions at the Sanford Health tobacco cessation agency. These variables offer insights into the characteristics of the study population and were subsequently incorporated into the analysis as predictors.

Table 2 summarizes the average confidence score for participants at each visit, stratified by whether the CO measurement is missing for each patient. The number of missing outcomes increases as the number of prenatal visits increases, and the confidence score decreases. This shows that participants who did not continue with the sessions had lower confidence scores. The table also reports the average confidence score among those who had the CO level reported. From Table 2, it can be observed that the confidence score increases as the number of prenatal visits increases for those with no missing outcome, while the CO level measure decreases. This is consistent with participants who completed more sessions having more confidence in quitting tobacco and ending up with lower CO levels. Unlike in the prenatal visits, there wasn’t a large increase in confidence score as the number of postpartum visits increased. However, we do see a slight increase in the mean confidence score for those with missing outcomes. There is also a slight decrease in confidence score among those with non-missing CO outcomes for later postpartum visits. Although neither trend is statistically significant, this pattern somewhat challenges the assumption that continued missingness in the postpartum period is primarily due to continued smoking.

It is also important to emphasize that missing not at random (MNAR) is an untestable assumption. As such, Table 2 alone cannot confirm or reject the presence of MNAR, nor can it provide information about the direction of potential bias, even if missingness is related to quit confidence. The MNAR assumption is specifically that missingness is associated with CO levels even after controlling for observed variables such as baseline confidence score.

Among those with no missing CO level measurement values, the average confidence score at each postpartum visit is relatively consistent, and the average CO level measurement is always under 3.

In addition to examining trends over time, Table 2 also highlights meaningful group comparisons. Notably, during the postpartum period, participants with non-missing CO values had higher confidence scores, which aligns with the idea that those more confident in their ability to quit were more likely to return for follow-up, while those who continued smoking may have been more likely to miss visits.

However, in the early prenatal period (Visits 1 and 2), the pattern appears reversed—participants with missing CO values had higher confidence scores than those with non-missing values. This initially seems to contradict the hypothesis that missingness is associated with continued smoking.

One possible explanation is that CO levels were substantially higher at the first visit, suggesting that more smokers attended those initial visits. Many participants dropped out after the first or second prenatal visit, and this early attrition may be driven by different dynamics than later attrition. For example, participants who missed early visits may have been more confident in their ability to quit and less likely to see continued engagement as necessary. And as noted previously the MNAR assumption is specifically that having a missing CO measurement is associated with CO level after controlling for observed variables.

Conversely, those with high CO levels at the first visit may have initially engaged with the program but later dropped out, potentially due to discouragement, lack of progress, or continued smoking. This suggests that the mechanisms driving missingness may differ between early and later visits, and may reflect both behavioral and motivational differences.

Main analysis

Figure 1 shows the effect of a one-unit increase in confidence score on CO level (y-axis) over time in days from the first prenatal visit to each follow-up visit (x-axis), while controlling for all other covariates. All seven models, which apply different assumptions about the missing data mechanism, indicate a negative relationship between confidence score and CO level that weakens over time and gradually approaches zero. This suggests that those with a higher confidence score are more likely to have lower CO levels, although the effect attenuates slightly over time. The models show consistent results in the effect of confidence score on CO level, except for the model using complete case analysis. The complete case analysis model shows greater differences in the effect of confidence score on CO level across time.

Table 2.1 in the supplementary material presents the estimated effect of confidence score on CO level across the number of days from the first prenatal visit, while controlling for all other covariates, using different missing mechanism assumptions. Overall, we observed a negative relationship between confidence score and CO level. This suggests that participants with higher confidence scores for quitting smoking tend to have lower CO levels, which likely reflects reduced recent smoking exposure. For instance, the model under MAR assumption shows that the effect of the confidence score at the first prenatal visit is −0.90 (95% CI: −1.37, −0.42), indicating that an increase in the confidence score is associated with a 0.9ppm decrease in expected CO level. Given the commonly accepted threshold of 4 ppm indicating likely smoking, a 1 ppm increase for every one unit increase in confidence score, which is on a scale from 1-10, is practically meaningful. The model under MNAR assumption with delta adjustment of 2 (expected value increase 2 if they are missing) shows that the effect of confidence score at the first prenatal visit is −1.02 (95% CI: −1.54,−0.5), indicating that an increase in the confidence score is associated with a 1.02ppm decrease in expected CO level.

Notably, the time starts at day 73 since the x-axis represents the number of days from the first prenatal visits to postpartum visits. All models show a positive relationship between the effect of confidence score on CO level and the number of days from baseline visit to postpartum visits, indicating that the effect of confidence score on CO level attenuated over time. We also observed a wider 95% confidence interval, indicating that there is higher variability in the estimated effect for postpartum visits. However, the impact of the confidence score is negligible, approaching zero, as illustrated in the supplementary material table 2.2. Discussion

A major finding of this study is the negative effect of confidence score on CO level, and this effect attenuated as the number of days from the first prenatal visit increased. Comparing various missing data mechanisms reveals that the complete case analysis showed a greater variation in the effect of confidence score on CO level across prenatal visits, as compared to the analysis under MAR and various delta-adjustment values for MNAR. This minimal disparity between MAR and MNAR results implies a low sensitivity of the outcomes to the MNAR assumptions.

One plausible explanation for this phenomenon is the behavioral pattern observed in patients with lower confidence scores. These individuals are likely to continue smoking and also demonstrate a higher likelihood of discontinuing their prenatal visits. The MNAR parameter adjustments appear to have a negligible impact on the study’s findings. This could be attributed to the inclusion of the confidence score as a predictive factor for missingness in the mixed effects model. This inclusion might be mitigating the influence of the MNAR parameter on the overall results.

Overall, our results were relatively consistent across multiple MAR and MNAR assumptions, suggesting that conclusions may be reasonably robust to the specific imputation model used in this dataset. However, this robustness may not generalize to other studies or populations, where missingness mechanisms could have a stronger influence on estimated effects. To address this uncertainty, we recommend that researchers and tobacco cessation programs routinely conduct sensitivity analyses under both MAR and plausible MNAR assumptions. This practice can improve the credibility of findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential impact of missing data.

The analysis using the postpartum dataset yields similar findings. However, it’s important to note that the influence of the confidence score in postpartum visits is relatively minor. None of the p-values indicated statistical significance.

Taken together, these findings underscore the value of including baseline confidence scores when evaluating smoking cessation interventions, especially in the prenatal context. While the association with CO level attenuates over time, early visits may represent a critical window for intervention.

Future studies should consider longitudinal assessments of confidence and explore how fluctuations in self-efficacy relate to engagement and smoking behavior over time. Additionally, incorporating other behavioral markers alongside CO levels may provide a more nuanced understanding of cessation trajectories.

Limitations

The percentage of missing data in the postpartum dataset is over 50%. For instance, in visit 1, 191 (84.5%) of the CO level data were missing in the postpartum dataset. When the proportion of missing data increases, the uncertainty around the imputed values also increases, which can lead to wider confidence intervals and potentially more estimates.

Conclusion

In this study, our primary focus was to examine the association between confidence scores and CO levels among participants enrolled in the North Dakota State Department of Health Maternal Tobacco Program. We also aimed to investigate how this relationship varied across time of visits. A significant concern was the potential for MNAR data, particularly regarding participants with missing CO levels due to not showing up for visits, as it could be associated with a higher likelihood of returning to smoking. To address this concern, we employed various methods to account for missing data, including complete case analysis, multiple imputation under the MAR assumption, and multiple imputation under a general MNAR mechanism using the delta-adjustment technique. These approaches allowed us to assess the potential impact of MNAR and provide more robust and reliable results. By investigating the MNAR assumption, we aimed to mitigate potential bias and enhance the validity of our findings, ensuring a more accurate understanding of the relationship between confidence scores and CO levels in this context.

This study also highlights the significance of assessing participants’ confidence scores during their initial baseline visit. Individuals with higher confidence scores tend to have lower CO levels following in-person sessions, suggesting reduced recent smoking exposure. Moreover, our results were consistent across a range of missing data assumptions, indicating that these findings are robust despite potential biases introduced by missing CO measurements. Consequently, tobacco cessation health centers can consider using baseline confidence scores to estimate participants’ likelihood of reducing smoking behavior. Furthermore, they can implement strategies—such as offering incentives—to support individuals with lower confidence scores during their initial visit, thereby enhancing motivation and promoting sustained engagement in cessation efforts.
