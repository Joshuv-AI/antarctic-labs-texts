Noble metal nanoparticles and their nanoalloys supported on carbon catalyze the chain-walking isomerization of terminal linear alkenes
Authors: Matea Baçic, Belén Lerma-Berlanga, Jordi Ballesteros-Soberanas, Ramón Manzorro, Vlad Martin-Diaconescu, Aarón Martínez-Castelló, Juan Carlos Hernández-Garrido, Judit Oliver-Meseguer, Antonio Leyva-Pérez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The chain-walking reaction of terminal alkenes is the simplest method to obtain internal alkenes. However, research during recent years has focused mainly on homogeneous rather than heterogeneous (solid) catalysts. A systematic study with different noble metal nanoparticles (Ru, Pd, Pt and Au) and their nanoalloys (RuPt and PdAu), supported on carbon, as catalysts for the isomerization reaction of terminal linear-chain alkenes, from 1-butene to 1-decene, was undertaken. The less expensive metal Ru could outperform the remainder of the noble metals (Pd, Pt and Au) in batch and flow systems. RuPt and PdAu nanoalloys were more active than the monometallic catalyst under specific reaction conditions. The reaction was scaled up to 2 kg in the liquid phase.

Introduction

Linear-chain terminal alkenes are among the most inexpensive, highly available organic feedstocks in the market. They are produced by massive processes, including ethylene oligomerization, naphtha cracking or Fischer–Tropsch reactions and, more recently, by more sustainable processes such as polyethylene waste valorization.1 This explains why the “chain-walking” isomerization reaction of terminal alkenes is the method of choice in industry for producing linear internal alkenes, which are widely used in the paper sizing, lubricant, polymer, adhesive, cosmetic and pharmaceutical industries.2 Industry requires solid catalysts for such high-volume isomerization reactions to implement safer, easy-to-scale and monitorable in-flow processes, and also to recover the expensive metal after reaction.3 However, homogeneous rather than heterogeneous catalysts have been mainly reported for the chain-walking isomerization reaction of alkenes in recent years. As an illustrative example, some representative studies published in 2025 have reported soluble catalytic complexes based on Ru aromatic diamine4 pyridine–oxazoline5 and phosphoramidite Pd,6 salicylaldimine and naphthaldimine Rh,7 pincer phosphine oxide Ir,8 iminopyridine Ni,9 Co hydride,10 N-heterocyclic carbene tungsten11 and borenium.12 Despite these advances, development of a solid recyclable catalyst remains challenging. This mismatch between academic and industrially suitable catalysts should be palliated. We intended to do so by performing a multi-metallic study13 with some common metals for alkene isomerization reactions and related processes (Ru, Pd, Pt and Au), in the form of supported nanoparticles (NPs), as solid catalysts for the isomerization reaction of linear-chain terminal alkenes.

The isomerization of alkenes in industry is usually carried out with acid solid catalysts, even though these solids can also promote the breaking and rearrangement of the linear chain to produce alkyl ramifications either on purpose (gasoline production) or in an undesired way (linear isomerization).14 The latter is the reaction of study herein, so we decided to use carbon as a neutral support to avoid catalytic interferences with metal NPs.15–18 In addition, we included two of the most common, stable and well-studied nanoalloys of these noble metals (i.e., RuPt19–21 and PdAu22,23) for the sake of comparison with the monometallic counterparts.24

Results and discussion

Synthesis of solid catalysts

Fig. 1 shows that the supported noble metal catalysts were prepared by impregnation of an aqueous solution of the corresponding soluble metal chloride (RuCl3, H2PtCl4 and HAuCl4; Pd was used as a commercial Pd/C sample) in a high-surface, mesoporous carbon support (Norit™ charcoal), dried at 100 °C in an oven and hydrogenated at 350 °C under a stream of H2/N2 (1 : 10, v/v).25 The amount of metal salt employed was calculated to obtain a final 5 wt% metal content on the solid, after hydrogenation. Samples were named “M/C”, with “M” indicating the metal NP, and the metal wt% was confirmed by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) measurements after disaggregation in an acid mixture (Table S1). The percentage of atoms on the surface for the different NPs was calculated using the average NP diameter determined from microscopy measurements and/or the corresponding powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) measurements after applying the Scherrer equation. For the mathematical calculation, the NPs were approximated as perfect nanocubes (see below). For the sake of comparison, a Ru catalyst without hydrogenation treatment but calcined in an oven (noH2-Ru/C) was also prepared. RuPt26 and PdAu27 nanoalloys were synthesized following the same protocol as their monometallic counterparts. The two metal solutions were added sequentially, in the order indicated by the sample names, with the heavier metal added at twice the wt% of the lighter metal to achieve a 1 : 1 molar ratio.

Synthesis of M/C solid catalysts, and also of the noH2-Ru/C sample (schematic).

Characterization of solid catalysts

Fig. 2 shows characterization of the two Ru-supported solids (i.e., Ru/C and noH2-Ru/C) by high-angle annular dark-field-scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). Both catalysts showed relatively similar average particle size and dispersion, around 1.5 ± 0.5 nm. PXRD confirmed the absence of Ru particles of size >3 nm according to the Scherrer equation (Fig. S1 in the SI). Fig. 2 also shows that while Ru/C shows the typical digital diffraction pattern (DDP) for Ru(0) nanoclusters, noH2-Ru/C showed the DDP for RuOx nanoclusters. Fig. 2 also showed the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns derived from selected regions of high-resolution HAADF-STEM micrographs. Phase attribution was performed by measuring the interplanar distances (d-spacings) from these FFT patterns. Ru/C exhibited lattice fringes characteristic of metallic Ru(0) planes (e.g., the [100] plane at 0.24 nm) whereas selected images of noH2-Ru/C showed d-spacings corresponding to RuOx nanoclusters.

(B) Same analyses for noH2-Ru/C. (C) EDX mapping of a representative Ru/C area, displaying the spatial distribution of Ru (red), C (green), and O (yellow).

The HAADF-STEM study also revealed the porous structure of the support before and after the hydrogenation treatment (Fig. S2), with a good correlation of the histograms at increasing magnifications from (1.1m× to >4.0m×). The particle dispersion was good (Fig. S3 and S4), and the presence of minor amounts of sub-nanometric clusters can be detected in both samples (Fig. S5 and S6).

Fig. 3 shows X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements for Ru/C and noH2-Ru/C samples after being exposed to air. Ru(ii) was the major species on the surface in both cases, with binding energies around 282.0 and 463.5 eV in the Ru 3d5/2 and Ru 3p3/2 XPS areas, respectively (see Fig. S7–S10 for a complete analysis of the different species in each material).28,29 Both samples showed how the Ru was reduced after use, with the reduced Ru species remaining stable. These results make sense considering the easy aerobic oxidation of small Ru NPs on the surface, even after reduction. O 1s XPS results confirmed oxidation on the surface of both supported Ru NPs (Fig. S11), and a Cl 2p signal could not be detected. Thus, we repeated the XPS measurement of the Ru/C sample but this time avoiding any contact with air. Results (Fig. S12) showed the expected lower binding energy for Ru(0), 280.7 and 462.3 eV in Ru 3d5/2 and Ru 3p3/2 XPS areas, respectively. The corresponding O 1s XPS results also confirmed the Ru(0) oxidation state in the Ru/C because the binding energy for the oxygen atom decreased from 532.4 to 531.8 eV (Fig. S13). These results were in good agreement with HAADF-STEM results. The signals around 285 eV could be attributed to C–O bonds or the Ru 3d5/2 signal, which hampered the analyses because the two bands overlapped. The activated carbon support possesses abundant C–O species (carboxylates, alcohols, aldehydes), so it is not easy to assign this signal.

Ru 3d5/2 (A) and Ru 3p3/2 (B) X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of fresh Ru/C (green line) and noH2-Ru/C (red line) after being exposed to air, and the same samples after being used in the gas phase isomerization of 1-decene 1 (see below), showing the Ru 3d5/2 (A) and Ru 3p3/2 areas (purple and blue lines for Ru/C and noH2-Ru/C, respectively).

Fig. 4 shows the X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) at the Ru K-edge and the corresponding Fourier-transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) of the noH2-Ru/C catalyst. The XANES spectrum (left) revealed a white line energy and intensity consistent with a II/III oxidation state, consistent with Ru oxide species, distinct from a Ru(0) metallic reference. The reduced Ru/C rapidly oxidizes the Ru atoms from the surface, and the size of the NPs (around 2–3 nm) was similar to that in the non-reduced sample. Hence, there was not a significant variation in the oxidation state between them according to XANES, only a very slight reduction within the instrument error, and a slightly more intense white line, which could have been due to very slight changes in the Ru coordination sphere. Thus, we also observed the characteristic band for Ru(ii). Loss of the characteristic Ru–Cl signal was also observed for both samples. These results were corroborated by the EXAFS measurement (bottom), which showed a first coordination shell at 1–2 Å, corresponding to Ru–O/C bonds. Overall, these results are in good agreement with HAADF-STEM and XPS results, and one can say that the samples Ru/C and noH2-Ru/C mainly differentiated only in the main oxidation state of Ru (i.e., (0) and (II), respectively).

Normalised X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES, top) at the Ru K-edge and Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS, bottom) spectra for Ru/C and noH2-Ru/C catalysts, compared with metallic Ru, RuCl3 and RuO2 as references.

The other supported metal catalysts were then characterized. The Pd/C (commercial sample), Pt/C and Au/C samples were analyzed by ICP-OES, PXRD, XPS and high-resolution microscopy to reveal that metal NPs in the reduced form were the main species present (Fig. S14–S16). Assessment of the metal atoms on the surface was carried out for all samples for comparison during the catalytic studies and to normalize the different NPs sizes (Fig. S17 and Table S1). Surface area measurements of the activated carbon employed (Norit™ type) were not performed because it typically has an internal surface area of >1000 m2 g−1 and pore volume of >0.5 cm3 g−1, which leads to wide mesopores which in principle, should have an irrelevant influence in the molecular traffic during reaction, including the metal (NP) species and reactants.

The RuPt and PdAu nanoalloys supported on carbon (impregnated in this order) were also characterized in a similar way (Table S1). The RuPt/C sample showed a bigger NP size (≈5 nm) (Fig. S18) than the monometallic samples (≈2 nm), but a similar metallic oxidation state. Formation of an intimate alloy was confirmed by EDX sweeping on HAADF-STEM spectra (where a good coincidence between the Ru and Pt atoms was found) but also by temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) experiments between −100 and 600 °C, where the RuPt/C sample showed an intermediate reduction temperature (150 °C) between Pt/C (−50 °C) and Ru/C (500 °C), with a small peak (450 °C) corresponding to RuO2, that accounted for ∼15% of the total integrated area (Fig. S18, bottom right).14 This result was in line with the partial oxidation on the surface observed for the Ru/C catalyst after exposure to air. The PdAu/C catalyst showed the same NP size as the monometallic counterparts (≈3 nm) but, in contrast to the other nanoalloy (i.e., RuPt/C), the catalyst was composed of separated Pd and Au domains, grouped into ∼20-nm particles, without any clear interaction between both metals (Fig. S19).

With all these metal NPs supported on carbon in hand, we evaluated their catalytic activity towards the chain-walking isomerization reaction of different linear terminal alkenes, in liquid or gas phases, and in batch or flow reaction conditions.

Catalytic results

Isomerization reaction in the liquid phase and in batch, with the Ru/C catalysts

Ru/C and noH2-Ru/C materials were tested for the isomerization reaction of the liquid linear chain terminal alkene 1-decene 1. The reactions were carried out under solvent-free conditions and monitored by gas chromatography (GC) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). These results were further confirmed by 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and attenuated total reflection Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. Fig. 5 shows that both solid catalysts were active for the reaction, but with different reaction profiles. The noH2-Ru/C solid catalyst showed high catalytic activity from the start of the reaction, with a typical Langmuir curve showing induction time, leading to complete formation of the internal decene products 2 within 40 min at 200 °C. Formation of a mixture of different internal alkenes from a terminal alkene is a common practice in industry, and the resulting mixture can be employed directly or further reacted to obtain the final active compound, for instance in paper sizing, adhesives or fragrances.30,31 In contrast to noH2-Ru/C, the Ru/C catalyst showed a clear induction time after which the catalytic activity became similar to the noH2-Ru/C sample. However, in a second use, the catalytic activity of Ru/C and noH2-Ru/C samples was nearly identical, without any induction time and gave product 2 quantitatively in 40 min. The catalytic similarity of the used noH2- and Ru/C samples became more evident in the third, fourth and fifth uses (Fig. S20). Identical results were obtained for the isomerization of 1-octene 3 to the corresponding internal octenes 4, performed at a lower reaction temperature (150 °C) because the boiling point of 3 was lower than that of 1 (Fig. S21). Hot filtration tests showed that the active Ru species stayed on the solid and did not leach into solution during the reaction (Fig. S21, bottom). Indeed, the isomerization of 3 could be scaled-up to 2 kg in an industrial pre-pilot plant, with full conversion and selectivity towards 4, at a reaction temperature of 170 °C and a reaction time of 16 h (Fig. S22). In all cases, mass balances accounted for >99% in the liquid phase. These results suggested that the Ru species originally present on Ru/C were not catalytically active for the isomerization reaction but evolved into the active species during the first few minutes of the reaction. These active Ru species appeared to resemble those supported on the noH2-Ru/C sample.

Kinetic results for the isomerization of liquid 1-decene 1 to the corresponding internal alkenes 2 catalyzed by Ru/C or noH2-Ru/C, fresh (left) and used (right), under the indicated reaction conditions. Error bars account for 5% uncertainty. Results refer to GC. Mass balances are >99%.

Isomerization reaction in liquid phase and in flow, with the Ru/C catalysts

Fig. 6 shows the results for the isomerization reaction of 1 to 2 in continuous mode, in a fixed-bed tubular reactor containing the corresponding Ru/C catalyst. Ru/C catalyzed the reaction after an induction time of 5 min. In contrast, noH2-Ru/C catalyzed the reaction at its maximum rate from the beginning of the experiment, without any detectable induction time. Deactivation occurred in both catalysts, and this deactivation could be alleviated after passing a neat N2 stream (N2 purge) over the used solid catalyst. Despite deactivation continuing with time, both Ru/C catalysts retained stable yields of 2 at ∼50% over a reaction time of 50 h.

Kinetic results for the isomerization of 1 to 2 in the liquid phase, in flow, under the indicated reaction conditions, catalyzed by Ru/C (top) or noH2-Ru/C (bottom). Discontinuous lines indicate stopping of the reaction and N2 purge. Mass balance accounts for >95%.

Fig. 7 reveals the TEM images of the spent Ru/C and noH2-Ru/C solid catalysts. Both of them showed very similar features even at the atomic scale, composed of very small (<2 nm) Ru NPs. Their corresponding particle size distributions were also very similar (Fig. 7, S23 and S24). These results were consistent with the catalytic activity observed for both Ru/C catalysts in both batch and flow reactions after the induction time of fresh Ru/C. The Ru 3d5/2, Ru 3p3/2 and O 1s XPS signals for spent Ru/C and noH2-Ru/C catalysts were at 280.7, 462.5 and 531.5 eV after use (Fig. 3 and S7, respectively), just the same values as for Ru/C not exposed to air. Besides, a new C 1s XPS signal appeared at 288.7 eV (see Fig. 2), compatible with adsorbed alkene compounds rather than carbide species.36,37 The presence of Ru(0) and adsorbed alkenes after the reaction in the liquid phase could explain the rapid deactivation observed for both catalysts, and that the mass balance was just ≈95%. To check this, we performed new experiments at 160–200 °C with 1-octene 3 (liquid), 1-hexene 5 (liquid) and 1-butene 7 (gas) to assess if smaller alkenes, which are more prone to adsorb on the catalytic surface, deactivated earlier the Ru/C catalysts. The results in Fig. 8 (see Fig. S25–S28 and Tables S2, S3) showed that the smaller the starting terminal alkene, the higher the deactivation, in the liquid or gas phase. In all cases, the cis/trans product distribution was nearly identical, ∼2.5 : 1.

Mass balances account for ≈95%.

These results, together, strongly suggested that the Ru catalytic cycle for the isomerization of terminal alkenes with the Ru/C solid catalyst started with Ru(ii) species, which removed the allyl H atoms to move the C–C bond, potentially passing through Ru(0) at some point of the catalytic cycle if an oxidative addition–reductive elimination step occurred.38 Although some single atoms were formed in situ, NPs were the main species throughout the reaction, basically preserving the size and shape, to finish in reduced forms (i.e., Ru(0)). The Ru/C catalysts suffered rapid deactivation in the liquid or gas phase after adsorption of carbonaceous residues, probably aromatics (see below). With these results in hand, we tested the gas-phase reaction at a higher reaction temperature (300 °C).

The reactions were performed in batch autoclaves for 22 h, which enabled precise control over variables such as temperature, pressure, reactant concentration and reaction time, particularly suitable for screening the different catalyst formulations. Simple heating of 3 in the presence of carbon did not give any isomerization product (entry 1), but the solid catalyst noH2-Ru/C demonstrated a good conversion of 88.1% alongside a selectivity for internal octenes 4 of 97.8% (entry 2). These results were very similar at a reaction temperature of 250 and 275 °C. Also, XANES and EXAFS of the recovered noH2Ru–C catalysts after the reaction, at 250 to 300 °C reaction temperatures, showed that the Ru NPs were stable and that the combination of Ru(ii) and Ru(0) was present after the reaction (Fig. S29 and S30).

The Pd/C catalyst exhibited a lower conversion rate (61.5%, entry 3) and good selectivity (98.5%). The average particle size of the Pd NPs was bigger than that of Ru, so the TON for noH2-Ru/C and Pd/C solid catalysts was quite similar. However, this TON was three times higher (1886) for Pt/C, which, undesirably, suffered a decay in selectivity (93.9%, entry 4). Examination of the final reaction mixture by GC-MS and NMR spectroscopy suggested that the byproducts formed with Pt/C corresponded to aromatic compounds, particularly ortho-xylene and ethylbenzene. These compounds can strongly interact with reduced noble metal NPs,39 which explains the deactivation found in the gas phase experiments at a lower reaction temperature (see above). Mechanistically, it has been reported that Pt facilitates the isomerization reaction through the formation of π-complexes with the alkene, stabilizing key intermediates and reducing the activation energy.40 These combined features, common to Ru,41,42 account for the high activity of Pt in the isomerization processes, which, however, is hampered by the tendency to dehydrogenate the internal alkenes and produce aromatics.43–47

At this point, the nanoalloy RuPt/C26 was tested. It showed higher catalytic activity and selectivity than the corresponding monometallic catalysts, with a remarkable TON of 1378 (entry 5). In accordance with this observation, Au/C was much less active in terms of conversion (22.2%, entry 6) but not in terms of TON with respect to Ru or Pd, and the nanoalloyed PdAu/C27 solid catalyst showed a much higher activity than the sum of the individual Pd/C and Au/C materials, with 89.8% conversion, 97.7% selectivity and TON = 1097 (entry 7). The low conversion of Au/C could be ascribed to the limited efficacy to activate alkenes. Indeed, Au(0) is characterized by a fully filled closed-shell electronic configuration that results in weak adsorption of alkenes, requiring sizes <5 nm on reducible metal oxides such as titanium dioxide (TiO2) to exhibit appreciable catalytic activity for the alkene isomerization reaction.48,49 In contrast, π-allyl formation, metal-H insertion followed by β-H elimination, and nucleometallation along with β-elimination of alkenes typically occurs on Pd sites,50–52 which does not effectively facilitate cyclisation, dehydrogenation or aromatization processes.53,54 The PdAu/C catalyst contained half as much Pd as its monometallic counterpart. To confirm that this result arose from the nanoalloyed nature of the carbon-supported catalyst, a new material containing double the amount of Pd and half the amount of Au was prepared. Results for the conversion of 3, yield of 4 and TON (assuming the same NP size) were nearly the same as for the previous PdAu/C catalyst, although with less selectivity (92.4%, entry 8). To further confirm that alloyed NPs and not sub-nanometric species were required to catalyze the reaction, a new sample of PdAu/C with nearly one order of magnitude lower amounts of both Pd and Au (≈0.7 wt%) was prepared. The conversion (57.4%), yield (54.4%), selectivity (94.7%) and TON (assuming complete access of the reactant to metal atoms, 725) were lower than those of the nanoparticulated PdAu/C sample (entry 9). This result suggested that sub-nanometric species, majorly formed when lower amounts of metals were supported, were not the main catalytic species for the isomerization of 3. Au can alter the center of the d-band of Pd, mitigating over-adsorption of alkenes and, simultaneously, weakening its ability to activate hydrogen.48,55 Hence, a balanced synergy that maximizes conversion while controlling product distribution can be achieved in PdAu NPs, suggesting that PdAu/C nanoalloys can be highly effective for the selective linear alkene upgrading.

The same trend was observed for the PdAu/C nanoalloys (which must be remarked it is a segregated alloy with differentiated particles in significant amounts). That is, a similar and lower catalytic activity with samples containing more or lower metal loadings, respectively, was reproduced with the RuPt/C nanoalloys (compare entry 5 and entries 11–12). These results further confirmed the catalytic activity for the isomerization reaction of the metal NPs in the gas phase at 300 °C. This catalytic behaviour of the carbon-supported metal NPs for the isomerization reaction was not limited to 1-octene 3. Indeed, the smaller linear alkene 1-hexene 5 yielded the respective internal olefin mixtures 6 in good yield (75.4%) and excellent selectivity (99.9%) when noH2-Ru/C was employed as the catalyst (entry 13).

Isomerization reaction in the gas phase and in flow, with the supported metal catalysts

The catalytic activity and stability of the metal NPs on carbon for the isomerization reaction in neat gas phase (300 °C) prompted us to test the reaction in flow. An in-house reactor (Fig. S31) allowed bubbling of the liquid alkene 3 to be introduced in the tubular reactor as a gas, at the selected reaction temperature, to maximize the throughput. First, an optimisation study was carried out with the noH2Ru/C catalyst to identify the most effective combination of reaction parameters for the isomerization reaction. We systematically examined the influence of bubbler temperature (65–90 °C), the total flow rate (5–15 mL min−1) and the reactor temperature (250–325 °C), both on conversion of 3 and product selectivity, after 5 h on-stream. Table 2 (entries 1–5, see Fig. S32–S36 for the corresponding kinetic plots) shows the influence of each parameter on the reaction performance, highlighting the pronounced effect of temperature on the isomerization yield. The optimal operating conditions under flow using a catalyst mass of 400 mg were determined to be a bubbler temperature of 65 °C, a total flow rate of 5 mL min−1, and a reactor temperature of 300 °C (entry 4). Under these conditions, not only the noH2Ru/C catalyst but also Pt/C and the bimetallic RuPt/C catalyst, with different metal loadings, were evaluated, even though individual optimization of the reaction parameters for the new catalysts might be necessary. The corresponding results are also summarized in Table 2. Pt/C was, as in the batch phase, more active but less selective than noH2Ru/C, losing activity rapidly with time (entry 6 in Table 2 and Fig. S37). Identical catalytic behavior was observed for the RuPt/C nanoalloys, which, however, improved the stability at lower metal loadings (entries 7–8 and Fig. S38). These results, overall, illustrated the ability of the supported metal NPs to catalyze the isomerization of 1-octene 3 through time in a fixed-bed tubular reactor.

Conclusions

The chain-walking isomerization reaction of different terminal linear alkenes, from 1-butene to 1-decene, was catalyzed by different noble metal NPs (Ru, Pd, Pt and Au) supported on a neutral support such as mesoporous carbon. The reaction proceeded in liquid or gas phases, under batch or in-flow conditions. Pt was the more active metal by atom on the surface, but Ru, Pd and Au were more selective, and Ru presented the better activity/selectivity/price balance among all of them. The catalytic activity and/or selectivity of the monometallic catalyst could be tuned and enhanced with RuPt and PdAu nanoalloys, also supported on carbon. The solid catalysts were easy to prepare, recoverable and reusable, and applicable to a 2-kg reaction in an industrial pre-pilot plant. These results suggest that supported metal NPs are truly heterogeneous catalysts for the chain-walking isomerization reaction of terminal linear alkenes, thereby expanding the catalytic use of noble metal-supported NPs for organic reactions of industrial interest,56 and opening the way to study other metal combinations57 or mesoporous inert solid supports.58 A future challenge is to achieve the selective chain-walking isomerization reaction of a terminal alkene to produce a single internal alkene positional isomer (i.e., exclusively the 2-alkene instead of a mixture) with a solid catalyst, currently efficient only with soluble metal complexes.59,60

Experimental

General

Materials and reactors

All chemicals, including the Ru salts, reagents and solvents, were of reagent grade, purchased from commercial sources and used as received. For high-temperature bath reactions, reinforced glass reactors were dried in an oven at 175 °C before use, and the canals of the microreactor were cleaned with n-hexane before use.

Physical techniques

The metal content of the samples was determined by ICP-OES (ICAP Pro; Thermo Scientific). Solids were disaggregated in aqua regia and later diluted in water and filtered (removal of carbon) before analysis. FT-IR spectroscopy, performed in a JASCO FT/IR-4000 system, was employed to record the IR spectra of the alkene mixture during the reaction (400 to 4000 cm−1) by dropping a small sample of the reaction mixture on an ATR crystal. GC and GC-MS were performed in gas chromatographs with 25-m capillary columns filled with 1 or 5 wt% phenylsilicone GC-2025 (Shimadzu), GC 6890N (Agilent Technologies) coupled with MS-5973 (Agilent Technologies). 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy was done at room temperature on a 400-MHz spectrometer (Ascend 400; Bruker). Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) spectroscopy of the different samples was done in a CubiX PRO (PAN Analytical) spectrometer, with a Cu K(α) radiation source, and a wavelength of 1.5406 Å. XPS of the solids was recorded with a SPECS spectrometer equipped with a Phoibos 150MCD multichannel analyzer using Mg Kα (1253.6 eV) irradiation. Samples were prepared by dropping the solid on the suspension (typically with a water solvent) onto a molybdenum plate, followed by air drying. The spectra were recorded at −175 °C and with an X-ray power of 50 mW to avoid photoreduction of the metal species. The residual pressure in the analytical chamber was maintained at <10−9 mbar during data acquisition. The binding energies were corrected for surface charging by referencing them to the energy of the C 1s peak of adventitious carbon set at 284.5 eV. STEM, in HAADF and analytical EDX modes, was performed in a double aberration-corrected Titan Cubed 60–300 microscope (Thermo Fisher) operating at 200 kV. A condenser aperture of 50 µm and a camera length of 115 mm were used, obtaining an electron probe with a convergence angle of 21.4 mrad. To get a high signal-to-noise ratio, a beam current of 120 pA was used. EDX hypermaps were recorded using the high-efficiency SuperX G2 detection system equipped in the microscope, which integrates four windowless detectors surrounding the sample and high-performance signal-processing hardware. XAS data at the Ru K-edge were taken at the CLAESS beamline of the ALBA synchrotron (Barcelona, Spain). Measurements were carried out at ambient temperature and pressure on pelletized samples in a cellulose matrix employing a Si311 DCM. The energy scale was calibrated to the first inflection point of Ru(0) taken as 22 117 eV. XAS data were processed with the Athena software package. EXAFS was extracted with the AUTOBK algorithm, using an Rbkg of 1 and a spline in the k range of 0–13.8 A−1. Fourier-transformed data are presented on k2-weighted data having a Hanning window (dk = 1) over a k range of 3–12 A−1.

Reaction procedures

Representative procedure for the liquid or gas-phase isomerization reaction in batch

The corresponding alkene (i.e., 1-decene 1, 2 mL, 11 mmol) was placed in an 8-mL vial equipped with a magnetic stirrer. Then, the metal catalyst (i.e., Ru/C or noH2-Ru/C, 0.05 mol%) was added, and the vial was closed with a cap. For batch reactors at >200 °C, double-walled reinforced autoclaves were employed (see General section). The mixture was placed in a steel block at 200 °C under magnetic stirring, during the corresponding time. Aliquots of the reaction mixture were removed to follow the reaction over time, filtered through a 20-µm nylon filter and analyzed by GC and NMR spectroscopy.

2-kg Isomerization reaction

The terminal linear alkene (1.91 kg) and noH2-Ru/C (942 mg) were added to a 15-L glass round-bottomed flask (“baby plant”) equipped with a mechanical stirrer. The system was closed, and the mixture was heated at 170 °C under atmospheric pressure. The required temperature was achieved after 1 h of heating. Then, aliquots were removed from the reaction mixture every 1–2 h through a sampler at the bottom of the reactor to monitor the reaction evolution by FT-IR. A conversion >95% was obtained after a 16 h reaction time.

Representative procedure for the liquid- or gas-phase isomerization reaction in flow

The gas-phase isomerization of terminal alkenes was investigated using a fixed-bed quartz tubular reactor operated under continuous flow. For that, 400 mg of catalyst was placed at the center of the reactor, supported between plugs of glass wool. Prior to each reaction, the system was purged with N2 (20 mL min−1 for 30 min) to remove air and moisture. The reaction temperature was varied between 250 and 325 °C, depending on the test conditions. The liquid terminal alkenes were introduced directly with a pump or via N2 carrier gas saturated in a temperature-controlled bubbler (65–90 °C) to generate a controlled vapour-phase feed. The total gas flow rate was adjusted between 5 and 15 mL min−1 to modulate the contact time and olefin partial pressure. These conditions ensured a steady alkene vapour feed, with the actual molar flow determined by the saturation temperature. Reaction products were collected directly in 2-mL vials or using a cold trap placed downstream, and analyzed offline by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy and GC, to quantify conversion and product selectivity.

Representative procedure for the liquid-phase isomerization of 4-allylanisole and 4-pentenoic acid

The corresponding alkene (11 mmol) was placed in an 8-mL vial equipped with a magnetic stirring bar. Subsequently, the RuPt/C catalyst (0.05 mol%) was added, and the vial was sealed with a cap. The reaction mixture was then placed in a steel block at 300 °C under magnetic stirring for the corresponding reaction time. Aliquots were periodically withdrawn to monitor the reaction progress over time, filtered through a 20-µm nylon filter, and analyzed by GC.
