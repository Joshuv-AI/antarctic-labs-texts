Epioxa: The First FDA-Approved epithelium-on-cross-linking therapy for keratoconus
Authors: Eman Maryam, Aiman Rafique, Bismillah Malik, Iqra Shareef, Wajeeha Batool, Bakhtawar Farooq, Abdul Moiz Khan, Sira Jam Munira
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Keratoconus is a degenerative eye disorder characterized by progressive thinning and anterior bulging, which leads to visual distortion. Corneal collagen cross-linking (CXL) is now considered the standard of care for preventing further disease progression. In addition, patients with corneal opacities or lens intolerance may be treated surgically.

Keratoconus pathogenesis is multifactorial and involves genetic, biochemical, and environmental factors. Corneal homeostasis is disrupted by altered cytokine activity and oxidative stress, which leads to keratocyte apoptosis and stromal thinning. Corneal structure is further weakened by an imbalance in collagen production and extracellular matrix remodeling. Genetic differences, including differences in TGF-β pathways, also contribute to this. All of these processes lead to progressive corneal ectasia and visual distortion[1,2]. Keratoconus is widespread worldwide, with the lowest prevalence of 0.17 per 1000 in the United States and the highest of 40 per 1000 in Iran[3,4].

Signs of keratoconus include progressive visual impairment, photophobia, and increased astigmatism. None of these can be corrected using spectacles with spherocylindrical standard lenses[5]. The major diagnostic clues are scissoring retinoscopic reflex, stromal thinning, and the existence of the Fleischer ring[6]. In acute corneal hydrops, a complication of keratoconus, there is a tear of the endothelium and the Descemet membrane. This results in localized opaque corneal edema that usually heals within a few months[7].

Although several treatment options are available, FDA-approved noninvasive treatments are the safest and most effective for stopping its development and progression (Fig. 1)[8]. Epioxa: a new therapeutic agent in the treatment of keratoconus

Epioxa (riboflavin 5’-phosphate ophthalmic solution) is a first-in-class treatment for progressive keratoconus, an oxygen-enhanced, topical, and cross-linking corneal therapy. Riboflavin is a photosensitizer that is administered to intact corneal epithelium and stimulated with ultraviolet-A (UV-A) in an oxygen-enriched environment. When subjected to UV-A, it produces reactive oxygen species (mainly singlet oxygen), which trigger the development of further cross-links between stromal collagen fibers (covalent in nature). This biochemical activity strengthens, stabilizes, and enhances the biomechanical properties of the corneal stroma, thereby maintaining its shape and preventing or reducing disease progression. The United States was the first nation to provide Epioxa approval, and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved it on October 20, 2025[9]. It started developing in early 2025 with a New Drug Application filing based on two biological pivotal Phase III multicenter, randomized clinical trials on its safety and efficacy, followed by regulatory approval, and a scheduled commercial launch in the first quarter of 2026[9,10].

Two as-yet-unpublished, prospective Phase III randomized, parallel-group, sham/vehicle-controlled, multicenter clinical trials (Study 1 [NCT03442751]; Study 2 [NCT05759559]) were performed to determine the safety and efficacy of EPIOXA epithelium on corneal collagen cross-linking (CXL). They were based on riboflavin 5’-phosphate ophthalmic solution, UV-A irradiation, and supplemental oxygen in patients with progressive keratoconus. All eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 2:1 ratio to either the CXL treatment or sham procedure/vehicle control (sham) group. The baseline and post-treatment eye observations were performed for up to 12 months. The treatment effect was demonstrated by differences in the change from the baseline maximum corneal curvature (Kmax) in diopters between the CXL and sham groups. Both eyes were treated in stages, where the second eye was always treated 1 week to 3 months after the first eye.

In Study 1, 280 eyes of 201 patients were randomized, with 279 eyes receiving the study intervention (189 eyes, CXL; 90 eyes, sham). A statistically significant decrease in Kmax values was observed at 6 months (mean difference, 1.0; 95% CI, 1.5 to 0.4; P < 0.01), which was sustained at 12 months (1.1; 95% CI, 1.6 to 0.6; P < 0.01) (Table 1). Study 2 involved 312 eyes from 208 patients, and participants were randomly assigned to the intervention group (200 eyes, CXL; 112 eyes, Sham). The findings were similar to those of Study 1, and significant improvements in Kmax values were observed at 6 months (0.6; 95% CI, 0.9 to 0.2; P < 0.01) and 12 months (1.0; 95% CI, 1.3 to 0.6; P < 0.01) (Table 1). Measurements of corneal flattening showed that younger patients (<30 years) had greater corneal flattening than older patients (≥30 years)[10].

Taken together, these two trials demonstrated that EPIOXA produces a clinically and statistically significant decrease in Kmax, suggesting that it could be an effective option for preventing or reversing the progression of keratoconus[10]. It is recommended that EPIOXA HD be administered as two drops to the eye every 60 seconds for 4 minutes in Step 1. Then, in step 2, two drops of EPIOXA were applied every 30 seconds for 6 minutes. Before UV-A treatment, the corneal rinsing and thickness were carefully measured. UV-A light treatment was started in a cycle: 1 second off, 1 second on, and continued for up to 11 minutes. Moisturizing drops were applied every 2 minutes and goggles were used throughout the treatment procedures[10,12]. Every patient should be taught the correct instillation technique, and prescribing physicians should monitor compliance to ensure maximum therapeutic benefit. Ophthalmologists should be informed of its therapeutic potential, and treatment principles should be updated to incorporate Epioxa as a clinical option. Eye care professionals require training and awareness to ensure appropriate use and patient adherence.

In conclusion, the introduction of Epioxa represents a significant advancement in the management of keratoconus, offering a non-invasive and effective means to stabilize corneal ectasia and delay disease progression. US FDA approval marked an initial step toward a new standard for corneal cross-linking therapy, making it a more accessible and effective treatment option for patients. Ongoing monitoring and real-world data from different clinical practices will be necessary to evaluate its long-term outcomes, refine its safety profile, and sustain its applicability. These efforts will help establish Epioxa as a standard of care, ultimately improving visual prognosis and quality of life for patients with keratoconus.
