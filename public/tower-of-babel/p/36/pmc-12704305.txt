Transcranial magnetic stimulation combined with mindfulness for pain and disability management in migraine patients: a protocol feasibility study☆
Authors: Fernanda Moura Vargas Dias, Renata Goltara Liboni Vescovi, Carolina Fiorin Anhoque, Maria Eduarda Pinheiro dos Santos, Christian Nogueira de Barros, Iaryssa Iris de Souza, Walter Gomes da Silva Filho, Jocemar Ilha, Fernando Zanela da Silva Arêas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMigraine is the second leading cause of disability worldwide, contributing to high healthcare costs, reduced work productivity, and diminished quality of life. Non-pharmacological therapies such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and mindfulness-based practices (MBPs) have independently demonstrated effectiveness in reducing headache frequency and medication use, offering safer and more tolerable alternatives to pharmacological treatments. This study aims to evaluate the feasibility of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) combining rTMS and MBPs for the management of pain and disability in women with chronic migraine. This double-blind, parallel-group, randomized protocol will include 24 participants assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either a sham group (sham rTMS + mindfulness) or an active intervention group (rTMS + mindfulness). Each participant will undergo eight sessions over four weeks, with assessments conducted at baseline and 30-day follow-up. Outcomes will include measures of pain, disability, mindfulness, anxiety, depression, acceptability, adherence, and safety, collected through validated questionnaires and session logs. In this trial, participants, outcome assessors, and data analysts will be blinded to group allocation. Due to the nature of the intervention, therapists administering rTMS cannot be blinded. Thus, the study is defined as double-blind rather than triple-blind. Feasibility outcomes will include recruitment and retention rates, protocol compliance, randomization and blinding success, and occurrence of adverse events. The results will inform the design of a larger-scale RCT. Given the potential of both rTMS and mindfulness in migraine treatment, their combination may lead to meaningful improvements in symptom management and quality of life in individuals with chronic migraine.Keywords: Migraine, Meditation, Neuromodulation, Transcranial magnetic stimulation, Chronic pain

Background

Migraine is classified as a primary headache disorder. According to the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-III), primary headaches are considered distinct clinical conditions that occur independently, without an identifiable underlying cause or secondary pathology [1]. The diagnosis of migraine is primarily clinical, based on the patient's medical history and physical examination, with complementary tests rarely required [2].

Individuals with migraine typically present with characteristic symptoms, including unilateral head pain of pulsating quality, lasting between 4 and 72 h, and of moderate to severe intensity [3]. The pain is often exacerbated by physical activity [4] and is commonly accompanied by associated symptoms such as photophobia, phonophobia, nausea and/or vomiting, and cutaneous allodynia [5].

Migraine is considered chronic when headache occurs on 15 or more days per month for more than three months, with migraine features present on at least eight of those days. It is classified as episodic when headache episodes occur on fewer than 15 days per month [2,6,7] (see Frame 1).

The clinical presentation of migraine typically involves four phases: the prodrome, aura, headache (pain and associated symptoms), and postdrome [8]. The prodromal phase may precede the headache episode by hours or even days [9]. The headache phase is marked by throbbing, pulsating, unilateral pain, often accompanied by photophobia, phonophobia, nausea, vomiting, and other symptoms [10].

The aura phase, which may or may not be present, can include visual, sensory, speech or language disturbances, as well as motor, brainstem, or retinal symptoms [11]. The postdrome phase occurs after the headache has resolved and is commonly described as a “migraine hangover,” lasting up to 48 h. Common symptoms during this phase include myalgia, fatigue, drowsiness, and difficulty concentrating [12]. Across all migraine phases, symptoms such as depressed mood, cravings for specific foods, and frequent yawning may occur [8].

Although pharmacological treatments remain the mainstay for migraine management, they are often nonspecific, associated with side effects, and may offer limited long-term efficacy [13]. Moreover, the excessive use of acute medications is the most frequent cause of chronic migraine, known as medication-overuse headache [14,15].

Non-pharmacological therapies have shown benefits such as reduced drug consumption, fewer headache episodes, and improved tolerability and safety compared to standard pharmacological approaches [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. From a biopsychosocial perspective, combining therapeutic strategies with lifestyle modifications, meditation-based interventions, and non-invasive neuromodulation offers a more integrative approach to migraine management [20]. In line with this, the International Headache Society has published specific guidelines to improve the methodological rigor of clinical studies evaluating neuromodulation devices for both acute and preventive treatment of migraine [21].

Among non-pharmacological approaches, mindfulness meditation has emerged as a promising mind-body intervention. It fosters nonjudgmental awareness and acceptance of internal experiences—such as thoughts, emotions, and bodily sensations—which may enhance pain perception and coping strategies [22,23]. Mindfulness training has been associated with increased psychological resilience, greater pain acceptance, improved stress management, and better quality of life in individuals with migraine [24,25]. However, mindfulness alone has shown limited evidence for significantly reducing headache frequency, intensity, or duration compared to standard care [26].

Given the lack of novel prophylactic pharmacological strategies, neuromodulation has gained traction as a top-down therapeutic approach [27,28]. In migraine populations, neuromodulation techniques have yielded encouraging results, including reductions in headache frequency, pain intensity, and medication use [16,17,19].

Specifically, non-invasive transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) has demonstrated potential in both the treatment and prevention of migraine attacks. It is hypothesized that magnetic pulses may inhibit cortical spreading depression and disrupt thalamocortical signaling, which are involved in migraine pathophysiology [[29], [30], [31]]. Additionally, stimulation of specific cortical regions has been shown to modulate pain perception and reduce attack frequency [30,32].

Migraine treatment must be individualized due to its complex nature, involving a combination of physiological mechanisms (e.g., genetic predisposition) and contextual factors such as lifestyle, emotional state, and comorbid conditions like obesity [33]. Therefore, multimodal treatment approaches that combine effective interventions—such as mindfulness and TMS—may enhance preventive strategies and patient outcomes [34].

Previous pilot studies and systematic reviews have suggested that the combination of neuromodulation techniques, including TMS, with mindfulness-based interventions may enhance treatment effects on pain perception and psychological outcomes. For example, Cavallero et al. (2021) demonstrated feasibility and positive preliminary effects of combining TMS with mindfulness in patients with depression, supporting the rationale for testing this approach in migraine populations. These findings provide indirect evidence for the efficacy of the proposed protocol and support its validation as a methodologically sound approach.

Moreover, although several studies have examined TMS and mindfulness independently, few have explored their combined effects. Preliminary reports suggest synergistic benefits when neuromodulation is paired with psychological interventions. The aim of this study is to present a feasibility protocol to evaluate recruitment, intervention procedures, acceptability, and outcome measures for a clinical trial combining transcranial magnetic stimulation with mindfulness-based practices for the treatment of pain and disability in women with chronic migraine.

Methods

This is a double-blind, parallel-group, single-center protocol study with two arms and an allocation ratio of 1:1. All assessments and intervention procedures will be conducted at the Clínica Escola Interprofissional of the Federal University of Espírito Santo (UFES), Brazil.

At the study center, non-pharmacological treatments typically include general lifestyle counseling and physical therapy-based strategies. The inclusion of mindfulness in both groups ensures that all participants receive a recognized non-pharmacological intervention, while the active TMS serves as the experimental variable. The decision not to provide a delayed active phase for the sham group was based on feasibility design constraints; however, participants in the sham arm will be offered referral to ongoing university extension programs after study completion to ensure ethical balance.

Participants will be recruited and randomly assigned to either a sham group (sham rTMS + mindfulness) or an active intervention group (rTMS + mindfulness). A total of eight rTMS sessions will be administered over four weeks, with two sessions per week. Each session will be followed by an audio-guided mindfulness meditation practice.

In Week 1, participants will be enrolled based on eligibility criteria, provide written informed consent, complete a sociodemographic questionnaire, and receive instructions on maintaining a headache diary. Thirty days after enrollment, baseline assessment scales will be administered, and the initial headache diary will be collected.

The intervention period will last four weeks (Weeks 5-8), with two sessions per week. At each session, adverse events and intervention safety will be monitored, and participants will submit a meditation practice diary.

On the final day of intervention (Week 9), the primary outcomes and treatment acceptability will be assessed, and participants will return their headache diaries. A follow-up assessment will be conducted four weeks post-intervention (Week 13), at which time participants will return their final headache diaries (see Fig. 1).

Patients

The sample will include 24 volunteer patients diagnosed with chronic migraine according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders criteria (ICHD-3, 2018), with symptoms persisting for more than 12 months. Participants will be recruited from the Vitória region, Espírito NSanto, Brazil, with registration conducted through an online form.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization will be computer-generated by a researcher uninvolved in participant recruitment. Allocation will be concealed using sequentially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes prepared prior to study commencement by a research assistant. Participants will be assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the ShamTMS+Mindfulness or TMS+Mindfulness group. Following baseline assessments, the treating therapist will reveal allocation by opening the corresponding envelope. Outcome assessors will be blinded to group assignment. Each participant will receive a unique code to ensure confidentiality throughout the trial.

Participants and evaluators will remain blinded to group allocation during the entire study. To maintain participant blinding, rTMS sessions will be conducted in separate rooms to prevent interaction between participants. Participants will not be informed whether they receive active rTMS or sham stimulation.

The Sham-TMS involves sensory and auditory stimulation via a sham coil positioned identically to active stimulation, matching duration but without effective cortical stimulation. Due to the nature of the intervention, therapists administering rTMS cannot be blinded, as coil orientation differs between sham and active stimulation.

Procedures

TMS therapy

Participants will be seated comfortably with their arms supported on a pillow during stimulation. A figure-of-eight coil (MC B-70, MagPro 30, Magventure, Denmark) will deliver biphasic pulses inducing an anteroposterior current via the MagPro 30 device.

Single-pulse TMS will be used to identify the optimal stimulation site over the left motor cortex corresponding to the right thumb’s short abductor muscle. Motor threshold (MT) is defined as the lowest stimulation intensity eliciting a visible muscle contraction, initially set at 35 % maximum output and increased by 5 % increments until consistent contraction is observed. MT will be determined before each session. Stimulation intensity will be set at 110 % of MT, targeting the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) at F3 according to the International EEG 10-20 system.

Each rTMS session will deliver 1600 pulses divided into 32 cycles of 5 s each, with 30-second intervals, at a frequency of 10 Hz. The experimental group will receive 20 min of rTMS twice per week for 4 weeks.

Following rTMS, participants will engage in 11-25 min audio-guided Mindfulness meditation sessions twice weekly for 4 weeks (total of 8 sessions). The meditations are prerecorded by a psychologist trained in Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), narrated by a slow female voice guiding focus and attention. Volume is adjustable, and participants will listen via headphones while seated or standing according to preference.

After each session, participants will complete a “Practice Diary” to record their experiences and reinforce awareness. The meditation audio files will be available for home practice with the same diary routine. The program targets specific Mindfulness mechanisms; session details are provided in Table 2.

The first session includes additional guidance on meditation objectives and addresses participant questions before and after treatment. Meditation duration will gradually increase from 11 to 25 min over the weeks. Participants are encouraged to maintain home practice throughout the 8-week period.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes will be feasibility of the adjuvant effect of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation to Mindfulness in the treatment of pain and disability in Migraine women. Feasibility will include compliance ( % of participants who completed the intervention), recruitment ( % of participants recruited in time) and retention rates ( % of participants who completed the evaluations), randomization success, blind-success, acceptability and the occurrence of any adverse events [35,36] (Table 3). Treatment is considered to be compliant when at least 80 % of the target number participate in 8 sessions of the protocol. Furthermore, this study was designed to detect clinically relevant treatment effects, which we consider corresponding to statistically large effects according to Cohen [37].

A safety monitoring procedure will be implemented under the supervision of the principal investigator, with oversight by the institutional ethics committee. Although no independent Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) is formally established due to the feasibility nature of the study and minimal risk intervention, safety procedures will comply with ethical and regulatory standards.

Adverse events will be monitored after each of the 8 sessions. Evaluators will ask about effects during, immediately after, and between sessions. Data will be reported qualitatively, with the number and type of events recorded. Participants will receive care for any damage and can continue free treatment via university extension projects. If serious or frequent adverse effects occur, the principal investigator may stop the study. Transcranial magnetic stimulation is a safe, well-tolerated, non-invasive neuromodulation with minimal effects such as headache, migraine, paresthesia, dizziness, vertigo, tinnitus, and temporary scalp discomfort [31].

Acceptability will be measured after 8 sessions using a 0-10 visual scale (0 = would not repeat, 10 = definitely repeat).

Secondary outcomes

An established and widely used, validated questionnaire will be applied to assess the pain severity and activity limitations.

Assessment

Assessments will be done 30 days before the first intervention, at protocol end, and 30 days post-treatment (follow-up).

After consent, individual anamnesis (identification, main complaint, medical history, personal background, medications) and a brief physical exam (blood pressure, weight, height) will be conducted using a standardized form.

Then, pain and scales for anxiety, stress, depression, migraine-related disability, quality of life, and subjective well-being will be assessed.

Pain assessment tools

Brief pain inventory (BPI)

Pain perception will be assessed using the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), which evaluates pain intensity, location, duration, and interference with daily life. The BPI includes 9 items across sensory (items 3-6) and reactive (items 9a-9 g) dimensions. Pain is rated on a scale from 0 (no pain/interference) to 10 (worst pain/max interference), and location is marked on a body diagram. Higher scores reflect greater pain or interference [38].

Headache impact test (HIT-6™)

The HIT-6™ assesses headache impact using 6 items on a 5-point Likert scale. Scores range from 36 to 78, with higher values indicating more impact: ≤49 (little/no impact), 50-55 (some), 56-59 (substantial), ≥60 (severe) [39].

Migraine disability assessment scale (MIDAS)

The MIDAS assesses migraine disability based on days lost in activities over 3 months. Scores classify disability as: 0-5 (none/little), 6-10 (mild), 11-20 (moderate), and 21+ (severe) [40].

Depression, anxiety, and stress scale (DASS-21)

The DASS-21 is a 21-item self-report scale assessing depression, anxiety, and stress over the past week on a 0-4 Likert scale. It differentiates normal from clinical symptoms by severity [41].

Five facet mindfulness questionnaire (FFMQ)

The FFMQ measures mindfulness through 39 self-reported items on a 5-point Likert scale, covering five dimensions: Observing, Describing, Acting with Awareness, Non-Judging, and Non-Reactivity [42].

Headache diary

The headache diary, based on the Brazilian Headache Society model, tracks episodes by recording date, intensity, location, duration, medication, and triggers. A monthly pain index is calculated (mild × 1, moderate × 2, severe × 3). Diaries are completed before, during, and 30 days after treatment.

Data collection and management

The principal investigator will collect all data using printed questionnaires and physical tests. A blinded researcher will conduct the assessments. Results will then be organized and entered into the study database.

Data analysis

In addition to the planned mixed-measures ANOVA, exploratory analyses will be conducted to evaluate effect sizes, confidence intervals, and sensitivity analyses. Given the small sample size typical of feasibility studies, the statistical approach focuses on estimating parameters and feasibility indicators rather than hypothesis testing. Descriptive statistics will be used to present recruitment, retention, adherence, and adverse events. Effect sizes will be interpreted following Cohen’s conventions. Furthermore, the analysis plan includes handling of missing data by applying multiple imputation where appropriate, and all analyses will be conducted using both intention-to-treat and per-protocol populations.

According to power calculations done with G*Power 3.1.9.7 and assuming an alpha error level of 5 %, a power of 95 %, and a moderate correlation among repeated measures (r ≥ 0.5), a sample size of n = 20 is required to detect significant intervention (stimulation vs. sham) × time (visit 0, visit 1, visit 2) interaction effects of this size. Assuming a drop-out rate of approximately 15 %, a sample of n = 24 participants will be initially recruited, 12 in each of the two groups “sham” and “stimulation.” Another just-conducted pilot trial on a TMS+Mindfulness intervention also reaches clinically relevant effects with a similarly small sample size [43].

This trial features a 2 × 3 factorial design with the factors group (stimulation, sham) and time (Visits 0, 1, 2). Main and interaction effects on the secondary endpoints will be statistically tested via a mixed-measures ANOVA. In case the preconditions for ANOVA use are not met, nonparametric alternatives will be employed. Global effects will be further elucidated by appropriate post hoc comparisons between individual conditions (i.e., per group or time point).

The significance threshold will be set to p < 0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons where needed. Furthermore, estimates of treatment effect size and confidence intervals will be reported in addition to significance testing results. These analyses will provide a treatment effect estimate on each outcome measure. Outcome measures at baseline, 4 and 8 weeks post-intervention will be entered into the model as the dependent variables with fixed effects of study arm, baseline outcome measures, time, and time point by study arminteraction.

Limitations

This study presents some limitations, potential confounding factors such as stress, sleep, and physical activity are not fully controlled. Reliance on self-reported outcomes may introduce bias.The sample will consist only of women with migraine, which restricts the generalization of the results.
