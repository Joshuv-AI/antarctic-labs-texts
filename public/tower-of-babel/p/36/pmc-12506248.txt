Mesenchymal stem cells and cancer stem cells promote colorectal cancer cell growth and biological functions via cell–cell indirect contact
Authors: Yang Liu, Jianing Zhao, Bo Yang, Xu Yang, Juan Chen, Jinyang Cheng, Xiaoshuang Yuan, Guangyang Liu, Xiao Xu, Yanju Li, Dongxin Tang, Feiqing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe effect of Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MSCs) on colorectal cancer stem cells (CCSCs) remains unclear. This study investigates the regulatory effects and mechanisms of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (huc-MSCs) on the biological functions of CCSCs.MethodsThe effects of huc-MSCs isolated from human umbilical cord tissues on CRC proliferation, apoptosis, the cell cycle, and CRC migration are investigated. The effect of CRC cell-huc-MSC interactions on angiogenesis is examined by analyzing pro- and anti-angiogenic factors. CSC-like sphere cells are used to examine the effects of MSCs on CCSC stemness. The influence of huc-MSCs on CCSC invasion is evaluated.ResultsProteins associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) are detected, as are the activation of protein kinase B (AKT) and phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase (PI3K) in CCSCs. Treatment with huc-MSCs increases CRC cell migration and proliferation. After co-culture with huc-MSCs, CRC cells express higher levels of pro-angiogenic factors and enhanced tube formation compared with controls. In addition, CCSC-huc-MSC co-culture induces PI3K/Akt pathway activation, increases their stem cell characteristics, upregulates EMT, and promotes invasion.ConclusionsCCSC-huc-MSC interactions enhance tumorigenesis via PI3K/AKT pathway activation, suggesting a novel CRC treatment target and providing insights into MSCs’ functions in the TME.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03993-7.

Introduction

According to GLOBOCAN 2020, colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks no. 3 among the most common malignancies and no. 2 among the major causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide (10.0% incidence rate of 10.0%; 9.4% mortality rate) [1]. Current evidence suggests that tumors arise because of genetic mutations in a small number of stem cells [2, 3]. These cells have the ability to self-renew, differentiate, proliferate, and replicate, and are known as cancer stem cells (CSCs), representing the main reason why tumors are difficult to cure [4, 5]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) has a vital function in tumorigenesis and progression [6]. Although the TME components vary in different tumor types, its landmark components include the cancer-related the extracellular matrix, blood vessels, stromal cells, and immune cells [7]. Tumor cell-TME interactions influence tumor growth and metastasis [8]. Therefore, increased knowledge regarding tumor cell-TME component associations might reveal the fundamental mechanisms of CRC.

Critically, TME-resident non-malignant stromal and immune cell interactions with tumor cells affect cancer pathophysiology [9]. Multiple studies indicate that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) exhibit chemotaxis toward the tumor microenvironment and interact with cancer cells through paracrine signaling, demonstrating both pro-tumor and anti-tumor properties. As a key component of TME, MSCs promote CRC occurrence and development through various mechanisms [10]. Li et al. proposed that MSCs can protect CRC cells from senescence by acting on the P53/P21 pathway [11]. In addition, CRC progression is promoted by MSCs, possibly via nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway activation mediated by AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (mTOR) upregulation [12]. The growth of distinct α-catenin-deficient CRC cells in xenografts is specifically affected by MSCs, potentially caused by release of the extracellular matrix [13]. Recent evidence suggests that human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (huc-MSCs) are pluripotent stem cells with regulatory roles in the TME, which can influence cancer regression and prognosis; however, the role of MSCs in CRC development remains to be explored. Consequently, we hypothesized that huc-MSCs could be significant in the CRC tumor milieu.

Numerous signaling pathways can directly impact the interaction between the TME and CSCs [14]. Among them, protein kinase B (AKT) and phosphatidylinositol-4, 5-bisphosphate 3-kinase (PI3K) signaling is vital for the division, growth, and survival of cells and is extensively linked to several forms of cancer [15, 16]. As a downstream effector of PI3K signaling, AKT interacts with phospholipids, allowing it to translocate across the inner membrane, followed by pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK)1/PDK2-mediated AKT phosphorylation [17]. AKT activity of affects the function of numerous substrates involved in the regulation of cellular survival and growth, cell cycle progression, apoptosis inhibition, stimulation of cellular growth, and cellular metabolism [18]. Thus, we speculated that MSC/cancer interactions might crucially involve the PI3K/AKT pathway. The relationship between intercellular contact and the biological functions of CRC is the particular focus of our investigation. Our results indicate a previously unknown function of MSCs in CRC progression by regulating CSC activity. The flow chart of the study is shown in Graphical Abstract.

Methods

Cell culture conditions

Human umbilical cord (UC) samples were obtained from women with three full-term pregnancies attending the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University. The use human UCs was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (approval number: 2023 lunshen 507) and obtain informed consent from the patient. Blood contamination was removed by rinsing the UCs thrice using phosphate buffered saline (PBS) under sterile conditions. The samples were sliced into 2-cm segments to allow removal of the UC amnion, veins, and arteries. We then excised the gelatinous tissue and sliced it into 0.5–1 mm3 fragments. The fragments were added to plastic flasks, left for 5 h, and then added with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10%). The obtained MSCs were then incubated at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere, with a Change of medium every 3 days. HCT116 (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), SW620 (ATCC), and HCT116-luc1 (Chinese Tissue Culture Collections (CTCC), Beijing, China) were cultured in DMEM. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, ATCC) were maintained in Endothelial Cell Medium (ECM, ATCC) with a Change of medium every 3 days. When the cells reached approximately 80% confluence, they were passaged using 0.05% trypsin and added to fresh culture flasks.

Osteogenesis and adipogenic lipid induction experiment

Third generation huc-MSCs in good condition were trypsin digested and seeded in a labeled 6-well plate at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well. When the cells reached about 40% confluence, the medium was replaced with 2 mL of osteogenic induction medium (DMEM medium containing 10% FBS, 0.1 µmol/L dexamethasone, 10 mmol/L β-glycerophosphate, 50 mg/L vitamin C, 100 mg/L penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin). Alizarin red staining was performed on the 14th day. The cells were fixed for 30 min using 40 g/L paraformaldehyde, washed thrice using PBS, and stained using 2% alizarin red S for 20 min at room temperature. The formation of mineralized nodules was observed under an optical microscope.

Healthy huc-MSCs from passage 3 were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well, and grown until they reached 100% confluence. The medium was replaced with 2 mL of adipogenic induction medium (DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 1 µmol/L dexamethasone, 60 µmol/L indomethacin, 0.5 mmol/L 1-methyl-3-isobutylxanthine, 100 mg/L penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin). The adipogenic induction medium was replaced every 3 days. Oil red O staining was performed on the 14th day. The induced cells were fixed for 30 min using 40 g/L paraformaldehyde, washed thrice using PBS, and then stained using oil red O staining solution for 30 min at room temperature. An optical microscope was used to observe the stained cells.

Co-culture of huc-MSCs and CRC cells

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, CRC cells and huc-MSCs were co-cultured using Transwell chambers (pore size 0.4 μm, Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA). Huc-MSCs suspended in DMEM were added to the upper chambers. In the lower chamber, CRC cells were cultured in DMEM. Twenty-four hours later, the CRCs were collected for functional and mechanism analysis.

Preparation of conditioned medium

MSCs (1 × 106) were cultured to obtain MSC Conditioned Medium (MSC-CM). The cells were rinsed thrice using 2 mL of PBS and then cultured in 5 mL of low sugar-DMEM with 10% FBS at 37 ℃ for 48 h. The control medium was L-DMEM with untreated cells, collected from tissue culture flasks. The collected medium was centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min at centrifugal force 1000 g and the supernatant was stored at −20 °C.

Sphere formation assay

To obtain tumor spheres, collected CRC single-cell suspensions were seeded into 6-well ultra-low attachment plates (Corning Life Sciences, Kennebunk, ME, USA) at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well, followed by growth in FBS-free DMEM (GlutaMAX™, Gibco) containing human epidermal growth factor (20 ng/mL) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), human basic fibroblast growth factor (20 ng/mL) (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), 1 × B-27 supplement (Gibco), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were then grown for 7 days at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere for the formation of spheres, with the renewal of the medium every 3 days. The number of sphere cells with diameter of 20–150 μm in different fields was counted under a microscope.

Cell viability assay

For the control group, MSC-CM group, and Co-Culture group, CRC cells were harvested and equal numbers of cells were seeded into 96-well plates. Cell counts were assessed using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assays every 24 h for 6 days. A microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to determine the absorbance at 570 nm.

Flow cytometry to immunophenotype huc-MSCs

Huc-MSCs were digested with trypsin, and cell suspensions of 1 × 106 cells/mL were prepared with PBS. Fluorescent group-labeled antibodies (or isotype control antibodies) were added to 100 µL of cell suspensions. The group-labeled antibodies comprised antibodies recognizing CD19 and CD34 (fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated), CD11b, CD45, CD90, CD73, HLA-DR, and CD105 (Phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated) (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). The cells were incubated at 4 ℃ for 30 min under low Light conditions. The cells were adequately washed using pre-cooled PBS, centrifuged, and the supernatant discarded. Each flow tube was filled with 400 µL of PBS to resuspend the cells, and the expression of cell surface markers was detected using flow cytometry (BD C6 Plus Cell Analyzer).

Flow cytometry to immunophenotype CSCs

To detect the surface markers of CSCs, after 72 h of co-culture, or treatment with MSC-CM or control media, the tumor spheres were digested with trypsin-EDTA, and cell suspensions of 1 × 106 cells/mL were prepared in PBS. Fluorescent group-labeled antibodies (or isotype control antibodies) were added to 100 µL of cell suspensions. The group-labeled antibodies comprised anti-CD133 (Allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated) and CD44 (FITC-conjugated) (1:11 dilution, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). The cells were incubated at 2–8 ℃ for 10 min and the antibody-labeled cells were rinsed and re-suspended in PBS, followed by flow cytometry analysis (BD C6 Plus Cell Analyzer).

Cell apoptosis analysis via flow cytometry

To detect the apoptosis of CSCs, after 72 h of treatment with co-culture, MSC-CM, or control media, the tumor spheres were digested with trypsin-EDTA, and cell suspensions of 1 × 106 cells/mL were prepared in PBS. Fluorescent group-labeled antibodies (or isotype control antibodies) were added to 100 µL of cell suspensions. AnnexinV-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) were added to the cell suspensions, respectively, and incubated at 2–8 ℃ for 15 min. After rinsing and resuspending in PBS, the labeled cells were subjected to flow cytometry (BD C6 Plus Cell Analyzer).

Flow cytometry to assay the cell cycle

To detect the cell cycle of CSCs, after 72 h of treatment with co-culture, MSC-CM, or control media, cells were collected and washed once with PBS, added with 1 mL of 70% ethanol, and fixed overnight at 4 ℃. After removing the ethanol, the cells were washed twice with PBS, then treated with RNase A (50 µg/mL) at 37 ℃ for 30 min, followed by staining with PI (50 µg/mL) for 10 min (protected from light), followed by detection using by flow cytometry, and analysis using Modfit software (Cytonome Verity, Bedford, MA, USA).

Wound healing assay

For the control group, MSC-CM group, and co-culture group, CRC cells were harvested, seeded in 6-well plates (5 × 105 cells/well), and incubated for 24 h in a 37 °C incubator. A scratch was made on the confluent cell monolayer using the tip of a 200 µL pipette, the cell debris was cleared away, and serum-free MSC-CM was added to the MSC-CM group, and serum-free culture medium was added to other groups. Photographs were taken after the scratch at 0 h and at 24 h of incubation. Image J software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to measure the width of the scratch, followed by calculation of the cell migration rate.

Assay of cell migration

An 8 μm well Transwell chamber (Corning Inc.) was used to assess cell migration. For the control group, CRC cells (2.5 × 104 cells/well) were added to the upper chamber, and the lower chamber was added with control medium. For the MSC-CM group, CRC cells (2.5 × 104 cells/well) were inoculated into the upper chamber, and the MSC-CM was placed in the lower chamber. For the co-culture group, CRC cells (2.5 × 104 cells/well) after co-culture with MSCs were inoculated into the upper chamber, and the lower chamber was added with control medium. The chambers were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2, washed with PBS, and the cells that had migrated through the porous membrane were fixed for 30 min using 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by crystal violet staining. The average number of cells that migrated across the filter membrane was then calculated.

Colony formation assay

Untreated CRC cells and cells co-cultured with MSCs (all n = 800) were inoculated into 6-well plates, treated with control medium or MSC-CM (untreated cells only, and placed in the CO2 incubator for 14 days at 37 °C. After discarding the medium and washing with PBS, the cells were fixed for 30 min using 4% paraformaldehyde, stained for 20 min using 1% crystal violet, and then photographed for clonal counting.

Edu staining for cell proliferation determination

The medium containing the 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) working solution was added to the cell culture for 24 h. After 4% paraformaldehyde fixation, TritonX-100 was added to permeate the cells, followed by the addition of Apollo® staining solution (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). The reaction was allowed to proceed for 30 min at 37 ℃ avoiding Light, after which Hoechst reagent was added and reacted for 10 min at 37 ℃ avoiding light to stain the nucleus. The stained cells were observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope to calculate the percentage of EdU-positive cells.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

For qPCR analysis, total RNA was extracted from the cells by using TRIzol reagent (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using an EasyScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). QPCR was performed using a Top Green qPCR SuperMix kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) in a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad).

Western blotting

Radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime, Beijing, China) containing protease inhibitors (Boster, Wuhan, China) was used to lyse the cells to extract the total proteins. Subsequently, the total proteins were subjected to polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, followed by transfer to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Then, 5% skim milk was used to block the membrane for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Next day, the membranes were rinsed twice using Tris-buffered saline-Tween20 (TBST), followed by incubation with Goat Anti-rabbit IgG-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Boster, No. BA1054, 1:5000) or Goat Anti-mouse IgG-HRP (Boster, No. BA1050, 1:5000) at room temperature for 1 h. After three washes with TBST, the membrane was exposed to an ECL (Electro-Chemiluminescence) chromogen and imaged using a membrane scanner. The primary antibodies recognized: CD133 (ABclonal, Wuhan, China; No. A0818, 1:1000), CD44 (ABclonal; No. A12410, 1:1000), OCT4 (Beyotime; No. AF2506, 1:1000), SOX2 (Beyotime; No. AF8034, 1:1000), NANOG (Beyotime; No. AF1912, 1:1000), E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology (CST), Danvers, MA, USA; No. 3195, 1:1000), N-cadherin (CST; No. 13116, 1:1000), Vimentin (CST; No. 5741, 1:1000), Caspase-3 (Beyotime, No. AF0081, 1:1000), BCL2 associated X protein (Bax) (Beyotime; No. AF1270, 1:1000), B-cell CLL/lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) (Beyotime; No. AF6285, 1:1000), tumor protein 53 (P53) (Boster; No. BM4026, 1:1000), P21 (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA; No. 10355-1-AP, 1:1000), P16 (Beyotime, No. AF1672, 1:1000), PI3K (Beyotime; No. AF1966, 1:1000), phosphorylated (p)-PI3K (Beyotime; No. AF1966, 1:1000), AKT (Beyotime; No. AF1777, 1:1000), p-AKT (Beyotime; No. AA329, 1:1000), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (Beyotime; No. AF0006, 1:1000).

Assay of tube formation

The angiogenic activity induced by indirect contact between CRC cells and huc-MSCs in vivo was assayed by tube formation. Matrigel (150 µL, Corning Inc.) at 4 ℃ was added to a pre-cooled 96-well plate and incubated for 1 h at 37 ℃. Trypsin was used to digest the HUVECs, which were resuspended in the corresponding cell medium according to the experimental grouping and adjusted to 1.5 × 104 using 10% FBS, 10% HCT116 cell CM, 10% SW620 cell CM, and 10% HCT116 cell-huc-MSC co-culture CM, respectively. Then, 150 µL of the cell suspensions were added to the wells of the 96-well Matrigel-coated plate. After 12 h, the formation of tube-like structures was evaluated and imaged under an inverted light microscope (× 100).

In vivo imaging of nude mice tumor xenografts

The fluorescence of tumor cells in the peritoneal cavity of nude mice was observed using a small animal in vivo imager on days 7, 14, and 21 after modeling, respectively. Before imaging, the nude mice were injected intraperitoneally with potassium fluorescein substrate (150 mg/kg), sedated by continuous inhalation of isoflurane after 10 min of free movement, and fixed supine on the IVIS@ Spectral Imaging System (Perkin-Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) for in vivo fluorescence imaging. Living Image software (Perkin-Elmer) was used to analyze the acquired images, and the photon counts and corresponding radiation values of the tumor cells were calculated.

In vivo tumorigenicity and tumor histology

Nude male BALB/c mice (4 weeks old, Weighing 20± 2 g) were reared in a specific pathogen-free grade animal laboratory for 1 week. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese (approval number: AHQU202100622A). Three groups of four mice each were randomly assigned. The same number of HCT116 CRC cells were cultured in control medium, MSC-CM, or by co-culture for 72 h. Cells were harvested and seeded into low adherence plates and subjected to suspension culture in serum-free medium to enrich CSCs. The enriched CSC cell spheres were digested into single-cell suspension and counted (cultured in MSC-CM: 2.3 × 105, co-culture: 2.35 × 105, or control medium: 2 × 105), and then mixed with PBS. The suspensions of single cells were injected subcutaneously into the mice’s right armpit. Every 7 days, the tumor volume was determined to construct a tumor volume change curve, using the formula: tumor volume (cm3) = V = 0.5 × a × b2, where a represents the long diameter of the tumor and b represents the short diameter of the tumor. Upon completion of the final measurement (at 3 weeks after injection), the mice were sacrificed, and the graft tumors in the axilla of mice were excised using tissue scissors. The volume of the excised tumor was determined as above and the tumor was weighed using a balance scale and recorded.

Statistical considerations

GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) was applied for all statistical analyses. Measurement data that conformed to a normal distribution or approximately conformed to a normal distribution were expressed as mean value ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{x}$$\end{document} ± s), multi-group comparisons were analyzed using one-way ANOVA (least significant difference (LSD) posthoc test); two-way comparisons were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Results

Isolation and immunophenotyping of huc-MSCs

Cells were visible under the microscope emerging from the edge of the tissue after five days of cultivation of human umbilical cord tissue (Fig. 1A). The morphology of these cells was typical of long spindle-shaped fibroblast-like cells, with a few cells in the form of polygonal protruding cells or acicular spindle shapes (Fig. 1B). We verified the differentiation ability of umbilical cord-derived MSCs. After being induced to undergo adipogenic differentiation, huc-MSCs displayed obvious lipid droplets according oil red O staining (Fig. 1C), and alizarin red staining revealed the presence of obvious calcium nodules after being induced to undergo osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 1D). The results suggested that huc-MSCs could be differentiated into adipocytes and osteocytes. Huc-MSCs highly expressed surface markers such as CD73, CD90 and CD105, but not CD34, CD19, CD11b, CD45, or HLA-DR (Fig. 1E). These results showed that we successfully obtained human MSCs.

Indirect co-culture with MSCs promotes CRC cell proliferation

CRC cell lines (HCT116 and SW620) and MSCs in Transwell chambers or treated with MSC-CM were used to create a CRC/MSC co-culture system, with monocultures of HCT116 and SW620 cells as controls. The indirectly co-cultured cells were subjected to MTT assays. Compared with the controls, the number of cells in MSC indirect co-culture on day 3 was increased significantly (Fig. 2A). The colony-forming ability of HCT116 and SW620 cells indirectly co-cultured with MSCs was enhanced significantly in comparison to that of cells cultured in control medium (Fig. 2B). Cell proliferation was assessed using EdU staining, which confirmed that indirect co-culture with MSCs induced an increase in the number of cells with active DNA synthesis among CRC cells (Fig. 2C).

Indirect co-culture with MSCs inhibits CRC cell line apoptosis

We used flow cytometry to evaluate whether the contact between MSC and CRC cells inhibits cell apoptosis. The indirect co-culture of HCT116 and SW620 cells with MSCs showed significantly less apoptosis compared with cells culture in control media (Fig. 3A). Western blotting showed that contact with MSCs upregulated the level of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and downregulated the levels of apoptosis-related proteins Bax and Caspase-3 (Fig. 3B).

Indirect co-culture of MSCs promotes the CRC cell cycle

Flow cytometry was used to determine whether the contact between MSC and CRC cells alters cell cycle progression. Indirect contact with MSCs significantly changed the distribution of cell cycle phases of diploid CRC cells, as indicated by an augmented percentage of S phase cells (Fig. 4A). The effect of MSCs on the CRC cell cycle was also evaluated by determining the levels of three negative regulators of the cell cycle. Co-culture with MSCs or MSC-CM caused HCT116 and SW620 cells to express lower levels of P53, P16 and P21 than cells cultured in control media (Fig. 4B). These findings implied that CRC proliferation associated with MSCs acts via a mechanism involving altered cell cycle progression.

Increased angiogenesis of CRC cell lines after indirect co-culture with MSCs

To determine the impact of MSCs on CRC angiogenesis, pro-angiogenic factor levels were detected in HCT116 and SW620 cells after treatment with MSCs-CM or after Transwell co-culture with MSCs. Western Blotting showed no significant increase in IL-6 and VEGFA levels in SW620 cells after treatment with MSCs-CM, but did indicate a slight downregulation of the IL-10 level. After co-culture with MSCs, the IL-6 and VEGFA level in SW620 cells increased significantly, while the IL-10 level decreased significantly. After MSC-CM treatment, the level of VEGFA in HCT116 cells did not increase significantly, IL-6 level in HCT116 cells increased significantly, whereas the expression of IL-10 decreased slightly. After co-culture with MSCs, the IL-6 and VEGFA level in HCT116 cells increased significantly, while the IL-10 level decreased significantly (Fig. 5A). By contrast, the tube formation assay showed that HUVECs treated with conditioned medium from CRC cells co-cultured with MSCs had an increased tube formation ability compared with HUVECs treated with conditioned medium from HCT116 and SW620 cells. These results indicated that co-cultured cells could markedly increase the ability of HUVECs to form tube-like structures (Fig. 5B). Thus, huc-MSCs might promote CRC angiogenesis mainly by upregulating pro-angiogenic factors.

Enrichment and identification of CSCs in CRC cells with serum-free medium

We established a colorectal tumor stem cell model by culturing HCT116 and SW620 cells in serum-free medium (SFM) containing certain growth factors. The first passage sphere cells of HCT116 and SW620 were produced after 14 days of sphere-forming culture enrichment in SFM. By separating these spherical cells into single cells and inoculating them in SFM again, the second passage sphere cells of HCT116 and SW620 were produced, which showed a faster sphere forming ability and a higher self-renewal ability (Fig. 6A). To determine if the spheroid cells were enriched for cells expressing markers associated with CSCs, sphere and parental cells were evaluated for the colorectal CSC-specific markers CD44 and CD133 using flow cytometry (Fig. 6B). Moreover, the expression levels of pluripotency markers (including OCT4, SOX2 and NANOG) between parent cells and sphere cells were evaluated (Fig. 6C). We observed that CD44, CD133, OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog levels in the experimental groups were higher than those of the control parental cells. These results suggested that the HCT116 and SW620 spheres induced by SFM have CSC-like properties.

Indirect co-culture with MSCs enhances CSC-enriched stem cell-like characteristics

We collected indirectly co-cultured cells for a sphere formation assay, to explore the MSCs’ effects on the stemness of CSCs. As revealed in Fig. 7A-G, the sphere-forming ability of CSCs was significantly promoted after indirect contact with MSCs, and the number of CSC spheres and sphere size were significantly increased compared with those in the control group. In addition, western blotting indicated a significantly higher level of the stemness marker CD133 in the HCT116/MSCs in the indirect co-culture system than in the mono-culture system. The content of stemness marker CD44 was significantly higher in the SW620/MSCs indirect co-culture system than in the mono-culture system, as shown in Fig. 7G. The above experimental results indicated that the indirect co-culture of MSCs with CSCs could enhance CSC stemness. This suggested that MSC co-culture provides a good environment to promote CSC stemness, and the correlation between the degree of malignancy of CSCs and MSCs possibly reflects upregulated tumor cell stemness.

Indirect co-culture of MSCs arrests epithelial-to-mesenchyme transition

To determine whether the indirect co-culture of MSCs affects the migration/invasion of CRC cells and their cancer stem cells, we examined the migration capability of CRC cells and the invasion capability of CSC-enriched CRC cells in normal culture or in indirect co-culture with MSCs. The results revealed that migration of CRC cells after indirect co-culture with MSCs was significantly enhanced compared with that of the control group, and a similar increase in invasion was found after CCSC indirect co-culture with MSCs (Fig. 8A, B). Western blotting of the levels of EMT-related proteins in HCT116CSC and SW620CSC cells showed that CCSC spheres indirectly co-cultured with MSCs had reduced levels of E-cadherin, an epithelial marker, but increased levels of Vimentin and N-Cadherin, which are mesenchymal markers (Fig. 8C).

The indirect co-culture of MSCs activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in CSC-enriched CRC cell lines

To further uncover whether indirect co-culture of MSCs acts on CSCs by regulating PI3K/AKT activation, we analyzed the phosphorylation of PI3K/AKT in CSC-enriched CRC cell lines after control DMEM, MSC-CM, or MSC Transwell culture using immunoblotting. The overall levels of PI3K and AKT were not changed significantly by culture in MSC-CM (Fig. 9A), whereas p-AKT levels in HCT116 CSCs and p-PI3K levels in SW620 CSCs increased significantly. The overall levels of PI3K and AKT were not changed significantly by MSC Transwell-culture (Fig. 9B), whereas p-PI3K and p-AKT levels in HCT116 CSCs, and p-AKT levels in SW620 CSCs increased significantly. Based on these results, contact between MSCs and CSCs promotes CRC progression via PI3K/AKT signaling activation.

MSC promotes tumor growth in a CCSC xenograft model in vivo

To further elucidate the regulatory role of huc-MSCs on the tumorigenic potential of CCSCs, we performed mouse xenograft experiments by subcutaneously injecting CCSCs with or without indirect contact with MSCs. As shown in the figure, compared with the control group, the tumors generated in the indirect co-culture group (using a Transwell system) and the MSC-CM group were significantly larger in volume, with abundant surface vasculature, a harder texture, and stronger invasiveness (Fig. 10A–C). In terms of tumor formation time, palpable tumor nodules were detected in the co-culture group as early as day 7 post-injection. In contrast, observable tumors did not appear in the control group until day 14 (Fig. 10D–F). Additionally, the tumor formation rates in the co-culture and MSC-CM groups were significantly higher than those in the control group, particularly in the Transwell co-culture group, indicating that huc-MSCs significantly enhance the tumor initiation capacity and malignant phenotype of CCSCs through indirect contact.

To explore the potential mechanisms, we performed molecular biological analysis on the transplanted tumor tissues. Western blot and immunohistochemistry results showed that after indirect contact with huc-MSCs, there were significant changes in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related markers in the tumor tissues, including upregulation of vimentin expression and downregulation of E-cadherin expression. Additionally, the expression levels of tumor stem cell markers (CD133, CD44, OCT4, and SOX2) were significantly elevated. Furthermore, the phosphorylation levels of key proteins in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway were significantly enhanced in the co-culture group and MSC-CM group (Fig. 11C–D), suggesting that the activation of this pathway may be an important mechanism by which huc-MSCs enhance CCSC stemness and promote tumorigenesis.

Discussion

Previous studies have confirmed that bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMMSC) conditioned medium-induced cancer promotion involves multiple mechanisms [12, 19]. Herein, the results showed that huc-MSCs can also significantly enhance the proliferation of human CRC cells HCT116 and SW620, whether via the use of conditioned medium or Transwell co-culture. MSCs have important immune functions, including immune regulation, autocrine and paracrine activity, and evasion of innate immunity. The mechanism by which human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUC MSCs) promote the growth and biological functions of colorectal cancer cells through indirect cell-cell contact may be related to their paracrine effects by improving the tumor microenvironment of colon cancer [20, 21]. The mediators released by mesenchymal stem cells can regulate the cell cycle of cancer cells, inhibit apoptosis, enhance cell proliferation, Angiogenesis, and metastasis promotion, and previous studies have shown that BMMSCs can enhance 5T2 MM cell proliferation by accelerating G1/S phase transition, regardless of the culture method, while steroid receptor coactivator protein 3 (SRC3) expressed in BMMSCs inhibits apoptosis of 5T2 MM cells via the cystatinase apoptosis pathway and the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway [22–24]. In comparison, our results showed that indirect co-culture of MSCs regulates the CRC cell cycle, increases the growth of CRC cells, and promotes their survival rate by enhancing G1/S phase transformation. Meanwhile, flow cytometry apoptosis assays showed that MSCs reduced the apoptosis of HCT116 and SW620 cells. To further validate the results, we examined cell cycle-related proteins (p53, p21 and p16). These proteins are well-established inhibitors of cell cycle protein-dependent kinases that negatively regulate cell proliferation and division, and are involved in cellular senescence [25–27]. We also examined apoptosis-related Bax, Bcl-2 and caspase-3 levels, which are key regulators of apoptosis and have been implicated in CRC initiation [28, 29]. Our study showed that indirect contact with MSCs resulted in decreased levels of the tumor suppressors P21, P16 and p53, increased levels of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2, and decreased levels of caspase-3 and the pro-apoptotic protein Bax. Thus, MSCs were demonstrated to promote CRC proliferation associated with altered levels of cell cycle and apoptosis regulators.

Evidence has shown that tumor growth correlates closely with angiogenesis [30]. In this study, HUVEC angiogenesis induced by CRC cell lines was promoted after indirect co-culture with MSCs. The important angiogenesis regulator, VEGF-A, promotes cell proliferation, migration, and neovascularization by mediating most of the steps in endothelial cell angiogenesis [31]. There is growing evidence that interleukin-6 (IL-6) drives cancer progression, metastasis, chemoresistance, angiogenesis, and EMT through its involvement in multiple inflammatory responses [32, 33]. In this study, we found a significant increase in VEGF-A and IL-6 levels in CRC cells after co-culture with MSCs. Interestingly, the levels of VEGF-A and IL-6 did not change significantly in CRCs cultured in MSC-CM. In addition to interleukin IL-6, IL-10 is considered one of the most important cytokines that regulate the proliferation and cell characteristics of tumor cells. IL-10 produced by tumor cells can inhibit macrophage-derived angiogenic factors, and hence, tumor growth and metastasis [34, 35]. As an inhibitor of tumor angiogenesis, IL-10 has attracted much research attention. Our results showed that indirect contact with MSCs reduced IL-10 levels in CRC cells, and most significantly after co-culture in Transwell chambers. Therefore, we concluded that indirect contact with MSCs decreased the level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, upregulated the level of angiogenic factors VEGF-A and inflammatory cytokine IL-6, and induced angiogenesis and tumor cell growth in vascular endothelial cells in vitro.

In the maturing theory of the mechanisms of cancer stem cells, CSC-targeted therapy has emerged as a major therapeutic method [36]. Based on the results of the previous experiments, we attempted to extend the focus of the current study from the interaction between MSCs and CRC cells to the interaction between MSCs and CCSCs. We obtained tumor stem cells with significantly higher stem cell characteristics than their parental cells using serum-free low-adhesion enriched cell spheroids, and explored the effect of indirect cell-to-cell contact between MSCs and CCSCs. According to the results, CSC-enriched CRC cells treated with MSCs had increased sphere-forming capacity and self-renewal compared with the control group cells. MSC–CCSC contact resulted in increased cancer “stemness”.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is the initiating step for invasion and distant metastasis [37]. In cancer, the biological characteristics of malignant cells can be maintained by PI3K/AKT pathway activation and EMT induction, either directly or by cooperating with other signaling pathways, to promote tumor cell invasion and metastasis [38–40]. Recently, it has been shown that PI3K/pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1)/AKT and PI3K/AKT/glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK-3β) signaling pathways are involved in the enhanced metastasis and EMT induced by ADAM metallopeptidase domain 12 (ADAM12) in CRC cells [41, 42]. In this study, we found that indirect contact with MSCs could intensify CCSC tumor cell migration and invasion. In addition to the migratory and invasive abilities of tumors, EMT correlates with the generation of CSCs and the development of chemoresistance [43, 44]. It has been shown that the radiosensitivity of prostate cancer radioresistant cells can be largely improved by combining BEZ235 (a PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway inhibitor) with radiation therapy [45]. In addition, EMT/CSC expression downregulation is associated with inhibition of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, which suggests that radiation resistance in prostate cancer is associated with increased EMT, CSC phenotypic enhancement, and activation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR. PI3K is a key regulator of the PI3K/AKT signal pathway. Its phosphorylation will activate AKT and its downstream effectors. The PI3K/AKT pathway can be activated abnormally through various mechanisms, leading to the occurrence and growth of tumors, reduced tissue apoptosis increased survival of cancer cells, reduced the resistance of tumor cells to chemotherapy, and promotion of tumor angiogenesis and metastasis [46]. We found that AKT and PI3K phosphorylation was stably activated under MSC Transwell co-culture. The expression of EMT markers is affected by PI3K/AKT pathway activation, including N-Cadherin and Vimentin, along with repressed E-cadherin protein levels.

However, this study still has certain limitations. Mesenchymal stem cells possess multifaceted immune functions, including complex characteristics such as immunomodulation, autocrine and paracrine activities, and evasion of innate immunity. This study explored the paracrine effects of hUC-MSCs solely through an indirect co-culture model, without investigating the regulatory role of MSCs on CRC and CSC under direct cell-contact conditions. Therefore, future research should incorporate direct co-culture experiments to more comprehensively elucidate the dual role of MSCs within the tumor microenvironment and their potential clinical application value.

Conclusions

Herein, we studied the functions of mesenchymal stem cells and CSC cells on the biological behavior of colorectal cancer. We demonstrated that MSCs promote CRC proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis via cell-cell indirect contacts. In addition, MSCs regulate EMT and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in CCSCs, thereby enhancing their stem cell properties and significantly promoting tumor growth in vitro and in vivo. Our experimental data suggested that (i) in vitro contact with umbilical cord MSCs promotes colon cancer cell proliferation and invasion; (ii) in vitro contact with umbilical cord MSCs enhances the stem cell properties of colon cancer stem cells, enhances malignancy, and increases the expression of genes associated with tumor development of cancer stem cells; (iii) umbilical cord MSC may promote colorectal cancer cell growth and Biological functions by activating the EMT and PI3K-AKT pathways. Our results provide new insights into the functions of umbilical cord MSCs in the TME.
