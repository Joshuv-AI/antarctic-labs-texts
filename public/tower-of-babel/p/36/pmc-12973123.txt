Generation of an Age‐Dependent and Harmonized 18F‐FDG Brain PET Atlas Using a High‐Sensitivity Short‐Axial FOV PET/CT System
Authors: Yidan Wei, Shixiang Zhang, Qingxiang Wen, Lijie Yin, Sen Yang, Peng Liu, Zhi Zhou, Liping Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT 18F‐FDG brain PET atlas is widely used in clinical and neuroscience research. However, variation in PET scanner technology can lead to misinterpretation and quantification errors with mismatched atlases. This study aimed to develop high‐resolution, age‐stratified FDG brain atlases harmonized across different effective image resolutions (EIRs) to support diverse PET/CT scanners. A total of 140 adults (aged 20–90 years, 72 males) were retrospectively enrolled and divided into seven age groups (n = 20). All underwent 18F‐FDG PET/CT at China‐Japan Friendship Hospital (Jan 2022‐May 2023) using the Siemens Biograph Vision 600 scanner. PET scans (4 min) were acquired 60 min after 18F‐FDG injection (4.44 MBq/kg). System EIR was measured using a Hoffman phantom, and images were smoothed to emulate 5.0–6.0 mm and 8.0–10.0 mm resolutions. Images were normalized to MNI space (1.0 mm) via ANTs, and subjects with outlier FDG uptake values were excluded. SUVRs were calculated using the whole cerebellum as reference. Age‐stratified and EIR‐harmonized atlases were created. Gray/white contrast (GWC), age correlations, and SUVR differences were analyzed. After quality control, 97 participants were included for atlas construction. Atlases for both EIRs were generated. GWC was higher at 5.0–6.0 mm than at 8.0–10.0 mm (e.g., 2.73 vs. 1.76 in 20–30 years). SUVRs in cortical and subcortical regions negatively correlated with age; brainstem SUVRs correlated only at lower resolution. SUVRs were higher at high resolution across regions. A set of age‐dependent, harmonized FDG‐PET atlases was developed and the quantitative metrics across age were characterized.

Introduction

Positron emission tomography (PET), especially in the form of 18F‐FDG PET, is central to the diagnosis and monitoring of neurodegenerative diseases and epilepsy, and is widely used in the clinic and in neuroscience research (Guedj et al. 2022; Law et al. 2019; Minoshima et al. 2022). Open‐source 18F‐FDG PET brain atlases have facilitated cross‐institutional collaboration and enhanced data consistency and have had success in detecting early metabolic abnormalities associated with Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases (Herholz et al. 2002). Such resources have also democratized access to high‐precision metabolic profiling (Della Rosa et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2021).

However, two primary limitations have restricted the utility of currently available FDG atlases. First, most atlases assume static brain metabolism and do not accommodate dynamic, age‐related changes (Deery et al. 2023; Lee and Kim 2022; Schulz et al. 2022; Statsenko et al. 2021), such as prefrontal cortex metabolic activity which is known to be high in adolescence but declines in the temporal lobes of older adults (Deery et al. 2023; Lee and Kim 2022; Schulz et al. 2022; Statsenko et al. 2021). A single static template may fail to reflect normal physiological variations, complicating the diagnosis of age‐related neuropathologies. Secondly, technological variability of PET/CT system intrinsic scanner resolution (Sanaat et al. 2023; Soret et al. 2007; Thomas et al. 2011) and reconstruction algorithms may introduce inconsistencies that distort metabolic assessments and lead to inaccurate quantification of regional brain activity. This is particularly problematic when data from a high‐resolution scanner are analyzed with a low‐resolution atlas or vice versa.

The construction of an age‐stratified atlas is necessary to address these challenges and PET data must be harmonized across different scanner resolutions. The concept of effective image resolution (EIR), explored by the Amyloid Imaging to Prevent Alzheimer's Disease (AMYPAD) consortium using 3D Hoffman phantom studies, is a promising approach (Ghonim et al. 2025; Shekari et al. 2023). EIR gives a metric to align PET data by evaluating the effects of different post‐reconstruction smoothing filters to harmonize brain PET images from various centers. Inter‐scanner variability is reduced with minimal impact on quantitative accuracy (Shekari et al. 2023).

The current study aimed to develop age‐dependent and scanner harmonized 18F‐FDG PET brain atlases using data from a high‐sensitivity, short‐axial field of view (FOV) PET/CT scanner. Atlases were designed to be age‐dependent to reflect metabolic changes across the lifespan and to be resolution‐adaptive to account for EIR differences. The aim was to enhance diagnostic accuracy in the clinic and to give reference material for the study of brain metabolism and neurological disease progression.

Materials and Methods

Participants

One hundred‐forty participants receiving clinically indicated whole‐body and brain 18F‐FDG PET/CT at the Department of Nuclear Medicine, China‐Japan Friendship Hospital between January 2022 and May 2023 were retrospectively enrolled. Participants were included if they met the following criteria: (1) aged older than 20 years; (2) no history of neurological psychiatric, and no history of major surgical procedures; (3) no history of underlying diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, or heart disease; (4) no structural or anatomical brain abnormalities as confirmed by MRI or PET images; (5) no use of medications that could potentially affect cerebral 18F‐FDG metabolism, such as corticosteroids or psychotropic drugs (Qian et al. 2025). All participants were carefully screened by experienced nuclear medicine physicians to ensure normal brain structure and metabolism.

Participants, aged 20–90 years, were divided into seven groups, each spanning 10 years (20–29, 30–39, … 80–89 years, n = 20). Written informed consent was given by all participants prior to enrollment. Ethical approval was granted by the institutional review board of the China‐Japan Friendship Hospital and conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

PET/CT Data Acquisition and Reconstruction

All participants had fasting blood glucose levels < 11.0 mmol/L prior to examination and received intravenous injection of 4.44 MBq/kg 18F‐FDG. Sixty minutes post‐injection, 4 min PET scanning was performed on a high‐sensitivity and short axial FOV PET/CT scanner (Biograph Vision 600 [Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN, USA]) to yield static brain data. Images were reconstructed into standardized uptake value (SUV) images using an ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm (6 iterations, 5 subsets) with a 440 × 440 matrix, a zoom factor of 2, time‐of‐flight (TOF) information (214 ps resolution), point spread function (PSF) correction and an all‐pass filter (i.e., no additional Gaussian smoothing applied during reconstruction).

Hoffman Phantom Preparation and System EIR Calculation

A 3D Hoffman brain phantom was filled with 18F solution to achieve a 4:1 gray‐to‐white matter activity concentration ratio and scanned on the same scanner with similar acquisition and reconstruction protocols as clinical scans. The intrinsic system EIR was determined and appropriate post‐processing smoothing filters selected by the method previously described by an AMYPAD sub‐study (Shekari et al. 2023). Reconstructed phantom images were smoothed with a series of Gaussian filters with varying full‐width‐at‐half‐maximum (FWHM) values. A discrepancy metric (normalized difference) was calculated for each smoothed image relative to an ideal, high‐resolution Hoffman phantom template. The FWHM that minimized this discrepancy was considered the system's intrinsic EIR. Two target EIR ranges were chosen for template generation of 5.0–6.0 mm, representing updated scanners with high TOF and spatial resolution, and 8.0–10.0 mm, representing scanners commonly used in the clinic.

EIR‐Matched 18F‐FDG Atlas

A set of two effective resolution‐matched FDG‐PET atlases was developed to standardize images among different scanners (Figure 1). A single high‐resolution 18F‐FDG Big Brain atlas was first constructed and two separate processing streams applied. The atlas was resampled and smoothed with a Gaussian kernel to achieve a specific target resolution and smoothness in each stream. Two final atlases with 1.0 mm isotropic spacing were produced, one with a FWHM value of 5.0 mm for 5.0–6.0 mm EIR scanners and a second with an FWHM of 8.0 mm for 8.0–10.0 mm EIR scanners. This adaptation of the AMYPAD protocol has been shown to reduce variability in image quality and lower the coefficient of variance in multi‐center studies.

Workflow for generating harmonized FDG brain PET templates from Big Brain data. Big Brain images are first resampled into a T1‐weighted MRI in MNI space, followed by mapping of the corresponding Big Brain FDG‐PET to the same standardized space. Gaussian smoothing with FWHM kernels of 5.0 and 8.0 mm was used to produce harmonized FDG templates at two EIRs. These dual‐resolution templates give standardized reference to enable resolution‐aware downstream analyses and facilitate cross‐dataset comparability.

Age‐Dependent FDG‐PET Atlas Construction

Age‐specific FDG‐PET atlases were created by a multi‐stage processing pipeline for each age group (Figure 2). Individual FDG‐PET scans were normalized to a resolution‐matched FDG atlas after smoothing with a specific kernel to match the resolution of the target atlas, either 5.0–6.0 mm or 8.0–10.0 mm. Three‐step iterative spatial normalization was performed by the Symmetric Normalization (SyN) algorithm (Tustison et al. 2021) using the SyNRA implementation within the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) toolkit. Quality control was performed by identifying and removing scans of poor registration quality by referencing brain regions from the standardized map represented by the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL3_v1) template (Rolls et al. 2020). Standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) were calculated for normalized and quality‐controlled PET images by normalizing regional 18F‐FDG uptake of each voxel to mean uptake for the reference region represented by the whole cerebellum. Validated SUVR maps were averaged for each age group and resolution condition to generate age‐stratified and resolution‐specific atlases.

Pipeline for construction of age‐dependent FDG‐PET atlases from age‐matched cohorts. Individual FDG‐PET scans were harmonized using calibrated EIR and aligned to harmonized FDG templates (EIR = 5.0 and 8.0 mm). Outlier detection within predefined brain parcellations excluded scans with atypical regional FDG distributions. Remaining quality‐controlled scans were averaged within each age stratum to yield age‐specific atlases at 5.0 and 8.0 mm EIR. Age‐related metabolic patterns were captured, resolution effects controlled and bias due to abnormal cases reduced.

Leave‐one‐out outlier detection was conducted for each AAL3 Volume of Interest (VOI) across all age bins to control for physiological variability and ensure data homogeneity within age groups (see processing workflow, Figure 2). Subjects that fell outside the statistical distribution of their respective groups were removed to minimize intra‐group variance. The resulting tracer uptake variability for all regions is consistent across different age groups and EIRs.

Atlas Characterization and Statistical Analysis

AAL3 atlas was used to define standardized volumes of interest (VOIs) which were consolidated into nine clinically relevant macroscale regions: frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital lobes, striatum, thalamus, brainstem, insula and cerebellum (see Supporting Information for details of label ID in AAL3_v1 template for individual VOIs). Mean SUVRs were extracted for these regions and global gray/white‐matter contrast (GWC) calculated for each atlas from the ratio of mean SUVR in cortical gray matter to mean SUVR in cerebral white matter.

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and relationships between regional SUVRs and age for each EIR condition were evaluated by Pearson correlation analysis. Comparisons between EIR 5.0–6.0 mm and 8.0–10.0 mm within each age group were assessed by a paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Analyses were performed using GraphPad (Version: 8.2.1, GraphPad Software Inc.).

The rate of change at voxel level was calculated to characterize spatial heterogeneity of age‐related tracer binding loss. A linear regression model was fitted for each voxel with SUVR as the dependent variable and age as the independent variable. The resulting slope coefficients (k), representing the annual rate of change (derivative of SUVR with respect to age), were extracted. These voxel‐wise gradients were projected onto the cortical surface using the nilearn python package for neuroimaging to visualize the topographical distribution of decline.

Demographic Data

A total of 140 participants, aged 20–90 years (72 males and 68 females), were initially enrolled. Ten participants were excluded due to suboptimal image quality, including motion artifacts or deviations in uptake time protocols. Leave‐one‐out analysis was performed across Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) regions within each age group to ensure data homogeneity. Thirty‐three subjects (approximately 4–5 per age group) generated significant scatter (outliers) in regional values and were excluded. The final cohort consisted of 97 participants, 46 males and 51 females (see Table 1 for demographic characteristics).

Determination of FWHM Parameters Corresponding to Target EIR

The intrinsic relationship between post‐processing parameters and PET system EIR was established by systematic analysis of the Hoffman phantom. Discrepancy values between reconstructed images and the ideal phantom template were analyzed and an inherent system resolution of 5.1 mm was determined for the Siemens Biograph Vision 600 system, which corresponded to the FWHM parameter of 5.1 mm for the post‐processing filter where the image discrepancy was at a minimum (Figure 3A). Systematic parameter coordination and optimization allowed the establishment of standardized criteria for post‐processing parameter selection in different clinical scenarios. A narrowband filter corresponding to 0 to 3.0 mm of FWHM is recommended for high‐resolution imaging requirements (target EIR: 5.0–6.0 mm) and a broader FWHM range of around 6.0 to 7.0 mm for routine clinical applications (target EIR: 8.0–10.0 mm) to balance imaging efficiency and resolution demands (Figure 3B). These findings give empirical evidence for context‐specific optimization of PET image post‐processing protocols.

EIR‐Dependent and Age‐Dependent SUVRs

The SUVR boxplot of different brain regions and age groups at 5.0–6.0 mm and 8.0–10.0 mm EIRs is shown in Figure 5. A gradual age‐related decline in SUVRs across cortical regions was seen at both EIR settings with smaller decreases in the striatum and thalamus and near‐stable values in the brainstem. Absolute SUVRs were consistently lower at 8.0–10.0 mm than at 5.0–6.0 mm. SUVRs differed significantly between the two EIR settings across all other VOIs except in the insula for the 80–90 age group.

Discussion

A set of opensource18F‐FDG PET brain atlases that integrates age‐specific metabolic profiles and harmonizes resolution across imaging devices is presented and represents an advance in neuroimaging. Previous FDG‐PET brain atlases have generated standardized reference frameworks for image analysis and publicly available atlases (Deery et al. 2023; Qian et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2022) are based on group‐averaged metabolic patterns, enabling visual interpretation and voxel‐wise comparisons. However, such atlases have generally given limited attention to age‐related physiological variability or to technical factors such as scanner resolution and differences arising from reconstruction. The atlases address some of the limitations of pre‐existing standardized atlases and enhance the utility of FDG‐PET for both clinical and research purposes by accommodating physiological and technical variability.

Age‐Dependent 18FDG‐PET Atlas

The atlas establishes age‐specific metabolic baselines and addresses some previous limitations of conventional FDG‐PET templates in aging populations. Age is a major factor in clinical dementia diagnoses from 18F‐FDG PET images since it can be difficult to distinguish normal age‐related changes from disease‐related abnormalities. The present work confirms a strong negative correlation between age and 18F‐FDG uptake in key brain regions such as the frontal and temporal gyri. Voxel‐wise analysis (Figure S1) gave results consistent with the findings of ROI‐based analysis.

EIR‐Based Harmonization

EIR‐based harmonization was adapted to address scanner technique variability in multicenter PET studies and to enable consistent cross‐device quantitative comparisons (Shekari et al. 2023). Absolute SUVR values show systematic resolution‐dependent deviations, such as the 5.9% difference in frontal SUVR between EIR groups (Table 2), but similarities in age‐related metabolic trends suggest that scanner‐specific signal bias was reduced by EIR‐based standardization and core biological features of aging were preserved. This approach aligns with the AMYPAD consortium's initiative to develop scanner‐agnostic PET biomarkers, a goal that is particularly important for harmonizing FDG‐PET data during multicenter studies.

Most VOI SUVRs in both EIR atlases were observed to decline gradually with aging, producing strong negative correlations (|r| > 0.903, Table 3), but high‐resolution data (EIR 5.0–6.0 mm) revealed no significant age‐dependence in brainstem FDG‐SUVR. The brainstem governs essential life‐sustaining processes, such as breathing and blood flow, and its metabolic activity is generally viewed as stable. Previous 18F‐FDG PET studies have shown age‐related changes in brainstem metabolism to be close to zero (Akamatsu et al. 2016; Beliveau et al. 2017). Verger et al. also found no age‐related variation in the pons by both conventional and digital PET, indicating the suitability of the brainstem as a reference region (Verger et al. 2021). The current high‐resolution data (EIR 5.0–6.0 mm) also showed no significant age‐dependence in brainstem FDG‐SUVR, confirming a physiologically reliable signal. By contrast, a negative correlation with age was seen at lower resolution (EIR 8.0–10.0 mm). We attribute this discrepancy primarily to partial‐volume effects (PVE). The brainstem is slender with irregular geometry and small cross‐sectional dimensions, making it very susceptible to PVE at reduced spatial resolution. In addition, parenchymal atrophy and expansion of surrounding cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) spaces, including the fourth ventricle, cerebellopontine cistern and subarachnoid space with age (Fujita et al. 2023), increase the brainstem‐CSF interface. This allows spill‐in of low‐activity CSF signal, leading to underestimation of regional metabolic values.

The SUVR discrepancy between the two templates was statistically significant and variability was greatest in the basal ganglia. These phylogenetically ancient structures rely on the high‐efficiency glucose transporter, GLUT3CV, to sustain a steady energy supply. However, if the adjacent lateral ventricles contain negligible radioactivity, spill‐in of low‐activity signal may cause an apparent reduction of 18F‐FDG uptake in the basal ganglia, giving an artifact of systematically underestimated SUVRs. This observation reinforces the advantage of using 18F‐FDG templates with smaller EIR for more accurate quantification.

GWC is of importance in assessing 18F‐FDG scan image quality. Enhanced GWC was observed when the EIR was reduced in the present study. An age‐related decline in GWC was seen in both templates, indicating the capture of metabolic dynamics and the generation of a high‐quality image template by the age‐stratified atlas. The atlas is expected to facilitate the distinction of pathological hypometabolism from changes attributable to normal aging, a challenge addressed by the AMYPAD studies.

Strengths, Limitations and Future Directions

The current findings emphasize the need for harmonized brain templates to accommodate inherent trade‐offs of PET imaging. High‐resolution models enhance the sensitivity to region‐specific microstructural alterations by preserving anatomical fidelity and low‐resolution models show robust signal averaging and improve statistical power in large‐scale, whole‐brain population studies. The harmonized framework addresses scanner heterogeneity in multicenter PET imaging and enables stratification of aging trajectories, preserving sensitivity to WM micro‐variations, such as Coefficient of Variation increases, and ensuring detection of global metabolic trends (e.g., |r| > 0.968). In addition, previous studies (Meyer et al. 2020) have shown that digital detectors combined with high TOF performance improve 18F‐FDG signal recovery in small physiological structures, leading to more stable quantitative measurements and clearer structural delineation. The present atlas‐construction pipeline exploits this advantage to give more stable and comparable SUV measurements before spatial normalization and EIR‐based grouping, facilitating robust group averaging and EIR matching. The framework represents a unified analytical foundation to balance biological precision with technical scalability and is suitable for cross‐platform metabolic research.

The main limitations of the current study include the lack of cognitive assessments, the absence of patient preparation and the lack of non‐OSEM imaging reconstruction parameters. First, cognitive assessments and amyloid PET testing were not available for the participants due to the retrospective nature of the dataset. Nevertheless, relatively stringent exclusion criteria were applied to ensure that only individuals representative of the normal population were included. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting a generally healthy population rather than a strictly cognitively unimpaired cohort (Qian et al. 2025). Second, sensory deprivation was not imposed during scanning. Sensory stimulation may alter metabolic activity in relevant brain regions and sleep deprivation may enhance spontaneous brain activity in the superior temporal gyrus and precuneus. Such non‐pathological variations may affect measurements of baseline metabolism. Third, the current reconstruction parameters (e.g., 440 × 440 matrix) met basic requirements but higher spatial resolutions and advanced reconstruction algorithms were not explored. Other reconstruction algorithms, such as Bayesian Penalized Likelihood, have been shown to give better GWC and lesion detectability than OSEM, especially for smaller lesions. In addition, recent deep learning models are able to infer T1‐weighted MRI images from PET scans to guide reconstruction, potentially improving image quality without a paired MRI scan. Such an approach may enhance sensitivity in detecting small lesions. The development of high‐spatial‐resolution imaging technologies, such as high‐matrix reconstruction and multimodal anatomy‐guided algorithms, to enhance PET image resolution and contrast recovery may facilitate future research. In parallel, future studies should extend these approaches to cognitively normal individuals through longitudinal neuroimaging follow‐up, enabling a better understanding of dynamic metabolic and structural changes over time. Moreover, implementing standardized sensory deprivation protocols and resting‐state functional validation to ensure baseline stability of metabolic activity in relevant brain regions would further enhance the robustness of such investigations. The current atlas may be a suitable clinical reference for spatial normalization and for visual and quantitative assessment (e.g., Z‐score‐based analysis) while also giving regional SUVR reference values within an age‐ and resolution‐matched framework.

Conclusion

A set of age‐dependent, effective‐resolution harmonized FDG‐PET brain atlases is presented for use at different ages and resolutions. A high‐sensitivity and high‐temporal‐resolution PET/CT scanner was used to establish the model, in order to accommodate scanner variations and GWC recovery. An age‐related decline in glucose metabolism by cortical regions and the striatum was demonstrated, and the impact of EIR on 18F‐FDG uptake statistics highlighted.
