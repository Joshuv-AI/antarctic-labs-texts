The Role of a Stroke Specialist Nurse in Early Supported Discharge and Secondary Prevention in the Community. A Scoping Review
Authors: Sarah‐Jane Byrne, David Williams, Declan Patton, Paul Murphy, Frances Horgan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundStroke represents a major source of mortality and morbidity globally. The role of a stroke Clinical Nurse Specialist (CNS) as an expert team member in early supported discharge for stroke, is not well described although it is well established in other models of after‐hospital and outreach specialist care in the community. In this scoping review we explore the evidence in relation to the role of the stroke nurse providing secondary prevention interventions in a community setting.ObjectiveThe aim of this scoping review is to explore the evidence in relation to the role of the stroke nurse providing secondary prevention interventions in a community setting.DesignScoping review of the available literature and narrative synthesis.MethodsA narrative scoping literature review was conducted in accordance with the five‐stage process of Arksey and O'Malley (2005) and the PRISMA‐ScR guidelines. The following databases were searched: Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and systematic literature searches, MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, Google Scholar, and grey literature. Search terms included stroke, stroke rehabilitation, community nursing, early supported discharge.ResultsA total of 2531 references were identified. Following the removal of duplicates (838), and checking each article for suitability, 2507 articles were excluded, and 24 articles were included in this scoping review. Six key themes emerged from the data collection: (1) Absence of Nursing Role following Hospital Discharge; (2) Stroke Nursing in Secondary Prevention and Management of Risk Factors (Modifiable and Non‐Modifiable); (3) Role of the Stroke Nurse in Secondary Prevention‐ Blood Pressure Management; (4) Role of the Stroke Nursing following Discharge Home; (5) Telephone Specific Interventions; (6) Blended Model‐ Telephone Calls and Home Visits. A narrative synthesis of these themes was conducted.ConclusionsNurses working in stroke care are key health educators in stroke programme prevention implementation. Stroke nurses specialising in the unique challenges and needs of stroke patients play an essential role in educating this population in the community. Stroke nurses as part of early supported discharge have expert knowledge and the opportunity to educate patients in the community to reduce their overall risk of having further strokes and improve their overall health.RegistrationThis scoping review protocol was registered in Open Science Framework on 5th April 2023. https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/UCZ6R and published in HRB Open on 8th January 2024 https://doi.org/10.12688/hrbopenres.13818.1.

Background

Stroke represents a major source of mortality and morbidity globally with over 15 million individuals affected worldwide by stroke every year (Feigin et al. 2017; Bayes et al. 2023). Nurses play a critical role in all aspects of stroke care including initial assessment, diagnosis, and provision of inpatient post‐stroke secondary prevention care until the patient is discharged home (Magwood et al. 2020; Middleton et al. 2015). Early supported discharge is a transitional care intervention that has been found to reduce the negative impact of stroke in relation to decreased length of hospital stay. Patients are discharged home earlier, and they receive 6–8 weeks of post‐stroke rehabilitation at home which accelerates functional recovery and improves patient, and caregiver satisfaction (Bettger et al. 2012; Langhorne et al. 2005; Olson et al. 2011; Teasell et al. 2009; Legg et al. 2011). Less attention has been given to the post‐stroke role of the clinical nurse specialist as part of an early supported discharge model of care (Hankey et al. 2002).

The rate of stroke recurrence is 25% within 5‐years (Hankey et al. 2002). Stroke readmission rates were significantly lower in patients who had a follow‐up visit led by an expert stroke nurse compared with patients who do not (Mitchell et al. 2022). Cochrane reviews have indicated that long‐term strategies to assist patients should be implemented such as educating patients regarding their stroke, secondary prevention, their prescription medication including the importance of adherence, and stroke awareness such as the Face Arms Speech Time campaign (Sug Yoon et al. 2001; Müller‐Nordhorn et al. 2006; McKevitt et al. 2010). In relation to smoking, studies have shown that smokers have 1.8 times higher stroke risk compared to non‐smokers and that 18% of all strokes can be attributed to smoking (Kozub 2010). Hypercholesterolaemia increases the risk of stroke by 1.8–2.6 fold (Kozub 2010). Physical inactivity increases the risk of stroke by 2.7 times, and it is recommended that people should be exercising for at least 30 min per day (Kozub 2010).

There is a wealth of literature regarding stroke nurses delivering secondary stroke prevention education to patients and the stroke care pathway which is discussed as being a comprehensive and personalised approach in the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence Guidelines (NICE: Clinical Guideline 68 2008) and Intercollegiate Stroke Working Party guidelines (Clinical Guideline 2023). The stroke clinical nurse specialist plays an important role in assisting the patient in reducing co‐morbid conditions through secondary prevention (e.g., hypertension, smoking, alcohol cessation and medication management) thus limiting the likelihood of recurrent stroke and assisting the patient in their adjustment to lifestyle (Middleton et al. 2015).

Recent guidelines such as the Irish National Stroke Strategy 2022–2027 (Health Service Executive 2022) recommend that a stroke nurse be part of the composition of early supported discharge teams. The role of a specialist nurse in early supported discharge teams is poorly described although well established in similar models of chronic care in community settings. A stroke nurse specialist or clinical nurse specialist in stroke can be defined as working in the specialty area of stroke and their specialist practice has a major clinical focus of care for only stroke patients and their families in hospital, community and outpatient settings (Jokiniemi and Miettinen 2020).

The aim of this scoping review is to describe the literature regarding the role of the stroke nurse providing secondary prevention nurse‐led interventions to stroke patients in a community setting. This area of research is novel as there are no prior systematic reviews or scoping reviews completed on the role of the stroke nurse as an expert in early supported discharge. This scoping review will review general stroke nurse‐led interventions in a community setting and highlight how this role would be applicable to the stroke nurse specialist on an early supported discharge team. This scoping review will provide the first narrative synthesis of the role of the nurse as an expert in early supported discharge.

Methods

Study Design

The protocol for this scoping review was published in HRB‐OPEN (https://doi.org/10.12688/hrbopenres.13818.1) The framework by Arksey and O'Malley (Arksey and O'Malley 2005) was used following the five steps for a rigorous scoping review: (1) identifying the research questions; (2) searching for relevant studies; (3) selecting studies; (4) charting the data; and (5) collating, summarising, analysing, and presenting the results. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) checklist was used to guide the reporting of this review (Arksey and O'Malley 2005).

The Central Research Question was identified as follows: What is the role of the stroke nurse providing secondary prevention interventions to stroke patients in a community setting?

As our study topic was broad with no prior systematic reviews or scoping reviews available all study types were included.

To identify relevant studies, a search strategy was conducted with the support of a specialist librarian. The Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and systematic literature searches including databases MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, Google Scholar, and grey literature were searched using keyword searches (Table 1).

Library search.

Studies were included if they included the following information: (1) studies with participants over the age of 18 years with a diagnosis of a stroke, (2) included any nurse‐led post‐stroke intervention in the community following discharge from acute care or rehabilitation hospital (3) included nursing interventions as part of an early supported discharge team, (4) included interventions in the form of nurse home visits or nurse telephone interventions to post‐stroke patients, (5) included post‐stroke nursing interventions such as information and education on modifiable risk factors and medication adherence. Studies were excluded if (1) participants were under the age of 18 years, (2) the focus was on the role of a stroke specialist nurse in early supported discharge (2) secondary prevention within the community setting, (3) were not published in English, (4) fell outside the defined inclusion criteria regarding relevance. The inclusion criteria were categorised according to the broad Population—Concept—Context (PCC) framework recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute for scoping reviews (The Joanna Briggs Institute 2015) as a less restrictive alternative to the PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparator and Outcome) framework recommended for systematic reviews.

Data was charted by extracting: (1) author/s and year, (2) country of study, (3) study aim, (4) methods, (5) study setting, (6) participants, and (7) key findings. Extracted data was entered into an Excel spreadsheet by the main researcher (SJB) and checked by a second author (FH).

Following data extraction, data was collated and summarized. Two reviewers SJB and FH completed title and abstract screening. A third reviewer (DP) acted as arbitrator for any disagreements (Figure 1). 25 studies were included for data extraction. The lead reviewer (SJB) independently charted the data from the retrieved articles via a standardized form developed from the JBI extraction tool (JBI: The Joanna Briggs Institute 2017) (Figure 2). The second reviewer (FH) checked a sample of 20% of the charted data. Both reviewers (SJB and FH) discussed the results and updated the charting form in an iterative process.

Data was analysed using the PCC framework (The Joanna Briggs Institute 2015) which was valuable in addressing our broad, exploratory question that goes beyond interventions or outcomes alone but how they integrate into developing a stroke nursing role within an ESD team in the community. This framework allowed us to comprise a thematic summary of the findings extracted from the included studies to answer the research question stated above. It became apparent in the literature that there were key moments where the absence of a nursing role post stroke in the community led to challenges for patients. Structured, nurse‐led interventions focusing on secondary prevention, including education on risk factors, lifestyle changes and medication compliance via in‐person or telephone were recurring patterns and key themes.

Results

The search identified 2535 articles, after removing 838 duplicate articles, 1697 articles were screened and 1611 were excluded during abstract screening. The full text of 86 articles was assessed and 61 were excluded due to various reasons (Figure 1). There was a total of 25 articles, published between 2000 and 2022 including 6 from the United States, 6 from Sweden, 3 from the United Kingdom, 2 from The Netherlands and 1 each from Canada, China, Denmark, France, Scotland, Japan, Taiwan. Studies included were RCT (n = 9), intervention designs (n = 4), prospective cohort studies (n = 2), and study protocols for RCTs (n = 3), overview (n = 1), quasi‐experimental (n = 1), systematic review (n = 1), RCT sub‐study (n = 1), descriptive review (n = 1), qualitative review (n = 1), and evaluation (n = 1). These articles described nurse‐led stroke aftercare in the community setting (Figure 2). Through charting the data, the themes that emerged have allowed the researchers to develop a narrative synthesis of the literature (Munn et al. 2018).

Absence of Nursing Role Following Hospital Discharge

11 articles (Kozub 2010; Sit et al. 2007; Lawrence et al. 2012; Jönsson et al. 2014; Mayo et al. 2000; Mitchell et al. 2022; Duncan et al. 2017; Towfighi et al. 2021; Irewall et al. 2021; Mendyk et al. 2018; Allen et al. 2009) described how the traditional approach to secondary prevention patient education leads to a lack of engagement from stroke patients in their rehabilitation phase and poses challenges to stroke patients when trying to involve patients to be advocates of their own health and adopting healthy habits for stroke reduction (Sit et al. 2007). A systematic review of three studies indicated that long‐term strategies to assist patients should be implemented such as educating patients regarding their stroke, their prescription medication including the importance of adherence and the Face‐Arms‐Speech‐Time campaign (Lawrence et al. 2012). In addition to and as part of secondary prevention, education regarding symptom recognition and the need to seek emergency treatment of stroke is important (Kozub 2010). A growing number of randomised control studies have addressed the issue that early supported discharge services comprising home‐based rehabilitation services and medical services are optimal for better patient outcomes (Widén Holmqvist et al. 1998; Rodgers et al. 1997; Rudd et al. 1997).

One study demonstrated that secondary prevention after stroke described how patients received oral and written information prior to hospital discharge and they required follow‐up interventions and counselling to deal with the changes in their life situation when they went home (Jönsson et al. 2014). Another concern highlighted was that without stroke nurse community follow‐up, patients did not follow up with their GP for medication review following stroke (Jönsson et al. 2014).

Stroke Nursing in Secondary Prevention and Management of Risk Factors (Modifiable and Non‐Modifiable)

Ten of the 25 articles (Kozub 2010; Sit et al. 2007; Lawrence et al. 2012; Verberne et al. 2022; Jönsson et al. 2014; Mayo et al. 2000; Wang et al. 2013; Flemming et al. 2013; Allen et al. 2009; Ellis et al. 2005) discussed the role of the community stroke nurse in providing secondary prevention of risk factors relating to hypertension, smoking, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, physical inactivity, obesity, alcohol, drug use, atrial fibrillation, oral contraceptive use, carotid disease (Kozub 2010; Lawrence et al. 2012). The inclusion of a behavioural component to education post‐stroke was the most used intervention (Hanger and Wilkinson 2001; Johnson and Pearson 2000; Sahyoun et al. 2004; Yip et al. 2004), with motivational interviewing used in one study in stroke patients with severe cognitive impairment (Flemming et al. 2013).

Five articles (Sit et al. 2007; Verberne et al. 2022; Jönsson et al. 2014; Mayo et al. 2000; Wang et al. 2013) described how the stroke nurse specialist in the community could structure the secondary prevention programme in relation to general risk factors. In the intervention groups, secondary prevention programmes focused on individual goal setting and action plans; participants select the risk behaviours they want to focus on, setting practical short‐term goals. In these studies, the risk factor management interventions were delivered by experienced stroke nurses and consultations with these nurses were on average 45–60 min, and over a 4‐week period on average, 2.5 nursing visits were received (Sit et al. 2007; Jönsson et al. 2014; Mayo et al. 2000). The stroke nurse provided education and support to patients on the Face Arms Speech Time campaign and information on their own personal stroke (Sit et al. 2007; Jönsson et al. 2014). The nurse recorded information regarding post‐stroke problems including fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, sleeping problems, vision deterioration, dizziness, imbalance, urological symptoms post‐stroke, medication compliance, driving, weight, nutrition, physical activity (Sit et al. 2007; Jönsson et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2013).

Role of the Stroke Nurse in Secondary Prevention‐ Blood Pressure Management

Seven studies (Sit et al. 2007; Kerry et al. 2008; Hornnes et al. 2011; Ovaisi et al. 2013; Ovaisi et al. 2011; Towfighi et al. 2021; Irewall et al. 2021; Ellis et al. 2005) described positive patient outcomes when receiving interventions from expert stroke nurses in the community with post‐stroke patients specifically in relation to blood pressure management. In the blood pressure‐specific post‐discharge monitoring studies, nurses visited the patients at home within 2 weeks–1 month following discharge and the patients' blood pressure was taken during these visits, If consistently elevated, specific actions were taken such as titration of their antihypertensive medications (Kerry et al. 2008; Irewall et al. 2021). In cases of elevated blood pressure, the nurse encouraged patients to consult their general practitioners with the recorded readings (Hornnes et al. 2011; Irewall et al. 2021). In cases where medication was missed, the nurse provided education on antihypertensive compliance (Kerry et al. 2008; Hornnes et al. 2011). The intervention group exhibited improvement in self‐health monitoring of blood pressure practices, with a rate of 83% compared to the control group's 43%. In one study (Hornnes et al. 2011) among 98 patients with high blood pressure, 92% followed the protocol and attended their general practitioner, resulting in medication alterations for 29%.

Authors Towfighi et al. (2021) described the role of the nurse in prescribing and titrating medications, emphasizing adherence, reinforcing self‐management skills, and providing stroke education. The intervention group exhibited significant improvements, with a decrease in mean systolic blood pressure, enhanced self‐reported reduction of salt intake, increased use of blood pressure monitors and enhanced statin utilisation compared to the control group (Towfighi et al. 2021).

Role of the Stroke Nursing Following Discharge Home

Five papers (Kozub 2010; Verberne et al. 2022; Jönsson et al. 2014; Flemming et al. 2013; Ellis et al. 2005) discussed how stroke patients receiving interventions from expert stroke nurses are a valuable addition to the usual stroke service. Routine follow‐up by nurses could systemically address the psychosocial outcomes that are recommended by current guidelines; however, these are not routinely provided (Verberne et al. 2022; Norrving et al. 2018). Participants reported that a contact person who is known to them, approachable and timely was important to them (Wang et al. 2013). In a study of 107 post‐stroke patients, a risk assessment and action plan including health counselling were conducted by a community nurse (Kozub 2010). The health counselling included providing education to patients to individually reduce their risk factors for stroke and instructions to call emergency services if they experience symptoms of a stroke. Participants were surveyed at 1, 3 and 6 months post intervention (Kozub 2010). At 6 months, patients' knowledge of their personal stroke increased from 52% to 98%, their knowledge of the Face Arms Speech Time campaign increased from 68% to 100%. 54% changed to a low fat low salt diet, 23% began weight loss measures and 34% had more awareness and took a more active role in monitoring their cholesterol (Kozub 2010). This study also highlighted the role that the stroke nurse in the community has regarding smoking cessation as 4 out of 7 smokers in the intervention group were attempting to quit (Kozub 2010).

Further studies report the positive impact of a stroke nurse in the community providing health promotion in relation to lifestyle and secondary prevention, reducing rehospitalisation of stroke patients once they are discharged home (Jönsson et al. 2014). Stroke community nurses that have prescribing authority may be a useful link between the stroke unit, the patient in the community, and the treating consultant. Early intervention post discharge regarding the side effects of medications and medication compliance is important (Jönsson et al. 2014). In one study where 92% of patients in an intervention group received information folders in the community by a nurse, only 16% required further follow‐up and 11% were referred on to other relevant healthcare professions (Verberne et al. 2022). The role of the stroke nurse in the community results in improved links between the hospital, primary care, and the community care of nursing home residents (Jönsson et al. 2014).

Telephone Specific Interventions

Five studies (Jönsson et al. 2014; Irewall et al. 2015; Mendyk et al. 2018; Ögren et al. 2018; Irewall et al. 2019) reported that nurse‐led telephone‐based secondary prevention follow‐up is more effective than standard care follow‐up in achieving modifiable risk factor levels for stroke. In two studies (Jönsson et al. 2014; Irewall et al. 2015), the nurse involved in the study contacted all participants at 1‐ and 12‐months post‐discharge. Prior to each telephone call, participants underwent blood pressure and lipid profile assessments at their nearest healthcare facility. The intervention follow‐up by the nurse included sharing information on blood pressure and lipid profile results, providing lifestyle counselling including physical activity, diet, smoking cessation and assessing medication compliance (Jönsson et al. 2014; Irewall et al. 2015). In contrast, the control group telephone contact lacked intervention, and blood pressure and lipid profile measurements were sent to the patients' general practitioners for assessment. In the intervention group, participants received additional follow‐up, involving medication titration and lifestyle counselling tailored to achieve treatment goals and target blood pressure and lipid profile values. Notably, at the 12‐month follow‐up, the intervention group exhibited a significant decrease in mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared to the control group (Jönsson et al. 2014; Irewall et al. 2015). Moreover, a larger proportion of intervention group participants achieved the systolic blood pressure target compared to the control group. The lipid profile levels showed a favourable trend, decreasing in the intervention group while increasing in the control group.

Going beyond the 12‐month follow‐up, a longer‐term study (Mendyk et al. 2018) is evaluating a structured nurse follow‐up post‐discharge, involving monthly telephone interviews during the first year and transitioning to every 3 months in the second year. Additional training sessions were scheduled at the 6, 12, and 24‐month marks for both patients and caregivers. In the intervention group, before discharge, participants and their caregivers engaged in two comprehensive 1‐h sessions addressing various aspects, including diet, exercise, smoking cessation, alcohol cessation, medication adherence, blood pressure management, and blood sugar management, with the program aligning with international guidelines. Upon discharge, participants received a brochure outlining key information. Monthly telephone follow‐ups were conducted during the initial 6 months post‐discharge. The primary outcome involves measuring systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure at 6 months, 1 year, and 2‐year visits by a nurse. Secondary outcomes included assessing the lipid profile, diet, waist circumference, medication compliance, and exercise habits. This multifaceted intervention sought to comprehensively address key factors in post‐discharge care, emphasising a holistic and guideline‐based approach to enhance patient outcomes.

One study (Ögren et al. 2018) implemented a comprehensive assessment of blood pressure and lipid levels at healthcare facilities over a span of 1, 12, 24, and 36 months post‐discharge. The study nurse reviewed both the intervention group and control group regarding their adherence to prescribed plans via telephone. The intervention group received telephone‐based counselling, coupled with a thorough evaluation of their pharmacological treatment. Individualised medication titration took place where necessary, with follow‐up occurring every 4 weeks via telephone until optimal outcomes were achieved. In contrast, the control group had treatment initiated during hospitalisation, with subsequent follow‐up conducted by the patients' general practitioners. The results indicated noteworthy disparities between the groups. In the intervention group, the mean systolic blood pressure was 121.8 mmHg, contrasting with the control group's 134.2 mmHg, and the diastolic blood pressure was 75.3 mmHg in the intervention group compared to 78.8 mmHg in the control group. Additionally, the intervention group exhibited a decrease in lipid profile by 0.2 mmol/L, while the control group experienced a 1.0 mmol/L increase. The difference in mean systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure widened during the first year but remained stable until the 36‐month mark (Ögren et al. 2018).

Blended Model‐ Telephone Calls and Home Visits

Seven (Bushnell et al. 2014; Boter et al. 2004; Mitchell et al. 2022; Fukuoka et al. 2015; Duncan et al. 2017; Flemming et al. 2013; Allen et al. 2009) out of the 25 articles discussed a blended model of both nurse home visits and telephone calls for patients post stroke. This blended model demonstrated its effectiveness in addressing the diverse needs of patients and carers, contributing to improved outcomes over the follow‐up period (Bushnell et al. 2014; Boter et al. 2004; Fukuoka et al. 2015; Duncan et al. 2017). Stroke nurses conducted face‐to‐face follow‐up and engaged in telephone follow‐up employing a standardised assessment that includes screening for new stroke symptoms, evaluating depression and falls risk, tracking rehabilitation progress, confirming access to primary care, and discussing diagnostic test results from the inpatient stay (Bushnell et al. 2014). The interventions addressed various baseline risk factors, including blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol, smoking, alcohol intake, and psychological factors (Fukuoka et al. 2015; Duncan et al. 2017). Patients and carers could participate in both the telephone calls and home visits. The nurses provided their contact information for additional support outside of scheduled times (Boter et al. 2004). These blended programs empowered patients and carers to advocate for themselves and enhance their adjustment to new limitations, supplemented by informal booklets and information leaflets (Boter et al. 2004). Patients were actively involved in their care, undertaking self‐measurements of blood pressure, weight, and lifestyle changes, and documenting these observations in the intervention group (Fukuoka et al. 2015). In contrast, the control group received conventional follow‐up care (Fukuoka et al. 2015; Duncan et al. 2017). Nurses documented time spent, problems addressed, and interventions applied, which were categorised as advising, informing, reassuring, encouraging, referring, sending/giving brochures, or other (including supportive listening). Results from 166 patients and 118 carers revealed positive outcomes, with only 7% of patients and 1% of carers finding the home visit unhelpful (Boter et al. 2004).

In one healthcare intervention study (Wang et al. 2013), the stroke nurses did not make prescriptive changes, but they alerted physicians when the initiation or modification of medications was deemed necessary. The stroke nurse interventions included individualised risk factor and behaviour education, dietary and exercise assessments, goal setting reviews, medication adherence checks, and education on the Face Arms Speech Time campaign for stroke symptoms (Flemming et al. 2013). The outcomes included a significant improvement in smoking cessation, with 2 out of 5 smokers in the intervention group quitting by week 6 compared to none quitting in the control group (Flemming et al. 2013). Statin compliance remained consistently high in both groups.

An expert stroke nurse clinic appointment in‐person or via telehealth was scheduled within 7–14 days of discharge, or sooner if deemed necessary for patients (Mitchell et al. 2022; Duncan et al. 2017; Allen et al. 2009). Nursing interventions included monitoring blood pressure, setting personalised risk factor goals, and determining the need for further stroke‐related investigations such as cardiac or blood pressure monitoring (Mitchell et al. 2022; Duncan et al. 2017; Allen et al. 2009). The results of the intervention were notable; there was a reduction in the time to clinic follow‐up from 42 days pre‐intervention to 30 days post‐intervention. Additionally, the post‐intervention group had a lower readmission rate of 9.9%, compared to the pre‐intervention group which had a 12% readmission rate within 30 days of discharge (Mitchell et al. 2022). One study reported that the most common post‐discharge problems requiring intervention by the expert stroke nurse were medication issues, uncontrolled blood pressure, depression, incontinence, visual problems, sleep disturbances, constipation, dizziness, swallowing difficulties, and falls (Allen et al. 2009). On average, each intervention group patient presented with 3 problems post discharge and on average the stroke nurse spent 4 h per patient (Allen et al. 2009).

Discussion

The role of the stroke nurse specialist delivering interventions such as education regarding modifiable risk factors, medication compliance, blood pressure management and informal psychological support to stroke patients in the community reflected positive outcomes for patients in the 25 studies. There was depth of information provided on the role of the clinical nurse specialist in stroke in the community in the 25 articles discussed which is beneficial to developing transferable guidelines and a pathway for the role of the stroke clinical nurse specialist in early supported discharge.

Stroke survivors and caregivers have reported concerns with the lack of follow‐up after stroke hospitalisation (Rath et al. 2020). Early supported discharge is the current standard of care in the UK and Canada using a hospital‐based MDT approach to care (Duncan et al. 2017). Discharge home without support services is not feasible or safe and patients remain as inpatients for longer than necessary (Mayo et al. 2000). Core components of the studies discussed in this review were the nurse home visits, and nurse telephone support provided to participants. These studies highlighted that where the nurse visited patients, this benefitted patients who may not have had transport to attend hospitals or support groups. One element in the management of stroke is to facilitate community reintegration and the sooner the patient can be discharged home, the sooner the patient can be reintegrated into the community (Mayo et al. 2000). Poststroke discharge care can often be fragmented due to the lack of an established care model and guidelines that ensure comprehensive assessment and follow‐up for common problems post‐stroke (Allen et al. 2009). This is where the expert stroke nurse has a role to play in the community, bridging the gap between discharge from hospital and follow‐up in the community. This can provide cost savings by reducing costs related to post‐stroke morbidity while at the same time improving patients' health outcomes and quality of life (Mendyk et al. 2018). It is important to optimise secondary preventative strategies in stroke so that as much prevention as possible can be achieved with minimal resources. Early supported discharge teams with a nurse specialist as part of the team provide efficiency, effectiveness, and value. This is important from a health economics point of view (Irewall et al. 2021). This approach ensures that healthcare resources are allocated effectively to improve patient outcomes and overall population health.

Clinical recommendations for stroke prevention are grouped by lifestyle modification, major risk factor intervention and preventative pharmacological intervention (Lawrence et al. 2012). Although healthcare professionals are aware of risk factors and secondary prevention in relation to stroke, the general population often lacks an understanding of these risk factors (Kozub 2010). Modifiable risk factors are targeted in secondary prevention programmes as they are often linked to persons' lifestyle behaviours (Kozub 2010). Evidence‐based guidelines recommend that secondary prevention interventions should include both the prescription of medication along with the active provision of information and education (Johnson and Pearson 2000; Galobardes et al. 2006). In all the intervention groups, the educational interventions carried out by the stroke nurses were designed to enable patients to advocate for their own health and adopt healthy habits for stroke reduction through participating in a learn–practice–feedback–learn approach (Hanger and Wilkinson 2001). Educational interventions by the stroke nurse in the community empower patients with the knowledge to provide self‐care and to continue self‐monitoring long term (Lawrence et al. 2012). The findings from this review highlight that expert stroke nurses providing interventions via telephone or in person are effective in achieving positive patient outcomes.

Post‐hospitalisation education secondary prevention programme for patients should be inclusive and adapted to the appropriate format for the patient to use and understand, as they navigate life post‐stroke. This review has demonstrated that following a structured nurse‐led intervention, there was a significant improvement in stroke recovery whereas no significant progress was made in the recovery of control groups who received no nursing intervention. Delivering nursing interventions in the home environment improved outcomes in terms of the effectiveness of early discharge home combined with home rehabilitation for function, community reintegration and health‐related quality of life. The role of the stroke nurse extends beyond providing practical support to patients but also involves providing informal psychological care to patients. The home environment allows for the needs of the patient and the family to be met, instilling a greater degree of confidence in the patient and their family about their functional ability in the house and the community.

Conclusions

Nurses are key health educators in stroke programme prevention implementation optimising nurse‐led management of post‐stroke issues such as medication and blood pressure management issues. Involving an expert stroke nurse in an already established therapy model of early supported discharge teams would lead to improved patient health outcomes, increased patient satisfaction, decreased resource use and decreased use of healthcare services, preventing patient readmission to hospital.

This scoping review has focused on how nurses in community settings can play a role in delivering a personalised and comprehensive stroke secondary prevention education to patients and the potential benefits to patients. Further investigation into the current role of the stroke clinical nurse specialists and advanced nurse practitioners should be explored to provide insight into the current practice and what could be transferred to the role of the stroke clinical nurse specialist in early supported discharge. This would enable a pathway to be developed and a secondary prevention education model to be explored.

Recommendations for Practice

To enhance community stroke care, it is recommended to integrate stroke nurse specialists into Early Supported Discharge (ESD) teams to aid smooth hospital‐to‐home transitions and support early recovery. Structured, nurse‐led interventions should focus on secondary prevention, including education on risk factors and lifestyle changes and medication compliance. Care delivery should be flexible, offering both in‐person and remote options and including psychological support as a core component. Clear care pathways, standardised protocols, and outcome monitoring are essential to ensure consistency and quality. Emphasis should also be placed on promoting health literacy, self‐management, and equitable access.

Limitations

There are limitations to this scoping review. This is a novel scoping review and there are no previous reviews completed of early supported discharge nurse specialists and their role; therefore there is no statistically significant unanimous evidence in the literature reviewed. There was a lack of specificity across the studies. There is assumed transferability with this role; however it does not consider variations in healthcare systems, patient populations, or resources across different countries. This study included only English papers. Translating non‐English studies would have required additional time, cost, and expertise, which was not feasible within the scope or funding of the project. Our research team would have lacked the ability to critically appraise and accurately interpret studies in other languages potentially compromising data quality.
