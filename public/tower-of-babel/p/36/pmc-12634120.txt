Evaluating administrative measures of school quality as mediators of the relationship between attending a segregated school and cognitive function among older Black individuals: the STAR study
Authors: Sirena Gutierrez, Rachel A Whitmer, Marilyn D Thomas, Kristen M George, Rachel Peterson, Lisa L Barnes, Isabel E Allen, M Maria Glymour, Jacqueline M Torres, Paola Gilsanz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearch highlights school segregation's impact on cognitive aging for older Black adults, yet the mediating role of school quality—reflecting systemic (dis)investment in segregated schools—remains unexplored. This study included 726 community-dwelling Black adults from the Study of Healthy Aging in African Americans. Participants self-reported segregated school attendance, while administrative measures of state-level school quality (term length, percent attendance, student–teacher ratio, composite z score) were linked to their grade-specific state of residence. We estimated the extent to which associations between segregated schooling and domain-specific cognition were mediated by school quality. Sensitivity analyses examined grade-specific effects. Attending a segregated school was associated with poorer school quality (e.g., βterm-length = −1.71 [−2.52, −0.91]) and lower semantic memory (β = −0.17 [−0.32, −0.02]). The school quality composite measure mediated 30% of the overall association with semantic memory (natural indirect effect: β = −0.05 [−0.09, −0.01]; direct effect: β = −0.14 [−0.30, 0.02]). Total effect estimates were imprecise for executive function and verbal episodic memory. Our results suggest that state-level (dis)investments in school quality may be an important mechanism by which school-based segregation contributes to late-life cognitive function. Interventions that target the upstream, structural drivers of school-based segregation and related disinvestments may be important strategies for reducing cognitive aging inequities.

Introduction

Extensive literature has established an association between education and late-life cognitive function,1-6 noting education as a plausible driver of cognitive and brain reserve.7,8 However, in the United States (US), educational experiences are highly racialized, stemming from a history of legalized school segregation spanning from the 1840s to the 1950s due to Jim Crow laws and structural racism beyond the South.9 The legacy of school segregation persists today as a result of an accumulation of discriminatory policies and systems (e.g., redlining, mortgage lending, school zoning, and funding), resulting in racially and socioeconomically stratified neighborhoods and, consequently, schools.10,11 Inequalities in educational experiences are a significant aspect of institutional racism in the US, although racial inequities in dementia persist even after accounting for educational attainment.12 Racialized school-related exposures beyond attainment, including school quality, can influence later-life cognitive functioning through structural and biopsychosocial pathways, leading to an increased risk of cognitive impairment.9 However, research on how school segregation shapes late-life cognitive outcomes is limited, with no studies rigorously evaluating the underlying mechanisms. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use causal mediation techniques to disentangle the role of administrative school quality indicators in the association of segregated school attendance and cognitive function in older Black Americans.

Structural racism operates through systems of power and historical disinvestment to produce racially segregated schools, which may negatively influence late-life cognitive outcomes by limiting access to quality education.13 Scholars have hypothesized that school segregation may adversely impact cognitive aging via mechanisms rooted in inequitable investments and resources allocated to schools primarily attended by minoritized students; as a consequence, segregated schools were more likely to experience shorter term lengths, lower attendance rates, overcrowded classrooms, and reduced teacher salaries, which are all key indicators of school quality.9,14 Despite some evidence suggesting a narrowing of racial school quality gaps,15,16 average funding per student was 1.6 times higher for White students than African American students in Southern states during 1951 to 1952.17 Improvements in school quality are resource-demanding, as students and teachers may spend more time in classrooms, reflecting the financial resources available to schools and correlating with more material covered and potentially more learning opportunities.18 While racially segregated schools often receive fewer resources due to systemic inequalities and discriminatory practices,15,16,19-21 it remains unclear how much of the impact of school segregation is due to this inequitable distribution of resources (relative to other mechanisms such as discrimination), which could be modified through various policies governing school funding and resource allocation. Details on the pathways linking school segregation and quality to cognitive aging can be found in Appendix S1.

This study expands prior research by investigating whether administrative school quality indicators—reflecting school resources—mediate the relationship between attending a segregated school and late-life cognitive function among older Black adults using causal mediation methods.22,23 We hypothesized that educational quality would account for a significant portion of the variation in the association between segregated school attendance and domain-specific late-life cognitive performance. However, we expected that measures of school quality would not completely explain this association, as state-level administrative indicators of educational quality do not capture the nuanced racialized educational experiences of Black Americans. Drawing on early childhood research,24 we hypothesized that school quality would be more relevant in earlier grades (e.g., greater proportion of the effect of segregation would be mediated by primary school quality). Additionally, we did not expect the indirect effect of school quality to be specific to a particular cognitive domain, given the universal benefit of school quality on multiple aspects of cognitive development and lifecourse cognitive performance.

Methods

Study population

Data came from the Study of Healthy Aging among African Americans (STAR) cohort, comprised of community-dwelling older adults residing in California’s San Francisco Bay area, predominantly in Oakland and Richmond. The STAR cohort is a longitudinal study of Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC) members who previously participated in the Multiphasic Health Checkup exams between 1964 and 1985, identified as Black or African American, and were aged 50 years or older on January 1, 2018 (mean age, 68 years; range, 53-94 years; 69% female). The study obtained approval from the KPNC Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided informed consent. Information about the study design, data collection, and methodology has been previously published.25,26

We used data from Wave 1 of the STAR cohort. From the 764 individuals at baseline in STAR, we excluded participants without information on segregated school attendance and school quality indicators for at least one grade (n = 9). We additionally excluded participants missing any of the baseline cognitive scores and anyone missing covariate information (n = 29). The final analytic sample for the overall analyses using information from all grades includes 726 participants. Grade-specific analyses excluded individuals without information on school segregation or school quality indicators for a given grade; grade-specific analytic sample sizes were 695 for 1st grade, 708 for 6th grade, 688 for 9th grade, and 599 for 12th grade. See Figure S1 for overall and grade-specific data flowcharts.

Measures

Segregated school attendance

During Wave 1 interviews, participants were first asked if they attended school in grades 1, 6, 9, and 12. For each grade participants reported having attended, they were asked, “Was this a segregated school?” For each grade, we created a binary variable (1 = yes, segregated; 0 = not segregated) indicating whether participants self-reported if they attended a segregated school at a given grade (e.g., attended a segregated school in 1st grade vs. did not attend a segregated school in 1st grade). Participants who did not attend a specific school grade were set to missing for these grade-specific measures.

We also created a binary variable (1 = yes, attended a segregated school; 0 = no, never attended a segregated school) indicating whether participants self-reported if they ever attended a segregated school in any grade vs. never attended a segregated school across all grades.

State-level school quality

We assessed three state-level administrative indicators of school quality as potential mediators: school term length (i.e., days in the academic year), student–teacher ratio (i.e., ratio of students to instructional staff), and attendance rates (i.e., fraction of enrolled children who attended on an average day). Average state-level educational quality data from 1905 to 1973 was obtained from various sources, including the Federal Digest of Educational Statistics and the Biennial Survey of Education in the United States.27 A previously compiled data set28 extracted four key data elements for each state from these historical data sources: number of teachers, enrolled students, days in the academic year, and average daily attendance to derive year- and state-specific school quality indicators. Fewer students per teacher, longer-term lengths, and higher attendance rates are conceptualized as reflecting higher school quality, as these conditions may allow students to receive more personalized attention and extended instructional times, leading to better academic outcomes.15,29-31 Further details on data extraction and interpolation have been previously described elsewhere.28

Each individual was linked to the state-level educational quality indicators using their birth year and self-reported school addresses for grades 1, 6, 9, and 12. We used the national average age of students at various grade levels32 in conjunction with the participants' birth year to estimate the year corresponding to the participants’ start of that grade level using September as the commonly used cutoff for age enrollment requirements (i.e., average age at that grade + birth year = year they attended that grade). We also created an average for each school quality indicator across all grades the participant attended (i.e., by summing the values across each grade divided by the number of grades attended).

Because the grade-specific education quality measures were highly correlated with each other (Table S1), we created grade-specific composite school quality scores that were the average of standardized (z scored) versions of the three education quality measures to the baseline sample. An overall composite school quality score was created by averaging the grade-specific composite school quality z scores across all grades the participant attended.

Cognitive function

Baseline cognitive function was assessed during in-person interviews with the Spanish and English Neuropsychological Assessment Scales (SENAS).33,34 We calculated domain-specific scores for verbal episodic memory (personal event recall), semantic memory (general factual knowledge), and executive function (problem-solving through organized thought); see Appendix S2 for details. Each cognitive domain score was standardized using the baseline sample to produce domain-specific z scores.

Covariates

We considered confounders that preceded experiences of school segregation and school quality. Potential confounders included baseline age (continuous), sex/gender (male/female), birth in the Southern US, and parental education. To account for geographical contextual differences that may have influenced early childhood experiences, we adjusted for being born in the southern US, where historical systemic sociopolitical conditions, such as Jim Crow laws, were in effect. For details on the definition of the covariates, see Appendix S2.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses compared school quality measures and covariates by segregated school attendance (Table 1). We first estimated the total association between segregated school attendance on domain-specific cognitive function, adjusting for covariates. We next evaluated whether school segregation measures were associated with administrative educational quality measures. Lastly, we calculated natural direct effect (NDE) and natural indirect effect (NIE) estimates of the overall segregated school attendance–cognitive function relationship.

Briefly, calculating the NDE and NIE required the specification of two models (see Appendix S2 for details). The first model regressed a specific measure of school quality (the mediator) on self-reported segregated school attendance (the exposure) and confounders of the exposure–mediator relationship. Predictions from this model were used to estimate the counterfactual mediator values for each respondent at counterfactual exposure values.

The second model regressed cognitive performance z scores (the outcome) on segregated school attendance, school quality, mediator–outcome confounders, and cross-product terms of the exposure and mediator. Counterfactual mediator values generated for each respondent based on the first model were used as inputs to this second model to predict outcomes under counterfactual exposure and mediator values.

The NDE was defined as the mean difference in domain-specific cognition contrasting scenarios in which 1) the full sample experienced school segregation and 2) the full sample did not experience school segregation, with state-level school quality set to values observed for respondents who did not experience segregation for both scenarios. The NIE was defined as the mean difference in domain-specific cognition under the scenario in which everyone had experienced school segregation, but contrasting values of state-level school quality levels observed for 1) respondents who did experience segregation and 2) for respondents who did not experience segregation. The proportion mediated was computed as the ratio of the NIE divided by the total effect. Percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated by repeating the analysis on 2000 resamples with replacement.

We performed several sensitivity analyses, including utilizing school quality variables generated with an alternate specification of the interpolation models. Additionally, to address potential residual bias from early-life conditions (e.g., other concurrent state-level policies), we performed a quantitative bias analysis by calculating e-values to estimate the minimum strength of unmeasured confounding (between both exposure–outcome and mediator–outcome relationships) needed to explain away the observed non-null total, indirect, and direct effect estimates.35 To better isolate the role of state-level policies driving inequities, we additionally adjusted for state-level average housing values for when participants were attending schools.

Analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Sample code is available on GitHub (https://github.com/sirenagtz/SchoolQualityMediation).

Results

Descriptives

Participants who had school segregation and school quality information for at least one grade averaged 68.8 (8.8 ± SD) years of age at their first interview and 70% were women (Table 1). Over one-third were born in a Southern state, and over half had at least one parent who completed high school. These sociodemographic characteristics followed similar patterns for the analytic samples constructed for grade-specific analyses.

Compared to those who did not attend a segregated school, participants who attended a segregated school were more likely to have been born in the South (e.g., 74% vs. 19% for 1st-grade analytical sample) and had less educated parents (e.g., 50% vs. 74% had at least one parent who completed high school for 1st-grade analytical sample) (Table 1).

Total association between school segregation on cognition

Ever attending a segregated school

Ever attending a segregated school (vs. never) was associated with lower semantic memory z scores (β = −0.17; 95% CI, −0.32 to −0.02). However, associations with executive function (β = −0.05; 95% CI, −0.20 to 0.10) and verbal episodic memory z scores (β = 0.08; 95% CI, −0.08 to 0.23) were null and imprecise (Table 2).

Grade-specific exposure to segregated schooling

While patterns were generally similar for grade-specific analyses, there were some deviations (Table S2). Attending a segregated school in 1st grade (β = −0.23; 95% CI, −0.39 to −0.06) and 6th grade (β = −0.31; 95% CI, −0.47 to −0.15) were each associated with lower semantic memory z scores. However, estimates were imprecise for attending a segregated school in 9th and 12th grade (β9th-grade = −0.15; 95% CI, −0.31 to 0.02 and β12th-grade = −0.11; 95% CI, −0.29 to 0.07). Attending a segregated school in 6th grade was associated with lower executive function z scores, but estimates were imprecise (β6th-grade = −0.15; 95% CI, −0.31 to 0.01) and crossed the null. There was no evidence of associations with other grades. All grade-specific associations with verbal episodic memory z scores were null.

Exposure–mediator relationship between school segregation and quality

Ever attending a segregated school

Ever attending a segregated school (vs. never) was associated with shorter average term length (β = −1.71; 95% CI, −2.52 to −0.91), lower average percent attendance (β = −0.02; 95% CI, −0.03 to −0.02), and a larger average student–teacher ratio (β = 1.00; 95% CI, 0.63 to 1.37), as well as a lower school quality composite z score (β = −0.62; 95% CI, −0.82 to −0.41; Table S3).

Grade-specific exposure to segregated schooling

Associations between grade-specific segregated school attendance and corresponding grade-specific school quality indicators were similar (Table S3).

Mediation analysis

Ever attending a segregated school

Under the assumptions for mediation analyses,22,36 the estimated direct effect of ever attending a segregated school, not mediated by an overall composite school quality z score, was −0.14 (95% CI, −0.30 to 0.02) for semantic memory. The composite school quality z score mediated or explained 30% of the association between ever attending a segregated school and semantic memory z scores (indirect effect: β = −0.05; 95% CI, −0.09 to −0.01) (Panel D, Table 2; Figure 1). Patterns were similar when examining the associations between individual indicators and semantic memory (Panels A-C, Table 2; Figure 1), and the proportion mediated by ranged from 33% for average term length to 46% for average percent attendance.

Although total associations between segregated school attendance and executive function and verbal episodic memory z scores were null, we proceeded with mediation analyses, as contemporary frameworks recognize that direct and indirect effects can plausibly offset one another (e.g., the detrimental impact of inequitable funding versus the psychosocial benefits of being shielded from interpersonal discrimination in integrated schools)—allowing for the interpretation of indirect effects even in the absence of a total effect.36 Our results generally confirm the absence of direct associations between segregated school attendance and executive function and verbal episodic memory z scores or indirect associations via school quality (Table 2; Figure 1). However, there was evidence of an indirect effect of the composite school quality z score on the overall association between segregated school attendance and executive function (β = −0.04; 95% CI, −0.07 to −0.00) explaining 69% of the association.

Grade-specific exposure to segregated schooling

Attending a segregated school during 1st and 6th grades had significant total, direct, and indirect effects on semantic memory z scores of relatively larger magnitudes to the overall exposure to segregated schooling (Table 3; Figure 2). We estimated that 21% to 26% of the association between attending a segregated school during those grades on semantic memory is mediated by school quality measures (NIE: β1st-grade = −0.05; 95% CI, −0.12 to 0.01; β6th-grade = −0.08; 95% CI, −0.15 to −0.01 and NDE: β1st-grade = −0.18; 95% CI, −0.36 to −0.01; β6th-grade = −0.21; 95% CI, −0.38 to −0.03). Attending a segregated school during 9th and 12th grades did not have significant total or direct effects (9th grade: β = −0.04; 95% CI, −0.23 to 0.14; 12th grade: β = −0.01; 95% CI, −0.21 to 0.21) but did have significant indirect effects via school quality (9th grade: β = −0.08; 95% CI, −0.15 to −0.01; 12th grade: β = −0.07; 95% CI, −0.13 to −0.00; Table 3; Figure 2). We estimated that school quality in 9th and 12th grades mediated 56% and 63% of the effect of grade-specific segregated school attendance on semantic memory.

Generally, grade-specific estimates for the associations of segregated school attendance with late-life executive function and verbal episodic memory z scores were similar to the overall estimates (Table S4). Additionally, results were generally similar when evaluating specific indicators of school quality as opposed to the composite school quality z score (Figure S2; Tables S5-S7), with just a few exceptions (e.g., student–teacher ratio estimates, 6th grade).

Sensitivity analyses

Results were consistent when utilizing school quality measures that were interpolated using a state fixed effect for Brown v. Board of Education (Appendix S3); however, as expected, the proportion mediated was slightly inflated. Sensitivity analyses showed that to explain away our overall association, the relationship of an unmeasured confounder to both exposure and outcome would need to be 2.1 times greater than the largest school segregation-semantic memory coefficient (i.e., corresponding to the association between segregated school attendance at 6th grade and semantic memory z scores) (Appendixes S4-S5). When indirectly accounting for other state policies contributing to inequities among Black Americans (e.g., residential segregation) by adjusting for state-level housing values, the conclusions remained consistent, though point estimates were slightly attenuated (Table S8).

Discussion

In a cohort of Black Americans aged 50 years and older currently living in Northern California, attending a segregated school during 1st and 6th grades was associated with lower semantic memory z scores in later life but not with executive function or verbal episodic memory. Across all grades, participants who reported attending a segregated school attended schools in states with lower school quality indicators that reflect (dis)investment in schools. School quality mediated between 21% and 63% of the association of grade-specific segregated school attendance and semantic memory z scores. Despite null total associations of segregated schooling on executive function and verbal episodic memory, school quality measures had significant indirect effects across all cognitive domains. On the other hand, the associations between segregated schooling and domain-specific cognitive function were not fully explained through these administrative measures of school quality, suggesting that there may be other structural (e.g., policies shaping racial and economic stratification that restrict access to high-quality education) and contextual factors (e.g., interpersonal discrimination) associated with the segregated schooling experience. To our knowledge, ours is the first study to decompose the mediating effects of school quality on the association between self-reported school segregation and late-life domain-specific cognitive function.

Our findings of an association between exposure to segregated schooling and lower semantic memory in later life are consistent with prior studies,37-40 although, in our findings, the overall association was limited to the semantic memory domain. This may be because racialized schooling experiences have a greater influence on cognitive processes shaped by social and contextual life experiences—effects that may be better captured by measures of semantic memory.41,42 Similar to prior work, our findings suggested that a large portion of this association was not mediated via school quality (i.e., segregated primary schooling has a direct effect on semantic memory). Specifically, a recent study of Health and Retirement Study (HRS) participants who resided in the US South during primary school40 found that attending a desegregated primary school was associated with improved executive function among Black participants compared to those who attended segregated primary schools, even after adjusting for state-level education quality. Our study builds on that work by formally quantifying the mediating role of school quality through causal mediation methods that do not require the assumption of no exposure-mediator interaction.22 Indeed, we found evidence of a multiplicative interaction between segregated schooling and school quality in this cohort, underscoring the utility of modern causal mediation methods for this work.

Quantifying additional school characteristics that might explain the relationship between school segregation and late-life cognitive function is challenging, largely due to the limited availability of historical individual-level data on school environments in most public cohorts of older adults, particularly those that include relevant cognitive measures. Given that de facto school segregation reflects patterns of racially and socioeconomically stratified neighborhoods,43 the impact of school segregation could also stem from the systematic disinvestment in schools (e.g., inequitable resources, social capital) and beyond, influencing cognitive outcomes via mechanisms, such as healthcare and other built environment features.44 Additionally, students in desegregated schools could have been exposed to interpersonal racism from staff and students. Following the Brown v. Board of Education decision, there was a mass displacement of Black teachers across the nation.45 The lack of diversity in the teaching workforce could have implications for Black students' academic success and health,46 as student–teacher racial concordance has been associated with higher teacher expectations, culturally relevant instruction, and positive role modeling, all impacting educational success.47-49 Furthermore, interpersonal racism, especially in early childhood, could significantly affect health behaviors and late-life health outcomes.50 Though STAR did not collect this information, heterogeneous experiences from segregated schooling (e.g., shaped by varying aspects of structural racism) may have different cognitive implications, given that participants attended school between the 1930s and the early 1980s across the US.

Our study has several limitations. First, the school quality variables we used as mediators are rough proxies for participants’ schooling environment. These state-average measures do not comprehensively capture all domains of school quality or reflect conditions at the school or individual level. However, to our knowledge, historical records on administrative indicators of school quality are not available at the individual level. Furthermore, the sociopolitical climate and the legacy of racially segregated schooling went beyond the inequitable allocation of funding. Structural racism operates across macrolevel systems and institutions (e.g., education, neighborhoods, job sector, healthcare) that disenfranchises marginalized communities and perpetuates health inequities.13,51 Future research should examine additional potential mediators, including the role of socioeconomic factors in mid-life and unexamined aspects of the school and peer environments. Second, our models assumed that no other unmeasured confounders and that no mediator–outcome confounders were affected by exposure (i.e., no intermediate confounding). Given that the mediators are temporally closely tied with the exposure window (schooling age), we are less concerned about additional mediator–outcome confounders beyond the exposure–outcome confounders. However, there may be residual confounding of early-life experiences between the exposure to segregated schooling and late-life cognition. Our sensitivity analysis shows that the degree of confounding had to be moderate to completely explain away these associations. Third, measurement error in the self-reported exposure to segregated schooling and the administrative indicators of our school quality mediators may have attenuated our results and reduced power, thereby potentially contributing to our relatively weak indirect effects and null results.52,53 Fourth, differences between STAR participants and the broader US population may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, the study sample consisted of older adults currently residing in California, 49% were born in 35 states outside of California. Finally, due to our sample size and eligibility requirement of residing in CA in adulthood, we are limited in our ability to explore both regional and birth cohort heterogeneity in our estimates, but future work in larger, nationally representative samples should consider how heterogeneous experiences of school segregation based on time and place may differentially influence late-life health outcomes. Given the limited empirical research to date on the mechanisms linking school segregation to cognitive disparities, our findings offer an important contribution to the literature, lay the groundwork for future inquiry in larger and more diverse samples, and provide evidence that may be valuable in future meta-analyses.

The present study suggests that racial segregation of schools influenced select aspects of late-life cognitive function (i.e., semantic memory but not verbal episodic memory) among older Black adults and that this was partly related to disparities in school quality indicators reflecting systemic inequities in resource allocation. However, the relationship between segregated schools in early life and lower late-life semantic memory function also appears to be driven by mechanisms unrelated to available measures of school quality. This evidence suggests that structural interventions aimed at improving schooling environments and quality (e.g., reforming school funding formulas) may reduce adverse late-life cognitive function among younger cohorts of Black Americans as they age, considering the widespread prevalence of de facto school segregation today.54 However, our results also suggest that policies should also address the systemic factors that contributed to the unequal distribution of such investments, including zoning laws and mortgage policies that disenfranchise marginalized communities, and the systems that lead to racialized socioeconomic segregation (e.g., housing, employment, earnings), which reinforce the racial stratification of public schools across the US. Recent quasi-experimental work55 shows that attending a segregated school in the 1990s had an impact on the mid-life cardiovascular health of Black Americans, which is associated with greater dementia risk. Therefore, current de facto racial segregation of schools, if associated with lower school quality for Black children, is likely to result in worse cognitive aging, higher dementia burden, and persistent racial inequalities in dementia risk if no structural interventions are implemented.
