Extracellular Matrix (ECM)-Regulated Molecular Switches: Tissue Inhibitors of Metalloproteinases in Synaptic Formation and Neuropathic Diseases
Authors: Shuchang Qi, Siyao Wang, Yishan Tan, Chunlin Pan, Xueyun Bi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynaptic formation, the cornerstone of neurological function, underpins complex behaviors and cognitive processes, with structural/functional aberrations implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders and neurodegenerative pathologies. Synaptogenesis involves dynamic interplay between cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) and extracellular matrix (ECM) components, which collectively regulate neuronal connectivity and plasticity. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their endogenous inhibitors, tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), emerge as critical regulators of these processes through ECM remodeling and modulation of cell surface receptor signaling. This review synthesizes current understanding of ECM-TIMP-MMP axes in synaptic development, highlighting their dual roles in physiological plasticity and pathological disruption across neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease), neuro-oncological disorders, and neuroinflammatory conditions. By dissecting the context-dependent functions and therapeutic implications of TIMP family members in synaptic maintenance and disease progression, this work provides a conceptual framework for advancing TIMP-based neurotherapeutic strategies and a theoretical basis for future exploration of TIMP as a potential therapeutic target for neurological disorders.Keywords: TIMPs, ECM, MMPs, Synapse formation, Neuropathic diseases

Introduction

In the mature brain, trillions of synapses form the basis of nervous system function (Poggio 1990). Although the number of neurons in the mammalian brain is counted in billions, the number of synapses is even greater than this. A synapse is a specialized cellular connection between neurons, or between neurons and effector cells, which enables communication between cells through synaptic transmission (Walmsley et al. 1998). The intricate interconnection of neurons via these synapses constitutes a complex neural network, which underpins various physiological functions of the nervous system. Thus, investigating the formation and functions of synapses holds substantial scientific significance for elucidating the mechanisms of brain development and the progression of brain-related diseases. Additionally, it establishes a robust theoretical foundation for the clinical management of neurological disorders.

Synapse formation is a complex and dynamic process that involves a series of biological events and complex molecular mechanisms (Colón-Ramos 2009; Salie et al. 2005). The formation of synapses involves multiple developmental events, including cell fate specification, cell migration, axon guidance, dendritic growth, synaptic target selection, and synaptogenesis (Jüttner & Rathjen 2005; Waites et al. 2005). Any impairment in the synapse formation process may lead to neurodevelopmental disorders manifested as motor dysfunction, neuropsychiatric problems, and learning and/or language disorders (Doernberg & Hollander 2016). In addition, neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease (PD), and Huntington’s disease (HD), have been associated with synaptic abnormalities (Dejanovic et al. 2024; Ye et al. 2023). The underlying mechanisms of these disorders are complex and remain under active investigation; however, current evidence demonstrates their intricate association with synaptic physiology and multiple potential genetic factors contributing to its pathophysiological alterations. For example, it has been shown that the assembly of the presynaptic active zone involves several protein families, including RIMs (Rab3-Interacting Molecules), Munc13, RBPs (RIM-binding proteins), α-liprin, and ELKS proteins (also called CAZ-Associated Structural Proteins [CAST]) (Radulovic et al. 2020; Wang et al. 1997; Wu et al. 2021). These proteins form large protein complexes responsible for docking and preparing synaptic vesicles for exocytosis, recruiting calcium channels to docked and prepared vesicles, as well as playing a role in synaptic plasticity (Rizalar et al. 2021). As synapses continue to form in the mature nervous system and continue to undergo dynamic remodeling throughout adult life to support learning and memory formation, this process involves the adaptation of synaptic structure and function to the needs of the nervous system (Südhof 2018). While the precise mechanisms governing synaptogenesis remain incompletely elucidated, emerging research over recent decades highlights the critical roles of extracellular matrix (ECM) components working in concert with cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) during synapse formation (Jang et al. 2017). This review systematically elucidates the functional contributions of ECM molecules and their endogenous inhibitors, tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), in both synaptogenesis and neurological disorders. Our synthesis provides critical insights into ECM-mediated mechanisms in neuropathological conditions and aims to establish a theoretical foundation for targeting TIMPs as potential therapeutic candidates.

Role of CAM in Synapse Formation

Cell adhesion molecules (CAM) are a class of cell surface-specific molecules that mediate cell-to-cell or cell-to-extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesion and are a collective term for numerous molecules that mediate cell-to-cell or cell-to-ECM mutual contact and binding (Südhof 2021). A variety of CAMs in synapses have been shown to play a role in transcellular signaling and cell adhesion by binding to their counterparts on the synaptic membrane or interacting with the corresponding molecules in the ECM to mediate the bi-directional organization of pre-and postsynaptic compartments, thus initiating the formation of synapses, coordinating the fixation and precise alignment of presynaptic and postsynaptic compartments as well as achieving plasticity of synaptic transmission (Südhof 2018). Members of the TIMP family can influence integrin-mediated adhesion and signaling by forming direct bonds with different integrin subtypes or by inhibiting their target Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). For example, the function of TIMP-2 can be mediated through its interaction with integrin receptors; the N-terminal domain of TIMP-2 (N-TIMP-2) can bind to α3β1 and is expressed on α (3) integrin-positive cells, suggesting its role in the transition from proliferation to neuronal differentiation. This process is crucial for synaptic plasticity and memory consolidation (Jaworski & Pérez-Martínez 2006; Stetler-Stevenson 2008; Tan et al. 2013).

In general, presynaptic CAMs are usually ‘hub’ molecules present at excitatory and inhibitory synapses, whereas postsynaptic CAMs are more diverse and numerous (Südhof 2017). The well-studied ones include Neuroligins, Neurexins, LRRTMs (Leucine Rich Repeat Transmembrane Neuronal Protein), Latrophilins, Teneurins, BAIs (Brain Angiogenesis Inhibitors), C1qls (C1q-like proteins), EphBs and Ephrin-Bs, N-cadherin, SynCAMs, SALMs (synaptic adhesion-like molecule), Netrin-Gs/NGLs (cognate trans-synaptic ligand/receptor pairs), and others. For example, Neurexins are Type I transmembrane proteins. They bind to members of the postsynaptic Neuroligins family via their LNS6 domain, promoting the formation of the postsynaptic density, and potentially influencing synaptic stability and transmission efficiency (Südhof 2017). In terms of synaptic plasticity, Neurexins and Neuroligins are necessary for synapses to possess the capacity for long-term potentiation (LTP) (Wu et al. 2019). Furthermore, as Matthew B. Dalva and colleagues stated in a commentary, "When pairs of SAMs interact, they can induce the formation of new synapses or modulate the function of existing synapses through signaling cascades or secondary protein–protein interactions" (Dalva et al. 2007). This indicates that these proteins are not merely static structural components, but dynamic regulators of synaptic function. They not only participate in synapse assembly but also influence neurotransmitter release mechanisms, the composition of postsynaptic membrane receptors, and synapse density/number.

Interaction Between CAM and ECM

During synapse formation, the relationship between CAMs and the ECM is crucial. The ECM not only provides structural scaffolding for cells but also regulates cell behavior through mechanotransduction, including migration, growth, and differentiation (Humphrey et al. 2014). Components of the ECM interact with or bind to specific receptors, participating in neurogenesis, neuronal migration, and axon growth, thereby regulating synapse formation, synaptic structure stability, and synaptic plasticity (Kerrisk et al. 2014). ECM components such as thrombospondins (TSPs), Glypicans, SPARC/Hevin, Neuronal pentraxins, C1q-like proteins, Agrins, Laminins, and Collagens provide essential structural support and biochemical environment, indirectly influencing synapse formation, synaptic transmission, and synaptic plasticity by affecting cell adhesion and migration (Kerrisk et al. 2014; Yang et al. 2023). Furthermore, multiple studies have also found that TIMPs can influence CAM-related signaling by modulating ECM remodeling. The ECM not only serves as a structural scaffold but also regulates signaling pathways for cell polarization, differentiation, survival, and proliferation by binding to cell surface receptors such as integrins, proteoglycans (e.g., syndecan, dystroglycan), and other CAMs (Chirco et al. 2006). By inhibiting the activity of MMPs, TIMPs maintain ECM homeostasis, thereby indirectly affecting the interactions between the ECM and CAMs, as well as downstream signaling (Vanhoutte & Heymans 2010). During the recovery phase following acute neurological injury, the activation of astrocytes leads to the upregulation of ECM molecule synthesis. This, combined with elevated TIMP protein expression, increases the aggregation of ECM molecules. This biphasic dysregulation of the local balance between extracellular proteases and the ECM activates multiple temporally overlapping signaling cascades, involving receptor-type protein tyrosine phosphatases, integrins, Toll-like receptors, CAMs, and ion channels, resulting in impaired synaptic plasticity and cognition (Ulbrich et al. 2021). They also promote synapse formation and stability by modulating CAM activity or interacting with CAMs (Soler-Llavina et al. 2011; Yang et al. 2023). Thus, ECM homeostasis is vital for synapse formation and in neurological diseases.

MMPs/TIMPs Play a Balancing Role

The ECM is a crucial structure that provides structural support to tissues and organs, creates a microenvironment for proteins, and triggers signaling pathways, connecting with the cell membrane. MMPs and their inhibitors (TIMPs) critically regulate ECM homeostasis. MMPs degrade ECM components (e.g., fibronectin, laminin), altering tissue mechanics to facilitate cell migration, neural regeneration, and synaptic formation (Van Lint & Libert 2007; Verma & Hansch 2007). Beyond ECM remodeling, MMPs fine-tune neuronal behavior by cleaving guidance cues/receptors to modulate signaling pathways. During cortical development, membrane-type MMPs (notably MT3-MMP) enable synapse assembly by proteolytically removing inhibitory surface molecules (Moracho et al. 2022; Sanz et al. 2018). However, MMP activity needs to be precisely regulated by their endogenous inhibitors, TIMPs. Because excessive MMP activity can lead to the destruction of ECM structure, thereby affecting normal neuronal functions and being associated with various neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, PD, and the repair process after injury (Dear et al. 2017).

TIMPs, as endogenous inhibitors of MMPs, regulate their activity by binding to MMPs, thereby ensuring appropriate degradation and remodeling of the ECM (Maskos 2005). The expression and activity of TIMPs are crucial for maintaining ECM balance, preventing tissue damage caused by excessive MMP activity. During neural development and disease states, changes in the expression levels and activity of TIMPs may affect neuronal growth and synapse formation (Baranger et al. 2014). Research has shown that TIMP-2 secreted by neurons reduces the accumulation of ECM around synapses, promoting the plasticity of dendritic spines and the migration of new neurons. Conversely, the absence of TIMP-2 leads to an excessively dense ECM network, which impedes neurogenesis and impairs memory (Ferreira et al. 2023). Furthermore, MMP-9 (which is inhibited by TIMP-1) induces LTP through the cleavage of integrin ligands. Simultaneously, TIMP-1 overexpression inhibits MMP-9, reduces the release of neurotrophic factors (such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor, BDNF), and hinders axonal regeneration (Ould-yahoui et al. 2009). Under pathological conditions, such as in the aging brain, decreased TIMP-2 expression and increased ECM complexity result in impaired neuronal migration and reduced synaptic plasticity, contributing to cognitive decline (Ferreira et al. 2023).

The TIMP–MMP interaction functions as a molecular switch controlling ECM remodeling. In the "off" state, TIMPs sterically block active MMPs’ catalytic sites, preserving ECM integrity and synaptic stability. When cues like calcium influx or cytokine signaling reduce TIMP activity or activate pro-MMPs (e.g., via MT-MMPs), the switch flips "on": unleashed MMPs cleave ECM components (laminin, fibronectin), enabling structural plasticity for dendritic spine/axonal reorganization (Ferreira et al. 2023). This binary switch thus converts extracellular signals into discrete “on/off”-ECM remodeling states, balancing synaptic plasticity with stability. This is of great significance for synaptic development, maintaining the normal physiological functions of neurons, and responding to nerve injuries (Fig. 1). By modulating the activity of MMPs and TIMPs, new therapeutic strategies for neural repair and regeneration can be provided. The following is a specific introduction to TIMP molecules.

Structure, Regulation, and Function of TIMPs

The primary role of the TIMPs is the inhibition of metalloproteinases, the family consists of four members in vertebrates: TIMP-1, TIMP-2, TIMP-3, and TIMP-4. Notably, TIMP-1 is encoded on the X chromosome, while TIMP-2, TIMP-3, and TIMP-4 reside on autosomes (Brew 2019; Moore et al. 2012). Despite structural homology—each ~ 21–30 kDa protein contains 12 conserved cysteine residues forming six disulfide bonds—TIMPs exhibit functional diversification through their modular architecture (Visse & Nagase 2003). The conserved heterodimeric structure comprises an N-terminal inhibitory domain (residues 1–110), which folds into a β-barrel motif to occlude MMP active sites, and a C-terminal domain (residues 111–194) featuring a parallel β-hairpin and β-loop-β motif that mediates non-inhibitory interactions, including binding to pro-MMP forms (Tuuttila et al. 1998). Mechanistically, TIMPs form tight 1:1 non-covalent complexes with MMPs, with the N-terminal cysteine residue coordinating zinc ions at the MMP catalytic site to inhibit proteolytic activity (Nagase et al. 2006) (Fig. 2). While the inhibitory domain is universally conserved, the C-terminal domain enables context-dependent functions, such as ECM anchoring (TIMP-3) or pro-MMP activation (TIMP-2), underscoring the structural basis for TIMP pleiotropy.

Structural model of the TIMPs binding to MMPs

The four TIMPs also have certain structural and functional differences. The first difference is in the amino acid sequence and structure, leading to a certain selectivity in their inhibition of MMPs (Brew 2019; Moore et al. 2012). Among human TIMPs, the sequence homology between subtypes ranges from 30 to 51%. TIMP-1 exhibits the lowest homology with other members (averaging 37%-40%), whereas TIMP-2, TIMP-3, and TIMP-4 share higher homology with each other (47–51%) (Peeney et al. 2022). The differences in homology primarily reside in the C-terminal domain and functional loop regions, leading to subtype-specific functions (such as substrate selection and subcellular localization). In contrast, the relative conservation of the N-terminal inhibitory domain maintains the core metalloproteinase inhibitory activity (Stetler-Stevenson 2008). The ability of TIMPs to inhibit MMPs is largely due to the interaction of wedge-shaped ridges on the N-terminal structural domain that bind within the active site cleft of the target MMPs. The structure of the C-terminal domain provides the ability for TIMPs to interact with the hemagglutinin structural domains of a number of MMPs, including the preforms of some MMPs, which can act as stabilizers.

All TIMPs are secreted, but only TIMP-3 integrates into the ECM (Cabral-Pacheco et al. 2020). TIMP-1 exhibits selective MMP inhibition, excluding type I membrane-bound MMPs (MMP-14, -15, -16, -24) or MMP-19, while preferentially binding soluble-type MMPs (MMP-1, -2, -3, -7, -8) and the GPI (glycosylphosphatidylinositol)-anchored MMP-17. In contrast, TIMP-2 modulates MMP activity through dual mechanisms: It inhibits select MMPs (MMP-2、-9、-14) via N-terminal interactions, while simultaneously promoting pro-MMP-2 activation on the cell surface by assembling membrane type I (MT1)-MMP and pro-MMP-2 into a ternary activation complex (Moore et al. 2012). TIMP-3 distinguishes itself as the only TIMP family member to irreversibly integrate into the ECM via electrostatic interactions with sulfated glycosaminoglycans, enabling broad-spectrum inhibition of both soluble and membrane-type MMPs, as well as ADAM family members (e.g., ADAM17/TNF-α-converting enzyme). This ECM anchoring confers TIMP-3 with unique anti-inflammatory properties, distinct from its MMP-inhibitory functions (Visse & Nagase 2003). And TIMP-4 selectively inhibits MMP-2, MMP-26, and MT1-MMP (Fig. 3). Expression profiling further highlights functional specialization: TIMP-1, TIMP-2, and TIMP-3 exhibit ubiquitous tissue distribution, whereas TIMP-4 displays restricted expression, predominantly in cardiac, adipose, and pituitary tissues (Moore et al. 2012). In summary, the structural heterogeneity and tissue-specific expression of TIMPs enable precise spatial and temporal control of proteolytic networks, with implications for therapeutic targeting in diseases characterized by dysregulated ECM remodeling and inflammation.

The Regulatory Mechanism of TIMP for Maintaining Synaptic Maturation and Plasticity

Modifications in synaptic structure and function spanning developmental circuit assembly and adult pathological decline are modulated by ECM proteolysis, with MMP/TIMP imbalances acting as cell-specific modifiers of neuronal–glial environments across lifespans (Łukomska et al. 2020). While TIMPs were historically studied in neurological diseases and injuries, recent advances have illuminated their critical roles in adult synaptic homeostasis and adaptive remodeling. Specifically, TIMPs orchestrate synaptic maturation and plasticity through MMP-dependent and MMP-independent pathways. Studies have shown that TIMP-1 regulates dendritic spine maturation by inhibiting MMP-9. This inhibition stabilizes the ECM around the synapse (via reduced β-dystroglycan cleavage) and drives structural and functional remodeling: AMPA receptors accumulate at synapses (increasing the ratio of AMPA/NMDA), and dendrites transition from elongated to mature mushroom shapes. This mechanism is crucial for the maintenance of LTP. During the LTP process (induced by chemical or electrical stimulation), uncontrolled MMP-9 can cause abnormal elongation of dendrites, while TIMP-1-mediated termination enables spine maturation and LTP consolidation. Thus, temporal control of protease activity is critical—MMP-9 initiates plasticity, and TIMP-1 terminates it to complete maturation (Magnowska et al. 2016).

Beyond its classical MMP-inhibitory function, TIMP-2 promotes neuronal differentiation and neurite outgrowth in the central nervous system (CNS) through a dual mechanism involving synergistic MMP-independent receptor signaling and ECM modulation. It binds to neuronal α3β1 integrin activates a cAMP/SHP-1-Rap1-ERK signaling cascade, which upregulates p35 expression and inhibits Cyclin D to directly drive neurite extension and cell cycle arrest (Pérez-Martínez & Jaworski 2005). Critically, this mechanism persists in the MMP-inhibitory-deficient TIMP-2C72S mutant, confirming its independence from canonical MMP inhibition. Additionally, TIMP-2 regulates ECM homeostasis (Ferreira et al. 2023), preventing the formation of physical barriers caused by abnormal aggrecan accumulation (manifested as impaired dendritic spine maturation and reduced mushroom spines), ensuring neurite plasticity and the migration of newborn neurons, while ECM degradation (e.g., via chondroitinase ABC) rescues the neurogenic defects caused by TIMP-2 deficiency. This spatiotemporal synergistic mechanism, rapidly initiating neurite growth via receptor signaling during the acute phase and maintaining an ECM plasticity microenvironment during the chronic phase, provides a new paradigm for targeted intervention in aging-related cognitive decline.

In contrast, the roles of TIMP-3 and TIMP-4 in synaptic plasticity remain poorly defined, with conflicting reports regarding their involvement in ECM remodeling or direct synaptic regulation. Further investigation is required to elucidate whether these TIMPs contribute to physiological neural functions or pathological synaptic dysfunction, particularly through interactions with MMPs or ECM components.

Research Advances in TIMPs in Neurological Disorders

TIMPs proteins play important functions in the nervous system, and alterations in their expression levels are closely related to many neurological diseases. A large number of studies have suggested the regulatory role of TIMPs proteins in neurodegenerative diseases, neuroinflammation, neurological tumors, etc., and disease development. For instance, in AD, dysregulation of TIMPs and MMPs leads to processing disorders of APOE and amyloid precursor protein (APP); in PD, this imbalance affects β-peptide (Aβ) metabolism. Furthermore, fragmentation of myelin proteins and degradation of the ECM, resulting from TIMP and MMP imbalance, are significant contributors to neuroinflammation and neurological tumors (Fig. 4).

Alzheimer’s Disease (AD)

Numerous studies have confirmed that the onset and progression of AD are closely linked to changes in the levels of the TIMPs family of proteins in the body. Most studies have highlighted the role of TIMP-3 and TIMP-1, while TIMP-2 and TIMP-4 lack direct research evidence.

Studies have shown that in the cerebral cortex of AD patients and model mice, the expression of TIMP-3 protein is elevated, especially around neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) and amyloid plaques (Dunckley et al. 2006; Hoe et al. 2007). However, recent studies found lower TIMP-3 levels in both plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of AD patients compared to individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and healthy controls. These CSF levels negatively correlated with cognitive decline (assessed by CDR scores) (Park et al. 2022). This downregulation may reflect AD progression, with decreased CSF TIMP-3 already observed in the MCI stage. This seemingly paradoxical expression pattern reflects the dual role of TIMP-3 in AD. Within AD brain tissue, TIMP-3 promotes the conversion of APP to Aβ by inhibiting the activity of ADAM10 (Jäkel et al. 2020). Concurrently, AD-associated neuroinflammation activates astrocytes and microglia, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β), which upregulate TIMPs to inhibit MMP-mediated matrix degradation (Park et al. 2022). As an extracellular matrix protein, TIMP-3 can be captured and retained by Aβ aggregates in the brain tissues, leading to its reduced levels in CSF. Furthermore, TIMP-3 levels in CSF/plasma show a positive correlation with Aβ42 and a negative correlation with pTau, reflecting a failure in neuroprotective capacity. Therefore, TIMP-3 levels may transiently elevate during the early stages of AD (e.g., MCI) to counteract neural damage. However, with disease progression, increased consumption leads to decreased CSF levels in later stages (Dewing et al. 2019).

Studies have also shown that in the CSF of AD patients, the level of TIMP-1 is decreased, while the activity of MMP-9 is increased, which is associated with vascular damage and cognitive decline (Park et al. 2022). Hippocampal TIMP-1 levels are reduced in APOE4/CCR5 knockout models, implicating receptor-mediated signaling. Gender-specific analyses reveal distinct CSF MMP/TIMP associations with amyloidosis and cognitive decline: MMP-3 in males versus MMP-10 and TIMP-4 in females (Aksnes et al. 2023). Collectively, these findings indicate AD-related TIMP/MMP dysregulation is modulated by genetic background, biological sex, and disease stage, highlighting the need for personalized strategies targeting protease–antiprotease networks in neurodegeneration.

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) and Other Neurodegenerative Diseases (ND)

Dysregulation of TIMPs and MMPs has been consistently implicated in the pathophysiology of PD and related neurodegenerative disorders. In PD patients, TIMP-1 levels are significantly elevated in the substantia nigra in response to α-synuclein aggregation-induced glial activation, but no significant changes are observed in the cortex or hippocampus (Irwin et al. 2013). Rat PD models show upregulated MMP-7/MMP-9 expression alongside decreased TIMP-3 levels in the midbrain substantia nigra, while elevated MMP-9 is detected in the hypothalamus – a pattern suggesting selective neuronal vulnerability (Spindola et al. 2020). Furthermore, in Huntington’s disease (HD), elevated striatal TIMP-1 expression correlates with mutant huntingtin (mHTT) aggregate-induced astrocyte activation (Zhu et al. 2022). In multiple sclerosis (MS), lesion areas exhibit increased TIMP-1 and TIMP-3 expression, aiming to suppress MMP-2/9-mediated myelin degradation; however, chronic upregulation impedes axonal regeneration (Cabral-Pacheco et al. 2020). Concurrently, aberrant MMP regulation contributes to the pathogenesis of MS, AD, and PD (Tokito & Jougasaki 2016). MMP-2 and MMP-9, expressed by neurons, astrocytes, and microglia in the central nervous system, play pathophysiological roles in blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption through microglial activation and further inflammatory cell progression (Liuzzi et al. 2002).

Collectively, these studies confirm the significant roles of MMPs and TIMPs in neurodegenerative diseases. They contribute to the pathogenesis of PD and neurodegeneration via α-synuclein/Tau/Aβ copathology.

Neurological Tumors and Neuroinflammation

Accumulating evidence highlights the TIMPs protein family as a critical regulator of neurological tumor progression and neuroinflammatory pathways. In glioblastoma (GBM), TIMP members exhibit functional divergence: TIMP-1 promotes angiogenesis by inhibiting MMP-9 and exerts anti-apoptotic effects via Bcl-2 upregulation (Eckfeld et al. 2019). TIMP-2 facilitates tumor cell invasion by suppressing MMP-2, thereby reducing ECM degradation (Han et al. 2021). TIMP-3 exerts anti-angiogenic effects by inhibiting MMP-9/VEGF and blocking endothelial cell migration (Das et al. 2014). TIMP-4 enhances immune infiltration in GBM by recruiting CD4 + T cells (Han et al. 2021). Collectively, TIMP-1 and TIMP-2 are significantly upregulated in GBM, driving malignant progression (pro-angiogenic, anti-apoptotic), whereas TIMP-3 and TIM-P4 suppress tumor development (anti-angiogenic, pro-immune infiltration). Clinically, elevated TIMP-1/2 expression correlates with poor prognosis and shorter overall survival (OS) in glioma patients. Conversely, TIMP-3/4 expression is associated with favorable outcomes (Han et al. 2021).

The TIMPs family also modulates neuroinflammatory processes. Studies (Han et al. 2021; Pagenstecher et al. 1998) indicate that TIMP-1 acts as a core protective factor in neuroinflammation. It is markedly induced around inflammatory foci, expressed by activated astrocytes to encapsulate lesions and form an "inhibitory barrier," thereby restricting inflammation spread. However, its chronic overexpression may impede tissue repair. Conversely, TIMP-1 levels in CSF may decrease, reflecting MMP/TIMP imbalance. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) further drive TIMP-1 upregulation via NF-κB pathway activation in astrocytes. Additionally, under physiological conditions, TIMP-2 is constitutively highly expressed in neurons, while TIMP-3 shows constitutive expression in the choroid plexus. During inflammation, neither TIMP-2 nor TIMP-3 is significantly induced, allowing them to maintain ECM stability. Thus, the inflammatory tumor microenvironment regulates TIMPs through TNF-α/NF-κB signaling and MMP-TIMP feedback loops. Targeting TIMPs requires precision design based on disease stage and microenvironmental context.

TIMPs in Clinical Applications and Treatment

Despite the absence of a fully established systematic protocol leveraging TIMPs proteins for neurotherapeutic applications, extensive research underscores their pivotal role in the pathogenesis and treatment of neurological disorders. Emerging strategies for clinical translation include the development of TIMP-encoding viral vectors, hydrogel- and nanoparticle-based delivery systems, small-molecule inhibitors/agonists, and tumor-targeted therapies, all of which represent promising avenues for disease intervention (Su et al. 2019; Takawale et al. 2017). Preclinical studies demonstrate that in AD treatment, exogenous TIMP-1 supplementation activates the ERK/CREB pathway and promotes BDNF release. Concurrently, nanoparticle carriers enhance BBB penetration, achieving a threefold increase in brain tissue distribution efficiency (Sarkar et al. 2023). Furthermore, elevated TIMP-2 expression in brain tissue enhances neuroprotective mechanisms, offering a potential therapeutic approach for PD (Chen et al., 2020). Intravenously administered TIMP-1 inhibits MMP-9-mediated BBB disruption and reduces α-synuclein (α-syn) spreading in PD and neuroinflammation models (Ashutosh et al. 2012; Raeeszadeh-Sarmazdeh et al. 2020). Additionally, targeting TIMP-1 mRNA with antisense oligonucleotides (ASO) suppresses its pro-angiogenic effects (via VEGF upregulation) by reducing overexpressed TIMP-1 in neuro-oncology contexts (Raeeszadeh-Sarmazdeh et al. 2020). Collectively, these findings suggest that enhancing endogenous neuroimmune defense mechanisms, implementing epigenetic precision regulation, and developing cell-specific targeted delivery systems constitute effective therapeutic strategies. This underscores the multifold therapeutic potential of TIMP modulation in treating neurological disorders.

Conclusion

The dynamic balance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their endogenous inhibitors, tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), within the extracellular matrix (ECM) is critical for synaptogenesis, synaptic remodeling, and neurological function. MMP-driven proteolysis enables plasticity and repair, while TIMP-mediated inhibition preserves ECM integrity and synaptic stability. Emerging evidence reveals that TIMPs not only orchestrate synaptic development—exemplified by TIMP-1-mediated dendritic spine maturation and TIMP-2-driven neuronal differentiation—but also exhibit multifaceted involvement in neurodegenerative disorders (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease), neuroinflammatory processes, and tumor pathogenesis. Although TIMPs have garnered significant attention as potential therapeutic targets, their clinical translation remains hindered by challenges in target specificity, molecular stability, and delivery efficiency. Looking forward, it will be critical to decipher the molecular cues that flip the TIMP–MMP “molecular switch”—i.e., toggling MMP activity “on” for remodeling versus “off” for preservation—so as to design precision interventions that restore ECM homeostasis in both neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders.
