Circulation patterns and molecular characteristics of respiratory syncytial virus among hospitalized children in Tianjin, China, before and during the COVID-19 pandemic (2017–2022)
Authors: Mengzhu Hou, Guangping Liu, Chao Meng, Lili Dong, Yulian Fang, Lu Wang, Ning Wang, Chunquan Cai, Hanjie Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the main pathogen that causes hospitalization for acute lower respiratory tract infections (ALRIs) in children. With the reopening of communities and schools, the resurgence of RSV in the COVID-19 post-pandemic era has become a major concern. To understand the circulation patterns and genotype variability of RSV in Tianjin before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, a total of 19,531 nasopharyngeal aspirate samples from hospitalized children in Tianjin from July 2017 to June 2022 were evaluated. Direct immunofluorescence and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) were used for screening RSV-positive samples and subtyping, respectively. Further analysis of mutations in the second hypervariable region (HVR2) of the G gene was performed through Sanger sequencing. Our results showed that 16.46% (3215/19,531) samples were RSV positive and a delayed increase in the RSV infection rates occurred in the winter season from December 2020 to February 2021, with the average RSV-positive rate of 35.77% (519/1451). The ON1, with H258Q and H266L substitutions, and the BA9, with T290I and T312I substitutions, are dominant strains that alternately circulate every 1–2 years in Tianjin, China, from July 2017 to June 2022. In addition, novel substitutions, such as N296Y, K221T, N230K, V251A in the BA9 genotype, and L226I in the ON1 genotype, emerged during the COVID-19 pandemic. Analysis of clinical characteristics indicated no significant differences between RSV-A and RSV-B groups. This study provides a theoretical basis for clinical prevention and treatment. However, further studies are needed to explore the regulatory mechanism of host immune responses to different lineages of ON1 and BA9 in the future.Keywords: Respiratory syncytial viruse (RSV), Child, Hospitalized, Molecular epidemiology, Genotype, COVID-19

Introduction

Human respiratory syncytial virus (HRSV) is a major pathogen involved in acute lower respiratory tract infections (ALRIs) in young children worldwide. Global epidemiological monitoring of HRSV estimated that there were approximately 33.0 million RSV-related ALRIs in children under 5 years of age globally in 2019, resulting in 3.2 million pediatric hospitalizations and 59,600 hospital deaths (Li et al., 2022). China is one of the five developing countries with the highest incidence of RSV infections in the world. A multicenter study performed by Zhu et al. reported that 17.17% of ALRIs caused by HRSV progressed to severe pneumonia from 2017 to 2020, which caused a heavy burden on public health, especially for children under 5 years of age (Zhu et al., 2022).

RSV is a single negative-stranded virus with a genome length of approximately 15.2 ​kb and contains 10 genes that encode 11 proteins, including NS1, NS2, N, P, M, SH, G, F, M2-1, M2-2, and L (Canedo-Marroquin et al., 2017; Collins and Melero, 2011). Among these proteins, G, a highly glycosylated transmembrane protein responsible for virus attachment and inducing the production of neutralizing antibodies, is considered the most variable (Anderson et al., 2021). During circulation, RSV usually undergoes insertions, deletions, duplications, stop codon usage changes and substitutions, especially in the second highly variable region (HVR2) of the C-terminal region of the G gene, which has been shown to harbor hypermutations at the population level and is widely used in phylogenetic analysis (Anderson et al., 2021). RSV can be divided into groups A (RSV-A) and B (RSV-B) according to reactivity to monoclonal antibodies (Mufson et al., 1985), and multiple genotypes can be further categorized based on the HVR2 sequence variability. To date, at least 15 RSV-A [GA1-7 (Peret et al., 1998, 2000), SAA1-2 (Venter et al., 2001), CB-A (Baek et al., 2012), NA1-4 (Cui et al., 2013) and ON-1 (Eshaghi et al., 2012)] and 30 RSV-B [GB0-5 (Peret et al., 2000; Ren et al., 2015; Munoz-Escalante et al., 2021), SAB1-4 (Arnott et al., 2011), URU1-2 (Blanc et al., 2005), BA1-14 (Dapat et al., 2010; Bashir et al., 2017; Abrego et al., 2017; Trento et al., 2003), CB1 (Cui et al., 2013), BA-CC (Zheng et al., 2017), NZB1-NZB2 (Matheson et al., 2006), and THB (Auksornkitti et al., 2014)] subtypes have been described, among which ON-1 and BA-9, with a duplication in the G gene HVR2 sequence, have shown a selective advantage and have circulated worldwide in recent years (Chen et al., 2021). Continued surveillance of RSV is crucial for understanding its molecular epidemiology and genetic polymorphisms.

Since the outbreak of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in December 2019, several measures, including wearing a mask, disinfecting hands, and maintaining social distancing, have been employed against the epidemic, and the circulation pattern of common respiratory viruses has changed. However, publications on the epidemiology of RSV during the COVID-19 pandemic in northern China are scarce. For this study, a total of 19,531 nasopharyngeal aspirate samples were collected from 2017 to 2022. We focused on changes in the rate of RSV positivity among hospitalized children before and during the COVID-19 pandemic and analyzed substitution sites and N-linked glycosylation sites in the HVR2 of the G gene to provide reliable data for clinical prevention and vaccine development.

Materials and methods

Patients and clinical samples

From July 2017 to June 2022, inpatients aged 0 to 14 with acute respiratory infections were enrolled at Tianjin Children's Hospital in China. Nasopharyngeal aspirate was obtained from each enrolled child. A total of 3215 RSV-positive patients were routinely screened from 19,531 patients using a direct immunofluorescence assay (D3Ultra DFA Respiratory Virus Screening&ID Kit, Ohio, USA), employing fluorescein labeling of specific monoclonal antibodies against RSV. For further genotyping, RSV-positive samples were collected randomly and stored at −80 ​°C. The flowchart of the study is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the study.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription

RNA of RSV-positive specimens was extracted using magnetic beads with a viral RNA⁄DNA purification kit (LANGDE, Wuhan, China), after which the viral RNA was reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA) using a reverse transcription kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). The cDNA was synthesized in a total volume of 20 ​μL containing 12 ​μL of template, 2 ​μL of 10 × King RT buffer, 1 ​μL of Fast King RT enzyme mix, 2 ​μL of FQ-RT primer mix, and 3 ​μL of RNase-Free dH2O. The reaction was performed at 42 ​°C for 15 ​min, followed by 95 ​°C for 3 ​min.

PCR protocols for gene amplification and sequencing

For genotyping, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using 2×Taq PCR Master Mix II (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Referring to previously published protocols (Zlateva et al., 2005; Reiche and Schweiger, 2009), the specific primer sequences and reaction conditions for RSV-A and RSV-B are shown in Supplementary Table S1. In the case of RSV-A, the HVR2 region of the RSV G gene was amplified by nested PCR. The first amplification was carried out at 94 ​°C for 5 ​min, followed by 40 cycles at 94 ​°C for 30 ​s, 58 ​°C for 30 ​s, and 72 ​°C for 60 ​s, and a final extension at 72 ​°C for 7 ​min. The nested amplification was carried out at 94 ​°C for 5 ​min, followed by 40 cycles at 94 ​°C for 30 ​s, 53 ​°C for 30 ​s, 72 ​°C for 60 ​s, and a final extension at 72 ​°C for 7 ​min. For RSV-B, the PCR conditions were 94 ​°C for 5 ​min, followed by 40 cycles at 94 ​°C for 30 ​s, 63 ​°C for 30 ​s, 72 ​°C for 60 ​s and, finally, 72 ​°C for 7 ​min. The expected fragment sizes of the RSV G gene for RSV-A and RSV-B amplicons are 391 bp and 800 bp, respectively. PCR products were sequenced and compared using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) program available on the NCBI homepage (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi).

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

Nucleotide sequences of the partial RSV G gene were aligned with reference sequences. Phylogenetic analyses of RSV-positive strains were carried out in MEGA5 and the evolutionary history was inferred by using the neighbor-joining method based on the Kimura 2-parameter model. The reliability of the tree was estimated with 1000 bootstrap replications. The partial RSV G gene sequences identified in this study are available at GenBank under accession numbers OR473574–OR473609 and PP353590–PP353608 (RSV-A); OR478103–OR478149 and PP353609–PP353618 (RSV-B).

Prediction of N-linked glycosylation sites

The partial G genes at the C-terminal of the RSV-A and RSV-B sequences obtained in this study for predicting N-linked glycosylation sites were obtained from https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetNGlyc-1.0/. N-Glycosylation can be predicted in the sequence context of Asn-Xaa-Ser/Thr (where Xaa is any amino acid except proline) if the potential exceeds the default threshold of 0.5.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 19.0 was used for statistical analysis. Abnormally distributed econometric data were represented by medians (quartiles), and case-counting data were represented as percentages (%). The clinical characteristics of the patients with RSV were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, and a P-value <0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Epidemiology of RSV

The trends in the epidemiology of RSV transmission before and during the COVID-19 pandemic among children in Tianjin are shown in Fig. 2. From July 2017 to June 2022, the rates of RSV infection in hospitalized children at Tianjin Children's Hospital were 10.47% (223/2130), 14.10% (588/4170), 13.52% (462/3417), 19.65% (777/3954), and 19.88% (1165/5860) in the calendar year of July to June of next year. Overall, 3215 (16.46%) samples were tested positive for RSV among 19,531 specimens. After the outbreak of COVID-19, there was a sharp decrease in the rate of RSV infection and hospitalization in March 2020, which continued until October 2020. This decline was possibly due to the regular circulation pattern of RSV or the stringent non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) implemented by the government to prevent and control COVID-19. With the reopening of communities and schools, the average infection rate of RSV in the winter months (December 2020 to February 2021) was 35.77% (519/1451), reaching its peak at 37.50% (99/264) in February 2021. In conclusion, the percentage of RSV-positive individuals from 2020 to 2022 was markedly greater than that from 2017 to 2019, although there was a decline in the short term after the COVID-19 outbreak.

RSV distribution in hospitalized children aged 0–14 years with ALRIs in Tianjin, China, from July 2017 to June 2022.

Our results indicated that the HRSV infections occurred mainly from December to February of the following year, with an average positive rate of 26.55% (2061/7762), accounting for 64.11% of the total positive cases (2061/3215), followed by autumn (September to November) and spring seasons (March to May). The infection rate of RSV in summer season (June to August) was lowest, with an average infection rate of 2.26% (73/3229), accounting for 2.27% (73/3215) of the total positive cases. There was a significant difference in HRSV positivity rates between different seasons (χ2 ​= ​1148.849, P ​< ​0.001), as shown in Fig. 2.

Clinical characteristics of patients

From 2017 to 2021, a total of 3215 samples were tested positive, with male children accounting for 61.12% (1965/3215) and female children accounting for 38.88% (1250/3215) of cases. The average percentage of males who were positive for RSV (16.92%, 1965/11,613) was slightly greater than that of females (15.79%, 1250/7918), and the difference was significant (χ2 ​= ​4.402, P ​< ​0.05). The rate of RSV positivity in hospitalized children aged 1–6 months was 27.42% (1353/4934), accounting for 42.08% (1353/3215), which was the highest among inpatients aged 0–14 years; There were significant differences between different age groups (χ2 ​= ​660.428, P ​< ​0.001). Additionally, infants aged 0–3 years were the main population affected by RSV, as shown in Fig. 3.

Age distribution of RSV in children aged 0–14 years with ALRIs in Tianjin, China, from July 2017 to June 2022.

The clinical characteristics of the patients with RSV-A and RSV-B in this study are shown in Table 1. There was no significant difference in clinical symptoms between children infected with RSV-A and RSV-B. Most patients simultaneously presented with symptoms of cough (100%, 525/525), wheezing (97.90%, 514/525), and fever (70.67%, 371/525). In addition, of 525 RSV-positive children, some had symptoms of diarrhea (12.38%, 65/525), cardiac injury (1.90%, 10/525), viral exanthem (1.33%, 7/525), and conjunctivitis (0.95%, 5/525). A total of 180 patients had underlying diseases, including allergies (12.00%, 63/525), premature delivery (9.90%, 52/525), congenital heart defects (5.71%, 30/525), anemia (6.29%, 33/525), and malnutrition (0.38%, 2/525), which are considered as risk factors for severe pneumonia. Overall, 17.71% (93/525) of the hospitalized children with RSV infection experienced progression to severe pneumonia.

RSV genotyping

A total of 525 RSV positive samples were genotyped, of which 353 cases were type A, accounting for 67.24% (353/525); 161 cases were type B (161/525, 30.67%). In addition, 11 patients (2.10%, 11/525) were coinfected with both RSV-A and RSV-B. As shown in Fig. 4, the temporal distribution of dominant RSV subtype was A-A-B-B-A from July 2017 to June 2022.

Phylogenetic analysis of genotype

A total of 112 positive samples were successfully sequenced, 55 of which were identified as the RSV-A genotype and 57 as the RSV-B genotype. The phylogenetic tree of representative strains is shown in Fig. 5. Based on the nucleic acid sequence of the G gene HVR2 C-terminus, phylogenetic cluster analysis was performed, and the results indicated that all sequenced samples classified as RSV-A belong to the ON1 genotype. The strains can be divided according to genetic distance into two lineages, with 45 strains clustering into a large branch with a bootstrap value of 80. The average p distance within the branch was 0.024. Compared with the reference strains OM062658 (Xiamen, China, 2018), ON219833 (Shanghai, China, 2019), and MZ961852 (Beijing, China, 2019), the strains all belong to the same lineage and were the dominant strains in China in recent years. A small number of strains isolated pre-COVID-19 were scattered into the branch of pedigree 2, with an average intragroup genetic distance of 0.033.

All samples classified as B belong to the BA9 genotype, which can also be divided into two lineages, 80.70% (46/57) of which clustered into lineage 1, with a bootstrap value of 66. The average genetic distance within the group is 0.017, indicating that lineage 1 of the BA9 genotype in this study is highly homologous to reference strains, such as OM062825 (Fujian, China, 2017), MZ516135 (British, 2018), OP320402 (Philippines, 2019), LC769227 (Japan, 2021), OK397497 (USA, 2021), and ON707439 (Shanghai, China, 2021). The reference strains, such as OM062841 (Fujian, China, 2018) and MN163125 (Zhejiang, China, 2018), clustered into the branch of pedigree 2, with an average intragroup genetic distance of 0.020.

Amino acid analysis

To better explore amino acid substitutions and distinguish different lineages, we compared the amino acid variations of the HVR2 G protein region with those of the initial isolates ON1 (JN257693, Canada, 2011) and BA9 (DQ227395, Argentina, 2005). As shown in Fig. 6, all strains in lineage 1 of the ON-1 genotype exhibited simultaneous H258Q and H266L substitutions. The common substitution sites for the ON1 genotype include E286K (6 strains), P300Q (6 strains), L274P (30 strains), T245A (12 strains), L289I (7 strains), L226I (6 strains), P230T (7 strains), and L289P (3 strains). The prediction results of N-linked glycosylation sites revealed that position 237 at the C-terminus of the ON1 G protein is a conserved N-linked glycosylation site.

All BA9 strains sequenced in our study contain P264T/I (56/1 strains), L267S/P (52/5 strains), I281T/A (56/1 strains), and F297S/P (56/1 strains) substitutions. In addition, more substitutions including L219P (7 strains), K221T (5 strains), N230K (3 strains), V251A (3 strains), T254I/M (42/1 strains), I270T (6 strains), A271V (15 strains), L272P (3 strains), T276A (10 strains), Y287H (5 strains), T290I (53 strains), N296Y (7 strains), T302I (9 strains) and T312I (46 strains), were detected in several cases. In lineage 2 of the BA9 strain, the predicted potential N-linked glycosylation sites are located at positions 296 and 312. In lineage 1, only position 296 is a predicted N-linked glycosylation site due to the substitution of T312I. In addition, 7 strains clustering in lineage 1 of BA9 contained both N296Y and T312I variations, resulting in no potential N-linked glycosylation sites being predicted at the C-terminus of the G protein.

Discussion

In this study, the prevalence of RSV in hospitalized children aged 0–14 years in Tianjin, China, was monitored for five consecutive years, and our results showed a dramatic decrease in the incidence rate of RSV infection from March 2020 to October 2020. However, a delayed increase in the RSV infection rate occurred from December 2020 to February 2021, with an average RSV-positive rate of 35.77% (519/1451). There have been some reports on the activities of RSV from September 2020 to January 2021 in southern China, such as Hangzhou (Guo et al., 2023), Guangzhou (Umar et al., 2023), Shanghai (Jia et al., 2022). In contrast, several countries and regions, such as the United States (Uhteg et al., 2022), Austria (Redlberger-Fritz et al., 2021), Japan (Maruo et al., 2022), and Hong Kong (Chiu et al., 2022), reported a significant decrease in the positive rate of RSV for the 2020–2021 RSV season from September 2020 to March 2021 due to the implementation of public health measures. A review about the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on global RSV infection rates revealed that RSV activity varied only in the China's mainland during the winter season from December 2020 to March 2021 (Chuang et al., 2023). These differences in the RSV epidemic between China and other countries or regions may be attributed to earlier COVID-19 outbreaks and community reopening in China. In addition, a delayed surge has been noticed in other countries and regions. In Tokyo, Japan, Ujiie et al. reported the largest increase in RSV cases in 2021 since monitoring began in 2003 (Ujiie et al., 2021). In Shanghai, Liu et al. reported that the RSV positivity rate increased from June 2020 to January 2022 as NPIs were relaxed (Liu et al., 2022). Our data indicated that the positive rate of RSV from 2020 to 2022 was markedly greater than that from 2017 to 2019, possibly because of reduced immunity and an increase in susceptible populations from a lack of exposure to RSV during the COVID-19 pandemic. Reicherz et al. confirmed this viewpoint by showing that levels of RSV antibodies and neutralizing antibodies in infants and women one year into the COVID-19 pandemic decreased significantly compared with those in the early stage of the pandemic (Reicherz et al., 2022).

The dominant subgroups of RSV in hospitalized children in Tianjin over five consecutive years were A, A, B, B, and A, which indicated the alternations in the predominance of RSV-A and RSV-B in 1- or 2-year cycles, reflecting the advantage of the heterologous strain in preventing previously induced immunity. In line with global genotype distribution, the RSV-A ON1 genotype and RSV-B BA9 genotype were identified as the dominant strains before and during the COVID-19 pandemic in this study. The ON1, which carries a 72-nucleotides duplication sequence inserted into the HVR2 region, was first isolated from nasopharyngeal swabs during the winter season from November 2010 to February 2011 in Canada (Eshaghi et al., 2012) and has rapidly spread worldwide. Similarly, the BA genotype, containing a 60- nucleotide duplication sequence at the C-terminus of the G gene, was first identified in Argentina in 1998 (Trento et al., 2003). Since then, the BA genotype has gradually replaced other genotypes and evolved into 14 other genotypes (BA1–14) (Dapat et al., 2010; Bashir et al., 2017; Abrego et al., 2017; Trento et al., 2003). In China, a multicenter study involving patients from 14 hospitals proposed that the ON1 and BA9 genotypes had completely replaced other genotypes by 2015 (Chen et al., 2021).

In our study, ON1 stains could be divided into two lineages, with 81.82% (45/55) of stains with H258Q and H266L substitutions clustered into a large branch belonging to the lineage 1. Sequences with H258Q and H266L substitutions were first identified in Fujian, China, in 2015. Since 2015, lineage 1 of ON1 has circulated rapidly in Asia, with continuous reports in Europe, America, Russia and Australia. In China, strains with H258Q and H266L substitutions have been isolated from Beijing, Zhejiang, Ningxia, Guangdong, Shanghai and Jilin (Chen et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2022). Within the BA9 genotype, lineage 1 accounts for 84.70% (46/57) of the population; it contains T312I, resulting in the loss of potential N-linked glycosylation sites at position 310. In addition, novel substitutions, such as N296Y (7 strains), K221T (5 strains), N230K (3 strains), V251A (3 strains) in the BA9 genotype and L226I (6 strains) in the ON1 genotype, emerged during the COVID-19 pandemic, indicating continuous evolution of the BA9 and ON1 genotypes. The HVR2 region of the RSV G protein contains multiple antigenic sites; accumulation of mutations may affect the immune response and confer a fitness advantage. Therefore, it is critical to continuously monitor RSV infection and understand the circulation pattern of RSV genotypes.

RSV infection is associated with bronchiolitis or pneumonia in children, and some cases even progress to severe pneumonia. In our study, the rate of severe RSV infection in hospitalized children was 17.71% (93/525). The phenotype and severity of RSV-associated disease are influenced by multiple factors, such as host factors, environmental factors, and viral factors (Tahamtan et al., 2020). Similar to several previous studies, our results showed no significant difference in the severity of RSV-A and RSV-B infections. However, several studies have reported different conclusions. Thielen et al. identified a new lineage with an N64D amino acid substitution in the SH protein associated with an outbreak of severe RSV-B disease among urban Minnesota children (Thielen et al., 2020). Tabatabai et al. revealed loss of 72 characteristic nucleotide repeats in ON1 strains in patients with immune-compromised hematological diseases (Tabatabai et al., 2018). The host immune response varies among different lineage backgrounds during the evolution of RSV, and the definition is not very clear. Further studies are needed to explore the molecular mechanisms of RSV infection in different lineage backgrounds.

There were also some limitations in this study. First, Tianjin Children's Hospital is a tertiary hospital located in a metropolis with a large sample size, while this was a single-center study, and cases in the community and outpatient clinics were not included, indicating selection bias. Second, only a limited number of sequences were analyzed, and some novel mutations associated with the severity of RSV infection may have been missed.

Conclusions

This study reveals the epidemic patterns of RSV before and during the COVID-19 pandemic in Tianjin. The ON1 strain, with H258Q and H266L substitutions, and BA9, characterized by T290I and T312I substitutions, are dominant strains, alternating in dominance every 1–2 years. The ongoing evolution of the BA9 and ON1 genotypes may enhance their replication and transmission capabilities, thereby posing challenges for vaccine development. Further studies are needed to continuously monitor the infection rate and genetic diversity of RSV and explore the regulatory mechanism of host immune responses to different lineages.
