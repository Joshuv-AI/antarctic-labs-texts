Bilateral Hemifacial Spasm with “Other Babinski Sign”
Authors: Lulup K Sahoo, Devidutta Dash, Monica Karan, Lohitha B Jasthi
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Hemifacial spasm (HFS) is a form of hyperkinetic movement disorder of peripheral origin. HFS is defined as involuntary tonic and/or clonic movement of muscles innervated by the facial nerve.[1] HFS usually starts with the involvement of periorbital muscles, leading to twitching of the eyelid and frequent blinking.[2] Subsequently, HFS involves the facial, perioral, forehead, and platysma muscles. HFS usually causes social awkwardness and affects vision due to involuntary eye closure. HFS is usually unilateral. Bilateral HFS is very rare, with a prevalence of 0.6%–5%.[3] Here, we report a case of bilateral HFS with the classical “other Babinski sign.”

A 62-year-old lady presented with twitching of the right eyelid 3 years before the first consultation, which subsequently involved the right side of the face with frequent twitching within a month. One year later, she developed similar twitching on the left side of the face. The severity of twitching is increasing day by day, causing visual disturbances owing to the tight closure of the eyelids. The facial twitching persisted during sleep. There was no history of facial palsy or trauma. She was not a known case of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or other chronic diseases. She had a history of intake of carbamazepine 600 mg per day without any significant recovery. On examination, she had intermittent asynchronous bilateral HFS of both tonic and clonic types, occurring sometimes on the right and sometimes on the left. At times, however, frontalis contraction occurred on both sides simultaneously, though asymmetrically [Video 1]. She also exhibited the “other Babinski sign,” characterized by raising of the eyebrow during eye occlusion on the side of the spasm [Figure 1]. The “other Babinski sign” was more prominent on the right side with either side movement, whereas on the left side, there was subtle eyebrow elevation with frontalis overactivity. The spasms worsened with stress, anxiety, speaking, and facial movements. There was no distractibility. The HFS grading scale was 9 out of 9, suggesting a more intense and severe form. A 1.5T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and magnetic resonance angiography with constructive interference in steady-state sequences were normal and did not show any neurovascular conflict (NVC). The routine hematological parameters were normal. She was treated with 60 units of injection botulinum toxin type A (4 units each into the bilateral medial and lateral pretarsal regions of the upper lid, lateral pretarsal of the lower lid, and lower lid midline pretarsal part of the orbicularis oculi; 5 units each into the bilateral zygomaticus major; 4 units each into the bilateral risorius; 2 units each into the bilateral mentalis; and 1.5 units into the bilateral orbicularis oris at 4 points). She had significant improvement with botulinum toxin, which lasted for 3 months.

The most common cause for HFS is NVC causing compression or irritation of the facial nerve.[1] The most common vessel causing NVC in HFS is the anterior inferior cerebellar artery, followed by the posterior inferior cerebellar artery and the vertebral artery. Secondary causes of HFS include Bell’s palsy, tumour compressing the facial nerve, and facial nerve injury.[1] The common mimickers of HFS are psychogenic HFS, hemimasticatory spasm, facial tics, oromandibular dystonia, facial myokymia, and blepharospasm[1] [Table 1]. Babinski, in 1905, described paradoxical synkinesis in HFS: “when orbicularis oculi contracts and the eye closes, the frontalis contracts at the same time, and the eyebrow rises during eye occlusion.”[4] This is called the “other Babinski sign” and is classical of HFS. The presence of this sign is helpful in distinguishing HFS from essential blepharospasm.[4] Psychogenic HFS is differentiated from organic HFS by the lack of the “other Babinski sign,” presence of tonic muscle contractions, downward deviation of the angle of the mouth, and predominant lower face involvement.[5]

Although, HFS is usually unilateral, multiple rare cases of bilateral HFS have been reported in the literature[3678910] [Table 2]. Bilateral HFS can sometimes be confused with other facial movement disorders such as facial tics, facial dyskinesia, blepharospasm, and psychogenic HFS. Bilateral HFS is characterized by involuntary, sequential but asynchronous facial muscle contractions on both sides.[8] Between the two sides of facial involvement, there is usually a long latency period, which helps differentiate bilateral HFS from other facial dyskinesia. Blepharospasm is differentiated from bilateral HFS by the presence of bilateral, usually symmetric and synchronous contractions of the orbicularis oculi muscle, whereas bilateral HFS is asynchronous.[9] There is also one reported case of synchronous bilateral HFS.[9] From India, only one case of bilateral HFS has been described by Misra et al.[10] out of a total of 250 patients with HFS. Our patient did not have any NVC on 1.5T MRI. The exact mechanism of HFS in the absence of NVC is unclear. In some patients, 3T MRI is better at identifying NVCs that were not detectable with certainty on 1.5 T MRI. As in our case 1.5T MRI was performed; a 3T MRI would have been more beneficial in diagnosing NVC.

Bilateral HFS, as a very rare condition, often creates a diagnostic dilemma. In our case, there was a long latency period between the two sides, and the facial movement was asynchronous, with the classical “other Babinski sign” suggesting bilateral HFS. Our patient did not have any vascular compression and responded well to botulinum toxin.
