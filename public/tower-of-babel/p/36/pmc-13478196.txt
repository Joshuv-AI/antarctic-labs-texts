TREM1 signaling amplifies neutrophil-mediated inflammation in inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Warrison Andrade, Hao Xu, Yutian Peng, Zhiyuan Yao, Jack Bevers, Rhyan Puno, Yongchang Shi, Victoria Pham, Meena Choi, Yonglian Sun, Alexis Scherl, Ruoyu Zhang, Alexandra Paun, Tao Sun, Yuxin Liang, Nandhini Ramamoorthi, Jianyong Wang, Dhaya Seshasayee, Gerald Nakamura, Man-Wah Tan, Hua Zhang, Mary Keir, Jason Hackney, Saiyu Hang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), complex immune dysregulation induces chronic inflammation. TREM1 is a receptor expressed on myeloid cells and is activated by ligands such as PGLYRP1 to amplify inflammatory responses. Here, we analyze the function of TREM1 signaling in neutrophil-driven inflammation, identifying two positive feedback loops. First, neutrophils activated by phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) or by bacteria release PGLYRP1, which forms a ligand complex with bacterial peptidoglycan and activates TREM1, thereby enhancing neutrophil degranulation, reactive oxygen species production, and NETosis. Second, TREM1 activation on monocytes promotes the secretion of chemokines and cytokines that recruit and activate additional neutrophils, further perpetuating inflammation. In vivo, TREM1 activation exacerbates colitis in mice, with the gut commensal bacterium Blautia faecis potentially contributing to human TREM1 activation in IBD. In biopsy samples from patients with IBD, an elevated TREM1 pathway signature is associated with inflamed tissue and treatment resistance in ulcerative colitis. Our findings thus highlight TREM1 as a regulator of mucosal inflammation, and a potential therapeutic target for IBD.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), poses significant challenges in clinical management due to the complexity of its pathogenesis and the heterogeneity of patient responses to treatments1–4. Despite the availability of various therapeutic options, such as antibiotics, aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, and biologics targeting inflammatory cytokines or integrins, their efficacy remains suboptimal5. For example, the remission rate of standard-of-care biologics for UC is approximately 20–30% in induction trials6, with about one-third of initially responding patients experiencing a loss of response within a year and a majority of patients losing response by the end of three years7. This highlights a substantial unmet need in IBD and underscores the importance of identifying mechanisms underlying therapeutic resistance and developing targeted interventions.

Recent studies have advanced our understanding of resistance mechanisms, revealing pathways such as OSM, IL-1 and IL-22 that are associated with resistance to anti-TNF or anti-P408–10. A critical finding across multiple studies is the association between the myeloid cell compartment, particularly neutrophils, and non-response to various treatments8,9,11–13. Additionally, recent work led by the Mehandru lab demonstrated a significant increase in neutrophils within the epithelial layer of inflamed tissue from UC patients, indicating their role in barrier destruction during active disease14. This consistency across studies of various therapeutic interventions strongly suggests a key role for neutrophils in treatment resistance in IBD, prompting us to investigate the functional role of these cells and identify pathways for potential neutrophil-targeted therapeutics to assist patients resistant to current treatments.

In this study, we investigate how TREM1 signaling shapes neutrophil‑driven inflammation in the intestine. We define TREM1‑dependent feedback loops between neutrophils and monocytes, show that gut commensal bacteria such as Blautia faecis activate TREM1 and disrupt epithelial barrier integrity, and test how TREM1 activation modulates colitis severity in mice. We also derive a TREM1 pathway signature from human myeloid cells and evaluate its association with inflammatory activity and treatment resistance in IBD. Together, these findings establish TREM1 as a central coordinator of myeloid-microbial interactions in the gut and support TREM1 as a potential axis for therapeutic intervention in IBD.

Results

The neutrophil secretome identifies a TREM1 self-amplification loop that mediates neutrophil hyperactivation

Unlike most leukocytes, mature granulocytes such as neutrophils exhibit low transcriptional activity, as many of their effector proteins are pre-packaged in granules, with transcriptional activity decreasing as the cells mature15,16. To gain a thorough understanding of neutrophil effector functions, we performed a proteomic analysis of the neutrophil secretome following activation with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA). PMA stimulation led to significant changes in the secreted protein profile, with 452 proteins showing increased abundance, including cathepsins, complement factors, and myeloperoxidase (MPO) (Fig. 1a,b, Supplementary Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Data 1). We also detected OSM secretion upon neutrophil activation, consistent with elevated OSM transcripts in neutrophils from inflamed IBD tissue13 and its functional role in preclinical mouse models of IBD10,17. Notably, both TREM1 and PGLYRP1 were upregulated (Fig. 1b). PGLYRP1, upon binding to bacterial peptidoglycan (PGN), forms a ligand complex that activates TREM118, a receptor predominantly expressed on neutrophils and monocytes, not on lymphocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Activation of TREM1 leads to its proteolytic cleavage and release as soluble form of TREM1 (sTREM1)19. The release of PGLYRP1 and sTREM1 upon PMA treatment suggests that TREM1 pathway is primed following neutrophil activation. To assess whether PGLYRP1 is secreted during a physiological neutrophil activation process, we incubated neutrophils with live Escherichia coli (E. coli). Similar to PMA, E. coli stimulated the secretion of PGLYRP1 to comparable levels (Fig. 1c), indicating PGLYRP1 as a soluble marker of general neutrophil activation. Immunofluorescence staining of PGLYRP1 in neutrophils revealed a punctate distribution pattern (Fig. 1d), consistent with previous findings that PGLYRP1 is a neutrophil granule protein20. Intriguingly, activation of TREM1 with either a plate-bound agonistic antibody (TREM1.ago)18 or the PGN/PGLYRP1 ligand complex further enhanced PGLYRP1 secretion (Fig. 1e,f). This demonstrates that TREM1 pathway activation constitutes a self‑amplification loop: neutrophil activation releases PGLYRP1, which primes the TREM1 pathway; when bacterial PGN becomes available, it forms a ligand complex with PGLYRP1 and activates TREM1 signaling, leading to further PGLYRP1 release.Fig. 1Neutrophil secretome identifies a TREM1 self-amplification loop.Whole‑blood neutrophils were isolated from healthy donors. a, b Proteomic analysis of the neutrophil secretome after 30 min stimulation with PMA or DMSO (n = 4 independent donors). a Volcano plot showing proteins with increased (red) or decreased (blue) abundance. b Selected inflammatory mediators; box plots show median (center line), interquartile range (box) and minimum/maximum values (whiskers). To investigate the functional role of TREM1 pathway in neutrophils, we examined key cellular processes associated with neutrophils function, including degranulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and NETosis. Activation of TREM1 with either TREM1.ago or the PGN/PGLYRP1 ligand complex promoted neutrophil degranulation, as measured by the cell surface localization of the primary granule protein CD6321 (Fig. 1g,h), and the release of myeloperoxidase (MPO) (Supplementary Fig. 1c). In addition to PGLYRP1, bacterial PGN can be detected by pattern recognition receptors such as NODs and NLRP322, potentially leading to neutrophil activation independent of TREM1. To confirm the TREM1-dependent activity of the PGN/PGLYRP1 complex, we developed a TREM1 neutralization antibody (anti-TREM1) (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Blocking TREM1 with this antibody significantly reduced the effects of the PGN/PGLYRP1 complex on neutrophil degranulation (Fig. 1h). Similarly, both the agonistic antibody and the ligand complex robustly activated ROS production in neutrophils (Fig. 1i-k). TREM1 activation also induced NETosis, as demonstrated by the Incucyte kinetic analysis (Fig. 1l), immunofluorescence staining of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) components, including extracellular DNA, citrullinated histone H3 (Cit-H3), and MPO (Fig. 1m), and quantification of MPO-DNA complexes in the supernatants as a biochemical measurement of NETs (Fig. 1n). These functional changes highlight the role of TREM1 in amplifying neutrophil-driven inflammation.

TREM1-activated monocytes cross-activate neutrophils

TREM1 is expressed on monocytes under homeostatic conditions and is further upregulated upon activation by external stimuli such as LPS (Supplementary Fig. 1b)19,23. Unlike neutrophils, monocytes do not exhibit significant self-amplification through PGLYRP1 release upon TREM1 activation (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Instead, TREM1 activation in monocytes leads to a substantial production of cytokines, chemokines, and other inflammatory mediators, as shown by bulk transcriptomic analysis of isolated human blood monocytes stimulated with TREM1.ago or the ligand complex (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Data 2). The correlation in transcriptomic changes induced by both TREM1.ago and the ligand complex suggest that these changes are largely mediated by TREM1 pathway (Fig. 2a). The moderate correlation coefficient between PGN/PGLYRP1 and the TREM1 agonistic antibody is expected, as the PGN/PGLYRP1 preparation contains not only the ligand complex but also free PGN. PGN is a known activator of multiple innate immune sensors, including NOD1, NOD2, and TLR2, which likely contribute to the observed transcriptional responses. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the genes induced by both treatments revealed enrichment in neutrophil functions (Fig. 2b). We further validated several key cytokines and chemokines at protein level by ELISA, showing that the TREM1 ligand complex induced production of TNF, TL1A, and IL-23 (Fig. 2c). These cytokines are clinically validated targets in IBD24–28, strongly implicating the pathogenic role of the TREM1 pathway in IBD. Additionally, chemokines such as IL-8, a major neutrophil chemoattractant29, and CCL20, which recruits CCR6-expressing Th17 cells that respond to IL-23 and TL1A30–32, were also elevated (Fig. 2c). Although IL-23 production in our system was restricted to monocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2b), neutrophils have also been reported as a significant source of IL-23 in colon biopsies from pediatric IBD patients33. To explore the potential interactions between monocytes and neutrophils through TREM1-induced effectors, we assessed the impact of supernatants from TREM1-activated monocytes on neutrophils (Fig. 2d). Monocytes activated by either TREM1.ago or the ligand complex produced soluble factors that induced ROS production in neutrophils (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 2c), predominantly mediated by TNF, as blocking TNF with an antibody (anti-TNF) significantly reduced ROS induction (Fig. 2e). To rule out the possibility of PGN/PGLYRP1 complex carryover from monocyte supernatants, we treated the supernatants with lysozyme to degrade PGN. Lysozyme treatment did not affect neutrophil ROS production, further confirming that the effect is mediated by monocyte-derived TNF rather than carryover of PGN/PGLYRP1 (Fig. 2e). Similarly, these activated monocytes produced soluble factors that enhanced neutrophil chemotaxis (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 2d). This chemotactic effect was largely dependent on IL-8, as blocking IL-8 (anti-IL8) inhibited the chemotactic response to the monocyte supernatant (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 2d). These findings reveal the second neutrophil activation loop mediated by TREM1 on monocytes: TREM1-activated monocytes secrete neutrophil chemoattractants such as IL-8, which directly recruit neutrophils, as well as CCL20, which potentially recruits Th17 cells. Additionally, they secrete TL1A and IL-23, which activate Th17 to produce IL-17, further promoting neutrophil recruitment. Recruited neutrophils can then be activated by TREM1 or monocyte-derived cytokines such as TNF, thereby amplifying the neutrophil axis during inflammation.

The gut commensal Blautia faecis activates human TREM1 and compromises barrier integrity

The bacterial cell wall component PGN from E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is known to induce TREM1 activation when it forms a complex with PGLYRP118. The human gut hosts trillions of commensal bacteria, and dysbiosis within this bacterial community is strongly linked to the development of IBD34,35. To identify commensal bacteria that strongly activate TREM1, we engineered a luciferase reporter cell line by overexpressing both TREM1 and the adaptor DAP12 in Jurkat cells (Jurkat-TD) (Supplementary Fig. 3a). We developed both murine and human TREM1 reporter, which specifically respond to murine or human TREM1 agonistic antibodies, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Our results showed that, in the presence of PGLYPR1, purified PGN from E. coli K12 strain, but not from S. aureus or Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis), can activate human TREM1 but not murine TREM1 (Fig. 3a). We screened culture supernatants of 15 different bacteria species isolated from IBD patients and found that Blautia faecis (B. faecis) specifically activated human TREM1, but not murine TREM1 (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Various strains of B. faecis exhibited similar activity (Fig. 3b). Importantly, lysozyme pre-treatment abolished the effects of the B. faecis culture supernatant, confirming that the activity was likely due to bacterial PGN (Fig. 3c). To investigate the functional role of B. faecis on monocytes and neutrophils, we incubated B. faecis strain MCC0939 live cells with recombinant PGLYRP1 and cultured them with either monocytes or neutrophils. When combined with PGLYRP1, MCC0939 triggered a strong TNF release, a response that was dependent on TREM1, as TREM1 neutralization eliminated this effect (Fig. 3f). In neutrophils, MCC0939 induced TREM1-dependent ROS production (Fig. 3g). To assess whether PGLYRP1 directly recognizes B. faecis, we examined binding of recombinant human PGLYRP1 to live B. faecis cells by immunofluorescence microscopy, which revealed clear surface binding, especially at the cell division sites (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Myeloid cells, such as monocytes and neutrophils, are closely associated with gut epithelial cells, and neutrophils can penetrate the epithelial layer to form crypt abscesses, potentially damaging the epithelial barrier in IBD14,36. Commensal bacteria approach the epithelium at sites of barrier damage37. We hypothesized that B. faecis, or its derived PGN, could interact with myeloid cells under such conditions, activating the TREM1 pathway and affecting epithelial barrier function. To test this hypothesis, we co-cultured monocytes and neutrophils in a transwell system with Caco-2 epithelial cells in a transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) assay (Fig. 3h). Activation of TREM1, either by the ligand complex PGN/PGLYRP1 or TREM1 agonistic antibody led to a significant reduction in TEER (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 3e). While B. faecis MCC0939 alone did not affect TEER, MCC0939 in the presence of monocytes and neutrophils drastically reduced TEER in a TREM1 dependent manner (Fig. 3j). Our findings indicate that TREM1 activation in myeloid cells by gut commensal bacteria compromises the integrity of the epithelial cell barrier.

TREM1 activation exacerbates colitis in mice

Having established the pathogenic role of human TREM1 function in myeloid cell-driven inflammation and barrier disruption through in vitro studies, we proceeded to investigate this pathway in vivo using a mouse model of colitis. Despite extensive testing, identifying the ligand for murine TREM1 (muTREM1) remains a significant challenge. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis shows a lack of binding between muTREM1 and mouse PGLYRP1 (Supplementary Fig. 4a). In addition, protein-protein interaction ELISA demonstrates that human PGLYRP1 directly binds to huTREM1 but not muTREM1, and that mouse PGLYRP1 does not bind to muTREM1 (Supplementary Fig. 4b-c), confirming that the PGLYRP1-TREM1 binding is species-specific. Size-exclusion chromatography and SDS-PAGE analysis further revealed that human PGLYRP1 forms a disulfide-linked dimer, whereas mouse PGLYRP1 remains monomeric during purification (Supplementary Fig. 4d-e), which may contribute to its inability to form a functional ligand complex with mouse TREM1. In addition, reported ligands like CIRP38, HMGB139, and necrotic cell lysate (NCL)39 (Supplementary Fig. 4f-g), as well as murine homologs PGLYRP1, PGLYRP2, PGLYRP3, PGLYRP4 (Supplementary Fig. 4h), failed to show bona fide binding or ligand activity for muTREM1. This challenge underscores the complexity of translating findings across species, as ligand-receptor interactions may not be conserved between humans and mice, complicating the investigation of TREM1-mediated pathways in preclinical models.

In line with the prominent role of the TREM1 pathway in myeloid-driven inflammation, we observed increased infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages in the colon lamina propria (Fig. 4f-g, i-j and Supplementary Fig. 4j), along with elevated levels of chemokines CCL2, CCL4, CCL5, and CCL11 in the colon tissue, which are involved in recruiting myeloid cells (Fig. 4l-o). Additionally, myeloid-related inflammatory cytokines such as TNF and G-CSF were elevated (Fig. 4p,q). Given our previous findings that human TREM1 induces the production of TL1A, IL-23, and CCL20 in monocytes (Fig. 2c), which are important for activating and recruiting Th17 cells32,40,41, we examined Th17 and cytokine IL-17A in the colitis model. Both Th17 cells and IL-17A levels were elevated (Fig. 4h,k,r and Supplementary Fig. 4k). This suggests that the muTREM1 pathway actively promotes myeloid activation and the Th17-IL17A axis, thereby contributing to the inflammatory cascade observed in colitis.

The TREM1 pathway is elevated in IBD and correlated with disease severity and treatment resistance

Our mechanistic elucidation and preclinical findings strongly demonstrate a pathogenic role for TREM1 in mediating neutrophil activation within the context of gut inflammation. To investigate whether the TREM1 pathway is associated with IBD, we first examined the cell-type-specific expression of TREM1 and PGLYRP1 in IBD tissue biopsy samples using a published single-cell RNA-seq dataset42. We found that TREM1 is predominantly expressed in neutrophils, with additional lower expression in other myeloid cells, while PGLYRP1 expression is largely restricted to neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 5a-b). Next, to directly assess the level of TREM1 pathway activity in patient genomics datasets, we generated a TREM1 pathway gene signature based on transcriptome analyses of both monocytes and neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 5c). This gene signature consists of the top 10 genes induced in monocytes by both TREM1 agonistic antibody and the PGN/PGLYRP1 ligand complex, combined with the top 10 genes induced in neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 5d, Supplementary Data 2,3).

We examined the TREM1 pathway signature in IBD patient samples curated from 7 publicly available clinical trials, comprising a total of 911 donors (474 in CD trials and 437 in UC trials). Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) analyses using the generated gene signature revealed that TREM1 pathway activity is significantly elevated in biopsy samples from both CD and UC patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Data 4). Moreover, TREM1 pathway activity is markedly higher in inflamed biopsies compared to non-inflamed biopsies (Fig. 5b), suggesting that the TREM1 pathway is associated with disease activity. In situ hybridization on colon biopsies confirmed increased TREM1 transcript signal in ulcerated regions of UC compared to non-inflamed normal tissue (Supplementary Fig. 5e). These analyses are cross-sectional and do not address longitudinal disease progression. Additionally, we measured the RNA level of PGLYRP1, the ligand component of TREM1, in blood samples from IBD patients. PGLYRP1 expression was significantly elevated in patients with active CD or UC compared to healthy controls (Fig. 5f,g). Discussion

Our study highlights the pivotal role of TREM1 activation in mediating neutrophil-driven inflammation, particularly within the context of IBD. We identify two critical amplification loops initiated by TREM1 (Supplementary Fig. 6). The first loop involves the activation of existing neutrophils, where neutrophil activation releases PGLYRP1. This step primes neutrophils for TREM1 activation: when PGLYRP1 encounters bacterial PGN, it forms a ligand complex that stimulates TREM1. Activated TREM1 on neutrophils triggers the degranulation, further releasing more PGLYRP1 from the existing neutrophils. This chain of events constitutes Loop 1, leading to the hyperactivation of neutrophils, characterized by increased production of ROS, proteases, and NETs. The second loop involves the recruitment of additional neutrophils, driven by TREM1-activated monocytes that secrete neutrophil chemokine IL-8 and lymphocyte chemokine CCL20, as well as cytokines like TNF, TL1A, and IL-23. Both TL1A and IL-23 activate Th17 to produce IL-17, which can induce additional neutrophil chemokines and adhesion molecules in various cell types, including fibroblasts, endothelial and epithelial cells9,46–48, recruiting additional neutrophils to the affected region and propagating the inflammatory process, thus forming Loop 2. These TREM1-driven processes culminate in extensive neutrophil recruitment and hyperactivation, ultimately leading to epithelial destruction. This mechanism underscores the significance of targeting the TREM1 pathway as a potential therapeutic strategy in addressing neutrophil-driven pathology in IBD.

The TREM1 pathway’s role in mediating microbe-immune cell interactions is particularly significant in the context of IBD. Dysbiosis, or the imbalance of the gut microbiome, has been implicated in IBD pathogenesis34,35. Our findings show that bacterial components, such as PGN from gut commensals such as B. faecis and E. coli, can activate TREM1, suggesting that commensal bacteria may negatively influence immune responses, especially when the epithelial barrier is compromised. While commensals are generally defined as microbes that coexist with the host under homeostatic conditions, this does not preclude their ability to stimulate the immune system in certain contexts. For example, B. faecis, or its PGN fragments, could translocate across a damaged epithelial barrier, activating TREM1 and contributing to inflammation. Similarly, Enterococcus faecalis, another gut commensal, has been shown to exacerbate inflammation under conditions of epithelial barrier dysfunction49. This interaction can establish a vicious cycle of inflammation and barrier destruction, as the disrupted barrier facilitates further microbial invasion, perpetuating immune activation and tissue injury. These insights align with the broader understanding that the gut microbiome plays a crucial role in modulating immune responses and maintaining intestinal homeostasis50,51. The disruption of this balance, as seen in IBD, can lead to unchecked inflammation driven by myeloid cells. Our study supports the notion that targeting specific pathways like TREM1 could mitigate this microbe-driven dysregulation of mucosal immunity and restore balance.

Furthermore, our findings suggest that TREM1 activation by B. faecis is not determined by Gram status alone but is likely driven by specific PGN fragments that engage PGLYRP1 to form a productive complex. PGNs vary widely across bacterial species in glycan modifications, peptide composition, and fragment generation. Recent studies show that PGLYRP1 selectively binds disaccharide-containing PGN motifs, such as GMTriP-K-like structure52, which are critical for downstream signaling. Consistent with this idea, recombinant PGLYRP1 shows clear surface binding to live B. faecis cells by immunofluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 3d), suggesting that B. faecis presents PGLYRP1‑permissive PGN motifs. We hypothesize that B. faecis and E. coli share PGLYRP1-permissive motifs, enabling TREM1 activation, while other Gram-positive species lack these features. Future studies isolating and characterizing B. faecis PGN fragments will clarify the structural basis of its TREM1 activation.

TNF, IL-23, and TL1A are pivotal cytokines involved in the pathogenesis of IBD. These cytokines play crucial roles in modulating immune responses and sustaining chronic inflammation within the gut. TNF is a well-established pro-inflammatory cytokine that promotes inflammation and tissue damage, and its blockade is a standard-of-care (SoC) in IBD treatment, with anti-TNF therapies like Infliximab and Adalimumab widely used in clinical practice for decades25,53,54. IL-23, part of the IL-23/Th17 axis, drives the differentiation and expansion of Th17 cells, which are implicated in various chronic inflammation and autoimmunity. IL-23 inhibitors, such as Ustekinumab (anti-p40) and Mirikizumab (anti-p19), have shown positive outcomes in clinical trials and are increasingly being adopted in the management of IBD26,28. TL1A has emerged as another critical player in IBD, promoting the expansion of Th17 cells and exacerbating the inflammatory milieu. Antibodies targeting TL1A are currently under investigation and have demonstrated promising results in clinical trials24,27. Importantly, our study shows that all three cytokines, TL1A, TNF, and IL-23, are directly induced by the TREM1 pathway, suggesting a pathogenic role for this pathway in IBD acting upstream of major inflammatory pathways. The induction of these cytokines through TREM1-mediated mechanisms highlights the potential of TREM1 as a key driver of the inflammatory processes underpinning IBD. Additionally, we observed elevated expression and pathway activity of TREM1 in patient samples, correlating with treatment nonresponse and disease severity. This is consistent with previous reports that blood levels of TREM1 predict anti-TNF non-response55. These associations suggest that TREM1 pathway activity may have utility as a disease‑associated signature that could support future biomarker development for disease activity and treatment response, but formal validation as a biomarker will require independent cohorts, longitudinal sampling and performance metrics such as AUROC. Furthermore, targeting the TREM1 pathway provides a promising therapeutic approach to address neutrophil-driven pathology in IBD patients that failed in SoC treatments. This further underscores the relevance of targeting the TREM1 pathway as an innovative therapeutic strategy, possible to disrupt the upstream signals that lead to the overproduction of these pathogenic cytokines, thereby reducing inflammation and improving clinical outcomes for patients with IBD.

All experiments in this study were conducted using total monocytes, without further subdivision into classical, intermediate, or non-classical subsets. While this approach captures the collective response of monocytes, it does not account for potential functional differences between subsets. Future studies investigating the specific contributions of monocyte subsets to TREM1-driven inflammation could provide additional mechanistic insights and refine our understanding of their roles in IBD pathogenesis.

Our study also highlights notable differences between human and murine TREM1 ligands, with important implications for preclinical modeling and the evaluation of clinical molecules. We found that the PGLYRP1 and PGN complex acts as a ligand for human TREM1 but does not activate murine TREM1. Additionally, while other ligands, such as cold-inducible RNA-binding protein (CIRP)38 and HMGB156, have been reported in the literature as TREM1 ligands, we were unable to reproduce these interactions in our studies. The identity of murine TREM1 ligands remains elusive, suggesting that there may be fundamental differences in the upstream trigger of TREM1 pathway between species. Despite these upstream differences, our data demonstrate that downstream TREM1 signaling outputs, including neutrophil hyperactivation and cytokine production, are conserved. The use of a multivalent agonistic antibody in the DSS model bypasses species-specific ligand differences and allows for the study of conserved inflammatory consequences of TREM1 activation in vivo. These findings underscore the challenges and nuances of using mouse models to study TREM1-mediated pathways and highlight the need for validation in humanized mouse models or in vitro human systems to ensure translatability of therapeutic strategies. Understanding these species-specific differences is critical for accurately interpreting preclinical data and successfully advancing TREM1-targeted therapies for IBD and other inflammatory diseases.

In conclusion, our study elucidates the mechanistic role of TREM1 in neutrophil hyperactivation and microbe-immune cell interactions, offering significant insights into the pathogenesis and treatment of IBD. These findings invite further exploration into therapeutic interventions targeting the TREM1 pathway and suggest that TREM1-related readouts may inform future biomarker development.

Methods

Ethics and consent for human data and samples

All human samples and data used in this study are de‑identified and were collected under institutional review board‑approved protocols with written informed consent, as detailed in the respective Methods subsections (“Primary human cells and cell lines”, “sTREM1 protein and PGLYRP1 RNA measurements for IBD patient blood samples” and “In situ hybridization for TREM1 in human colon biopsies”). No directly identifying information or combinations of indirect identifiers that would allow re‑identification of individual participants are reported. Age and other numerical data are presented in aggregate, and sex is reported only at the group level in accordance with Nature Portfolio’s policy on sex and gender reporting.

Mice

C57BL/6 J mice (JAX: 000664), aged 6–8 weeks, were obtained from Jackson Laboratory. All mouse studies were approved by the Genentech Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Mice were maintained individually ventilated cages in a specific pathogen-free facility, with a 14:10 light/dark cycle. Animal rooms were temperature and humidity controlled between 68–79 °F (20.0–26.1 °C) and 30–70% relative humidity, with 10–15 room air exchanges per hour. Animals had free access to autoclaved chow and water. Upon arrival, mice were acclimated and then randomly reassigned and group‑housed in separate cages according to treatment condition (e.g. DSS + isotype, DSS + muTREM1.MVago, untreated controls). Mice were euthanized at experimental endpoints by CO₂ inhalation followed immediately by cervical dislocation, in accordance with Genentech IACUC‑approved protocols. For the DSS experiment, only female mice aged 6-8 weeks were used to ensure stable group housing and minimize aggression-induced stress during experimental regrouping. A total of 10 mice per treatment group (DSS + isotype, DSS + muTREM1.MVago) and 4 naive control mice were included. Future studies should examine sex effects on TREM1-mediated colitis. Colitis was induced by adding 2.5% (w/v) colitis-grade DSS (MP Biomedicals, cat. #C83725) to the drinking water for 7 days. Water consumption was weighed daily to determine average intake per cage, and body weight and clinical condition were monitored daily according to IACUC-approved humane endpoint criteria. In the antibody treatment groups, mice received intraperitoneally injections of either muTREM1.MVago or isotype control at 1 mg/kg three times per week, starting on the first day of DSS administration. Colon length and density were measured on day 10; colon density was calculated as colon weight divided by length.

Primary human cells and cell lines

All studies involving human blood donors were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by WCG IRB (Western Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board), under protocol “Samples for Science (S4S)”, WCG IRB protocol number 20080040 (Genentech sponsor protocol CEHS‑CP 307.2). All volunteers were Genentech/Roche employees who met the S4S eligibility criteria and provided written informed consent before sample collection.

Primary human neutrophils and total monocytes were purified from whole blood obtained from healthy donors (both male and female, 30–50 years of age). Neutrophils and total monocytes were isolated using the EasySep Direct Human Neutrophil Isolation Kit (Stemcell Technologies, cat. #19666) and EasySep Direct Human Monocyte Isolation Kit (Stemcell Technologies, cat. #19669), respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions. All biological materials were handled in accordance with institutional biosafety policies. Cell lines used in this study were obtained from the Genentech cell bank. The NFAT-NanoLuc Jurkat reporter line and lentiviral constructs encoding human or murine TREM1 and DAP12 were generated in‑house at Genentech using standard cloning methods. Plasmids and cell lines are available from the corresponding author upon request. Jurkat NFAT‑nanoLuc, HEK293T and Caco‑2 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, cat. #11875-093) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, cat. #26170-043), 2 mM L‑glutamine (Gibco, cat. #35050061), 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco, cat. #15140-122) and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, cat. #15140-122) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and confirmed negative. Reporter cell lines Jurkat NFAT‑nanoLuc‑huTREM1/DAP12 (Jurkat‑TD human) and Jurkat NFAT‑nanoLuc‑muTREM1/DAP12 (Jurkat‑TD murine) were generated by lentiviral transduction followed by antibiotic selection.

Bacteria strains

Escherichia coli was grown in Luria-Bertani media (BD Difco, cat. #244620) with 180 rpm shaking at 37 °C. Staphylococcus aureus US300 was grown overnight in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) media (BD Difco, cat. #211825) with 250 rpm shaking at 37 °C. The panel of 15 gut commensal bacteria used for TREM1 activity screening was selected from species that we were able to isolate and routinely culture anaerobically from our in-house microbiome collection originally provided by Microbiotica Ltd57 isolated from IBD patients. These strains were grown at 37 °C, anaerobically in Yeast Casitone Fatty Acids (YCFA) media58, including Blautia faecis strain MCC0939, Blautia faecis strain MCC1424, Alistipes finegoldii MCC1353, Alistipes finegoldii MCC2969, Eisenbergiella massiliensis MCC4554, Eisenbergiella massiliensis MCC5271, Eisenbergiella tayi MCC4555, Eisenbergiella tayi MCC5257, Peptostreptococcus stomatis MCC4686, Peptostreptococcus stomatis MCC4354, Dorea formicigenerans MCC1977, Clostridium paraputrificum MCC0489, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii MCC5397, Hungatella hathewayi MCC0493, Tyzzerella nexilis MCC0477, Akkermansia muciniphila MCC0913, Bacteroides intestinalis MCC3066, Prevotella copri MCC1427, Roseburia intestinalis MCC1480 and Sutterella wadsworthensis MCC4457. After overnight culture, bacteria were centrifuged at 4000 x g for 10 min, and supernatant was collected and filtered using a 0.22 µM syringe filter. For in vitro infection, bacteria were measured by absorbance, OD600, on SpectraMax Plus (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA).

Luciferase reporter assay

For TREM1 reporter assays, human or murine Jurkat‑TD cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS and harvested at log phase. Cells were resuspended in assay medium at 1 × 10^6 cells/mL, and 100 μL (1 × 10^5 cells) were plated per well in white 96‑well flat‑bottom plates (Corning). Cells were stimulated as indicated (e.g., plate‑bound agonistic antibodies, PGN-PGLYRP1 complexes, bacterial supernatants) and incubated for 16–18 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After stimulation, plates were equilibrated to room temperature for ~10 min, and an equal volume (100 μL) of Nano‑Glo Luciferase Assay reagent (Nano‑Glo Luciferase Assay System, Promega, cat. #N1110) diluted in Nano‑Glo buffer according to the manufacturer’s instructions was added to each well. Plates were gently mixed on an orbital shaker for 2–3 min and incubated for an additional 5 min at room temperature. Luminescence was measured using a SpectraMax or equivalent plate luminometer (Molecular Devices) with an integration time of 0.5–1 s per well. Background signal from unstimulated cells was subtracted, and data were reported as relative light units (RLU).

Antibody generation and characterization

Human and murine TREM1 antibodies, and isotype control monoclonal antibodies were developed and produced in-house at Genentech using standard technology. Lead candidates were selected based on the inhibitory or agonistic activity measured using Jurkat-TD(human) or Jurkat-TD(murine), respectively. For blocking activity screening, Jurkat-TD(human) cells were pre-incubated with various clones in a dose-response curve (0.001 to 100 nM) and stimulated with 6 μg/mL PGN from E. coli K12 (PGN-ECndss, InvivoGen, cat. #tlrl‑pgnk), PGN from Staphylococcus aureus (InvivoGen, cat. #tlrl-pgns2) and Bacillus subtilis (InvivoGen, cat. #tlrl-pgnb3), plus 3 μg/mL PGLYRP1 (R&D Systems, cat. #2590‑PGB). After stimulation, luciferase activity was measured using the Nano‑Glo Luciferase Assay System. For murine TREM1 agonist activity, several clones were incubated with Jurkat-TD(murine) cells, and luciferase activity was measured. Upon selecting the leading candidate, a murine TREM1 multivalent agonistic antibody (muTREM1.MVago) with 4 Fab units was generated. Agonist activity was assessed in a dose-response curve (0.001 to 100 μg/mL) and luciferase activity measured.

In vitro culture and stimulation

For Jurkat-TD cell activation, cells (105) were cultured in a 96-well plate with either human TREM1 agonist antibody (TREM1.ago) (R&D Systems, clone 193015 and cat.#MAB1278) or mouse TREM1 agonist antibody (muTREM1.ago) (R&D Systems, clone 174031 and cat. #MAB1187) bound to the plate (5 μg/mL), or stimulated with 3 μg/mL PGLYRP1 combined with PGN from Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), or Escherichia coli (E. coli) (6 μg/mL). After 16-18 h of incubation, luciferase activity was measured using the Nano‑Glo Luciferase Assay System. For cytokine production, neutrophils or monocytes were plated in a 96-well plate with plate-bound TREM1.ago or isotype control (5 μg/mL) or stimulated in vitro with 3 μg/mL PGN-ECndss alone or combined with 2 μg/mL PGLYRP1. Cytokine levels and MPO were measured by ELISA (R&D).

Bacterial experiments

For TREM1 activation using bacterial culture supernatants, Jurkat parental, Jurkat-TD cells were used. Bacterial supernatants (30 μL) were incubated with 3 μg/mL PGLYRP1 (R&D) for 1 h, and the mixture was added to 105 cells per well in a 96-well assay plate (Costar, cat. #3917). Cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, and luciferase activity was then measured using the Nano‑Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega). For lysozyme treatment, bacterial supernatants were treated with 100 μg/mL lysozyme (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #10837059001) for 30 min at 37 °C prior to incubation with PGLYRP1. For TREM1 activation using live bacteria, S. aureus and B. faecis MCC0939 and MCC1424 were incubated with 3 μg/mL PGLYRP1 (R&D) for 1 h, or bacteria (MOI 50) were incubated with freshly purified neutrophils for 18 h. The mixture was then added to 105 cells per well in a 96-well assay plate. Jurkat cells were incubated overnight, and luciferase activity was then measured using the Nano‑Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega). Monocytes and neutrophils were pre-incubated with 10 ng/mL anti-TREM1 blocking antibody and then infected with B. faecis (MOI 10) alone or pre-incubated with 3 μg/mL PGLYRP1 for 1 h. TNF-α secretion by monocytes was measured by ELISA after 16 h of infection, and ROS production was measured in real-time for neutrophils. For PGLYRP1 secretion, neutrophils (105) were infected with E. coli (MOI 10) for 4 h or stimulated with 10 ng/mL PMA (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #P1885) for 1 h. PGLYRP1 levels were measured by ELISA (R&D).

Neutrophil degranulation

Freshly isolated neutrophils from healthy donors were preincubated for 30 min with TREM1 blocking antibody (anti-TREM1) and stimulated for 2 h with 3 μg/mL PGN-ECndss plus 2 μg/mL PGLYRP1. Neutrophils were cultured with either plate-bound TREM1 agonist antibody (TREM1.ago) or isotype control at 5 µg/ml for 1 h. Translocation of degranulation markers to the plasma membrane was assessed using flow cytometry after cell treatment. Degranulation was measured by CD63 translocation to the cellular membrane.

ROS detection

ROS production was measured by Lucigenin-dependent chemiluminescence (LDCL) assay. Briefly, neutrophils (0.2 × 106) were stimulated with plate-bound TREM1 agonist antibody (TREM1.ago) and the complex formed by PGN/PGLYRP1 with or without anti-TREM1 blocking antibody. Neutrophils were also stimulated with supernatant from TREM1-activated monocytes or Blautia faecis preincubated with PGLYRP1, and 10−4 M Lucigenin (MCE, cat. #HY-D0720) was added. The reaction was monitored in real time using a luminometer (FlexStation 3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) at 37 °C in intervals of 5 min. The results are measured as Relative Light Units (RLU).

NETosis measurement by Incucyte

For the NETosis assay, freshly purified neutrophils were cultured in a 96-well plate with either plate-bound TREM1.ago or isotype control antibodies (5 µg/ml) in the presence of Cytotox Green Dye (Sartorius, cat. #4633) (250 nM) to visualize NET release (extracellular DNA). NETosis was evaluated in real time for 24 h using the Incucyte Live-Cell Analysis System, and data was collected every 30 min. PMA (10 nM) was used as a positive control. Fluorescent masking incorporates green objects of all sizes (no area filter), but cells undergoing NETosis yield a larger average object area than regular cell death. By excluding smaller objects (dead cells), the NETosis signal can be distinguished from other types of cell death.

Quantification of MPO-DNA complex for NET measurement

The quantification of NETs was performed by measuring MPO-DNA complexes using an ELISA-based method. Briefly, an ELISA plate was coated with an anti-MPO antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, # PA5-16672) to capture MPO-DNA complexes from plasma or neutrophil supernatants. After incubation, bound DNA within the complexes was detected using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# P11496), and fluorescence was measured with a microplate reader. The fluorescence intensity, proportional to the concentration of MPO-DNA complexes, was compared to a standard curve to determine NET levels in the samples.

Supernatant from TREM1-activated monocytes for neutrophil chemotaxis and ROS

Supernatant from TREM1-activated monocytes was employed as a chemoattractant to assess Neutrophil chemotaxis and ROS induction. Monocytes were stimulated either with plate-bound TREM1 agonist antibody or isotype control. Alternatively, monocytes were preincubated with an anti-TREM1 blocking antibody (10 ng/ml) and subsequently stimulated with a complex composed of 3 µg/ml PGN-ECndss and 2 µg/ml PGLYRP1. Supernatants were collected 18 h post-stimulation. For chemotaxis assays, 150 µl of the collected supernatant was pretreated with an anti-Human IL-8 antibody (0.2 µg/ml, R&D, cat. #MAB208-SP) for 30 min and then transferred to the bottom chamber of an HTS Transwell-96 permeable support plate (Corning, cat. #3387) with a 5.0 µm pore size. Neutrophils (105) were placed in the upper chamber, and migration was analyzed after 2 h. To quantify Neutrophil migration, 105 neutrophils were seeded directly into the bottom chamber to represent 100% migration. Quantification was performed using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, cat. #G7570). Recombinant Human IL-8 (10 ng/ml, PeproTech, cat. #200-08) served as a positive control for migration. For ROS production assays, the same supernatants were preincubated with the anti-TNF-alpha neutralizing antibody (200 ng/ml, Sino Biological, cat. #10602-MM0N1) before being added to Neutrophils (0.2 × 106). ROS production was then measured in real time by Lucigenin‑dependent chemiluminescence (MCE, cat. #HY-D0720) using a FlexStation 3 plate reader at 37 °C, with readings taken every 5 min, and data reported as relative light units.

ELISA and Luminex

The levels of TNF-α, TL1A, IL-8, IL-23, PGLYRP1, CCL20, and MPO in Monocyte or Neutrophil supernatants were assessed using ELISA kits (TNF ELISA (R&D Systems, cat. #DTA00D), IL‑8 ELISA (R&D, cat. #D8000C), IL‑23 ELISA (R&D, cat. #D2300B), TL1A ELISA (R&D, cat. #DTLA00B), CCL20 ELISA (R&D, cat. #DM3A00), PGLYRP1 ELISA (R&D, cat. #DY1910), MPO ELISA (R&D, cat. #DY3174)) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. For cytokine measurements in a DSS-induced colitis model, colonic tissue was collected and incubated at 4 °C for 15 min in 1x Cell Lysis Buffer (Cell Signaling, cat. #9803S) containing a complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, cat. #11836170001). The samples were homogenized using a TissueLyser III (QIAGEN, cat. #85210) at 30 Hz for 6 min. Lysates were centrifuged twice at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove debris. The processed samples were analyzed for cytokine and chemokine levels using the MILLIPLEX Mouse Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel (Millipore, cat. #MCYTOMAG‑70 K).

Human and mouse mature PGLYRP1 purification and electrophoresis analyses

Human and mouse mature PGLYRP1 constructs engineered with a N-terminal Flag tag were expressed in HEK293 cells and captured from the supernatant using a 10-ml anti-Flag column prepared in-house. The column was washed with a buffered solution at pH 7.5 containing 25 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.1% v/v Triton X-114 and 0.1% v/v Triton X-100. Bound proteins were eluted using a buffered solution at pH 3.0 containing 50 mM sodium citrate, 150 mM NaCl, pH 3.0 and neutralized using 1 M arginine, 400 mM succinic acid, pH 9.0. Eluate was subjected to size exclusion chromatography using either Superdex S200 column for human PGLYRP1 or Superdex S75 column for mouse PGLYRP1 connected to an AKTA Pure Fast Protein Liquid Chromatography (FPLC) system, with phosphate-buffered saline used as running buffer. Peak fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE in the presence (reducing) or absence (nonreducing) of DTT in the sample buffer.

Isolation of cells from the colon

For colon digestion, 2 cm of distal colon tissue was harvested on day 10 and washed with Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) to remove mucus and fecal residue. The tissues were then subjected to a non-enzymatic digestion with 2 mL of RPMI supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum FBS, 20 mM EDTA and 1 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT). After incubating on a shaker at 37 °C for 30 min, the epithelial cell layer was removed by vigorous shaking for 15 s. The colon pieces were then resuspended in 2 mL RPMI medium supplemented with 1% FBS, 2 mg/ml collagenase II (Gibco, #17101015), 0.5 mg/ml Dispase (Gibco, #17105041) and 100 µg/ml Dnase I (Roche, #10104159001). The mixture was incubated at 37 °C while shaking for an hour. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer for downstream flow cytometry analysis.

Flow cytometry

For assessing TREM1 expression in human white blood cells, whole blood from healthy donors was first treated with RBC Lysis Buffer (Cell Signaling). The resulting total cells were incubated with a FACS buffer containing Fc block (1:10 v/v, Miltenyi #130-092-575) and stained with, Live/Dead Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain (1:1000 v/v, Invitrogen #L34957) 30 min, APC-conjugated anti-human CD354/TREM1 antibody (clone TREM-26, 1:200 v/v, BioLegend #314909). After staining, cells were washed three times with a FACS buffer (300 x g, 5 min) before acquisition. Flow cytometry data were acquired using a BD FACSymphony A5 Cell Analyzer equipped with laser lines 405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm, 640 nm and analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1 software (Tree Star). Representative gating strategies are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1b.

For in vivo experiments, colon-isolated cells were resuspended in a 96-well round-bottom plate and incubated with FACS buffer containing Fc block (1:10 v/v, Miltenyi #130-092-575) for 20 min. Primary antibody staining was performed at 1:400 v/v and Live/Dead staining (Invitrogen, #L34955) at 1:2000 v/v for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. The antibody panel consisted of: Live/Dead Aqua (1:2000), Anti-CD45-BV510 (clone 30-F11, 1:400, Biolegend #103138), Anti-CD11b-PE-Cy7 (clone M1/70, 1:400, Invitrogen #46-0112-82), Anti-Ly6G-FITC (clone 1A8, 1:400, Biolegend #127607), Anti-Ly6C-APC (clone AL-21, 1:400, Biolegend #128016), Anti-CD64-BV605 (clone X54-5/7.1, 1:400, Biolegend #139323), Anti-CD4-BV421 (clone GK1.5, 1:400, BD #562891), Anti-TCRβ-PerCP-Cy5.5 (clone H57-597, 1:400, BD #561393), Anti-IL-17A-PE (clone TC11-18H10.1, 1:400, Biolegend #506904). Cells were then washed three times with FACS buffer, centrifuging at 300 x g for 5 min after each wash. For intracellular staining, Foxp3 staining kit (Invitrogen, #00-5523-00) was employed following the manufacturer’s manual. Flow cytometry acquisition was performed using a BD FACSymphony A5 Cell Analyzer equipped with laser lines 405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm, 640 nm. Compensation was performed using single-stained controls. Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1 software (Tree Star). Gating strategies are provided in Supplementary Fig. 4j-k. A complete list of antibodies used for flow cytometry, including clones, fluorophores, vendors, catalog numbers and dilutions, is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence staining of PGLYRP1 binding to Blautia faecis

Blautia faecis strains were cultured at 37 °C for approximately 16 h in YCFA medium inside a Coy anaerobic chamber under a headspace of 85% N₂, 5% H₂, and 10% CO₂. The resulting bacterial cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated for 10 min in PBS containing 0.5% BSA and 0.05% Tween-20. For protein binding, the samples were incubated in PBS containing 60 μg/mL hPGLYRP1 (R&D Systems, Cat# 2590-PGB) for 1 h at room temperature, washed twice with PBS, and subsequently incubated with a biotinylated hPGLYRP1 detection antibody (1:100 dilution, R&D Systems, Cat# 842768) and 5 μg/mL Hoechst 33342. Following two additional PBS washes, the cells were incubated with streptavidin-conjugated Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000 dilution, Invitrogen, Cat# S21381) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Stained bacterial cells were washed three times with PBS and mounted between a coverslip and a 1% agarose pad for visualization. Confocal imaging was performed using a Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted confocal microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc.) equipped with an UltraVIEW VoX system (PerkinElmer Inc.) and a 100× oil immersion lens objective (numerical aperture [NA], 1.40). Micrographs were captured at designated time points using an ORCA-Flash 4.0 complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS) camera (Hamamatsu Photonics), acquired using Volocity software (Quorum Technologies), and processed using Fiji.

TEER assay

Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was used as an indicator of monolayer confluence and integrity. Caco-2 cells were plated in HTS Transwell 96 Permeable Supports with 0.4 µm polyester membrane (Corning, cat. #7369). TEER of the Caco-2 monolayers was measured routinely using the REMS AutoSampler (World Precision Instruments) prior to experiments and was only included when the TEER value exceeded 500 Ω·cm². Monocytes (3 × 105) plus neutrophils (6 × 105) were stimulated in the bottom chamber with plate-bound TREM1 agonist antibody or isotype control. Alternatively, monocytes were preincubated with an anti-TREM1 blocking antibody (10 ng/mL) and subsequently stimulated with a complex formed by 3 µg/mL PGN-ECndss and 2 µg/mL PGLYRP1 or infected with B. faecis (MOI 10). TEER values were recorded immediately before stimulation (baseline) and 24 h post-stimulation. Control conditions included B. faecis or the PGN/PGLYRP1 complex without monocytes and neutrophils.

Murine TREM1 binding assay and activity

The binding interaction of murine TREM1 with murine PGLYRP1 or CIRP1 was assessed using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) on a BIAcore 8k+ instrument (Cytiva). Recombinant mouse TREM1-Fc protein (R&D, cat. #1187-TR) was captured on a Protein A biosensor chip (Cytiva) at approximately 700 response units (RU). A 3-fold serial dilution of murine PGLYRP1 (R&D, cat. #2696-PGB) or CIRP (US Biologicals, cat. #517846) (ranging from 22.2 nM to 600 nM) was injected over the chip surface in HBS-EP buffer (0.01 M HEPES, pH 7.4, 0.15 M NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 0.005% v/v Surfactant P20) at 25 °C with a flow rate of 30 µL/min for 2 min followed by a 10 min dissociation period. Association rates (Kon) and dissociation rates (Koff) were calculated using the BIAcore Evaluation Software, employing a simple one-to-one Langmuir binding model. The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was derived as the ratio Koff/Kon. For the activation analysis of murine TREM1, Jurkat-TD cells were treated with murine PGLYRP1 (R&D), PGLYRP2, PGLYRP3, or PGLYRP4 (5 µg/mL), combined with PGN-ECndss (10 µg/mL). Alternatively, cells were treated with murine CIRP (US Biological), murine HMGB1 (Acro biosystem, cat. #HM1-M52H6), or necrotic cell lysate (NCL). After 16–18 h of incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, luciferase activity was measured using the Nano‑Glo Luciferase Assay System. Plate-coated murine TREM1 agonist (muTREM1.ago) served as a positive control.

Proteomics of neutrophil secretome and analysis

For neutrophil secretome analysis, 3 × 106 freshly purified neutrophils were stimulated with either vehicle control (DMSO) or PMA (10 ng/mL) for 1 h. Cells were then centrifuged twice at 400 x g for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and filtered using an Ultracentrifugal Filter with a 10 kDa MWCO (Millipore) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The flow-through was discarded, and the retentate was processed for proteomic analysis.

Protein concentration was measured using Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #23200). Proteins ( ~ 14 µg/condition) were reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 37˚C for 1 h followed by alkylation with 15 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) at room temperature (RT) for 20 min in the dark. Samples were diluted to a final concentration of 2 M urea prior to digestion with 0.5 µg Lys-C(FUJIFILM Wako, cat. #129‑02541) at 37˚ C for 2 h followed by 0.5 µg Trypsin Gold, mass spectrometry grade (Promega, cat. #V5280) digestion at 37˚C overnight.

The digest mixtures were acidified with 20% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) followed by desalting using SDB StageTips (GL Sciences, cat. #5010‑27720). Samples were loaded onto the tips followed by washes with 2 × 100 µL 0.1% TFA and equilibration with 3 × 100 µL HEPES buffer (100 mM, pH 7.2). On-tip TMT labeling was performed using TMTpro 16‑plex reagents (Thermo Fisher, cat. #A44520), at 5:1 reagent:peptide ratio by centrifuging at 300 g until all the reagents went through. The flowthrough was collected and re-loaded once more. The tips were washed with 3 × 100 µL of 5%ACN/0.1% TFA, and labeled peptides were eluted with 2 × 20 µL of 60% ACN/0.1% TFA. Peptides from each condition were combined followed by drying in the SpeedVac and subjected to high pH reverse phase fractionation on the Agilent 1100 HPLC (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). The peptide mixture was reconstituted in solvent A (5% ACN/50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, pH 8.0) and separated on a Zorbax 300Extend-C18, 3.5 µm, 4.6 × 150 mm column (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. A gradient from 15–45% solvent B (90% ACN/50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, pH 8.0) was applied over 49 min with a total run time of 75 min. Ninety-six fractions were collected at 0.63 min interval and every 25th fraction was combined into a set of 24 fractions. Fractions were acidified with 20% TFA, dried and desalted using SDB StageTips prior to mass spectrometry analysis.

Desalted peptides were reconstituted in 2% ACN/0.1% formic acid (FA)/water and loaded onto Aurora Series 25 cm × 75 um I.D. column (IonOpticks) using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 RSLC nano Proflow system (ThermoFisher). Peptide separation was performed at 300 nL/min with a 2-step linear gradient where solvent B (0.1% FA/2% water/ACN) was increased from 4% to 30% over 68 min then from 30% to 75% B over 4.9 min with a total analysis time of 95 min. Peptides were analyzed using an Orbitrap Eclipse instrument (ThermoFisher Scientific, San Jose, CA). A real-time search against a human database was employed using an in-house instrument API program called InSeqAPI59–61. Protein-closeout was employed (3 distinct peptides/protein/run). Precursor ions (MS1) were analyzed in the Orbitrap (250% normalized AGC target, 120,000 mass resolution, 50 ms maximum injection time) with 10 most abundant species selected for MS2 fragmentation. Ions were filtered based on charge state ≥ 2 (z = 2, 3 & 4–6) and monoisotopic peak assignment and dynamic exclusion (45 s ± 10 ppm) was enabled. Each precursor was isolated at a mass width of 0.5 Th followed by fragmentation using collision-induced dissociation (CID at 30 NCE), 150% normalized AGC target with a maximum injection time of 100 ms. Multiple fragment ions were isolated using synchronous precursor selection (SPS) prior to MS3 HCD fragmentation (45 NCE, SPS notches = 8, AGC target = 3.0E5, maximum injection time of 400 ms). MS3 scans were analyzed in the Orbitrap at 50,000 resolution. Raw datafiles were deposited into the MassIVE repository with the identifier: MSV000099142.

MS/MS data was searched using the Comet search algorithm against a concatenated forward-reverse target-decoy database (UniProtKBconcat, downloaded Oct 2023) consisting of Homo sapiens proteins and common contaminant sequences. Spectra were assigned using a precursor mass tolerance of 20 ppm and fragment ion settings for low resolution MS/MS with a fragment ion bin tolerance and bin offset of 1.0005 and 0.4, respectively. Static modifications included carbamidomethyl cysteine ( + 57.0215 Da), TMT tag ( + 304.2071 Da) on both the N-termini of the peptides and lysine residues. Variable modifications included oxidized methionine ( + 15.994 Da) and TMT tag on tyrosine residues ( + 304.2071 Da). Trypsin specificity with up to 1 miscleavage was specified. Peptide spectral matches were filtered at 1% false discovery rate (FDR). The TMT reporter ion quantification was performed using an in-house Mojave module62 by calculating the highest peak within 20 ppm of theoretical reporter mass windows and correcting for isotope purities.

Statistical analysis of Mass Spectrometry data

The R package MSstatsTMT v.2.0.1 was used to preprocess PSM-level quantification before statistical analysis, to have protein quantification and to perform differential abundance analysis63. Briefly, the preprocess filtered out TMT peaks in MS3 scans whose sum was less than 30,000 across all 16 channels. Peptides shorter than 7 residues were also filtered out. The abundance of each identified protein was estimated by using a model fitted with Tukey Median Polish summarization, with imputation of missing values below a censoring threshold of 28. MSstatsTMT estimated log2(fold change) and the standard error by linear mixed effect model for each protein. The inference procedure was adjusted by applying an empirical Bayes shrinkage. To test the two-sided null hypothesis of no changes in abundance, the model-based test statistics were compared to the Student t-test distribution with the degrees of freedom appropriate for each protein and each dataset. The resulting p-values were adjusted to control the FDR with the method by Benjamini-Hochberg.

Bulk RNA sequencing

For bulk RNA sequencing of monocytes upon TREM1 activation, freshly purified monocytes (3 × 105) were stimulated with plate-coated TREM1 agonist antibody or isotype control, or alternatively with 3 µg/mL PGN-ECndss plus 2 µg/mL PGLYRP1 for 4 h in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen), and samples were prepared for sequencing as described. Briefly, total RNA was quantified with the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quality was assessed using RNA ScreenTape on TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies). cDNA library was generated from 2 nanograms of total RNA using the Smart-Seq V4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (Takara, cat. #634890). 150 picograms of cDNA were used to make sequencing libraries with the Nextera XT DNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, cat. #FC‑131‑1024). Libraries were quantified with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, cat. #Q32852) and the average library size was determined using D1000 ScreenTape on TapeStation 4200 (Agilent, cat. #5067‑5582). Libraries were pooled and sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) to generate 30 millions single-end 50-base pair reads for each sample. RNA-seq reads data has been deposited to Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE308014).

sTREM1 protein and PGLYRP1 RNA measurements for IBD patient blood samples

IBD patient samples and associated clinical data were obtained from the EMBARK study45. The EMBARK cohort included both male and female patients with Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis; sex was recorded in the clinical metadata and considered in descriptive summaries, but no sex‑stratified analyses were performed in this study. The EMBARK study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and was approved by the institutional review board or independent ethics committee at each participating site; all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Serum sTREM1 was analyzed using standard enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (R&D Systems, cat. #DTRM10C). RNA expression from blood was measured using the Fluidigm platform. Briefly, cDNA synthesis was performed on 100 ng total-RNA using an iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-rad, cat. #1708891). Gene-specific pre-amplification was performed (Applied Biosystems) of target genes and housekeeping genes HPRT1, RPL19 and GAPDH. Fluidigm was performed using the BioMark 48.48 Dynamic Arrays (Fluidigm Corporation) using the manufacturer’s protocol. Data were collected using the BioMark Data Collection Software and CT values were obtained using the BioMark RT-PCR Analysis Software (V.2.1.1, Fluidigm). The relative abundance (ΔCT) to the geometric mean of HPRT1/GAPDH/RPL19 was calculated: 2^ − (average Ct gene − average CT geomean [HPRT1/GAPDH/RPL19]). Gene-specific pre-amplification and qPCR were performed using commercially available TaqMan probe sets for HPRT1 (cat: #4331182), GAPDH (cat. #4331182), RPL19 (cat. #4331182), and PGLYRP1 (cat. #4331182); no custom oligonucleotides were designed for this study.

Data integration and analysis

Publicly available microarray data (E-GEOD-23597, E-GEOD-52746, GSE112366, GSE16879, GSE42296, GSE73661, GSE92415) were downloaded from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and other publicly available repositories, combining studies on CD, UC, and healthy controls. Metadata, including disease state, treatment type, tissue type, and treatment response, were standardized to ensure consistency across datasets. Sex/gender information was in metadata when available. Gene expression data were processed to identify common genes across all datasets, excluding ribosomal protein genes. Expression metrics were batch-corrected using linear modeling to account for differences in datasets and Affymetrix platform types. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to confirm the removal of batch effects.

The single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) scoring was performed with the TREM1 pathway gene signature, which includes 14 genes (RAB3IL1, MMP10, CCL7, SLC26A11, SERPINB2, IL36RN, SUCNR1, MGLL, CXCL5, NLN, VEGFA, GNG11, TFPI2, TNFSF15). 5 genes (TM4SF19, GEM, PLPP3, SLC22A18AS, FNIP2) were excluded from the signature due to their absence in the microarray datasets. The ssGSEA analysis was performed on the batch-corrected public microarray data using the gsva package (v1.50.0). Statistical significance across different groups was determined using the Wilcoson rank-sum test, implemented with the built-in R function. All visualizations were created using the ggplot2 package (v3.5.2).

Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNAseq) data from Crohn’s disease patient biopsies42. Processed scRNAseq data were obtained from the accompanying public repository and analyzed using R (v4.2.2) and the Seurat package (v4.3.0). Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes defined in the original publication. TREM1 and PGLYRP1 expressions were quantified on a per-cell basis using normalized transcript counts. The percentage of cells expressing each gene within cell populations was calculated and visualized.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism or built-in R functions. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired t-tests were used to assess statistical significance. In cases where the variances of the two groups differed significantly, as determined by an F-test, Welch’s correction was applied to the t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was used as appropriate. Non-parametric tests, such as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test were applied where data did not meet normality assumptions. Correlations were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients, depending on the distribution of the data. Statistical significance P values are indicated on the graphs, and all data are reported as mean ± SD. Sample sizes were chosen based on prior experience with similar experimental systems and reflect standard practices in the field. No formal statistical method was used to predetermine sample size.
