Reinforcing measles control in the Americas amid mass gatherings: detection, response, and traveler health implications
Authors: Pan American Health Organization
Published: 2026

17 JUNE 2026, WASHINGTON, D.C. —

More than 184 000 suspected measles/rubella cases have been reported worldwide in 2026, reflecting widespread transmission across all WHO regions. In the Americas Region, confirmed cases have reached 20 521 by EW 20 -- with 25 deaths -- representing a four-fold increase from the number reported in the same period in 2025. Against this backdrop, mass gatherings and cross-border travel act as amplifiers of transmission, particularly in populations with suboptimal vaccination coverage. Strengthening active case-finding, maintaining high immunity levels, and ensuring pre-travel vaccination are essential to reducing the risk of measles importation and onward spread.

These trends are further characterized by the global distribution and confirmation of cases across regions. Across the six WHO regions, 100 239 (54.3%) measles cases were confirmed by laboratory testing, clinical or epidemiological criteria. The WHO South-East Asia Region accounted for 29% of reported cases, followed by the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region with 21%, while the WHO African Region and the Americas each accounted for 19% (Figure 1).

In the Region of the Americas, cases were reported by 16 countries and one territory. Altogether, the 5 123 cases reported in 2025 and 20 521 confirmed cases in 2026 represent the highest regional measles activity since 2019, when the Americas recorded the highest number of confirmed cases in the previous 22 years (n = 23 269) (Figure 2). By EW 20 of 2026, the largest numbers of confirmed cases were reported by Mexico (n=10 920, including 13 deaths), Guatemala (n=6209, including 12 deaths), the United States of America (n=1 952), Canada (n=1 018), Peru (n=301), and Bolivia (n=70) (Figure 3).

Available data on confirmed cases in 2026 in the Americas show a higher proportion among people aged 20–29 (24%), followed by those aged 10–19 (20%) and 30–39 (16%). However, the incidence was highest among children under one year of age (9.7 cases per 100 000 population), followed by children aged 1–4 years (4.1 per 100 000) and 5–9 years (3.0 per 100 000). Forty-five percent of cases were unvaccinated, and for 45%, vaccination status was unknown or unavailable. The source of infection was identified as unknown in 36% of cases, related to imported cases in 23%, endemic in 6%, and imported in 2%.

DISCUSSION

Global and regional increases in measles highlight the need to achieve and maintain ≥95% coverage with two doses of the MMR (measles, mumps, and rubella) vaccine, while urgently identifying and closing population immunity gaps as well as strengthening timely surveillance and effective rapid response capacities. These actions are critical to preventing the reintroduction and sustained transmission of the virus in countries of the Americas, and to reestablishing elimination status where endemic transmission has resumed.

Recommendations on vaccination, surveillance and rapid response, mass gatherings, and international travel should be tailored to the epidemiological situation of each country or territory. Measures vary depending on whether a country is experiencing community transmission, limited transmission chains, or no confirmed cases.

Disseminating recommendations through appropriate channels is also part of the response, particularly in the context of international travel, large-scale events with broad international participation, and the occurrence of other public health events. In this regard, public awareness campaigns may be considered to promote travelers’ health before and after travel, encourage responsible behaviors regarding measles vaccination, and raise awareness of the signs and symptoms of this disease. Depending on the context, these initiatives may involve health services, travel health providers, and other travel-related stakeholders.

RECOMMENDATIONS

Countries with areas of community transmission should implement indiscriminate mass vaccination activities, administering one additional dose of measles containing vaccine for high-risk groups including infants from 6 to 11 months, complete two-dose vaccination schedules, and maintain outbreak response vaccination strategies in areas without community transmission. Surveillance, rapid response and multisectoral coordination capacities should be strengthened to implement outbreak response measures.

Countries with areas having limited transmission chains should aim to stay ahead of virus transmission by rapidly closing vaccination gaps in affected and at-risk areas. Outbreak response vaccination, using selective vaccination strategies such as ring or mop-up interventions, should begin upon identification of a suspected case, without waiting for laboratory confirmation, including completion of the two-dose schedule. Suspected cases, contacts, and imported or travel-related cases should be managed in accordance with national surveillance and rapid response protocols, including notification through the International Health Regulations National Focal Point as appropriate.

Countries with no confirmed cases should maintain vaccine and supply stockpiles for rapid vaccination response to imported cases. Vaccination intensification activities should be based on measles and rubella risk analysis to address vaccination gaps, while routine vaccination services should be strengthened to achieve at least 95% coverage. Surveillance should be strengthened in priority areas in health services and the community by implementing active case finding. Operational plans for preparedness and rapid response should also be reviewed and adjusted as needed.

For mass gatherings and international travelers, countries should review surveillance performance and vaccination coverage to identify areas of highest risk and implement preventive actions. In the context of the 2026 FIFA World Cup™, as well as other mass gathering events, countries should increase surveillance sensitivity and provide information and vaccination services to travelers. Travelers aged six months or older who cannot provide proof of vaccination or immunity should be advised to receive one dose of measles-rubella vaccine, preferably two weeks before traveling to areas with documented transmission and should be informed about symptoms and actions to take if illness develops.
