Remote ischemic postconditioning improves cognitive dysfunction after subarachnoid hemorrhage by driving metabolic reprogramming of border-associated macrophages through the IL-33/ST2 axis
Authors: Jiru Zhou, Xiaoguo Li, Peiwen Guo, Zichao Huang, Jianjun Zhong, Yuanshu Li, Chongjie Cheng, Daochen Wen, Yajun Zhu, Xingwei Lei, Wenyan Li, Zhi Chen, Yujie Chen, Xiaochuan Sun, Zongduo Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a critical cerebrovascular disease with high mortality and morbidity. Despite advances in treatments that have reduced mortality, survivors frequently suffer from persistent cognitive dysfunction. Border-associated macrophages (BAMs) play essential nutritional and immunoregulatory roles in neurological diseases; however, their dynamic alterations following SAH and their effects on cognitive outcomes remain unclear. Remote ischemic postconditioning (RIPostC) is an emerging neuroprotective approach, but its mechanisms in the context of SAH have not been fully elucidated. This study aimed to investigate the role of BAMs in cognitive dysfunction after SAH and to explore the effects and underlying mechanisms of RIPostC intervention.MethodsA mouse model of SAH was established and combined with multiomics analyses to systematically assess the spatiotemporal changes in BAMs during the subacute phase after SAH and their relationship with cognitive dysfunction. The effects of RIPostC on BAMs abundance, immunoregulatory function, and cognitive outcomes were evaluated. The molecular mechanisms were further examined using chimeric bone marrow (BM) mice.ResultsIn the subacute phase following SAH, BAMs abundance was reduced and correlated with the severity of myelin damage and cognitive impairment. RIPostC treatment significantly increased BAMs abundance, promoted myelin regeneration, and improved cognitive function. Mechanistically, RIPostC induced amino acid metabolic reprogramming in BAMs via the IL-33/ST2 axis, thereby increasing the proliferation, migration, and myelination of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs). These beneficial effects were markedly diminished in WT/ST2 KO chimeric mice.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the critical involvement of BAMs in cognitive dysfunction after SAH and shows that RIPostC ameliorates cognitive deficits by modulating BAMs through the IL-33/ST2 axis. These findings provide new insights into the pathophysiology of SAH, support targeted interventions involving BAMs and the IL-33/ST2 axis, and offer important evidence for the clinical translation of RIPostC as a safe and noninvasive therapeutic strategy for SAH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03587-0.Keywords: Subarachnoid hemorrhage, Border-associated macrophages, Remote ischemic postconditioning, Metabolic reprogramming, Cognitive dysfunction

Introduction

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, aSAH) is one of the common critical cerebrovascular emergencies [1]. With advances in clinical diagnosis and treatment, surgical techniques, and neurocritical care, the mortality rate of SAH has decreased from 30 to 20% [2, 3]. However, survivors still experience long-term cognitive dysfunction [4, 5]. The incidence of cognitive dysfunction after SAH is high, and its duration is prolonged; even patients with mild cases may have varying degrees of cognitive impairment [6]. Previous clinical studies have shown that these adverse cognitive outcomes are closely related to microstructural abnormalities in white matter (myelin damage) and neuroinflammation after SAH [1, 7, 8]. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear and require further investigation.

Border-associated macrophages (BAMs) reside in brain boundary tissues, including the meninges, perivascular spaces, and choroid plexus. They constitute a myeloid cell population with a distinct developmental trajectory and immune-regulatory functions, possessing a unique lineage and immunomodulatory roles that differ from microglia, and serve as a window for CNS–peripheral immune interactions [9–11]. This cell population participates in immune surveillance, metabolism, and nutritional support, and serves as a bridge for peripheral-to-central immune communication [12]. Under pathological conditions of neurological diseases, BAMs secrete cytokines that influence the activity of immune cells in the brain parenchyma, participate in remodeling of the blood‒brain barrier, regulate neural plasticity, and promote myelin regeneration [13]. These characteristics position BAMs as potential upstream regulators of white matter integrity and cognitive function following subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). However, their spatiotemporal dynamics and functional states during the subacute phase of SAH, along with the association between these states and cognitive outcomes, remain to be systematically elucidated.

Remote ischemic postconditioning (RIPostC) is a clinically non-invasive (cuff-based) neuroprotective strategy that activates endogenous protective and immunomodulatory pathways through brief intermittent limb ischemia–reperfusion processes [14]. Previous studies have shown that RIPostC can reduce brain injury and improve cognitive function in small vessel or ischemic brain lesions by modulating peripheral-central immune cross-talk [14–16]. Whether RIPostC confers benefits following SAH, particularly its potential to influence the brain-boundary immune microenvironment (such as cerebral vascular macrophages) during the subacute window of active white matter remodeling, warrants further investigation.

Building on the above gaps in clinical and basic research, we propose that the dynamic changes of BAMs in brain boundary tissues during the subacute stage after SAH may represent the key hub linking SAH-related inflammation with white matter injury and long-term cognitive impairment. RIPostC may be able to modulate the immunometabolic state of BAMs at this stage to support oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs)-related myelin repair. Accordingly, this study will sequentially test this hypothesized chain using flow cytometric cell sorting, integrative multi-omics analyses, and other methods, and will investigate the therapeutic effects and mechanistic basis of RIPostC in improving cognitive dysfunction.

Methods

Animals

Wild-type adult male C57BL/6 J mice (8–12 weeks old, weighing 20–26 g) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Centre of Chongqing Medical University, and adult C57–Il1rl1 knockout mice (ST2 KO mice) were purchased from Jiangsu Jicui Pharmachem Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. All the mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF)-rated animal laboratory at Chongqing Medical University under conditions of 22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, and a 12-h light‒dark circadian cycle. The rats were provided ad libitum access to clean drinking water and standard chow. All experimental personnel were trained by the Experimental Animal Centre of Chongqing Medical University and held animal experiment permits. The experimental procedures strictly adhered to the Guidelines for Animal Experiments of Chongqing Medical University and the international ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. All the animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (IACUC) (Approval No. IACUC-CQMU-2024–04072), and the animal welfare guidelines were strictly followed.

Establishment of a mouse SAH model

All experimental animals used in this study were randomly grouped via Microsoft Excel software. Anesthesia was induced with 3% isoflurane and maintained with 2% isoflurane. Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) was induced via a modified endovascular perforation method [17]. A mouse SAH model was established. The Sham and SAH groups differed only in the final puncture step: the Sham group did not puncture the vessel, whereas the rest of the procedures were identical. The specific steps were as follows: after the neck skin was disinfected and prepared, a 1 cm incision was made in the midline of the neck, and the fascia was bluntly separated; the left thyroid gland was turned up to protect the left side; the carotid delta was exposed; the vagus nerve and the common, internal, and external carotid arteries were freed; a micro arterial clip (Yangzhou Kanghong Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Model KH-200) was used to occlude the common and internal carotid arteries; the external carotid artery was ligated and transected; and a 5–0 nylon wire was threaded through the carotid stump. After the operation was completed, the stump and carotid bifurcation were tightened with 5–0 nylon thread; the arterial clip was loosened, the external and internal carotid arteries were straightened, and the puncture wire was advanced 10–12 mm via microscopic forceps; if resistance was encountered, an additional 2-mm advancement was performed to pierce the vessel; the wire was withdrawn after 10 s, the stump of the external carotid artery was tightened to stop the hemorrhage, and the skin was closed to complete the surgery. SAH severity and hematoma volume were evaluated in terms of SAH grade and hemoglobin level at 24 h. Mice with SAH grades ≤ 7 were excluded from the study [18].

RIPostC treatment in mice

In this study, RIPostC treatment of the mice began 72 h after successful modeling. The mice were anesthetized and fixed, and after skin preparation and disinfection of both lower limbs, the skin was cut with ophthalmic scissors to expose the femoral artery, which was then clamped with an arterial clip. Successful femoral artery occlusion was confirmed when the skin of both lower limbs became pale and the skin temperature decreased by > 3 °C [19]. The arterial clips were loosened after 10 min. One cycle consisted of 10 min of occlusion followed by 10 min of reperfusion, with 3 cycles per day for 7 consecutive days. Body weight, diet, and activity level were monitored daily during the treatment period, and the skin was closed with 5–0 nylon sutures at the end of the treatment period. In the sham-treated group, the procedure was identical to that in the RIPostC group except that the femoral artery was not occluded. The entire procedure was strictly aseptic to prevent infection.

Radiation and bone barrow transplantation

To generate WT/ST2 KO bone marrow chimeric mice, 6-week-old C57BL/6 wild-type donor mice and C57-Il1rl1 knockout recipient mice were irradiated with 950 rads of γ-rays (⁶⁰Co source, dose rate 100 rads/min). Two hours after whole-body irradiation, 5 × 10⁶ bone marrow cells (derived from 6-week-old C57BL/6 J or ST2 knockout donors) were injected into recipient mice via the tail vein. Six weeks after transplantation (to ensure stable bone marrow reconstitution), chimeric mice were subjected to SAH modeling as described above [20].

BAMs depletion

Clodronate Liposome(DAKEWE, China, F70101C-A-2) were injected into the subarachnoid space. This substance induces apoptosis upon cellular uptake and was used to deplete BAMs two days prior to subarachnoid hemorrhage. Liposomes without clodronate acid served as vehicle controls. For lateral ventricle injections, animals were anesthetized using the aforementioned method, secured in a stereotaxic apparatus, and after disinfection and skin preparation, the cranial sutures along with the anterior and posterior fontanelles were exposed using a scalpel and forceps. Following bilateral stereotaxic coordinates, the skull was marked and drilled at the designated points. Subsequently, using aglass microelectrode injection pump (RWD, China, R-480), inject 4 μl of liposomes into each lateral ventricle at a rate of 0.2 μl/min. After injection, allow the solution to remain for 10 minutes before slowly withdrawing the glass microelectrode. Suture the scalp, place the mouse on a warming pad, and transfer it to a housing cage upon regaining consciousness.

Immunofluorescence staining

After 7 days of RIPostC treatment, the mice were anesthetized via the same method, and the brain tissues were fixed with precooled PBS (Servicebio, China) and 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China). The head was cut off and skinned, and the skull was immersed in precooled 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24–48 h. The brain tissues were removed and dehydrated in 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions; after dehydration, they were placed in molds, frozen with Tissue-Tek® OCT Compound (Sakura Finetek, USA), and sectioned (16 μm thickness) with a Leica CM1950 cryosectioning machine. The meningeal tissue at the top of the skull was gently removed with forceps and stored in PBS. After blocking with QuickBlock™ Immunostaining Blocking Solution (Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China, Cat. No. P0260) for 15 min, the following primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C: anti-Lyve1 antibody (5 μg/ml, mouse, Abcam, ab33682); recombinant anti-mannose receptor antibody [EPR25215–277] (1:50, rabbit, Abcam, ab300621); MBP antibody (F-6) (1:50, mouse, SANTA CRUZ Biotechnology, sc-271524); NF-H recombinant Superclonal™ antibody (2 μg/ml, rabbit, Thermo Fisher, 711,025); PDGFRα antibody (5 μg/ml, goat, RD systems, AF1062); and lectin (594, vector labs, DL-1207–5). The secondary antibodies were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h and included the following: Donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 555 (4 μg/ml, Thermo Fisher, A-31572); Donkey anti-Goat IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™647 (1–10 μg/ml, Thermo Fisher, A-21447); Donkey anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488 (0.2 μg/ml, Thermo Fisher, A-21202); and FITC-labeled ghost pen cyclic peptide (Solarbio, CA1620). Finally, the slides were mounted with anti-quenching mounting medium containing DAPI (Southern Biotech, 0100–20). Images were acquired using an ultra-high-resolution laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM880, Zeiss). Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined by referencing mouse anatomical Bregma coordinates, with rectangular ROIs uniformly placed in the cortex/hippocampus/corpus callosum regions, avoiding areas of strong vascular autofluorescence. Thresholding and quantification were uniformly performed via batch processing using Fiji (ImageJ) software.

Transfection with shRNAs

First, perform Il1rl1 gene knockdown in cells using Il1rl1(ST2) shRNAs (Jikai Company, China). Seed cells into a 96-well plate. After cells adhered, add transfection medium (containing HiTransG and shRNAs) and incubate at 37℃ for 16 h. Replace with complete medium and continue culture. Once lentiviral expression stabilized, perform puromycin selection and continue culturing to establish specific monoclonal cell lines. Non-silencing (NS) siRNA was used as a control.

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus-maze test was performed 1 day after RIPostC treatment. The elevated plus-maze test consisted of 2 open arms (30 cm × 5 cm) and 2 closed arms (30 cm × 5 cm × 20 cm) arranged vertically, with the base 50 cm above the ground. One hour before the experiment, the mice were transferred to the test room to adapt to the environment and avoid noise interference. The mice were placed in the center of the maze with their heads facing the open arms and allowed to move freely for 5 min. The percentage of open-arm entries was calculated as (open-arm entries/total arm entries) × 100% and recorded via the Any-maze system. The maze was cleaned with 75% alcohol after each round to eliminate odor.

Y maze test

The Y maze test was performed 14 days after SAH and after completion of the EPM test. The device consisted of 3 equal-length arms (40 cm × 10 cm × 15 cm) with a signal light at the end of each arm. One unexplored arm was randomly designated the "new arm", and the mice were placed in the center of the maze and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The Any-maze system recorded the residence time and the number of alternations in the new arm. The spontaneous alternation rate was calculated as (actual number of alternations/theoretical maximum number of alternations) × 100%. The equipment was cleaned with 75% alcohol after each round.

Morris water maze test

The Morris water maze test (to assess learning and memory) was conducted 14 days after SAH, after which the EPM and Y maze experiments were completed. The circular pool was 120 cm in diameter and 45 cm in height, with a water temperature of 22 ± 2 °C. The platform was divided into four quadrants, with a white platform (9 cm diameter) placed in the third quadrant. The platform was submerged 1.5 cm below the water surface in the orientation navigation experiment and was removed in the spatial exploration experiment; the curtain around the pool had four unique patterns as spatial references. In the water maze experiment, mice with abnormal swimming ability were excluded.

The first part of the experiment was conducted for 5 consecutive days. Each mouse was placed in the water with its back to the water maze from a different quadrant 4 times per day and then allowed to swim freely in the water maze for 1 min to search for the platform hidden below the water surface. If the mice found the platform within 1 min, they were allowed to remain on it for 10 s to learn. If the mice did not find the platform within 1 min, they were guided onto it and allowed to remain for 15 s. ANY-maze analysis software automatically recorded experimental data such as avoidance latency (in seconds) from day 1 to day 5, which represented the learning ability of the mice.

Luminex liqid suspension microarrays

On day 14 after modeling and intervention, 1 ml of blood was collected from the orbital sinus into an anticoagulant tube and centrifuged (3000 × g for 10 min) to obtain the serum. Serum was tested via the use of a Bio-Plex Pro Mouse Cytokine Grp (#M60009RDPD) on a Luminex 200 system according to the manufacturer's protocols at Wayne Biotech in Shanghai, China, and the experiment was repeated three times. This experiment included 24 cytokines: G-CSF, GM-CSF, GRO/KC, IFN-γ, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-10, IL-12 (p70), IL-13, IL-17A, IL-18, M-CSF, MCP-1, MIP-1α, MIP-3α, RANTES, TNF-α, VEGF, and IL-33.

Isolation of BAM cells

Fresh brain tissue was obtained from WT/WT chimeric mice and WT/ST2 KO chimeric mice. Mice were labeled with CD45-EV450 (euthanized 3 min after intravenous injection of CD45-EV450, Elabscience, China, E-AB-F1136Q) to exclude residual intravascular cells. High-quality brain tissue single-cell suspensions were prepared using a single-cell suspension preparation instrument (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., China) and the Adult Mouse Brain Tissue Gentle Dissociation Kit (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., China,, DHABE-5003). Subsequently, total CD11b+ macrophages were positively sorted using CD11b + magnetic beads (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., China, K1306-10). Collected total cells were stained with CD3-FITC (Elabscience, China, E-AB-F1013C), Ly6G-PE (Elabscience, China, E-AB-F1108D), and CD206-APC (Elabscience, China, E-AB-F1135E). The Ly6G−/CD3−/CD206+ cell population was then isolated via flow cytometry. Bone marrow-associated macrophages (BAMs) were isolated from bone marrow chimeric mice via the above procedure. ST2 expression in the chimeras was validated using Alexa Fluor® 488-labeled mouse ST2/IL-33R antibody (R&D Systems, FAB10041G).

Flow cytometry for cell cycle assays

Cell cycle analysis was performed using the Cell Cycle Assay Kit (Red Fluorescence) (Elabscience®, China, E-CK-A351) via flow cytometry. RNase A reagent was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol chilled overnight at −20℃, mixed thoroughly, and stored on ice. After treating cells according to the experimental protocol, collect approximately 5 × 105 cells. Centrifuge at 300 × g for 5 min, discard the supernatant. Wash with 1 mL PBS (Servicebio, China), centrifuge at the same speed, discard the supernatant, and resuspend cells in 0.3 ml PBS. Add 1.2 ml of −20℃ anhydrous ethanol, mix thoroughly, and store at −20℃ for 1 h or overnight. Centrifuge at 300 × g for 5 min, discard supernatant. Resuspend cells in 1 ml PBS (Servicebio, China) and incubate at room temperature for 15 min. Centrifuge at the same speed for 5 min, discard the supernatant. Resuspend cells in 100 μl RNase A reagent and incubate at 37℃ for 30 min. Add 400 μl PI reagent (50 μg/ml), mix thoroughly, and incubate at 2–8℃ in the dark for 30 min. Proceed immediately to instrument analysis, recording red fluorescence at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm.

Oxygen consumption (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurements with Seahorse

OCR and ECAR of BAMs obtained by flow cytometric sorting were measured using Seahorse XF Analyzers (Agilent Technologies). For the OCR assay, after washing, cells were placed in XF DMEM base medium (Agilent Technologies, 103,575–100) supplemented with 25 mM D-(+)-glucose and 4 mM L-glutamine, incubated at 37 °C, and assessed with the XFp Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent, 103,010–100) containing 1 μM oligomycin, 1 μM carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and 0.5 μM rotenone/antimycin A. For the ECAR assay, after washing, cells were placed in XF DMEM base medium (Agilent, 103,575–100) containing 2 mM L-glutamine but no D-(+)-glucose, incubated at 37 °C, and analyzed using the XFp Glycolysis Stress Test Kit (Agilent, 103,020–100) containing 10 mM glucose, 1 μM oligomycin, and 50 mM 2-deoxyglucose. OCR and ECAR values were normalized to protein concentration, and data were analyzed with Agilent Wave software (version 2.6.0.31).

Isolation, culture and differentiation of primary OPCs

PDGFRα+ OPCs were isolated from the mouse cortex at P2‒P4, trypsin-digested, filtered and sorted with PDGFRα+ magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany, 130‒101‒502), inoculated into different vessels and cultured in medium containing specific growth factors. The medium was changed at different time points to achieve different experimental purposes, such as OPC proliferation and differentiation [21].

The obtained cells were used for EdU labeling combined with flow cytometry, Transwell invasion assays, cytoskeletal staining, Sholl analysis, immunofluorescence staining, MBP +/Olig2 + cell ratio counting, Astral DIA proteomics analysis, and nontargeted metabolomics analysis.

Reference-based transcriptome analysis

The number of cells required for RNA extraction was 3 × 10⁶–1 × 10⁷ cells per reaction. The specific operation was as follows: the adherent cells were removed from the incubator and the medium was discarded after microscopic examination to confirm good conditions; an equal volume of room temperature 1 × PBS (prepared in enzyme-free water) was added, the mixture was washed for 1 min in a flat area, the PBS was discarded, and the procedure was repeated once; Invitrogen TRIzol (Invitrogen, U.S.A., 15596026) was used to extract RNA, 1 ml of lysate per approximately 1 × 10⁷ cells was added, and the lysate was repeatedly aspirated until the mixture was clear, free of cell clumps and had good fluidity (if the mixture was sticky, approximately 100 μl of lysate was added at a time until the mixture met the standard). Finally, the lysate was transferred to a –192 °C threaded port freezing tube and stored at −80 °C for long-term storage. The assay was paired-end sequenced according to the manufacturer's protocol via an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 (LC Bio Technology Co., Ltd. Hangzhou, China) following standard operation with the PE150 sequencing mode. The downstream raw data in fastq format were quality controlled via fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) software, including the removal of junctions, duplicates and low-quality sequences, with default parameters. The sequencing data were aligned to the Mus musculus genome (GRCm39) via HISAT2 (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2) to generate files in bam format. Genes or transcripts were assembled via StringTie software (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/) and quantified with FPKM (FPKM = total_exon_fragments/mapped_reads(millions) × exon_length(ΚΒ)). The R package edgeR (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html) was used to analyze DEGs between samples, with DEGs defined as those with a fold change > 2 or < 0.5 and a p value < 0.05. Finally, the genes were analyzed for GO and KEGG enrichment via DAVID software (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/).

Astral DIA-based proteomic analysis

The total protein amount of the samples needed for this experiment should be more than 20 µg. The procedure was as follows: when the cells were cultured to 70–90% coverage, the medium was discarded, the dishes were washed three times with 10 ml of PBS, and the dishes were placed on ice after being inverted and drained of liquid. The cells were subsequently digested via trypsin (T1300; Solarbio, China), sterile PBS solution was added to suspend the cells, and the cell suspension was transferred to a 15 ml centrifuge tube. The cells were centrifuged at 400–1000 × g for 5–10 min at 4 °C, the PBS was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in 1 ml of PBS and transferred to a new 1.5 ml centrifuge tube. Finally, the samples were centrifuged again under the same conditions, and as much of the supernatant as possible was removed. The collected samples were placed in a foam box with dry ice and transported to Hangzhou Lianchuan Biotechnology Co. The assay was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol. Peptides were separated on a Thermo Scientific Vanquish Neo UHPLC liquid phase system, and the separated peptides were transferred to a Thermo Astral mass spectrometer for acquisition in DIA (Data Independent Acquisition) mode. DIA-NN software (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-019-0638-x) (version 1.9) was used to identify and quantify proteins in the DIA mass spectrometry data.

Nontargeted metabolomic analysis

The number of cells used in this experiment was 5 × 10⁶–1 × 10⁷. For sample preparation, the cells were removed from the incubator and observed microscopically to confirm their robust growth status, and the culture medium was then discarded. Precooled 1 × PBS (prepared with enzyme-free water) was added, and the cells were washed by gently shaking the Petri dish for 1 min. After discarding the PBS, this washing procedure was repeated twice. The dish was placed on ice, and additional precooled 4 °C PBS was added. The cells were scraped to one side via a cell scraper, the dish was tilted to allow the buffer to converge, and the cell suspension was pipetted into a precooled centrifuge tube. The cells were subsequently centrifuged at 200–1000 × g for 5–10 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was discarded. Finally, the cells were transferred to −192 °C threaded cryovials, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 1 h, and stored in a −80 °C refrigerator or transported on dry ice. The following steps were avoided during the experiment: vortex shaking, high-speed centrifugation, ultrasonication, and repeated freeze‒thaw cycles. Cell counting must be performed prior to sample collection to ensure consistent starting cell numbers; if counting is not feasible, samples of equal weight or volume should be used. For targeted metabolic analyses, cell counting is recommended, as it is necessary to normalize data to sample volume.

Positive and negative ions were collected from the metabolites via electrospray ionization (ESI) via a high-resolution Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) operated in both positive and negative ionization modes. The raw mass spectrometry data were converted to mzXML format via ProteoWizard’s MSConvert software. Peak extraction and retention time alignment were performed via XCMS software (version X.X.X), with quality control metrics applied to ensure reproducibility (e.g., coefficient of variation < 20% for quality control samples). Feature annotation was conducted via CAMERA, which groups ions into putative compounds on the basis of their isotopic patterns and adduct formation. Metabolite identification was achieved by integrating both MS1 (accurate mass) and MS2 (fragmentation spectra) information. MS1 data were matched against an in-house standard library (mass accuracy < 5 ppm) and public databases (HMDB, KEGG), while MS2 spectra were compared to reference spectra via metaX software (version X.X.X.). Candidate metabolites were annotated with confidence levels following the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines (level 1: authenticated by reference standard; level 2: putatively annotated; level 3: putatively characterized compound class; level 4: unknown). Differentially abundant metabolite analysis, including fold change calculation (≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5) and statistical testing (Student’s t test, p < 0.05, FDR-corrected), was performed via metaX. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted via the KEGG and Reactome databases to interpret the biological significance of the identified metabolites.

Flow cytometry detection of EdU-incubated OPCs cocultured with BAMs

Oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) were cocultured with brain-associated macrophages (BAMs) for 24 h. EdU (Elabscience, cat. no. E-CK-A377) was then added at a final concentration of 10 μM, followed by an additional 2-h coincubation. A negative control group (without EdU treatment) was included to verify staining specificity. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature and then washed three times with 3% BSA-PBS (5 min per wash). Permeabilization was performed with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min, after which the click reaction mixture was added. The cells were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Following three additional washes with 3% BSA-PBS, DAPI staining was performed for 10 min to visualize the nuclei. EdU signals were detected via fluorescence microscopy at excitation/emission wavelengths of 590/617 nm. The proportion of EdU⁺ cells (EdU⁺ cells/total DAPI⁺ cells × 100%) was quantified by counting at least 5 random fields of view per sample, with a minimum of 3 biological replicates per group.

Transwell invasion assay

A 6.5 mm Transwell chamber (Corning, NY, USA) with an 8-µm pore size was used to assess the cell migration capacity. Chambers were precoated with Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA; Cat. No. 354234) at a concentration of 50 μg/cm2 and allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were seeded into the upper chamber at a density of 3 × 104 cells/well in serum-free medium, while the lower chamber was filled with complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) as a chemoattractant. Following 6 h of incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator, nonmigrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed via a cotton swab, and the membrane was washed twice with ice-cold PBS. The membranes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and stained with hematoxylin solution (Jiangsu Biyuntian, China; Cat. No. C0105S) for 10 min. Excess stain was rinsed off with distilled water, and the membranes were air-dried. Migrated cells were counted in 5 randomly selected fields of view per membrane via a light microscope (200 × magnification). The migration index was calculated as the average number of migrated cells per field, with 3 biological replicates and 2 technical replicates per group [22].

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, Inc., Boston, MA, USA) and SPSS 26.0 (IBM®, New York, USA). The results are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. Data normality was assessed via the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Statistical significance between groups was analyzed via Student's t test; for multiple comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way analysis of variance was performed, followed by Tukey's post hoc test (when the data were normally distributed) or Dunn's post hoc test (when the data were not normally distributed).

The abundance of BAMs progressively decreases during the subacute phase of SAH and is associated with the severity of cognitive dysfunction

We first used CD206 immunolabeling to observe the spatiotemporal distribution characteristics of BAMs in the meninges and perivascular spaces after SAH (Fig. 1A-C). Statistical analysis revealed that the abundance of BAMs increased significantly during the acute phase (1 day) after SAH and progressively decreased during the subacute phase (7–14 days) (Fig. 1D). To further investigate the relationship between BAMs and cognitive dysfunction, we used intracerebroventricular injection of clodronate liposomes to deplete BAMs in mice (SAH-CLO-Lip group) and then established an SAH model. Myelin damage in white matter regions after SAH was evaluated by immunofluorescence staining for platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRα), neurofilament 200 (NF200), and myelin basic protein (MBP). Compared with the SAH-Vehicle group, the SAH-CLO-Lip group presented a significant reduction in myelin density (MBP+ area) and neurofilament density (NF200+ area), along with a marked increase in denuded axons (MBP-/NF200+), indicating more severe myelin structural damage after BAM depletion (Fig. 1E-G). Furthermore, we assessed the cognitive function of the mice via the Morris water maze, Y maze, and elevated plus maze. Compared with those in the SAH-Vehicle group, the latency of the mice in the SAH-CLO-Lip group in the water maze was lower, and both the time spent in the target quadrant and the number of platform crossings were significantly lower (Fig. 1H). In the elevated plus maze test, the proportion of entries into the open arms was significantly lower (Fig. 1I). In the Y maze, both the time spent in the novel arm and the alternation rate significantly decreased (Fig. 1J). These findings indicate that depletion of BAMs leads to significant impairments in both long-term and short-term spatial learning and memory abilities in mice. In summary, these results suggest that the depletion of BAMs after SAH is associated with cognitive dysfunction.

RIPostC treatment significantly increased the abundance of BAMs in brain border tissues after SAH and improved cognitive dysfunction

First, it is necessary to evaluate the safety of the RIPostC treatment method. We used the PSI to assess lower limb blood perfusion. Statistical analysis revealed that our RIPostC treatment method did not reduce lower limb blood perfusion levels after treatment (Fig. 2B and C, Figure S2). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining confirmed that RIPostC treatment significantly increased the abundance of BAMs in the meninges and perivascular spaces during the subacute phase of SAH (Fig. 2D-F). In terms of myelin structure, RIPostC treatment increased neurofilament density (NF200+ area) and myelin density (MBP+ area) in the white matter region, with an increase in myelinated nerve fibers (MBP+/NF200+) and a significant reduction in denuded axons (MBP−/NF200+) (Fig. 2G and H). Compared with SAH group mice, SAH-RIPostC group mice performed better in tests of long-term and short-term spatial learning and memory abilities in the water maze, Y maze, and elevated plus maze (Fig. 2I-K). These results indicate that RIPostC treatment can significantly increase BAM levels after SAH and improve cognitive dysfunction in mice.

RIPostC treatment increases the abundance of BAMs by increasing IL-33 levels

To investigate the molecular pathways by which RIPostC treatment increases the abundance of BAMs, we used Luminex liquid suspension chip technology to measure the levels of inflammatory cytokines in mouse cerebrospinal fluid (Fig. 3A). Compared with SAH treatment, RIPostC treatment reduced the levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1a, IL-1b, IL-6, IL-12, and IL-18 in the CSF but increased the levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4, IL-10, and IL-33 (Fig. 3B). Given that the increase in IL-33 was the most significant, we measured peripheral blood IL-33 levels via ELISA, and the results revealed that RIPostC treatment stabilized IL-33 levels during the subacute phase (Fig. 3C). To further verify whether IL-33 could increase the abundance of BAMs, we first depleted BAMs in mice via intracerebroventricular injection of CLO-Lips and then intranasally administered recombinant IL-33 (2 μg/mouse) every day after SAH (Fig. 3A). Compared with those in the SAH-Vehicle group, the number of BAMs in the CLO-Lip-rIL-33 group was significantly greater (Fig. 3D-F). Behavioral results demonstrated that mice in the CLO-Lip-rIL-33 group exhibited improved short-term and long-term spatial memory and learning abilities, as assessed by the water maze, Y-maze, and elevated plus maze, compared to the SAH-Vehicle group (Fig. 3G-I). In summary, these findings suggest that RIPostC treatment may promote an increase in BAMs by increasing IL-33 levels in peripheral blood and cerebrospinal fluid.

RIPostC treatment does not increase the abundance of BAMs after SAH in WT/ST2 KO mice

To further verify the effect of IL-33 on BAMs, we first generated ST2 (IL-33 receptor) knockout (KO) mice (Fig. 4A). Both WT mice and ST2 KO mice were irradiated to deplete hematopoietic stem cells, after which bone marrow reconstitution was performed using hematopoietic stem cells from either WT or ST2 KO donors. The recipient mice were allowed to recover for six weeks. According to previous studies, the donor bone marrow replaces the recipient mouse’s peripheral immune cells according to the donor genotype, whereas the recipient’s microglia and oligodendrocyte precursor cells retain the recipient genotype [20]. For the experimental groups, bone marrow from WT mice was used to reconstitute the bone marrow of recipient mice to control for the off-target effects of irradiation (Figure S3).

Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that, compared with WT/WT treatment, RIPostC treatment did not increase the number of BAMs in the meninges or perivascular spaces of WT/ST2 KO mice (Fig. 4B-D). Moreover, in the cognitive behavioral tests, WT/ST2 KO mice exhibited more severe impairments in both long-term and short-term spatial learning and memory abilities after SAH modeling than WT/WT SAH mice did (Fig. 4E-G). Furthermore, we collected cortical brain tissue from WT/WT RIPostC and WT/ST2 KO RIPostC mice for transcriptome sequencing. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed numerous pathways associated with MHCII immune cell functions (Fig. 4H). Studies utilizing transgenic mouse models and high-throughput sequencing technology have demonstrated that BAMs distributed in the perivascular and perimembranous spaces exhibit key defining markers characterized by MHCIIhigh and CD206high states, and are recruited from the bone marrow [10, 11, 23]. This indirectly confirms that the transcriptomic characteristics of BAMs in the WT/ST2 KO chimeric mice constructed in this study align with the trends observed in previous high-throughput sequencing studies. On the other hand, GESA enrichment analysis also indicated that genes in pathways related to memory, learning, activation of innate immune response, and monocyte differentiation were significantly downregulated in WT/ST2 KO RIPostC mice (Fig. 4I). This suggests that the WT/ST2 KO RIPostC transcriptome profile aligns with our cognitive neurofunctional test results.

In summary, after SAH, the abundance of BAMs in WT/ST2 KO mice was not increased by RIPostC treatment. These findings suggest that RIPostC treatment may enhance BAM coverage and improve cognitive dysfunction after SAH through the IL-33/ST2 axis.

The IL-33/ST2 axis promotes the proliferation of BAMs by driving their metabolic reprogramming

To further clarify the specific mechanism by which the IL-33/ST2 axis affects BAMs, we first induced SAH in WT/WT and WT/ST2 KO mice and administered RIPostC treatment. On day 14 we intravenously injected CD45-EV450 (euthanized 3 min after intravenous injection of CD45-EV450) to subsequently exclude residual intravascular cells by flow cytometry. Brain tissue was removed to obtain a high-quality single-cell suspension. CD11b+ magnetic beads were then used for positive selection of total CD11b+ macrophages. Ly6G−CD3−CD206+ BAMs were then isolated by flow cytometric sorting. The obtained BAMs were used for proteomic and metabolomic analyses or for co-culture experiments with OPCs (Fig. 5A and Figure S4).Cluster analysis of the differentially expressed proteins revealed that the significantly downregulated proteins were involved mainly in cell division and mitosis regulation (such as TPX2, NUSAP1, KIF11, and TOP2A), fatty acid metabolism and energy metabolism (ACSF2 and ACADM), and immune regulation and signal transduction (CSF1R and COLEC12) (Fig. 5C). The significantly upregulated proteins were related primarily to glycolysis regulation (HK1), transcriptional regulation and stress response (JUND, FOSL2), and cytoskeletal remodeling and cell motility (RHOC, TUBB2A, EHD1) (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that the cellular metabolic patterns in the WT/ST2 KO group shifted, with a reduced proliferative capacity and altered immune and stress responses. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that terms related to cell cycle and metabolism pathways—such as Oxidative phosphorylation, Metabolic pathways, and Citrate cycle (TCA cycle)—were significantly enriched (Fig. 5C). GSEA revealed significant differences in pathways such as positive regulation of the mitotic cell cycle, negative regulation of cytokine production involved in the inflammatory response, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and glycerolipid metabolism (Fig. 5D).

Furthermore, we validated the proteomics findings through cell experiments. First, we used Seahorse analysis to assess the metabolic levels of BAMs (Fig. 5E). The results showed that BAMs from WT/ST2 KO chimeric mice exhibited simultaneous decreases in oxidative phosphorylation (OCR) and glycolysis (ECAR), indicating that the overall energy metabolic capacity of WT/ST2 KO BAMs (including mitochondrial oxidation and glycolysis) is in a low-flux state, suggesting that the IL-33/ST2 axis may be involved in regulating the mobilization of BAMs energy metabolism. Furthermore, by constructing an ST2 shRNA monoclonal RAW264.7 cell line, we evaluated the effect of the IL-33/ST2 axis on cell proliferative capacity (Fig. 5F). Using flow cytometry to analyze cell cycle phase distribution and proliferative activity, the analysis indicated that after rIL-33 intervention, the NS_rIL-33 group showed a shortened G0/G1 phase and significantly increased proliferative activity (Fig. 5G and H). The ST2 shRNA_Vehicle group showed G0/G1 accumulation, reduced G2/M, and significantly suppressed proliferative activity, and the ST2 shRNA_rIL-33 group could not rescue the pro-proliferative effect induced by rIL-33 (Fig. 5G and H). These results support the proteomics finding that the IL-33/ST2 axis promotes macrophage proliferation.In summary, proteomic analysis revealed that after SAH, the IL-33/ST2 axis may drive metabolic reprogramming of BAMs to exert regulatory effects on these cells.

To further analyze the metabolically reprogrammed state initially characterized by proteomics, we conducted non-targeted metabolomics analysis on BAMs. First, KEGG annotation was performed on significantly differentially expressed secondary metabolites, revealing the most prominent clustering of metabolites in the amino acid metabolism pathway (Fig. 6A). The heatmap of differential metabolites displayed the top 30 altered metabolites, including amino acids and their derivatives (Pyroglutamic acid, Tryptophanamide, D-Citrulline, Cadaverine, etc.), organic acids and their derivatives (Pyruvic acid, Monoethyl carbonate, 12S-HHT, 11,12-Epoxyeicosatrienoic acid, 20-Hydroxy-6Z,15Z-eicosadienoic acid, etc.), lipids and their derivatives (LysoPS(18:0/0:0), LysoPC(18:0/0:0), Tiglylcarnitine, Propenoylcarnitine, etc.) (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, functional analysis was performed on all differentially expressed metabolites. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated significant differences in fundamental metabolic pathways including amino acid metabolism (Biosynthesis of amino acids, Arginine and proline metabolism, Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, Valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, etc.), energy metabolism (Citrate cycle, etc.), and Nucleotide metabolism, with amino acid metabolism showing the highest enrichment (Fig. 6C). Nutritional BAMs driven by the IL-33/ST2 axis can promote the proliferation, migration, and myelination of OPCs

On the basis of previous findings, further clarification of how the BAMs microenvironment improves cognitive dysfunction after SAH is necessary. To this end, we used PDGFRα magnetic beads to positively select PDGFRα+ OPCs from the brain tissue of 7-day-old WT neonatal mice, cocultured them with previously obtained BAMs, and evaluated the effects of BAMs on OPC proliferation, migration, and myelination (Fig. 7A). EdU flow cytometry analysis and Transwell migration assays revealed that BAMs from the WT/ST2 KO group inhibited the proliferation and migration abilities of OPCs (Fig. 7B and C). Furthermore, by continuously culturing OPCs in a PDGF-AA and bFGF microenvironment for 1, 3, and 5 days and labeling the cytoskeleton with phalloidin-FITC, Sholl analysis revealed that, compared with those in the vehicle group without BAMs, BAMs from the WT/WT group presented increased complexity of the OPC cytoskeleton, whereas BAMs from the WT/ST2 KO group presented inhibited cytoskeletal formation (Fig. 7D-I). Additionally, we first induced OPCs with T3 for 2 days before they were cocultured with BAMs (Fig. 7J). Statistical analysis of immunofluorescence staining revealed that coculture with BAMs from the WT/WT group significantly increased the proportion of OLs (MBP+/Olig2+), whereas coculture with BAMs from the WT/ST2 KO group impaired OLs formation (Fig. 7K). In summary, these experiments suggest that WT/WT BAMs promote the proliferation, migration, and myelination of OPCs, whereas WT/ST2 KO BAMs inhibit these effects.

Furthermore, the level of myelination was evaluated via immunofluorescence staining of frozen brain sections from mice after SAH (Fig. 8A). Discussion and conclusions

This study yielded several important new findings. Using a mouse model system, we revealed the spatiotemporal dynamics of BAMs after SAH and their relationship with cognitive dysfunction. We found that the abundance of BAMs progressively decreases during the subacute phase (7–14 days) after SAH and is closely associated with myelin damage and cognitive impairment. More importantly, we demonstrated for the first time that RIPostC can significantly increase the abundance of BAMs through the IL-33/ST2 axis, drive amino acid metabolic reprogramming, and promote the proliferation, migration, and myelination of OPCs, thereby improving cognitive dysfunction in mice after SAH. These findings provide new insights into the pathological mechanisms of cognitive dysfunction following SAH and lay the foundation for potential therapeutic strategies.

The characteristics of the tissue microenvironment have a profound impact on the phenotype and features of BAMs. Previous studies using high-resolution techniques such as mass cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing, and lineage tracing have gradually clarified the marker characteristics of BAMs, although some controversies remain [9, 11, 24]. At the protein level, BAMs can be distinguished from microglia by increased expression of markers such as CD206 and F4/80 [10, 11]. Both MHC IIhigh BAMs and MHC IIlow BAMs in the meningeal and perivascular spaces exhibit high levels of CD206 expression [25, 26]. Therefore, on the basis of previous research, this study used CD206 to label and sort BAMs from the meningeal and perivascular spaces via flow cytometry.

We found that during the acute phase of SAH, BAMs are significantly recruited to the meninges and perivascular spaces, which is similar to the findings of R Loch Macdonald and Xiaoyu Wang [27, 28]. In the subacute phase, we observed a progressive decrease in BAM abundance, which was associated with the degree of cognitive dysfunction. R Loch Macdonald reported that depletion of BAMs during the acute phase can alleviate inflammation around major arteries and reduce microthrombus formation after SAH but significantly impairs the functions of erythrophagocytosis and clearance [27]. These findings suggest that BAMs play a complex immunoregulatory role during the acute phase after SAH, participating in the acute immune storm while also performing clearance functions. Therefore, on the basis of the immunoregulatory characteristics of RIPostC, we improved the timing of RIPostC intervention, initiating treatment from the subacute phase after SAH to avoid the uncertain effects that may arise during the acute phase.

We found that RIPostC treatment increased the abundance of BAMs after SAH through the IL-33/ST2 axis. Furthermore, combined multiomics analysis of BAMs isolated from brain tissue revealed that this regulation is achieved through amino acid metabolic reprogramming. Integrated omics analysis revealed that, in BAMs from WT/ST2 KO chimeric mice, arginine/proline metabolism, glutamate/pyruvate metabolism, branched-chain amino acid metabolism, and aromatic amino acid metabolic pathways were significantly suppressed, whereas the lysine degradation pathway was markedly increased. Previous studies have suggested that arginine/proline metabolism drives the regulation of posttranslational protein modifications (such as methylation) in macrophages, thereby affecting the functional execution of immune cells [29, 30]. Methylation of arginine residues regulates cell survival and transcription during early differentiation stages, which is crucial for myelination [31–34]. In addition, arginine participates in polyamine metabolism, and its synthesis ensures a sufficient nutritional supply for multiple metabolic pathways, maintaining adequate levels of various metabolites and building blocks to provide energy for biosynthesis in a coordinated manner, thus supporting the proliferation process [35]. Our experimental findings also confirmed that the abundance of BAMs in WT/ST2 KO chimeric mice was significantly lower than that in WT/WT mice. Glutamate/pyruvate metabolism, branched-chain amino acid metabolism, and lysine metabolism provide intermediates for the tricarboxylic acid cycle, driving macrophage energy remodeling and regulating macrophage polarization by modulating glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation [35–39]. This finding closely aligns with our nontargeted metabolomics KEGG enrichment analysis, which revealed significant changes in the TCA cycle pathway. Aromatic amino acid metabolism in macrophages can regulate the local immune microenvironment, promote oligodendrocyte maturation, and enhance remyelination of demyelinated lesions [40, 41]. Jeffrey K. Huang reported that myeloid cell-associated aromatic amino acid metabolism promotes myelin regeneration in mouse models of multiple sclerosis. These findings are highly consistent with our results. Through co-culture of BAMs and OPCs, we found that BAMs from the WT/ST2-KO group suppressed OPC proliferation and migration and inhibited myelin development after T3 intervention. In summary, BAMs play diverse and complex roles in energy remodeling, proliferative activity, and myelin nutrition and regeneration through amino acid metabolic reprogramming.

This study has the following limitations. First, owing to the complexity of the BAMs immune microenvironment, we are unable to fully clarify the specific molecular mechanisms underlying the interactions between BAMs and OPCs. Although this study, through integrated multiomics analysis, revealed the nutritional support role of amino acid metabolic reprogramming in myelin regeneration, the specific key factors of BAMs that directly affect the proliferation, migration, and differentiation of OPCs have not yet been identified by any single molecule, and further exploration is needed in the future. Second, this study observed the spatiotemporal dynamics of BAMs via techniques such as immunofluorescence staining, but detailed evolution and lineage changes require single-cell RNA sequencing and more precise lineage tracing via relevant transgenic mice to achieve continuous and dynamic observations.

Existing clinical studies on cognitive dysfunction after SAH indicate that abnormalities in white matter structure and persistent neuroinflammation are key factors leading to long-term cognitive impairment in patients. In this study, we used RIPostC, a safe, noninvasive, and feasible immunomodulatory approach, and confirmed that it can increase the abundance of BAMs after SAH in a mouse model and improve cognitive dysfunction. Our research team previously reported that RIPostC treatment can improve endothelial cell injury, enhance cerebral blood flow perfusion, and improve cognitive function in humans [42]. The team is currently conducting a clinical trial related to SAH (ChiCTR2100053696), which will further evaluate the effect of RIPostC treatment on cognitive function recovery in patients and explore the optimal therapeutic time window and protocol.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidates the spatiotemporal dynamics of border-associated macrophages following subarachnoid hemorrhage and their relationship with cognitive dysfunction (Fig. 9). Our results demonstrate that the abundance of BAMs significantly decreases during the subacute phase after SAH, and this reduction is directly associated with demyelination and cognitive impairment. Remote ischemic postconditioning markedly increases BAMs numbers via the IL-33/ST2 axis, drives amino acid metabolic reprogramming, and promotes the formation of neuroprotective BAMs. Consequently, this enhances the proliferation, migration, and myelination of oligodendrocyte precursor cells, ultimately improving cognitive deficits in mice after SAH. Overall, this study not only reveals the immunometabolic mechanisms underlying cognitive dysfunction following SAH but also provides a novel theoretical basis and potential translational pathway for targeting BAMs and the IL-33/ST2 axis as therapeutic interventions.
