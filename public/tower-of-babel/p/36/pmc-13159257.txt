Effects of alpha-lipoic acid supplementation on patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials
Authors: Ting Li, Huabao Liu, Jin Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeveral studies have suggested the promising effects of alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) as an antioxidant in patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). However, the results of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have been contradictory. This meta-analysis examined the effect of ALA supplementation on NAFLD patients.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were systematically searched to include all eligible RCTs published up to February 2026. Weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to compare the changes in liver enzymes, obesity measures, lipid profiles, glycemic indices, and adipocytokines between the ALA and placebo groups using a random-effects model.ResultsSeven RCTs comprising 414 participants were analyzed. In the overall analysis, serum levels of liver enzymes, lipid profiles, glycemic indices, adipocytokines, and obesity measures were not significantly different between the intervention and placebo groups. Moreover, subgroup analyses by age of participants, sample size, baseline body mass index (BMI), method of NAFLD diagnosis, dose of ALA, and duration of follow-up consistently revealed no beneficial effect of ALA on the outcomes. Meta-regression and sensitivity analyses supported the findings.ConclusionsThe findings indicated that ALA supplementation did not improve liver enzymes, lipid profiles, glycemic indices, adipocytokines, and obesity measures in patients with NAFLD.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02238-6.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), characterized by excessive hepatic fat accumulation in the absence of significant alcohol intake, is the most prevalent chronic liver disease globally [1]. NAFLD represents a spectrum of liver diseases, ranging from simple hepatocellular steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), which may progress to fibrosis cirrhosis, and ultimately hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. The prevalence of NAFLD has increased rapidly in recent years, affecting around 32% of the general population worldwide [3]. NAFLD typically develops in the context of metabolic syndrome traits such as obesity, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance (IR) [4]. Genetic variants, unhealthy diets, as well as oxidative stress and inflammation also play a role in its pathogenesis [5, 6]. Despite increased understanding of NAFLD pathophysiology, currently, there is no approved drug to treat NAFLD [7]. Lifestyle modifications, such as weight reduction and physical activity, are first-line therapy; however, they often face challenges because of poor motivation and difficulty in long-term adherence [8, 9]. This highlights the urgent necessity to explore novel therapeutic approaches to address patients with NAFLD.

Alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) is a mitochondrial antioxidant that is distinguished for its solubility in both aqueous and lipid environments, which allows it to exert broad antioxidant effects across various cellular compartments [10]. In small amounts, ALA could be endogenously synthesized from octanoic acid in the mitochondria, playing a role as a cofactor for mitochondrial α-ketoacid dehydrogenase complexes, which are involved in the regulation of energy metabolism [11]. Accumulating evidence from recent investigations has shown the promising therapeutic efficacy of ALA in NAFLD [2, 12, 13].​ Several preclinical and clinical studies have revealed that ALA supplementation could improve oxidative stress and inflammation in NAFLD by stimulating hepatic antioxidant activity and upregulating enzymes involved in oxidative removal, such as glutathione (GSH) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) [13, 14]. In addition to its antioxidant properties, ALA may improve metabolic markers by improving insulin sensitivity and lipid profiles, consequently leading to a reduction in hepatic fat accumulation [12, 15]. On the other hand, some studies have reported that ALA may be effective in weight loss [16], supporting its multi-faceted beneficial effects for NAFLD management. However, the results of the available randomized clinical trials (RCTs) are contradictory. In the study by Ebrahimi-Mameghani et al. [17], intervention with 1200 mg ALA for 12 weeks significantly reduced IR and serum levels of insulin, compared to the placebo. In another study, supplementation with 300 mg of ALA for 6 months reduced IR and triglyceride (TG) concentrations [18]. Nevertheless, Tutunchi et al. [19] failed to find a significant effect of ALA on aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), fasting blood sugar (FBS), fasting insulin (FI), IR, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL). Heterogeneity in the results of the previous studies could be due to differences in sample size, population characteristics, as well as dose and duration of treatment. Moreover, most trials are relatively small and short-term, limiting the generalizability of their results. Given the growing prevalence of NAFLD and its substantial metabolic and hepatic complications, clarifying the effectiveness of ALA is of significant clinical relevance. A comprehensive meta-analysis can synthesize the current evidence, account for heterogeneity across studies, and provide more precise estimates for the effect of ALA on metabolic and liver-related outcomes. This approach will help inform clinical practice and guide future research by identifying optimal dosing, treatment duration, and patient populations most likely to benefit from ALA supplementation. Therefore, this meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of ALA supplementation on metabolic outcomes of patients with NAFLD.

Methods

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement guidelines to conduct this study [20].

Search strategy

A systematic search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library to identify all relevant studies published up to February 15, 2026. We used Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and related keywords without any language or publication date restrictions for the search. The complete search strategy is presented in Table S1. Moreover, the reference lists of all eligible studies were manually screened to identify any potentially missed studies. We did not search for grey literature in this meta-analysis. Only peer-reviewed published studies were included to ensure reliability of the synthesized evidence. All identified studies were imported into EndNote (version 7), and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were subsequently evaluated, and the full texts of potentially relevant articles were screened for eligibility based on predefined inclusion criteria.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following PICOS criteria. (1) Population: patients with NAFLD; (2) Intervention: patients received ALA; (3) Comparator: the control group received placebo; (4) Outcomes: trials reported at least one of the following outcomes: liver enzyme [aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and AST/ALT ratio], lipid profile [triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)], glycemic measures [fasting blood glucose (FBS), fasting blood insulin (FBI), Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR)], anthropometric measures [waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), body mass index (BMI), fat mass (FM), visceral fat (VF), and fat-free mass (FFM)], and adipocytokines [leptin, adiponectin, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)]; and (5) Study design: studies were RCTs in design.​ Excluded studies were in vitro and animal studies, reviews, overlapped or republished citations, and studies where their data were not extractable, and studies with irrelevant interventions or outcomes. Study selection was conducted by two independent reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third author. Agreement between the two reviewers during the screening and study selection process was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic to determine inter-rater reliability. The kappa coefficient was 0.83 for title and abstract screening, 0.86 for full-text assessment, and 0.92 for data extraction, indicating a high level of agreement between reviewers.

Data extraction

A predefined data extraction form was applied to extract data. The extracted data from each study included the first author’s name, study design, country, publication year, sample size, diagnosis of NAFLD, dose, duration of treatment, mean age, sex of subjects, and effect sizes for the outcomes. Data were extracted by two authors independently, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion among all investigators.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias (RoB) for the included trials was assessed with the use of the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool 2 (RoB 2) [21]. It evaluates five domains: bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each domain is judged as low risk of bias, some concerns, or high risk of bias, leading to an overall RoB judgment for each outcome. Quality assessment was independently carried out by two authors, and inconsistencies were resolved through a discussion involving all reviewers.

Statistical analysis

Effect sizes were presented as weighted mean differences (WMD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Changes in the mean values with standard deviations (SDs) in the intervention (end – baseline) and placebo (end – baseline) groups were used to compute the pooled estimates. In cases where the changes in mean and SD for the intervention and placebo groups were not reported in the original trial, the change from baseline to the end of the intervention was calculated for each group as the difference between the mean at the end of the intervention and the mean at baseline (Mean change within group = Mean end - Mean baseline). When the SD for change from baseline value was not available, SD for change was calculated using 95% CI of the change as: SD= ((Upper Limit−Lower Limit)/ 3.92)×√n [22]. Effect sizes were pooled with the use of a random-effects model suggested by DerSimonian and Laird method, which accounts for both within- and between-study variability [23]. Heterogeneity across effect sizes was examined using Cochran’s Q and the I² statistic, with I² > 50% indicating a significant heterogeneity [24, 25]. Sensitivity analysis was carried out for outcomes with ≥ 3 effect sizes by sequentially excluding individual studies from the main analysis to identify the effect of the single studies on the pooled estimates. Subgroup analyses were performed according to duration of follow-up, dose of ALA, method of NAFLD diagnosis, age of participants, baseline BMI, and sample size to explore sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated by visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s test. Meta-regression analysis was conducted to assess the effect of sample size, dose of ALA, duration of follow-up, age of subjects, proportion of male participants (%), baseline BMI, and quality score of RCTs on the pooled results. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA (version 16.0), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study characteristics

The literature search through multiple databases yielded a total of 343 publications, of which 73 duplicates were removed. Screening of titles and abstracts excluded 254 irrelevant studies. Then, 16 potentially relevant full-text studies were assessed, and 9 additional studies were excluded because they were reviews or animal studies, had republished or incomplete data, or had irrelevant interventions. The process of study selection is illustrated in Fig. 1. Overall, 7 publications [17–19, 26–29], published between 2013 and 2025, were eligible for inclusion in this meta-analysis. The studies by Ebrahimi-Mameghani et al. [17], Hosseinpour-Arjmand et al. [27], and Rahmanabadi et al. [28] were published from the same RCT and had overlapping data. To handle overlapping data from multiple publications of the same RCT, we adopted a conservative approach to avoid double-counting. Among the three publications from the same trial, we primarily included the publication by Rahmanabadi et al. [28], as it reported the majority of relevant outcomes. For the other two publications [17, 27], we only extracted outcomes that were not reported in Rahmanabadi et al. [28]. This approach ensured that each participant’s data contributed only once to each outcome in the meta-analysis, thus preventing duplication and preserving the integrity of the pooled estimates.

Flow diagram of the study

Of the 7 included publications, 4 studies were conducted in Iran [17, 19, 27, 28], one in the USA [18], one in Mexico [29], and the other one in Italy [26]. All included RCTs recruited both male and female participants, with male frequency ranging from 26% to 52.4%. Participants’ ages ranged from 35 ± 6 to 61 ± 3.8 years. The sample size of the included RCTs was between 45 and 101 subjects, and the total number of participants was 414 subjects (ALA group: 217, placebo group: 197). The mean baseline BMI of patients was between 29 ± 0.57 and 33.8 ± 3.7 kg/m2, and 5 out of 7 included publications examined obese participants (BMI > 30 kg/m2). Intervention duration was between 8 and 48 weeks with a daily dose of 300 to 1200 mg oral ALA. The diagnosis of NAFLD was based on ultrasonography, FibroScan, or liver biopsy. Regarding the outcomes, data were reported for ALT in 5 studies, AST in 4 studies, AST/ALT ratio in 3 studies, ALP in 2 studies, GGT in 2 studies, BMI in 4 studies, WC in 3 studies, WHR in 3 studies, FM, VF, and FFM in 2 studies, TG in 5 studies, TC in 3 studies, LDL-C in 4 studies, HDL-C in 4 studies, FBS in 4 studies, FBI in 4 studies, HOMA-IR in 5 studies, leptin in 2 studies, adiponectin in 2 studies, and TNF-α in 2 studies. The detailed characteristics of the analyzed RCTs are reported in Table 1.

Risk of bias assessment

The results of the RoB evaluation are presented in Fig. S1. All studies were randomized, placebo-controlled trials. The randomization method was reported in all studies except Contreras et al. [29]. Regarding blinding, the study by Basu et al. [18] was an open-label clinical trial with no blinding, while the other RCTs were reported as double-blinded. The appropriateness of blinding of participants and researchers was unclear in two RCTs [26, 29]. Information about withdrawals and dropouts was not mentioned in the study by Basu et al. [18], while all other RCTs reported satisfactory data on withdrawals and dropouts. Of the included studies, three studies had some concerns regarding bias arising from the randomization process [18, 26, 29]. One study had some concerns regarding bias due to deviations from intended interventions [29]. Two studies had some concerns regarding bias due to missing outcome data [28, 29]. Two studies had some concerns regarding bias in the measurement of the outcome [18, 26]. Moreover, two studies had some concerns regarding bias in the selection of the reported results [18, 28]. Overall, three studies had low RoB [17, 19, 27], three had some concerns [26, 28, 29], and one study [18] had high RoB.

Meta-analysis

The forest plots for the overall effect of ALA supplementation on liver enzymes and obesity measures are presented in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. Moreover, the results of the pooled analysis for lipid profile, glycemic indices, and adipocytokines are presented in Fig. 4. When all effect sizes were pooled using a random-effects model, compared with placebo, ALA supplementation revealed no significant effect on ALT (WMD: 1.43, 95% CI: -1.43 to 4.28), AST (WMD: -0.49, 95% CI: -1.61 to 0.64), AST/ALT (WMD: 0.03, 95% CI: -0.10 to 0.17), ALP (WMD: 21.13, 95% CI: -24.53 to 66.78), GGT (WMD: -32.40, 95% CI: -99.07 to 34.27), BMI (WMD: -0.36, 95% CI: -0.99 to 0.27), WC (WMD: -0.11, 95% CI: -2.19 to 1.96), WHR (WMD: 0.00, 95% CI: -0.01 to 0.02), FFM (WMD: 0.76, 95% CI: -0.25 to 1.76), FM (WMD: 0.39, 95% CI: -1.10 to 1.88), VF (WMD: -1.07, 95% CI: -2.53 to 0.39), HDL-C (WMD: 1.25, 95% CI: -0.75 to 3.24), LDL-C (WMD: -0.11, 95% CI: -2.74 to 2.52), TC (WMD: 2.87, 95% CI: -8.46 to 14.20), TG (WMD: -17.27, 95% CI: -46.85 to 12.31), FBS (WMD: 0.07, 95% CI: -3.08 to 3.21), FBI (WMD: -0.29, 95% CI: -2.95 to 2.36), HOMA-IR (WMD: -0.18, 95% CI: -1.24 to 0.88), leptin (WMD: -4.18, 95% CI: -11.69 to 3.33), adiponectin (WMD: 1.84, 95% CI: -0.99 to 4.68), and TNF-α (WMD: -1.17, 95% CI: -3.45 to 1.12). Subgroup analysis by age of participants, sample size, baseline BMI, method of NAFLD diagnosis, dose of ALA, and duration of follow-up consistently did not identify a beneficial effect of ALA on liver enzymes, obesity measures, lipid profile, glycemic indices, and adipocytokines in any of the subgroups (Table 2). There was significant heterogeneity across the studies on AST/ALT, ALP, GGT, VF, TC, TG, FBI, HOMA-IR, and adiponectin (I2 ≥ 50, P < 0.10). For outcomes with low heterogeneity, we also performed analyses using a fixed-effect model. The results remained non-significant and were consistent with the random-effects findings (data not shown).

Meta-regression, sensitivity analysis, and publication bias

In the meta-regression analysis, the effect of ALA on the outcomes was not influenced by sample size, dose of ALA, duration of follow-up, age of subjects, frequency of males (%), baseline BMI, and quality score of RCTs. The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the overall pooled effect estimate remained stable after sequential exclusion of each study. No single study significantly altered the magnitude or direction of the combined effect size, indicating that the results of the meta-analysis are robust. The results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in Supplemental File 1 (Figures S2–S14). Moreover, excluding Basu et al. [18] due to high RoB did not alter the results. Egger’s regression test did not identify evidence of publication bias for all outcomes.

Discussion

Several animal and human studies have suggested that ALA supplementation may have promising therapeutic effects in NAFLD. The present meta-analysis examined the impacts of ALA supplementation on patients with NAFLD by systematically pooling the results of available RCTs. The results showed that supplementation with ALA was not effective in improving liver enzymes, lipid profiles, glycemic indices, adipocytokines, or obesity-associated parameters compared to placebo.

In our subgroup analyses, we evaluated whether participant age, baseline BMI, sample size, NAFLD diagnostic method, ALA dose, and intervention duration influenced the effects of supplementation. Across all subgroups, ALA supplementation did not demonstrate significant improvements in liver enzymes, obesity measures, lipid profiles, glycemic indices, or adipocytokines. However, some outcomes showed notable heterogeneity, including AST/ALT, ALP, GGT, visceral fat, TC, TG, fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and adiponectin. This heterogeneity may reflect differences in study design, participant characteristics, intervention protocols, and diagnostic methods among the included trials. For example, variations in baseline metabolic status, NAFLD severity, or adherence to ALA supplementation could have influenced the response in specific subgroups. These findings suggest that while no subgroup appeared to benefit from ALA supplementation, the observed variability underscores the need for larger, well-designed RCTs to explore potential effect modifiers and better understand individual responses.

Although many in vitro and animal studies have indicated the beneficial impacts of ALA in NAFLD [13, 30–32], there are a few RCTs, with inconsistent results, examining ALA efficacy in humans. Ebrahimi-Mameghani et al. found that 1200 mg/day of ALA for 12 weeks significantly reduced HOMA-IR compared to placebo. No significant within-group or between-group changes were found for TNF-α [17]. In the study by Gianturco et al. [26], monotherapy with 400 mg/day of ALA for 24 weeks did not improve BMI, liver enzymes, glycemic parameters, and lipid profile compared to placebo. The RCT by Hosseinpour-Arjmand et al. showed that ALA supplementation at a dose of 1200 mg for 12 weeks significantly lowered TG compared to placebo. ALA significantly decreased FBS, TC, LDL, ALT, AST, and ALP compared to baseline values; however, differences with the placebo group were not significant [27]. Rahmanabadi et al. revealed that intake of 1200 mg of ALA for 12 weeks significantly reduced serum levels of FBI and leptin and increased adiponectin concentrations compared to placebo. Both ALA and placebo significantly improved obesity-related parameters and liver enzymes compared to baseline values, with no between-group differences. However ALA significantly decreased FBS compared to baseline values, the differences with the placebo group were not significant [28]. Moreover, in the study by Tutunchi et al. Both ALA and placebo significantly improved BMI, WC, and ALT compared to baseline values, with no between-group differences. ALA significantly reduced LDL-C, but there was no significant difference from the placebo. No beneficial effect was also found on WHR, glycemic parameters, TG, HDL-C, TC, and AST/ALT [19]. The inconsistent results could be due to differences in study design, ALA dose, duration of supplementation, small sample sizes, baseline characteristics of patients such as BMI and disease severity, methods used for NAFLD diagnosis, and outcome assessment. In addition, methodological limitations, including lack of blinding and incomplete reporting, might play a role in the heterogeneity of the results. Furthermore, most significant results reported by previous RCTs were within-group changes in the ALA group, while the differences between the ALA and placebo groups were not commonly statistically significant. Our analysis of all available studies did not support the use of ALA in patients with NAFLD.

Experimental studies have proposed antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, lipid-modulating, and insulin-sensitizing properties of ALA [32–35], suggesting that it may be effective in modulating metabolic irregularities in several endocrine disorders such as NAFLD. ALA is known as a potential antioxidant due to its ability to scavenge reactive oxygen species and improve the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as GSH and SOD [36, 37], thus decreasing oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in the liver. Moreover, ALA inhibits the Nucleotide-Binding Domain, Leucine-Rich–Containing Family, Pyrin Domain–Containing-3 (NLRP3) inflammasome pathway, resulting in downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reduced hepatic fat accumulation [12]. ALA also stimulates the Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) pathway and prevents nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling, which leads to a reduction in inflammation and oxidative stress in hepatocytes [38, 39]. IR is a leading contributing factor in the pathophysiology of NAFLD and results in disruption in glucose and lipid metabolism [40]. ALA could increase glucose uptake in both adipocytes [41] and skeletal muscle cells [42] by increasing translocation of the glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) in 3T3-L1 adipocytes and GLUT4 in L6 myotubes (33). ALA is shown to modulate insulin signaling pathways by stimulating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3-kinase), and protein kinase B (Akt) activities in the skeletal muscle [43] and adipose tissue [41]. In addition, experimental studies have indicated that ALA induces peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) transcriptional activity, leading to the attenuation of lipogenesis and cholesterol biosynthesis and improvement in fatty acid β-oxidation [32]. Accordingly, it has been hypothesized that ALA may improve metabolic parameters in endocrine disorders. Nevertheless, in the clinical setting, in line with our findings, a previous meta-analysis revealed that ALA was not superior to placebo to improve metabolic outcomes, including glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), TG, HDL, LDL, and TC in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [44]. A meta-analysis by Luo et al. found that ALA supplementation has no significant effect on HOMA-IR, FBS, and lipid profile in overweight/obese adults [45]. In another meta-analysis on patients with stroke, ALA intake significantly reduced FBS, while no significant effect was found on lipid profile [46]. A meta-analysis also showed that, in patients with polycystic ovary syndrome, ALA supplementation improved FBS and HOMA-IR levels compared to placebo, while no significant effect was observed on BMI, FBI, and sex hormones [47]. These findings imply that although ALA has encouraging mechanistic actions in preclinical studies, its efficacy in improving complex metabolic parameters in human endocrine disorders like NAFLD is limited.

The current meta-analysis has some strengths. To the best of the author’s knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis examining the effect of ALA supplementation on patients with NAFLD. All included studies were RCTs, and only one study had a high RoB, which increases the reliability of the pooled estimates. We comprehensively examined various outcomes, including hepatic, metabolic, and inflammatory biomarkers, to yield a more comprehensive analysis. Furthermore, subgroup, meta-regression, and sensitivity analyses supported the results, showing the reliability of the findings. A rigorous and systematic search strategy was also applied across multiple major databases to minimize the likelihood of missing relevant studies. However, our study has several limitations. First, significant heterogeneity was observed across the studies for some outcomes, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. We applied a random-effects model to pool data, accounting for both within-study and between-study variability. Subgroup analysis revealed that heterogeneity originated from the differences in sample size, dose, duration of follow-up, baseline BMI, method of NAFLD diagnosis, and age of participants. Moreover, some RCTs had methodological limitations, such as unclear blinding or incomplete reporting of allocation concealment, which may contribute to the observed heterogeneity. Second, the limited number of the included trials and their small sample sizes may have restricted statistical power to detect small but clinically relevant effects, raising the possibility of false-negative findings. Third, most included studies had relatively short follow-up durations, preventing conclusions about the long-term effects of ALA supplementation in patients with NAFLD. Accordingly, additional well-designed RCTs with higher sample sizes and longer follow-up durations are required to confirm our results. Fourth, both visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression test have limited statistical power and are not recommended for definitive assessment of publication bias when fewer than 10 studies are included in a meta-analysis [48]. In our study, all outcomes included fewer than 10 effect sizes. Therefore, although funnel plots and Egger’s regression tests were performed as exploratory analyses, the results should be interpreted with caution. Due to the small number of included studies, these tests may lack sufficient power to detect potential asymmetry. Fifth, the study protocol was not registered in a public database (e.g., PROSPERO). However, to minimize the risk of selective reporting, we followed a predefined and systematic methodology for literature search, study selection, data extraction, and outcome assessment. Lastly, data on clinical outcomes such as hepatic fibrosis and steatosis score were insufficient and presented non-uniformly among the RCTs, which prevented us from analyzing these outcomes. Future studies should present a comprehensive and standardized reporting of clinically significant outcomes to enable the precise evaluation of the intervention effects.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis identified that ALA supplementation has no significant positive effect on the levels of liver enzymes, lipid profiles, glucose metabolism indices, adipocytokines, and obesity-related parameters in patients with NAFLD. However, ALA has exhibited anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, and insulin-sensitizing functions in preclinical studies, existing clinical trials do not support ALA supplementation as an effective adjunct therapy for the treatment of NAFLD. Additional RCTs with higher sample sizes are required to better understand the clinical efficacy of ALA in patients with NAFLD.
