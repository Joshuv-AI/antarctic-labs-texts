Sympathetic hyperactivation following endoscopic third ventriculostomy: a rare and underrecognized postoperative complication. Illustrative case
Authors: Salvatore Marino, Fabrizio Pignotti, Giampiero Muggianu, Giuseppe Grande, Katia Musu, Fabio Zeoli, Jacopo Ciccani, Liverana Lauretti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Sympathetic hyperactivation, also referred to as sympathetic storm or paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity, is characterized by an excessive and dysregulated activation of the sympathetic nervous system. It manifests clinically with episodes of hypertension, tachycardia, hyperthermia, diaphoresis, and increased respiratory rate, often accompanied by metabolic alterations. While transient sympathetic excitation is a normal component of the physiological stress response, sustained hyperactivation may lead to significant cardiovascular and neurological complications.

Sympathetic hyperactivity has been described in association with various CNS pathologies, including subarachnoid hemorrhage, traumatic brain injury, and hydrocephalus.1–4 In particular, in patients with hydrocephalus, distortion or compression of diencephalic and brainstem structures involved in autonomic regulation may predispose to abnormal sympathetic discharge.5,6 Surgical procedures addressing these regions, such as endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV), ­inherently involve manipulation near the hypothalamus and periaqueductal gray (PAG) matter—areas critically implicated in sympathetic control.

In this paper, we describe the case of a patient with a long-standing overt ventriculomegaly in adults (LOVA), who developed life-­threatening acute sympathetic hyperactivation suddenly after awakening from ETV surgery. To the best of our knowledge this is the first report of such a complication in an adult patient.

Illustrative Case

A 57-year-old patient with no relevant comorbidities was referred to our department following a brain CT study performed after a recent syncopal episode, which revealed marked supratentorial ventricular enlargement. On admission, neurological examination demonstrated an enlarged head circumference, an ataxic-spastic gait, mild hyposthenia of both the upper and lower limbs, urinary incontinence, and mild short-term memory impairment.

MRI confirmed pronounced dilatation of the lateral and third ventricles, with a callosal angle of 41°, an Evans index of 0.6, and temporal horn diameters of approximately 1 cm. The cerebral aqueduct appears asymmetric, with a narrower proximal segment compared with the distal portion, associated with turbulent flow void. Additional findings included inferior bulging of the third ventricle floor and a partial empty sella (Figs. 1 and 2). These radiological and clinical features were consistent with LOVA with a patent aqueduct. ETV was selected over ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement primarily on the basis of preoperative MRI findings indicating a high likelihood of ETV success. These included bowing of the floor of the third ventricle, bowing of the lamina terminalis and pineal recess, and a favorable prepontine cistern. Additional clinical considerations included the patient’s cranial morphology—namely macrocrania with frontal and parietal bossing—suggestive of long-standing hydrocephalus and an increased risk of subdural hematoma following shunt placement. The decision was further supported by the patient’s preference.

The procedure was uneventful. Right ventriculostomy was successfully performed on the first attempt, and no major bleeding occurred during this step. On ventricular entry, the CSF was clear, and the main anatomical landmarks were clearly identifiable. No vascular malformations or other pathological structures were observed. Intraoperative CSF pressure was not measured; however, the CSF outflow during ventriculostomy was mild.

The stoma at the floor of the third ventricle, precisely at the premammillary recess, was created by blunt dissection using endoscopic forceps and subsequently enlarged with a Fogarty balloon catheter (Fig. 3). No cautery was used throughout the procedure. Minor bleeding from a small vessel at the floor of the third ventricle was encountered and was effectively managed with gentle irrigation using warm saline while maintaining a patent outflow. In the immediate postoperative period, on emergence from general anesthesia, the patient developed acute autonomic dysfunction manifested by generalized tremors, tachycardia (> 150 beats/min), arterial hypertension, tachypnea, and moderate hyperthermia. Neurological examination revealed no abnormalities: the patient was awake, with preserved consciousness, appropriate verbal response, and no new focal deficits.

Because of the persistence of autonomic instability, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit. Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis on admission showed the following: pH 7.54, partial pressure of CO2 (pCO2) 17.2 mm Hg, partial pressure of O2 (pO2) 95.5 mm Hg, lactate 9 mmol/L, glucose 189 mg/dL, and normal electrolytes. A 12-lead electrocardiography study revealed sinus tachycardia, and transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no significant abnormalities. Given the difficulty in controlling autonomic responses, primarily severe and persistent tachycardia unresponsive to medication, endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation were performed under general anesthesia. Continuous infusions of propofol and remifentanil at high doses were initiated, resulting in partial hemodynamic improvement, although tachycardia (120 beats/min) persisted. The patient did not develop any neurological symptoms, remaining fully aware and able to comprehend and respond until reintubation. For these reasons, and considering the complete absence of neurological symptoms, no external ventricular drain was placed; the patient was instead monitored using radiological imaging as well as “neurological windows” with temporary sedation pauses to allow a brief neurological assessment.

A subsequent ABG demonstrated the following: pH 7.40, pCO2 29.2 mm Hg, pO2 144 mm Hg, lactate 4.6 mmol/L, glucose 145 mg/dL, and potassium 3 mmol/L. Brain CT and MRI excluded hemorrhage, infarction, or other acute postoperative complications. CSF analysis, obtained via lumbar puncture, was negative for both biochemical and microbiological abnormalities. Serum creatine phosphokinase was 636 U/L, and serum myoglobin was 619.6 ng/mL. Urinary catecholamines (adrenaline and noradrenaline) were within the normal range.

In the first 24 postoperative hours, during sedation weaning, a progressive increase in heart rate, blood pressure, tachypnea, and body temperature was observed. These manifestations subsided following the administration of deep sedation at maximal doses (propofol up to 15 mg/kg/hr).

The patient was gradually transitioned to pressure support ventilation mode, with progressive reduction of pressure support. Propofol infusion was discontinued, and dexmedetomidine was initiated at 0.5 µg/kg/hr. Lactic acidosis persisted (lactate 4.7 mmol/L).

By postoperative day 2, a marked clinical improvement was observed, with normalization of vital parameters. Sedation was discontinued, and the patient was successfully extubated.

The patient was subsequently transferred to the neurosurgical ward, where the postoperative course remained uneventful. A 4-week neuromotor rehabilitation program led to improved postural stability, gait, and memory function compared with the preoperative condition, along with a marked reduction in urinary incontinence. No further autonomic disturbances were observed during the remainder of the hospitalization.

Discussion

This case highlights the occurrence of acute sympathetic hyperactivation immediately after ETV, emphasizing the potential vulnerability of periventricular autonomic centers during the procedure. A full-blown sympathetic storm following ETV is exceptionally rare and remains poorly characterized in the literature.

Observations

The central autonomic network integrates multiple supratentorial and brainstem structures responsible for maintaining homeostatic sympathetic tone. The hypothalamus plays a pivotal role in modulating sympathetic outflow, as demonstrated by disease secondary to hypothalamic lesions.7 The main structures involved are the paraventricular nucleus; dorsomedial and posterior hypothalamic areas, which project to the medullary reticular formation and spinal intermediolateral columns, mostly through the medial forebrain bundle; and the dorsal longitudinal fasciculus. These structures run through the inferior and lateral walls of the hypothalamus,8–10 as seen in Fig. 4. The PAG matter contributes to autonomic integration, coordinating cardiovascular and respiratory responses to stress. During ETV, surgical instruments traverse the foramen of Monro and operate on the floor of the third ventricle, specifically in the premammillary recess, in close proximity to these critical structures. Even subtle mechanical or thermal irritation of the hypothalamus or PAG could hypothetically transiently disrupt autonomic regulation, resulting in autonomic dysregulation.

An additional consideration is the role of CSF dynamics in modulating autonomic function. The third ventricle is surrounded by circumventricular organs, mainly the median eminence, organum vasculosum lamina terminalis, subfornical organ, and area postrema, sensitive to pressure and flow variations.11 Sudden changes in intraventricular pressure or osmolarity during fenestration of the ventricular floor may alter the mechanical and neurochemical environment of these areas. Rapid decompression or changes in CSF pulsatility could influence hypothalamic or brainstem nuclei involved in baroreceptor reflexes and sympathetic tone regulation.

Despite the abovementioned possibilities of sympathetic hyperactivation, this complication remains exceedingly rare in the context of ETV. Guo and Cao12 described 3 pediatric patients with severe communicating hydrocephalus who developed transient sympathetic storm immediately following ETV combined with choroid plexus cauterization. Moreira Marques et al.13 reported on an adult patient with obstructive hydrocephalus who experienced recurrent episodes of hypotension and hypothermia several years after multiple shunt revisions and two ETVs, suggesting a chronic dysautonomic state. In another report, Muzien et al.14 described a young patient who developed neurogenic pulmonary edema during an ETV procedure, further emphasizing the potential for autonomic instability when operating near periventricular structures.

In our case, the patient presented with LOVA, a condition characterized by chronic and compensated ventricular enlargement. It is conceivable that, over the patient’s lifetime, the hypothalamic nuclei and periventricular white matter fibers had adapted to a specific ­microenvironment—one defined by altered CSF pressure, parenchymal tension, and metabolic balance. Surgical restoration of normal CSF flow and pressure dynamics may have abruptly modified this long-established equilibrium, triggering excessive sympathetic discharge from structures that were chronically exposed to abnormal mechanical and neurochemical conditions. This hypothesis may partially explain why such events are seen only in highly selected patients, despite the widespread use of ETV.

At present, there is no reliable method to predict which patients may experience sympathetic hyperactivation after ETV. It is also unclear whether subtle preoperative signs, such as episodic blood pressure variability, sweating, or temperature instability, could serve as sentinel indicators of latent autonomic dysfunction. Future studies should aim to identify clinical, radiological, or physiological markers of sympathetic vulnerability, particularly in patients with chronic hydrocephalus or hypothalamic compression.

Postoperative sympathetic hyperactivation must be distinguished from other conditions presenting with similar autonomic or hemodynamic instability. Differential diagnoses include emergence agitation, inadequate analgesia, malignant hyperthermia, serotonin syndrome, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, and acute intracranial events such as hemorrhage. Unlike these entities, sympathetic hyperactivation is typically characterized by episodic surges of autonomic activity in the absence of new structural lesions or metabolic disturbances. Recognition of this pattern is critical to avoid unnecessary diagnostic procedures and to guide appropriate supportive management.

Awareness of this potential complication is particularly relevant for neurosurgeons performing ETV and other endoscopic procedures near the third ventricle. Preventive strategies may include gentle manipulation of the ventricular floor, avoidance of prolonged contact or thermal injury near the hypothalamic region, and gradual restoration of CSF flow to minimize abrupt pressure shifts.

In the postoperative setting, close hemodynamic and autonomic monitoring is advised, especially during emergence from anesthesia, to promptly identify and manage sympathetic overactivity. In our case, deep sedation proved effective in controlling sympathetic hyperactivation, and after 3 days the patient achieved complete recovery of hemodynamic and metabolic stability.

Lessons

Acute sympathetic hyperactivation is an exceedingly rare postoperative complication of ETV, particularly in adults. Because the floor of the third ventricle lies in close proximity to hypothalamic and periventricular autonomic pathways, even minimal mechanical, thermal, or pressure-related perturbation may transiently disrupt autonomic regulation. Patients with chronic ventriculomegaly, such as those with LOVA, may be especially vulnerable due to long-standing adaptive changes in periventricular structures and CSF dynamics.

Recognition of this condition is critical, as its clinical ­presentation—sudden tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, tachypnea, diaphoresis, and metabolic derangements—can mimic more common postoperative complications such as inadequate analgesia, emergence agitation, malignant hyperthermia, infection, or intracranial events. A normal neurological examination and unremarkable imaging should prompt consideration of autonomic dysregulation.

Early identification allows initiation of appropriate supportive measures, including deep sedation and autonomic modulation, which can rapidly stabilize the patient and prevent potentially life-threatening cardiovascular or metabolic consequences. This case underscores the importance of heightened vigilance for autonomic disturbances following ETV and highlights the need for further clinical reporting to better define risk factors, mechanisms, and optimal management strategies.
