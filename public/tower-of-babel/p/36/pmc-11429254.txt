Exploring experiences and coping strategies of the surveillance of indeterminate pulmonary nodules: a qualitative content analysis among participants in the SCAPIS trial
Authors: Ying Dai Ydrefelt, Elisabeth Andersson, Anetta Bolejko
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo elucidate experiences and coping strategies among adults in the surveillance of indeterminate pulmonary nodules detected with CT in the population-based Swedish CardioPulmonary bioImage Study (SCAPIS).DesignA qualitative study of conventional content analysis.SettingsThe study was conducted at a university hospital in a southern region of Sweden. The SCAPIS setting is similar to the first round of a population-based lung cancer screening programme.ParticipantsParticipants in SCAPIS who had experienced psychosocial consequences of the surveillance were eligible. Participants of both genders, current, former and non-smokers and of different follow-ups in the surveillance were included. Face-to-face semi-structured interviews with 19 participants were performed using an interview guide with open-ended questions. The participants were aged 56–68 years. Nine were women, 6 and 13 were non-smokers and smokers or former smokers, respectively, and all participants had undergone at least one follow-up of the lungs in the surveillance programme.ResultsThe results depicted an emotional and mental journey for the participants from being distressed when informed about the need of surveillance, and realising their risks of getting sick if they did not take care of their own health, to eventually gathering the strength to cope with the situation, so the surveillance was finally valued with trust and satisfaction. The experiences and coping strategies in the surveillance programme developed a revelation of the value of health consciousness among the participants.ConclusionThe study results demonstrated that a surveillance programme of pulmonary nodules might develop health consciousness among people. Still, some individuals might experience psychosocial consequences of the surveillance of indeterminate nodules. Therefore, healthcare professionals should be facilitated to perform person-centred communication to support individuals under surveillance. Preventive care to engage individuals as partners in the management of their own health should receive more attention and needs to be explored.

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) is the most common cancer and the leading cause of cancer death worldwide.1 Also, it remains the disease that takes the most lives in cancer deaths in Sweden.2 Curative treatment has become available at early stages of the disease,3 which implies screening for early detection of LC might have the potential to improve the prognosis and decrease mortality in LC.3 Screening is defined as a test or examination provided to a population to identify individuals who may have a disease but have not yet shown the signs or symptoms of the disease.4 Further diagnostic workups might be needed to confirm whether a finding in the screening test is truly a pathology.4 In the LC screening context, the detection of indeterminate pulmonary nodules requires additional workup, that is, a surveillance programme with repeated follow-ups that can last up to 3 years, depending on the characteristics of the findings.5 6 In Sweden, it is considered that LC screening for high-risk groups (ie, smokers and former smokers) might be motivated, but the Swedish National Board of Health is calling for evidence in the context of Swedish population.3 How the surveillance programme is experienced by asymptomatic individuals should be addressed.

The recent development within medical imaging makes it possible to detect LC at its asymptomatic stage with CT of low radiation dose (LDCT).7 8 Two clinical trials, the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST)9 and the Dutch-Belgian LC screening trial (NELSON)10 concluded that screening of the high-risk population with LDCT reduces mortality of LC. However, both trials reported that high rates of LDCT follow-up were needed to monitor indeterminate pulmonary nodules. But in only very few individuals the surveillance resulted in screening detected LC.9 10 In the NLST, nearly one-quarter of the LDCT scans during the three rounds of annual LDCT resulted in the need of surveillance, while 96% of the nodules monitored with the surveillance were benign.9

Systematic reviews demonstrated that there may be psychosocial consequences of screening, particularly after the indeterminate nodules were detected.11 12 In the Swedish context, LDCT of the lungs was performed on all participants at entry to the Swedish CardioPulmonary bioImage Study (SCAPIS).13 Indeterminate pulmonary nodules were followed up according to the surveillance programme developed in the pilot trial of SCAPIS.14 The study setting is similar to the first round of an LC screening programme and thus, provided the context to investigate the psychosocial effects of LC screening in a Swedish population. Increased probability for anxiety about LC (OR 3.96), experiencing a sense of dejection (OR 1.25) and thoughts about existential values (OR 1.30) were reported among those in surveillance of indeterminate pulmonary nodules and who eventually did not receive an LC diagnosis when the surveillance programme ended.15

Lazarus and Folkman defined coping as ‘constantly changing cognitive and behavioural efforts to manage specific external and/or internal demands that are appraised as taxing or exceeding the resources of the person’.16 Since psychological effects were reported,15 the surveillance of pulmonary nodules could be considered as a demanding situation for an individual. A thematically analysed interview study aiming at identifying key consequences of indeterminate findings in LC screenings reported experiences of self-blame, stigmatisation and being introverted.17 An interview study in a context of early recall in breast cancer screening also found demanding psychological experiences where the participants were imaging the worst, had fatalistic thinking and experienced fear of death.18 Accordingly, it is of vast importance to study coping strategies and how to support people undergoing surveillance in cancer screening. In addition, an explorative study of how individuals experience and cope with such a situation would provide additional evidence on the effects of LC screening. To the best of our knowledge, naturalistic inquiries19 conducted in conjunction with LC screening trials are scarce. The current study is the second part of the project on the psychosocial effects of LC screening15 in the context of SCAPIS.

Aim

This study aimed to elucidate experiences and coping strategies among adults in the surveillance of indeterminate pulmonary nodules detected with LDCT.

Method

Study design

Conventional content analysis as set out by Graneheim and Lundman20 was applied in this study. It is an inductive approach that is appropriate to apply to explore and describe a phenomenon when existing knowledge is limited and does not provide clues for any hypothesis. This research design allows the researcher to become immersed in the qualitative data (eg, narratives) and thus, new insights emerge.20 21

Participants

Participants were recruited from a Swedish municipality in a southern region, Malmö, which is one of the sites in the SCAPIS study.13 Those who had followed the surveillance programme of indeterminate pulmonary nodules and did not get an LC diagnosis when the surveillance was completed were eligible. Details of the surveillance programme are provided in publications related to indeterminate pulmonary nodules detected in SCAPIS.14 15 In brief, all had a follow-up LDCT 3 months after baseline tomography. Depending on the findings, a 2-year LDCT follow-up or an annual examination in 3 years is added. A few participants were even called for a clinical follow-up during the surveillance. Having experienced psychosocial consequences of the surveillance reported in the first study15 of the project was used for purposeful selection to the current study. In addition, the purpose was to select participants of both sexes, current, former and non-smokers, and who had undergone different types of follow-up in the surveillance programme (table 1). Those who were registered in the LDCT records as not speaking Swedish were excluded. It was estimated that a sample size of 15–20 participants would achieve sufficient variation of the data and strength to elucidate the aim of the study.22

In total, 46 individuals were selected and invited to participate. Twenty-three of those invited returned the signed informed consent. Two were not available for interview. Thus, 21 interviews were performed. Two interviews were excluded due to the participants’ insufficient proficiency in Swedish. Hence, this study included 19 participants. Data collection

A study information letter with a consent letter and a prestamped envelope attached was sent by mail. Following receipt of a signed informed consent, the individual was approached to arrange a face-to-face interview. A semi-structured interview guide was applied during the interview. The guide was developed on the basis of research experience of the authors. Open-ended questions were used. The questions addressed the experiences, thoughts and feelings of the participants during their participation in the surveillance, as well as after the surveillance was completed. The questions also included what the participants were doing to cope with their thoughts and feelings, what kind of support they had received and their attitudes towards the surveillance programme and reflections on LC. The participants were allowed to talk freely, yet the questions helped to encourage reflections on the surveillance.

In the first five interviews, two authors, YDY and EA, were present. YDY led the interviews while EA sat in the background and asked supplementary question(s) when needed. This approach was used so the researchers could support each other in taking notes and asking probing questions. The intention was also to verify the interview guide, but no adjustment of the guide was needed. It was also concluded that the support from the second interviewer was seldom needed, while two interviewers might put the participant in an uncomfortable position and limit the narratives. While the quality of the interviews was judged as good, the remaining interviews were conducted by one author, YDY. Two interviews were conducted in the participant’s residence, and others in a conference room in the hospital where the LDCT follow-ups were performed. The interviews lasted between 30 min and an hour. All the interviews were voice-recorded and were transcribed verbatim. Notes on silences, sighs, laughs and the like were included.

Data analysis

The data were analysed according to the methodology of Graneheim and Lundman.20 First, the interview transcripts were read repeatedly to gain an overall and naïve view of the experiences of surveillance and of coping with it. During the reading, meaning units, which were defined as words, sentences and paragraphs together with the signs of emotions that reflect the same content, were identified. The meaning units were condensed in the next step, in other words, shortened without losing the essential meaning. Meaning units that were related to each other in their content were then clustered to be labelled with a code. These analysis procedures were performed by YDY and discussed with another author AB who performed the coding of two interviews. After a discussion of meaning units and codes, the analysis was continued by YDY. This was a forwards-backwards process, and the codes were revised until they precisely reflected the content of the units. Codes that shared a commonality were gathered into preliminary subcategories and categories. Finally, the tentative analysis results were discussed by all three authors and the final subcategories and categories were defined. Through interpretation of the underlying meaning of the meaning units, codes, subcategories and categories, the theme emerged. Such an analysis process addresses the manifest and latent content of the data, which means that both the description of the visible and obvious components and the interpretation of the underlying meaning were included.20 NVivo 12 Plus was used to organise the data and assist the analysis process.

Results

The analysis resulted in four categories and in each category, there were four subcategories, altogether reflecting experiences and coping strategies among the study participants. The theme that represents the overall meaning of the experiences and coping strategies was a revelation of the value of health consciousness among the participants. Experiencing emotional distress

The surveillance was experienced as a situation of uncertainty, which led to the feelings of unease. Thoughts on what the nodule in the lungs could be, cancer or not cancer, came up from time to time or just stayed in the mind of the participants. Distress was also expressed, along with feelings of anxiety about having LC. The participants were worried about having a fatal disease. They experienced nervousness, fear and sadness as well.

The thoughts popped up, the information from the letter popped up. What can it be? It’s an unpleasant situation. (Participant 9)

The first few days were hysterical actually, they said that I had a dot on my lungs, which might be the same as cancer, that’s at least how I saw it. I can still recollect when it washed over me. (Participant 1)

Another expression of the emotional distress was the participants’ frustration of not getting support. The participants criticised unclear and fragmented information from the healthcare professionals. Not having a family member or a friend to share the experience with was another reason to feel frustrated.

He said, we’ve seen a change and then he said a few words in Latin that I didn’t even come close to knowing what they were about, and I might have asked a question or so, but yes it could be anything, he didn’t know, and then of course I had the little… it was spinning a little in my head ‘what is this?’ (Participant 17)

The emotional distress was further revealed by the participants’ efforts to repress their thoughts so that the reality could be escaped. This experience was concealed from the participants themselves so that questions, comments or cares which might cause distressful feelings could be avoided. Close family members, especially children, were protected from knowing. Efforts were made not to think about the nodule since it was perceived as unnecessary to worry about something that one could not change or do anything about.

Why put thoughts and effort into something that you can’t change. You’ve got an appointment in two months. You can’t come earlier. There is no need to go around worrying or… (Participant 9).

Becoming conscious of the importance of taking care of own health

The unexpected detection of the nodule made the participants perceive the threat of getting sick. The detection of the nodule led to reflections on sickness and death. The suffering of being sick was concerning. Current and former smokers thought about the consequences of smoking, not only the risk of developing LC but also their potential risk of other smoking-related diseases. After the programme ended, some individuals continued to feel threatened because they believed that something that should not be in their body was still present. Meanwhile, the participants became more aware of their own risks of getting LC. Both current and former smokers directly related the pulmonary nodule to smoking. Participants had also reflected on working conditions or harmful materials they had used and identified those as risk factors. Participants, especially non-smokers, searched through their health history to understand why they had nodules and what could be risk factors. Thus, a feeling of guilt that one had not taken care of one’s own health arose. This feeling of guilt was strongly expressed by current smokers. Besides, participants regretted careless lifestyle that was harmful to health, for example, having experimented with drugs and overconsumed alcohol.

But before I didn’t think I’d get lung cancer, or I’d die. It’s a bit like that. But after now I think anyone (can get sick), you don’t know what’s in there. (Participant 12).

I’m a smoker and have a job with a lot of crap and mould and so on… dust and stuff like that. So of course, you think about it anyway, right? (Participant 5)

I started smoking when I was 11, 12 years old. Stopped when I was 45 and (smoked) one and a half packs a day at the end, almost up to two packs, some days. So, I know that I have ruined the body, so that… I have no one to blame but myself. (Participant 4)

While feeling the risks of being fatally sick were so close, the participants started to evaluate the need for health surveillance. Although conflicting thoughts on whether it was really worthwhile to know earlier that one is sick was mentioned during the interview, the participants were quite consistent in saying that it was good to know if one was sick or not. To be able to do something about the disease was the obvious reason to have surveillance.

I kind of understand why you’re doing this investigation, because it’s not completely uncomplicated to do screening like this, but at the same time I think that if I had to do it all over again, I would, because I think that the value of knowing is always greater. (Participant 1)

Bringing out own strength and power to cope with the distress

In reflecting of the experience, the participants praised themselves for being strong and rational. The participants tried to think positively and not let the negative take over. Social interactions were a frequently mentioned part of the coping process. Meanwhile, spiritual faith was mentioned as another source of power that helped in this situation. Through focusing on a busy everyday life, thoughts about the nodule and surveillance were forgotten.

Experiencing satisfaction with and feeling trust in surveillance

The participants expressed high trust in the healthcare system and the professional competence of the healthcare professionals who worked for SCAPIS throughout the surveillance programme. The professional and caring way to meet the participants was considered as a great support.

I trust those who have conducted the examinations and want to do this supplemental examination. I felt more, more secure, more secure than worried because I thought it’s good that they have found something and then take the responsibility to follow it up. (Participant 7)

The participants felt confident that they did not have LC after receiving results indicating that the pulmonary nodule no longer needed monitoring. This confidence may not have been immediate, but over time, their doubts faded. Meanwhile, the participants felt grateful for life and wished to enjoy life. Hence, the participants were happy about their health status and wished to improve their health. The participants valued health surveillance and wished to get professional help to have their health under control.

So, what I would like is therefore professional help that the lungs and heart are under control. (Participant 4)

Discussion

This study provided narrative reflections on experiences and coping strategies among individuals who had LDCT screening of their lungs and were having surveillance of indeterminate pulmonary nodules in the SCAPIS trial. The trial setting is similar to the first round of an LC screening programme and thus provided the context to study experiences of LC screening in Sweden. The results depicted an emotional and mental journey from being distressed when informed about the need for surveillance and realising one’s risk of getting sick if not taking care of own health, to eventually gathering one’s strength and power to cope with the situation so that the surveillance was finally valued with trust and satisfaction. Overall, the experiences led to the awareness of the importance of taking care of one’s health.

The experiences among study participants in their emotional and mental journey throughout the surveillance programme can be related to the conceptualisation of stress, cognitive appraisal and coping by Lazarus and Folkman.16 Stress is considered as a relationship between the person and the environment. The person makes an individual assessment of whether one’s resources have been exceeded and whether there has been a decrease in well-being. If this is the case, the relationship between the person and the environment is thus perceived as stressful. This process is called cognitive appraisal. Since a pulmonary nodule was an indeterminate finding, without any symptoms or painful treatments, it might be assumed that it was not perceived as a harm. The experiences of emotional distress and the stressful awareness of the risks of losing healthiness were more likely the result of a threat appraisal.16

The results showed that the healthcare system and healthcare professionals may have an important role in an individual’s appraisal and coping process, since participants seemed to trust the healthcare system, and the healthcare professionals were considered as a great support. High levels of trust in the healthcare professionals while communicating about LC screening were previously reported, and so trust was interpreted as a result of the healthcare professionals treating the individual as a person and considering the individual’s values and preferences.23 Yet, we also observed a lack of satisfactory communication, for example, not being able to let the individual understand what a pulmonary nodule is and why surveillance was needed. Several studies in the setting of clinically detected pulmonary nodules reported the same problem in the context of clinician communication with patients.2426 Also, the assumption of healthcare professionals that too much information would be difficult for the individual to understand was identified as one of the barriers to communication in LC screening.27 In contrast, our results showed that detailed information from healthcare professionals was highly appreciated, and the participants embraced the opportunity to know more about their health status. Thus, we would suggest that education is needed to facilitate the healthcare professionals with their person-centred communication skills. Person-centred care means the involvement of the individual as a partner in the person’s care and decision-making, which also includes having knowledge of the values, preferences and social circumstances of the individual.28

We interpreted the coping outcomes among participants as a revelation of health consciousness. In addition to the thankfulness and satisfaction associated with surveillance, the participants expressed an essential key desire to live a healthy life. The healthcare system is expected to provide recommendations and support to prevent the development of diseases, not least in screening, with its purpose of detecting a fatal disease, such as LC, in its asymptomatic and curable stage.4 Participants in the current study emphasised the benefits of early detection and treatment of LC despite the experiences of distress. A systematic review of women’s values and preferences concerning breast cancer screening showed similar results and concluded that women value the possibility of an early diagnosis higher than harms, like receiving an indeterminate finding that required further surveillance.29 However, it is still an ethical challenge that while some individuals benefit from screening, others may suffer from psychosocial consequences without gaining any benefits from screening.30 Still, efforts to improve screening are being made, for example, in the LC screening context, it has been suggested to optimise the threshold for surveillance of the nodules and to characterise the nodules with computer-aided analysis methods.31 However, we suggest that decision-makers in healthcare should also emphasise the importance of preventive measures such as engaging people in taking care of their health in everyday life. For instance, it has been reported that the emphasis on early diagnosis of cancer has influenced the public’s perception of cancer symptoms and has encouraged the behaviour of listening to one’s own body in daily life. The author suggested using the concept of symptom experiences to explore the relationship between biomedical knowledge and embodied experiences.32 As another example, oncology nurses in Japan investigated how to help people understand the relationship between the perception of cancer and lifestyle factors, using a theory of health as a development of one’s consciousness. Instead of viewing cancer as a disease that invades the human body, cancer was explained as a manifestation of lifestyle disharmony. Through dialogue and reflections, people were encouraged to recognise and reflect on their lifestyle patterns and thus, changes towards a harmonious lifestyle might occur. The potential of helping people to prevent cancer through lifestyle change was suggested.33 To develop health consciousness and engage an individual as a partner in the management of health, which is the core of person-centred care,28 may provide inspiration for future research on primary cancer prevention.

Strengths and limitations

Our overall aim was to explore the experiences of surveillance of indeterminate pulmonary nodules detected with LDCT in the context of LC screening. This study is the second part of the project, subsequent to an observational cross-sectional study.15 By using the findings of the first study to make a purposeful selection of the participants, we achieved a concurrent triangulation as it is described in the mixed method procedures.34 We suggest that using this strategy increased the credibility of the study results and gave insights into the phenomenon of experiences of surveillance. The interview data complemented the quantitative findings. In addition, we performed a purposeful selection to include a diverse group of participants—men and women, individuals with different follow-up stages in the surveillance programme and smokers, former smokers and non-smokers. Although we excluded non-Swedish speakers, we were able to include participants with a non-Nordic ethnic background. Such information was spontaneously retrieved during the interviews. In the analysis process, triangulation was performed with the involvement of all the authors. The three authors held continuous dialogues to reflect on the research questions and discussed the coding and categorisation process to share their different insights into the analysis. Thus, the credibility of the study results could be strengthened.1922

The first author of the study, YDY, has limited experience with naturalistic inquiry. On the other hand, the last author, AB, is experienced in conducting qualitative studies.18 35 36 Also, we started the study with some preunderstanding of the study topic since both YDY and the second author EA had worked with CT examinations in the SCAPIS trial and the surveillance programme. Furthermore, all three authors were engaged in the first study of the project.15 Our preunderstanding might have influenced the credibility of the study, but at the same time, our experiences and knowledge of the topic provided us with reflections on the phenomenon, which gave the basis of the formulation of the research questions. Our involvement in the surveillance programme as healthcare professionals made us spectators in the dialogue and partners who could interact with the participants, which is in accordance with the philosophy of qualitative methodology.37 The research team was aware of the possible impact of preunderstanding on the results, and this issue was handled by continuous reflection and discussion during the data collection and data analysis process. The data were collected over a long period of time, which might have introduced inconsistency in the data, which in turn might have influenced the dependability of the study results.20 However, the presence of two interviewers made it possible to verify the interview guide and discuss the quality of the content of the interviews. Consistency of the data was also pursued through continuous dialogue within the research team.20 The credibility and dependability of the study might have been affected by recall bias since at least 2 years had passed from the baseline LDCT examination in SCAPIS to the time of the interview. A study of prospective longitudinal design would be needed to give depictions of live experiences in such a context. We chose to only select participants from the Malmö site while the SCAPIS trial was conducted in six Swedish municipalities. This might have limited the transferability of the study results. Yet Malmö is the third largest municipality in Sweden, and it is recognised for the diversity of its inhabitants in terms of ethnicity, culture and socioeconomic situation.

Conclusion

The study results demonstrated that the surveillance programme of indeterminate pulmonary nodules detected with CT might develop health consciousness among people. Still, some individuals might experience psychosocial consequences of surveillance. Therefore, healthcare professionals should be facilitated to perform person-centred communication to support individuals in a surveillance programme. Preventive care to engage individuals as partners in the management of their own health should receive more attention and needs to be explored.
