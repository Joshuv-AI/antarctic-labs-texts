Outcomes in offspring of interventions to treat substance use in pregnancy: protocol for an umbrella review
Authors: Joshuan J. Barboza, Oriana Rivera-Lozada, Cesar Bonilla-Asalde, Danis Castillo-Esquivel, Nancy Calzada-Gonzales, Felipe Montecino-Rojas, Oscar Jorge-Zapata, Mahycol Bravo-Ramirez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubstance use during pregnancy is linked to adverse neonatal and longer-term outcomes in offspring, yet evidence syntheses have primarily focused on maternal outcomes. An umbrella review is needed to consolidate and critically appraise findings from systematic reviews on how interventions to treat substance use in pregnancy affect outcomes in offspring of pregnant women and pregnant individuals.MethodsWe will conduct an umbrella review of systematic reviews (with or without meta-analysis) that include quantitative primary studies of pregnant women and pregnant individuals with substance use who receive pharmacological, psychosocial/behavioural, integrated care, or community-based interventions. Searches will cover MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and Epistemonikos from inception. Two independent reviewers will perform title/abstract screening, full-text screening, and data extraction at every stage; a third reviewer will arbitrate disagreements. Methodological quality of included systematic reviews will be assessed using AMSTAR 2; risk of bias of primary studies will be reported as appraised in the original systematic reviews. Overlap of primary studies will be quantified using the Corrected Covered Area (CCA), with attention to multi-arm studies and to whether overlapping primary studies actually answer the same PICO question. We will narratively synthesise findings (avoiding vote counting) and, where systematic reviews report pooled estimates, we will summarise these without conducting de novo meta-analyses of primary studies. Certainty of evidence will be reported using existing GRADE assessments, complemented by a structured umbrella-level appraisal that incorporates large effects and dose-response gradients when relevant.DiscussionThis umbrella review will clarify which interventions have the strongest evidence of benefit (or harm) for neonatal, congenital, neurodevelopmental, and behavioural/mental health outcomes in offspring, identify evidence gaps and methodological limitations, and inform clinical guidance and maternal-child health policy.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD420261321678Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-026-03239-1.

Background

Substance use during pregnancy is a significant public health problem worldwide and has been consistently associated with a wide range of adverse consequences for both mothers and their children [1]. Beyond the effects on maternal health, there is growing concern about the short-, medium- and long-term outcomes for offspring prenatally exposed to substance use and to interventions aimed at treating such use during pregnancy [2]. Adverse outcomes most frequently reported in children include premature birth [3], low birth weight [4], neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS) [5], growth disorders [6] and various neurodevelopmental disorders that may persist beyond the perinatal period [7].

Evidence indicates that opioid use disorder (OUD) during pregnancy is associated with adverse neonatal outcomes and an increased risk of infant mortality [8, 9]. In particular, the use of pharmacological therapies for the treatment of OUD has been linked to significant maternal benefits, as well as specific neonatal outcomes, including differences in birth weight [10] and the occurrence of neonatal withdrawal syndrome [11]. Taken together, these findings highlight the complex clinical balance between the benefits of treatment for the mother and the potential risks to the newborn, underscoring the need to systematically evaluate the effects of these interventions on offspring.

Prenatal alcohol exposure is a well-established teratogenic factor and remains one of the leading preventable causes of foetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD) [12]. In addition to characteristic neurocognitive impairments [13], such exposure has been associated with intrauterine growth restriction [14], preterm birth [15], and other adverse outcomes in the newborn [16]. Although the direction of the effect described in the literature is consistent, the magnitude and certainty of the evidence vary depending on the specific areas of development assessed and the context of the interventions implemented during pregnancy. Similarly, prenatal exposure to other psychoactive substances, such as cocaine and methamphetamine, has been linked to low birth weight and prematurity [17, 18], as well as neurobehavioural manifestations in newborns [19, 20], reinforcing the importance of timely interventions aimed at reducing or modifying such exposures and potentially improving outcomes for offspring.

To reduce substance use during pregnancy and mitigate its adverse effects, a wide variety of interventions have been implemented, including pharmacological treatments [10], psychosocial strategies [21], and behavioural approaches [22]. Medications for the treatment of opioid use disorder (MOUD), such as methadone and buprenorphine, are widely recommended during pregnancy, as they improve adherence and continuity of care and are considered the therapeutic standard for untreated OUD, with documented benefits in maternal and perinatal care [23]. However, NAS remains a frequent and predictable outcome in exposed offspring, raising important questions about the overall impact of these interventions on neonatal and child development outcomes [24]. Psychosocial interventions, including motivational interviewing and various forms of counselling, are routinely recommended to increase participation in treatment services and reduce substance use during pregnancy [25, 26], although their specific effects on outcomes in offspring have been less systematically synthesised [21].

In recent years, there has been a substantial increase in the number of systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating interventions targeting substance use during pregnancy [27, 28]. However, most available systematic reviews have focused on maternal outcomes, such as abstinence, reduction in consumption, or retention in treatment, while offspring outcomes have been evaluated less frequently or indirectly [29, 30]. Several methodological studies have pointed out that the comparison and overall interpretation of these systematic reviews may be limited by the heterogeneity of the populations and interventions evaluated, variability in the definition and measurement of outcomes, the potential overlap of primary studies, and differences in the approaches used to assess the certainty of the evidence [31, 32]. These limitations make it difficult to translate findings into clinical practice and policy.

In this context, an umbrella review is warranted to synthesise and critically appraise the available evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the effects of interventions for substance use during pregnancy on offspring outcomes. This approach will allow for a structured comparison of reported outcomes, an appraisal of the methodological quality of the included systematic reviews, the identification of overlap between their primary studies, and an assessment of the overall certainty of the evidence for each relevant outcome in offspring. The findings of this umbrella review will inform clinical practice, support decision-making in maternal and child health policies, and define priorities for future research in this field.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses will be conducted following the methodological guidance from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for umbrella reviews [33] and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [34] and its extension for overviews of reviews (PRIOR) [35]. The protocol was reported following PRISMA-P [36] and has been registered prospectively with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420261321678). The PROSPERO record will be updated to ensure full alignment with the present, peer-reviewed protocol.

The population of interest comprises pregnant women and pregnant individuals of any age with documented substance use. Following reviewer feedback, we have prioritised exposure during pregnancy and have explicitly distinguished pre-pregnancy exposure to maximise clarity. We will adopt an inclusive approach with respect to participant characteristics and care contexts. Geographic location, socioeconomic status, and the type of healthcare service in which the intervention is delivered (community primary care centres, specialised hospitals, addiction treatment services) will not constitute exclusion criteria, recognising that substance use during pregnancy crosses all strata. Trimester of intervention initiation (first, second or third) and pregnancy planning status (planned vs. unplanned) will likewise not restrict eligibility, given the substantial heterogeneity of access to and engagement with maternal care services worldwide.

Concerning the substances of interest, we have adopted a comprehensive approach reflecting the complexity of substance use during pregnancy. We will include alcohol in any quantity or pattern of consumption, from occasional use to heavy or binge drinking; all forms of tobacco and nicotine, including traditional cigarettes, electronic cigarettes/vaping devices, and smokeless tobacco. Opioids include both illicit opioids (e.g. heroin) and the non-medical or illicit use of prescription opioids; chronic, supervised opioid agonist therapy (e.g. methadone, buprenorphine) administered as treatment for OUD will be considered an intervention rather than an exposure (see Interventions below). Cannabis or marijuana, stimulants (cocaine, methamphetamine, amphetamines), sedatives (benzodiazepines, barbiturates), and other illicit psychoactive substances reported in primary studies will be included. Polysubstance use will be eligible, as it reflects real-world patterns and may require equally complex interventions.

Interventions

Pharmacological treatments

Pharmacological interventions will include opioid agonist therapy with methadone or buprenorphine in pregnant women and pregnant individuals with opioid dependence. We will include nicotine replacement therapy in all formulations approved for smoking cessation, explicitly comprising transdermal nicotine patches/plasters, gum, lozenges, inhalers, sublingual tablets, and nasal sprays. Medications for alcohol use disorder such as naltrexone, acamprosate, or disulfiram will be included, as will any other pharmacological agents used for the management of substance use during pregnancy.

Psychosocial and behavioural interventions

This category includes cognitive behavioural therapy, motivational interviewing, and contingency management as structured intervention strategies. Brief interventions designed to leverage limited clinical encounters will be included along with psychoeducation programmes that provide information about substances and pregnancy. Family therapy, group therapy, and individual counselling in their various formats will complete this set of interventions focused on modifying behaviours and patterns of consumption.

Integrated healthcare models and community programmes

Comprehensive prenatal care programmes that incorporate substance use management into obstetric care represent a relevant intervention model. Coordinated care interventions linking multiple services, multidisciplinary team approaches, and case management services facilitating access and continuity of care will fall under this category. In addition, community-based treatment programmes, home-visit programmes, and self-help groups (e.g. Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous) will be considered. These interventions are characterised by implementation in non-traditional clinical settings and a central role of the community in the therapeutic process.

Interventions will be considered regardless of delivery format (individual, group, or remote via digital or telephone platforms). Intensity (number of sessions or total duration), implementation setting (inpatient, outpatient, community-based), theoretical framework, and the type of provider administering the intervention will not constitute exclusion criteria.

Comparators

Eligible comparators include usual or standard antenatal care as defined in each care context, no intervention, placebo (in trials evaluating pharmacological treatments), alternative active interventions (head-to-head comparisons), different doses or intensities of the same intervention, and deferred-intervention or waiting-list groups. Single-arm primary studies without a comparator will not be included as the unit of evidence; however, systematic reviews that synthesise both controlled and single-arm primary studies will be eligible, and we will only extract data from comparative analyses (i.e. the controlled subset). The specific definition of each comparator will be accepted as reported by the authors of the original systematic reviews.

Outcomes

Neonatal outcomes

Systematic reviews must report at least one offspring-related outcome to be eligible. During the neonatal period (birth to 28 days of life), anthropometric measures will include birth weight, low birth weight (< 2500 g), very low birth weight (< 1500 g), body length, head circumference, and classification as small or large for gestational age. Gestation-related outcomes include gestational age at delivery, preterm birth (< 37 weeks), and very preterm birth (< 32 weeks). Clinical indicators include Apgar scores at one and five minutes, admission to neonatal intensive care, and length of hospitalisation. Neonatal complications of interest are neonatal withdrawal syndrome, respiratory distress syndrome, the need for mechanical ventilation, and seizures. Mortality outcomes include foetal death, neonatal death, and perinatal mortality.

Congenital and developmental anomalies

This domain covers structural defects present at birth affecting different organ systems, including cardiac malformations, neural tube defects, orofacial anomalies, and limb malformations, among others. Foetal alcohol spectrum disorders and their associated phenotypes constitute a specific group of outcomes linked to alcohol consumption during pregnancy. Other malformations specifically associated with substance use during gestation will also be included.

Neurodevelopmental outcomes

From infancy through childhood, multiple dimensions of neurological and cognitive development will be examined. Cognitive development will be assessed using IQ scores and rating scales such as the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development. Language development will include both expressive and receptive skills. Motor development will cover gross and fine motor skills, and the achievement of developmental milestones. Executive functions include attention, working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility.

Behavioural and mental health outcomes

Behavioural problems will be classified into externalising behaviours (aggression, conduct problems) and internalising behaviours (anxiety, depression, social isolation). Attention and activity outcomes cover diagnosis or symptoms of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. Social-emotional development includes social competence, emotional regulation, and peer relationships. Mental health diagnoses such as depression, anxiety disorders, and other psychiatric conditions will be considered. Substance use in adolescence or early adulthood, including initiation and development of substance use disorders, will also be examined.

Study designs of primary studies

Systematic reviews may include randomised controlled trials, quasi-randomised trials, and non-randomised controlled trials as experimental designs. Eligible observational designs include prospective or retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies. Before-and-after studies and other comparative designs will also be considered. Systematic reviews that include only case series or individual case reports will be excluded.

Other inclusion criteria

Only systematic reviews published in peer-reviewed journals will be included. No language restrictions will be applied; articles in languages other than English will be translated for assessment and inclusion. No publication date restrictions will be established, in order to capture all available evidence.

Summary of exclusion criteria

We will exclude narrative reviews without systematic methodology, scoping reviews, rapid reviews, primary studies, study protocols, editorials, commentaries, conference abstracts, and systematic reviews that do not report extractable data on offspring outcomes.

Information sources

A comprehensive search will be conducted in multiple electronic databases from inception to the search date. Databases will include MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (CDSR), and Epistemonikos [37]. Reference lists of all included systematic reviews will be screened manually to identify additional eligible records.

Search strategy development

The search strategy will combine controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree) and free-text terms related to substance use, pregnancy, interventions, and review type. A preliminary search string will include terms such as: (“substance use” OR “drug dependence”) AND “pregnancy” AND “intervention” AND (“systematic review” OR “meta-analysis”). The strategy will be developed and piloted in MEDLINE/PubMed by an information specialist; precision and recall will be checked against a set of known relevant systematic reviews, and adjustments will be made before adapting the strategy to the syntax of each remaining database. The PRESS (Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies) checklist [38] will be used to peer-review the final MEDLINE strategy. Full search strategies for all databases will be provided as supplementary material.

Study records

Data management

All records identified through electronic database searches will be imported into Rayyan (Rayyan Systems Inc.), an online platform that supports systematic review screening and data management [39]. Rayyan will be used to store references, manage duplicates, and facilitate collaboration among reviewers throughout the screening and selection process. Duplicate records will be identified and removed using Rayyan’s automated deduplication function, with manual verification when required. The number of records at each stage of the umbrella review (identification, screening, eligibility assessment, inclusion) will be documented and reported using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Selection process

Titles and abstracts of all retrieved records will be screened independently and in duplicate by two reviewers using the predefined eligibility criteria. Records considered potentially eligible by either reviewer will be advanced to full-text assessment. Full-text records will likewise be assessed independently and in duplicate by two reviewers. Disagreements at either stage will be resolved through discussion and, if consensus cannot be reached, by a third reviewer (JJB). Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be documented and reported in the PRISMA flow diagram. Inter-reviewer agreement will be calculated using Cohen’s kappa for the full-text screening stage.

Data collection process

Data extraction will be performed independently and in duplicate by two reviewers using a standardised data extraction form developed for this umbrella review and pilot-tested on three systematic reviews before full deployment. The form follows JBI methodological guidance for umbrella reviews. Discrepancies in extracted data will be resolved by discussion between the two reviewers, with reference to the original systematic reviews; persistent disagreements will be arbitrated by a third reviewer (JJB).

Assessment of methodological quality

AMSTAR 2 for systematic reviews

The methodological quality of the included systematic reviews will be appraised independently and in duplicate by two reviewers using the AMSTAR 2 instrument (A MeaSurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews) [40]. AMSTAR 2 comprises 16 methodological domains; 7 are considered critical for determining overall confidence in the results of each systematic review: protocol registration before initiation (Item 2), adequacy of the literature search (Item 4), justification for excluding individual studies (Item 7), risk of bias assessment of included primary studies (Item 9), suitability of the meta-analytic methods (Item 11), consideration of risk of bias when interpreting results (Item 13), and assessment of publication bias (Item 15). The remaining domains (Items 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, and 16) will be considered non-critical. Each item will be rated as “yes”, “partial yes”, “no”, or “no meta-analysis was performed”, as appropriate.

Two specific operationalisations have been adopted in response to peer-review recommendations: first, for Item 11 (suitability of the meta-analysis method), when the systematic review under appraisal did not perform a quantitative meta-analysis, we will explicitly judge the suitability of its narrative synthesis. Vote counting based on direction of effect (or on statistical significance) will be considered an inappropriate synthesis method, given that it weights large and small primary studies equally and ignores risk of bias [41]. Narrative syntheses that follow the SWiM (Synthesis Without Meta-analysis) reporting guideline [42] and account for effect direction, magnitude, and confidence will be considered adequate. Second, for Item 15 (assessment of publication bias), when the systematic review includes fewer than 10 primary studies in any meta-analysis, funnel-plot-based assessments of publication bias will be considered uninformative; in such cases the item will be rated as “no meta-analysis was performed/not applicable”, in line with Cochrane guidance [43], to avoid penalising systematic reviews that correctly refrained from misleading small-study analyses.

We will not exclude systematic reviews based on their methodological quality. Based on the number and nature of the critical and non-critical weaknesses identified, the overall confidence in the results of each systematic review will be rated as high, moderate, low, or critically low, in accordance with AMSTAR 2 guidance. Discrepancies between the two reviewers will be resolved by discussion or, if consensus cannot be reached, by a third reviewer (JJB). Results will be presented in tabular format showing domain-level ratings and overall confidence ratings for each included systematic review.

Assessment of overlap of primary studies

To avoid double counting of evidence, the overlap of primary studies included across multiple systematic reviews will be quantified using the Corrected Covered Area (CCA), calculated as CCA = (N − r)/(r × c − r), where N is the total number of publications included across all systematic reviews (including duplicates), r is the number of systematic reviews, and c is the number of unique primary studies [44]. CCA values will be interpreted using the established thresholds (slight overlap, 0–5%; moderate, 6–10%; high, 11–15%; very high, > 15%), and a citation matrix indicating which primary studies are included in each systematic review will be presented as a supplementary table.

Three considerations raised at peer review will be incorporated when assessing overlap. First, when the same primary study is included across systematic reviews, we will inspect whether the same arm(s) of the primary study are being used to answer the same comparison; multi-arm trials in which different systematic reviews extract data from non-overlapping arms (e.g. “methadone vs. buprenorphine in one review and buprenorphine vs. placebo in another”) will not be treated as true overlap for the purpose of evidence selection. Second, where systematic reviews answer slightly different PICO questions, the systematic review whose included primary studies most closely match the relevant PICO will be prioritised over the systematic review with the largest number of included primary studies, recognising that a larger primary-study count does not necessarily translate into a more accurate answer. Third, in situations of high or very high overlap that cannot be resolved through PICO match, priority will be given to more recent, methodologically rigorous systematic reviews with higher AMSTAR 2 ratings; overlapped primary studies will be flagged explicitly to ensure transparent interpretation of the evidence base [45].

Data synthesis

We will present a structured summary of the results of the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses. For each systematic review, the summary will include (i) substance type (alcohol, tobacco/nicotine, opioids, cannabis, stimulants, other substances, polysubstance use); (ii) intervention category (pharmacological, psychosocial and behavioural, integrated care models, community programmes); and (iii) offspring outcome domain (neonatal, congenital anomalies, neurodevelopment, behavioural and mental health). For each combination, we will report the number of primary studies, number of participants, outcome measures, summary effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals, measures of heterogeneity (τ2, I2) and the original authors’ conclusions.

In line with current guidance for umbrella reviews [33, 35], we will not undertake new meta-analyses pooling primary-study data. Instead, we will summarise the pooled estimates already reported by the included systematic reviews. Where multiple systematic reviews report meta-analyses of the same intervention-outcome pair, we will tabulate the estimates side by side and discuss reasons for any discrepancies (e.g. differences in eligibility criteria, primary studies included, statistical models). Evidence summary tables will be used to present syntheses of the overall evidence for each type of intervention and outcome.

Sensitivity considerations will be qualitative and based on review-level features (recency, AMSTAR 2 rating, comprehensiveness of the search, risk of bias of the underlying primary studies). We will avoid vote counting and any synthesis approach that weights primary studies of unequal size or risk of bias equally.

Assessment of the certainty of evidence

We will extract and report the GRADE certainty ratings already provided by the included systematic reviews [46]. For outcomes for which no systematic review has applied GRADE, or for which assessments are inconsistent across systematic reviews, we will perform a structured umbrella-level appraisal informed by GRADE. Because the unit of evidence is the systematic review (not the primary study), and because we will not re-extract primary-study data, this appraisal is presented as a modified rather than a fully de novo GRADE: we will judge risk of bias (using the original systematic reviews’ appraisal of their primary studies), inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias on the evidence as reported. Importantly, we will explicitly consider GRADE’s upgrading domains—large magnitude of effect, dose-response gradient, and the influence of plausible residual confounding—because in this vulnerable population (pregnant women, pregnant individuals, and their offspring) these signals are particularly informative for clinical decision-making and should not be omitted. The certainty of evidence will be classified as high, moderate, low, or very low.

When systematic reviews examining similar interventions and outcomes report conflicting findings, we will compare the characteristics of their included primary studies, examine differences in methodological quality (AMSTAR 2), differences in eligibility criteria, outcome definitions, and analytic approaches, describe the reasons for the discrepancies transparently, and indicate which body of evidence is most reliable based on its recency, methodological quality, and comprehensiveness.

Missing data

Because the unit of analysis is the systematic review, the relevant “missing data” are review-level items (e.g. missing pooled estimates, confidence intervals, heterogeneity statistics, GRADE judgements, or risk of bias appraisals of primary studies). When such information is not reported in an included systematic review, we will (i) consult any published protocol and supplementary material of that review; (ii) contact the corresponding author of the systematic review by email, with one reminder after 2 weeks; and (iii) record the unanswered items as a limitation. We will not impute missing data, recompute pooled estimates, nor extract data from individual primary studies to fill review-level gaps. Items that remain unresolved will be reported transparently in evidence summary tables.

Ethics approval

Ethics approval is not required for this umbrella review, as it synthesises previously published research and does not involve collection of primary data from human participants.

Discussion

The findings of this umbrella review will have significant implications for research, clinical practice, and public policy by identifying areas where there is robust evidence that can confidently support clinical guidelines, recognising research gaps where evidence is absent or of very low certainty, identifying methodological limitations of existing systematic reviews that should be addressed in future research, and highlighting offspring outcomes that have been understudied despite their clinical relevance. This synthesis will inform the development of clinical guidelines for the treatment of substance use in pregnancy, facilitate risk-benefit counselling for pregnant women and pregnant individuals considering treatment options, guide the allocation of resources towards interventions with the strongest evidence of benefit to offspring, contribute to the design of service delivery models that optimise outcomes for both mothers and offspring, and establish funding priorities for research aimed at addressing the critical evidence gaps identified.
