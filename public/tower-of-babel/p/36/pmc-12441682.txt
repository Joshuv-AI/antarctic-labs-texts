Seasonal shifts in pan-Arctic Ocean downward radiation and key drivers
Authors: Yujun Liu, Haiyan Li, Yijun He, Yingying Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe Arctic, highly sensitive to climate change, is warming rapidly due to increasing greenhouse gas emissions, resulting in a significant rise in downward radiation over the pan-Arctic Ocean in recent decades. However, the key drivers and their relative contributions remain unclear. This study identifies and quantifies the seasonal impacts of four critical environmental factors—temperature, sea ice concentration (SIC), specific humidity, and latent heat flux—on downward radiation. Results show that temperature dominates during the cold season (October–March), contributing 39.33%, while in the warm season (April–September), temperature and SIC are primary contributors, at 42.18% and −30.57%, respectively. Notably, latent heat flux has a contrasting seasonal effect: it enhances radiation in the cold season but suppresses it in the warm season. These findings reveal the mechanisms underlying Arctic radiative changes and offer valuable insights into the region’s climate response under global warming.Subject areas: Earth sciences, Atmospheric science, Climatology

Introduction

The Arctic is one of the most climate-sensitive regions globally, with its temperature rising significantly faster than the global average due to the surge in carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions released by human activities.1,2 According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change Sixth Assessment Report, the global average temperature has been increasing at a rate of 0.12°C per decade since the mid-20th century.3 However, the near-surface air temperature in the Arctic has been rising at a rate approximately four times that of the global mean, a phenomenon known as Arctic amplification.4 Although this amplification occurs throughout all seasons, it is particularly pronounced in autumn and winter.1,5,6 Among the profound environmental changes occurring in the Arctic, downward radiation plays a particularly critical role. Clear-sky downward longwave radiation (DLR) accounts for approximately 82% of the surface warming during the cold season.7 Moreover, longwave downward radiation in the Arctic has been increasing at a rate of 4–5 W m−2 per decade, further intensifying the rise in surface temperatures.8

It is well known that environmental factors such as sea ice, cloud cover, humidity, and temperature all contribute to changes in pan-Arctic downward radiation.9,10 The downward radiation is the sum of downward shortwave radiation (DSR) and DLR. The reduction of sea ice has led to the exposure of more open water, which directly affects the energy balance of the ocean surface and, in turn, influences heat exchange at the air-sea interface.11,12,13 Such changes have significant implications for both regional and global climates.14,15,16 According to satellite observations, the extent and thickness of Arctic sea ice have significantly declined. Since 1979, the sea ice extent in September has sharply decreased by 39.7%. Moreover, since 2007, the Arctic sea ice has transitioned from a thicker and more deformed ice cover to a thinner and more uniform one.17,18 The reduction in sea ice not only alters surface albedo but also increases the exchange of heat and moisture between the ocean and atmosphere, further accelerating sea ice melt and affecting the radiation balance in the Arctic region.19,20 Specifically, as sea ice melts, ocean evaporation and atmospheric humidity increase, causing water vapor to absorb more longwave radiation from the surface. This, in turn, increases DLR and raises surface temperatures.21,22 These effects are not only significant in summer and early autumn but also exhibit a noticeable trend in winter.23,24,25 Additionally, the rise in atmospheric temperatures in the Arctic enhances DLR, reinforcing a positive feedback loop.26,27 DSR is primarily influenced by cloud cover, as clouds reflect and absorb solar shortwave radiation, thereby affecting the amount of radiation reaching the surface.28,29 When cloud cover in the Arctic Ocean increases, it reduces the shortwave radiation reaching the surface, which leads to more heat being retained in the atmosphere, further exacerbating the rise in temperatures.30,31,32 The seasonal variation in downward radiation in the Arctic region is highly pronounced, particularly the radiative differences between the cold and warm seasons.33,34,35,36 During the cold season, the pan-Arctic sea ice concentration (SIC) is higher, resulting in a higher albedo and less radiation absorption. In contrast, during the warm season, due to sea ice melt and rising temperatures, radiation absorption significantly increases, leading to the accumulation of energy at the ocean surface, which further exacerbates the feedback effects of climate change. These seasonal variations significantly influence the Arctic’s radiation patterns and climate responses.37,38 Additionally, changes in radiation in the Arctic not only affect the local climate but may also induce feedback effects on the global climate system.39,40 As radiation intensity fluctuates, the temperature and atmospheric circulation patterns in the Arctic will also undergo corresponding changes,41,42,43,44 which may influence the climate in mid-latitude regions and trigger extreme weather events.45,46,47,48,49,50

Previous studies on Arctic radiation changes have explained the contribution of downward radiation to Arctic warming and the underlying mechanisms.4,20,27,29 However, the key environmental factors driving changes in downward radiation in the pan-Arctic Ocean, as well as their seasonal variations and relative contributions, remain unclear. Understanding these factors is crucial for comprehending the dynamics of downward radiation changes in the region. To address this gap, we utilize a combination of advanced analytical methods to identify and quantify the key environmental factors influencing pan-Arctic downward radiation changes. By analyzing contribution variations during the cold season (October–March) and warm season (April–September), we aim to uncover the dynamic patterns of these factors. This research advances our understanding of the spatiotemporal characteristics and driving forces of pan-Arctic Ocean radiation under climate change, offering a robust foundation for forecasting future Arctic climate changes and their global implications.

Results

Downward radiation changes under CO2 surge

The Arctic climate system is highly sensitive to greenhouse gas changes. Recent increases in atmospheric CO2, driven by human activities,1,51 have intensified the greenhouse effect and Arctic amplification,52,53 leading to a significant rise in CO2 concentration at a rate of 18.74 ppm per decade (Figure 1A). As greenhouse gas concentrations rise, the atmosphere’s ability to absorb and re-radiate energy strengthens, trapping more heat and enhancing downward radiation in the Arctic. Warming is most prominent near the surface, with a decrease at higher altitudes.54 As a result, the downward radiation reaching the surface is primarily emitted from the lower atmosphere, with an average annual increase of 2.33 W m−2 per decade (Figure 1B). The spatial distribution of the downward radiation increase across the pan-Arctic is uneven, with higher growth rates observed in the eastern Barents Sea, reaching up to 4.35 W m−2 decade−1 (Figure 1C). Additionally, the temporal distribution of radiation increase is not uniform. The greatest growth occurred between 1992 and 2010, while recent years have seen a slowdown in the growth rate (Figure 1D).

The uneven spatial and temporal trends in downward radiation are more pronounced during both the cold and warm seasons. Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of average downward radiation across the pan-Arctic Ocean from 1980 to 2023, extending from the Arctic pole to the outer regions, with lower-latitude ocean areas receiving higher surface downward radiation. During the cold season, downward radiation in the pan-Arctic Ocean is lower, with the average radiation north of 80°N in the polar circle reaching a minimum of 177.85 W m−2. During the warm season, downward radiation across the entire Arctic Ocean increases significantly, with an average increase of 225.55 W m−2 compared to the cold season (Figures 2A and 2B). In recent years, the downward radiation across the pan-Arctic Ocean has shown an overall increasing trend. During the cold season, the Barents Sea region exhibits the greatest increase in downward radiation, reaching up to 10.37 W m−2 decade−1, with the highest growth occurring from 1994 to 2010. Although the warm season also shows an overall increasing trend, a notable negative growth trend is observed in the Barents Sea region, at −3.08 W m−2 decade−1. Since 2008, both cold and warm season trend matrices show a slowing of the downward radiation growth trend in the pan-Arctic Ocean, with some regions even showing negative values (Figures 2C and 2F).

The responses of atmospheric and oceanic to radiation variations

In recent years, the rapid changes in downward radiation over the pan-Arctic Ocean have triggered a series of alterations in key environmental factors. The spatial correlations between downward radiation and variables—including temperature, latent heat flux (negative [positive] values indicate heat released [absorbed] by the ocean to [from] the atmosphere), specific humidity, and SIC—exhibit notable spatial heterogeneity. Temperature and specific humidity show overall positive correlations with downward radiation, with coefficients of 0.81 and 0.78, respectively. In contrast, SIC shows a weak negative correlation of −0.11, with particularly low correlations observed in the Atlantic inflow region of the Arctic Ocean (Figures 3A, 3C, and 3D). Latent heat flux displays both positive and negative spatial correlations (Figure 3B). Specifically, strong positive correlations (0.79) are found in the Atlantic and Pacific sectors of the Arctic Ocean, whereas the central Arctic Ocean exhibits strong negative correlations (−0.81).

The downward radiation in the Arctic exhibits a pronounced seasonal contrast, with significant differences in climate state distribution and trends of key environmental factors between the cold and warm seasons. Overall, during the cold season, temperatures and specific humidity in the pan-Arctic Ocean are relatively low, while the ocean releases more heat to the atmosphere, and SIC remains higher (Figure 4). The distribution is also uneven. Specifically, in the cold season, the central Arctic Ocean experiences lower temperatures, averaging around 256.54 K, and lower specific humidity, averaging about 1.06 g kg−1 (Figures 4A and 4I). In contrast, the Atlantic opening of the pan-Arctic Ocean exhibits higher atmospheric temperatures, reaching up to 275.73 K. Meanwhile, the ocean releases more heat to the atmosphere, with latent heat flux reaching a minimum of −9.8 × 106 J m−2, and specific humidity peaking at 4.17 g kg−1 (Figures 4A and 4I). During the warm season, air temperature over the pan-Arctic Ocean increases significantly, averaging 268.98 K, accompanied by a rise in specific humidity to an average of approximately 3.1 g kg−1. In contrast, both the ocean-to-atmosphere heat transfer and SIC decrease, with SIC in the cold season dropping from an average of 0.79 to 0.72 (Figures 4F and 4N).

Overall, the trend changes of the related environmental factors during the cold season are significantly greater than those in the warm season. Atmospheric temperature increases at a rate of 0.44 K decade−1 during the warm season, compared to 0.97 K decade−1 in the cold season. The variation in latent heat flux is also more pronounced in the cold season, with a rate of −5.57 × 104 J m−2, compared to only −0.99 × 104 J m−2 decade−1 in the warm season. SIC is the only factor exhibiting a pronounced declining trend in the warm season, decreasing at a rate of 0.025 per decade, while in the cold season the rate is 0.017 per decade. Specific humidity displays two distinct spatial patterns: it tends to decrease over the central Arctic Ocean, but increases markedly along the sea ice margins, especially near the Barents Sea, where the growth rate reaches up to 0.15 g kg−1 decade−1 during the cold season. Notably, the most pronounced seasonal changes in key environmental factors—whether in the cold or warm season—occur in the Arctic Atlantic inflow zone and along the marginal sea ice zones. Even in the warm season, where the overall trends are weaker, high-magnitude changes remain concentrated in these two critical regions. This spatial pattern closely corresponds to the recent distribution of downward radiation trends across the pan-Arctic region (Figures 2C and 2D).

Attribution of radiation changes based on information flow

To investigate the influence of the different environmental factors on downward radiation over the pan-Arctic Ocean, this study conducted causality analysis by calculating the information flow between pan-Arctic Ocean downward radiation (X1) and key environmental factors (X2) from 1980 to 2023. The results show that all environmental factors exhibit regions where T2→1 is significantly less than 0 in the Arctic Atlantic inflow zone (Figure 5). These negative-value regions strongly correspond to areas with the slowest growth trends in downward radiation (Figure 1C). Notably, the stabilizing effect of atmospheric temperature on downward radiation in this region is significant, with the lowest value reaching −0.31 (Figure 5A). This phenomenon may be attributed to the ocean absorbing heat as temperatures rise, which is then released as longwave radiation, while an increase in specific humidity reduces incoming shortwave radiation, thus slowing the growth rate of downward radiation.

Conversely, regions with positive information flow between environmental factors and downward radiation are primarily concentrated in the Barents Sea and near the marginal sea ice zone. These regions coincide with areas of significant trends in downward radiation and related environmental factors, indicating that pronounced atmospheric and oceanic changes can exert unstable impacts on downward radiation (Figures 1C and 4). Near the Barents Sea, information flows T2→1 for temperature, latent heat flux, and specific humidity all exhibit distinctly positive regions (Figures 5A–5C), highlighting the strong driving effects of these factors on downward radiation changes. However, the contribution of SIC to annual downward radiation appears to be insignificant (Figure 5D), likely due to the near absence of sea ice in the Arctic Atlantic inflow zone of the Arctic Ocean, which statistically offsets its influence. To further assess the importance of SIC and other environmental factors, we employed the CNN-STLSTM-CNN deep learning model for additional analysis and verification.

Discussion

Evaluation of model prediction accuracy

Based on the CNN-STLSTM-CNN neural network model, environmental factors from 1980 to 2023 were used to predict downward radiation values in the pan-Arctic Ocean. After de-normalizing the predictions, Figure 6A displays the prediction errors for the CNN-STLSTM-CNN model on the test set. The model’s predicted radiation values exhibited a high correlation of 0.99 with the true values. The optimal root-mean-square error (RMSE) for both the training and test sets was 0.04. Compared to observations, the model exhibits a slight tendency to underestimate radiation values (Figures 6B–6G). In the central Arctic Ocean, which is perennially covered by sea ice, the underestimation reaches up to 10.84 W m−2 during the cold season and 9.49 W m−2 in the warm season. In the Arctic Atlantic inflow zone, the model slightly overestimates radiation by approximately 5.84 W m−2 in the cold season, while a more pronounced underestimation of around 12.26 W m−2 occurs in the warm season. In the Barents Sea, an overestimation of up to 13.72 W m−2 is observed during the warm season. Overall, the CNN-STLSTM-CNN model effectively captures the spatial distribution patterns of downward radiation across the pan-Arctic Ocean.

Seasonal contributions of environmental factors

After completing model training, a sensitivity analysis was performed to quantify the impact of environmental factors on downward radiation changes. A 10% fluctuation was applied to temperature, latent heat flux, specific humidity, and SIC to assess their respective influences across the pan-Arctic Ocean. The weight matrices after perturbation were extracted, and the percentage contribution of each factor to downward radiation was calculated accordingly (Table 1). On an annual scale, temperature and specific humidity contribute positively to changes in downward radiation, while latent heat flux and SIC exert negative contributions. In terms of absolute contribution weights, temperature ranks highest (52.83%), followed by SIC (33.53%), specific humidity (8.61%), and latent heat flux (5.03%). These results suggest that increases in temperature and specific humidity are the primary drivers of enhanced downward radiation over the pan-Arctic Ocean, whereas increases in SIC and latent heat flux likely exert a suppressing effect on radiation growth. Furthermore, to quantify the seasonal contributions of environmental factors, the model was used to apply a 10% increase to each factor individually during the cold and warm seasons, allowing for a comparison of their impacts on downward radiation over the pan-Arctic between seasons. The results (Table 1) reveal pronounced seasonal differences in the roles of these factors in Arctic climate variability. Increases in temperature and specific humidity consistently enhanced downward radiation in both the cold and warm seasons, whereas increases in SIC suppressed its growth across seasons. Notably, the contribution of latent heat flux displayed a marked seasonal reversal, shifting from a substantial negative contribution in the cold season (−34.23%) to a positive contribution in the warm season (20.24%). This suggests that increased ocean-to-atmosphere heat release enhances downward radiation during the cold season but acts to suppress its growth during the warm season.

This seasonal shift is closely associated with the environmental changes occurring during the warm season over the pan-Arctic Ocean, including increased air temperature, latent heat flux, and specific humidity, as well as a significant reduction in SIC. During the cold season, high SIC enhances surface albedo, reflecting solar radiation and thereby suppressing downward radiation. Consequently, the increase in downward radiation is primarily influenced by DLR. When latent heat flux increases (i.e., becomes more negative, indicating greater ocean-to-atmosphere moisture release), atmospheric specific humidity tends to rise, which strengthens DLR. Furthermore, the release of latent heat during water vapor condensation warms the lower atmosphere, further amplifying the increase in DLR (Figure 7).55 A possible reason is that the temperature difference between the atmosphere and the ocean in the cold season, along with stronger upper-ocean vertical mixing, promotes the release of seasonal heat stored in the subsurface Arctic Ocean.56 In the warm season, the absolute contributions of temperature, specific humidity, and SIC to downward radiation increase by 2.85%, 0.76%, and 10.38%, respectively, compared to the cold season, indicating their more prominent roles. Although the contribution of latent heat flux decreases in the warm season, the seasonal reversal of its effect—from a positive driver in the cold season to a suppressing factor in the warm season—highlights the influence of radiation-induced sea ice melt and near-saturation humidity conditions. Higher temperatures and reduced SIC during the warm season enhance ocean-to-atmosphere moisture transport, which promotes increased cloud cover.57,58 This, in turn, strengthens solar radiation reflection and weakens the upward trend in downward radiation (Figures 7 and 2D).

Implications of the contributions of DLR and DSR to downward radiation

It is evident that changes in downward radiation are jointly influenced by both DLR and DSR. Overall, DLR plays a dominant role in shaping the intensity and spatial distribution of downward radiation, although the influence of DSR becomes more pronounced during the warm season (Figure 8). For example, in the cold season, the spatial pattern of downward radiation derived by considering only DLR (excluding DSR trends) closely resembles the actual cold-season distribution (Figures 8A and 2A). Similarly, in the warm season, the DLR-only scenario also aligns well with the observed spatial pattern of downward radiation (Figures 8C and 2B). However, when only DSR is considered (excluding DLR trends), the resulting spatial pattern clearly highlights areas of reduced downward radiation in the warm season—particularly the Arctic Atlantic inflow zone and the Barents Sea (Figure 2, Figure 8D, 2B, and 2D), suggesting that shortwave radiation suppresses the overall increase in downward radiation in these regions. The annual contributions of DLR and DSR further reinforce this conclusion (Figures 8C and 8D). In the central Arctic Ocean, where sea ice coverage remains extensive, the contributions of both DLR and DSR to downward radiation are relatively weak. In contrast, in the Atlantic inflow region, DLR contributes significantly to the increase in downward radiation. In the Barents Sea and adjacent areas, where specific humidity, latent heat flux, and cloud cover strongly interact, the contribution of DSR to the increase in downward radiation remains limited.

These results indicate that the Barents Sea is the most sensitive region for air-sea interactions in the Arctic Ocean.59,60 During the cold season, the influx of heat from the Atlantic accelerates sea ice melt, and increases in temperature and atmospheric moisture drive a significant rise in DLR, making this region a hotspot for downward radiation enhancement (Figure 2C). In the warm season, both temperature and humidity in the Barents Sea are significantly higher than in the cold season, and most of the sea ice has melted. Under such high-humidity conditions, increased cloud cover enhances the reflection of shortwave radiation, resulting in a weakening trend in downward radiation in this region during the warm season.

Limitations of the study

This study systematically quantifies the seasonal contributions of key environmental factors (temperature, latent heat flux, specific humidity, and SIC) to downward radiation over the pan-Arctic Ocean. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all datasets were obtained from reanalysis and satellite remote sensing products. Although ERA5 and similar datasets provide relatively high spatial and temporal resolution, the scarcity of observations in high-latitude, ice-covered regions and uncertainties in data assimilation may introduce biases in both the magnitude and trends of radiation fluxes and associated environmental variables. Second, while the CNN-STLSTM-CNN deep learning model exhibited generally strong predictive performance, regional biases remain, potentially due to limitations in the input feature set and the model’s generalization capability. Finally, this study did not explicitly quantify the spatial heterogeneity of environmental factor contributions across different subregions of the pan-Arctic Ocean. Future research should integrate multi-source observational datasets with high-resolution models to improve predictive accuracy, assess the contributions of key factors in specific subregions, and differentiate their respective roles in modulating DLR and DSR, thereby advancing the understanding and prediction of Arctic radiation change mechanisms.

Method details

Data selection

The reanalysis and remote sensing data products used in this study span from 1980 to 2023 and include the following: Atmospheric CO2 concentrations in dry air (xCO2) provided by the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS), with a spatial resolution of 0.7 ° × 1.4 ° and a temporal resolution of 3 h hours (https://ads.atmosphere.copernicus.eu/); Monthly surface solar radiation downwards, surface thermal radiation downwards, specific humidity, temperature (950 hPa), and surface latent heat flux from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) ERA5 Reanalysis, with a spatial resolution of 0.25 ° × 0.25 ° (https://www.ecmwf.int/en/forecasts/dataset/ecmwf-reanalysis-v5). Daily sea ice concentration (SIC) data provided by the National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC), with a spatial resolution of 25 km × 25 km, derived from passive microwave data (https://nsidc.org/data/g02202/versions/4).

Feature selection

Feature selection was initially performed using Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) combined with 5-fold cross-validation,61,62 ensuring robust validation of the model’s generalization capability throughout the process. The 5-fold cross-validation divided the dataset into training and testing sets, and each iteration calculated the Mean Squared Error (MSE). The importance of each feature was assessed, and the least important features were removed. This process was repeated until all features were evaluated. Ultimately, four environmental factors most strongly associated with the changes in downward radiation over the Pan-Arctic Ocean were selected: temperature, latent heat flux (negative (positive) values indicate heat released (absorbed) by the ocean to (from) the atmosphere), specific humidity, and SIC.

Liang-Kleeman Information Flow

To assess the causal relationships between the primary environmental factors and the downward radiation over the Pan-Arctic Ocean, the Liang-Kleeman Information Flow method was used.63,64 This method is particularly well-suited for evaluating complex nonlinear systems in climate change.65,66,67 In this method, causality is diagnosed through the time ratio of information flow from one sequence to another (Equation 1). For two time series, X1 and X2, the maximum likelihood estimate of the information flow rate from X2 to X1 is denoted as T2→1. If T2→1 equals zero, X2 does not cause X1; if T2→1 is non-zero, it indicates a causal relationship between X1 and X2. A nonzero T2→1 can be either positive or negative. A positive T2→1 means that X2 increases uncertainty in X1, whereas a negative T2→1 indicates that X2 stabilizes X1.

Here, Cij is the sample covariance between Xi and Xj, and Ci,dj is the sample covariance between Xi and the series derived from Xj. Specifically, "the series derived from Xj " refers to the time derivative of Xj (i.e., d Xj/dt).

CNN-STLSTM-CNN model

To better address the challenges posed by climate change, deep learning methods have been introduced into Arctic climate prediction and analysis.68,69,70,71 In recent years, neural network models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and Spatiotemporal Long Short-Term Memory Networks (STLSTM) have been widely applied in climate science, effectively capturing complex spatiotemporal patterns and the nonlinear relationships between environmental factors.72,73,74 Therefore, we designed a hybrid deep learning model, CNN-STLSTM-CNN, by combining CNN and STLSTM to predict Arctic Ocean radiation.

The model is configured with a time step of 3, four feature channels (temperature, latent heat, specific humidity, and SIC), and a convolution kernel size of 5×5. The architecture begins with a 2D convolutional layer containing 32 kernels to extract spatial features. This is followed by two stacked STLSTM units, each incorporating convolutional and dropout layers (Dropout = 0.3). Through these convolutional layers, the 128 feature channels are processed further to capture both spatial and temporal dependencies in the data. The output from the STLSTM layers is then fed into another convolutional layer for additional feature extraction, ultimately producing the predicted radiation values. This design allows the model to effectively leverage spatial and temporal patterns in multi-dimensional climate data, improving the accuracy of Arctic Ocean radiation predictions and capturing the weight changes of key influencing factors.

The dataset was randomly split into training and testing sets at an 80%:20% ratio to enhance the model’s generalization ability. The learning rate was set to 0.001, with a batch size of 15, and the model was trained for 400 epochs. To prevent overfitting, an early stopping mechanism was implemented with a patience value of 10 epochs, meaning training stopped early if the validation error did not improve for more than 10 consecutive epochs. The model utilized Mean Squared Error (MSE) as the loss function, and the trends of both training and validation loss were recorded throughout the training process.

Temporal decomposition diagnostic method

To diagnose the respective contributions of downward shortwave radiation (DSR) and downward longwave radiation (DLR) trends to the overall trend of downward radiation over the Pan-Arctic Ocean, this study employed a temporal decomposition diagnostic method.75,76 The diagnostic metric F represents the field over the region of interest and is used to compare the relative impacts of DLR and DSR trends under different combinations. Specifically, F(DLR, DSRd) denotes the field resulting from trend-preserved DLR and detrended downward shortwave radiation (DSRd), while F(DLRd, DSR) denotes the field generated by detrended DLR (DLRd) and trend-preserved DSR. The combination F(DLRd, DSRd) serves as the baseline field, with both DLR and DSR detrended. The difference between F(DLR, DSRd) and F(DLRd, DSRd) is interpreted as the portion of the field driven solely by the DLR trend, and other combinations can be interpreted in a similar manner.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using MATLAB and Python for statistical computations and deep learning modeling. For Figure 3, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between downward radiation and environmental factors (atmospheric temperature at 950 hPa, surface latent heat flux, specific humidity, and SIC) during 1980–2023, with statistical significance assessed using a two-tailed t-test at the 95% confidence level. Figure 5 employed the Liang–Kleeman Information Flow method to quantify causal relationships between environmental factors and downward radiation, with significance also evaluated at the 95% level via permutation-based statistical testing. The prediction accuracy of the CNN-STLSTM-CNN model (Figure 6A) was assessed on the 2018–2023 test set using root mean square error (RMSE) and Pearson correlation coefficient, achieving a high correlation (r = 0.99) and low RMSE (0.04) for both training and test sets.
