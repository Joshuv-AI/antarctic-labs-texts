Silicosis in Teflon Coating Workers: An Emerging Occupational Hazard—A Case Series
Authors: Indranath Ghosh, Dipti Gothi, Umesh C. Ojha, Priyank Goyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Silicosis is a well-recognized occupational lung disease commonly associated with high-risk industries, such as mining and quarrying. However, cases continue to emerge from nontraditional sectors. We report a case series of two patients employed in the Teflon coating industry who developed silicosis while working in this industry. Case 1 presented with exertional breathlessness and recurrent hemoptysis for one yr with crackles and clubbing. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) thorax showed right upper lobe aspergilloma. Case 2 presented with a dry cough and hemoptysis for six months with inspiratory crackles and clubbing. HRCT thorax showed bilateral fibrobronchiectasis in upper lobes. These cases underscore the need to reevaluate occupational health policies, implement stringent workplace evaluations, and expand compensation eligibility for workers in these industries.

INTRODUCTION

Silicosis is a progressive fibrotic lung disease caused by prolonged inhalation of respirable crystalline silica dust. It has traditionally been linked to occupations, such as mining, sandblasting, and quarrying.[12] The pathogenesis involves chronic inflammation and fibrosis triggered by silica deposition in the alveoli.[3] Addressing high-risk industries, less attention has been paid to unconventional sources of exposure.

Teflon coating is not typically considered high-risk for silicosis. However, surface preparation techniques such as sandblasting using silica-containing materials or surface preparation with such materials can generate hazardous levels of respirable silica dust. Silica in Teflon is a composite material where silica nanoparticles are added to polytetrafluorethylene. Inadequate protective measures and lack of awareness may place these workers at significant risk, as these manufacturing units may produce hazardous levels of respirable silica dust.[4]

We present two cases of chronic silica-induced lung disease in patients working in Teflon coating factories to highlight a novel source of occupational exposure for silicosis.

CASE SERIES

Case 1

A 38-year-old gentleman, employed as a spray painter for the past 15 years in the Teflon coating division of a home appliance manufacturing unit, presented with exertional breathlessness and recurrent hemoptysis of one year’s duration. His job involved spray painting Teflon-coated roti makers and irons [Figure 1]. He intermittently used an N95 mask; no other personal protective equipment or safety education was provided. His medical history was notable for treated pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) 10 years ago. Another person, also employed at the same factory, had similar respiratory complaints.

Clinical examination revealed bilateral crackles and clubbing. Chest radiograph showed a fibrocavitary lesion in the right upper zone and diffuse nodular opacities in both lungs [Figure 2]. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) revealed bilateral tiny nodules, mosaic attenuation, and a fibrocavitary lesion in the right upper lobe with aspergilloma [Figures 3 and 4].

A pulmonary function test (PFT) showed a restrictive ventilatory defect. FEV1/FVC (forced expiratory volume in one second/forced vital capacity: 82.88; FEV1: 1.29 L [40.55%]; FVC: 2.19 L [54%] with reduced DLCO (diffusion capacity of carbon monoxide): 60.9% and elevated RV/TLC (residual volume/total lung capacity) ratio: 49.9%. Bronchoscopy did not show any significant abnormality. The bronchial aspirate was subjected to KOH staining. It showed Aspergillus species in the bronchial aspirate. A diagnosis of silicosis with post-tubercular fibrocavitary disease and chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) was made.

The patient was requested to attend the special medical board (occupational disease) following the procedures adapted by the Employees’ State Insurance Corporation (ESIC).

Case 2

A 43-year-old gentleman, brother of the above-mentioned gentleman, with 15 years of occupational exposure to Teflon spray in the same factory, presented with a six-year history of persistent dry cough and multiple hemoptysis episodes. He was a lifelong nonsmoker and nondiabetic. He had a history of treated sputum-positive PTB 13 years ago. CPA was diagnosed and treated three years ago. He has used an N95 mask only since the COVID-19 pandemic. No other protective equipment or workplace safety training was provided.

Serial chest radiographs showed progressive bilateral nodular opacities. HRCT demonstrated diffuse tiny nodules with fibrobronchiectatic changes, predominantly in the upper lobes [Figures 5 and 6]. Serial PFTs revealed a severe restrictive defect with FEV1/FVC (forced expiratory volume in one second/forced vital capacity): 87.09; FEV1: 1.91 L [56%]; and FVC: 2.19 L [54%], along with reduced DLCO of 68%. RV/TLC was markedly elevated [88%], suggesting air trapping. Clinical findings: bilateral basal inspiratory crackles present with clubbing.

Transbronchial lung biopsy showed aggregates of histiocytes with foreign-body-type giant cells, suggestive of a chronic inflammatory reaction consistent with inhalational injury. He was started on antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib) but continued to deteriorate and is now bedridden due to advanced respiratory failure under domiciliary rehabilitation instructions. He was also requested to attend the special medical board (occupational disease) of ESIC.

DISCUSSION

Silicosis remains a significant occupational disease, often underdiagnosed in workers exposed in nontraditional settings.[5] The two patients presented in this series worked in Teflon coating units, a sector not generally associated with silica exposure. However, precoating processes, involving sandblasting and surface roughening, can generate respirable crystalline silica.[67]

These cases underscore an important, yet under-recognized, occupational health hazard: The development of silicosis in workers engaged in Teflon coating processes, a field not traditionally classified as high-risk for silica exposure. Although Teflon itself is inert and not a direct source of crystalline silica, certain preparatory procedures integral to the coating process—such as surface treatment using silica-containing abrasives—can generate respirable silica dust. Inadequate respiratory protection, limited industrial hygiene measures, and poor awareness of the risks compound the likelihood of harmful exposure.

Both patients in this series exhibited radiological and functional patterns consistent with silicosis, including diffuse bilateral nodular opacities, reduced diffusion capacity (DLCO), and progressive lung function decline. One patient also had histopathological findings supporting inhalational lung injury. Coexistent post-tubercular sequelae and a history of CPA in both individuals likely complicated their clinical course but do not preclude the diagnosis of silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis, particularly in the context of clear occupational exposure. Post-TB sequelae and coexisting aspergillosis significantly complicate the clinical and radiological diagnosis of silicosis in Teflon workers. They mimic silicosis radiologically, worsen lung damage, and obscure the true cause of symptoms—making careful, multidimensional diagnostic evaluation essential.

A key concern raised by this series is the absence of awareness regarding the health risks and preventive measures among the workers, regulatory enforcement, and compensation. The roles of employee, employer, and healthcare service provider need to be revisited, and simplification of the procedure will circumvent the delays.

These cases highlight the urgent need for regulatory bodies to reassess industry-specific safety standards, extend silica exposure monitoring to nontraditional sectors, and ensure equitable access to occupational compensation and rehabilitation for affected workers.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this study highlight the critical need for integrated medical surveillance and comprehensive workplace monitoring in environments with significant silica exposure, such as Teflon manufacturing units. Post-TB sequelae and coexisting aspergillosis emerged as major confounding factors that obscure the clinical and radiological diagnosis of silicosis, often leading to delayed detection, misclassification, and inappropriate management. These cases emphasize an emerging occupational hazard: silicosis in Teflon coating workers due to the use of abrasive material like silica leading to pneumoconiosis. Workplace monitoring must prioritize continuous silica exposure assessment, enforcement of permissible exposure limits, engineering controls, and worker training to effectively reduce inhalational hazards.

Overall, this study reinforces that only a dual approach—combining enhanced medical surveillance with rigorous workplace exposure control—can ensure early detection, correct diagnosis, and prevention of silicosis in high-risk worker populations. Such an integrated strategy not only protects worker health but also reduces the public health burden of preventable occupational lung diseases in silica-exposed industries.

There is an urgent need to reassess occupational health frameworks, utilization of nonfibrogenic material, process, and reinforcing protective measures, and broaden silica surveillance to include apparently nontraditional industries. Regulatory oversight and equitable compensation for affected workers are essential to prevent further morbidity in this vulnerable population.
