Prolonged elevated heart rate and 28-day in-hospital mortality in patients with asthma-related critical illness: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Le Tang, Jiaqi Niu, Simeng Zhu, Xiaoju Liu, Hairong Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProlonged elevated heart rate (PeHR) has been reported to be associated with adverse outcomes in several critical illnesses, but its prognostic significance in patients with asthma-related critical illness remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality in this population.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using two large intensive care unit (ICU) databases, the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD). The MIMIC-IV cohort included 2,355 ICU patients with a recorded diagnosis of asthma or status asthmaticus, and the eICU-CRD cohort included 1,575 patients as an external validation cohort. PeHR was defined as an hourly mean heart rate >100 beats per minute (bpm) for at least 11 hours within any consecutive 12-hour window. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality. A 24-hour landmark analysis, subgroup analyses, and multiple sensitivity analyses were further performed. As an exploratory supplementary analysis, elastic-net models based on routinely available variables from the first 24 hours after ICU admission were developed to stratify the risk of 28-day in-hospital mortality after the 24-hour landmark and to assess the incremental predictive value of PeHR.ResultsIn the MIMIC-IV cohort, 589 patients (25.0%) met the PeHR definition. The 28-day in-hospital mortality rate was higher in the PeHR group than in the no-PeHR group (11.4% vs. 2.2%). In the fully adjusted Cox model, PeHR was independently associated with increased 28-day in-hospital mortality [hazard ratio (HR), 2.75; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.76–4.28; P<0.001]. This association was also observed in the eICU-CRD external validation cohort (HR, 1.70; 95% CI: 1.10–2.90; P=0.041). In the 24-hour landmark analysis, the association was attenuated but remained statistically significant in both cohorts (MIMIC-IV: HR, 1.46; 95% CI: 1.11–1.93; P=0.009; eICU-CRD: HR, 1.34; 95% CI: 1.05–1.71; P=0.02). Sensitivity analyses generally supported the main findings. Exploratory elastic-net models showed good risk-stratification performance in both internal and external validation, but adding PeHR did not materially improve discrimination, precision-recall performance, or calibration.ConclusionsAmong patients with asthma-related critical illness, PeHR was independently associated with higher 28-day in-hospital mortality, with a similar direction of association observed in the external validation cohort and in the 24-hour landmark analysis. PeHR may serve as an easily obtainable and interpretable marker of short-term mortality risk; however, its incremental predictive value in multivariable prediction models appears limited and requires further prospective validation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Prolonged elevated heart rate (PeHR), defined as an average heart rate >100 beats per minute sustained for at least 11 hours within any consecutive 12-hour window, was independently associated with increased 28-day in-hospital mortality in patients with asthma-related critical illness.

• This association was observed in both the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV and eICU Collaborative Research Database cohorts and remained statistically significant in the 24-hour landmark analysis, although the effect size was attenuated.

• Sensitivity analyses generally supported the main findings. Exploratory elastic-net models showed good risk-stratification performance, but adding PeHR provided limited incremental predictive value.

What is known and what is new?

• PeHR has been reported as a prognostic marker in several critical illnesses, but evidence in asthma-related critical illness remains limited.

• This study provides evidence from two intensive care unit databases that PeHR is associated with short-term mortality in patients with asthma-related critical illness.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• PeHR may serve as a simple, non-invasive, and interpretable bedside marker of short-term mortality risk.

• PeHR should be interpreted as part of a broader clinical assessment rather than as an isolated predictor.

Introduction

Asthma is a heterogeneous disease characterized by chronic airway inflammation and variable airflow limitation, affecting over 300 million people worldwide and posing a significant public health burden (1). Although most patients achieve disease control with standardized treatment, some may experience life-threatening acute severe exacerbations requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission. Mortality among severe asthma ICU patients ranges from 6% to 8.4%, with higher rates in those requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) (2,3). Therefore, early risk stratification may assist clinicians in targeting timely interventions for vulnerable patients.

Heart rate, as an important physiological indicator reflecting autonomic nervous activity and cardiovascular stress status, is closely associated with prognosis across various critical illnesses. Studies have demonstrated that heart rate affects patient outcomes not only during stable periods, but its dynamic change patterns may carry even greater prognostic significance (4). Schmidt et al. (5) first proposed the concept of “prolonged elevated heart rate (PeHR)”, defined as a heart rate exceeding 100 beats per minute (bpm) sustained for at least 11 hours within any 12-hour window. Compared to single time-point measurements, PeHR better reflects the patient’s sustained sympathetic activation and cardiovascular stress burden.

In recent years, this concept has been validated across multiple critical illnesses. Studies have found that PeHR is independently associated with 90-day mortality in acute

Pancreatitis (6), adverse outcomes in severe pulmonary embolism (7), and 28-day mortality in acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) (8). This cross-disease consistency suggests that PeHR may represent a universal systemic stress burden phenotype in critical illness.

However, research on PeHR in asthma remains a knowledge gap. Although asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are both obstructive airway diseases, they differ substantially in pathophysiology. Asthma is characterized by airway hyperresponsiveness and variable, often reversible airflow limitation, whereas COPD is primarily characterized by persistent airflow limitation. Therefore, findings from COPD populations may not be directly applicable to patients with asthma-related critical illness. In addition, β2-agonists, a cornerstone therapy for acute asthma exacerbations, can induce pharmacological tachycardia, thereby adding complexity to the interpretation of heart-rate-based markers. These considerations support the need to specifically evaluate the prognostic significance of PeHR in patients with asthma-related critical illness.

Concurrently, advances in machine learning technologies have provided new tools for clinical risk prediction. Compared to traditional scoring systems, machine learning algorithms can more effectively capture complex non-linear relationships among variables and have demonstrated superior predictive performance in critical illnesses such as sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) (9,10).

Based on the above, this study aims to: (I) evaluate the association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality among patients with asthma-related critical illness using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) databases; (II) assess the robustness of this association through external validation, 24-hour landmark analysis, subgroup analyses, and multiple sensitivity analyses; and (III) develop and externally validate exploratory elastic-net models based on early ICU variables for risk stratification of 28-day in-hospital mortality after the 24-hour landmark, with additional assessment of the incremental predictive value of PeHR. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE and TRIPOD reporting checklists (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1301/rc).

Methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective cohort analysis using two open-access ICU databases: the MIMIC-IV (version 3.1) and the eICU-CRD (version 2.0). Both databases provide deidentified ICU data and are freely accessible for research purposes via PhysioNet. The MIMIC-IV database contains detailed clinical records of patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts, between 2008 and 2022. The eICU-CRD aggregates data from 208 hospitals across the continental United States collected between 2014 and 2015 via the Philips eICU telehealth program. One of the authors (L.T.) completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) certification (certification No. 15032374) and obtained authorized access to extract data from these databases. This study was exempt from institutional review board approval because it exclusively used deidentified data from publicly available databases. The requirement for informed consent was waived accordingly. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Patients with asthma were identified using diagnosis records based on International Classification of Diseases, 9th revision (ICD-9) codes 493.xx and 10th revision (ICD-10) codes J45.xx and J46.xx. Patients with asthma or status asthmaticus recorded at any diagnostic position were screened. Eligible patients were adults aged ≥18 years, had an ICU stay of at least 24 hours, and had adequate heart-rate data during the first 24 hours after ICU admission. Only the first eligible ICU admission was retained. Patients were excluded if they had cirrhosis, end-stage renal disease, malignancy, or a history of atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or supraventricular tachycardia. The same eligibility criteria were applied to MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed using pgAdmin 4 (version 8.12) through Structured Query Language (SQL) queries. Data from different tables were linked using database-specific identifiers, including subject_id, hadm_id, and stay_id in MIMIC-IV, and patient unitstayid in eICU-CRD.

The extracted variables were grouped as follows: (I) demographics: age, sex, and race; (II) vital signs: mean values of temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) during the first 24 hours after ICU admission; (III) laboratory tests: the first valid measurements obtained within the first 24 hours after ICU admission, including hemoglobin, red blood cell count, white blood cell count, platelet count, glucose, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, sodium, potassium, and chloride. Additional variables, including albumin, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, coagulation parameters, cardiac biomarkers, arterial blood gas variables, lactate, fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), and arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/FiO2, were also extracted to assess data availability; (IV) concurrent diagnoses: pneumonia, COPD, acute respiratory failure, and sepsis; (V) chronic comorbidities: myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, rheumatic disease, peptic ulcer disease, diabetes mellitus, and renal disease; (VI) treatments during the first 24 hours after ICU admission: β2-agonist use, cumulative albuterol-equivalent dose, systemic corticosteroid use, vasoactive-drug use, renal replacement therapy, and IMV. Individual vasoactive agents were further extracted, including norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine, dobutamine, phenylephrine, and vasopressin. Respiratory-support mode was defined as the highest level of respiratory support active at the 24-hour landmark and was classified as invasive ventilation, non-invasive ventilation, high-flow nasal cannula, supplemental oxygen, or no documented respiratory support; (VII) Disease-severity scores: Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), both calculated using data from the first 24 hours after ICU admission; and (VIII) temporal and outcome variables: ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, and 28-day in-hospital mortality.

To reduce potential bias, variables with ≥20% missingness in either database were excluded from the primary analyses. These variables included albumin, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, coagulation parameters, cardiac biomarkers, arterial blood gas variables, lactate, FiO2, PaO2/FiO2, and cumulative albuterol-equivalent dose. Missing values in the remaining predictor variables were imputed using the missForest algorithm. PeHR status, outcome variables, event times, eligibility variables, and database identifiers were not imputed.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was 28-day in-hospital mortality, defined as death occurring during hospitalization within 28 days after ICU admission. Mortality status was ascertained from hospital death records available in the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD databases. Patients who were discharged alive before day 28 were censored at the time of hospital discharge, whereas patients who remained hospitalized beyond 28 days without in-hospital death were censored at day 28.

For the 24-hour landmark analysis, patients who died or were discharged before 24 hours after ICU admission were excluded. Follow-up began at the 24-hour landmark and ended at in-hospital death, live hospital discharge, or day 28 after ICU admission, whichever occurred first.

For the exploratory machine-learning risk-stratification analysis, the prediction target was 28-day in-hospital mortality after the 24-hour landmark.

Definition of PeHR

The definition of PeHR was established based on existing clinical criteria and prior literature (8). PeHR was defined as an average heart rate exceeding 100 bpm sustained for at least 11 hours within any 12-hour window. To account for potential censoring due to death during an episode of elevated heart rate, patients who exhibited persistently high heart rates immediately prior to death were also classified into the PeHR group (extended definition). Patients who did not meet these criteria were assigned to the non-PeHR group.

For the 24-hour landmark analysis, a strict early PeHR definition was used: PeHR was defined as an average heart rate exceeding 100 bpm sustained for at least 11 hours within any 12-hour window during the first 24 hours after ICU admission.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality by visual inspection of quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using Welch’s two-sample t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and between-group differences were assessed using Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to estimate 28-day survival probability according to PeHR status, and survival differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test.

To construct a parsimonious multivariable Cox model, we used a hybrid variable-selection strategy combining data-driven screening and clinical judgment. Candidate covariates were first evaluated using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation and the 1-standard-error criterion (λ1se), as well as the Boruta algorithm. Variables consistently selected by both methods were considered data-driven candidate covariates. Sex was retained a priori based on clinical relevance. The exposure of interest, PeHR, was included in all Cox models and was not subjected to variable selection.

After variable selection, multicollinearity among variables included in the fully adjusted Cox model was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). For multi-level categorical variables, the generalized VIF (GVIF) was used, and the dimension-adjusted GVIF was calculated for comparability across terms. A VIF or dimension-adjusted GVIF equivalent ≥5 was prespecified as indicating potentially concerning multicollinearity. No variable exceeded this threshold, and therefore all selected variables were retained in the final models.

Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression models were used to evaluate the association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality. Model 1 included PeHR only. Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3, the main multivariable model, was further adjusted for temperature, SOFA score, SAPS II score, vasoactive-drug use, and respiratory-support mode. Model 4 additionally adjusted for β2-agonist use. The LASSO and Boruta variable-selection results are shown in Figures S1,S2, and the multicollinearity diagnostics are shown in Table S1.

The PH assumption for the multivariable Cox model was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals for each covariate and for the global model. Because the global test suggested evidence of non-PH, we performed a PH-corrected Cox sensitivity analysis. In this model, PeHR was retained as a fixed effect, and time-interaction terms with log-transformed follow-up time were added for covariates showing evidence of non-PH, including SOFA score, SAPS II score, and vasoactive-drug use. As an additional analysis not relying on the PH assumption, restricted mean survival time (RMST) was estimated with a truncation time of 28 days, using both unadjusted and covariate-adjusted pseudo-value regression models.

Subgroup analyses were performed using the multivariable Cox model for 28-day in-hospital mortality. Prespecified subgroups included age, sex, sepsis, pneumonia, acute respiratory failure, SOFA score, β2-agonist use, systemic corticosteroid use, vasoactive-drug use, and IMV. When the stratification variable was also included as a covariate in the main model, it was omitted from the corresponding subgroup model. Interactions between PeHR and subgroup variables were evaluated using likelihood ratio tests.

To reduce potential immortal-time bias and reverse causation from terminal tachycardia, a 24-hour landmark Cox analysis was additionally performed. Patients who died or were discharged within the first 24 hours after ICU admission were excluded. In this analysis, PeHR was defined using only heart-rate data from the first 24 hours after ICU admission, requiring an average heart rate exceeding 100 bpm sustained for at least 11 hours within any 12-hour window. Heart-rate measurements after the 24-hour landmark were not used to define exposure status. Follow-up began at the 24-hour landmark and ended at in-hospital death, live hospital discharge, or day 28 after ICU admission, whichever occurred first. The same sequential Cox modeling strategy was applied: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 additionally adjusted for temperature, SOFA score, SAPS II score, vasoactive-drug use, and respiratory-support mode at the 24-hour landmark; and Model 4 further adjusted for β2-agonist use.

Sensitivity analysis

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to examine the robustness of the association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality. First, to address potential reverse causation related to terminal tachycardia, we repeated the conventional Cox PH analyses after removing the extended PeHR definition. In this analysis, PeHR was defined only by the sustained heart-rate criterion, without classifying patients as PeHR solely because of persistent tachycardia immediately before in-hospital death. The same sequential Cox models used in the primary analysis were applied.

Second, because β2-agonist therapy may increase heart rate and may also reflect disease severity, we evaluated its potential influence using a three-step strategy. β2-agonist use was first included as an additional covariate in Model 4. We then performed subgroup analyses stratified by β2-agonist use and tested the interaction between PeHR and β2-agonist use using likelihood ratio tests. Finally, we fitted an extended Cox model using a counting-process start-stop format, in which β2-agonist exposure was modeled as a time-varying covariate. β2-agonist exposure was coded as 0 before the first recorded administration and 1 thereafter; patients without recorded β2-agonist use remained in the unexposed stratum throughout follow-up. The model included PeHR, time-varying β2-agonist exposure, and their interaction term, with adjustment for the same covariates as in the multivariable Cox model. Because β2-agonist administration may be affected by changes in clinical status, this analysis was interpreted as exploratory.

Third, to assess the influence of asthma phenotype definition, we conducted an exploratory broader phenotype-restricted analysis. This restricted cohort included patients with asthma recorded at any diagnostic position, documented β2-agonist or systemic corticosteroid use during the first 24 hours after ICU admission, and no pneumonia or sepsis as the principal or admission diagnosis. The conventional Cox analyses were repeated in this restricted population, both including and excluding the extended PeHR definition.

Fourth, to evaluate whether the results were sensitive to the duration threshold used to define elevated heart-rate burden, we performed an alternative duration-threshold analysis in the 24-hour landmark cohort. All definitions used hourly mean heart rate during the first 24 hours after ICU admission and retained the threshold of >100 bpm. The tested definitions included a 6-hour threshold, the primary 11/12-hour threshold, and a near-continuous 24-hour threshold defined as heart rate >100 bpm in at least 22 of the first 24 hourly bins. Follow-up began at the 24-hour landmark, and adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using the fully adjusted landmark Cox model.

To assess the consistency of the findings in an independent cohort, external validation was performed in the eICU-CRD cohort (n=1,575). The validation analyses included comparison of baseline characteristics, Kaplan-Meier survival curves with log-rank tests, multivariable Cox PH regression, and 24-hour landmark analysis. The external validation used the same covariate-selection and model-building strategy as the primary analysis to evaluate the robustness of the association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2.

Exploratory machine-learning risk-stratification analysis

As an exploratory supplementary analysis, we developed and externally validated elastic-net models for early risk stratification of 28-day in-hospital mortality after the 24-hour landmark. The prediction target was in-hospital death occurring after the 24-hour landmark and within 28 days after ICU admission. Patients who died or were discharged within the first 24 hours after ICU admission were excluded from this analysis.

The MIMIC-IV landmark cohort was used for model development and internal validation, whereas the eICU-CRD landmark cohort was used as an independent external validation cohort. Internal validation in MIMIC-IV was performed using repeated nested five-fold cross-validation. Specifically, the outer cross-validation loop was used to estimate model performance, and the inner cross-validation loop was used to select the elastic-net penalty parameters. To avoid information leakage, missing-value imputation, variable standardization, preprocessing, and hyperparameter tuning were performed using only the training folds within each outer cross-validation loop, and the same preprocessing parameters were then applied to the corresponding test fold. Candidate predictors were prespecified according to clinical relevance and routine availability within the first 24 hours after ICU admission, including demographics, vital signs, laboratory tests, early treatments, disease-severity scores, respiratory-support status, and PeHR. No univariable outcome-based screening or external feature-selection procedure was performed before cross-validation. Coefficient shrinkage and variable selection were conducted intrinsically within the elastic-net modeling framework, with the regularization parameters tuned by cross-validation in the training data. To evaluate the incremental predictive value of PeHR, two models were constructed: a clinical model without PeHR and a clinical model additionally including PeHR.

Missing predictor values were imputed using the median estimated from the training data, and continuous variables were standardized using the mean and SD estimated from the training data. Elastic-net penalized logistic regression was used for model development. For external validation in eICU-CRD, the final model was refitted in the full MIMIC-IV landmark cohort, with imputation, standardization, and model parameters fixed, and was then directly applied to the eICU-CRD cohort without retraining or hyperparameter tuning.

Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). Overall prediction error was evaluated using the Brier score, and calibration plots were generated to describe agreement between predicted probabilities and observed risks. To describe the relative contribution of predictors, standardized coefficients from the elastic-net model including PeHR were examined; coefficient magnitude was interpreted as predictive contribution rather than causal effect.

All machine learning model development and explainability analyses were performed using Python software (version 3.14.2).

Results

Study population

In MIMIC-IV, 8,021 adult ICU patients with a recorded diagnosis of asthma or status asthmaticus were initially identified. After applying the eligibility and exclusion criteria, 2,355 patients were included in the final cohort. A total of 5,666 patients were excluded because of an ICU stay <24 hours (n=1,778), non-first ICU admission (n=1,610), cirrhosis, end-stage renal disease, or malignancy (n=903), a history of atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or supraventricular tachycardia (n=985), or >10% missing heart-rate data during the first 24 hours after ICU admission (n=390).

In eICU-CRD, 3,279 adult ICU patients with a recorded diagnosis of asthma or status asthmaticus were initially identified. After applying the same eligibility and exclusion criteria, 1,575 patients were included as the external validation cohort. Patients were excluded because of an ICU stay <24 hours (n=893), non-first ICU admission (n=276), cirrhosis, end-stage renal disease, or malignancy (n=207), age <18 years (n=5), a history of atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or supraventricular tachycardia (n=238), or >10% missing heart-rate data during the first 24 hours after ICU admission (n=110). In the MIMIC-IV cohort, patients with PeHR were younger and had higher heart rate, temperature, respiratory rate, white blood cell count, glucose level, SOFA score, and SAPS II score than those without PeHR. Pneumonia, acute respiratory failure, and sepsis were more frequent in the PeHR group, whereas several chronic comorbidities, including myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular disease, and renal disease, were less frequent. Patients with PeHR were also more likely to receive β2-agonists, vasoactive drugs, IMV, and renal replacement therapy, and they had longer ICU and hospital stays. The 28-day in-hospital mortality rate was higher in the PeHR group than in the no-PeHR group (11.4% vs. 2.2%, P<0.001).

Similar patterns were observed in the eICU-CRD external validation cohort. Patients with PeHR were younger, had greater early physiological derangement, higher prevalences of acute respiratory failure and sepsis, longer ICU and hospital stays, and higher 28-day in-hospital mortality than those without PeHR (6.9% vs. 3.1%, P<0.001).

Relationship between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality

Figure 2 shows the Kaplan-Meier curves for 28-day in-hospital survival stratified by PeHR status. In the primary MIMIC-IV cohort (Figure 2A), patients with PeHR had a significantly lower in-hospital survival probability during follow-up than those without PeHR (log-rank test, P<0.001). Correspondingly, 28-day in-hospital mortality was higher in the PeHR group than in the No PeHR group (11.4% vs. 2.2%). This association was further supported in the external eICU-CRD cohort (Figure 2B), where patients with PeHR also showed lower in-hospital survival probability and higher 28-day in-hospital mortality than those without PeHR (6.9% vs. 3.1%; log-rank P=0.02), supporting the external consistency of the primary findings. Cox PH regression models were used to evaluate the association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality, as shown in Table 2. In the MIMIC-IV cohort, PeHR was significantly associated with an increased risk of 28-day in-hospital mortality in the crude model (Model 1: HR, 3.29; 95% CI: 2.20–4.93; P<0.001). The association remained significant after adjustment for age and sex (Model 2: HR, 4.42; 95% CI: 2.92–6.69; P<0.001). In the main multivariable model adjusted for age, sex, temperature, SOFA score, SAPS II score, vasoactive-drug use, and respiratory-support mode, PeHR remained associated with a higher mortality risk (Model 3: HR, 2.75; 95% CI: 1.77–4.28; P<0.001). Additional adjustment for β2-agonist use did not materially change the estimate (Model 4: HR, 2.75; 95% CI: 1.76–4.28; P<0.001).

In the eICU-CRD external validation cohort, PeHR was also associated with increased 28-day in-hospital mortality across all models. The HRs were 1.73 (95% CI: 1.07–2.81; P=0.03) in Model 1, 2.30 (95% CI: 1.40–3.77; P=0.001) in Model 2, 1.70 (95% CI: 1.10–2.91; P=0.041) in Model 3, and 1.70 (95% CI, 1.10–2.90; P=0.041) in Model 4. These findings suggest that PeHR was independently associated with higher 28-day in-hospital mortality among ICU patients with a recorded diagnosis of asthma, and the association was observed in both the derivation and external validation cohorts.

The PH assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. In Cox Model 3, the global test was significant in both cohorts, suggesting evidence of non-PH (MIMIC-IV: P=0.01; eICU-CRD: P=0.008). However, the PeHR-specific test was not statistically significant in either cohort (MIMIC-IV: P=0.054; eICU-CRD: P=0.63). The observed non-proportionality was mainly driven by severity-related covariates, including SOFA score, SAPS II score, and vasoactive-drug use (Table S3).

To address this issue, we performed a PH-corrected Cox sensitivity analysis in which PeHR was retained as a fixed effect and time-interaction terms were added for SOFA score, SAPS II score, and vasoactive-drug use. The association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality remained robust after PH correction, with an adjusted HR of 2.67 (95% CI: 1.73–4.12; P<0.001), which was similar to the conventional Model 3 estimate (HR, 2.75; 95% CI: 1.77–4.28; P<0.001) (Table S4). As an additional analysis not relying on the PH assumption, RMST analysis was performed with a truncation time of 28 days. Compared with patients without PeHR, patients with PeHR had fewer survival days within 28 days. The RMST difference was −2.55 days in the unadjusted analysis (95% CI: −4.01 to −1.08; P<0.001) and −2.41 days after covariate adjustment (95% CI: −3.94 to −0.89; P=0.002) (Table S5).

In the 24-hour landmark Cox analysis, 2,352 patients from MIMIC-IV and 1,574 patients from eICU-CRD were included after excluding patients who died or were discharged within the first 24 hours after ICU admission. In the MIMIC-IV cohort, 329 patients met the early PeHR definition, among whom 21 deaths occurred within 28 days. PeHR was associated with increased 28-day in-hospital mortality in the crude model (Model 1: HR, 1.53; 95% CI: 1.13–2.07; P=0.006). The association remained significant after adjustment for age and sex (Model 2: HR, 1.65; 95% CI: 1.03–2.72; P=0.043) and in the main multivariable model adjusted for age, sex, temperature, SOFA score, SAPS II score, vasoactive-drug use, and respiratory-support mode at the 24-hour landmark (Model 3: HR, 1.46; 95% CI: 1.11–1.93; P=0.009). Additional adjustment for β2-agonist use did not materially change the estimate (Model 4: HR, 1.46; 95% CI: 1.11–1.93; P=0.009).

In the eICU-CRD cohort, 350 patients met the early PeHR definition, with 16 deaths occurring within 28 days. PeHR was also associated with increased mortality risk in the landmark analysis. The HRs were 1.36 (95% CI: 1.14–1.63; P<0.001) in Model 1, 1.61 (95% CI: 1.21–2.13; P<0.001) in Model 2, 1.34 (95% CI: 1.05–1.71; P=0.02) in Model 3, and 1.34 (95% CI: 1.05–1.71; P=0.02) in Model 4. Overall, the association between early PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality was attenuated compared with the conventional Cox analysis but remained statistically significant in both cohorts (Table 3).

Significant interactions were observed for sepsis status, acute respiratory failure, and SOFA score. The association between PeHR and mortality was stronger among patients without sepsis (HR, 3.58; 95% CI: 2.08–6.17; P<0.001) than among those with sepsis (HR, 1.78; 95% CI: 0.83–3.78; P=0.14; P for interaction =0.01). Similarly, the association was stronger among patients without acute respiratory failure (HR, 4.64; 95% CI: 2.32–9.28; P<0.001) than among those with acute respiratory failure (HR, 1.84; 95% CI: 1.02–3.31; P=0.042; P for interaction =0.03). A significant interaction was also observed across SOFA strata, with a stronger association in patients with SOFA score ≤3 (HR, 6.16; 95% CI: 2.55–14.88; P<0.001) than in those with SOFA score >3 (HR, 1.98; 95% CI: 1.19–3.30; P=0.009; P for interaction =0.02).

No statistically significant interactions were observed for age, sex, pneumonia, β2-agonist use, systemic corticosteroid use, vasoactive-drug use, or IMV. Notably, β2-agonist use did not significantly modify the association between PeHR and mortality (P for interaction =0.92).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses generally supported the main findings, although the strength and precision of the estimates varied across different analytic settings.

After removing the extended PeHR definition, the association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality was attenuated in MIMIC-IV but remained statistically significant. The fully adjusted HR was 2.08 (95% CI: 1.34–3.22; P=0.001) in MIMIC-IV and 1.70 (95% CI: 1.10–2.90; P=0.041) in eICU-CRD (Table S6). These results suggest that the observed association was not solely driven by the extended definition related to preterminal tachycardia.

The potential influence of β2-agonist use was assessed through additional covariate adjustment, subgroup analysis, and time-varying covariate modeling. Additional adjustment for β2-agonist use did not materially change the main Cox estimates, and β2-agonist use did not significantly modify the association between PeHR and mortality in subgroup analysis. In the time-varying β2-agonist analysis, PeHR remained associated with higher mortality both before and after the first recorded β2-agonist administration, with adjusted HRs of 2.72 (95% CI: 1.55–4.77) and 2.87 (95% CI: 1.07–7.68), respectively. No significant interaction was observed between PeHR and time-varying β2-agonist exposure (P for interaction =0.92 by likelihood ratio test; P=0.92 by Wald test) (Table S7).

In the exploratory broader phenotype-restricted analysis, the direction of association in MIMIC-IV was consistent with the primary analysis, but estimates were imprecise because of the limited number of events. In the fully adjusted model, the HR was 2.72 (95% CI: 0.58–12.74; P=0.20) when the extended PeHR definition was included and 2.73 (95% CI: 0.58–12.76; P=0.20) after removing the extended definition. In eICU-CRD, fully adjusted models were not estimable because of sparse events and model separation (Table S8).

Finally, alternative duration thresholds were examined in the 24-hour landmark cohort. The primary 11/12-hour definition showed the most consistent association across both cohorts, with fully adjusted HRs of 1.46 (95% CI: 1.11–1.93; P=0.009) in MIMIC-IV and 1.34 (95% CI: 1.05–1.71; P=0.02) in eICU-CRD. The 6-hour threshold was significant only in MIMIC-IV, whereas the near-continuous 24-hour threshold was not statistically significant in either cohort, likely reflecting fewer exposed events (Table S9).

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study based on the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD databases, we systematically evaluated the prognostic significance of PeHR among patients with asthma-related critical illness. The main findings were as follows. (I) PeHR was independently associated with increased 28-day in-hospital mortality in both the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD cohorts, and the association persisted after adjustment for disease severity, respiratory-support status, vasoactive-drug use, and β2-agonist use. (II) This association was attenuated but remained statistically significant in the 24-hour landmark analysis, supporting the robustness of the finding after reducing potential immortal-time bias and reverse causation from terminal tachycardia. (III) Sensitivity analyses, including analyses excluding the extended PeHR definition, PH-corrected Cox models, RMST analysis, β2-agonist-related analyses, phenotype-restricted analyses, and alternative duration-threshold analyses, generally supported the main results. (IV) Exploratory elastic-net models based on routinely available variables from the first 24 hours after ICU admission showed good performance for early mortality risk stratification; however, adding PeHR did not materially improve predictive performance beyond established clinical variables. These findings suggest that PeHR may serve as an interpretable marker of short-term mortality risk in patients with asthma-related critical illness, while its incremental value in multivariable prediction models appears limited.

Prior studies have established independent associations between PeHR and adverse outcomes across diverse critical illness populations, including subarachnoid hemorrhage (5), acute pancreatitis (6), severe pulmonary embolism (7), and AECOPD (8), suggesting that PeHR may represent a disease-agnostic physiological signature of systemic stress burden. However, these investigations have largely focused on conditions driven by systemic inflammation or hemodynamic instability, whereas evidence in asthma-related critical illness remains limited. The present study extends the PeHR literature to an asthma-related critical care population by showing that PeHR was associated with increased short-term in-hospital mortality, with a similar direction of association observed in an independent external cohort. The pathophysiological basis for PeHR-associated mortality in severe asthma is multifaceted. Severe dynamic hyperinflation and resultant auto-PEEP elevation (1) substantially increase right ventricular afterload and, through ventricular interdependence, impair left ventricular filling, thereby reducing stroke volume (11). Compensatory tachycardia ensues to maintain cardiac output, rendering PeHR a sensitive indicator of critical airflow limitation and cardiopulmonary reserve depletion (12,13). Concurrently, sustained hypoxia and hypercapnia elicit excessive sympathetic activation, perpetuating tachycardia while increasing myocardial oxygen demand and the risk of arrhythmia (5,14). Systemic release of inflammatory mediators—including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)—further contributes by directly impacting the cardiac conduction system and promoting non- pharmacological tachycardia (15,16). Of note, PeHR patients in this study were younger yet more acutely ill, raising the possibility that younger patients with severe asthma may be particularly prone to sustained tachycardia as a consequence of heightened inflammatory activity and sympathetic drive; the mechanisms underlying this observation merit dedicated investigation.

The concomitant use of β2-agonists introduces an important interpretive challenge. Meta-analytic evidence demonstrates that β2-agonists meaningfully increase heart rate and lower serum potassium, and are associated with an elevated risk of adverse cardiovascular events (9); overreliance on short-acting β2-agonists (SABAs) has also been linked to increased exacerbation rates and mortality (17). In the present study, documented β2-agonist use was more frequent among patients with PeHR, suggesting that both medication exposure and underlying disease severity may contribute to elevated heart-rate burden. To address this issue, we evaluated β2-agonist use using complementary approaches. Additional adjustment for β2-agonist use in Model 4 did not materially change the association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality. In subgroup analysis, β2-agonist use did not significantly modify the association between PeHR and mortality. Furthermore, when β2-agonist exposure was modeled as a time-varying covariate, PeHR remained associated with higher mortality risk both before and after the first recorded β2-agonist administration, and the PeHR × time-varying β2-agonist interaction was not statistically significant.

Taken together, these findings suggest that although β2-agonist therapy may contribute to the occurrence of the PeHR phenotype through pharmacologically mediated tachycardia, the observed association between PeHR and higher mortality risk is unlikely to be fully explained by documented β2-agonist use alone. Nevertheless, residual confounding by treatment intensity cannot be excluded because detailed information on dose, route, frequency, and cumulative exposure was incompletely captured. Future studies with more granular medication data are warranted.

Another noteworthy finding was that the association between PeHR and mortality varied across clinical severity strata. In subgroup analyses, stronger associations were observed among patients without sepsis, without acute respiratory failure, and with lower SOFA scores, whereas the associations were attenuated among patients with sepsis, acute respiratory failure, or higher SOFA scores. These patterns may reflect differences in the clinical meaning of persistent tachycardia across illness contexts. In patients with sepsis or advanced organ dysfunction, elevated heart rate is common and may be driven by systemic inflammation, fever, vasodilation, shock, and vasoactive therapy (17), which could reduce the discriminatory value of PeHR as a distinct risk phenotype. By contrast, in patients without overt sepsis or severe organ dysfunction, sustained tachycardia may be a more specific marker of unresolved cardiopulmonary stress, increased work of breathing, or inadequate physiological compensation.

These subgroup findings suggest that the prognostic meaning of PeHR may vary according to clinical context. We therefore performed several sensitivity analyses to further examine whether the main association was robust to alternative exposure definitions, bias-reduction strategies, and phenotype restrictions.

The robustness of the observed association was supported by several sensitivity analyses. After removal of the extended PeHR definition, the association between PeHR and 28-day in-hospital mortality was attenuated but remained statistically significant, suggesting that the finding was not solely driven by preterminal tachycardia. Similarly, the 24-hour landmark analysis, which defined PeHR using only early heart-rate data and started follow-up after the landmark, reduced concerns about immortal-time bias and reverse causation while still showing a significant association in both cohorts. Additional analyses addressing PH violations, including PH-corrected Cox models and RMST analysis, yielded consistent conclusions. The broader phenotype-restricted analysis and alternative duration-threshold analysis further suggested that the association was directionally consistent across analytic definitions, although estimates became less precise in smaller or more restrictive subgroups. Taken together, these analyses support the robustness of the PeHR-mortality association, while also indicating that its magnitude depends on exposure definition, cohort composition, and clinical context.

In the exploratory machine-learning analysis, elastic-net models based on routinely available variables from the first 24 hours after ICU admission showed good performance for risk stratification of 28-day in-hospital mortality after the 24-hour landmark in both internal and external validation. Variables reflecting disease severity and early respiratory status contributed most strongly to the model, whereas PeHR showed a positive but relatively small standardized coefficient. Adding PeHR to the clinical model did not materially improve discrimination, precision-recall performance, or calibration, suggesting that its incremental predictive contribution was limited once established severity measures and early ICU variables were included.

These findings indicate that early routinely collected ICU data can support mortality risk stratification in asthma-related critical illness. PeHR may still be clinically useful as an easily interpretable bedside marker of sustained physiological stress, particularly when considered alongside disease severity, respiratory support, and treatment context.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective observational study, this study cannot establish causality, and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. This is particularly relevant for treatment-related factors, such as β2-agonist dose, route of administration, frequency, cumulative exposure, and prehospital medication use, which were incompletely captured in the databases. Second, patients with asthma were identified using ICD diagnosis codes recorded at any diagnostic position. Therefore, although we use the term “asthma-related critical illness” for readability, the study population should be interpreted as ICU patients with a recorded diagnosis of asthma rather than a strictly defined cohort admitted primarily for acute severe asthma or status asthmaticus. Although phenotype-restricted sensitivity analyses were performed, some degree of diagnostic or phenotypic misclassification may remain. Third, the primary outcome was 28-day in-hospital mortality rather than 28-day all-cause mortality. Because post-discharge deaths could not be reliably captured, patients discharged alive before day 28 were censored at hospital discharge. This may have underestimated the true 28-day mortality burden and introduced potential informative censoring, particularly if discharge practices differed across hospitals or between databases. Fourth, the PeHR definition was based on a standardized threshold used in previous studies, whereas the optimal heart-rate burden threshold may vary according to baseline heart rate, age, treatment exposure, and illness severity. Although landmark and sensitivity analyses were performed to reduce the influence of terminal tachycardia and exposure-timing bias, reverse causation cannot be completely excluded.

Fifth, several physiologically relevant variables, including arterial blood gas indices, lactate, FiO2, PaO2/FiO2, and cumulative albuterol-equivalent dose, were not included in the primary models because of substantial missingness. In addition, both MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD are derived mainly from ICUs in the United States, and caution is needed when generalizing these findings to other healthcare systems. The machine-learning analysis was exploratory, and its clinical utility requires further validation.

Conclusions

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study of ICU patients with a recorded diagnosis of asthma, PeHR was independently associated with increased 28-day in-hospital mortality. This association was observed in both the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD cohorts and remained significant after multivariable adjustment, additional adjustment for β2-agonist use, and 24-hour landmark analysis. Sensitivity analyses further supported the robustness of the association, although the effect estimates were attenuated in analyses designed to reduce reverse causation and immortal-time bias.

In exploratory machine-learning analyses, elastic-net models based on routinely available variables from the first 24 hours after ICU admission showed good risk-stratification performance for 28-day in-hospital mortality. However, adding PeHR to the clinical model did not materially improve predictive performance, suggesting that PeHR may serve primarily as an interpretable clinical risk marker rather than a strong incremental predictor in multivariable prediction models.

Overall, PeHR may help identify ICU patients with asthma who are at higher short-term mortality risk, but further prospective studies are needed to clarify its clinical utility and potential role in early risk assessment.
