Analysis of salivary metabolites and microbial characteristics in patients with dental fluorosis
Authors: Yingying Zhai, Dong Wang, Zhenzhen Li, Tianqiang Cui, Rongxiu Zhang, Yudong Liu, Huaqing Liu, Shanshan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to characterize specific alterations in the salivary microbiome and metabolome associated with dental fluorosis (DF), using dental caries (DC) as a comparative pathology.Materials and methodsA multi-omics analysis was performed on saliva samples from 99 individuals with DF, 100 with DC, and 100 healthy controls (HC). Lipidomic profiling and 16S rRNA gene sequencing were integrated, and the resulting datasets were analyzed using multivariate statistical approaches and correlation analyses.ResultsClear separation was observed in both the molecular and microbial profiles of saliva among the DF, DC, and HC groups. The DF group showed significantly higher levels of LPA (18:4) and 15-oxoETE, along with enrichment of Enterobacter, Alloprevotella, Leptotrichia, Aggregatibacter, Klebsiella, Selenomonas, Peptostreptococcus, Peptostreptococcaceae_[XI][G-5], Actinomyces graevenitzii, Prevotella pallens, Leptotrichia buccalis, Desulfobulbus sp._HMT_041, Prevotella intermedia, Ruminococcaceae_[G-1] bacterium_HMT_075, and Peptostreptococcus stomatis. However, the core microbial taxa and the identified differential metabolites showed only weak correlations.ConclusionsThe salivary microbiome and lipidomic profiles in individuals with DF differed significantly from those observed in both caries patients and healthy controls. The core microbial taxa and differential metabolites identified in the DF group were associated with various systemic diseases and inflammatory processes, respectively.Clinical relevanceThe salivary multi-omics signatures identified in this study provide new insights into the potential long-term health risks associated with dental fluorosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00784-026-06957-x.Keywords: Fluorosis, Metabolites, Saliva, Microorganisms

Introduction

Excessive fluoride exposure during tooth development, from pregnancy through approximately eight years of age, can result in dental fluorosis (DF) [1]. DF is not solely an oral condition; it also has multi-dimensional effects on patients’ quality of life. Tooth discoloration and structural abnormalities can compromise dental aesthetics and speech, while also contributing to psychological challenges such as social anxiety and reduced self-confidence [2]. The reduced wear resistance of fluorotic enamel may impair masticatory efficiency and impose dietary limitations [3]. The disease shows clear geographic clustering. In China, prevalence rates have been reported at 30.5% in Jilin Province [4]and approximately 50% in northern Anhui [5]. Internationally, DF affects up to 98% of children aged 6–12 years in Mexico and 53.4% of those aged 8–12 years in Thailand [6, 7].

DF is pathologically characterized by disrupted enamel formation and mineralization. This clinically manifests as a spectrum of changes ranging from chalky white opacities to brown staining and, in severe cases, pronounced enamel defects. Alterations in the enamel surface may influence microbial colonization and biofilm development on teeth [8, 9]. Saliva serves as a critical medium for the transmission and redistribution of microorganisms within the oral cavity [10]. Growing evidence indicates that salivary microbiome dysbiosis is closely linked not only to oral conditions such as periodontitis and oral lichen planus but also to systemic disorders, including respiratory diseases, gastritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and Alzheimer’ s disease [11–17]. These associations are mediated through biological pathways such as the oral-lung and oral-gut axes, highlighting the broader significance of the salivary microbiome. Therefore, characterizing the salivary microbiome in cases of dental fluorosis may help clarify microbial alterations associated with this condition and provide insight into its association with both oral and systemic health.

Dental caries (DC) is a plaque-mediated condition, and individuals with caries display distinct alterations in their salivary microbial communities [18–20]. These findings further emphasize the relevance of the salivary microbiome in oral pathology. However, they also indicate that the high prevalence of DC may confound investigations of microbial patterns associated specifically with DF. To address this issue, this study employed a dual-control design, including both healthy individuals and patients with DC, to more accurately identify core salivary microbial signatures associated with DF. Moreover, while microbiome analyses can characterize microbial composition and functional potential, they do not directly capture metabolic activity. Metabolomic profiling can complement this limitation. Therefore, this study also integrated metabolomic analysis to characterize salivary metabolite profiles in individuals with DF and to explore potential associations between salivary metabolites and microbial taxa.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was carried out at Bengbu Medical University in Bengbu, China, with approval from the Ethics Committee ([2021] KY172). Before the trial, all participants or their guardians provided written informed consent. Using data from China’ s Fourth National Oral Health Survey, the sample size was determined as N=(Zα/2/δ)²×P(1-P) to enable accurate assessment of prevalence. For a survey on DC prevention among university students, the specific parameters and outcomes were as follows: α = 0.05, Zα/2=1.96, and δ = 5%. The Fourth National Oral Health Survey has estimated that 44.42% of Chinese children aged 15 have permanent DC [21]. The prevalence of DC among university students was estimated at 50%. Based on this assumption, the calculated minimum size of the sample necessary for surveying DC in this population was 385. For the DF survey among university students, assuming α = 0.05, Zα/2=1.96, δ = 5%, and P = 13.4%, the minimum size of the sample was 178.

The study population was consistent with that described in our previously published article [5], although the objectives of this study differed. Oral examinations were conducted in 971 young individuals, of whom 957 completed the full questionnaire. The questionnaire collected data on sex, parental educational levels, frequencies of tooth-brushing and dental flossing, levels of sugar consumption, and utilization of toothpaste with fluoride. All oral examinations were performed by qualified dentists (Kappa > 0.8). The diagnosis of DC was in accordance with the International Caries Detection and Assessment System II (ICDAS) [22] (Table S1). Dean’s Index was utilized to assess the extent of DF, with classifications of normal (0), questionable (0.5), very mild (1), mild (2), moderate (3), or severe (4) [23] (Table S2). From the enrolled population, 100 healthy controls, 99 individuals with dental fluorosis, and 100 patients with DC were selected for subsequent lipidomic analysis. Participants were included only if they did not present with recurrent aphthous ulcers, pulpitis, periodontal disease, or other oral disorders; systemic or genetic diseases; or a history of antibiotic use or tobacco exposure within the previous six months.

Saliva collection

Before saliva collection, participants were instructed to abstain from food for at least two hours and to rinse their mouths to eliminate residual debris or food particles. Previous studies have shown that different forms of stimulation can alter the composition of collected saliva samples [24, 25]. Thus, unstimulated saliva is generally recommended when investigating disease-associated salivary biomarkers [26, 27]. For this reason, unstimulated saliva was used in this study. Each participant provided 3 mL of saliva, of which 1 mL was designated for microbiota investigation. Within 4 h of collection, the samples were shipped to the Bengbu Medical University laboratory, where they were kept at −80 °C.

Extraction and detection of lipid-soluble metabolites

After thawing on ice, frozen samples were vortexed for 10 s. One milliliter of MTBE: MeOH extraction solvent was combined with a 200 µL aliquot of each sample. After 15 min of vortexing, 100 µL of water was introduced, followed by additional vortexing for 1 more minute before centrifuging (12,000 rpm, 10 min). After collection and drying of the upper organic layer (500 µL), the precipitate was mixed with 200 µL of ACN: IPA for analysis on an LC-ESI-MS/MS system with a Thermo AccucoreTM C30 column and an ExionLC AD UPLC unit, connected to a QTRAP® mass spectrometer. The mobile phase was (A) acetonitrile/water with 0.1% formic acid and 10 mmol/L ammonium formate, and (B) acetonitrile/isopropanol with the same additions. Elution involved 80% A at 0 min, dropped linearly to 70% A by 2.0 min, 40% A at 4 min, 15% A at 9 min, 10% A at 14 min, and 5% A at 15.5 min (kept until 17.3 min). From 17.3 to 20 min, there was a quick re-equilibration to the starting 80% A. The temperature of the column was held constant at 45 °C, with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. ESI-QTRAP-MS was used for detection, with a 2 µL injection volume. Both positive and negative ionization modes were used for data acquisition with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray source, using ion spray voltages of 5500 V (positive) and − 4500 V (negative) and an ion source temperature of 500 °C. The collision gas (CAD) was adjusted to medium pressure, while the settings for nebulizing gas (GS1), turbo gas (GS2), and curtain gas (CUR) were 45, 55, and 35 psi, respectively. Polypropylene glycol (10 µmol/L for QQQ mode, 100 µmol/L for LIT mode) was used to calibrate the system. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) utilized a collision gas pressure of 5 psi, and the declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) were individually optimized for each transition. Dynamic MRM was employed based on established metabolite retention times.

Salivary DNA extraction and 16S rRNA sequencing

Meitewei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) processed all saliva samples for Illumina NovaSeq sequencing, DNA extraction, and library preparation. DNA was isolated from the saliva using the CTAB/SDS method. In short, specimens were defrosted at 4 °C and then centrifuged. After mixing with 1 mL of CTAB buffer and 20 µL of lysozyme, samples were dissolved at 65 °C in a water bath. After centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 min), 950 µL of the resulting supernatants were combined with equivalent amounts of phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1, pH 8.0). Following re-centrifugation, equivalent volumes of chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (24:1) were mixed with the supernatants. The supernatant was combined with isopropanol and incubated at −20 °C to precipitate nucleic acids, followed by a second centrifugation. Centrifugation was used to recover the precipitated DNA, and 1 mL of 75% ethanol was used twice to wash the pellet. Following two to three minutes of air drying at room temperature, the DNA was dissolved in ddH2O and subjected to 15 min of RNase A treatment at 37 °C. The 16S rRNA gene’s hypervariable V3–V4 regions were used in this study [28]. Forward (CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG) and reverse (GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT) primers with associated barcode sequences were used to amplify these areas. Libraries were generated using the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) with subsequent sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Throughout the experiment, both positive (ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Standard D6300) and negative (ddH2O) controls were processed to check for potential contamination [29].

Salivary lipidomics analysis

Raw data were assessed using Analyst® 1.6.3. (SCIEX). The reproducibility of lipid extraction and the stability of instrument performance were assessed by evaluating the total ion current (TIC) profiles of pooled quality control (QC) samples under both positive and negative ion modes. Metabolites were identified by matching the experimentally obtained retention time (RT), precursor ion (m/z), and product ion (m/z) with reference standards in established mass spectral databases, including MassBank, Backpack, the Netware Database (MWDB), and Metlin. All detected lipids were systematically categorized and annotated according to the guidelines of the Lipid Maps® consortium. Quantitative analysis of target lipids was conducted in MRM mode using triple quadrupole (QQQ) detection, employing characteristic ion pairs to ensure high specificity. Chromatographic peaks were analyzed using MultiQuant® (SCIEX), and peak area values were used to represent the relative abundance of each analyte.

Microbiome analysis

Bioinformatic processing was carried out as follows. Barcode and primer sequences were first removed using Perl v5. Merging of paired-end reads was undertaken using FLASH v1.2.7, and the merged sequences were treated as raw reads. Quality filtering was performed using fastp to generate high-quality clean reads. Chimeric sequences were subsequently removed with VSEARCH to obtain valid reads. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were inferred using the DADA2 algorithm within the QIIME 2 platform with default parameters. Taxonomic classification and subsequent analyses were conducted using the eHOMD database (https://www.homd.org/).

Statistics

Sex, parental educational level, frequencies of tooth-brushing, flossing, sugar intake, and fluoride toothpaste use, and the presence of DF and DC were examined for correlations using SPSS 20.0 (IBM, New York, USA). Differential metabolites were screened according to the following criteria: in two-group comparisons, metabolites were selected using the criteria of FDR-adjusted q-value < 0.05 (calculated using the Wilcoxon test) and a fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67. Stacked plots of differential lipids, correlation analyses, and alpha-diversity indices were generated using GraphPad Prism 10. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA; R 4.2.0), orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA; R 3.5.1), t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE; Python 3.9.21), and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP; Python 3.9.21) were conducted using Metware Cloud (https://cloud.metware.cn), a free online data analysis platform. Partial correlation coefficients (PCCs) were used to adjust for potential confounding factors. PCCs and Spearman correlation coefficients were computed using R 4.4.1. Venn diagrams were generated using Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html) to perform secondary classification and screening of the identified lipids. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis was conducted using R 4.4.1. GraphPad Prism 10 was used to create heatmaps and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.

Results

Study design

Oral examinations were performed on 971 young adults, of whom 957 individuals (515 females and 442 males) completed the full questionnaire. Participants were allocated to four groups: the healthy control (ICDAS score = 0 and Dean’ s Index = 0, n = 160), DC (ICDAS score > 0 and Dean’ s Index = 0, n = 347), and DF (ICDAS score = 0 and Dean’ s Index > 0, n = 160) groups, as well as a comorbidity group with both DC and DF (ICDAS > 0 and Dean’ s Index > 0, n = 290). From this cohort, 300 individuals were selected for salivary lipidomics and 16S rRNA gene sequencing analyses. Within the DF group, all 65 individuals with a Dean’ s Index ≥ 2 were included, although one sample was excluded due to insufficient saliva volume. Similarly, 36 participants were randomly selected from the 52 individuals with a Dean’ s Index = 1, yielding a total of 100 DF samples. Among these, one sample failed quality detection, and 99 cases (38 females and 61 males) were ultimately included in the DF group analysis. For the dental caries (DC) group, 100 participants (76 females and 24 males) were randomly selected from individuals with ICDAS > 4. The healthy control (HC) group consisted of 100 individuals (46 females and 54 males) randomly selected from the healthy population. In total, 299 participants (160 females and 139 males) were included in the final lipidomics and 16S rRNA gene sequencing analyses (Fig. 1, Table S3). To explore the lipidomic and microbial characteristics of saliva in dental fluorosis, 99 DF cases, 100 DC cases, and 100 HC individuals were selected from the original cohort of 957 university students. Saliva samples from these participants were subjected to integrated lipidomics and 16S rRNA gene sequencing analyses. Logistic regression was used to examine potential factors linked to the two oral conditions (DC and DF). The results indicated marked associations between sex and fluoride-containing toothpaste and DC, whereas no other variables showed significant correlations with caries. In comparison, dental fluorosis showed a sex-related difference (Table S4 and Table S5).

Workflow of this study

Comparative analysis of differential lipids

Eight hundred and eighty-nine lipid metabolites were identified overall. Classification at the Class I level showed that glycerophospholipids (GP) constituted the largest proportion (47.13%), followed by glycerolipids (GL) at 22.95% (Fig. 2a). Among the 20 most abundant metabolites, triglycerides (TG) were dominant across all three groups, with free fatty acids (FFA) representing the next most prevalent category (Fig. 2b). The OPLS-DA model indicated distinct separation among the metabolic profiles of the three groups, with the greatest distinction observed between the DC and HC groups. The DF group occupied an intermediate position between them (R²Y = 0.967, Q² = 0.919) (Fig. 2c). In total, 16 lipids were identified as being more strongly associated with DC (relative to DF and HC). In comparison, 40 lipids were more closely linked to DF (relative to DC and HC) (Fig. 2d). Lipids with an intergroup fold change greater than four were selected for further analysis, resulting in the identification of 10 characteristic lipids in the DC group and 17 in the DF group. Because DC was used as a positive control to identify DF-specific lipids and to explore their microbial associations, subsequent analyses focused on classifying the 17 DF-specific lipids into Class I and Class II categories (Fig. 2e).

Validation analysis of the 17 characteristic lipids in the DF group

To assess the capacity of the 17 selected characteristic lipids to distinguish DF from other health conditions, two dimensionality reduction approaches, t-SNE and UMAP, were applied to visualize group differences in the DF vs. DC and DF vs. HC comparisons (Fig. 3a–d). In the DF vs. HC analysis, both methods showed clear clustering and separation of the two groups within the two-dimensional space. DF samples were mainly distributed on the left side of the plots, whereas HC samples were concentrated on the right, indicating distinct molecular profile differences between DF and HC. A similar pattern was observed in the DF vs. DC comparison, where the two groups were also clearly separated, further suggesting differences in their metabolic compositions. Correlation analysis between metabolites and disease status revealed that, in the DF vs. HC comparison, only LPA (18:4) (PCC = 0.927, p < 0.001) and 15-oxoETE (PCC = 0.432, p < 0.001) were positively associated with DF. All other metabolites were negatively correlated with DF and included several classes such as carnitines, phosphatidylethanolamines (PE), and hexosylceramides (HexCer) (Fig. 3e). In the DF vs. DC comparison, LPA (18:4) (PCC = 0.917, p < 0.001) and 15-oxoETE (PCC = 0.268, p < 0.001) again showed positive correlations with DF. In comparison, the remaining metabolites showed negative correlations (Fig. 3f). These metabolites demonstrated strong discriminatory performance in distinguishing DF from both DC and HC, with area under the curve (AUC) values ≥ 0.686 (Fig. 3g and h).

Analysis of core microbiota

No significant differences in the Shannon, Simpson, or Inverse Simpson (InSimpson) diversity indices of genera were observed among the three groups (Fig. 4a). It was observed that 136 genera were shared across all groups. Unique genera included 18 in the DF group, 2 in the DC group, and 1 in the HC group (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, 10 genera were common to both the DF and DC groups, 23 to the DF and HC groups, and 4 to the DC and HC groups. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) at both the genus and species levels demonstrated clear separation between the DF and DC, and DF and HC, groups (p = 0.001) (Fig. 4c; Fig. S1). Figure 4d presents the top 16 DF-associated genera and species identified based on LDA scores (q < 0.05). Among these taxa, the DF group showed significant enrichment of nine and seven core microbiota at genus and species levels, respectively. The enriched genera were Enterobacter, Alloprevotella, Leptotrichia, Aggregatibacter, Staphylococcus, Klebsiella, Selenomonas, Peptostreptococcus, and Peptostreptococcaceae_[XI][G-5]; the enriched species were Actinomyces graevenitzii, Prevotella pallens, Leptotrichia buccalis, Desulfobulbus sp.HMT_041, Prevotella intermedia, Ruminococcaceae[G-1] bacterium_HMT_075, and Peptostreptococcus stomatis (Figs. 5 and 6).

Discussion

Northern Anhui is recognized as a region with a high prevalence of DF. In our previous investigation conducted at Bengbu Medical University, the prevalence of DF among university students in northern Anhui reached 47% (450/957) [5]. As a result, understanding the oral microecological characteristics of individuals with DF is important for safeguarding the health of populations living in areas with high fluoride exposure. In this study, a relatively homogeneous cohort of university students was selected to minimize demographic variability. DC, a highly prevalent plaque-associated bacterial disease, was included as a positive control to facilitate clearer identification of the microecological features of DF, a non-plaque-induced dental disorder.

Our results demonstrated that the salivary metabolic profiles of individuals with dental fluorosis differed significantly from those of both healthy controls and patients with dental caries. LPA (18:4) and 15-oxoETE were significantly elevated in the DF group. LPA serves as an important bioactive phospholipid mediator in multiple inflammatory diseases [30]. Notably, its biology in oral fluids has attracted increasing research interest. Studies have shown that LPA modulates oral epithelial barrier integrity via upregulating adhesion molecule expression in human oral keratinocytes, enhancing cellular migration, and suppressing cell proliferation [31]. Individuals with periodontal disease exhibit markedly elevated levels of multiple LPA species (including LPA 18:0, 16:0, 18:1, and 20:4) in saliva and gingival crevicular fluid, implying a potential role for these lipid mediators in periodontitis pathogenesis [32]. Further research identified that LPA 18:1 markedly regulates the expression of both pro‑inflammatory (IL‑6, IL‑8) and anti‑inflammatory (IL‑11) mediators in human gingival fibroblasts, supporting its potential involvement in controlling periodontal inflammation [33]. However, contrary results have also been reported; for instance, a marked reduction in gingival crevicular fluid LPA level was detected in periodontitis patients relative to healthy control subjects [34]. These findings suggest that LPA may act as an important modulatory factor in the local oral microenvironment, participating in the regulation of inflammatory responses in periodontal tissues. 15-oxoETE is another lipid mediator associated with inflammatory regulation, demonstrating both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties through mechanisms that include activation of the Nrf2–ARE axis and inhibition of the IKKβ/NF-κB pathway [35]. The elevated levels of both 15-oxoETE and LPA (18:4) detected in saliva suggest a persistent or ongoing local inflammatory response within the oral microenvironment of individuals with dental fluorosis.

This study demonstrated that the salivary microbial community structures differed significantly among individuals with DF, DC, and healthy controls in terms of both genera and species. Several microorganisms showing enrichment in the DF group have previously been reported to be closely linked to both local oral conditions and systemic diseases. For instance, Klebsiella and Enterobacter can colonize the oral cavity and are recognized as important pathogens involved in both hospital-acquired and community-acquired infections [36–40]. Aggregatibacter, Peptostreptococcaceae_[XI][G-5], and Desulfobulbus sp._HMT_041 are associated with periodontitis [41–44]. Prevotella pallens has also been reported to be associated with Parkinson’ s disease, halitosis, and Helicobacter pylori infection [45–47]. In comparison, Prevotella intermedia, in addition to periodontal disease, has been confirmed to be associated with respiratory diseases, knee osteoarthritis, and neurological diseases [48, 49]. Some microorganisms enriched in the dental fluorosis group have been confirmed to be associated with extra-oral diseases. For example, although Peptostreptococcus stomatis is an oral commensal bacterium, it has been reported to be associated with colorectal cancer [50, 51]; Leptotrichia is closely associated with head and neck cancer [52]; and Alloprevotella has been reported to be associated with chronic osteomyelitis and digestive system tumors [53, 54]. In summary, the microorganisms enriched in the saliva of individuals with dental fluorosis include not only opportunistic oral pathogens but also taxa linked to systemic inflammation, degenerative disorders, and even tumor-related conditions.

Saliva samples from 42 adolescents from Datian Junior High School in Qinglong County, Guizhou Province, China, were subjected to a comparative microbiome analysis by Wang Q. et al. (with 9, 14, and 19 in the healthy, mild DF, and moderate/severe DF groups, respectively). The three groups’ microbial beta diversity varied significantly, as reported earlier [55]. Ajrithironga et al. performed a similar microbiome comparative analysis of dental plaque samples from 24 children aged 6–15 years in Chiang Mai, Thailand (16 in the severe dental fluorosis group and 8 in the healthy group). Also, they observed significant differences in beta diversity [8]. However, the microorganisms significantly enriched in the dental fluorosis population identified in this study did not align with the results of the two previous studies. This disparity could be explained by variations in the age of the study population, regional variations, sample type (saliva or dental plaque), and sample size. With Peptostreptococcus stomatis, Ruminococcaceae_[G-1] bacterium_HMT_075, Leptotrichia, Aggregatibacter, and Peptostreptococcaceae_[XI][G-5] all significantly enriched in the DF group [56], with the findings (2021 cohort) being largely in agreement with our microbiome results based on an independent 2022 cohort. However, that study did not integrate metabolomics data for multi-dimensional analysis, which is the innovation of this study.

The highly abundant salivary metabolites (15-oxoETE and LPA (18:4)) and the enriched abundance of opportunistic pathogens in the oral microbial community of the dental fluorosis population suggest that this population may face a potential higher risk of oral inflammation. However, additional correlation analysis showed weak overall relationships between the significantly enriched metabolites in the dental fluorosis group and the extremely abundant microbial taxa (at the genus/species level). These results suggest that host secretion or metabolic regulation may be the primary source of core metabolites, rather than the catabolic activities of specific bacteria. As a result, there are no significant direct correlations between microbial abundance and these metabolites.

The development of DC has been linked to oral hygiene practices, sugar consumption, and parents’ education [57–60]. Logistic regression analysis indicated that using toothpaste with fluoride was positively associated with a higher DC risk. However, considering the cross-sectional design of this study, this relationship may reflect reverse causality: individuals with caries may be more likely to adopt preventive behaviors, such as using fluoride toothpaste. Females showed a significantly higher risk of dental caries than males, consistent with findings from the 2022 epidemiological survey [56]. An association was also observed between paternal education level and dental fluorosis. As an important indicator of family socioeconomic status, the father’s education level may indirectly influence fluoride exposure by affecting the family’s living environment. Therefore, strengthening health education for families with lower levels of education may be important in preventing and managing DF. Because metabolomic and microbiome profiles can be influenced by sex [61, 62], and the cohort included in the omics analyses of this study displayed a significant sex imbalance, partial correlation analysis was applied to minimize the influence of sex and other background variables. This approach estimates the partial correlation coefficient (PCC) between variables while controlling for one or more covariates, allowing the independent relationships among the variables of interest to be more accurately assessed [63, 64].

This study has several limitations. First, DF is a pathological condition resulting from exposure to excessive fluoride during tooth development (before the age of 8). The participants’ current levels of fluoride exposure were not assessed. Therefore, the salivary metabolite and microbial profiles observed in individuals with dental fluorosis cannot be directly linked to fluoride exposure status at the time of sampling. Second, despite our implementation of rigorous quality control procedures, we cannot completely rule out the potential for artificial sources of LPA. In addition, causal inference is limited by this study’s cross-sectional methodology. Future longitudinal cohort studies and functional experiments are needed to confirm whether dental fluorosis-related disturbances in the saliva microbiome and the enrichment of several disease-associated bacterial communities actually impact patients’ long-term oral health outcomes or increase the long-term risk of systemic diseases through mechanisms such as the oral-gut or oral-lung axes.

Conclusion

This study identified features of the metabolome and microbiome in the saliva of individuals with DF, using healthy individuals and those with DC as paired controls. Furthermore, the core microbiota identified were linked to a number of previously documented dental and systemic illnesses, including lung infections, intestinal problems, and periodontitis. These results suggest that this population may face potential long-term health hazards; further prospective cohort studies are required to confirm this.
