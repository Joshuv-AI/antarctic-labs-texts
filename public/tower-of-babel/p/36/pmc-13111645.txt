Stochastic modeling of epigenetic memory
Authors: Simone Bruno, Domitilla Del Vecchio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Here, we review mathematical models of epigenetic memory, focusing on chromatin modifications as key mechanisms to achieve long-term maintenance of epigenetic states. After reviewing the main stochastic modeling frameworks, we focus on stochastic models of chromatin modifications to analyze residence time in memory states, which underpins the long-term maintenance of epigenetic information. We review these concepts through increasingly complicated chromatin modification circuits, including histone modifications, DNA methylation, and their combination.

Introduction

Epigenetic memory is the ability of cells with the same genotype to keep different phenotypes through subsequent cell divisions and time1,2. It is critical in multi-cellular organisms and allows to maintain the identity of all the different cell types of our body, avoiding inter-conversions or loss of phenotype, which could lead to illnesses such as cancer3–5. Cell identity is often encoded in patterns of gene expression, with cell-fate specific genes being active in the corresponding cell types and silenced in the other cell identities6. In addition to being critical for safeguarding the gene expression patterns of our cell types from deleterious changes, it also covers a central role in a variety of other biological processes. These include the immune response, which leads to persistent changes in the gene expression of immune cells following antigen exposure7–9, memory formation in the brain, which often relies on long-term changes in the gene expression of brain cells that modulate synaptic plasticity10,11, and it could become a powerful tool for synthetic biology12.

Given its importance, molecular mechanisms and models of epigenetic memory have been researched ever since. In this review, we provide an overview of approaches to model epigenetic memory, including multistability in gene regulatory networks (GRNs) involved in cell fate decisions, chromatin modification circuits, and their combination. We argue that critical questions in epigenetic memory, particularly concerning the temporal maintenance of epigenetic states, necessitate the use of stochastic models for their investigation. We thus review common stochastic modeling formalisms used to address this and related questions mainly through examples pertinent to memory. We then focus on an in-depth treatment of chromatin modifications as a way to lock gene expression states. Chromatin modification processes can be regarded as synergistic with GRNs, wherein GRNs create the set of possible gene expression states, each corresponding to a different cell identity, and chromatin modifications help lock each state in place. We thus review published models of chromatin modification circuits, highlighting how parameter regimes and the features of the circuit architecture control maintenance of gene expression states.

Why stochasticity matters in modeling epigenetic memory

In the context of dynamical systems, memory is captured by the concept of multistability, that is, the coexistence of multiple attractors of the trajectories of a dynamical system13–15. In a multistable system, the long-term behavior depends on the initial system’s configuration. In this sense, the system “remembers” where it started from. A typical example is that of a bistable system, in which two deterministically stable steady states co-exist, which can be achieved by positive feedback15. In practice, however, biomolecular systems are subject to intrinsic noise within the biomolecular reactions16–18. When this noise and the consequent stochastic effects are not negligible, the system is better described in terms of the molecular count nX of species X, rather than its concentration x. The two are related by x = nx/Ω, where Ω represents the system volume. In the example above, the Hill function results from the quasi-steady state approximation of the reversible binding of the transcription factor U to the gene expressing X. When U is bound, RNA polymerase is recruited to the gene to enhance transcription13. This process is stochastic in nature since the factor U will bind to the gene with some probability that depends on how many molecules of U, nU, and how many copies of the gene are in the system. The more copies, the higher is the probability that a molecule of U will “bounce” against one of the gene copies. Similarly, RNA polymerase will be recruited to the gene with a probability that increases with the number of RNA polymerase molecules. As a consequence, the emergent dynamical behavior of biomolecular systems is often shaped by the presence of this intrinsic noise and the bistable system is an example of this. In fact, even when the system’s state nX is located at one of the two deterministically stable equilibria, noise will be continuously pushing it out of the equilibrium. In a situation in which the other equilibrium is “nearby”, the state can jump all the way to this new equilibrium under the influence of noise (Fig. 1c). It is even possible that the state will jump back and forth so often that even if the deterministic system is bistable, the trajectory spends most of its time transitioning back and forth. In this situation, the frequency at which the nX values are visited in time can be highest at some intermediate value, thereby resulting in a stationary distribution of nX that is weakly bimodal (two peaks that are not well separated) as seen in Fig. 1c, or even unimodal (one peak only). By contrast, when the two deterministically stable equilibria are further from each other, it will be less probable that the noise will push the state nX all the way to the other stable state. In this situation, transitions between the states will have very low probability, which results in a stationary distribution of nX values that has two almost isolated peaks (Fig. 1d).

This qualitative reasoning can be made quantitative by computing the transition rates between the two deterministically stable equilibria, defined as the inverse of the mean first-passage times (MFPTs)19 between the two states. For a deterministic system, the transition rate between the two stable equilibria is equal to zero since trajectories cannot spontaneously leave a stable steady state. In a stochastic system, however, these rates are non-zero. More specifically, when the two steady states are more separated, MFPTs are larger and the transition rate is lower, thus closer to the deterministic limit (Fig. 1e).

Since epigenetic memory requires that the system’s state resides for long time in one of its stable attractors, one critical question has been what molecular mechanisms and related system’s properties ensure that transitions between stable states have low probability. The stochastic modeling frameworks that we review in the next section help address this question.

Let X(t) be a random vector whose i-th entry Xi(t) denotes the molecular count of species Si at time t, ni(t). The stochastic dynamics described by the CME can be studied not only by solving the CME analytically or numerically, but also through direct simulation of the underlying reaction events. In this context, a sample path refers to one possible realization of the system’s stochastic time evolution, specifying the state of the system at all times over the simulation interval. Exact sample paths can be generated via Gillespie’s Stochastic Simulation Algorithm (SSA)23 (Fig. 1c, d), which, at each step, (i) computes all reaction propensities aj(n) for the current state n, (ii) draws the waiting time to the next reaction from an exponential distribution with rate ∑ℓaℓ(n), (iii) selects the next reaction index j with probability aj(n)/∑ℓaℓ(n), and (iv) updates the state to n + νj, where νj is the net change vector for reaction j. These steps are repeated until the desired final time is reached (see the SI - Section S.1 for the MATLAB code corresponding to this system).

In summary, the SCRN framework provides an exact, discrete-state description of biomolecular systems, capturing both rare switching events and molecular fluctuations.

Stochastic differential equations

While the SCRN/CME framework described above keeps track of the number of molecules of each species over time, it can often be challenging to work with, if not intractable, especially for complex reaction networks13,24. As a consequence, a number of approximations to the chemical master equation have been developed to provide analytically tractable descriptions of the stochastic system’s dynamics. One such approximation is given by Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs), which treat molecular counts as continuous variables and model intrinsic noise via Gaussian perturbations. Models of molecular mechanisms underlying epigenetic memory

In this section, we review stochastic models of molecular mechanisms implicated in epigenetic memory. The Waddington epigenetic landscape offers a conceptual understanding of the natural cell differentiation process28 and has served as a source of inspiration for several mathematical models of epigenetic memory. Within the epigenetic landscape metaphore, different cell types are represented by the location of valleys in a hilly landscape. Differentiating cells are spheres that start at the top of the landscape when they are pluripotent and then roll down the hill, loosing their potential, to reach the different valleys representing terminally differentiated cell types. Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) have been implicated in the definition and maintenance of different cell types by shaping a potential landscape6,29,30. Examples include the positive feedback network with transcription factors (TFs) Oct4, Sox2, and Nanog, which acts to maintain the pluripotent stem cell state31, the mutual antagonism network comprising TFs PU.1 and GATA1 (Fig. 3a), which controls the differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) into myeloid/lymphoid or erythroid/megakaryocyte cell types32, and many others6.Fig. 3Epigenetic memory by GRNs and chromatin modifications.a The PU.1/GATA1 GRN governing the switch between erythroid and myelomonocytic fates32. b Nullclines of a two-state ODE model of the GRN in a (see SI - Section S.1 for the model details and parameter values). c Probability distributions π of the GRN in panel a obtained by simulating with the SSA23 the set of chemical reactions corresponding to the ODE model used to plot the nullclines in (b). The dynamical properties of these GRNs have been investigated through deterministic models in which the system’s state is given by the concentrations of the TFs constituting the GRN. Systems of differential equations thus describe the rate of change of these TFs’ concentrations, which is given by production and decay33. The production term typically takes the form of a Hill function for activation, repression, or combinatorial regulation13,30. An example of this is given in Fig. 1a. Under certain structural properties, such as the presence of positive feedback loops15, multi-stability arises, wherein each stable steady state represents a configuration of TFs expression associated with a specific cell fate30. For example, under certain parameter regimes, the PU.1/GATA1 GRN can admit three stable steady states, in which the stable attractor state where GATA1 > PU.1 would represent the erythroid fate, the attractor where PU.1 > GATA1 would represent the myeloid fate, and the middle attractor can be interpreted as the bipotential progenitor cell32 (Fig. 3b). Each of these stable steady states can be also regarded as minima of a potential landscape, recalling the Waddington epigenetic landscape34,35.

From a qualitative point of view, the depth of the wells in the potential landscape influences how probable is for a state residing at the bottom of the well to be displaced by the influence of noise36. If the noise intensity is large, or the depth of the well is low, then the probability of exiting the state increases and therefore it also becomes more probable to reach a different steady state or alternate cell fate. We illustrate this point by showing stochastic simulations performed with the SSA of the PU.1/GATA1 GRN for cases of lower and higher noise level (Fig. 3c). In this example, we consider a parameter regime in which the deterministic dynamics exhibit two stable steady states. While the stationary probability distribution for lower noise level shows two clearly distinct peaks, indicating binary memory, the distribution for higher noise level does not show clear peaks, indicating that the state will just fluctuate between the two deterministically stable steady states. Given that in practice noise can be large since genes and TF levels can be at low molecular counts, it has remained a question what mechanisms allow a system to lock the state for long time.

A line of work called the “slow promoter kinetics” has addressed this question by abstracting the gene’s promoter as either being bound or not by a TF and by then assuming that the kinetics between bound and unbound states are much slower than gene expression. The physical justification for this assumption is that bound and unbound states are “locked” in these configurations due to the conformation of the chromatin, which makes a gene be open or closed for transcription18,37,38. Nevertheless, questions remain as to how the promoter states may be preserved during the process of cell division and DNA replication since many DNA-bound proteins are displaced during DNA replication39, and hence the gene state may be lost.

Therefore, researchers have considered the possibility that additional molecular mechanisms may be at play to maintain epigenetic information, which do not require the continuous presence of TFs bound at a gene’s promoter. Chemical modifications to histones, the protein complexes around which the DNA is wrapped, and DNA methylation are such possible mechanisms1,40. In particular, histone modifications have an ability to affect gene expression by controlling chromatin compaction and hence the accessibility of a promoter to transcriptional machinery. Activating modifications keep a loose chromatin structure that is permissive to transcription, while repressive histone modifications and DNA methylation tighten chromatin and shut down gene expression40,41. Most importantly, histone modifications can self-propagate through cell divisions and time since they recruit, through so called “reader” proteins, writers of the same modification (ref. 42, Fig. 3d). As a consequence, after DNA replication, any modified histone in the daughter cell can modify a nearby unmodified new histone, thereby recapitulating the histone modifications of the mother cell. Similarly, DNA methylation has an ability to copy itself on the daughter DNA strand after DNA replication (Fig. 3e). This is possible by the activity of the DNMT1 enzyme, which quickly copies the CG methylation pattern on the nascent DNA strand, thus counteracting the dilution of DNA methylation that would otherwise occur due to DNA replication and cell division43.

Stochastic models of chromatin modification circuits

In this section, we review published stochastic models of chromatin modification to capture epigenetic memory. We first consider histone modifications only, then review stochastic models of DNA methylation dynamics, and then we focus on models that couple histone modifications with DNA methylation in order to determine how their interactions affect epigenetic memory.

Histone modifications models

Histone modifications are chemical changes to histone proteins, which are structural components of chromatin, the complex of DNA and proteins in the nucleus. These modifications, including acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, and others, alter how DNA is packaged and how genes are expressed40. These modifications can be modeled by enzymatic reactions wherein an unmodified histone can be de novo modified by the action of writer enzymes (lysine methyltransferases, KMTs, for methylation and histone acetyltransferases, HATs, for acetylation)2, and can be recruited to DNA by sequence-specific TFs (ref. 44, Chapter 6). These modifications can be actively removed by eraser enzymes that can be recruited by opposing modifications (histone demethylases for de-methylation and histone deacetylases for de-acetylation)2. A key feature of histone modifications is that they can be copied through a read-write process, wherein a modified histone is bound by a reader protein, which, in turn, recruits writers of the same modification (ref. 45, Chapter 22 and ref. 46). An example of opposing modifications, for which these molecular properties have been characterized in details in the past years42,47–50, is H3K4 methylation/acetylation, histone modification associated with active chromatin state (ref. 45, Chapter 3), and H3K9 or H3K27 methylation, histone modifications associated with repressed chromatin state51–53.

One of the first mathematical models describing histone modifications is the one proposed by Dodd et al.54, then reused in multiple follow-up studies55–61. In this model, each nucleosome is an individual unit that can be unmodified, modified with activating or repressive histone modifications. Then, in the resulting circuit, whose diagram is represented in Fig. 4a, each modification auto-catalyzes itself and inhibits the other one, generating positive feedback loops. Consistent with experimental observations, these studies revealed that, despite the fast histone modification kinetics, chromatin states remain remarkably stable. They also demonstrated that positive feedback loops, generated by the read-write mechanisms of histone modifications, are essential for generating bistability and thereby preserving memory. Finally, the authors also highlighted that nonlocal recruitment, i.e., the fact that histone modifications can enhance the establishment of the same modification on nonadjacent nucleosomes, is required to reduce the threshold of positive feedback strength needed for bistability and provides robustness against dilution during DNA replication54. This finding has been further supported by more recent studies62–65, some of which also highlighted how cell-cycle duration strongly affects memory robustness, with shorter cycles increasing the effective dilution of chromatin marks and thereby reducing memory stability63. Other studies66–68 adopt a slightly different modeling approach, focusing on the interplay of transcription with a single histone modification (H3K27) and its different methylation levels, rather than on the direct mutual inhibition between activating and repressive marks. More precisely, these works hypothesize that transcription itself antagonizes the activity of PRC2, a KMT that catalyzes methylation of H3K27, without requiring activating histone modifications, by promoting H3K27 demethylation. Nevertheless, consistent with earlier studies54 and experimental data50,69, they conclude that mutual antagonism between transcription and PRC2-mediated methylation creates positive feedback and bistability, yielding stable active (low H3K27me3) and repressed (high H3K27me3) states.

In more recent studies70,71, the authors developed a SCRN model of histone modifications, explicitly coupling activating and repressive marks via recruited erasure and analyzed how time-scale separation controls both the stationary distribution and the mean duration of memory. Their stochastic analysis shows that, as basal erasure becomes slow relative to auto-catalysis (small ε in Fig. 4a), the stationary distribution becomes strongly bimodal, with peaks in correspondence to the active and repressed states, indicating the emergence of binary memory. Furthermore, for smaller ε, the duration of this memory increases. The model also includes gene expression from the activating mark (H3K4me3) and shows that TF-mediated positive autoregulation adds another positive feedback loop that extends active state memory and improves robustness to repressive inputs70.

Other SCRN models have been developed with the same interactions, but also with higher granularity, tracking each H3-H4 tetramer face as an independent unit rather than treating the nucleosome as a whole. For instance72, developed a SCRN model for the dynamics of the HMR locus in Saccharomyces cerevisiae73, in which each tetramer face can carry H4K16 acetylation (associated with gene activation) or H3K79 methylation (associated with gene silencing).

Finally, motivated by the presence of bivalent promoters in development and cancer74,75, other studies developed a stochastic simulation model to investigate the effect of the coexistence of activating (H3K4me3) and repressive (H3K27me3) marks on the same promoter76, and in positively autoregulated genes77,78. Compared to the models described above, this framework explicitly couples bivalency with gene expression and confirms that positive feedback leads to bistability. It further shows that bivalency can increase the effective cooperativity (can make the gene’s self-activation more switch-like), thereby extending the bistable regime77.

DNA methylation models

DNA methylation is a covalent modification in which a methyl group is added to the 5-carbon of cytosine, most commonly at CpG dinucleotides in mammalian cells79. This epigenetic mark plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression, and often serves as a defining feature of the repressed chromatin state80. This modification is de novo established by DNA methyltransferases DNMT3A/DNMT3B81 and is maintained during DNA replication by maintenance enzyme DNMT182,83. Demethylation can occur passively, through DNA replication, or actively, via TET enzymes51,84,85. The DNMT1-mediated maintenance mechanism results in low, often negligible, demethylation rate86,87, enabling DNA methylation patterns to be stably propagated over many cell divisions and then making this modification a central component of long-term epigenetic memory.

Some of the earliest mathematical descriptions of DNA methylation dynamics were developed in the 1990s and early 2000s88,89. These frameworks were deterministic, population models describing how the fractions of unmethylated, hemimethylated, and fully methylated CpG dyads change over cell divisions. The model by Otto and Walbot88 was primarily theoretical, exploring how maintenance fidelity (the probability that a methylation mark on the parent strand is correctly copied to the daughter strand during DNA replication) and de novo methylation influence the long-term stability of methylation patterns. Genereux et al.89 extended this framework in a more quantitative direction by fitting it to experimental data, enabling site-specific estimation of maintenance and de novo methylation rates. Both studies highlighted maintenance methylation by DNMT1 as a key determinant of maintenance fidelity since, although highly efficient, it is imperfect, and this leads to gradual methylation loss.

Building on these early population-level frameworks, more recent deterministic models coupled DNA methylation with gene expression and expanded the set of methylation states to capture active demethylation pathways90–92. More precisely, these frameworks extended the classical three-state description (unmethylated, hemimethylated, and fully methylated CpGs) to include intermediate oxidation states produced by ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes. These enzymes actively convert methylated cytosines into forms not recognized by DNMT1 during replication51,84,85. As a result, the maintenance machinery cannot restore these marks, and thus these cytosines are gradually lost over successive cell divisions, providing an active pathway for methylation erasure51.

The mechanisms considered in these deterministic models were later adapted to develop stochastic DNA methylation models. In particular70, proposed a SCRN model, whose high-level structure was also outlined in the review by ref. 93, in which the basic unit is a CpG, either methylated or unmethylated, with two enzymatic reactions governing state transitions: de novo establishment by DNMT3A/DNMT3B and erasure via passive and active mechanisms (Fig. 4b). The active erasure term accounts not only for the effect of TET enzymes (as in previous models) but also incorporates the inhibitory effect of MBD proteins on TET-mediated DNA demethylation94–96. Finally, some stochastic models, suitable only for computational analysis, have been developed to address specific biological questions. For instance, in ref. 97 a three-parameter stochastic decay model was introduced to study age-related changes in DNA methylation, modeling the purely stochastic flipping of CpG methylation states over time at the single-cell level.

Combined histone modifications - DNA methylation models

Building upon the histone modification and DNA methylation circuits that we separately described above (Fig. 4a, b), the SCRN model in ref. 70 integrates them by including the known molecular cross-talks between these two types of modification, resulting into the complete chromatin modification circuit showed in Fig. 4c. More precisely, the complete circuit incorporates the recruited erasure between activating histone marks and DNA methylation, wherein methylated CpGs recruit methyl binding proteins such as MeCP2, which binds with HDACs, and MBD2, which binds with the NuRD complex, both promoting histone deacetylation (refs. 98,45, Chapter 21). Furthermore, it includes that methylated H3K4 can recruit TET1 to nearby methylated CpGs, enhancing active demethylation (ref. 51, Chapter 17 and ref. 85). Additionally, the model accounts for the cross-catalysis between DNA methylation and repressive histone mark H3K9 methylation. In fact, MBD1 and MeCP2 bind methylated CpGs and recruit H3K9 methyltransferases (SETDB1 and Suv39H1)98,99, while DNMT3 binds HP1 (H3K9me3 reader), allowing H3K9me3 to enhance DNA methylation100.

The stochastic analysis of this model70,101 shows that when the basal decay of all chromatin marks is sufficiently slower than the auto and cross-catalysis of these marks, memory emerges, that is, active and repressed gene states can coexist. The duration of this memory is stochastic, with a mean value that increases as the time-scale separation grows, with a larger effect observed for the repressed state. This bias towards the repressed state represents the main difference from the histone-modification-only circuit71 (Fig. 4a) and arises from the coexistence and cross-catalysis between repressive histone modifications and DNA methylation, and it is further strengthened by the relatively slower decay rate of DNA methylation86.

In a follow-up study102 aimed at disentangling the contributions of the individual repressive marks, reduced models were developed to isolate each modification and determine its individual effect. The results show that the absence of DNA methylation substantially reduces the bias of the system’s stationary distribution toward the repressed chromatin state and shortens the temporal persistence of that state’s memory. The absence of repressive histone modifications also reduces this bias, although to a lesser extent due to the slower decay rate of DNA methylation86, and, in addition, decreases the variability in the time required to reactivate a repressed gene in response to an activating input, indicating that these modifications contribute to the high variability of state reactivation time102.

More recently, new studies aimed to investigate whether chromatin modifications can support analog epigenetic memory, that is, the possibility for a gene to stably maintain a continuum, graded range of expression levels, and to provide a mechanistic understanding of the molecular feedback structures that enable it. To this end, in refs. 87,103 a model based on the SCRN framework from70 was developed (Fig. 4d), with two key modifications: DNA methylation was assumed to have negligible decay86,87 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu^{\prime} =0$$\end{document}μ′=0 in Fig. 4c), and the positive feedback from H3K9me3 to DNA methylation was removed (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha^{\prime} =0$$\end{document}α′=0 in Fig. 4c), consistent with experimental observations86,87. Their analysis showed that analog memory is possible with this topology, with DNA methylation determining the long-term mean gene expression level, and histone modifications modulating its variability87,103. This is because, under these conditions, DNA methylation neither decreases (due to negligible demethylation) nor increases (due to the removal of positive feedback from H3K9me3), remaining effectively “frozen” at a given level. Consequently, it acts as a stable input that sets the mean gene expression level, while variability arises solely from the dynamics of histone modifications, which remain the only marks whose levels can still change. Specifically, when histone marks’ erasure is slow relative to auto-catalysis, the histone modification circuit becomes bistable at intermediate DNA methylation grades, producing broader gene expression distributions under the influence of noise.

The SCRN model proposed in ref. 70 was also integrated into gene regulatory networks. For instance, its chromatin modification circuit was incorporated into the OCT4 gene regulatory network to determine how specific chromatin modifiers (TET1 and JMJD2) affect the efficiency and variability of the cellular reprogramming process104.

In other works, stochastic simulations were conducted to investigate the effect of DNA methylation on bivalent promoters105. This study identified histone modification kinetics as a major driver of gene expression heterogeneity, consistent with ref. 102. In this model, DNA methylation removes both activating and repressive histone marks. This occurs because a bivalent promoter is characterized by the simultaneous presence of H3K4me3 and H3K27me376, and for the latter, unlike for H3K9me3, experimental studies have shown lack of cross-catalysis with DNA methylation86. Consequently, the introduction of DNA methylation eliminates both modifications, leading to an inaccessible, fully repressed promoter state. Active DNA demethylation can restore accessibility, after which the promoter may stochastically resolve into either an active or a repressed configuration, depending on the transcription factor context and histone modification dynamics105.

Conclusions and Discussion

We have provided an overview of commonly studied molecular mechanisms of epigenetic memory emphasizing how stochastic modeling is critical for understanding the underpinning of long-term maintainance of epigenetic states. We have then focused the review on a more detailed description of chromatin modifications as a major molecular process implicated in epigenetic memory. Using the stochastic chemical reaction network modeling framework, we have described how time scale separation among the constituent processes dictate binary memory and long-term maintenance of ON/OFF gene expression states. We have then discussed how analog memory arises when the positive feedback loop between DNA methylation and repressive histone modifications is broken. The summary table of Fig. 4e reports how the key parameters of each circuit type affect the stationary distribution and mean first-passage times (MFPTs) between active and repressed states.

There are other mathematical models that have been used to study cell fate decisions and maintenance, which we have not covered in this review. These include the Fokker-Planck Equation (FPE) model, which is an alternative, yet equivalent, formulation of the CLE. The FPE also treats molecular counts as continuous variables, but rather than directly describing the evolution of the state space, it describes the time evolution of the probability density function13,21. Another one is the Probabilistic Boolean Network (PBN) model of GRNs, that, in contrast to SCRN and SDE models that track continuous molecule counts, adopts a qualitative, logic-based stochastic approach. More precisely, PBNs model each gene as simply “on” or “off” and focus on the probabilities of observing particular binary expression patterns106–108. There are also additional biomolecular processes that could be implicated in epigenetic memory that we have not described in this review. These include long non-coding RNAs, for which the reader is referred to recent reviews109, and chemical modifications to the RNA. RNA methylation, in particular, is known to crosstalk with DNA methylation by recruiting editors of DNA methylation, which could affect the DNA methylation grade and its stability110–112. These processes could be added to the chromatin modification model in future work to provide experimentally testable hypotheses.

A major motivation for creating low-dimensional, often reduced, models of biological processes is to produce experimentally testable hypotheses and to guide the design of engineered systems. For example, if time scale separation between constituent processes drives the extent of the residence time in memory states, one could design experiments to change these timescales, for example by altering cell growth or enhancing the removal of specific marks. These perturbations could be done within an endogenous system59,113 or could be applied in a more specific way on an engineered genetic test system86,87. The advantage of the latter approach is that perturbations to epigenetic states can be targeted specifically to the system, while minimizing the effect of genetic and cellular context, and that changes in system’s parameters could be achieved by engineering different genetic variants of the same test system. This model-driven “build-to-understand” approach allows for systematic investigation of how system parameters affect behavior, in a similar way to what can be done in a mathematical model. Discrepancies between experiment outcomes and mathematical model predictions typically result in the addition, removal, or modification of molecular processes represented in the model, thereby providing substantial biological information. A limitation of such engineering approaches is that experimental findings need to be recapitulated within endogenous settings in order to assess broader biological relevance.

Although this review focused on chemical reaction models of chromatin dynamics, there is growing appreciation that the spatial organization of chromatin affects epigenetic memory by influencing long-range recruitment and spreading of epigenetic marks114. Indeed, some studies started to investigate the interactions between the three-dimensional (3D) structure of the genome and chromatin modification processes115–118. So far, these frameworks have focused mostly on a single type of modification or a single regulatory mechanism. A unified and tractable model that integrates 3D chromatin architecture, multiple interacting epigenetic modifications, such as histone modifications and DNA methylation, and their downstream effects on gene expression still remains to be developed. In particular, modeling frameworks that include the noise intrinsic to the chromatin modification reactions as well as the spatial dynamics of chromatin spreading remain largely unexplored. Yet, these will be necessary to dissect how epigenetic memory takes shape at the genome level.

Validating these models experimentally on either endogenous gene loci or on semi-synthetic chromosomal reporter systems will likely require high resolution in the granularity at which chromatin modifications can be assessed in space and time. Although in principle single-cell nucleosome, methylome, and transcriptome sequencing (scNMT-seq)119 can determine the correlation between DNA methylation and gene expression120, temporal data of single genes in single cells will be necessary, and will require also information on histone marks. Single-cell chromatin state profiling is starting to appear121,122, but methods to obtain these measurements concurrently with DNA methylation and transcription quantification will be needed.

Finally, precisely editing the chromatin state via targeted recruitment of writers or erasers of chromatin marks is still difficult due to several factors including lack of accurate control of the concentration of chromatin editors, problems with DNA accessibility, and context-dependent interactions of these editors with the molecular players of chromatin modification circuits87,123. Addressing these challenges will be essential for making theoretical predictions more easily testable in defined experimental settings.

Looking ahead, predictions gained from these models may ultimately guide the design of engineered systems that enable to program epigenetic states with high accuracy. These systems may be employed for directed cellular differentiation into tissues to repair damage, for creating organoids that could be used for medical applications or laboratory studies, or for training immune cells to target specific antigens.
